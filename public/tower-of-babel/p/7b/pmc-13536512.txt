The Ties That Rhyme: Duality in Symbolic and Structural Networks of Grime Music
Authors: Tom R. Leppard, Andrew P. Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDo birds of a feather really sing together? Musicians face two competing pressures in the pursuit of success: conforming to genre norms to meet audience expectations and distinguishing themselves to attract the attention of listeners. These opposing logics may shape how artists choose their collaborators. Partnering with similar artists can reinforce genre alignment, while working with dissimilar collaborators may help artists stand out. This paper explores how these dynamics play out through the lens of homophily in musical collaboration. Drawing on network analysis, we develop a framework for measuring cultural proximity using song‐level and collaboration data sourced from Spotify's API. Focusing on the understudied genre of Grime, we investigate whether the pull of similarity—the tendency to form homophilous ties—overrides competitive pressures within the genre.

Introduction

The field of music presents a unique opportunity to explore the extent to which creators take risks with collaborative partners because there is a benefit associated with standing out, but a risk associated with novelty. Sociologists of culture and organizations have routinely discussed how legitimacy processes are implicated in the success or failure of various fields (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Godart et al. 2023; Hannan and Freeman 1984; Zuckerman 1999). Developing novel forms of culture involves the blending of styles, creating novel connections, and exposing one's way of cultural production to new audiences that may or may not appreciate the new direction. These risks manifest a ‘liability of newness’ (Stinchcombe 1965) that shapes artists' decisions regarding collaboration. Newness here refers to stylistically heterophilous collaboration partnerships leading to novel forms of cultural production that may present great rewards for successful connections, but higher risks of failure. Standing out too far from others in the genre by mixing too many styles can erode the popularity of artists (Silver et al. 2022; Younkin and Kashkooli 2020). Therefore, collaborating with dissimilar alters may be attractive, but there is the risk of standing out so far as to transgress the taste and expectations of listeners.

One potential solution to the liability of newness, suggested by McPherson et al. (2001), is homophily. Here, homophily refers to collaborations with stylistically similar others leading to a homogeneity of cultural production. One way to ensure the survival of a genre of music is to reinforce the musical boundaries associated with the genre. Artists actively work together to ensure they construct a shared memory with other artists (Gibson 2014) and present listeners with a coherent set of musical characteristics to easily identify the genre from others (Peterson 1999). This could be deemed as less risky for artists since it may serve to reify the boundaries of a genre creating a secure space for similar artists to perform their music. However, since the success of musicians relies largely on attracting listeners, there may be a penalty of ‘fitting in’ too much as well. If artists work together with other similar artists, do they become lost in a crowd of others and therefore unidentifiable by listeners?

We turn to the case of Grime music, a UK‐based rap genre that emerged from the Reggae and Garage scene in the early 2000s to examine why artists choose particular collaborators. We theorise that homophily related to cultural production ‐ in this case the similarity of artists' songs ‐ could overcome the risks associated with newness inherent to the music industry (see Silver et al. 2022). Conversely, it is possible that Grime artists select culturally dissimilar collaborators to ensure that their identity does not dissolve through collaborating with many similar alters.

To explore the types of collaborators that Grime artists select, we use a unique dataset of collaborations coded from the Spotify profiles of 122 UK‐Grime artists alongside song‐level data scraped using the Spotify developer API from 2001–2022. We present a process whereby we identify the cultural proximity of artists based on their usage of similar sonic forms each year. We then construct a pairwise dataset of all artists indicating the co‐usage of musical characteristics in their songs in any given year. Finally, given the pairwise nature of our data, we use dyadic logistic regression to examine whether proximity (or co‐usage) of musical characteristics impacts the propensity of artists to work together.

Background Literature

Culture and Structure in Creative Industries

Across creative industries, there is a recurring duality between cultural similarity and structural connectivity. Cultural similarity refers to shared aesthetic, symbolic, or stylistic conventions that locate creators within a recognisable field. Structural connectivity refers to the networks and affiliations that link those creators together (Crossley 2008; Hodson 2019; Park et al. 2007; South et al. 2021). These two dimensions are intertwined where similarity informs the connective structure of artists who ‘band together’ intro genres of similar music (Lena 2014), and social structure, in turn, reinforces what counts as legitimate similarity (Peterson 1999).

The emergence of creative groups and genres illustrates this duality of structure and culture. Cultural fields develop through the joint use of points of difference and points of parity that position creators as legitimate members of a field while differentiating them from others. Within any creative field, subgroups form around distinctive aesthetic markers such as musical styles, visual motifs, linguistic codes, or thematic concerns. These constitute the field's symbolic boundaries. For example, schools of art (Roberts and Strandvad 2022), fashion movements (Godart et al. 2023), literary genres, and musical styles (Lena and Peterson 2008) all depend on the shared cultural symbols that define who belongs and who does not (see also Bourdieu 1993; Becker 1984).

In the case of music, a genre's distinctiveness is reinforced by the creators who affiliate with it and reproduce its conventions. As more artists adopt and perform the genre's stylistic codes, they collectively solidify its identity. Newcomers signal their belonging by demonstrating points of parity with established participants mirroring shared sounds, lyrical themes, or aesthetics that mark them as authentic members of the in‐group (Gibson 2014). Artists may also demonstrate affiliation through symbolic ties to one another, such as lyrical references or collaborations. Hodson (2019) shows how artists reference each other in lyrics, producing a symbolic network that maps the genre's cultural field, with its most central nodes corresponding to its most prominent figures.

These processes underscore the mutual constitution of culture and structure. In other words, that artists collectively produce the symbolic markers that define the genre, and these shared markers form the basis of social connectivity among them. Cultural similarity thus has a dual function of signalling individual authenticity and reinforcing collective boundaries. Importantly, the success of individual creators often depends on the popularity of the group to which they belong (Deshmane and Martínez‐de‐Albéniz 2023). In this sense, affiliation with a genre is not merely symbolic since it structures a creator's opportunity, creative identity, and access to audiences.

A parallel duality exists among consumers of culture. Here, cultural similarity refers to shared taste, and shared taste in art and cultural expression encourages social interaction (Lizardo 2006; Vaisey and Lizardo 2010). For example, book clubs emerge through shared interests in reading, genre preferences, and the collective belief in the value of discussing literature (Long 2003). Within the world of music, N. Mark (1998) demonstrates in his paper ‘Birds of a Feather Sing Together’ that individuals form social ties based on shared musical preferences. While Mark's study illustrates the duality between cultural taste and interaction, its title also suggests a potential third duality—that of cultural production and collaboration. In the current study, we extend the logic of the duality between cultural similarity and social structure to the creators themselves. We examine how the same principle of similarity (being of ‘the same feather’) might shape patterns of artistic collaboration (‘singing together’).

To explore this, we next turn to theories of homophily in social networks and consider how this principle operates in creative industries. We then situate homophily within the competitive ecology of music, where artists must balance the benefits of similarity (shared audiences, legitimacy and genre coordination) against its risks (competition for the same listeners and creative redundancy). Understanding how cultural similarity influences both collaboration and competition reveals how creative fields sustain themselves and evolve over time.

Competition and Risk in Creative Industries

The structure of the music industry is shaped by similarity and proximity, as artists coalesce around shared social foci. Musicians, as Lena (2014) notes, ‘band together’ around a musical genre, which serves as both a cultural and structural anchor, generating collectives of creators within and across industries (see also Giuffre 1999; Lutter and Weidner 2021; Park et al. 2007). Yet, this very similarity also generates competition among creators whose success depends on attracting a larger audience than their counterparts. As Montanari and Mizzau (2007, 36) observe, the creative industries are a ‘complex, turbulent, and highly competitive sector.’ Scholars have characterised this environment as a ‘creative ecology’ (Godart et al. 2023; N. Mark 1998; N. P. Mark 2003; Roberts and Strandvad 2022) because its niche‐like structure mirrors the dynamics of competitive ecosystems (McPherson 1983; Popielarz and Neal 2007).

Within this ecology, creators compete for consumers. Artists strive for popularity vying for more accolades than their peers, and genres themselves contend for listeners (N. Mark 1998). This competition creates a tension. To succeed, artists must conform to consumer expectations by signalling their legitimate membership in a genre (Peterson 1999) while also differentiating themselves from others to attract attention (Silver et al. 2022; Younkin and Kashkooli 2020). These dual pressures—conformity versus distinction—produce competing expectations for how cultural similarity might shape collaboration.

To study this tension, we conceptualize stylistic similarity through the sonic characteristics of artists' songs—the measurable attributes of sound, rhythm, and energy that capture the ‘style’ of musical production (discussed below). This approach enables us to examine whether artists who produce sonically similar music are more or less likely to collaborate. On the one hand, the safety of similarity may draw stylistically similar artists together; on the other, differentiation may push artists toward stylistic diversity.

Homophily and the Safety of Similarity

Across numerous social settings, homophily—the tendency for similar actors to connect—is one of the most robust principles of network formation. Simmel (1955) first described how social structure emerges through propinquity, the tendency of proximate individuals to interact. Building on this, network scholars have demonstrated that similarity in attributes, whether demographic, cultural, or attitudinal, facilitates tie formation (Blau 1977; McPherson 1983; McPherson et al. 2001). Social foci such as workplaces, schools, or religious groups concentrate interaction (Feld 1981), and within these foci, homophilous subgroups emerge among those who share salient similarities (Kossinets and Watts 2009; Lizardo 2006; Uzzi and Spiro 2005). For example, sharing world views tend to form friendships (Vaisey and Lizardo 2010) and similarity in scholarship leads to job opportunities (Gondal 2018).

Extending this logic to creative settings, we consider whether similarity in cultural products rather than similarity in people can generate social connections. In music, stylistic proximity between artists may serve as a catalyst for collaboration. Similar sound profiles lower the costs of coordination, as artists share compatible tempos, rhythms, and production norms. Collaborating with stylistically similar others also reduces the risk of audience alienation. Listeners often prefer familiarity (Peterson 1999; Silver et al. 2022), and artists who deviate too far from genre conventions face a ‘liability of newness’ (c.f. Stinchcombe 1965). By working with stylistically similar peers, artists can innovate within recognizable bounds while ensuring that their collaborations align with audience expectations.

Moreover, uncertainty may encourage homophily. Kanter (1993) observed that the uncertainty in elite corporate settings drives homophilous networks of managers. In the music industry, the uncertainty of success as artists vie for popularity may further reinforce stylistic homophily. Just as male managers in uncertain environments sought predictability by hiring other men (Kanter 1993), musicians may prefer stylistically similar collaborators to minimize creative and commercial uncertainty. Stylistic similarity lowers the transaction costs of collaboration, allowing for smoother coordination and clearer creative alignment. Put simply, singing with ‘birds of the same feather’ (N. Mark 1998) could be a safer and more predictable choice.

Heterophily and the Strategy of Difference

There is the possibility that the ecology‐like context of the creative industry might invert the logic of homophily. In a genre already characterized by high stylistic homogeneity, working with similar others may represent a liability of sameness rather than a liability of newness. Artists are at risk of blending into the crowd when their music and collaborators are too alike, costing them the opportunity to attract new listeners through innovation. While in inter‐genre collaborations ‘newness’ is drastically different (i.e., different musical forms like rap vs. opera), same genre artists are already producing, to some degree, similar music. Therefore, homophily in this instance means that artists elect to work with alters whose products are sonically very similar to their own. Thus, within‐genre variations of musical style are small but meaningful stylistic distinctions for listeners (Silver et al. 2022). Thus, diversity in collaborations can become a key avenue through which artists can gain a competitive advantage if they maintain their genuine musical identity in their performance but introduce newness through heterophilous collaboration.

In this sense, collaborating with stylistically different peers may serve as a strategy of differentiation. An artist working with others who produce similar music means that they are working with their closest competitor. Since similar artists often target overlapping audiences, working together can expose each artist's listeners to a direct competition ‐ akin to Coke partnering with Pepsi (two leading brands in cola production). Such collaboration may dilute individual brands and redirect audience attention toward others within the same niche. Instead, by partnering across stylistic subspaces, artists can reach new audiences, generate novelty, and distinguish themselves in a field of similar alters. Cultural similarity in music, therefore, might precipitate arm's length relationships between artists because the competition is sharpest when they overlap. Stylistic differences between collaborators may offer strategic advantage in the struggle for visibility and distinctiveness within competitive cultural markets.

Grime the Case and Sample

To test these competing hypotheses, we turn to an understudied genre of music called Grime. Grime is a genre of rap music that emerged from London in the United Kingdom during the early 2000s. It began as an underground genre from the east side of London. Artists like Wiley and Dizzee Rascal formed groups of rappers who would share a microphone on pirate radio sets like Rinse FM (Target 2019) or during live underground shows. From these groups individual artists branched off and began building their own career with Dizzee Rascal releasing the first ever Grime album in 2003. Since then, Grime has grown in the number of artists who joined the genre and has garnered mainstream success through artists like Stormzy (Stormzy 2018). Despite its mainstream success, Grime has maintained many of its grassroots characteristics like the artists' collaborative nature. It is not uncommon for Grime songs to feature multiple artists. Similarly, Grime artists regularly engage in shows of dominance over other artists in meets called ‘clashes.’ These clashes occur either through recorded songs (like the Lord of the Mics series of artist‐artist clashes), direct matchups via radio platforms or live events. Artists continue this ordinance today (Target 2019) with some of the most notable Grime clashes occurring in the early 2020s between Grime godfather Wiley and new star Stormzy (Mahale 2020).

At the time of its emergence, the UK rap scene was dominated by a genre called Garage music. Early on, Grime artists were turned away from performing at Garage events which drove them underground and, eventually, to create their own genre. The process of the genre evolving from Garage included the definition of cultural forms and characteristics of what Grime music is and how it differed from other genres. Early on, songs like ‘Wot u call it’ by Wiley, and ‘That's not me’ by Skepta made it known that Grime music positioned itself as different from mainstream music at the time. Most importantly, Grime purportedly adopted a much faster tempo, grungier instrumental, and higher overall energy than other music genres of its time. For example, Grime artists pride themselves on rapping between 138 and 140 beats per minute (Target 2019). This differentiation through the usage of musical characteristics typifies the evolutionary process of music (Crossley 2008; van Venrooij 2015) where a genre emerges from another, differentiates itself and solidifies new conventions that they become known for. In this instance, the musical uniqueness of Grime with its standard of high energy, 140 beats per minute, high frenetic beat, and aggressive valence differentiated these underground rappers from any others during the early 2000s.

Grime is an appropriate case for such a study for two main reasons. First, the unique musical profile of Grime music mean that it is easy for artists to identify when others are authentic. Second, since artists in the genre collaborate frequently, it provides a good opportunity to examine whether there exists a duality between similarity in the symbolic (musical similarity) networks and in the structural networks (collaborative). In other words, whether the artists who collaborate with others gravitate towards others who are signalling similar cultural forms or keeping them at arm's length.

The Sample

This study pulls all focal data from the streaming service Spotify. There are multiple platforms, streaming services, and charts where these artists may be operating. However, for us to isolate a relationship between two types of networks, the cultural and the structural, it is necessary to ensure that the context of the networks is the same. Therefore, we pull both song data and collaboration data from Spotify.

To identify Grime artists on Spotify we triangulated six methods in hopes of gathering a representative sample. We mixed random, purposive, and archival techniques. First, we use the get_genre function on the R package Spotifyr which yielded 50 artists associated with the Grime genre on Spotify.Second, we listed the 46 artists from the Grime Classics playlist from Spotify. Third, we turned to the four volumes of This is Grime UK and listed the 179 artists from those. Fourth, we list all 70 artists who are on the Lord of The Mics albums available on Spotify (volumes 3–8). Fifth, we took a list of Grime crews from Target (2019) and gathered the names of artists associated with those crews from sources like hiphopdatabase.fandom.com or Wikipedia.com. This gave us 146 artist names. Sixth, we gathered the 63 names of the artists from Collins and Rose (2016) ‘This is Grime’ book. Once we removed duplicates gathered across these methods, we were left with 377 artists. However, since we focus on intra‐genre collaborations within Spotify, we restricted the sample to artists who have Spotify profiles and those who are listed as Grime artists from the get_artist function on Spotifyr's package. This process left us with a final analytic sample of 122 Grime artists.

Mapping the Symbolic and Structural Networks in Music

The symbolic connections that exist between artists could involve a variety of dualities. For example, through a mutual affiliation between artists and record labels, individual musicians and bands, or even between artists and sociopolitical topics referenced in song lyrics. Given Grime's focus on specific types of musical characteristics (like its claim to a fast tempo and energy, see Target 2019), in this current paper we explore the similarity of musical style and form as a potential driver for dual connectivity that exists between Grime music and the musicians. Since our focus is on the duality between musical form and collaboration, we collected data on the musical characteristics of the songs produced by the 122 artists in the sample alongside the collaborations that occurred between these artists across 22 years (2001–2022).

Musical Networks

We collected data on every song available on Spotify from the 122 artists in the sample using Spotify's API and the Spotifyr packages get_artist_audio_features function. This yielded 83,186 songs. We then took the average of each characteristic, so each artist had one value for each metric each year. We recognise that an artist may produce songs that span multiple genre categories. An artist might produce one album that has only Grime songs on it or an album that blends multiple conventions across genres. Using a mean for continuous measures of the musical characteristics of that artist, therefore, captures this variation, but is subjected to the outliers of an artist's musical range. For example, an artist may produce most of their music at 140 bpm but a few songs at 90 bpm and have an average that may not truly represent the type of music they record most. Meanwhile, a modal approach to this would not offer a true representation of the artist's tendency to engage in multiple conventions. In other words, it would not capture the variation an artist has, if any. We want to capture the tendency of artists to group together with others based on their similarities. Since the balance of conventions with unconventional musical characteristics appears to be a salient factor that might influence this (Silver et al. 2022), capturing an artist's variety is more demonstrative of their blending of conventionalities. Therefore, we use the artist's mean for the continuous measures and mode for the categorical musical characteristics.

The Spotify developer's API records 11 musical characteristics of each song. Specifically, it measures acousticness, danceability, energy, instrumentalness, key, liveness, loudness, speechiness, tempo, time signature, and valence. A detailed description for each of these musical characteristics is given below from the Spotify website (Spotify Developer):

Acousticness

A metric between 0 and 1 that measures whether the song is acoustic. A measure close to one indicates that the song is likely acoustic. An acoustic song is one that primarily uses instruments like acoustic guitar, piano, or violin, rather than electronic or synthetic sounds.

Danceability

A metric between 0 and 1 that measures how danceable the musical element to the song is. For example, how strong the beat is, the rhythm, and the tempo. A value of 1 indicates high danceability.

Energy

A metric between 0 and 1 that measures that captures the feel of a song, meaning the intensity and activity of the song. A high energy song has a value close to 1. High‐energy tracks are generally fast, loud, and have complex rhythms, often found in rock, electronic, and dance music.

Instrumentalness

A metric between 0 and 1 that measures the extent to which a song contains more instrumental elements than vocal elements. Songs closer to 1 are very likely only instrumental.

Key

A categorical variable that shows the key in which the song is recorded (e.g., in the key of G).

Liveness

A metric that indicates whether the song has a live audience. It is measured between 0 and 1 with higher levels indicating a strong likelihood that the song is live.

Loudness

A metric from negative to positive that indicates the decibels of the song. Each track is given an average decibel metric from throughout the whole song. The range varies but, according to Spotify Developer, a typical range is between −60 and 0.

Speechiness

A metric between 0 and 1 that measures the extent to which the song has spoken word in it.

A song closer to 1 is likely entirely made up of spoken words, rather than lyrics delivered through singing or vocal melodies.

Tempo

A metric indicating how many beats per minute are in the song. Songs range from 62.796 to 199.9 beats per minute in the sample of Grime artists and songs. For songs with multiple tempo changes, Spotify's analysis provides a single representative tempo value, usually reflecting the dominant or most consistent tempo throughout the track.

Time Signature

The time signature is a notational convention used in music to specify how many beats are in each measure and what note value constitutes one beat. It is usually written as a fraction such as 4/4 (most common), 3/4, or 6/8.

Valence

A metric between 0 and 1 that captures the emotional quality of the song. Songs with a high valence (closer to 1) sound happy and cheerful compared to a low valence that sound sad.

From the above list of musical characteristics, we identify symbolic connections between artists who create similar music which took two steps. First, we took the average (mean for continuous, mode for categorical) and applied them as descriptors of the artist's songs that year. For example, we assigned an artist an average tempo for each year. Second, we identified ties between artists based on their co‐usage of the musical characteristics. This presented a problem, defining co‐usage. Harkening back to N. Mark (1998) verbiage, how can we define ‘being of the same feather?’ One approach would be to consider artists connected if their average musical characteristics were the same. Following the tempo example, this would mean that an artist would share a tie if their average beats per minute for a year was exactly the same. While possible, this would be very rare due to the fine‐grained nature of the data. These averages were collected to the nearest 100th decimal place and therefore artists matching the exact tempo would be very exceptional. Furthermore, a difference of one beat per minute is, to the untrained ear, unrecognisable and thus, considering artists whose average tempo was one beat per minute apart seemed inappropriate.Therefore, it seemed more suitable to consider co‐usage in terms of proximity to one another. In other words, considering an artist to share a connection to another artist if their average characteristic was within a certain margin to another's. To do this we assumed a conservative approach where we considered an artist culturally proximate to another artist if their average tempo for a given year was within five beats per minute to another, and whether their other musical characteristics were within 0.1 of another on Spotify's scaling procedure. For categorical variables such as Time Signature and Key, we denoted proximity by modal shared characteristic.

This process yielded annual networks each year from 2001–2022 where edges exist between artists who share average musical characteristics within that year. Since the ties between artists represent proximity to one‐another's average characteristic, it is more appropriate to consider the connections between artists in terms of their cultural similarity or proximity to one another than direct overlap. We further transform this network into a dyadic dataset where each available pair of artists in a given year are represented. After deletion on missing variables, this process yields 40,404 artist‐pairs across all 22 years under consideration. This dyadic dataset includes dyad level characteristics based on the network, including whether (1) or not (0) a given dyad had a tie on any possible symbolic dimension represented by Spotify's musical characteristics, as well as whether (1) or not (0) the dyadic pair collaborated on a track in that year.

Further, from this list of characteristics, we develop an additive Similarity Index that takes the total summed value of ties in a given dyad‐year, indicating the weighted degree of overlap between individual artists in a given year. This measure captures the extent of similarity across musical characteristics for each pair. Figure 1 below shows the Pearson correlation coefficient between each musical characteristic and the Similarity Index. Fascinatingly, this plot demonstrates scant overlap among musical forms—a dyad‐pair sharing a tie on one musical characteristic, tempo, for example, does not lead to an inference that the dyad‐pair will share other musical characteristics.

Finally, separate from the musical similarity but as a control for potential music industry pressures associated with collaboration, we collected data from the artist archives of UK top 100 charts from www.officialcharts.com. We constructed a network based on whether artists featured in the charts during the same year (1 = both artists charted that year, 0 = both artists did not chart that year). Note that ties in this network represent both instances where collaborative albums charted or if artists happened to chart in the same year. This control enables us to capture whether artists who chart are more likely to collaborate with one another (i.e., a homophily of successful artists).

Collaboration Networks

As previously noted, there can be many ways that musicians work with other musicians. Furthermore, the structure of the musical industry is such that it is largely influenced by intermediaries such as record labels, radios (Deshmane and Martínez‐de‐Albéniz 2023), and platform algorithms (Prey et al. 2022; Werner 2020). Therefore, isolating the structural connections between individual musicians is incredibly complicated and might actually require a multiplex network itself to capture the multitude of different types of ties that could exist. In this paper, we define collaboration as an artist working directly with another on their song. We recognise that there are also indirect ways to capture collaboration like shoutouts (Hodson 2019), shared memory (Gibson 2014), or myriad different structural ways that individual artists or bands could share talent (Diani and Sacchetti 2023; Gleiser and Danon 2003). However, for this exploration, we simplify the connections between artists to only include the instances where an artist appears on another's song on Spotify.

We constructed directed edgelists for each year from the profiles of the 122 artists in the sample. This involved searching through the profiles of each artist and noting every time the name of another artist would appear. When gathering collaborations, we determined the direction of the collaboration by the appearance of artists on other's profiles. An indegree collaboration counts as one where an alter artist features on a song released on a host's profile. Meanwhile, outdegree collaborations are counted as those where an artist appears on another's profile. Since our sample involves an unbalanced panel with artists releasing their debut on Spotify at different times within this period, or then not releasing material on their profiles every year, we count every incidence of an artist's appearance on the work of others (their outdegree) and then count them as active on Spotify after the date of their debut release. We treat active artists as present in the network from the time of their debut release even if they did not release something in a given year. For example, Roll Deep performed with The Streets in 2002 but then did not release their first material until 2005. We counted the outgoing collaboration in 2002 and then count them as active every year after 2005. We present descriptive statistics for all variables in our analytical sample below:

Modelling

To examine the factors influencing the presence of collaborative ties between pairs of artists in our two‐mode network, we use a dyadic logistic regression model. Dyadic logistic regression is particularly suited for analysing binary outcomes within dyads, where the observations are pairs of entities and the outcome variable is dichotomous (1 = presence of an edge, 0 = absence of an edge). In dyadic data, observations are inherently non‐independent because each entity can appear in multiple dyads. Dyadic logistic regression accounts for this non‐independence by properly modelling the correlations between dyads involving the same entities. To address interdependencies between actors and alters we include fixed effects as well as standard errors clustered on a unique ID for each dyad‐pairing (Aronow et al. 2015). We employ a relatively straightforward modelling approach where we include our focal independent variable, our Similarity Index as the main predictor of collaboration in Model 1. This model seeks to isolate the bivariate (inclusive of fixed effects), association between artist similarity and collaboration. Model 2, then, breaks down the Similarity Index into its constituent parts, examining the association between each musical characteristic (e.g., tempo, modal key) on the probability of collaboration. This approach allows us to determine if any particular musical characteristic is dominating any association found in the Similarity Index (i.e., that it is tempo, in particular, driving collaboration, rather than similarity per se). We also explore the effect of charting in the same year in Model 2 (Table 1).

Results

We present results from our statistical analysis in Table 2 below. Results from Model 1, testing the association between overall additive similarity index and collaborative ties using the dyadic logistic regression procedure reveal a statistically significant (p < 0.001) association between pair similarity and pair collaboration. Results of the reported odds ratio demonstrate that a one‐unit change in the similarity index increases the odds of collaboration by 38%. Figure 2 presents the predicted values based on Model 1 to explore how the odds of collaboration change across the range of the similarity index. Examining the margins plot demonstrates a steady increase across values of the similarity index. According to the model, artist pairs with the maximum similarity score (11) have a predicted probability of roughly 0.8 of collaborating. These results provide strong evidence that greater similarity between artists is associated with an increased likelihood of collaboration. This offers strong evidence that the more similar artists are, the more likely they'll be to collaborate with one another on songs.

Discussion and Conclusions

We set out to examine whether artists' cultural proximity increases their propensity to collaborate (a convergence of cultural forms), or whether culturally similar artists avoid one another in favour of more dissimilar alters. This question reflects the broader tension in creativity between novelty and conformity. Using Grime as a case study, our models provide strong evidence for cultural convergence in music, supporting Hypothesis 1a and the homophily paradigm. Conversely, we find no support for Hypothesis 1b—which, drawing on the competitive dynamics of the music industry and artists' efforts to carve out distinct positions (N. Mark 1998; N. P. Mark 2003), proposed that culturally proximate artists might keep each other at arm's length. Instead, greater overall similarity (captured by the Similarity Index), as well as similarity in musical features such as Tempo, Danceability, Energy, Speechiness, Liveness, Valence, and modal Key in one period, increases the likelihood of collaboration in the next. This indicates a link between the symbolic ties formed through shared musical forms and the structural ties created through collaboration. Finally, the positive association between shared chart success and collaboration suggests either that success encourages artists to join forces or that labels and industry pressures promote connections among successful artists.

The overall finding is particularly curious given the literature documenting the competitive nature of the music industry. A burgeoning theory within the sociology of culture argues that creative industries are characterised by ecological niches wherein individuals compete with others over scarce resources (Godart et al. 2023; N. Mark 1998; N. P. Mark 2003; Popielarz and Neal 2007; Roberts and Strandvad 2022). In this instance, musicians are grouped into genres and compete for dominance over that genre. Such competition would suggest that collaborations, especially with culturally similar others, would be risky. If an artist collaborates with another who produces similar work, their followers ‐ whose taste would likely also include the culturally similar alter ‐ may be introduced to the music of the alter and prefer them. However, results from this paper suggest that cultural similarity does not inhibit collaboration but rather predicts it.

This study also contributes to Breiger's (1974) extension on duality by examining how symbolic connections to others through the co‐usage of cultural forms influences the propensity of individuals to work with each other within a cultural field. Future studies may adopt our approach to capturing latent sonic characteristics in a field to examine similar dualities across cultural fields. While this approach is not unique (Vlegels and Lievens 2017), this paper demonstrates that there is a duality between musical form and musician collaboration. There exists a symbolic connection between the artist and their art which predicts structural connections to similar others. Specifically, the models demonstrate that signalling one's position within a cultural field like music that is characterised by niches of individuals who signal their belonging through the usage of agreed upon musical characteristics (Feld 1981; N. Mark 1998) attracts culturally similar alters together. This type of cultural convergence shows that individual actors within their cultural field observe the signals of others and pursue connections to those who demonstrate similarity.

One limitation of the current study is that it is limited to only the Grime genre. There are several characteristics of Grime that make it an interesting case for such a study ‐ like its collaborative but competitive nature. However, future research could adopt the approach we laid out to examine whether cultural similarity predicts collaboration in different genres of music. Such a study would discover whether artists in different genres keep similar alters at arm's length or if all genres experience a similar cultural convergence. A second limitation to this paper is that the models do not account for potential intermediaries that might exert influence over which artists work with which. Our decision to simplify the types of collaborative connections to isolate only the appearances of artists on produced songs might be influenced by latent factors like record labels, producers, and others who may impact the collaborative homophily we observe. This type of a study represents a further duality that could exist between artists—the shared network of their record labels. It is possible that the record labels which these grime artists sign to (if any) are connected and therefore decide contractually which artists can work together. There exist multiple such symbolic or indirect connections that could exist between artists truly opening the doorway to the dharma of duality (Lee and Martin 2018) that exists between musicians. However, for the purposes of this paper, it suffices to say that there exists a duality between musical characteristics and musician collaborations.

Additionally, the current study does not take into consideration the stages associated with the lifecycle of the Grime genre. Lena and Peterson (2008) examine the stages of a genre in terms of its development and success in the industry moving from local, leaderless genre (avant‐garde) into mainstream success and beyond. The current study does not consider whether the observed behaviour varies across these stages but examines the entire life of the genre from 2001–2022. Future researchers may wish to adopt our models and examine whether the tendency of musically similar artists to collaborate may be particularly salient during certain stages of a genre's tenure. For example, Grime artists may flock together more during avant‐garde or scene base stages of the genre when the drive to forge collective identity and reinforce symbolic boundaries to establish their scene. Meanwhile, in later stages, once the genre experiences mainstream success, perhaps there is less pressure to cohere and more room to differentiate collaboratively. Such a study would reveal industrial pressures and the role of industry in shaping the duality of music similarity and structural connectivity.

In conclusion, people's propensity to group together with culturally similar others and form homophilous networks (McPherson 1983; McPherson et al. 2001) overrides any potential risks posed by collaborating with potential competitors in the music industry. In answer to our question posed towards N. Mark’s (1998) classic theory‐building paper on the music industry, we can confidently say that our findings suggest that ‘birds of a feather’ do indeed sing together.
