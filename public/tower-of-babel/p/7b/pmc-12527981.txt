Manifest Wolff–Parkinson–White Syndrome With a Posteroseptal Accessory Pathway Showing Right Posteroseptal Ventricular Insertion but Requiring Left Atrial Ablation for Retrograde Conduction Block
Authors: Yuma Tanaka, Takayuki Sekihara, Takafumi Oka, Akira Yoshida, Shigetaka Kusumoto, Masaki Takashina, Yasushi Sakata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCareful evaluation of the atrial insertion site of the accessory pathway is required for the retrograde conduction block, irrespective of the delta wave morphology and the successful ablation site for the antegrade conduction block. Left‐sided ablation may be needed even when the antegrade conduction block is achieved by right‐sided ablation.Keywords: accessory atrioventricular pathway, catheter ablation, paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome

Introduction

Pre‐ablation prediction of the accessory pathway (AP) location is an essential process for catheter ablation of manifest Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) syndrome. In particular, determining whether to approach the left atrium during ablation of manifest WPW syndrome with a posteroseptal AP is an important problem. The previously reported algorithms utilize the morphology of the delta wave to identify the AP location [1, 2]. However, delta wave morphology only reflects the ventricular insertion site, and the atrial insertion site may be distant from the ventricular one due to the oblique course [3]. We present a case of manifest WPW syndrome with a posteroseptal AP showing right posteroseptal ventricular insertion but requiring left atrial ablation for retrograde AP conduction block. This case also highlights the usefulness of high‐resolution mapping for delineating the oblique course of the posteroseptal AP.

Case History/Examination

A 46‐year‐old man underwent catheter ablation for paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia. The patient's baseline body surface electrocardiogram during sinus rhythm showed manifest WPW syndrome with a posteroseptal AP (Figure 1A), and previously reported algorithms indicated the ventricular insertion site of the AP at the right posteroseptum [1, 2].

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

An electrophysiological study and ablation were performed under conscious sedation. On baseline examination, antegrade conduction showed ventricular preexcitation (Figure 1B) and an AH jump‐up phenomenon. Retrograde conduction lacked decremental properties, and the earliest atrial activation site (EAAS) was located at the proximal coronary sinus (CS) (Figure 1C). Programmed single atrial extrastimulus induced short RP' narrow complex tachycardia. The retrograde atrial activation sequence was identical to that during baseline ventricular pacing, and the proximal CS was 20 ms earlier than the His region (Figure 1D). The tachycardia was reset by His‐refractory ventricular single extrastimulus without altering the atrial activation sequence. Right ventricular entrainment pacing demonstrated a V‐A‐V response, and the post‐pacing interval after AH interval correction was 86 ms. Consequently, the narrow complex tachycardia was diagnosed as orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia via the posteroseptal accessory pathway. High‐resolution maps of the right posteroseptum were created using the Rhythmia HDx mapping system (Boston Scientific, Natick, MA, USA) during right atrial and right ventricular pacing (Figure 2A,B). The maps indicated that the ventricular and atrial insertion sites of the AP were separately located at the right posteroseptum and the anterior side of the proximal CS, respectively.

The earliest ventricular activation was observed in the right posteroseptum, and the earliest atrial activation site was observed in the proximal CS. (C) Successful antegrade accessory pathway conduction block established by the ablation at the right posteroseptum. EAAS, earliest atrial activation site; LAO, left anterior oblique. Ablation was initiated by targeting the ventricular insertion of the AP. Radiofrequency (RF) ablation at the right posteroseptum successfully eliminated antegrade AP conduction, and the delta wave disappeared (Figure 2C). However, additional ablation in the surrounding area, including cryoablation near the His bundle, failed to eliminate retrograde AP conduction. RF ablation at the anterior side of the proximal CS transiently blocked retrograde AP conduction but could not establish a complete retrograde AP block. Therefore, a transseptal approach and left atrial mapping were performed. The EAAS of the left atrium at the 6 o'clock position of the mitral annulus, which is close to the transient successful ablation site within the CS, showed earlier activation than the proximal CS (Figure 3A). RF ablation at this site eliminated the retrograde AP conduction (Figure 3B). The residual ventriculoatrial conduction with concentric activation pattern was proved to be a retrograde fast pathway conduction by administering 20 mg of adenosine triphosphate (Figure 4).

(B) Successful retrograde accessory pathway conduction block established by the ablation at the EAAS in the LA. From the third beat, the atrial electrogram of the His region became earlier than the proximal CS. The dotted red lines represent the onset of the atrial electrogram of the His region. The block of the residual ventriculoatrial conduction by 20 mg of adenosine triphosphate infusion. This finding indicates that the residual ventriculoatrial conduction was via the atrioventricular node and not via the accessory pathway. Abbreviations are as in Figures 1 and 2.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

We ended the session without any complications. The patient has been free from symptomatic arrhythmia recurrence for 16 months after the procedure.

Discussion

This case demonstrated manifest WPW syndrome with a posteroseptal AP showing right posteroseptal ventricular insertion but requiring left atrial ablation for retrograde AP conduction block. The findings in this case highlighted the limitations of using delta wave morphology to guide AP ablation for retrograde conduction block. In this case, AP localization algorithms based on the body surface electrocardiograms indicated that the AP was located on the right posteroseptum [1, 2]. As predicted by these algorithms, ablation at the right posteroseptum eliminated antegrade AP conduction; however, retrograde AP conduction persisted.

The oblique course of posteroseptal APs typically shows atrial insertion at the left side of the ventricular insertion [3]. We attempted additional ablation lateral to the successful antegrade conduction site guided by high‐resolution maps, but this failed to eliminate retrograde AP conduction. Most of the posteroseptal manifest APs have an oblique course in the case of failure to block retrograde or antegrade conduction of APs from the right side. The left posteroseptal region should be mapped for successful ablation. Chiang et al. reported that a left atrial approach was necessary if the difference between ventricular‐atrial times recorded on the His bundle catheter and the CS catheter was 25 ms or more [4]. However, this criterion was not met in this case (20 ms, as shown in Figure 1D). These findings indicate the necessity for left atrial mapping when right‐sided ablation is ineffective, regardless of the reported criteria. High‐resolution mapping of both the right posteroseptum and left atrium may be helpful in delineating the oblique course of the posteroseptal accessory pathway.

In conclusion, this case highlights the necessity of carefully evaluating the atrial insertion of the AP, irrespective of the delta wave morphology and the successful ablation site for antegrade conduction block. AP localization algorithms based on body surface electrocardiograms provide information on ventricular insertion only. The location of the atrial insertion should be evaluated separately using findings from intracardiac electrocardiograms and 3‐dimensional mapping. Regardless of pre‐ablation predictions, a left atrial approach should be considered if right‐sided ablation is unsuccessful.
