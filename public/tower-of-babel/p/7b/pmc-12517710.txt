Challenges and opportunities for drug development in rare pulmonary diseases like cystic fibrosis: an industry perspective
Authors: Zachary M Sellers, Alan H Cohen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewThere is a critical need for new therapies addressing the high unmet needs of individuals with rare lung diseases. This review examines the challenges industry sponsors face in developing therapeutic products for rare lung diseases, using cystic fibrosis as an example.Recent findingsSince the development of cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators, the drug development landscape for cystic fibrosis has changed. New challenges include defining success in an era of small molecule CFTR modulators, recruitment from a small, ultra-rare population, limited experience with novel trial designs and biomarkers, and fluctuations in funding opportunities.SummaryWhile challenges to drug development in rare lung disease, including cystic fibrosis, these challenges also present opportunities for innovation amongst industry sponsors, researchers, foundations/advocacy groups, regulators, and funders. INTRODUCTION

Rare lung diseases, like alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (A1ATD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and cystic fibrosis (CF), collectively affect approximately 1.2–2.5 million people in North America and approximately 1.5–3.0 million people in Europe [1]. Over the last decade, a handful of drugs have been approved for rare lung diseases, most being small molecule CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) correctors and potentiators (commonly termed, “CFTR modulators”) for CF. While not limited to targeting lung disease in CF, CFTR modulators can significantly improve lung function (as measured by forced expiratory volume in 1 s, FEV1), pulmonary exacerbations, reduce the need for lung transplants and decrease mortality [2–8]. However, not all individuals with CF are genetically eligible for CFTR modulators nor will all people who are eligible tolerate them or have access to them for socioeconomic reasons. Nonetheless, in the current era, we are faced with an increasingly ultra-rare population of individuals with CF who, left with only symptomatic treatments, continue to face advancing lung disease and functional decline. This article will highlight some of the current challenges and opportunities in developing new therapeutic interventions for rare lung diseases, focusing on this ultra-rare population of individuals with CF, as seen through the lens of an industry sponsor.

Setting expectations of “success”: cures vs. iterative improvements

In drug development, it is imperative to be clear on how “success” is viewed in the eyes of various stakeholders, including patients, clinical providers, patient advocacy organizations, government regulators, internal company leadership, and investors (public or private). Definitions of success often differ amongst these shareholders. Balancing and establishing priorities oftentimes presents an intricate process of trade-offs and novel discoveries through a product's lifecycle. There currently is no cure for CF or CF lung disease. With the identification of the CFTR gene in 1989 [9], there became an intense interest in developing a genetic cure. While curing monogenic diseases is theoretically simpler than multigenic diseases, correcting the more than 2000 CFTR variants, across five classes of distinct mutations, has proven challenging. New advances in nucleic acid therapeutic tools (e.g., RNA/DNA delivery and gene editing) are bringing us closer to realizing this vision. However, while pursuing bold advances to develop the next generation of treatments and cures, it is critically important to also consider therapies that will provide clinically meaningful improvements for the health and well being of those with CF, by stabilizing and/or impeding the rate of decline in lung function of those with CF pulmonary disease.

The history of CF therapeutics highlights the impact that iterative therapeutic advances can have on those with CF. In the pre-CFTR modulator era, inhaled mucolytics reduced pulmonary exacerbations [10], which are important drivers of lung function decline. With CFTR modulators we have also seen the benefits of iterative therapeutic advances. Ivacaftor only treated a small group of individuals with CF, however, lessons learned from its development provided the foundation for the development of subsequent modulators. Lumacaftor/ivacaftor, the first CFTR modulator to target a broader population (those with F508del homozygous variants), had nominal benefits on FEV1, yet we observed decreases in pulmonary exacerbations, improvements in the decline of FEV1 and reductions in hospitalization or use of intravenous antibiotics [6,11,12], all of which are clinically meaningful metrics. The subsequent development of elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) expanded the population treated from those with two copies of F508del variants to those only containing one. Additionally, ETI enhanced overall lung health through its increased ability over prior CFTR modulators (i.e. lumacaftor/ivacaftor and tezacaftor/ivacaftor) to correct F508del and other CFTR variants. The significant benefits of ETI would not have been possible without the earlier therapies that provided incremental clinical improvements, slowed CF lung disease progression, and deepened our understanding of CFTR and lung biology. We must be cautious in balancing the allocation of time and resources to the pursuit of transformative discoveries that may result in large leaps forward with smaller, seemingly iterative advances that are clinically meaningful and can save lives now. We should be careful not to add additional barriers to the already complex task of developing therapeutics for CF lung disease.

Small patient populations

CF is a rare disease, affecting approximately 40 000 people in the United States and approximately 162 000 people globally [13,14]. We are also cautiously aware that there are likely significant populations of undiagnosed individuals with CF in areas of the world where CF has not traditionally been considered to exist (e.g., South and East Asia, Middle East). There are shared and unique challenges in developing therapies for rare diseases compared to more prevalent diseases. Despite CF being characterized and defined as a “rare disease”, it is still one of the more common genetic disorders, particularly among people of European descent. Through the Orphan Drug Act, the FDA defined rare diseases as those affecting fewer than 200 000 people in the US [15]. CF may be more common than many other rare diseases, depending on the ancestral origin of the individual. When considering individuals with CF who are not eligible for, are unable to tolerate, or have inadequate responses to CFTR modulators, this becomes a true “ultra-rare” population, a disease that affects less than 1/50 000 people (~6800 people in the United States). While regulatory agencies have worked with industry sponsors for the last 40+ years to overcome some of the challenges and inherent obstacles in rare disease drug development, there remain many more hurdles to overcome.

Clinical trials for rare and ultra-rare diseases often face recruitment challenges due to the limited number of eligible patients per site, necessitating many trial sites, which increase both the time and cost associated with trial completion. Social media-focused disease centric organizations and patient advocacy groups can play important roles in making eligible patient populations aware of clinical trials that they may benefit from. Because these patient populations are small, they tend to be tight knit and well connected to the latest opportunities through parallel communications by virtue of these channels. While it is exhilarating to see multiple sponsors developing therapies for a given ultra-rare population, it also adds additional burden in recruiting multiple different studies from a small, limited pool of individuals. Additionally, for nucleic acid-based therapies, timing of when subjects may participate in other trials may be longer than traditional small molecule focused clinical trials due to longer presence of delivery vehicle, nucleic acid product, or immunological memory. This calls for innovative and efficient trial designs that make the most of the limited pool of eligible participants. The timing of serial study eligibility as well as criteria used to best define inclusion of subsequent Sponsor-lead efforts is also not universally clear and agreed upon, further complicating making initial study participant enrollment in early novel mechanism of action studies.

Limited experience in cystic fibrosis with trial designs in small populations

To date, drug trials in CF have been large, randomized, placebo-controlled trials (RCTs) involving 100 s of individuals per arm. While RCTs are typically considered the gold-standard for clinical trials, in rare diseases these may not be possible or even ethical to do. Likewise, regulatory agencies have historically requested multiple Phase 3 studies be performed to give greater confidence in the therapeutic benefit of the intervention. With an increasing ultra-rare population of individuals with CF without transformative therapies for their lung disease, performing multiple RCTs for regulatory approval is not feasible. As with other rare diseases, it is becoming increasingly important in CF trials to consider innovative trial designs, ideally those allowing for smaller numbers of study participants over shorter timeframes. These may include platform trials, adaptive designs, or use of external natural history controls (Table 1).

In cases where therapeutic development programs share a common technological platform, it may be possible to pool safety data or utilize a similar control cohort to minimize exposure to ultra-rare populations. In instances where there is limited natural history data, it may be more appropriate for individuals to serve as their own control vs. a distinct control arm of additional subjects. Where there is natural historical data, such as patient registries, this prospectively collected data may serve as control data, assuming it is collected in a rigorous manner and is representative of the cohort participating in the interventional trial. There are multiple CF registries worldwide. These distinct patient data sets can and should potentially serve as important “natural history” like assets for clinical trials in CF, especially for drug development programs targeting small populations of people with CF. Evaluating how existing registries may collect patient data to not only inform care centers on clinical practice (its current primary intent), but to also play integral roles in supporting drug development may enhance the impact of CF patient registries on the health of those with CF. Consideration for inclusion of participant consent for potentially wider and more intentional future trial applications, while maintaining and respecting confidentiality, should be a future objective for all forward-thinking rare disease registry efforts in the future.

In addition to different trial designs that can be leveraged for rare diseases, accelerated approval pathways may also provide benefits to drug development programs. The accelerated pathway was established by the FDA in 1992 in response to the HIV/AIDS epidemic to facilitate expedited approval of drugs for serious diseases with a high unmet need. It involves using a surrogate endpoint for conditional approval, usually followed by additional or longer studies using established, clinically meaningful endpoints. An accelerated approval pathway requires a surrogate endpoint with reasonable correlation to an approvable endpoint. This pathway can be helpful when the approvable endpoint may take a long time to manifest a difference to the comparator group, but the surrogate marker shows an earlier signal. An example provided in the FDA's guidance document on accelerated approvals is the use of sputum culture conversion from positive to negative during treatment of pulmonary tuberculosis as a surrogate endpoint for the prediction of clinical benefit achieved from resolution of infection [16▪]. The full clinical benefits from resolution of pulmonary tuberculosis infection may take years to realize. Evidence of sufficient treatment through sputum culture provides a reasonable likelihood of future benefit. To date, no CF drugs have been approved using the accelerated approval pathway. For CF lung disease, there are currently no established surrogate endpoints being used in clinical trials for initial approval. In-vitro short-circuit current data has been used for label expansion after initial approval of CFTR modulators [17]. This is undoubtedly a missed opportunity, especially for discrete populations of people with CF who continue to have significantly worse clinical outcomes, including survival and quality of life, with limited therapeutic options beyond supportive care. There is a need to identify and develop biomarkers of lung disease in CF that could serve as additional, complementary endpoints in regular approval pathways or as surrogate markers in an accelerated approval pathway.

Limited approvable biomarkers and endpoints in cystic fibrosis lung disease

In CF drug trials, the most common primary endpoints have been changes in FEV1 (or percentage predicted FEV1, ppFEV1), sweat chloride, and quality of life (as measured by revised CF quality of life questionnaire, CFQ-R), with additional supportive endpoints of number pulmonary exacerbations and changes in BMI. For lung-directed therapies, (pp)FEV1 and CFQ-R are the predominant endpoints that have been readily used and accepted by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to evaluate efficacy in CF. FEV1 is a measure from spirometric testing that has been shown to be correlated with lung health and predictive of survival [18]. Fundamentally, FEV1 measures airflow through the large and mid-size conductive airways. While effort-dependent, individuals with CF have extensive experience with performing interpretable spirometry through routine clinical care, and the inclusion of a placebo arm in a study can mitigate concerns regarding effort levels providing undue influence on measures. In clinical trials where FEV1 is measured at a single timepoint prior to intervention and then compared to subsequent timepoint(s) after intervention, day-to-day variation in FEV1 may complicate distilling biological benefits of interventions vs. regression to the mean phenomenon [19]. This may have a significantly negative and unintended impact on small studies where these variations may get effectively addressed in larger studies.

Calculating changes in FEV1 over time, typically as an annualized rate of change, may be used to mitigate the impact of natural FEV1 variability. This approach does not rely solely on a single measure. Instead, it accounts for multiple measures and the change that may occur. In general, individuals with CF tend to experience a decline in FEV1 over time [20–22]. A larger rate of decline has been associated with decreased survival [23]. Thus, examining if an intervention alters that rate of decline, either by comparing to one's own rate of decline or to a reference population, may indicate potential benefit of a therapy. The predominant mode of analyzing longitudinal changes in FEV1 has been to calculate FEV1 slope, which assumes a constant rate of change. However, FEV1 decline may not be linear [18,24–27] complicating the accuracy of slope calculations that are based on assumptions of changes occurring at the same rate. FEV1 slope measures may be linear over relatively short periods, but not over longer durations [28]. While FEV1 slope has not been used as a primary endpoint in sponsored CF clinical drug trials, a long-term hypertonic saline trial used a “lung function” slope measure, consisting of FEV1, forced vital capacity (FVC), and forced expiratory flow at 25–75 percentage of FVC (FEF25–75) as the study primary endpoint. Outside of CF, FVC slope was effectively employed as the basis for approval of both pirfenidone and nintedinib for IPF, as opposed to requiring a survival endpoint, which was unfeasible from a practical and economic perspective at the time. It is reassuring to note that both therapies have been proven to offer a survival benefit through the use of sponsor-supported registries following both drug approvals. Thus, there are successful regulatory precedents for the use of lung function slope measures in the context of rare lung diseases with limited therapeutic options and high morbidity and mortality.

FEV1 is not the only measure of lung function. Pulmonary plethysmography, which measures lung volumes in a sealed ’body box,’ can assess aeration, air trapping, and lung function in clinical care. However, its use in multicenter trials can introduce significant variability across sites, making it unsuitable as a primary or secondary endpoint in sponsor-driven studies. Airway oscillometry (or impulse oscillometry) measures how airways resist airflow (resistance) and how elastic lungs are (reactance) during tidal breathing. It allows evaluation of lung function in small children who can’t perform forced maneuvers and can measure distal airway function. While it has been used in asthma [29,30] and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [31] studies, and may be considered for rare lung diseases like IPF [32] or primary ciliary dyskinesia [33], it may be less suitable for CF [33,34]. Multiple breath washout (MBW) is another test of lung function that relies on tidal breathing and is not effort dependent. MBW measures ventilation homogeneity, specifically in the terminal airways, providing functional evaluation of small airway physiology/disease. Most commonly, lung clearance index (LCI) is calculated at the time point in which 2.5% of an inert gas is cleared from the lungs (LCI2.5). In CF, LCI2.5 has been shown to be a sensitive measure of early development of lung disease in the small airways, sometimes even preceding changes in FEV1, and correlates with CFQ-R [35,36▪]. It has been increasingly employed in CF clinical trials in the USA, Europe, and Australia. LCI has been effectively employed as a primary endpoint in Europe, with support by the European Medicines Agency, for the approval of CFTR modulators in children, and as a secondary endpoint for label expansion studies by the FDA in the US. However, since there is limited clinical experience using MBW in adults with CF lung disease and in those with severe, compromised lung function, ongoing work is being done to better understand how MBW measures can be employed in these populations, which remain a key target population for current and future drug trials.

There has been intense interest in the use of noninvasive radiographic testing, including computed tomography (CT) or MRI as surrogate markers of lung disease in CF and non-CF rare lung diseases. Each of these modalities has pros and cons, depending on the population studied [37] and have been used in nonregistrational clinical trials using a variety of image analytical tools. For registrational trials, imaging has not yet been accepted by regulatory agencies as a primary endpoint, although it has been effectively used for defining target populations for controlled regulatory studies, including IPF lung disease. Yet, there has been a call from rare lung disease communities to prioritize lung imaging as a viable endpoint. Recently, the Alpha-1-Antitrypsin Foundation issued a statement to the FDA recommending they reconsider their stance on CT densitometry as a surrogate endpoint in AIATD lung therapy trials [38]. This has the potential to help advance drug development and greater therapeutic options for marginalized and underserved populations, such as those with A1ATD and CF.

Whether it be novel ways to use traditional spirometric measures, additional measures of lung function (e.g., LCI), radiological or new biomarkers of disease, it is important for rare lung disease trials to be able to employ a variety of potentially complementary measures that allow for the detection of real and sustained biological changes in small populations. Numerous other tests are under development or validation as endpoints for clinical trials (Table 2). Tests may be deemed suitable for clinical research, or even clinical practice, but may not meet regulatory standards for consideration as primary or secondary endpoints. Developing the extent of validation data required for regulatory acceptance of biomarkers or surrogate endpoints can be time and cost prohibitive for sponsors in rare diseases. Expanding the number of potential measures as endpoints in trials requires close collaboration with sponsors, researchers, foundations/patient advocate groups, all in alignment with US and international regulatory agencies.

Funding for rare lung disease research and clinical development

Robust and steady funding is critical to sustained drug development in any area given the often-lengthy timelines required. Funding may arise from a variety of sources, including, but not limited to grants (public or foundations/charities), loans, philanthropy, venture capital investment, or business partnerships, depending on the stage of development. Securing funding for rare disease drug development can face distinct challenges compared to large market opportunities [39▪▪]. Return on investment is central to nearly all investors, although what that expectation for return looks like and the timeline for return may differ amongst funding sources. Swings in financial markets, interest rates, and regulatory policies may heavily influence investors’ desire to invest in rare disease biotechnology and pharmaceutical ventures. While regulatory incentives like the FDA's Orphan Drug Designation and the allure of high-priced therapies have spurred investment in rare disease drug development, uncertainties around regulatory pathways and reimbursement can still deter sustained investment. The additional challenges of a small to modestly sized potential treatment population, oftentimes widely distributed globally may detract funders from investing in rare disease therapeutic development and focus on larger, less risky (or at least perceived less risky) diseases.

Specific challenges (and opportunities) for gene therapy in cystic fibrosis lung disease

With scientific advancements in gene therapy and lung biology, there are exciting potential opportunities to leverage gene therapy to treat, or even cure, rare lung diseases, including CF. Fundamentally, to have sustained improvement in CF lung disease, one must be able to effectively deliver a working CFTR gene product throughout the airways, since CFTR is expressed in small and large airways and airway submucosal glands and achieve durable expression or achieve clinically meaningful expression levels upon re-dosing, as needed. Furthermore, delivery into the physiologically relevant cell types is likely also important to achieve clinically meaningful improvements in lung health. Single-cell sequencing and analytics have increased our understanding of what cell types in the airways express CFTR, however, there remain gaps in our scientific knowledge regarding the lifespan of different airway cells and how CFTR-expressing and nonexpressing cell types may interact with each other for physiological processes. Animal models can provide experimental opportunities not permissible in humans, but confirming findings in humans is important as notable differences in lung biology and its impact on inhaled drugs have been observed between different animal models and humans [40▪,41,42]. Gene therapy has the potential to not only help advance our understanding of the pathophysiology of CFTR-related lung disease, but to also serve as conduit for effective treatments and cures.

CONCLUSION

The development of novel therapies for rare lung diseases remains an important area for drug development to improve the quality and length of life for those suffering from these diseases. While there remain challenges along the drug development life cycle for bringing forward therapeutics for these diseases, these challenges also present new opportunities for innovation and collaboration between industry sponsors and other stakeholders.
