Helping children cope with a mother's breast cancer diagnosis: ‘The telling box’ – A pilot study
Authors: Joyce M.J. de Vos-Houben, Femke Bruls, Christel Heiyerjans, Rianne Roijen, Trijntje Völlink, Esther C. Bakker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDiscussing cancer with children presents challenges. Evidence underscores the importance of transparent communication in aiding children's coping mechanisms amidst a parent's diagnosis. ‘The telling box’ intervention was developed to assist mothers in discussing cancer with their children. This study aimed to explore nurses' and mothers' perspectives on the ‘telling box’.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with six oncology nurses and five mothers diagnosed with breast cancer to assess their experiences and opinions regarding ‘the telling box’.Results and conclusionBoth nurses and mothers emphasize the significance of open communication with children. Nurses identified ‘the telling box’ as a valuable resource for starting discussions about cancer with children, but lack of time and knowledge were factors preventing them from distributing the boxes. The current design of the box did not meet the mothers' needs, and the timing of its distribution was suboptimal. We recommend education of nurses on the use of ‘the telling box’ and customizing its content based on the child's age and treatment stage of the mother.InnovationThis study offers important insights from both patients and healthcare professionals. ‘The telling box’ was developed through collaborative efforts involving healthcare professionals and patients.

Introduction

In the Netherlands, 59,000 women received a diagnosis of breast cancer in 2021 [1]. This malignancy stands as the most prevalent cancer among Dutch women, with a statistical likelihood of 1 in 7 women encountering this diagnosis during their lifetime. Of the total diagnosed cases in 2021, 6500 women (11 %) fell within the age bracket of 25–49, a demographic wherein a notable proportion are mothers with dependent children. Pink Ribbon, an entity affiliated with The Dutch Cancer Society (DCS; known as the ‘KWF foundation’ in Dutch), asserts that over a quarter of breast cancer patients have children residing at home. It is expected that the number of children with a parent who has cancer will further increase in the future [[2], [3], [4]].

Effective communication plays a pivotal role in aiding children in coping with their mother's breast cancer diagnosis. While broaching the subject of cancer with children can present challenges, compelling evidence underscores that candid and open communication stands as the most efficacious approach in helping children navigate a parent's cancer diagnosis [5]. Nevertheless, parents often express unmet needs concerning guidance and support from oncology professionals in communicating about cancer with their children [[6], [7], [8]]. Several studies show that a significant proportion of parents did not receive assistance in discussing cancer with their children, despite expressing a desire for such guidance regarding both their diagnoses and the broader impacts of cancer within the family unit [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Additionally, research advocates for the incorporation of routine assessments to ascertain the familial circumstances of parents with cancer, identifying their support requirements, and providing information and assistance as part of standard care. Psychosocial interventions tailored for affected families can offer support where needed [4,6,8,10]].

Meriggi et al. (2017) describes strategies to aid parents in communicating with their children about cancer. Such guidance should consider factors such as the children's age, the family's communication style, their perceptions of illness and treatment, as well as the parents' capacity to address their children's needs and reactions. The study underscores the significance of transparent and honest communication about the illness, emphasizing the need for parents to afford themselves compassion, recognizing that there is no singular correct approach to divulging such information [5]. Many parents attest that open communication can significantly alleviate distress in their children, as children who receive sincere explanations about their parent's illness tend to place greater trust in family members [9]. Furthermore, maintaining daily routines for the child and family wherever feasible is important. Semple and McCance (2010) highlight recurring and significant issues such as parental guilt, challenges in communicating with children, and the importance of upholding household routines [7].

It is important to distinguish between psychosocial support and interventions. While psychosocial support centers on providing emotional and social aid primarily through empathetic listening and understanding, interventions constitute structured and purposeful actions designed to address specific problems or challenges, often with professional guidance. Both play crucial roles in assisting individuals in coping with difficulties and enhancing their overall well-being. In the context of assisting parents in communicating with their children about cancer, the Maastricht UMC+ Comprehensive Cancer Center (MCCC) developed an intervention known as ‘the telling box’ in collaboration with parents and professionals. This intervention aims to support mothers in communicating with their children about their breast cancer diagnosis. The development process involved consultation with the Toon Hermans House, an organization providing support for individuals affected by cancer, and the Center for Youth and Family, a resource for parenting, child development, and family well-being. Furthermore, focus group interviews conducted within the MCCC revealed a perceived lack of guidance among women with breast cancer regarding communication with their children, particularly concerning the effects of treatment on family dynamics and emotional challenges for both parent and child [11]. Evaluation of a training program for nurses associated with the MCCC highlighted a demand among mothers for informative materials facilitating discussions with their children at home, underscoring an existing gap in available resources [12]. Based on these insights, ‘the telling box’ was developed to include informational materials for children, a communication package addressing disease-related discussions within the family, and a game aimed at fostering conversations about cancer. Additionally, items such as a backpack keychain, a ‘worry eater’, and memory box were included to provide children with outlets for their thoughts, concerns, questions, and cherished memories. The intervention also directs users to online patient information resources regarding how to disclose a cancer diagnosis to children, offering general advice on what information to provide and guidance on when, where, and how to broach the topic. This resource also addresses strategies for managing both the parent's and child's emotions, offering age-specific recommendations for infants, toddlers, preschoolers, school-age children, and adolescents.

This study aimed to evaluate the initial experiences of nurses and mothers with the ‘telling box’ intervention, developed to facilitate communication between mothers and their children about breast cancer. Although the results did not fully meet the expected outcomes in terms of immediate uptake or ease of use, the findings offer valuable insights into how such interventions can be further refined to better meet the needs of families—demonstrating the concept of a ‘Brilliant Failure’, where unexpected challenges lead to meaningful learning and improvement.

Methods

A qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews to explore the initial experiences and perceptions of both nurses and mothers using ‘the telling box’. The pilot study involved 30 instances of the telling box, which were obtained through funding by the MCCC and its patient panel. ‘The telling box’ (Fig. 1) comprised various items tailored to the ages of children within the family unit, as depicted in Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11: a ‘worry eater’ (a plush toy featuring a zipper, designed to metaphorically consume worries inscribed or drawn on paper), the book “Mama Has Cancer”, a keychain featuring a miniature backpack with a zipper, a small wooden container for safeguarding valuable items, the workbook “When Your Mother or Father Has Cancer”, a dice game aimed at teenagers (“Good Riddance”), a card set addressing issues for partners and patients (“Living Together with a Disease”), and a conversational toolkit for children (“Talking About Being Ill”). Participants and procedure

Two groups of participants who used ‘the telling box’ were interviewed: six oncology nurses and five women with breast cancer (see Results for more details).

Oncology nurses counseling women with breast cancer or gynecological cancer were included. The oncology nurses were asked by the researchers to participate in an online interview (Microsoft Teams).

Patients included were women having breast cancer. Due to the availability of 30 telling boxes provided by the MCCC, priority was given to cancer types exhibiting higher prevalence rates and a considerable proportion of patients with dependent children at home. All participants were required to be proficient in Dutch, capable of providing informed consent, devoid of intellectual disabilities, aged between 20 and 50 years, and responsible for one or more children aged 4–18 years residing in their household.

Within the clinical setting, oncology nurses identified eligible patients and discussed with them how to communicate their diagnosis to their children. Subsequently, these nurses presented ‘the telling box’ along with the requisite patient information and consent forms for the study. After receiving the box, patients were also informed by e-mail containing all the information about the study along with a consent form. A reminder email was sent after 2–3 weeks. Patients who returned the consent form were subsequently contacted via phone to confirm their participation, after which arrangements were made for online interviews via Microsoft Teams.

Ethical approval for all aspects of the study was obtained from the local medical ethics committee of the Maastricht UMC+ (METC 2021–2537).

Interviews

Online interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide that was developed in advance by the research team (see supplementary files). The interviews were conducted by two researchers who were not directly involved in the primary care process (CH & RR) and took place between 3 and 6 months after the diagnosis. The duration of the interview was approximately one hour. During the interview, first some general information was collected (age, number of children, family composition, date of diagnosis, and current date). The first topic of the interview investigates women's experiences in communicating about the diagnosis. An example question from the women's interview guide is: “What was it like for you to talk to your child about the diagnosis?”

The experiences with ‘the telling box’ were also discussed, with questions about the moment of receiving ‘the telling box’ (e.g., ‘How did you feel at the moment you received the telling box?’), the appreciation and usage of ‘the telling box’ (e.g., ‘Which parts of the telling box helped you the most? Which ones did not hold much value for you, and why?’). Finally, women were asked for suggestions for improvement regarding ‘the telling box’ and the communication process around the cancer diagnosis.

The aforementioned topics were adjusted for the interview guide for nurses to investigate the observations and experiences of nurses regarding women's communication with children about the diagnosis and their experiences with ‘the telling box’.

Data analysis

Audiotaped semi-structured interviews were transcribed by Amberscript. The scripts were checked and, if needed, corrected by the researchers. The transcripts were then coded and analyzed with ATLAS.ti-software by two researchers (CH & RR), who consulted each other in case of doubt. Thematic analysis was used to identify, analyze, and report patterns (themes) within data [14]. After a first, global inspection of the data, we used an iterative process to code the data. First, the data were organized according to the themes from the interview schedule. The following themes from the interview schedule were used to start the iterative process: discussing cancer with the children, moment of receiving the box, appreciation of the box, use and points of improvement. Then, data were labeled in a more interpretive way by adding codes in more detail. Codes were grouped in order to look for patterns or relationships among the codes. Finally, the data were interpreted and reported to address the research questions. We aimed to include enough participants to achieve data saturation. However, due to the low number of participants, data saturation could not be guaranteed. This limitation is discussed in the Discussion.

Experiences related to communicating the diagnosis

All mothers encountered challenges when discussing the diagnosis with their children. Nearly all women initiated these conversations shortly after receiving the diagnosis and preferred an open communication approach. One woman chose to broach the topic after receiving her treatment plan, aiming to equip herself with adequate information to address potential questions from her children. While engaging in these discussions, all women endeavored to tailor the information to suit their children's comprehension levels, yet refrained from discussing (potential) metastases and death. Most mothers mentioned their regular hospital visits and conveyed to their children that doctors were working towards their recovery.

Some children raised concerns about the possibility of losing their mother. All women deliberately used the term “cancer” during their discussions, partly to preempt their children hearing it from other sources in their environment.“We just said that mommy's breast is sick. We never clearly discussed the metastasized part. A child can't do anything with that, what does that mean to her actually? We just named the creature by its name; Mommy has breast cancer and has to go to the hospital a lot. But the doctors are going to do everything they can. I never said that I would get better, since we didn't know that for sure”. (M3).

Both nurses and mothers emphasized the importance of transparency and honesty in communication with children. While the interviewed mothers described the communication as “natural,” they also acknowledged its intensity in retrospect.“Well yes, it was very weird that the conversation was about me and about the cancer, but it was not very different from our other conversations.” (M4).“Those are conversations you have to have with your child. Of course I can only speak for myself, the communication went well, because I was in this survival mode. But now, when I look back, I think, oh, I don't want to have these kind of conversations with my child.” (M3).

However, in the interviews, it is notable that, from the sample, most mothers did not receive the box immediately after diagnosis but rather approximately 1, 2, 25, and 85 weeks after diagnosis (see Table 1). The mothers are not unanimous about the best timing; three mothers (M1, M2, and M5) consider the first contact after diagnosis the best timing for receiving the box. One mother (M1) explains this as follows:“If I had received the box a month or so after diagnosis, I would have missed the information and would have possibly thought: I wish I had had it sooner, it was nice to have the box at home and to use it whenever it suited us.” (M1).

Two mothers (M3 and M4) think it is better to receive the box not immediately after diagnosis, but for instance after one or two treatments with chemotherapy.

One mother describes it as follows: “Yes, you shouldn't do that at the beginning, because it makes no sense (…) At the moment of such a diagnosis (…) you are so overwhelmed by emotion that you can't think logically or rationally.” When asked what would have happened if she had received the box at diagnosis, she responded: “I think that if you receive the box then, you just put it somewhere and forget about it, you don't open it. And how confronting is it that you've just heard that you have cancer? You can't even believe yet that you have cancer, and then suddenly you have a booklet saying ‘mom has cancer’.” (M3) The decision to postpone the use of the box was also influenced by the overwhelming amount of information received by the mother around the time of diagnosis. “Maybe I didn't use the box a lot because in the beginning you receive so much information, it is quite overwhelming. So perhaps it is better to receive the box a bit later.” (M4).

Opinion patients and nurses about ‘the telling box’

Several nurses described ‘the telling box’ as a good addition to patient care (N1,N2,N3) and appreciated being able to offer something (N1, N3, N4). They expressed satisfaction (N4) and pride (N2) about the box, describing it as complete (N5). Providing ‘the telling box’ to patients facilitated nurses in addressing the topic of communicating with children about cancer diagnoses. It was seen as a supportive tool to make topics discussable within the family (N1, N3, and N4), where mothers can choose from different methods/techniques (N1, N2, and N5).

One point of concern mentioned several times by the nurses was the doubt whether ‘the telling box’ is sufficiently appropriate for the age group (N2, N4, and N6). Some nurses had the impression that mothers could start using it immediately (N1 and N3) and described it as practical: “And that is an advantage of ‘the telling box’, we can give it to our patients and then they have the information, the book or the links to useful websites. You can give the patients something that is helpful.” (N5).

One nurse believed that the contents of the box were not immediately clear and therefore not immediately usable. “I think you have to read a bit about what everything is for. Ideally, I would like a box that is immediately clear when opened. But that's my shortcoming. If I continue, for example, you have a set of cards, and you have to print and cut out the cards.” (N6).

Regarding the contents, the “worry eater” was described positively by one nurse because a cuddly toy can offer affection and warmth to the child (N2). However, another nurse found it to be a creepy toy for children (N6). Two nurses were also positive about the booklets (N3 and N6). Three nurses mentioned not being fully aware of the contents of ‘the telling box’ or the variant for older children (N1, N2, and N3). Lack of time and knowledge were factors preventing the nurses from distributing the boxes.

Four out of the five patients expressed appreciation for the availability of ‘the telling box’ and believed it provided value.“My six year old daughter was very excited about the box. And yes, the worry eater and the keychain backpack, she still drags them around.” (M5).

“My six year old daughter was very excited about the box. And yes, the worry eater and the keychain backpack, she still drags them around.” (M5).

Each item contained within ‘the telling box’ was individually discussed. The ‘worry eater’ garnered positive feedback from both patients and children. Similarly, the key chain with the small backpack was well-received. One mother expressed the view that these items, being colorful and tangible, were particularly appealing to children.

The book “Mama Has Cancer” was utilized by three mothers, with two of them expressing satisfaction with its content. However, one patient found the book did not accurately reflect her own situation and treatment journey.

Regarding the workbook “When Your Mother or Father Has Cancer,” while appreciated, the mothers felt it was more suitable for slightly older children and found it quite extensive, better suited for patients undergoing a more prolonged treatment trajectory.

The card set for partners and patients, the dice game, and the talking package for children were not utilized by the five interviewed mothers.

Points of improvement of ‘the telling box’

Both nurses and patients expressed concerns about the content of the box and its suitability for children of different ages. Suggestions were made to tailor the box's content to the individual patient's circumstances, including the number of children in the family and the specific phase of the disease. It was proposed that the box's content should be continuously evaluated and adjusted to meet evolving needs—an ongoing process.

Two mothers found the content of the box overwhelming and recommended developing a more personalized approach to prevent information overload (M4 and M5). This would allow for customization based on the patient's treatment trajectory and disease phase.”I think that the disease process can be divided in stages and that the information could be adjusted to the stage you are in. And give the information in stages.” (M4).

”I think that the disease process can be divided in stages and that the information could be adjusted to the stage you are in. And give the information in stages.” (M4).

Two mothers suggested including information tailored for children aged 0–5 years and recommended adding small toys for young children. Another mother questioned the decision to prominently display the word “cancer” on the outside of the box, finding it distressing. (The telling box - Talking about cancer with your family).

Mothers also provided recommendations for expanding the box's contents, including a stuffed animal for older children (>10 years), a social map to locate local psychosocial support, and information about organizations hosting free events. Additionally, two mothers suggested the possibility of discussing the use of the box with a healthcare professional afterward to assess communication with the children and determine if additional support is needed. They expressed a desire to learn more about children's reactions and how to effectively address them.“Maybe to discuss with the nurse what you can do as a parent with your child's reactions. Perhaps a tutorial ‘how to..’ Because now I have to deal with it myself without any instruction or help. A professional might pick up different signals from my child.” (M5).

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

Discussing cancer with children can be exceptionally challenging, yet ample evidence suggests that open and honest communication plays a pivotal role in aiding children's coping mechanisms following a parent's cancer diagnosis [5]. In an effort to assist mothers in broaching the topic of breast cancer with their children, an intervention known as ‘the telling box’ was devised. This study aimed to examine the experiences and perspectives of both mothers facing breast cancer and nurses supporting them during their treatment trajectory, as they engage with ‘the telling box’.

The primary finding of our study reveals that, despite ‘the telling box’ being co-developed with parents undergoing cancer treatment and having children at home, it did not fully meet the expectations of the interviewed mothers. What constitutes ‘optimal information’ varies among patients, highlighting the necessity for tailoring the contents of ‘the telling box’ to each patient's unique needs, preferences, and capabilities [10,15,16]. Ellis et al. identified three factors as critical in intervention development: children need age-appropriate information about the parents' cancer, children require support in communicating with parents, family members and healthcare professionals and children need an environment where they feel comfortable sharing positive or negative emotions and can have their experiences normalized among peers [17]. While the content of ‘the telling box’ fulfilled most of these criteria, such as providing age-appropriate information, incorporating various communication aids, and including details about peer support, customization according to each patient's individual requirements is necessary.

One significant challenge encountered during our pilot study was determining the optimal timing for distributing the box. The appropriate moment varies depending on the treatment trajectory and the individual needs of each patient. Sinclair et al. assert that the delivery and timing of information, resources, and support regarding communication with children in families affected by a mother's breast cancer are suboptimal and may overwhelm these families [15].

The interviewed nurses consider ‘the telling box’ a valuable tool. Providing ‘the telling box’ to patients facilitated nurses in addressing the topic of communicating with children about cancer diagnoses. Research by Sinclair et al. similarly underscores that healthcare professionals acknowledge the importance of supporting mothers facing cancer. But in their research they also show that this support often fails to align with the mothers' actual needs [15]. Previous research indicates that while interventions yield positive outcomes, only a minority of families actually receive them. Ernst et al. found that although 73 % of parents with cancer expressed a desire for information or psychosocial support related to their children, only 9 % received support focused on their family situation and children [18].

Inhestern et al. identify several factors contributing to this gap, including practical barriers such as limited time, transportation difficulties, and healthcare insurance issues, as well as emotional factors such as concerns about emotional overload for themselves or their children [10]. Our pilot study also revealed challenges related to time constraints among nurses and emotional readiness and information overload among patients, particularly immediately following diagnosis. In the review by Inhestern et al. facilitators for the utilization of psychosocial interventions are also discussed, such as when a healthcare professional recommends their use. Direct explanation and personal contact with healthcare professionals have been shown to increase patients' uptake of the intervention. Parents' trust in the intervention is also supported by healthcare professionals who demonstrate effective communication skills and familiarity with the intervention. Additionally, factors such as the need for support and behavioral changes in children were identified as facilitating factors for intervention uptake. Moreover, effective collaboration between medical specialists and healthcare professionals providing psychosocial interventions was found to be a crucial prerequisite [10].

The main limitation of this pilot study is its small sample size, which limits generalizability and may have led to insufficient data saturation. Specific themes might nog fully emerge in a small sample like this, for instance not all relevant dimensions of the topic may have been revealed. Future research with a larger and more diverse sample is needed to explore additional perspectives and to ensure saturation is achieved. We also need to consider the possibility of non-response bias. Patients who were interviewed consistently emphasized the significance of openness and honesty when communicating with children. The mothers who did not participate in our study may hold divergent perspectives on this subject. Furthermore, we should consider parental report with caution, since own burden and less psychosocial availability of parents may lead to underestimation of children's burden, as is described by Inhestern et al. [19]. Despite positive feedback from nurses about the usefulness of ‘the telling box’, its limited distribution suggests barriers to implementation. Possible reasons for this could include time constraints, inadequate training or education on the use of the tool, and a lack of designated personnel responsible for its dissemination. To address this limitation, we could provide training sessions to educate healthcare professionals about the benefits and proper use of ‘the telling box’ [20]. Additionally, assigning a dedicated staff member, such as a nurse coordinator, to oversee the implementation and distribution of the box could help ensure its consistent use.

Innovation

The innovative aspects of our study lie in its exploration of both patient and healthcare professional perspectives, as well as the co-creation of ‘the telling box’ with healthcare professionals and the patient panel of the MCCC. This approach mirrors the recommendations put forth by Ellis et al. for patient-centered intervention development, emphasizing the importance of incorporating diverse perspectives and co-creating solutions with end-users [17]. It underscores the importance of systematically evaluating interventions to ensure they meet the needs of families affected by cancer. By doing so, we can enhance efficacy and contribute to the ongoing refinement and development of interventions like ‘the telling box.’ Since expressing their thoughts and feelings can be challenging for children, the inclusion of play and game-based activities in ‘the telling box’ can help children construct and express their thoughts and emotions. Alexander et al. showed that these approaches facilitated discussion and helped children comprehending and articulating their thoughts [21].

From the findings of this study, several recommendations emerge. Firstly, adjusting the quantity of information provided and tailoring the contents of the box even more to the child's age are crucial steps. Additionally, the timing of box distribution should be flexible to accommodate the diverse needs of parents. Educating healthcare professionals in the use of the intervention and effective communication skills can also improve intervention uptake. Furthermore, involvement of healthcare professionals in distributing the boxes and encouraging patients to utilize the boxes is essential [20]. It can also be worthwile to explore the potential for a digital or app-based version of ‘the telling box’ to offer greater flexibility. It might allow for adaptive content that updates based on the family's evolving needs. Such a tool could be supplemented by interactive components such as videos, games, or forums for peer support.

To enhance response rates in future studies, we suggest expanding the inclusion period and scheduling interviews after the active treatment trajectory. Providing participants with the option to attend interviews either face-to-face or online may also improve response rates.

Moving forward, we recommend continuing this research with a focus on a systematic approach for further development of ‘the telling box’. Intervention mapping could be employed as a methodological framework to guide this process effectively [22].

Conclusion

Although the ‘telling box’ did not initially meet all the needs of parents as anticipated, this study serves as a ‘Brilliant Failure’. The unexpected outcomes provide crucial insights into areas for refinement, guiding future improvements that may better support families dealing with cancer. Both nurses and mothers emphasize the significance of openness and honesty in communicating with children. While nurses have found ‘the telling box’ to be a valuable tool facilitating in addressing the topic of parents communicating with children about cancer, our study reveals that the current design does not fully meet the needs of the interviewed women. The timing of receiving the box was perceived as suboptimal, and preferences varied greatly among participants. From the nurses' perspective, lack of time and knowledge were factors preventing them from distributing the boxes. The results from this study can be used to inform future intervention research and clinical practice to develop tools tailored to the individual circumstances and preferences, supporting parents and children in effectively communicating with each other.
