Public funding for mitochondrial donation: An Australian public deliberation
Authors: Ainsley J. Newson, Jane Williams, Giuliana Fuscaldo, Ashleigh Hill, Ezra Kneebone, Karinne Ludlow, Catherine Mills, Megan Munsie, Sarah Norris, Paul Scuffham, Liz Sutton, David R. Thorburn, Chris Degeling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Mitochondrial donation (MD) is a reproductive technique that aims to allow individuals at-risk of having a child with mitochondrial DNA disease avoid this outcome. Research to inform possible clinical use of MD is underway in Australia and births following the use of this technique have been announced in the United Kingdom. However, how the availability of MD will be funded in the mid- to long-term remains uncertain. One factor impacting funding decisions is public sentiment, yet there is scant evidence globally regarding attitudes toward MD funding. We sought to discern attitudes of informed members of the Australian public to how the provision of MD should be funded.MethodsWe held three community juries to gauge public views on how MD should be funded. A community jury involves providing a diverse group of citizens with expert testimony and facilitating deliberation to arrive at a position.ResultsForty-two jurors participated across three juries. All juries voted by majority to support public funding for MD. Each jury made slightly different funding choices: one preferred full public funding, another preferred co-payment, while the third was divided among full public funding, co-payment, and no public funding. Reasons in favour of public funding comprised value for money, equity (i.e., the fair and just distribution of MD) and promoting innovation. Reasons against were opportunity cost, that MD wasn’t necessary, and ethical objections to MD. Jurors also devised conditions for future funding: external review, capped services, better funding for alternative interventions and means testing.ConclusionsShould the current Australian MD research trial enable clinical provision, assuming that our participants’ views are consistent with those of most Australians when informed of the trade-offs, benefits and costs, then it is likely that there will be strong public support for governments to fund access. Introduction

The term ‘mitochondrial disease’ (mito) describes a group of inheritable metabolic conditions arising from disease-causing gene variants (mutations) in either the nuclear (around 13% of cases) or mitochondrial (87% of cases) genome [1]. Around 1 in 4,300 people are thought to have mito globally [2], although many remain undiagnosed and so true prevalence is challenging to discern. One reason why mito is under-diagnosed is that the disease can cause any symptom in any organ at any age. And while some symptoms of mito can be treated, morbidity and mortality are high and there is presently no cure. Mito arising from mutations in the nuclear genome can be inherited from either or both genetic parents, whereas mitochondrially encoded mito is inherited matrilineally: that is, embryos inherit mitochondria only from the contributing oocyte.

People with known nuclear mutations for mito can opt to reduce their chances of having an affected child using established techniques of prenatal diagnosis (PND) or pre-implantation genetic testing for monogenic conditions (PGT-M). Until relatively recently, however, options to mitigate the transmission of mitochondrially encoded mito have been limited [3, 4]. While techniques such as PND and PGT-M are possible for some people who have or carry mitochondrially encoded mito, they do not always give an accurate indication of the chance a child may develop mito. PND and PGT-M are also not effective for individuals with a high mitochondrial mutation load, as this significantly increases the chance that all their embryos may contain mutated mitochondria.

The importance of being genetically related to one’s child is contested, but is valued by most prospective parents [5]. As a result, researchers have sought to identify additional options to both mitigate the transmission of mitochondrially encoded mito and offer people the chance to have a genetically related child who also has a reduced chance of developing mito. For some, choosing to reduce the chance their child will develop mito, while also preserving a genetic link between the affected parent and child, is preferable to alternatives such as whole oocyte donation from an unaffected woman.

Mitochondrial donation (MD; also known as mitochondrial replacement therapy) is an in vitro fertilization (IVF) based reproductive procedure that aims to address the shortcomings of alternative procreative options for those with mitochondrially encoded mito. MD involves removing the nuclear DNA from an oocyte obtained from the intending mother and inserting it into a donor oocyte (with unaffected mitochondria) which has had its own nuclear DNA removed [6]. The nuclear DNA of the intending mother is retained in MD. For those with a high chance of transmission, MD is the only procedure that allows the possibility of having a child with a reduced chance of developing mito and who is genetically related to the intending mother [7]. There are a suite of possible techniques of MD, some of which involve embryo destruction [6].

People with mito strongly desire to avoid passing this condition to their children, and support the introduction of MD even if they don’t plan to access it themselves [8, 9]. Patients place significant hope that the technology will prevent suffering for future generations and trust scientists and regulatory bodies. Many are keen to access the technology, and want support to be able to do so [9, 10]. However, some are also ambivalent or uncertain about aspects of MD, such as its long-term safety, and some feel an obligation to use it [10]. For these reasons, in countries where this technology is specifically legalized, offers of MD are being made within a highly structured clinical or trial environment, including counselling.

Two countries globally have enacted specific laws to enable MD: the United Kingdom (UK) in 2015 and Australia in 2022. In the UK, MD is regulated by the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1998 (as amended), including a comprehensive licensing regime. The birth of at least one child from MD in the UK was made public in 2023, and papers detailing the births of eight babies were published in 2025 [11, 12].

In Australia, the Mitochondrial Donation Law Reform (Maeve’s Law) Act 2022 [13] amended the Research Involving Human Embryos Act and is now in force as consolidated legislation. The law allows the phased introduction of MD as a reproductive option for those with mitochondrially encoded mito. As with the UK, people in Australia who meet stipulated conditions, including there being a significant probability of transmitting a serious form of mito, and existing alternatives (such as PGT-M) not being appropriate, [14] may be able to access MD. Also as similar to the UK, counselling will be required, and ongoing monitoring of children born following the procedure is also incorporated into the regulatory regime. Under the Australian law, any future clinical roll out of MD is contingent upon the prior completion of a successful clinical trial, which is now underway [15]. If the trial is successful, calculations by a member of our research team (DT) suggest that MD will be used by around 10 Australian families per year.

In the UK, provision of MD has to date been funded by numerous bodies, including the National Health Service and the Wellcome Trust. In Australia, if the current trial is successful and MD is introduced into clinical practice, it is expected that the IVF component of the treatment would be funded as per existing arrangements. Access to IVF and other assisted reproductive technologies (ART) in Australia is currently funded through various subsidies, rebates (such as those offered by some state governments), as well as co-payments via the Medical Benefits Schedule (MBS). The MBS is one component of ‘Medicare’; the national funding system for health care delivered outside public hospitals. Almost all people undergoing ART in Australia are liable for out-of-pocket expenses, with some of these recoverable through private health insurance [16]. Regardless of how ART is procured, out of pocket expenses for MD in Australia will likely be several thousand dollars per cycle.

It remains unclear how the MD technique itself might be funded in Australia. The Australian statutory bodies that advise government regarding public funding of health technologies and medicines draw on a range of evidence, including public opinion and patient acceptability, when making decisions about funding [17, 18]. The social and ethical aspects of MD, like any new intervention, will also be relevant to the government’s acceptance of the funding advice they receive from the statutory bodies [19–22].

Little is known globally about public views on how MD should be funded. A qualitative study with a range of participants in Canada showed that 21 of 32 participants felt MD should be government funded, but the reasons for this were not explored in depth [8]. Using a deliberative democracy methodology, we convened three community juries to ascertain the views of informed members of the Australian public about the acceptability of, and policy options for, the most likely funding models for MD. Community jury processes are designed to allow decision-makers to hear people’s considered opinions, values and preferences [23, 24]. Involving citizens in a high-quality dialogue about a key issue can lead to increased public support for the resulting policy [25, 26]. Decision-makers can learn what an informed public wants, and why, in relation to a particular issue [24].

Methods

Community juries comprise a panel of citizens who examine and deliberate on an issue of public significance, over two to five days [23]. A jury consists of around 12 to 15 individuals and serves as a microcosm of the broader public [27]. They are usually oriented around a specific issue – typically formulated as a ‘charge’ for jurors to consider [28]. Jurors hear from a variety of expert witnesses, are given the opportunity to ask questions, and deliberate together to provide evidence of the likely acceptability and perceived legitimacy of different policy alternatives to assist policymaking [27, 29]. Consensus in jury decision-making is encouraged but not required and a decision can also be determined by a vote. Community juries have been used in Australia and elsewhere to consider various issues relating to medical technologies [30, 31] including the permissibility of MD [32].

The community jury process is usually designed to extend the thinking of participants beyond their own interests, to contemplate public goods and collective community needs. A robust and reliable deliberative process must, at a minimum: provide balanced and factual information; ensure that diverse perspectives are considered (including when there are conflicts or marginal views); and enable free and open discussion, challenge and debate [33]. The jury method assumes that people can think rationally and change their views should the evidence warrant it [33].

While a community jury cannot statistically represent ‘the general public’, it can derive a sense of what an informed public would advise [34, 35]. Community jury participants are recruited to capture diversity of experiences and backgrounds, and the deliberation process is ideally organized so as to mitigate power imbalances [36]. As such, community juries can capture and reflect key community considerations about current and proposed policy directions [23, 37].

The question for this jury

Jurors were asked to decide how MD should be funded in Australia, assuming the outcome of the current trial results in MD being offered clinically to those who meet eligibility requirements. The question for this community jury

Australia is a large country, and budgetary constraints precluded us from running a large national citizens’ jury process. To capture what informed groups of Australians from different settings thought about funding decisions for MD, as well as their values and underpinning reasons for holding the positions they took, we ran three smaller community juries, one each in the states of Queensland, Victoria and New South Wales. Jury 1 was held in Townsville (a medium-sized coastal city and the administrative centre for the far north of the state of Queensland, population of ~ 200,000); Jury 2 was held in Melbourne (capital city of the state of Victoria, population ~ 5 million); and Jury 3 in Tamworth (a small inland city in the state of New South Wales, population ~ 60,000). All were held over two days (Saturday-Sunday) between March and May 2023. The jury process is laid out in Supplementary Material Table 1.

Juror recruitment

Human Research Ethics Committee approval was obtained from the Social Sciences Human Research Ethics Committee at the University of Wollongong, Australia (Reference 2022/264). An independent professional research service (Taverner Research Group) was engaged to recruit jury participants from each of the study sites. Potential participants were contacted using either random telephone digit dialling or a targeted social media advertising strategy. People aged under 18, those known to have mito or who have close friends or family with mito, those who were pregnant (or had a pregnant partner) and those who had used assisted reproduction technologies in the last 4 years were excluded, to mitigate possible participant distress. When the recruitment company made contact and established that a person was a potential participant, they revealed the relevant date, location and honorarium for the study, and the study purpose. Upon agreement, basic participant information was collected using a structured screening instrument (See Supplementary Material).

Initial recruitment and screening produced a pool of potential jurors, who were then selected purposively. Each jury was selected to promote gender balance and to include a range of ages, socioeconomic and cultural diversity within each jury [34, 36]. Jurors provided written informed consent to participate at the commencement of each jury and were remunerated for their time (AUD$200 per participant, per day of participation).

Expert testimony provided to the community juries

To facilitate standardisation between juries, testimony from six experts was pre-recorded as a series of 10- to 20-min-long video presentations and played on Day 1 of all juries (see Supplementary Material for the jury schedule; presentations available on request). Experts were selected following extensive discussion among the research team and following consultation with external advisors. Experts each had relevant experience and expertise, and as a cohort could provide balanced information supporting different perspectives on the potential impacts, benefits, and costs of MD funding strategies. After each presentation, the expert was available by web-conference for jurors to ask questions, with discussion facilitated by a researcher (JW).

For the first hour of Jury Day 2, jurors discussed and debated the Day 1 evidence, aided by a researcher (CD) acting as facilitator. Juries then deliberated alone for a further 90 min (to promote group autonomy, authenticity and mitigate the risks of facilitation bias), and were instructed to reach a verdict on the question posed. The verdicts, as well as jurors’ underpinning reasoning and any dissenting views, were then reported to the research team in a final feedback session facilitated by a researcher (CD). This included cross-checking and exploring, with the deliberative group, jurors’ reasoning for the position taken. Each point was reviewed by the jury to ensure accuracy. Throughout the jury, a variety of techniques were used to ensure that jurors had equitable opportunity to express their views. Our research and reporting processes for these Community Juries were cross-checked against the CJ Checklist protocol [38].

Data collection and analysis

The three juries are the units of analysis in this study. All jury deliberations (facilitated and un-facilitated) and expert question and answer sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed as an accurate record of proceedings. To track changes in the positions held by individual jurors, participants completed an anonymous ballot at 4 time-points during jury proceedings: (i) beginning of Day 1; (ii) end of Day 1; (iii) beginning of Day 2; and (iv) end of the jury. During the final session researchers recorded the verdict and reasons on a flipchart. Each point was reviewed by the jury to ensure accuracy.

Community jury verdicts

All three juries voted by majority in support of some level of public funding (i.e., options A or B, Fig. 1) to assist patient access to MD in Australia (Fig. 2). Support for full public funding (option A) was strongest in Tamworth, whereas most participants in Melbourne supported a model requiring a user co-payment (option B). In Townsville, the vote was split almost evenly across the three options.Fig. 2Final vote tallies on preferred funding options, by city

Final vote tallies on preferred funding options, by city

Results of time-point ballots (see Supplementary Material, Table 2) demonstrate that the balance of the vote changed throughout each jury’s deliberations. Support for some level of public funding for MD was present from the beginning of each jury. In Melbourne and Tamworth this support consolidated after deliberation; however, in Townsville the group polarised during independent deliberation, with each position being supported relatively equally and with the same reasons being given both in favour of, and against, a particular position. Juror reasons for supporting or rejecting the different MD funding models were fairly consistent across the three juries, with some underlying differences in the values drawn upon to justify their position.

Reasons for public funding of MD

Overall, three key reasons were raised in support of public funding for MD: value for money, equity and promoting innovation.

Public funding for MD (Option A or Option B) is value for money

Jurors who were in favor of providing some level of public funding for MD reasoned that the cost to the government would be relatively minor compared to the benefits it provided. In Melbourne and Tamworth, where there was strong support for public funding of MD, the jurors who held this view argued that benefits would also accrue to the broader health care system because preventing mito offered greater value for money than caring for people with the disease. In other words, these jurors felt there would be savings to the health system if MD were available. Underpinning this was the view that Australia is a wealthy country and can afford to support MD. These jurors also expressed that universal healthcare is what Medicare is for and so providing (at least some) public funding is consistent with existing arrangements. Further, these jurors saw restricting MD only to people with sufficient private resources to pay for it as elitist and that any shift to a wholly privatised healthcare system ought to be resisted.

Public funding for MD (Option A or Option B) promotes equity

Jurors also felt that equity was important and that funding MD from public money would be fair and equitable for all. On this view, public funding would ensure that people who can benefit from MD would not have additional cost-related barriers to accessing it. Some jurors explicitly noted that people already have out of pocket expenses for the use of IVF technologies. On this basis, not providing some level of public funding for individuals to access MD could be seen as a form of discrimination against people with genetic ‘bad luck’. This discrimination, these jurors suggested, would be compounded by economic disadvantage. Notably, maximising equity was a driver of the jurors who were in favour of public funding for MD without co-payment (Option A).

Public funding for MD promotes medical innovation

A minority of jurors in Melbourne and Tamworth partly based their support for public funding for MD access on the potential for this activity to promote scientific innovation and treatments for other conditions.

Points on which there was some disagreement or difference

Jurors were divided about two further reasons for public funding for MD: the necessity of a co-payment to promote equity in access and the value of being genetically related to one’s children.

The need for co-payment by those using MD

There was some disagreement among those in favour of public funding about whether a user co-payment was warranted (the difference between options A and B). Even though promoting equity was a priority, most jurors in Townsville and Melbourne supported a model of public funding for MD services that involved co-payment. One reason for this was an acknowledgement of the many other conditions that require funding. Therefore, it was important to spread resources across the population and a co-payment was one way to achieve this. All jurors in Townsville and Melbourne who were in favour of a co-payment model reported they saw value in the consistency with existing funding models for IVF.

The importance of genetic parenthood

Those jurors in Townsville who supported public funding for MD unconditionally (option A) reasoned that having genetically related children is sufficiently important to warrant government support. They argued that children born following MD will be very much wanted and to not support the use of a beneficial technology seems ungenerous.

In contrast, jurors in Melbourne and Tamworth discussed the value of genetic relatedness but reached an alternate position that genetically related children are not a necessity. The key concerns that underpinned their reasoning on how the service should be funded were more clearly focused on the interests of the future child, particularly their health and the impact of growing up with a parent who was unwell with, or had died from, mito.

Reasons against public funding for MD

The seven jurors in Townsville and Tamworth who voted against providing any level of public funding for MD services (Option C) did so on the basis that: it would undermine equity, alternatives to MD exist and that MD has unresolved moral and safety issues.

Public funding for MD has opportunity costs and undermines overall equity

Jurors who held this ‘opportunity cost’ focused view argued that only a few people would benefit from MD. They reasoned that there are many other unmet medical needs – public dental care was given as an example – and it is important to distribute funds for health services so that the most people benefit. For some, this concern had a temporal dimension: spending money on a genetic condition (mito) now may limit the affordability of other interventions for other conditions in the future.

MD is a want, not a need

Most of the jurors who were against public funding for MD claimed that it is a want, not a need. To support this position, these jurors reasoned that there are alternatives to MD (e.g. using donor oocytes) – and these options should be revisited or supported.

Moral and safety objections to MD

A small number of jurors in Townsville and Tamworth raised moral objections to public funding of MD because they felt that (certain methods of) it destroyed a potential life. The juror who raised this concern in Tamworth added that their position would change to supporting funding if a MD technique was used that did not lead to embryo discard.

Those jurors in Townsville who supported Option C also argued that public funds should not be used to support access to novel and ethically contentious interventions where the initial success rate and long-term outcome were uncertain. These jurors also worried that public funding of MD might normalize other (unwanted) forms of genetic and germline modification. In contrast, none of the jurors in Melbourne raised any such concerns, nor linked them to funding decisions.

Conditions for public funding to support individuals to access MD

For those jurors who voted for some level of public funding (Options A and B), their support depended on four conditions: external review, capping services, also funding alternate pathways to MD and (in Townsville only) introducing means testing to access public funding.

External review

All Melbourne and Tamworth jurors wanted a review conducted on the cost and effectiveness of MD. This included establishing unobtrusive systems for monitoring of the long-term health of the child (an aspect that is already included in the regulatory regime).

Caps on services

All jurors in all locations wanted a limit placed on the number of publicly funded MD/IVF cycles an individual could access. This condition was provided as a guiding principle only, and no set cap was agreed upon. Reasons given included: concerns about some people overusing the technology, likely low success rates, and impacts on women and their families if unlimited use was allowed.

Increased funding of alternative pathways for people to become parents

Given barriers to adoption and the potential for low rates of success with MD technologies, all jurors in all locations thought that governments should provide greater support for alternative pathways for people with mito to become parents.

Means testing and prices

Some members of the Townsville jury wanted access to government funding for MD to be asset and means tested to make sure those with significant resources did not have access to public funds for treatment. This idea was discussed and then rejected by the groups in Melbourne and Tamworth who reasoned that health care in Australia is not traditionally subjected to means testing. They also noted that the Medicare safety net (a monetary threshold beyond which individuals or families pay less for healthcare expenses [39]) offers additional means to provide support for out-of-pocket expenses. Jurors in Tamworth wanted price caps on MD services provided by private clinics, and for people with private health insurance to have the insurer billed, to reduce the demand on the public purse.

Discussion

We have described three community juries that assessed the attitudes and perspectives of members of the Australian public towards how future clinical provision of mitochondrial donation (MD) could be funded in Australia. Our findings suggest that should the current Australian clinical trial of MD [8] lead to subsequent health care system rollout, and assuming that our participants’ views are consistent with those of most Australians when informed of the trade-offs, benefits and costs, then it is likely that there will be public support for governments to fund or support people (who meet eligibility criteria) to access MD. However, it is also likely that some in the community will raise objections to governments funding access to MD.

After our juries were held, a report commissioned by the Mito Foundation in Australia has estimated the overall cost of mito to the Australian population to be over AUD1 billion per annum [40]. All three juries justified support for public funding of MD based on value for money, in that funding MD would lead to potential savings for the health system (and related bodies, such as Australia’s National Disability Insurance Scheme) through prevention of morbidity and mortality.

The promotion of equity also featured strongly in the juries’ reasoning. Jurors wanted governments to even out any possible disparities between those who could, and could not, access MD. More broadly, jurors wanted people with a high chance of having a child with MD to have access to a technology that may enable a healthy life. They also reflected on the cost to families in caring for a person with mito. In Australia, while the expenses accrued in caring for people with mito has coverage from Medicare and the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), funding gaps remain that require private contributions. Evidence suggests that Australians pay some of the highest out-of-pocket health expenses worldwide [41]. Additionally, a recent survey of Australian parents of children with mito found that many have a restricted capacity to work (due to caring responsibilities) while also covering out-of-pocket expenses of up to AUD25,000 per year [42].

Most jurors also strongly valued the fact that much health care in Australia has public funding, and they saw this as an important public good. They felt that Medicare exists for all Australians and that it should not discriminate against those with genetic conditions. They also recognized that different funding models can create different barriers. A co-payment funding model, for example, has been found to generate financial and sociocultural barriers to access for women from disadvantaged groups in Australia [43]. Nevertheless, other deliberative engagement with community members, clinicians and consumers on reforming Australian ART funding models indicates public support for requiring a co-payment from service users, with measures in place to provide access for those unable to pay [44].

The value of genetic relatedness as a rationale for MD was conspicuous in its comparable absence in most of the jurors’ reasoning. The concept and value of genetic relatedness was mentioned in discussions (and raised by expert witnesses, including the patient expert witness), but in their discussions jurors gave greater attention to arguments based on value for money and equity. Jurors’ de-prioritization of the place that genetic kinship should have in debates over MD contrasts with academic debates in fields such as bioethics, where the (dis)value of genetic relatedness or genetic kinship has been, and continues to be, highly contested [45, 46]. This concept has also featured in empirical research eliciting attitudes to MD in Canada [8]. Nevertheless, when it comes to making policy decisions, public concerns about genetic relatedness may not be as salient or important to the implementation of MD as some experts may initially have thought.

Our findings also suggest that members of the public will think about funding for MD in relation to how existing interventions (such as IVF, including using intracytoplasmic sperm injection) are funded. MD tended to be seen as a ‘bolt on’ to IVF by our jurors, and an IVF clinic is involved in the current Australian research trial of MD [15]. As we have described, IVF and other forms of ART (including PGT-M) already have some public funding in Australia; therefore MD was seen as a further intervention on top of this existing provision. For the majority of our jurors, there was not sufficient justification for departing from existing funding models for similar techniques when it came to funding MD. Instead, they opted for a policy position influenced by existing funding models. This arguably also reflects how future decisions about funding will be made, given that it is rare to have policy made without reference to existing similar health technologies in the same jurisdiction. As a result, there is likely to be low endorsement among informed Australians for MD to have no public funding at all.

A further implication of our findings is that fundamental objections to MD on grounds such as religion or safety will persist among community members, and that these views will also influence decisions about public funding even in a regulatory landscape where the intervention has been legalized. Noting that the population of regional cities such as Townsville and Tamworth tends to be much more socially conservative than the residents of metropolitan centres, these considerations were raised by some jurors in these settings and shaped their decisions. Diversity in views and value commitments is likely to persist, and there will always be a small number of people for whom techniques like MD are unacceptable.

The motivation for these juries was the relevance of public opinion to funding decisions in Australia. However, it is also important to recognize that public opinion may be influenced by over-simplified or erroneous assumptions about health and illness [47]. As such, patient attitudes and experiences are also relevant, and are actively sought during Australian funding decision-making processes [18]. As noted, patients support MD generally and endorse public funding for it [8]. In our community juries, we also included an expert witness presentation from a person with lived experience of mito (AH), with the aim of offering jurors direct access to patient testimony and perspectives. However, helping patients to navigate decisions about using this technology will remain complex in the near term. Australian funding bodies will need to navigate a wide range of considerations, drawing on evidence not just about mito but other reproductive techniques too (such as PGT-M) [48–50] when making decisions about supporting access to all reproductive care, including MD.

These community juries involved a deliberative approach to promote bidirectional information exchange between experts and lay publics, to facilitate participants to develop and support a set of positions in response to a policy question. A strength of this method as compared with most other forms of social research is that it promotes extended deliberation and debate amongst a group of diverse community members, based on information relevant to the question. As a result, outcomes of community juries can indicate what an informed public are likely to choose or think about the issue, given enough time and information, as well as an opportunity for engagement and reflection with others.

Community juries do have a comparatively small sample size, but the methodology nevertheless ensures high quality deliberation. All participants in these juries were required to be able to speak and read English, which may have precluded some community members from participating. Jurors had to choose from three pre-determined funding options, as opposed to developing their own ideas about funding. However, these options were the most appropriate and feasible options for the Australian health care context. Additionally, jurors were able to (and did) give reasons and conditions to qualify or modify their preferred funding option.

Conclusion

This community jury on public funding for mitochondrial donation identified a range of views. Findings suggest that there will be support for public funding of MD among informed members of the public, grounded in principles of equity, value for money, and support for medical innovation. While views varied on the specifics of funding models, and some concerns were raised, the deliberative process suggests support for inclusive and fair access to MD. These findings offer evidence for policymakers in Australia and elsewhere who will consider the future clinical implementation and funding of MD.
