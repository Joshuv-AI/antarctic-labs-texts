MoS2-CdS composite for photocatalytic reduction of hexavalent chromium and thin film optoelectronic device applications
Authors: Sayani Kar, Surajit Ghosh, Tanusri Pal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We introduce an enhanced light-harvesting MoS2-based nanocomposite exhibiting improved solar light induced photocurrent generation, both in solution and solid phases. The MoS2-CdS composite was synthesized via easy to achieve, cost effective, two-step solution process for the photocatalytic potassium dichromate [Cr(VI)] reduction and photocurrent generation in thin-film optoelectronic devices. Incorporating 10 wt% MoS2 into the composite increased the degradation efficiency of CdS from 43.9 to 91.9%. Furthermore, the MoS2-CdS composite demonstrated a 2.88-fold increase in the degradation rate constant and a 2.15% enhancement in the apparent quantum yield compared to controlled CdS. Additionally, the electrical power consumption per order decrease in Cr(VI) reduced from 25.74 kWh m−3 for controlled CdS to 9 kWh m−3 aimed at 10 wt% MoS2-CdS composite, indicating optimal synergy between the counterparts of MoS2-CdS in its composite. The resulting thin-film device with 10 wt% MoS2-CdS exhibited robust photocurrent generation and nonlinear I–V characteristics under solar illumination, attributed to the unique electronic properties of the MoS2-CdS heterojunction, influencing carrier transport via band alignment and interface carrier trapping effects. Moreover, photocurrent generation increased linearly with illumination intensity, and dynamic photo response studies revealed rapid photocurrent generation under illumination. Furthermore, the optoelectronic key parameters of CdS, including photosensitivity, photoresponsivity, and detectivity, were enhanced by factors of 5.09, 3.33, and 12.3, respectively, upon composite formation with MoS2. This study offers novel insights into developing high-performance and cost-effective bimetallic sulfide photocatalysts for efficient solar light-induced photocurrent generation in both solution and solid phases.

Introduction

Water pollution containing heavy metals poses an especially severe hazard to humanoid health and welfare1. Chromium (Cr), a prevalent heavy metal in wastewater, is categorized into two oxidation states: Cr(VI) and Cr(III)2,3. Hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)], known as a highly hazardous metallic contaminant, is discharged into the surroundings through diverse industrial activities including dyeing, leather tanning, electroplating etc.4–6. The increase of Cr(VI) via the food chain poses significant risks to human well-being, attributed to its mutagenic and carcinogenic effect7,8. It poses a risk nearly a thousand times greater to human health compared to Cr(III)1. The World Health Organization (WHO) has established 50 parts per billion (ppb) as the highest permissible concentration of Cr(VI) in drinking water9. Several methods, like photoreduction10, electrochemical treatment11,12, bioreduction13, flotation14, adsorption in porous MOF15 etc. have been developed for Cr(VI) elimination or reducing Cr(VI) toxicity in the wastewater from industry and to convert it into a less harmful form, Cr(III). Among these methods, photocatalytic reduction also regarded as an environmentally friendly, cost effective, and sustainable strategy, making use of renewable solar energy for addressing Cr(VI) pollution16,17. The key factor for effectively reducing by a photocatalyst is the efficient generation of electrons under illumination. To date, several oxide, sulfide and heterojunction semiconducting photocatalysts have been developed to maximize the harnessing of solar energy and efficiently reduce Cr(VI)18–23. Although these photocatalysts show potential, metal oxides typically have wide bandgaps, which limit their response to UV light. Additionally, the high recombination rates of photoinduced charge carriers result in the inefficient utilization of photogenerated electrons under solar illumination, thus impacting the reduction of Cr(VI).

Cadmium sulphide (CdS) has recently attracted significant research attention for photocatalytic and optoelectronic applications due to its narrower bandgap that aligns well with the visible spectrum of solar light24,25. It is extensively studied as a photocatalyst for toxic Cr(VI) reduction26. However, the practical application of CdS photocatalysts remains constrained by the fast recombination probability of the photo induced electron–hole (e–h) pairs27. Recently, the attachment of CdS with co-catalysts has demonstrated effectiveness in promoting charge separation, lowering overpotentials, enhancing active sites, and mitigating photo corrosion28. This could be attributed to the synergistic effects of various factors, such as enhancing active sites, suppressing charge recombination and reverse reactions, and reducing activation energy29. Heterostructures combining CdS and MoS2 (Molybdenum disulfide) explores an area with limited research, and only a few researchers have investigated the role of MoS2 cocatalysts in photocatalytic hydrogen evolution and dye degradation30–33. The photocatalytic reduction of Cr(VI) using MoS2-CdS composite materials has not been thoroughly explored. The synergy of MoS2 and CdS could exhibit promising potential for creating the MoS2-CdS composite as an effective optoelectronic material for reducing Cr(VI). Additionally, the photocurrent generation in MoS2-CdS composite thin film devices has yet to be investigated.

In this study, a simple low-cost, solution processable, easy to achieve, solvothermal process was utilized to synthesis the MoS2-CdS composite for Cr(VI) photoreduction and to study the photocurrent generation in thin film devices. The optimum ratio of MoS2 and CdS in the composite was determined to achieve the highest efficiency of the composite. The apparent quantum yield was rigorously estimated, providing an enhanced understanding of photocatalyst efficiency. Additionally, the energy efficiency of the photocatalytic degradation process was assessed through the calculation of electrical energy per order. The underlying reasons for the enhancement of degradation efficiency of CdS after the attachment of MoS2 have also been elucidated. Furthermore, key optoelectronic parameters, such as photosensitivity, photoresponsivity, and detectivity, were extensively evaluated in thin film devices fabricated with the MoS2-CdS composite. These advancements offer valuable insights into developing high-performance, cost-effective, and solution-processable materials for solar light-induced optoelectronic devices. This study may offer novel insights into a simple and cost-effective strategy for reducing Cr(VI) and provide a deep understanding of the photocurrent generation in MoS2-based composite materials.

Experimental section

All analytical grade chemicals are used as received. Sodium molybdate dihydrate (Na2MoO4·2H2O), thiourea [NH2CSNH2], cadmium acetate dihydrate [Cd(CH3COO)2·2H2O] and potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7)were received from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethylenediamine [EN], from Merck.

Synthesis of layered MoS2

At first bulk MoS2 was prepared by reacting Na2MoO4·2H2O with NH2CSNH2 by solvothermal method. At first, 0.5 g Na2MoO4·2H2O and 0.94 g NH2CSNH2 were dissolved in 40 ml double distilled water (DDW) by 30 min stirring. Then the mixture was transferred into a 50 mL autoclave. Then the sealed autoclave loaded with the mixture solution was placed in a preheated (200 °C) oven for 24 h. Following this, the precipitate of MoS2 was gathered via centrifugation and collected after washed repeatedly with DDW and ethanol.

Finally black dust sample was collected by drying the washed product at 60 °C for 12 h. Few-layered MoS2 obtained via a liquid exfoliation by using ultrasonication process. In detail, 1.5 g of synthesized MoS2 was dispersed into 30 ml DDW and left for ultrasonication for 10 h by using probe sonicator. Finally, the exfoliated solution dried at 80 °C to obtain the few-layered MoS2.

Synthesis of layered MoS2-CdS composite

1 mM of Cd(CH3COO)·2H2O and 3 mM of thiourea were dissolved in 30 ml mixture solution of EN and DDW (2:1 ratio v/v). The above mixture was kept under vigorous stirring for 15 min to dissolve properly so that it will form a homogeneous mixture at room temperature. After that previously prepared layered MoS2 solution added to the mixture and stirred for another 15 min. This homogeneous mixture was loaded to a stainless-steel autoclave and heated up to 170 °C for 12 h in a preheated oven. The autoclave was left to cool to room temperature after the reaction. The sediment of the synthesized product was centrifuged with DDW for several times at 8000 rpm to remove the un-reactant components. After being thoroughly washed the sample was dried for 6 h at 60 °C to get the powder form of the MoS2-CdS for characterization and experiments. By varying the quantity of as prepared layered MoS2, three sets of MoS2-CdS composites were synthesized. The calculated MoS2 mass ratios of 5, 10 and 15 wt% in the composites were signified by 5 wt% MoS2-CdS, 10 wt% MoS2-CdS (written as MoS2-CdS in the text) and 15 wt% MoS2-CdS respectively. Materials characterization

To ascertain the crystalline arrangements of the synthesized materials Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded by using a rigaku-miniflex-II (operating at 40 kV and 15 mA) with Cu Kα radiation. A JEOL JEM-2100F operated at 200 kV was employed for TEM (transmission electron microscope) and HRTEM (high-resolution TEM imaging and to record selected area electron diffraction pattern (SAED). A Renishaw inVia Raman Microscope equipped with a laser source having excitation wavelength at 532 nm was utilized to record Raman spectra. The absorption spectra of CdS and MoS2-CdS composite in the UV–Vis range were recorded by an Agilent Cary series. A Perkin Elmer LS 55 photoluminescence (PL) spectrometer was employed to record the steady state emission spectra with 450 nm excitation wavelength. The N2 adsorption isotherms were assessed using a Quantachrome Autosorb iQ-MPx analyzer. The multi-point BET technique was employed to determine the surface area, while the pore diameter and the pore volume were calculated using the BJH formula.

Photocatalytic experiment

The photocatalytic experiment involved utilizing synthesized photocatalysts to reduce toxicity of Cr(VI). The experimental setup consisted of a glass photoreactor connected to a cold-water bath to ensure a constant temperature throughout the experiment. 30 mg of photocatalyst was dissolved in a 60 mL aqueous solution that contains 3.2 mg of K2Cr2O7. The entire setup was conditioned in darkness for 30 min before initiating the photocatalytic reaction to achieve equilibrium of adsorption–desorption process among the catalysts and the hazardous chemicals. The adsorption of Cr(VI) using the MoS2-CdS composite was conducted entirely in darkness for the entire 100-min duration of the experiment. Additionally, the self-degradation of Cr(VI) without the catalyst was examined during the illumination period. An optical power meter (Newport 843-R) was utilized to measure the intensity of the illumination source. A solar-light simulator served as illuminating source of light. The photocatalytic experiment commenced upon turning on the light, and every ten min, 5 mL of solution were extracted from the reactor and centrifuged for 3 min to remove the catalyst residue. The concentrations of Cr(VI) ions in suspension were determined through UV–Vis spectral analysis.

Optoelectronic measurement

A device for generating photocurrent in a MoS2-CdS composite under simulated solar irradiation was fabricated on an ITO-coated glass substrate having a very thin channel measuring 60 μm × 1 cm on it. A thin layer of MoS2-CdS was formed by drop casting the aqueous dispersion of MoS2-CdS onto the channel. Two electrodes were then positioned across the channel, which was subsequently connected to an electrometer (Keithley 2611A) for measuring photocurrent under simulated solar light irradiation (utilizing a solar-light simulator, Newport, Oriel) at room temperature.

Light-assisted catalytic reactions predominantly occur on the catalyst’s surface hence the analysis of the surface area of the catalyst is highly imperative36,37. The BET surface area and pore size distribution of MoS2, CdS, and MoS2-CdS nano-composite were determined via N2-adsorption–desorption analysis38. Figure 2C presents the adsorption–desorption isotherms and BJH distributions of pore sizes for all examined samples. Notably, MoS2, CdS, and MoS2-CdS exhibit BET specific surface areas of 13.13, 16.9, and 51.76 m2 g−1, respectively. As illustrated in inset of Fig. 2C, the average pore size of the BJH specimen measures as 1.53 nm for the MoS2-CdS composite. This observation provides a plausible rationale for the promotion of active site expansion and specific surface area facilitated by heterojunction development, thereby having possibility of enhancing photocatalytic performance. Indeed, the indispensability of adsorption in initiating photocatalytic processes underscores its critical role. Consequently, the current trend in photo-catalyst synthesis emphasizes the augmentation of specific surface area.

The light harvesting efficacy of as-synthesized CdS and MoS2-CdS composite were examined by analysing the absorption spectra in the UV–Vis range (Fig. S1B, of SI). As shown from the figure controlled CdS has two discrete absorbance peaks at 258 nm and at 494 nm respectively. The bandgap energy for CdS obtained as 2.4 eV from (αhν)2 vs. hν plot (Tauc plot)41,42 (inset of Fig. S1B, of SI) where α, h and ν represents the absorbance coefficient, Planck constant, and frequency of light, respectively. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of any optical material provide a comprehensive insight into the energy band structure and inherent flaws within the samples. Additionally, they reveal intricate details about the various electronic states that a material can exhibit, particularly in relation to doping schemes, and growth conditions. The room temperature PL spectra of the CdS and MoS2-CdS are presented in Fig. S1C, of SI. The PL spectra of CdS nanorods illustrates a prominent band edge peak at approximately 516 nm, indicative of either the inter-band transfer of charge carriers or the recombination of bound electron -hole pairs.

The peak detected at 619 nm is associated with the internal defects resulting of the interplay of cadmium vacancies or interstitials, and sulphur vacancies45,46. Whereas, for MoS2-CdS, the peak intensity significantly diminishes due to the diverse interface interactions between CdS and MoS2, indicating an effective suppression of charge carrier recombination induced by light. These findings suggest that MoS2-CdS nanohybrid serves as a promising visible light harvester. When exposed to light, CdS nanocrystals absorb photons, initiating the generation of electron–hole pairs. MoS2, in turn, can capture photoexcited holes upon interaction, leading to enhanced photocatalytic activity. This mechanism facilitates increased photocatalytic efficiency by mitigating the recombination probability of photo induced carriers within the CdS nanoparticles.

To achieve adsorption–desorption equilibrium, the necessary amount of photocatalysts was added to the toxic Cr(VI) solution under stirring condition in darkness for 30 min. Subsequently, a photocatalysis experiment was conducted for 70 min under light exposure. The adsorption behaviour of the MoS2-CdS in the dark and the self-degradation of the Cr(VI) under light exposure were investigated and are depicted in Fig. S2A and S2B of SI, respectively. In the presence of MoS2-CdS composite, a slight change in Cr(VI) concentration was observed (see Fig. S2A in SI) when light was absent, likely due to adsorption on the catalysts' surface. Conversely, there was no significant alteration in Cr(VI) concentration in the absence of photocatalysts during the 100 min light exposure period (refer to Fig. S2B of the SI), indicating that self-photo-reduction of Cr(VI) did not occur within our experimental timeframe. The quantum yield serves as a pivotal parameter for evaluating the efficiency of a photocatalyst. In our approach, we assessed this yield by quantifying incident photons using an optical power meter. However, accurately measuring the photons absorbed by a photocatalyst within a dispersed system poses challenges due to scattering effects. Alternatively, the quantum yield of the photocatalytic reaction can be defined as the count of polluted molecules, Cr(VI) (in the present case) degraded per absorbed photon. However, photons in this context aren’t solely absorbed but also scattered or reflected by suspended particles in the photoreactor. To address this, the absorbed photon flux is replaced with the incident photon flux, representing the photons reaching the reactor surface. Consequently, apparent quantum yield (AQY) is computed instead of the quantum yield, with its calculation outlined in the equation below51–53:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AQY = \frac{{{\text{number}}\,{\text{of}}\,{\text{reacted}}\,{\text{molecules }}}}{{{\text{number}}\,{\text{of}}\,{\text{incident}}\,{\text{photons}}}} \times 100\%$$\end{document}AQY=numberofreactedmoleculesnumberofincidentphotons×100%

Figure 3C compares the recorded AQY for MoS2, CdS, and the MoS2-CdS composite as 0.08, 0.20, and 0.43%, respectively towards photo induced reduction of Cr(VI) in K2Cr2O7. This suggests an increase in the AQY of CdS by 2.15 times upon amalgamation with MoS2. Nevertheless, it's crucial to acknowledge that the AQY is notably lower than the true quantum yield (QY), given that only a portion of incident light photons is absorbed by the photocatalyst.

In other words, it quantifies how the combined effect of multiple catalysts working together is greater than the sum of their individual effects. This synergy can arise from various factors such as improved electron transfer, increased active site accessibility, or cooperative effects in catalytic reactions. The synergy factor is often used to assess the efficiency and effectiveness of catalyst combinations in catalytic processes. The remarkable efficiency in separating e–h pairs within the composite, attributed to electron transfer from the conduction band (EC) of light-activated CdS to that of MoS2, likely underpins the superior photocatalytic activity of the CdS composite. The proposed reaction mechanisms are elucidated in Fig. 5. The relative positions of the valency band (EV), conduction band (EC), and Fermi level (EF) of CdS and MoS2 are depicted in Fig. 5. In this illustration, the EC of MoS2 is situated below that of CdS, while the EV of MoS2 is positioned above that of CdS (left in Fig. 5). Upon the attachment of CdS with MoS2, a built-in potential is established within the composite56, with the direction from MoS2 to CdS (Middle in Fig. 5). When exposed to simulated solar light, excitons form in the attached CdS, subsequently dissociating into free charge carriers, electrons, and holes. Moreover, a small quantity of electrons and holes is also generated in MoS2 under illumination due to the presence of a small amount of MoS2 in the composite. However, these photogenerated charges in CdS and MoS2 tend to recombine, thereby diminishing the efficiency. Notably, photogenerated electrons in CdS swiftly transfer from the EC of CdS to the EC of MoS2 due to favorable band positions. Considering that the EV position of CdS is higher than that of MoS2, and this favorable EV position facilitates the movement of holes from CdS to MoS2 also. Consequently, there is a possibility of charge carrier recombination in MoS2 as both electrons and holes accumulate in MoS2 from CdS and its own photo generated e–h pairs. Nevertheless, the built-in potential developed in the composite hinders the migration of holes from CdS to MoS2. Accordingly, electrons transfer from CdS to MoS2, leaving the holes in CdS, as illustrated in Fig. 5 (right). Examining the durability of catalysts is essential for any photocatalytic process. To assess this, the recyclability of the synthesized MoS2-CdS catalyst was tested over four cycles in photocatalytic reduction reactions involving aqueous Cr(VI) (see Fig. S5A of SI). Remarkably, no discernible change in the photocatalytic performance of MoS2-CdS was observed, indicating its long-lasting nature as a photocatalyst for Cr(VI) reduction. Furthermore, the XRD pattern of the material remained unchanged throughout the photocatalytic reduction process (see Fig. S5B of SI). This stability implies that MoS2-CdS is highly resistant to corrosion during Cr(VI) reduction, suggesting promising applications in the treatment of contaminated water.

Photocurrent generation in thin film device

Figure 6A depicts a cartoon representation of the thin film optoelectronic device, alongside the electrical measurement setup. We have prepared MoS2-CdS and controlled CdS thin film device for photocurrent measurements. Each device underwent measurements under uniform experimental conditions, including bias voltage sweeping from 0 to + 2 V, then reverse sweeping from + 2 V to 0 V, followed by another sweep from 0 to − 2 V, and back to 0 V, conducted in both darkness and light illumination (at 100 mW/cm2). The I–V characteristics of MoS2-CdS optoelectronic devices are presented in Fig. 6B. Both the dark current and photocurrent shows nonlinear I–V characteristics both in forward and reverse bias. The nonlinear features in I–V curves can be attributed to the unique electronic properties of the heterojunction formed among MoS2 and CdS, where carrier transport is influenced by band alignment and carrier trapping effects at the interface57,58. The development of heterojunction among MoS2 and CdS creates an interface where charge carriers (electrons and holes) are spatially separated more efficiently, leading to enhanced photocurrent generation. Similar nonlinear I–V characteristics also observed in MoS2/p-Si junction59. As depicted in Fig. 6B, a hysteresis loop akin to I–V characteristics is evident in the MoS2-CdS device under both dark and light conditions. Similar hysteresis type I–V characteristic was observed in polythiophene based electronic devices60. Yow-Jon Lin reported hysteresis-type I–V properties in (ITO)-Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) based devices 61. Under dark condition when the biased voltage is increased from 0 to + 2 V, thermally activated free carriers (at room temperature) present in the MoS2-CdS matrix and some of them may get trapped at defects or impurities like sulphur or molybdenum vacancies, oxygen contamination etc. present in the MoS2-CdS. When the bias voltage is reversed from + 2 V to 0 V, the trapped charges may not be immediately released (de-trapped), leading to a delay in the decrease of conductivity. Similarly, when the bias voltage is further decreased to − 2 V and then back to 0 V, the de-trapping process may still be ongoing, resulting in a delay in the increase of conductivity. This trapping and de-trapping of charge carriers contribute to the hysteresis-like behaviour observed in the I–V characteristic. In presence of light, in addition to the thermally generated carriers, photon absorption can generate additional charge carriers affecting the conductivity of the photoactive materials. The dynamics of charge generation, recombination, and trapping/de-trapping processes under varying bias voltages and light conditions can further exacerbate the hysteresis-like behaviour in the I–V curve. But in the present case no dominating hysteresis-like behaviour is observed under illuminating condition. Conclusion

In summary, the present study outlines a two-step, cost-effective solvothermal synthesis method to produce a solution-processable MoS2-CdS heterojunction nanocomposite. This composite serves as a promising electronic material for the photocatalytic Cr(VI) reduction as well as for the development of thin film photodetector device under sunlight exposure. The built-in potential created within the heterojunction effectively separates photoinduced charges, thus reducing recombination probabilities within the composite. Highest synergy was achieved with the 10 wt% MoS2-CdS composite, exhibiting a 2.09-fold increase in degradation efficiency and a 2.88-fold increase in degradation rate constants compared to controlled CdS. Additionally, the apparent quantum yield improved by 2.15 times, while the electrical power consumption reduced by 2.86 times in the MoS2-CdS composite compared to controlled CdS. This investigation underscores the pivotal role of the heterojunction in modulating charge carrier transport, separation, and recombination mechanisms, consequently leading to improved photocatalytic performance and enhanced photocurrent generation. The MoS2-CdS composite thin-film devices results in robust photocurrent generation and nonlinear I–V characteristics, driven by their unique electronic properties and heterojunction effects. Additionally, enhanced photocurrent generation with increasing illumination intensity and rapid dynamic photo response underscore the potential for practical applications. Moreover, the significant enhancement in optoelectronic key parameters photosensitivity, photoresponsivity, and detectivity of CdS increased 5.09, 3.33, and 12.3, times respectively after the formation of composite with MoS2 highlights the promise of bimetallic sulfide photocatalysts for efficient solar light-induced photocurrent generation in optoelectronic devising.
