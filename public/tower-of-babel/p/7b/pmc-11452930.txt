Effects of frailty on patients with oral and maxillofacial space infection: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Shiyuan Liu, Heli Shen, Xiaoge Zhang, Wei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate the implications of frailty as a predictive factor for outcomes among patients with oral and maxillofacial space infection.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted to analyze 348 medical records, gathering data on several key aspects. These included the etiology of infection, the location of inflamed areas, the treatment administered, and the ultimate treatment outcomes. Additionally, the study collected information on the Symptom Severity (SS) score, frailty score, age, gender, the presence of systemic diseases, alcohol consumption, and smoking history.ResultsA total of 155 patients were classified as frailty, while 193 patients were classified as non-frailty. We found a significantly different in age, BMI, hospitalization expenses, length of hospital stay, SS, fibrinogen and admission to ICU between the frail group and the non- frail group.ConclusionsFrailty serves as a valuable predictor of outcomes among patients with oral and maxillofacial space infections. By identifying high-risk patients, frailty can be employed as a clinical tool to guide perioperative care, ultimately optimizing patient outcomes. Notably, frail patients often require more ICU treatment.

Background

Oral and maxillofacial space infection is a common disease in dentistry. Its characteristics are acute onset, rapid progression, and often involving multiple space. When unable to receive timely and effective treatment, it can lead to adverse outcomes [1]. With the progress of population aging, the incidence rate of oral and maxillofacial space infection has gradually increased [2]. Improper treatment can lead to rapid disease progression and ultimately result in patient death. In recent years, expert consensus has been proposed to standardize related treatments [3]. The consensus provides detailed explanations on auxiliary examinations, timing of medication treatment, and timing of surgery. However, there is a lack of evaluation and analysis of the overall condition of patients, including physiological and psychological changes related to aging.

Frailty manifests as a decline in organ function and a decrease in tolerance. It is a comprehensive physiological and psychological state. In medical activities, frailty is a disadvantageous factor that can lead to adverse outcomes. With the intensification of population aging, frailty has become an undeniable health burden [4, 5]. Frailty can affect anyone, but it is especially prevalent among those with chronic illnesses. frailty can be divided into “aging-related frailty (ARF)” and “non-aging-related frailty (NARF)” [6]. The ARF is more commonly employed, and it is generally deemed as frailty if an individual is over 60 years old and reliant on the assistance of others for daily activities [7]. However, this method of classification lacks precision, prompting the proposal of numerous evaluation scales for assessment purposes.

The Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) is a subjective frailty assessment tool that examines distinct domains such as comorbidity, functionality, and cognition [8]. The Brief Multidimensional Prognostic Index (Brief MPI) based on comprehensive geriatric assessment has been proven to predict mortality rates in patients [9]. It has found widespread application across various settings. But these two models necessitate additional patient evaluation, which hinders rapid implementation. It is difficult to apply risk decisions before conducting operations. The preoperative modified frailty index is evaluated based on biochemical indicators such as albumin and creatinine. It can evaluate the occurrence of postoperative complications [10].

Although frailty index has been used to evaluate various surgical related treatment decisions. However, there is still a lack of research on oral and maxillofacial space infections. We conducted a retrospective study on patients with oral and maxillofacial space infections using 5-factor related frailty indicators to discover the potential role of frailty indicators in disease treatment [11, 12].

Methods.

From January 2021 to December 2023, we conducted a retrospective review of 348 patients who were admitted to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery for the management of maxillofacial space infections. The inclusion criteria of this study were as follows: Adhere to the following OMSI diagnostic criteria: Local tissue inflammation characterized by erythema, edema, hyperthermia and tenderness. Compromised functions such as difficulties in mouth opening, swallowing, or breathing. Puncture or CT scan confirms the presence of purulent material. Laboratory examinations point towards an underlying infectious process [3]. The exclusion criteria of this study were as follows: clinical data missing. The following data were extracted from medical records: age, gender, smoking history, alcohol consumption, presence of systemic diseases, the number of infected spaces, length of hospital stay, hospitalization expenses, the source of infection, and the type of treatment administered.

For each patient, we documented their score on the new five-factor index [13], which assigns one point for each of the five comorbid variables. These variables include diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or current pneumonia, hypertension requiring medication, and non-independent functional status. According to the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, non-independent functional status refers to the need for assistance in any activities of daily living, such as bathing, feeding, dressing, and mobility. Patients were scored as either 0 (not meeting the frailty criteria) or 1 (meeting the frailty criteria) for each of these items. The scores for the five tasks were then summed. Patients with a total score of 0 were categorized as non-frail, while those with a score above 1 were classified as frail [11]. Utilizing a quintet of indices, we stratified the cohort of frail patients into a scoring system ranging from 0 to 5 points. The Symptom Severity (SS) score of odontogenic infection was used in this research [14].

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.22 (IBM) and GraphPad Prism V.8.0. Continuous variables (age, BMI, hospitalization expenses, length of hospital stay, SS, fibrinogen, White cell count, Neutrophils cell count, Lymphocyte cell count, Monocytes cell count, Albumin, C reactive protein) are reported as means and standard deviations. Categorical variables (admission to ICU, gender, smoking history, alcohol consumption) are reported as percentages and frequencies. For continuous variables, an unpaired t test or Welch’s t test were used, while the Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables. Utilizing Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to assess the presence of statistically significant differences among multiple groups. By employing Pearson correlation analysis, the relationships between variables are evaluated. Linear regression analysis is conducted with the length of hospital stay as the dependent variable and the continuous variables as independent variables to assess the relative risk associated with each factor. A p-value below 0.05 is considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 348 patients participated in this study. A total of 155 patients were classified as frail based on a score above 1, indicating the presence of frailty characteristics, while 193 patients with a score of 0 were categorized as non-frail, indicating the absence of frailty-related factors. All treatments are completed by the same group of doctors. The treatment protocols for complications associated with frailty are formulated by pertinent departments, and this research does not delve into the progression of such complications. Dental infections are the most common cause of infection. Most patients have accumulated multiple fascial spaces in infection. Patients in both groups were not significantly different in cause and space of infection (Table 1).

Discussion

With the advancement of economy and technology, population aging is an important public safety event faced by countries around the world. Physical functions gradually decline with age [15]. Correspondingly, the incidence of various diseases and the incidence of poor prognosis also increase accordingly. Frailty is a complex and multidimensional biopsychosocial syndrome that occurs with age, characterized by a decline in physiological reserve and multi organ system function [5, 16, 17]. In departments such as thoracic surgery, orthopedics, and general surgery, frailty indicators can be used to evaluate the prognosis of patients and guide clinical physicians in making more reliable clinical decisions [11, 18–20]. The multidimensional evaluation model based on the CFS scale has been widely applied, and expert consensus believes that it has good practical value [21]. However, those models are quite complex and requires extensive training for personnel to obtain frailty data. In clinical emergency management, we need a rapid evaluation method even more urgently [22–24].

A frailty index that can be evaluated solely through medical history and physical examination would be a more useful tool for clinicians and researchers. The 5-item frailty index is developed from the 11-item frailty index and has been studied to have the same clinical efficacy [13, 15, 25]. The 5-item fractional index can reduce clinical evaluation time and has great value for emergency patients.

With the increase in the elderly population, the incidence of maxillofacial space infections is gradually increasing. Meanwhile, advanced age is also a related factor for adverse outcomes [26]. The treatment of maxillofacial space infection requires the involvement of various treatment methods, including surgery, the use of antibiotics, and the control of underlying diseases. We need multiple examination results and clinical manifestations to make appropriate clinical decisions. We introduced 5 frailty indices to evaluate the admission status of patients with maxillofacial space infections.

Previous studies have shown that age, inflammatory markers, and underlying diseases are all factors that affect adverse outcomes in patients with maxillofacial space infections [27–29]. We need to integrate these results to evaluate the patient’s prognosis and develop appropriate treatment plans. Due to the rapid progression of the disease, delayed treatment can further lead to adverse outcomes. We used frailty indicators and SS scores to integrate clinical data for rapid evaluation. Our results confirm that frailty indicators can significantly distinguish patient populations. Patients rated as frail often have longer hospital stays and higher expenses. This indicates a poor healing process for the patient. Frail individuals experience more severe systemic inflammatory reactions, as indicated by the difference between blood routine and CRP. The decrease in albumin suggests that frail patients require higher nutritional support, which is extremely important for their recovery [28, 30]. The elevation of fibrinogen represents a more dangerous hypercoagulable state in frail patients, and it requires appropriate anticoagulant therapy intervention to prevent thrombosis. At the same time, this increase also indirectly reflects the severity of the infection [31].

The SS scores is used to assess the severity of patients with maxillofacial space infection. It is a factor that affects the prognosis of patients [32]. The main advantage of SS scoring is that it can quickly assess patients’ inflammatory conditions and improve clinical response speed. We used SS scores for evaluation in our study and achieved similar research results. Further confirmed the application value of SS score in clinical practice. We found that frail patients have higher SS scores. This suggests that we need to focus on these patients to avoid adverse outcomes.

The length of hospital stay is an important indicator for evaluating the overall treatment outcome of patients. We found that age, SS score, incision, frailty score, and ICU treatment are influencing factors for prolonging hospitalization days. Among them, the incision, ICU treatment, and frailty score have a significant impact coefficient [33]. This suggests that frailty score may play a role in predicting the length of hospital stay for patients. However, further research is needed to elaborate on this effect to generate a consensus on diagnosis and treatment that can guide clinical practice.

After subdividing the frailty score, we observed that as the score escalated, hospitalization days, hospitalization expenses, CRP, fibrinogen, among others, exhibited an upward trend, whereas albumin levels demonstrated a downward trend. This phenomenon underscores the detrimental effect of an elevated frailty score on clinical prognosis, suggesting that patient treatment may necessitate longer durations and entail heightened risks [11]. Consequently, the need for ICU treatment has intensified further. The insufficient sample size and the limited number of patients with high frailty scores are issues that need to be addressed in future research, and data collected from multicenter studies can help alleviate this deficiency.

ICU is an important part of the treatment process for patients with maxillofacial space infections. When patients experience breathing difficulties, swallowing difficulties, sepsis, coma, etc., ICU treatment will be considered. In our study, 67 patients received ICU treatment. They have more severe hypoalbuminemia and inflammatory reactions. This is the reason why they need ICU treatment. In our research, we observed no significant different in laboratory data between frailty and non-frailty individuals within the ICU patient cohort. Nonetheless, frail patients tend to have extended hospitalization periods and increased costs, which points to a diminished capacity for recovery from illness. This is related to their more underlying diseases, higher age, and inability to take care of themselves. Given the rapid progression of OSMI, it is crucial to take this into account when making clinical decisions. Prompt initiation of ICU intervention for frail patients can significantly enhance the safety of their medical care.

We evaluated the role of frailty indicators among patients aged 60 and above. The duration and cost of hospitalization remain the most crucial indicators. Additionally, various biochemical markers also exhibited notable differences. We contend that the new five-factor frailty index is not solely reliant on age for frailty assessment [34]. Rather, it belongs to the NARF, which emphasis on predicting surgical-related prognosis indicators. Thus, its application in predicting outcomes for emergency surgeries merits promotion.

In our study, there were no significant differences in the source of infection, affected intervals, smoking history, alcoholism history and gender among different groups. This indicates the universality of frailty indicators among patient populations from different sources. We can quickly identify critically ill patients by using frailty index scores. We only need simple training to fully master this scoring system. By using a scoring system in the emergency department, diagnosis and treatment plans can be quickly formulated. This can effectively reduce the burden of emergency operations.

Conclusions

In summary, frailty emerged as a significant risk factor for oral maxillofacial space infection. The frailty index serves as a predictive tool for assessing outcomes in these patients. Notably, the frail group incurred longer hospital stays and higher hospitalization expenses compared to the non-frail group. ICU treatment is a necessary consideration for frail patients. Our research can provide some reference for rapid clinical decision-making.
