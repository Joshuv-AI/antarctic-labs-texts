MD2NMR: Linking molecular dynamics with NMR relaxation
Authors: Houfang Zhang, Tiejun Wei, Anna R Panchenko, Catherine A Musselman, Yunhui Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxation parameters from molecular dynamics (MD) simulation trajectories is essential for quantitatively linking atomistic-scale motions to experimental observables. Here, we introduce MD2NMR, an open-source Python framework for calculating longitudinal (R1) and transverse (R2) relaxation rates, as well as rotational correlation times (τc), directly from atomistic MD trajectories. The framework implements efficient algorithms for evaluating the internuclear vector time correlation functions, spectral density functions, and their frequency-dependent components, with explicit treatment of both global and internal motions. MD2NMR supports multiple trajectory formats and provides user-defined analysis parameters, ensuring broad applicability and ease of use. Benchmarking against ubiquitin, GB1, GB3, and histone H3/H4 tails shows that MD2NMR reproduces experimental relaxation behavior with good accuracy, achieving Pearson correlation coefficients of up to 0.89 with a root-mean-squared error (RMSE) of 0.75 s−1 for well-characterized small systems and up to 0.79 with an RMSE of 6.08 ns for histone H3 tail for rotational correlation times, while maintaining computational efficiency. By combining rigorous theoretical foundations with practical flexibility, MD2NMR establishes a robust platform for validating MD simulations that is extensible to diverse biomolecular systems. MD2NMR is freely available as an open-source Python package with documentation and examples provided.

Significance

Bridging molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxation measurements is essential for validating force fields and accurately characterizing biomolecular motions, especially in flexible or intrinsically disordered regions. However, MD-derived relaxation parameters are highly sensitive to analysis choices such as the selection of correlation-function fitting models and simulation conditions, limiting reproducibility and confidence in MD-NMR comparisons. We present MD2NMR, an open-source framework that computes NMR relaxation rates directly from the MD trajectories, and in this study we systematically assess how methodological choices influence the resulting relaxation parameters. Benchmarking on ubiquitin, GB1, GB3, and the histone H3/H4 tails identifies the key determinants of reliable relaxation parameter calculations and achieve reasonable agreement with experimental data. MD2NMR provides a robust platform for validating MD simulations and is extensible to diverse biomolecular systems.

Introduction

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have become an indispensable approach for probing the structural and dynamical properties of biomolecules at atomic resolution.1,2 When combined with experimental data, MD simulations enable rigorous, quantitative validation of molecular models and provide mechanistic interpretations of experimental observables. Yet a major barrier to broader adoption lies in connecting raw MD trajectories with experimentally measurable observables in a reproducible, standardized, and user-accessible manner, particularly for observables that depend on motions occurring over a wide range of timescales.

Solution nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is uniquely suited to bridge this gap, providing atomistic-level characterization of biomolecular structure and dynamics in solution.3,4 In particular, NMR residue-specific spin relaxation rates, longitudinal (R1) and transverse (R2), are sensitive to both global rotational diffusion and internal motions. A commonly used model-free formalism enables computation of residue-specific parameters describing the magnitude and timescale of motions under the assumption that internal motions are decoupled from overall macromolecular tumbling.5 This framework is valid primarily when internal motions occur on timescales faster than global rotational diffusion and the overall tumbling is approximately isotropic.5 While the model-free framework remains widely used, it represents only one of several available strategies for interpreting relaxation data, and alternative computational approaches are increasingly being developed to overcome its limitations.6 In the model-free treatment, the key parameters are closely related to the spectral density function, J(ω), which is obtained from the Fourier transform of the time correlation function of internuclear dipole-dipole coupling.5,7,8 From these quantities, the effective rotational correlation time (τc) can be determined, providing direct insight into motional amplitudes and timescales. Such analyses are particularly valuable for highly flexible or intrinsically disordered regions, such as histone tails, whose conformational dynamics are inaccessible to static structural methods and therefore require an integrated MD-NMR framework.9,10,11,12,13,14

Computing NMR observables from raw MD trajectories is nontrivial. It involves precise evaluation of time correlation functions, separation of global and internal motions, and numerical integration over relevant frequency regimes.11,15,16 Several computational methods have been proposed to extract relaxation parameters from MD simulations, including ab initio predictions for proteins such as histone H4 tails.7,12,17 Nevertheless, existing implementations are often system specific, computationally cumbersome, or lack the flexibility needed to accommodate diverse biomolecular systems and simulation protocols.

Here, we introduce MD2NMR, a Python-based computational framework for calculating R1, R2, and τc directly from atomistic MD trajectories. The method implements efficient algorithms for computing dipole-dipole time correlation functions, spectral density functions, and relaxation rates, with rigorous treatment of rotational diffusion and internal motions. MD2NMR supports multiple trajectory formats, provides fine-grained control over analysis parameters, and is compatible with widely used MD packages. We validate the method by computing NMR relaxation parameters for three well-characterized benchmark systems, namely, ubiquitin, immunoglobulin-binding domains of protein G (GB1 and GB3), and intrinsically disordered histone H3 and H4 tails, and compare the results with experimental data, demonstrating good agreement and reproducibility.

Materials and methods

Framework of MD2NMR package

We developed a computational package, MD2NMR, to calculate NMR observables from MD trajectories using the spectral density function approach (Figure 1).12,15,18 In brief, the time autocorrelation function C(t), which describes relaxation due to dipole-dipole interactions between nuclei, is computed directly from the MD-generated atomic coordinates. For instance, the autocorrelation function of the backbone amide can be obtained in this manner. The spectral density function J(ω) is then obtained by transforming C(t) into the frequency domain using a fast Fourier transform.

Schematic overview of the MD2NMR workflow for calculating NMR observables from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Starting from MD trajectories, the time autocorrelation functions of relevant atomic vectors are computed and fitted with multiexponential models to derive spectral density functions. These functions are then used to calculate NMR relaxation parameters such as R1, R2, and τc, enabling direct comparison with experimental NMR data.

To account for the global tumbling, all MD frames are aligned to a reference structure, for instance using the Cα atoms. Subsequently, global rotational diffusion can be reintroduced analytically by multiplying the resulting C(t) with an exponential decay function, exp(−τ/τrot), where τrot represents the rotational correlation time of the overall molecular system. This value can be directly estimated from the MD trajectories using HYDROPRO, a tool that computes the hydrodynamic properties of rigid macromolecules.11,19 The MD trajectories are divided into multiple segments of equal simulation time lengths, and C(t) is calculated for each segment. The averaged C(t) across all segments is then fitted to a tri-exponential model to derive the corresponding spectral densities and compute NMR observables, including the longitudinal (R1) and transverse (R2) relaxation rates, as well as residue-specific rotational correlation times (τc). This method explicitly incorporates relevant physical parameters such as Larmor frequencies, gyromagnetic ratios, NH bond lengths, and chemical shift anisotropy.

Validation of the computational framework

To validate the accuracy and robustness of our computational framework, we applied MD2NMR to three small, well-characterized proteins (ubiquitin, GB1, and GB3) and to the more challenging task of characterizing the dynamics of the intrinsically disordered N-terminal tails of histones H3 and H4 in the context of nucleosome. In all systems, we computed residue-specific backbone 15N NMR observables.

We first calculated relaxation parameters for ubiquitin, GB1, and GB3. For each MD trajectory, the initial 200 ns were discarded to ensure equilibration, and the remaining 300 ns were used for analysis. Autocorrelation functions were computed directly from the trajectories without aligning structures to a reference conformation; therefore, overall tumbling was not removed. The resulting autocorrelation functions were fitted using a two-exponential model with a truncation time of τmax = 82 ns to obtain spectral density functions and the corresponding NMR relaxation parameters. A resonance frequency of 600 MHz was used for the calculations, and the results were compared with experimental data obtained at the same frequency. For these small proteins, we computed residue-specific backbone 15N R1 and R2 and compared them with experimental values reported previously.26,27,28

We next extended the validation to a more complex system: histone tails in the nucleosome. For the nucleosome, we computed R1 and R2 relaxation rates, as well as residue-specific rotational correlation times (τc), for the H3 and H4 N-terminal tails using MD2NMR. These analyses were performed on multiple published trajectories generated under varying conditions, including different simulation lengths, water models, and sampling intervals (Table S1),9,29 as well as on an additional trajectory set generated in this work (simulation set 3). Unless stated otherwise, we used three exponentials for fitting, τmax = 82 ns, and divided each trajectory into ∼180 ns segments for segment averaging. Resonance frequencies of 600 MHz, 800 MHz, and 850 MHz were used for the calculations to enable comparison with experimental data acquired at different frequencies.

Finally, we systematically assessed how methodological choices affected the calculated relaxation parameters for histone tails. Specifically, we evaluated the sensitivity of the computed R1 and R2 relaxation rates, as well as residue-specific τc, to the two main categories of parameters (Table S2). The first category encompasses parameters and options that influence the autocorrelation function C(t) and spectral density function J(ω) calculations within our computational pipeline. Specifically, these are (1) the τmax value used to separate fast and slow decay regimes in the autocorrelation function; (2) the treatment of global tumbling of the nucleosome, which can be removed from the trajectory and analytically reintroduced using an exponential decay function; (3) segment averaging, whereby the full trajectory is divided into short time segments and the resulting autocorrelation functions are averaged to improve the computational efficiency; and (4) the number of exponential terms (n_exp) used in fitting of the total autocorrelation function.

The second category includes parameters and options related to the MD simulation setup, namely the total trajectory length, the time interval between frames, and the water model used for simulation. A detailed overview of these parameters is provided in Table S2. The resulting relaxation parameters were then directly compared with experimentally measured R1 and R2 rates and τc values for the H3 and H4 tails (Table S3), thereby enabling us to benchmark the predictive accuracy of our framework under biologically relevant conditions.

MD simulations

The MD trajectories used to validate the MD2NMR package fall into two categories: (1) simulations of small proteins (ubiquitin, GB1, and GB3) and (2) simulations of nucleosome systems, comprising simulation sets 1–3 (Table S1).

Small proteins (ubiquitin, GB1, and GB3)

Starting structures were taken from the Protein Data Bank: ubiquitin (PDB: 1UBQ), GB1 (PDB: 1PGA), and GB3 (PDB: 1P7F).30,31,32 All simulations were performed with AMBER202433 using the AMBER ff14SB force field for proteins and the optimal point charge (OPC) water model.34 Each protein was solvated in a rectangular OPC water box with a minimum distance of 10 Å between any protein atom and the box edge. Na+/Cl− ion pairs were then added to reach a final salt concentration of 150 mM.

After solvation, each system was energy minimized using 20,000 steps of steepest descent followed by 20,000 steps of conjugate-gradient minimization. Systems were subsequently heated to 310 K in the NVT ensemble using Langevin dynamics with collision frequency γ = 1 ps−1, followed by 500 ps of equilibration in the NPT ensemble. Pressure was maintained at 1 atm using the Berendsen barostat. All bonds involving hydrogen were constrained with SHAKE, and a nonbonded cutoff of 10.0 Å was applied. For each protein, three independent production simulations were performed, each 500 ns in length.

Nucleosome systems

Simulation sets 1 and 2 were taken from previously published studies (Table S1).9,29 Simulation set 1 comprises a nucleosome containing 147-bp Widom 601 DNA with human histones, whereas simulation set 2 comprises a nucleosome with 187 bp of human native DNA and Xenopus laevis canonical core histones. Simulation set 3 was generated in this work. The starting model was constructed from the nucleosome structure (PDB: 3AFA,35 with the DNA sequence replaced by the KRAS DNA sequence. Missing residue coordinates of the histone tails were modelde by linearly extending. Simulations used AMBER ff14SB for proteins, OL15 for double-stranded DNA, and TIP4P-Ew water.36,37,38 Notably, the histone and DNA sequences differ across the three simulation sets, whereas the experimental datasets correspond to human histones in 147-bp Widom 601 nucleosome core particles. Among the simulated systems, simulation set 1 most closely matches the experimental conditions.

The nucleosome system was solvated in a TIP4P-Ew water box with a minimum distance of 20 Å between any nucleosome atom and the box edge. Na+/Cl− ion pairs were added to reach 150 mM. After solvation, the systems were energy minimized using steepest descent minimization protocol for 20,000 steps, followed by another 20,000 conjugate gradient minimizations. The system was then gradually heated to 310 K in the NVT ensemble using Langevin dynamics with collision frequency γ = 1 ps−1 and subsequently switched to the NPT ensemble for 500 ps to reach equilibrium.

To increase the simulation speed, we introduced a second phase in which we stripped the equilibrated system of water and resolvated it using the protocol described above, including the NVT and NPT equilibration steps. This step was intended to reduce the water-box size and the number of water molecules in the system, given that the initial structure contained extended histone tails and occupied a large water box, while histone tails collapsed on DNA after 200 ns. Specifically, we reduced the number of water molecules from approximately 550,000 to ∼150,000–180,000, which improved computational efficiency. The Berendsen barostat was used for constant pressure simulation at 1 atm. The SHAKE bond-length constraints were applied for hydrogen involving bonds, and the cutoff distance was set to 10.0 Å. Additionally, the production run was performed up to 1,500 ns with hydrogen mass repartitioning, which repartitions mass to increase the hydrogen mass to 3.024 u to stabilize the system on top of SHAKE and increase the time step of simulation to 4 fs.39,40 Three independent production runs were carried out to ensure statistical robustness.

Results

MD2NMR shows reasonable accuracy and predicts the NMR relaxation parameters

To evaluate the accuracy of MD2NMR for calculating the NMR relaxation parameters, we applied the framework to both well-characterized benchmark proteins (ubiquitin, GB1, and GB3) and the more complex nucleosome system and then systematically compared the computed results with corresponding experimental measurements.

For the small protein systems, we computed R1 and R2 relaxation rates and compared them with experimentally measured values reported in the literature.26,27,28 Across all three proteins, MD2NMR showed strong agreement with experiments (Table S4). For ubiquitin, the Pearson correlation coefficients were 0.86 (slope = 0.81; root-mean-squared error [RMSE] = 0.3 s−1) for R1 and 0.89 (slope = 1.09; RMSE = 0.75 s−1) for R2. GB1 and GB3 likewise showed strong correspondence between calculated and experimental relaxation parameters, with Pearson correlation coefficients up to 0.86 (slope = 2.3; RMSE = 1.27 s−1) and 0.74 (slope = 0.9; RMSE = 0.94 s−1), respectively (Figure 2).

Our predictions show good agreement with experimental data for the residue-specific τc. Namely, for histone H3 tails, the highest Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) reached 0.79, with an average PCC of 0.64 across tested simulations (Table S5). Simulation set 1, which closely mimics the experimental conditions in terms of DNA length, histone and DNA sequences, and ion concentrations, yielded the best correlation. Conversely, simulation sets 2 and 3 exhibited weaker agreement with experiments, likely due to differences in simulation parameters and coarser temporal sampling. For histone H4 tails, simulation set 2 provided the highest correlation with experimental τc values (PCC = 0.70, Figure S1 and Table S5), although the computed τc tended to be systematically overestimated. We also directly compared the computed R1 and R2 values with experimental data: overall, R2 shows better agreement with experiments with PCC up to 0.74, whereas R1 tends to be underestimated and showed low PCCs in some cases. This suggests that the simulations capture residue-to-residue relative trends reasonably well, while one or more systematic effects shift the overall scale of the predicted relaxation rates.

Beyond comparisons with experimental data, we benchmarked MD2NMR against existing computational approaches for estimating NMR relaxation parameters from molecular simulations. While several methods have been reported previously, many are not open source, not readily usable, or not tailored to protein systems. We identified two tools suitable for direct comparison: HYDRONMR and NCP-MDAnalysis.11,43 HYDRONMR is designed to compute NMR relaxation parameters for small, nearly rigid macromolecules by treating the molecule as a rigid body undergoing hydrodynamic rotational diffusion. The rotational diffusion tensor is inferred from static atomic coordinates (typically extracted from PDB structures), and the resulting global tumbling dynamics are then used to estimate relaxation rates.43 In contrast, NCP-MDAnalysis was developed specifically to calculate NMR relaxation parameters in nucleosome systems11,12 (https://github.com/OOLebedenko/NCP-MDAnalysis).

Accordingly, we compared MD2NMR with HYDRONMR for the small-protein systems and with NCP-MDAnalysis for the nucleosome systems. MD2NMR substantially outperforms HYDRONMR across all three small proteins. For ubiquitin, MD2NMR achieves PCCs of 0.86 for R1 and 0.89 for R2, whereas HYDRONMR yields markedly lower correlations (0.23 for R1 and 0.08 for R2; Table S4). For the nucleosome systems, MD2NMR and NCP-MDAnalysis yielded similar relaxation rates.

Impact of parameter choices on NMR observable calculations

To evaluate the robustness of our computational framework, we systematically examined the sensitivity of computed R1, R2, and residue-specific τc to two categories of parameters (Table S2). The first category comprises parameters related to fitting the autocorrelation function and deriving the spectral density J(ω), while the second includes MD simulation setup parameters. Tests were conducted under conditions closely matching the experimental environment (histone and DNA sequences, DNA length, solvent composition, temperature, and ionic strength) using simulation set 1 and experimental set 1 for benchmarking.

Among fitting-related parameters, τmax and the number of exponential terms (n_exp) exerted the greatest influence on NMR parameters (Figures 4 and S3). In particular, these choices strongly influenced the quality of the multiexponential fits to the time autocorrelation functions, thereby affecting the reconstructed decay of C(t), the resulting spectral density functions, and ultimately the predicted relaxation rates. τmax had a pronounced effect on the absolute values of τc but only a minor influence on the Pearson correlations between calculated and experimental results. Very small τmax values (e.g., ∼10 ns) truncate contributions from slower motions present in the MD trajectories, whereas larger τmax values lead to overestimation of τc and R2. We further tested τmax values over a broad range (2–400 ns) and found that the influence of τmax on the computed relaxation parameters becomes much smaller once τmax exceeds 50 ns (Figures 4 and S3). In this work, we use τmax = 82 ns as the default, corresponding to half of the rotational correlation time of the nucleosome. Motions significantly slower than τmax contribute only weakly to the spectral density, and fitting beyond this timescale primarily introduces artifacts due to statistical noise rather than meaningful dynamics (Figure S4).

Regarding n_exp, fitting autocorrelation function C(t) with three exponential components yielded relaxation parameters in best agreement with experimental data (Figures 4 and S3). Using two exponentials led to poorer goodness-of-fit values, evidenced by increased chi-squared (χ2) values and reduced coefficients of determination (R2) between the model and raw C(t) data (the autocorrelation function values computed directly from the MD trajectories). While employing four exponentials further improved the goodness of fit of the C(t), it led to a noticeable decrease in the correlation between the calculated and experimental relaxation rates, suggesting potential overfitting. This may also suggest that additional exponentials capture spurious slow modes present in finite trajectories rather than physically meaningful motions. By contrast, three exponentials provide enough flexibility to describe the multiscale decay of local motions (fast picosecond side-chain fluctuations, intermediate nanosecond loop motions, and slower backbone reorientations). Thus, three exponentials offer an effective compromise between model expressiveness and robustness, consistent with Kämpf et al.7 Moreover, a recent study likewise suggests that the required number of exponentials rarely exceeds three.17 We also note that imperfections in MD simulations can contribute to residual discrepancies between computed and experimental relaxation rates. Moreover, for finite MD trajectories, the estimated C(t) at long lag times carries substantial statistical uncertainty because the number of independent frame pairs decreases as the lag time increases. This noise-dominated regime can bias multiexponential fitting and, consequently, the resulting spectral density J(ω).

In contrast, trajectory segmentation (n_split) had only a minor impact on the final results, but it substantially reduced memory consumption and improved computational efficiency (Figure S3 and Table S6). We found that segmentation can increase computational speed by ∼3- to 4-fold. However, the length of each segment must be significantly longer than the chosen τmax value to ensure that slow motions are properly captured and to avoid truncation artifacts in fitting of the autocorrelation function. In this study, a segment length of range 180–1,800 ns was tested.

To compare the effects of different treatments of global tumbling on the calculations for the nucleosome, we applied two approaches: (1) using autocorrelation functions computed directly from the trajectory without removing global tumbling and (2) using internal correlation functions obtained after removing global tumbling through structural alignment, before reintroducing overall motion by multiplying the internal correlation function by a monoexponential decay describing isotropic global rotation (Figures S3e and S3f). Our results indicate that both treatments yield relaxation parameters in reasonable agreement with experimental data and yield comparable PCCs for τc. Notably, when global rotational tumbling was removed and then reintroduced, the absolute values of the calculated τc showed improved agreement with experimentally measured values.

For the MD setup parameters, both the sampling interval (dt), a time interval between frames in the trajectory used for autocorrelation function calculations, and the total trajectory length can affect the accuracy of the calculated relaxation rates (Figure 5). Small dt values had negligible effects; however, increasing dt progressively reduced the correlation with experimental data, likely due to insufficient temporal resolution for autocorrelation function fitting. For instance, at τmax = 50 ns, a dt of 40 ps provides 1,250 data points for fitting, whereas a dt of 1 ns yields only 50 points, thereby compromising fitting quality. When larger dt values are used, a proportionally larger τmax should be chosen to preserve adequate temporal resolution.

Longer trajectories consistently improved the accuracy of NMR relaxation parameter predictions by enhancing conformational sampling, as reflected by higher Pearson correlation coefficients with the experimental data (Figures 5c and 5d). In contrast, results obtained from the first 500 ns of the simulation trajectory showed a marked decrease in the PCCs between calculated and experimental τc values. This suggests that for histone tail conformational sampling, this time represents a lower bound (Figure S5).

The choice of water model, although only two were tested (OPC and TIP4P-Ew), also influenced the calculated τc values, exerting relatively minor effects on both their absolute magnitudes and the correlation with experimental results (Figure S5). Among the models tested, the OPC water model consistently produced the most accurate and reliable results. This may reflect its improved representation of dielectric properties and hydrogen-bond dynamics and offers a noticeable improvement in producing realistic structural ensembles of intrinsically disordered proteins.44,45 Although the water model plays a secondary role compared to τmax or trajectory length, it remains a non-negligible factor for quantitative agreement with experiments.

Discussion

Integrating MD simulations with NMR spectroscopy provides a powerful framework for characterizing biomolecular dynamics at atomic resolution. Direct calculation of NMR relaxation parameters from MD trajectories quantitatively bridges simulations and experiments, enabling mechanistic insights into molecular motions across different timescales. Despite the development of several programs and software packages aimed at achieving this integration, many are either not publicly accessible for direct use, tailored for nonprotein systems, or tend to be inconvenient for practical applications. This gap highlights the need for user-friendly and readily available tools that can facilitate the seamless integration of MD simulations with NMR spectroscopy, enhancing our ability to study biomolecular dynamics effectively.

Our tool, MD2NMR, offers a user-friendly and flexible platform for extracting relaxation rates (R1 and R2) and residue-specific τc from the MD data, showing good agreement with experimental measurements for well-characterized benchmark proteins and more complex nucleosome systems. MD2NMR combines rigorous treatment of both global and internal motions, supports multiple trajectory formats, offers fine-grained control over analysis parameters, and is compatible with widely used MD packages. This establishes MD2NMR as a reliable resource for both MD simulation validation and NMR data interpretation. Additionally, MD2NMR incorporates several strategies to enhance computational efficiency while providing practical flexibility to users. This allows users to conveniently customize the parameters used for NMR relaxation-rate calculations. Specifically, all parameters listed in Table S2 can be adjusted via a single command, enabling straightforward tuning of the analysis for various biomolecular systems and experimental conditions.

It is important to highlight that, when comparing MD2NMR-calculated values with experimental data for both small proteins and nucleosomes, we generally observe high PCCs yet noticeable discrepancies in the absolute relaxation rates. In particular, computed R2 values tend to be systematically overestimated, whereas R1 is often underestimated and, in some cases, shows lower PCCs. This combination of strong correlation but biased magnitudes suggests that the simulations capture residue-to-residue relative trends reasonably well, while one or more systematic effects shift the overall scale of the predicted rates. For ubiquitin, the MD2NMR-calculated values match quite well with the experimental data. However, for GB1, the MD2NMR data are systematically higher than the experimental values, whereas for GB3, the calculated R2 rates are systematically lower. For GB1 and GB3, these discrepancies may, at least in part, be attributed to differences between the temperatures used in the MD simulations and those in the NMR experiments. Specifically, the experimental measurements were performed at 25°C for ubiquitin, 35°C for GB1, and 24°C for GB3.26,27,28

Several factors may contribute to these observations. First, limitations inherent to the MD simulations—including the chosen force field and water model, trajectory length, and the trajectory saving interval—can all influence the accuracy of the underlying dynamics and, thus, the predicted NMR observables. Second, finite sampling affects estimation of the time correlation function C(t): at long lag times, C(t) becomes increasingly noise dominated due to the reduced number of statistically independent time intervals. This noise can bias multiexponential fitting of C(t) and, consequently, the derived spectral density J(ω), with a particularly strong impact on low-frequency components that contribute substantially to R2. Third, R1 and R2 probe distinct relaxation mechanisms and timescales. R2 is generally more sensitive to slower motions and conformational exchange and may therefore appear to agree better with experiments when these slower processes are partially captured in the available sampling.46 In contrast, R1 is dominated by higher-frequency (fast, picosecond-to-nanosecond) motions and is thus especially sensitive to the accuracy of fast local dynamics in the force-field/solvent model, thermostat/friction effects, and inaccurate sampling of short-time motions—factors that can lead to systematic underestimation of R1. In addition, nucleosomes display highly complex dynamics and the intricate interactions between DNA and histone proteins, including DNA unwrapping from a histone octamer, complicating the modeling of its behavior.9,47 Moreover, nucleosomes are large anisotropic systems, not at equilibrium, so time autocorrelation function approximation has certain limitations for such systems.

To avoid ambiguity regarding “timescales”, it is important to note that the relaxation times T1 and T2 (where T1 = 1/R1 and T2 = 1/R2) are typically in the millisecond-to-second range and represent macroscopic magnetization recovery/dephasing time constants, not the timescales of the underlying molecular motions. The relevant molecular motional processes that generate relaxation occur on much shorter timescales (picosecond-to-nanosecond for fast local fluctuations and nanosecond-to-microsecond for slower tail reorientation and exchange-like dynamics) and affect R1 and R2 through the spectral density J(ω).

Other than this resource, a key contribution of this work is the systematic analysis of factors affecting NMR relaxation calculations, including parameters governing the calculation of autocorrelation function C(t) and spectral density function J(ω), as well as those related to MD simulations. We showed that maximal autocorrelation time (τmax), which determines the timescale window over which slow and fast motions are integrated into the spectral density, has remarkable influence on the magnitudes of the calculated relaxation rate values. Using overly short τmax values truncates contributions from slow motions; however, for finite MD trajectories, the estimated C(t) at long lag times has large statistical uncertainty (due to fewer pairs of frames separated by a lag time t), and this noise-dominated region can bias multiexponential fitting and, hence, the calculated J(ω). In our analyses of nucleosome, we found that a τmax value of approximately 50–200 ns probably provides a balanced compromise between capturing slow motions and avoiding noise-related artifacts. Furthermore, fitting of C(t) using three exponential components provided the best agreement with experimental data for nucleosome, as reflected in improved goodness-of-fit metrics. Using four or more exponentials can probably lead to overfitting, introducing artifacts that reduce the correlation between calculated and experimental relaxation parameters.

On the other hand, MD simulation setup parameters are equally important. Adequate temporal resolution is required to capture fast picosecond-to-nanosecond fluctuations that dominate R1 and R2 relaxation, and, as we show, sufficiently long trajectories enhance conformational sampling and equilibration, thereby improving the agreement with experimental data. It should be noted that the parameters in the current analyses were optimized using nucleosome-based simulations and validated against experimental data for histone tails. For other biomolecular systems, these parameters may need to be adjusted and reparameterized to accommodate system-specific differences. MD2NMR allows users to modify many analysis settings and is compatible with commonly used MD software, making it straightforward to set up and optimize parameters for different biomolecules.

When selecting analysis and simulation parameters for different biomolecules, the overall rotational correlation time (τrot) can serve as a natural reference for benchmarking. For a nucleosome, τrot is approximately 163.4 ns, while for proteins and other large biomolecular complexes, τrot typically ranges from tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. The maximum autocorrelation time (τmax) used in fitting could be on the order of 0.5–1 × τrot, which may capture most relevant slow motions. MD trajectories should also span several multiples of τrot to ensure adequate sampling of slow dynamics. It is important to note that NMR experiments have intrinsic timescale limitations: motions much slower than τrot contribute weakly to relaxation observables. This limitation partly explains the overestimation of calculated τc from trajectories, as simulations may include slower motions that contribute little to NMR measurements.

We show that while the choice of water model had relatively minor effects on the calculated τc values, it still noticeably influences both their absolute magnitudes and the correlation with experimental results. Among the two water models tested (OPC and TIP4P-Ew), the OPC water model yielded the most accurate and reliable results for histone tails. Given that only two water models were examined here, systematic benchmarking of additional water models and force fields is warranted, particularly because some models may overestimate relaxation rates by favoring peptide-peptide over peptide-water interactions.7,11 For other biomolecular systems—such as small folded proteins, protein-RNA complexes, or membrane proteins—the optimal choice for water model and force field may differ, highlighting the need for careful system-specific validation to ensure accurate NMR predictions.

Looking ahead, MD2NMR provides a foundation for community-standardized workflows. Future developments will focus on expanding the support for additional NMR observables, incorporating advanced spectral density models, and implementing automated parameter optimization. Beyond the commonly used model-free approaches, in the future we plan to integrate recently developed methods such as isotropic reorientational eigenmode dynamics (iRED) and the dynamic detector (DD) approach. iRED can handle anisotropic tumbling by calculating correlation functions as averages over spherical harmonics, while the DD approach can be adapted to account for anisotropic motions by analyzing trajectories in small frames of reference focused on distinct motion types.48,49 Combined with systematic benchmarking of simulation protocols, these enhancements will further improve prediction reliability and broaden MD2NMR’s applicability to diverse biomolecular systems.
