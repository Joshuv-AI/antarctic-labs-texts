The mediating role of digital fatigue in the effect of job satisfaction on motivation among Turkish nurses
Authors: Arzu Güngör Tolasa, Ayşegül Türkmenoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDigitalization in healthcare has reshaped nursing practice but also introduced psychosocial challenges. While well-designed digital systems can enhance workflow efficiency and satisfaction, excessive digital demands may lead to technostress and digital fatigue, reducing nurses’ motivation. This study aimed to examine the mediating role of digital fatigue in the relationship between job satisfaction and motivation among nurses.MethodsThis descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted between July and October 2025 with 350 nurses working at a hospital in İzmir, Türkiye. Data were collected using the Participant Information Form, Job Satisfaction Scale, Nurse Work Motivation Scale, and Technostress Creators Scale for Health Professionals. Analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 with descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, correlation, and mediation analysis.ResultsMost participants were female (86.3%) and under 30 years old (68.6%). Over half (52%) reported experiencing technology-related fatigue or burnout. Job satisfaction was positively associated with both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (p < .001) and negatively associated with technostress (p < .05). While job satisfaction significantly predicted both technostress (β = 0.083, p = .033) and motivation (β = −0.156, p = .000), technostress did not significantly predict motivation (β = 0.004, p = .882), indicating that digital fatigue did not fully mediate this relationship. Older and more experienced nurses reported higher job satisfaction and lower technostress levels.ConclusionJob satisfaction remains a primary driver of motivation among nurses. However, in digitally intensive clinical environments, digital fatigue poses a significant risk to sustaining motivation. Healthcare institutions should address both organizational and technological factors by improving digital system usability, offering training, and implementing strategies to mitigate digital fatigue to enhance nurses’ motivation and retention.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Digitalization has become an integral component of modern healthcare systems, transforming nursing practice through the widespread use of electronic health records, digital monitoring systems, and clinical decision-support technologies. When effectively designed and implemented, these digital tools have the potential to improve workflow efficiency, patient safety, and care coordination [1]. However, the increasing reliance on digital technologies has also introduced new psychosocial demands for nurses, contributing to elevated stress levels and challenges in maintaining work life balance. Recent studies indicate that intensive and continuous digital system use is associated with increased cognitive load, emotional strain, and burnout among nurses, particularly in technology-intensive clinical environments such as intensive care units and emergency departments [2, 3].

One of the most prominent psychosocial consequences of healthcare digitalization is technostress, defined as stress experienced due to the use of information and communication technologies [4]. Technostress manifests through multiple dimensions, including techno-overload, techno-invasion, techno-complexity, techno-insecurity, and techno-uncertainty. These dimensions reflect increased digital workload, blurred boundaries between work and personal life, difficulties in adapting to complex systems, and concerns about technological competence [5, 6]. Recent empirical studies conducted in diverse healthcare settings consistently demonstrate that technostress is associated with adverse outcomes such as burnout, fatigue, reduced job satisfaction, and diminished psychological well-being among nurses [4, 7].

Job satisfaction remains a critical determinant of nurses’ professional performance, motivation, retention, and quality of care. Contemporary evidence suggests that job satisfaction is influenced by organizational policies, professional autonomy, perceived competence, interpersonal relationships, and institutional support [6]. Within traditional organizational models, higher job satisfaction is generally expected to enhance work motivation. However, emerging research indicates that in digitally intensive work environments, high levels of job satisfaction do not always translate into increased motivation. Favorable organizational conditions may coexist with technology-related stressors, which can undermine motivational processes despite overall satisfaction with the job [2, 8].

In recent years, the concept of digital fatigue has gained attention as a specific form of cognitive and emotional exhaustion resulting from prolonged and intensive exposure to digital technologies. Digital fatigue has been associated with reduced attention, diminished psychological resilience, impaired work engagement, and increased risk of burnout among healthcare professionals [3, 7]. Importantly, digital fatigue is not merely a direct outcome of technology use but may also interact with traditional occupational factors, shaping how job-related resources influence motivational outcomes [7, 8].

The Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model provides a robust theoretical framework for understanding these relationships. According to the JD–R model, job demands such as excessive digital workload, complex documentation requirements, and constant connectivity require sustained cognitive and emotional effort and may lead to strain and exhaustion. In contrast, job resources such as job satisfaction, autonomy, and organizational support play a motivational role and foster engagement and performance [9, 10]. Within this framework, digital fatigue can be conceptualized as a mechanism through which technology-related job demands weaken the positive motivational effects of job resources [9].

Despite the growing literature on technostress and digital fatigue, empirical studies examining their mediating role in the relationship between job satisfaction and motivation among nurses remain limited. Most existing research has focused on direct effects on burnout, satisfaction, or performance, while the underlying mechanisms linking satisfaction and motivation in digitally transformed healthcare environments have received less attention. Recent reviews highlight the need for integrative, theory-driven models that incorporate both traditional organizational factors and contemporary digital stressors to better explain nurses’ motivation [1, 4, 8].

Therefore, guided by the JD–R model, this study aims to examine the mediating role of digital fatigue (technostress) in the relationship between job satisfaction and work motivation among nurses. By positioning digital fatigue as a theoretically grounded mediating mechanism, the study seeks to contribute to the literature by clarifying how digital job demands interact with job resources to shape motivational outcomes in modern nursing practice.

Method

Research design

The researchers designed this study as a descriptive and cross-sectional investigation. The researchers conducted the study between July 1 and October 1, 2025, involving 350 nurses working at a hospital in İzmir who voluntarily agreed to participate. The researchers distributed an online survey link to the nurses who consented and requested them to complete the questionnaire electronically. The study sample consisted of nurses who had been actively working for at least six months in intensive care units, clinical wards, outpatient clinics, or other specialized care units, and who had internet access. Nurses on medical leave, maternity leave, unpaid leave, military leave, or administrative leave, as well as those on compensatory time off for night shifts, were excluded from the study. The researchers informed all participants about the purpose and scope of the study and obtained their consent to participate through a confirmation item at the beginning of the questionnaire. The data collection process took approximately 15 min per participant. To ensure anonymity and protect the confidentiality of responses, the researchers disabled email address collection and electronic IP tracking during the data collection process.

Population and sample

The research population consisted of all nurses employed at the hospital (N = 1,900). The researchers included all nurses employed at the hospital (N = 1,900) as the study population and calculated the sample size using the known population formula [n = N t² pq / d² (N − 1) + t² pq]. A confidence level of 90%, a standard deviation of 0.05, and a margin of error of 5% were adopted, with the p-value set at 0.5. Based on these parameters, the researchers determined that at least 238 participants were required. The researchers completed the study with 350 nurses who voluntarily agreed to participate, had been employed at the hospital for at least six months, and fully completed the questionnaire.

Data collection

The researchers collected the study data using the “Participant Information Form,” the “Job Satisfaction Scale,” the “Nurse Work Motivation Scale,” and the “Technostress Creators Scale for Health Professionals.”

Participant Information Form: The researchers developed this form based on a comprehensive review of the relevant literature. It consists of 12 items addressing the sociodemographic characteristics of the participating nurses [11, 12].

Technostress Creators Scale for Health Professionals: The Technostress Creators Scale for Health Professionals initially developed by Tarafdar et al. (2007), simplified by Alam (2015), and adapted into Turkish by Türen, Erdem, and Kalkın (2015) was used to measure stress arising from the use of information and communication technologies in the workplace. The 14 item, five-point Likert scale includes three subdimensions: Techno-Overload, Techno-Complexity, and Techno-Uncertainty. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.95) in this study, confirming its reliability for assessing technology-related stress among nurses in digital healthcare environments [12–15].

Job Satisfaction Scale: The Job Satisfaction Scale, developed by Şahin and Durak (1994) and later validated by Batıgün and Şahin (2006), was used to evaluate employees’ job satisfaction. The 32-item scale, scored on a five-point Likert system (0%–100%), includes seven subdimensions: Salary and Rewarding, Promotion and Career Opportunities, Management and Organizational Policies, Working Conditions, Nature and Suitability of Work, Communication and Social Relations, and Performance Evaluation and Feedback. The scale provides a comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of job satisfaction and has demonstrated strong psychometric properties [16–18]. The present study showed excellent internal reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.97).

Nurse Work Motivation Scale (NJMS): The Nurse Work Motivation Scale, developed and validated by Mete, Ünsar, and Tunalı (2011), was used to measure nurses’ motivation levels. The 25-item, three-point Likert scale assesses three dimensions: Intrinsic Motivation, Extrinsic Motivation, and Achievement/Competence Motivation. The scale has shown strong psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.78 to 0.92 and 0.96 in the present study, indicating excellent reliability for evaluating nurses’ motivation in healthcare settings [19].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Frequency and percentage distributions were calculated for sociodemographic variables. Differences in mean scores between two groups were examined using independent samples t-tests or the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the normality of data distribution. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied. Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to determine the direction and strength of associations between study variables. Mediation analysis was performed to examine whether technostress mediated the relationship between job satisfaction (independent variable) and nurse work motivation (dependent variable). This analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (version 4.2), Model 4, developed by Hayes. The indirect effect was tested using the bootstrapping method with 5,000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval. Mediation was considered statistically significant when the confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero. The bootstrapping approach was preferred as it does not require the assumption of normality and provides robust estimates of indirect effects. All statistical analyses were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Ethical approval

The researchers obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of İzmir City Hospital (Decision No: 2025/309, dated 7 July 2025) prior to conducting the study. All participants received detailed information about the research and provided their informed consent electronically through the online survey platform. The researchers conducted the study in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Among the participating nurses, 68.6% were under the age of 30, and 86.3% (n = 302) were female. Additionally, 69.1% (n = 242) were single, and 81.1% held a bachelor’s degree. A majority of the nurses, 77.4% (n = 271), worked in clinical settings. The researchers examined the work units and found that 18% (n = 64) of the nurses worked in internal medicine clinics, 15.7% (n = 55) in surgical clinics, 29.1% (n = 102) in intensive care units, and 19.7% (n = 69) in other departments. Moreover, they found that 72.9% (n = 255) of the nurses used electronic health record (EHR) systems daily. Moreover, 72.9% (n = 255) reported using electronic health record (EHR) systems daily. Regarding perceptions of digital technologies, 46.3% (n = 162) stated that using digital systems somewhat facilitated their work, while 50.6% (n = 177) considered themselves competent in using such systems. Furthermore, 52% (n = 182) reported experiencing fatigue or burnout related to technology use within the past six months. The researchers found that the most frequently used digital systems among nurses were patient monitoring devices (81.4%, n = 281) and automated medication dispensing systems (78.3%, n = 274). In contrast, the least frequently used systems daily were the hospital information management system (19.7%, n = 69) and digital appointment systems (15.4%, n = 54) (Table 1).

The scores of the Nurse Work Motivation Scale, Job Satisfaction Scale, and Technostress Scale were compared according to sociodemographic and professional variables. Statistically significant differences were observed across several variables. Specifically, age, years of professional experience, educational level, department of employment, current position, experience of fatigue or burnout related to technology use, frequency of working with electronic health record systems, perceived facilitation of work through digital systems, and level of digital system use were found to significantly influence the mean scores of specific scales.

The detailed results of these differences are presented in the corresponding table. Overall, higher job satisfaction scores were observed among nurses who were older, had longer professional experience, higher educational attainment, and were employed in specific units. In addition, higher job satisfaction and lower technostress levels were observed among nurses who perceived digital systems as facilitating their work and who considered themselves competent in their use (Table 3).

Correlation analyses were conducted, and several strong positive relationships among the scales were identified. A strong positive correlation was observed between Intrinsic Motivation and Extrinsic Motivation (r = .682, p = .000), as well as a robust positive correlation between Intrinsic Motivation and the total Nurse Work Motivation Scale (r = .862, p = .000). Discussion

This study examined the relationships between nurses’ job satisfaction, work motivation, and technostress levels, and evaluated the potential mediating role of technostress in the relationship between job satisfaction and motivation. The findings revealed a significant negative relationship between job satisfaction and work motivation, a weak positive relationship between job satisfaction and technostress, and no significant direct or mediating effect of technostress on motivation. In addition, high levels were observed in certain technostress subdimensions, particularly techno-invasion and techno-uncertainty. Collectively, these results suggest that technological stressors constitute an important contextual factor in nurses’ work experiences and that traditional job satisfaction mechanisms may operate differently in digitally intensive healthcare environments [1, 2].

The relationship between job satisfaction and motivation

The negative relationship identified between job satisfaction and work motivation represents one of the most noteworthy findings of this study and contrasts with the dominant assumption in the literature that these constructs are positively related. Previous studies have consistently reported that higher job satisfaction is associated with increased motivation, engagement, and performance among nurses [20–22]. However, in the present study, an inverse association was observed (β = −0.156, p < .001), indicating that higher levels of job satisfaction were associated with lower levels of work motivation.

Further examination of the study results suggests that this relationship cannot be attributed to methodological or measurement-related issues. The correlation matrix including all scale subdimensions demonstrated that intercorrelations remained well below multicollinearity thresholds, supporting the discriminant validity of job satisfaction, motivation, and technostress constructs [11]. This finding indicates that the observed negative association reflects a substantive phenomenon rather than an artifact of overlapping constructs or scoring errors.

From a theoretical perspective, this finding can be meaningfully interpreted within the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model [9–23]. According to this framework, job satisfaction represents a global evaluative appraisal of working conditions and available resources, whereas work motivation particularly intrinsic motivation is shaped by perceived autonomy, task meaningfulness, and opportunities for professional growth [24]. In highly digitalized healthcare settings, such as contemporary nursing environments, increased digital workload and constant interaction with complex information systems may alter this relationship. Nurses may report relatively high job satisfaction due to job security, organizational support, or professional identity, while simultaneously experiencing erosion of intrinsic motivation resulting from digital interruptions, workflow fragmentation, and cognitive overload [10].

This interpretation is further supported by the composition of job satisfaction observed in the present study. High satisfaction scores were largely driven by extrinsic and organizational factors such as organizational policies, physical working conditions, and interpersonal relationships. In contrast, intrinsic motivational resources appeared weaker. This pattern is consistent with Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory, which posits that hygiene factors may prevent dissatisfaction but are insufficient to sustain motivation over time [25, 26].

Similar findings have been reported in studies indicating that improvements in extrinsic conditions alone do not guarantee intrinsic engagement or sustained motivation among nurses [6].

The role of technostress and digital fatigue

The findings related to technostress provide important contextual insight into the satisfaction–motivation relationship. More than half of the participants reported experiencing technology-related fatigue or burnout within the past six months, highlighting the substantial psychosocial burden imposed by healthcare digitalization. High mean scores in techno-invasion and techno-uncertainty suggest that blurred work–life boundaries and continuous adaptation to system changes are particularly salient stressors for nurses [5, 27].

Although technostress did not emerge as a statistically significant mediator between job satisfaction and motivation, its pervasive presence may nevertheless weaken the motivational function of job satisfaction. In digitally intensive environments, job satisfaction may increasingly reflect adaptation or acceptance of working conditions rather than active engagement. This interpretation aligns with prior research indicating that prolonged exposure to digital systems can transform job resources into job demands, thereby undermining motivational processes even when satisfaction levels remain stable [4, 28, 29].

Importantly, the lack of a direct effect of technostress on motivation does not imply that technostress is inconsequential. Rather, its effects may manifest more strongly in outcomes such as burnout, emotional exhaustion, job performance, or intention to leave [1, 3, 7]. Emerging literature on techno-eustress further suggests that when adequate training, autonomy, and system usability are provided, technology-related demands may be perceived as challenges rather than threats, potentially buffering their negative impact on motivation [5, 30, 31].

Demographic and institutional context

The study also demonstrated that demographic and professional characteristics significantly influenced job satisfaction, motivation, and technostress levels. Senior nurses with longer professional experience reported both higher job satisfaction and higher technostress, particularly in relation to techno-insecurity and techno-uncertainty. This pattern suggests that while experience may enhance satisfaction through professional competence and organizational status, it may also increase vulnerability to stress associated with rapid digital transformation [32]. Furthermore, significant differences across clinical units underscore the contextual nature of technostress and motivation. Nurses working in high-intensity units such as intensive care and emergency departments experienced higher levels of technostress, consistent with previous findings emphasizing the cumulative effects of technological load and time pressure in these settings [33, 34].

Implications for practice

Taken together, these findings indicate that job satisfaction and work motivation should not be treated as interchangeable or uniformly aligned outcomes in digitally intensive healthcare environments. Instead, they represent distinct psychological processes that may diverge when digital job demands exceed nurses’ adaptive capacity. Healthcare institutions should therefore focus not only on improving extrinsic working conditions but also on strengthening intrinsic motivational resources through autonomy enhancing practices, participatory decision-making, and professional development opportunities. Additionally, targeted interventions to reduce digital fatigue such as unit-specific system design, continuous digital skills training, and responsive technical support are essential to sustaining motivation and well-being among nurses in modern healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This study provides novel evidence regarding the complex relationship between job satisfaction, job motivation, and technostress among nurses working in digitally intensive healthcare environments. Contrary to the dominant assumption in the literature that job satisfaction uniformly enhances motivation, the findings demonstrated a significant negative direct effect of job satisfaction on job motivation. Comprehensive psychometric analyses, including a full correlation matrix of all subdimensions, confirmed that this relationship was not attributable to measurement error, scoring miscalculation, or subscale confounding.

Furthermore, although job satisfaction was positively associated with technostress, technostress did not significantly predict job motivation, and the indirect effect was not statistically significant. These results indicate that technostress did not mediate the relationship between job satisfaction and job motivation. Instead, technostress appears to function as a contextual job demand that coexists with satisfaction but does not independently explain motivational outcomes. Taken together, these findings underscore that job satisfaction and job motivation are distinct psychological constructs that may diverge under conditions of high digital job demands. In technology driven clinical settings, job satisfaction may increasingly reflect organizational stability, adaptation, or acceptance, whereas motivation particularly intrinsic motivation remains sensitive to autonomy, task meaning, and perceived professional competence. As digital systems intensify cognitive load and workflow fragmentation, satisfaction may persist while motivational engagement gradually declines. From a practical and policy oriented perspective, the results suggest that organizational strategies aimed solely at increasing job satisfaction are insufficient to sustain or enhance motivation among nurses. Healthcare institutions should complement satisfaction oriented interventions with motivation focused workplace practices, including the optimization of digital system interfaces, reduction of unnecessary technological complexity, and the development of unit-specific.
