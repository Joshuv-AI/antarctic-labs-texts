Extracellular Vesicles as Orchestrators of Osteoimmunomodulation in Peri‐Implantitis: Implications for Bone Regeneration
Authors: Marco Bonilla, María Carretero‐Fernández, Juan Sainz, Francisco Mesa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPeri‐implantitis, characterized by inflammatory bone loss, is a leading cause of dental implant failure. Current regenerative strategies face limitations due to the persistent inflammatory microenvironment. Mesenchymal stem cell‐derived extracellular vesicles (MSC‐EVs) have emerged as promising acellular therapeutics with dual osteogenic and immunomodulatory capacities, offering a novel approach to address the complexity of peri‐implant bone defects.MethodsThis narrative review synthesizes preclinical evidence on MSC‐EVs for peri‐implant bone regeneration. It examines methods for EV isolation from various sources. Furthermore, it analyzes advanced engineered delivery systems, such as peptide‐functionalized implant surfaces and bioactive scaffolds, designed to enhance local retention and therapeutic efficacy.ResultsMSC‐EVs orchestrate bone regeneration through multifaceted mechanisms. They directly stimulate osteogenesis by transferring pro‐osteogenic miRNAs and proteins, activating key pathways like PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β‐catenin. Crucially, they modulate the osteoimmune environment by reprogramming macrophages toward a reparative M2 phenotype, suppressing pro‐inflammatory Th1/Th17 responses, and expanding regulatory T cells (Tregs). Concurrently, MSC‐EVs inhibit bone resorption by favorably modulating the RANKL/OPG balance and directly interfering with osteoclast differentiation. They also promote neovascularization, a critical factor for healing in ischemic zones. Preclinical models demonstrate that localized delivery via engineered systems significantly enhances osseointegration, bone‐implant contact, and regeneration of functional bone‐vessel units.ConclusionMSC‐EVs represent a promising and multifaceted therapeutic strategy for bone regeneration in peri‐implantitis, simultaneously targeting inflammation, bone resorption, and osteogenesis. While robust preclinical evidence supports their role in halting bone loss and promoting structured regeneration, successful clinical translation requires further standardization, optimization of delivery, and validation in human trials.

Introduction

Progressive loss of peri‐implant bone represents the central pathological hallmark of peri‐implantitis and the primary determinant of long‐term dental implant failure. Although implant therapy has become a predictable and widely accepted modality for oral rehabilitation, its success may be compromised by the development of peri‐implant diseases, among which peri‐implantitis constitutes the most severe entity [1]. This condition is defined as a pathological inflammatory process affecting the tissues surrounding a functional osseointegrated implant and is characterized by bleeding and/or suppuration on probing, increased probing depth, and progressive loss of peri‐implant supporting bone [2].

Although peri‐implantitis shares certain etiological factors with periodontitis, it should not be regarded simply as an implant analog of periodontitis. Anatomical and biological differences in peri‐implant tissues confer a generally more aggressive clinical course and a more limited response to conventional therapies. This distinctive microenvironment renders peri‐implant bone defects a significant challenge for guided bone regeneration approaches, since, despite achieving clinical improvements such as reduced bleeding on probing, decreased probing depth, and limited vertical bone fill, current evidence does not allow definitive recommendations regarding specific regenerative protocols [3, 4]. In this context, therapeutic success appears to depend not only on the induction of bone formation, but also on the effective control of chronic inflammation, appropriate flap design, thorough implant surface decontamination, and soft tissue stability, all of which are critical determinants of achieving functional re‐osseointegration [5].

Over the last decade, exosomes have emerged as key players in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering. These extracellular vesicles (EVs), released by most cell types, function as sophisticated systems of intercellular communication [6]. Their lipid bilayer membrane encapsulates proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, including microRNAs (miRNAs), capable of modulating the behavior of recipient cells and promoting processes such as proliferation, migration, cellular differentiation, and immune response regulation [7]. Compared with cell‐based therapies, EVs offer relevant advantages, including lower immunogenicity, greater stability, and the absence of risks associated with direct cell administration. Consequently, mesenchymal stem cell (MSCs)‐derived EVs have been positioned as particularly promising acellular therapeutic agents for tissue regeneration [8].

Interest in their application for peri‐implantitis is supported by growing evidence from the field of periodontal regeneration. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that EVs derived from periodontal ligament stem cells, bone marrow‐derived MSCs, and adipose‐derived stem cells exhibit marked osteogenic and immunomodulatory potential [9]. Mechanistically, these vesicles promote osteoblastic migration and differentiation, inhibit osteoclastogenesis, and modulate inflammatory responses, favoring, among other effects, the polarization of macrophages toward a reparative M2 phenotype [10, 11]. This multifunctional profile renders EVs particularly attractive candidates for addressing the biological complexity of peri‐implant bone defects.

On this basis, it is hypothesized that local administration of EVs may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for bone regeneration in peri‐implantitis by combining direct osteogenic effects with favorable modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. Therefore, the aim of this narrative review is to synthesize and critically analyze, for the first time to the best of our knowledge, the available scientific evidence—both preclinical and emerging clinical—regarding the therapeutic potential of EVs for peri‐implant bone regeneration.

Search Strategy

A literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE up to February 2026. Search terms included combinations of: “peri‐implantitis,” “dental implants,” “osseointegration,” “exosomes,” “extracellular vesicles,” “mesenchymal stem cells,” “bone regeneration,” “osteoimmunology,” “macrophage polarization,” and “delivery systems.” Reference lists of relevant articles and reviews were manually screened to identify additional studies. Priority was given to peer‐reviewed original research and systematic reviews investigating MSC‐derived EVs in bone regeneration, immunomodulation, and implant‐related models, with a focus on preclinical evidence and emerging clinical applications. Due to the narrative nature of this review, no formal quality assessment or meta‐analysis was performed.

Current Therapeutic Approaches for Peri‐Implantitis

The management of peri‐implantitis has evolved considerably in recent years, reflecting a more comprehensive understanding of its multifactorial etiology and the development of evidence‐based therapeutic protocols. Contemporary treatment strategies aim to arrest disease progression, preserve implant function, and, when feasible, regenerate the lost peri‐implant supporting tissues. Current clinical algorithms generally follow a stepwise approach, beginning with non‐surgical debridement and risk factor control, and progressing to surgical intervention in moderate to advanced cases [12].

Non‐surgical therapy primarily focuses on mechanical disruption of the biofilm and decontamination of the implant surface. This includes the use of specialized instruments such as titanium or carbon fiber curettes and ultrasonic scalers, often combined with chemical antiseptics (e.g., chlorhexidine or povidone‐iodine) and, in selected cases, adjunctive local or systemic antibiotics [13]. Although non‐surgical approaches may achieve disease stabilization in a proportion of cases—approximately 40% according to current evidence—their effectiveness in advanced peri‐implantitis is limited [14].

Surgical management can be broadly categorized into reconstructive (regenerative) and resective approaches. Reconstructive strategies aim to restore the lost bone–implant interface through guided bone regeneration (GBR) principles, combining meticulous implant surface decontamination with bone grafting materials and barrier membranes [15]. Structured regenerative workflows, such as the Biologic Reset Protocol (BRP), integrate prosthetic and surgical phases to optimize outcomes in advanced intrabony defects [16]. In contrast, resective approaches—including implantoplasty and apically positioned flaps—seek to eliminate the inflammatory compartment and create a maintainable peri‐implant environment [17]. While effective in reducing pocket depth and facilitating plaque control, these approaches may result in additional loss of supporting tissue [18].

Recent consensus efforts have further refined clinical decision‐making. A landmark 2024 consensus conference jointly organized by the Academy of Osseointegration and the American Academy of Periodontology synthesized current evidence into unified recommendations. Key conclusions emphasized the critical importance of identifying and modifying risk factors such as a history of periodontitis, smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, and inadequate plaque control [19]. The peri‐implant soft tissue phenotype—particularly a keratinized mucosa height ≥ 2 mm—was also highlighted as a determinant of disease prevention and treatment success. Moreover, the consensus underscored the necessity of individualized surgical strategies based on defect configuration and severity, as well as the essential role of long‐term supportive peri‐implant maintenance therapy [19].

In parallel, the work of Hom‐Lay Wang has been pivotal in standardizing regenerative surgical techniques, implant surface decontamination strategies, and the application of biologic agents, thereby shaping contemporary standards of care centered on early diagnosis, thorough debridement, and defect‐specific surgical protocols [20, 21]. Additionally, research led by Georgios Kotsakis has drawn attention to the pathogenic role of titanium particles and corrosion byproducts in peri‐implant inflammation [22, 23]. This line of investigation highlights the need to minimize iatrogenic surface damage during debridement and has expanded our understanding of the host response to implant‐derived debris, opening new perspectives for targeted therapeutic interventions.

Sources and Methods for EVs Isolation

Main Cellular Sources

MSCs represent the most extensively investigated source of EVs due to their intrinsic capacity to secrete vesicles with potent paracrine effects on tissue regeneration. Dental‐derived MSCs have gained particular relevance in periodontal and peri‐implant regeneration [24]. A recent scoping review analyzing 944 articles concluded that the main EVs sources for bone regeneration around dental implants include dental pulp cells, periodontal ligament cells, gingival fibroblasts, stem cells from exfoliated deciduous teeth, and stem cells from the apical papilla, reporting positive preclinical outcomes in 100% of the evaluated studies [25]. Furthermore, osteogenic preconditioning of MSCs prior to EVs isolation represents an effective strategy to enhance their biological activity. EVs derived from human MSCs pre‐differentiated for 10–15 days have been shown to exhibit an altered miRNA profile and to significantly improve bone regeneration when incorporated into titanium alloy scaffolds [26].

Beyond MSCs, EVs derived from cells of the innate and adaptive immune system also contribute to peri‐implant regeneration. M2 macrophages favor osteogenesis, whereas M1 macrophages may induce apoptosis of stem cells under stress conditions; neutrophils promote coagulation and early angiogenesis; mast cells facilitate lymphocyte activation and inflammatory regulation; and EVs from natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells, T lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes coordinate immune responses and regulate endothelial function, thereby optimizing implant integration and stability [27].

Additional sources include platelet‐derived EVs, which are enriched in pro‐angiogenic and wound‐healing growth factors [28].

Centrifugation‐Based Methods

One of the most widely used techniques is differential ultracentrifugation, which has long been considered the gold standard for EV isolation. This method separates vesicles through stepwise increases in centrifugal force and is suitable for processing large sample volumes. However, it is associated with several well‐recognized limitations, as it is labor‐intensive, time‐consuming, and may compromise EV structural integrity due to high shear forces [30, 33]. A more refined variant is density gradient ultracentrifugation, which employs media such as sucrose or iodixanol to separate particles according to density, achieving higher purity and reduced vesicle damage, albeit at the expense of greater technical complexity and longer processing times [24].

Size‐ and Filtration‐Based Methods

These methods rely on strategies that exploit the nanometric size of EVs. Ultrafiltration uses membranes with defined molecular weight cut‐offs and represents a simple and accessible approach [24], although it is prone to membrane clogging and the loss of smaller vesicles [30]. In contrast, tangential flow filtration allows the sample to flow parallel to the membrane surface, minimizing filter cake formation and enabling efficient processing of large volumes with substantially higher yields (up to 92.5‐fold greater than conventional ultracentrifugation) [33].

Another alternative is size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC), in which particles are separated while traversing a porous stationary phase; EVs, with larger hydrodynamic size, elute earlier than soluble proteins and other contaminants. SEC is particularly valued for preserving vesicle structure and bioactivity, although residual contamination with similarly sized lipoproteins may occur [36].

Precipitation‐Based Methods

Polymer‐based precipitation techniques—most commonly using polyethylene glycol or commercial kits (e.g., ExoQuick) – reduce EV solubility, inducing aggregation followed by sedimentation through low‐speed centrifugation. Although this approach is rapid, inexpensive, and suitable for parallel processing of multiple samples, the main limitation of this approach is its relatively low purity, as protein aggregates, immune complexes, and other contaminants are frequently co‐precipitated [36, 37].

Affinity‐Based Methods

When high specificity is required, immunoaffinity capture employs antibodies, aptamers, or ligands targeting exosomal surface markers such as CD63, CD81, or CD9 [41]. Nevertheless, high cost, dependence on high‐quality antibodies, and potential epitope masking after binding substantially limit scalability and therapeutic applicability [36].

In accordance with the recommendations of the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) [42], this review employs the generic term EVs to describe the heterogeneous population of particles released by cells. The specific term “exosomes” is reserved for contexts where studies have demonstrated an endosomal biogenesis origin or enrichment in canonical exosomal markers (e.g., CD9, CD63, CD81) following rigorous characterization. We acknowledge that the isolation methods discussed in this section, such as differential ultracentrifugation and size‐exclusion chromatography, yield heterogeneous EV populations rather than pure exosome preparations.

Mechanisms of Action of EVs in the Peri‐Implant Microenvironment

Peri‐implantitis creates a hostile environment characterized by chronic inflammation, bone loss, and impaired regenerative capacity. MSC‐EVs have emerged as a multifaceted therapeutic strategy capable of counteracting these processes at molecular and cellular levels [43]. This section outlines the key mechanisms through which MSC‐EVs exert their effects, based on major experimental findings reported in the literature and summarized schematically in Figure 1: (1) direct stimulation of osteogenesis, (2) modulation of the immunoinflammatory microenvironment, (3) inhibition of bone resorption, and (4) neovascularization.

M2 macrophages secrete VEGF and BMP‐2, creating a feedback loop that recruits more host mesenchymal stem cells to the site. Besides, EVs suppress Th1/Th17 and expand Tregs. (D) Mechanism IV: MSC‐EVs promote VEGF, CD31, and endomucin, restoring blood supply and stability in “ischemic zones”.

Immunomodulation and Control of the Inflammatory Microenvironment: From a Hostile to a Reparative Niche

A well‐established immunomodulatory mechanism of MSC‐EVs is their ability to induce macrophage polarization from a proinflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti‐inflammatory, reparative M2 phenotype [44]. M1 macrophages, typically induced by stimuli such as IFN‐γ and LPS, contribute to the perpetuation of inflammation and degradation of peri‐implant tissues [45]. Conversely, MSC‐EVs promote the transition to M2 macrophages [44], which support inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration. In this regard, the study by Wei Liu et al. demonstrated that EVs enriched with miR‐181b suppress PRKCD expression in macrophages, leading to activation of the p‐AKT pathway and acting as a “switch” that drives M2 polarization. These M2 macrophages, in turn, secrete key osteogenic factors such as VEGF and BMP‐2, stimulating osteoblastic differentiation of adjacent bone marrow MSCs [46].

Crucially, the therapeutic relevance of targeting macrophage polarization in peri‐implantitis has been directly validated in recent implant‐specific preclinical models, providing a disease‐relevant foundation for MSC‐EV‐based strategies. In a mouse ligature‐induced peri‐implantitis model, Ginesin et al. investigated the role of macrophages. Systemic macrophage depletion using liposomal clodronate resulted in a striking preservation of peri‐implant bone, with treated animals exhibiting significantly higher bone‐implant contact and bone volume fraction [47]. These quantitative, clinically relevant endpoints demonstrate that modulating the macrophage compartment alone is sufficient to attenuate inflammatory bone loss and improve osseointegration in peri‐implantitis.

Complementary studies have explored pharmacological agents that promote a reparative macrophage phenotype. Exogenous administration of Galectin‐9, a potent inducer of M2 polarization, significantly reduced alveolar bone resorption and decreased the M1/M2 ratio in a rat peri‐implantitis model induced by LPS and ligature [48]. Similarly, dipyridamole was shown to attenuate experimental peri‐implantitis in rats by suppressing M1 macrophage polarization, as evidenced by reduced expression of IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, alongside decreased bone loss [49]. These findings collectively establish that steering macrophages away from a pro‐inflammatory M1 state and toward an anti‐inflammatory, pro‐reparative M2 phenotype is a viable therapeutic strategy in implant‐specific disease contexts.

Importantly, at this stage, the main biological outcome is the shift in immune phenotype rather than metabolic reprogramming per se, which is discussed in detail in Section 3.2.

The immunomodulatory capacity of MSC‐derived EVs can be potentiated through cellular preconditioning strategies. In this approach, MSCs are exposed to specific stimuli to modify the functional profile of the EVs they subsequently release. In low‐grade chronic inflammation models, such as diabetes, preconditioning MSCs with TNF‐α has been shown to increase EV production and enrich their anti‐inflammatory cargo. As a result, these EVs exhibit a greater ability to reverse proinflammatory macrophage polarization and promote their transition toward an M2 phenotype, associated with inflammation resolution and tissue repair [50].

Beyond pharmacological and depletion approaches, cell‐based therapies have also shown promise in peri‐implantitis models. Kuntjoro et al. demonstrated that transplantation of hypoxia‐preconditioned human umbilical cord MSCs in a rat peri‐implantitis model significantly reduced inflammatory markers (TNF‐α, NFATc1) while enhancing osteogenic markers (osteocalcin, COL1α1) [51]. Although this study utilized MSCs rather than their secreted EVs, it supports the broader concept that MSC‐derived therapies can ameliorate inflammation and promote regeneration in the compromised peri‐implant environment.

Beyond their effects on macrophages, MSC‐EVs also significantly modulate lymphocyte responses. Several studies have shown their capacity to suppress the activation of proinflammatory T helper subpopulations, such as Th1 and Th17, while promoting the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [52, 53].

Moreover, recent evidence suggests that MSC‐EVs can modulate the detection thresholds of the innate immune system by regulating pattern recognition receptor signaling and early inflammatory activation cascades, thereby shaping the initial immune response to the peri‐implant microbial challenge [54]. MSC‐EVs functionally interact with Toll‐like receptors (TLRs), contributing to the coordination between innate and adaptive immunity, promoting macrophage polarization toward anti‐inflammatory phenotypes, and finely modulating T cell activation [54, 55]. This mechanism indicates an advanced level of immune control that may be key to limiting excessive inflammation and fostering a peri‐implant microenvironment more conducive to tissue regeneration, although further studies are needed to precisely elucidate the molecular interactions involved.

Emerging evidence directly implicates EV‐mediated communication in the tissue response around titanium implants. Macrophage‐derived EVs are efficiently internalized by MSCs and can dictate their inflammatory or reparative programming [56]. This provides a direct mechanistic link between immune cell‐derived EVs and the behavior of reparative cells at the implant interface, underscoring the translational potential of EV‐based immunomodulation for peri‐implantitis.

Based on this framework, the emerging concept of “exosomal indices” allows extrapolation of findings from periodontitis to peri‐implantitis. In periodontitis patients, disease severity has been associated with a pathological exosomal profile, characterized by reduced CD81, Immune Index, and IL‐10 levels, indicating exhaustion of immunoregulatory mechanisms in advanced stages [57]. It is plausible that a similar imbalance occurs in peri‐implantitis, representing a potential target for MSC‐EV‐based therapies aimed at restoring local homeostasis.

Finally, the relevance of MSC‐EVs extends beyond modulation of suspended immune cells to direct interactions with implants. In a rat tibial osseointegration model, the study by Lei Fan et al. provides direct evidence of this effect through the functionalization of polyetheretherketone (PEEK) surfaces. Incorporation of EVs into sulfonated PEEK transformed its surface from hydrophobic to highly hydrophilic, significantly reducing the contact angle, which in vivo translated into a marked reduction in fibrous capsule formation and a substantial increase in bone‐implant contact. These findings underscore that MSC‐EVs not only act as immunomodulatory agents but also enhance the biocompatibility and osteointegration of biomaterials, a particularly relevant aspect for regenerative treatment of peri‐implantitis [58].

EV‐Driven Immunometabolic Reprogramming in Peri‐Implant Tissue: A Metabolic Shift From Chronic Inflammation to Tissue Repair

Building on the immunomodulatory effects described above, EVs are key regulators of immune metabolism and microenvironmental remodeling in chronic inflammatory settings, including peri‐implantitis. Although direct mechanistic evidence in peri‐implantitis remains limited, insights from oncology, metabolic disease, and other inflammatory models provide a relevant conceptual framework suggesting that EV‐mediated immunometabolic regulation may also operate in peri‐implant tissues.

Growing evidence indicates that extracellular vesicles can sustain inflammatory responses or promote their resolution, depending on their cellular origin, molecular cargo, and target cell context. This dual capacity positions EVs as mediators of a metabolic transition from chronic inflammation to tissue repair and regeneration [59] in peri‐implant tissue. Unlike the immune phenotype modulation described in Section 3.1, this process is largely mediated through metabolic pathway reprogramming within immune cells, affecting energy production, substrate utilization, and biosynthetic capacity. This shift is dynamic and progressively reshapes immune cell function and tissue behavior during peri‐implant inflammation and healing.

Molecular Basis of EV‐Mediated Metabolic Reprogramming

EVs are intercellular vesicles carrying bioactive cargo that reshapes immune cell metabolism. This cargo includes proteins, enzymes, lipids, and nucleic acids such as mRNAs, miRNAs, long non‐coding RNAs, and DNA fragments. Upon uptake by recipient immune cells, EVs modify intracellular metabolic pathways, resulting in sustained changes in cellular activation state and functional phenotype [60]. Experimental evidence from cancer and inflammatory disease models demonstrates that EV transfer modulates key metabolic processes, including glycolysis, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid oxidation, and amino acid metabolism, reprogramming immune cell bioenergetics [61]. While these mechanisms have been extensively characterized in other biological contexts, their direct investigation in peri‐implantitis remains scarce. Nevertheless, the conserved nature of these metabolic pathways supports the hypothesis that similar EV‐driven metabolic reprogramming may contribute to immune regulation in peri‐implant tissues.

Studies in oncology and inflammatory disease models show that EVs induce metabolic rewiring in immune cells, including macrophages, T lymphocytes, and dendritic cells [62]. In cancer, tumor‐derived EVs promote glycolysis‐dominant immunosuppressive phenotypes, whereas in a regenerative context related pathways support anti‐inflammatory and tissue‐repair metabolic profiles. Thus, EVs act as regulators of the immune metabolic state.

At the molecular level, EVs influence metabolic and inflammatory pathways, including PI3K‐AKT, NF‐κB, and JAK–STAT cascades [63]. EV miRNAs targeting PTEN and HIF‐1α–related networks shift the balance between glycolytic and mitochondrial respiration [64]. EVs also modulate AMPK and mTOR signaling, integrating nutrient sensing with immune activation and favoring anti‐inflammatory cellular states.

Macrophage Polarization and the Glycolysis–OXPHOS Transition

Macrophages are key targets of EV‐mediated immunometabolic regulation. M1‐like macrophages rely on aerobic glycolysis, whereas M2‐like macrophages depend on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation [65]. EVs from mesenchymal stromal cells or reparative immune cells shift macrophages from glycolytic M1 toward oxidative M2 phenotypes [66]. Although these mechanisms have not yet been directly demonstrated in peri‐implant lesions, macrophage polarization represents a central feature of peri‐implant immune responses, suggesting that EV‐mediated metabolic shifts may plausibly contribute to the regulation of inflammation and tissue repair in this context.

This effect, observed in multiple models, is mediated by miRNA cargo that activates PI3K/AKT and suppresses NF‐κB signaling [44]. The shift reduces TNF‐α and IL‐6 expression and promotes inflammation resolution and tissue repair [44, 66]. Mitochondrial reprogramming also enhances macrophage survival and pro‐resolving mediator production, contributing to the long‐term stability of the reparative phenotype [67, 68]. Overall, EV‐driven macrophage reprogramming toward anti‐inflammatory and pro‐regenerative states is well supported [69].

Lipid Metabolism Remodeling Through EV Cargo

Beyond glucose metabolism, EVs regulate lipid handling in immune and stroma cells by transferring bioactive lipids, enzymes, and regulatory RNAs. They function as active vehicles of lipid metabolic reprogramming, functioning simultaneously as carriers of signaling molecules and metabolites [70, 71]. Most of these observations originate from oncology, metabolic disease, or vascular inflammation models; however, they provide mechanistic insight into how EV‐mediated lipid signaling could influence immune and stromal cell metabolism in peri‐implant tissues.

EV cargo reprograms fatty‐acid oxidation (FAO), lipogenesis, and cholesterol turnover [72], modulating FAO, cholesterol biosynthesis, and adipogenic signaling [71]. Tumor‐ and adipose‐derived EVs enhance FAO in immune and stromal populations [70, 71]. Conversely, stem cell–derived EVs restore lipid homeostasis by activating AMPK signaling and promoting PPARα/CPT‐1A‐mediated FAO while suppressing SREBP‐1c and FASN pathways [73].

In inflammatory contexts, lipid cargo exerts bidirectional effects. Hepatocyte‐ or macrophage‐derived EVs carrying miR‐434‐3p or ox‐LDL‐associated cargo promote pro‐inflammatory macrophage activation and disrupt local lipid metabolism, thereby amplifying chronic vascular and adipose tissue inflammation [74]. In contrast, mesenchymal stromal cell‐derived EVs enriched in miR‐21a‐5p and let‐7 family members promote M2 polarization and improve lipid handling in vascular and hepatic tissues [74, 75]. Adipocyte‐derived EV miR‐34a suppresses M2 polarization and enhances pro‐inflammatory macrophage activation, further underscoring the context‐dependent nature of EV‐mediated immunometabolic regulation [76].

In inflamed peri‐implant tissues, coordinated regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism may help balance immune activation with regenerative demands, supporting re‐osteointegration and tissue stability [77].

Spatiotemporal Regulation of Immune Responses by EVs

EVs coordinate immune responses, extracellular matrices, and transmitting regulatory signals locally and at a distance [59, 70, 78]. Their sustained release enables long‐term immune coordination, particularly in chronic inflammation.

Immune cell‐derived EVs regulate macrophage polarization and T‐cell activation, shifting the balance between TNF‐α/IL‐6 and IL‐10/TGF‐β [44, 71, 79]. Sustained EV signaling reshapes cytokine and metabolic landscapes toward anti‐inflammatory conditions [65, 66, 79].

In peri‐implantitis, where persistent microbial stimuli drive chronic inflammation, EV‐mediated immunometabolic coordination may facilitate transition toward reparative responses and long‐term tissue stability. Targeting these pathways may enhance immune homeostasis and regeneration.

Integrated Perspective and Translational Implications

EV immunometabolism links inflammatory tone, cellular metabolism, and tissue repair. Accumulating evidence from multiple biological systems supports EVs as central regulators of immunometabolic reprogramming [80]. Although peri‐implant–specific studies are still limited, these findings provide a mechanistic framework suggesting that EV‐mediated metabolic regulation may represent an important component of peri‐implant immune dynamics. By modulating macrophage polarization and metabolic pathways, they support the transition from chronic inflammation to repair.

From a therapeutic standpoint, engineering EV cargo, surface markers, and delivery kinetics offers opportunities for targeted immunoregenerative strategies. Approaches include loading anti‐inflammatory or metabolic modulators and enhancing tissue specificity. These strategies aim to restore immune homeostasis and enhance regeneration in chronic inflammatory settings [81].

Innate Immune Sensing Modulation: Pattern Recognition Receptors, TLR Signaling, and Inflammasome Regulation

Regulation of TLR (TLR2/TLR4) Signaling Pathways

TLRs are the main interface between peri‐implant tissues and microbial pathogen‐associated molecular patterns, initiating and amplifying innate immune responses. Among these receptors, TLR2 and TLR4 are particularly relevant in peri‐implant inflammation, as they detect bacterial lipoproteins and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) derived from oral biofilms, activate MyD88‐dependent signaling pathways, induce NF‐κB nuclear translocation, and drive the production of pro‐inflammatory mediators such as TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 [77].

MSC‐EVs fine‐tune TLR‐mediated immune activation by modulating receptor abundance and downstream signaling rather than broadly suppressing innate immunity. Stem cell‐derived EVs interact with TLR signaling machinery through their miRNA cargo, enabling context‐dependent regulation of microbial responses [55].

Experimental models show that MSC‐EVs reduce TLR4 surface expression in LPS‐stimulated macrophages, attenuating receptor sensitivity and limiting excessive inflammatory signaling. This effect involves transfer of miR‐146a and miR‐181b, targeting TRAF6 and IRAK1 within the TLR cascade and constraining MyD88‐dependent NF‐κB activation [55, 82].

Thus, EV‐mediated regulation dampens cytokine amplification while preserving antimicrobial competence, a relevant mechanism in peri‐implant niches where persistent microbial and biomaterial‐derived signals sustain chronic inflammation and progressive bone loss [83, 84].

The immunomodulatory capacity of MSC‐EVs depends on parental cell priming. EVs from TNF‐α–preconditioned gingival MSCs more effectively modulate TLR‐driven macrophage responses, promote M2 polarization, and suppress inflammatory bone loss, consistent with MSC polarization concepts [85, 86].

By mitigating sustained TLR4‐driven signaling, MSC‐EVs favor transition from M1 toward reparative M2 phenotypes, linking innate immune sensing to osteoimmune remodeling and peri‐implant bone healing [87].

Modulation of NF‐κB and MyD88‐Dependent Inflammatory Cascades

The MyD88‐dependent NF‐κB pathway is a central mediator of inflammatory bone destruction in peri‐implantitis. Activation of TLR2 and TLR4 by LPS or implant‐derived debris recruits MyD88 and activates NF‐κB, inducing TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β in macrophages and osteoclast precursors and promoting osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption [88].

EVs from activated monocytes or M1‐polarized macrophages amplify this inflammatory cascade via miR‐155 [89] and dysregulated miR‐146a [90], enhancing TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB signaling in endothelial and osteoblast‐lineage cells. This upregulates ICAM‐1, VCAM‐1, and CCL2 [89] and sustains a pro‐osteoclastogenic microenvironment through increased RANKL and impaired osteoblast function [91].

Conversely, umbilical cord blood‐derived EVs attenuate inflammation and bone resorption by delivering miR‐182‐5p, directly targeting the MyD88/NF‐κB axis and reducing TNF‐α and IL‐1β while enhancing osteogenic activity [92]. Other miRNAs, including miR‐146a, miR‐21, miR‐181c, and antagonists of miR‐155, suppress MyD88, IRAK1, TRAF6, or IKKβ, thereby inhibiting NF‐κB activation and reducing pro‐inflammatory cytokine expression [82].

At the cellular level, NF‐κB inhibition promotes macrophage polarization toward an M2‐like phenotype with increased IL‐10 and TGF‐β and reduced pro‐inflammatory mediators, restoring immune homeostasis in peri‐implant tissue [82, 93]. Suppression of NF‐κB also limits RANKL expression and osteoclast differentiation while relieving repression of Wnt/β‐catenin and BMP/Smad osteogenic pathways [24].

Inflammasome Activation Control: NLRP3 and IL‐1β Axis

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key driver of chronic inflammation and peri‐implant bone loss characteristics [94]. Activated by LPS, titanium particles, and damage‐associated molecular patterns, it assembles with ASC and pro–caspase‐1, leading to IL‐1β maturation, pyroptosis, and osteoclast‐driven bone resorption [95]. Peri‐implantitis biopsies show elevated NLRP3, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β expression correlated with inflammatory infiltrate and probing depth [94].

Human alveolar bone–derived mesenchymal stem cells (hABSCs) also activate NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes [96]. LPS induces NLRP3, CASP1, IL‐1β, and AIM2 expression. Titanium ions alone weakly activate IL‐1β but synergize with LPS to markedly enhance inflammasome activation, with NLRP3 predominating over AIM2 [97].

Regulation of Neutrophil Activity and NETosis in Peri‐Implant Inflammation

Neutrophil Recruitment and Chemotactic Modulation

Neutrophils are the predominant acute inflammatory cells at peri‐implant sites [98], rapidly recruited through chemotactic mediators such as IL‐8, C5a, and leukotriene B4 [99]. Subcrestal implant–abutment interfaces show higher neutrophil densities than supracrestal designs, correlating with increased peri‐implant bone loss [100]. In peri‐implantitis, their relative proportion may decrease [101], but absolute numbers and transmigration increase, elevating IL‐1β and TNF‐α [102, 103].

Titanium particles disrupt chemotactic directionality and velocity [22, 104], impairing pathogen clearance and sustaining chronic peri‐implant inflammation. Simultaneously, nanoparticles enhance priming, ROS production, and chemotactic sensitivity, resulting in exaggerated yet inefficient recruitment that amplifies tissue damage [105].

Control of ROS Production

In peri‐implant lesions, neutrophils generate excessive ROS through NADPH oxidase and mitochondrial pathways in response to LPS and titanium particles [106]. M1 macrophages amplify oxidative stress through TLR4 signaling and cytokines (TNF‐α and IL‐1β), with upregulation of NOX4 in diseased tissues [106, 107].

Endogenous antioxidants are often insufficient [108], leading to oxidative stress that disrupts extracellular matrix remodeling, impairs bone homeostasis, and promotes bone loss. Hypoxia may influence ferroptosis‐related ROS pathways, but sustained inflammation maintains redox imbalance [109].

Inhibition of NET Formation and Tissue Damage Propagation

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) trap pathogens but induce fibroblasts pyroptosis (NLRP3, caspase‐1, gasdermin D, and IL‐1β), amplifying inflammation and bone loss [110]. DNase I degrades NETs and reverses pyroptosis in vitro [110].

PAD4 deficiency or pharmacological NET resolution prevents fibrosis and improves osseointegration [111]. Therapeutically, DNase I reduces NET accumulation and fibrotic tissue formation in vivo [112]. Nano‐based NET inhibitors and Resolvin D2 (RvD2) further suppress NET‐driven inflammation [113, 114].

Adaptive Immune Modulation: Treg–Th17 Balance and Osteoimmune Crosstalk

Suppression of Pro‐Inflammatory Th1/Th17 Responses

Peri‐implantitis shows excessive Th1/Th17 activation [115]. Th1 (IFN‐γ) sustains macrophage activation [116], while Th17/IL‐17 promotes RANKL expression, neutrophil recruitment, and osteoblast dysfunction [117].

MSC‐EVs suppress T‐bet (for Th1) and RORγt (for Th17), reducing IFN‐γ, IL‐17, and TNF‐α [118, 119], selectively dampening osteoclastogenic pathways.

Expansion of Regulatory T Cells (Tregs)

Tregs are central to immune tolerance, expressing the transcription factor FoxP3 and secreting IL‐10 and TGF‐β [120]. EVs enhance Treg differentiation from naïve T cells and strengthen the suppressive capacity of existing Tregs. This is achieved through the transfer of molecules that activate and sustain the Treg genetic program [121].

The resulting expansion of the Treg population establishes an immunoregulatory tone within the peri‐implant environment, which suppresses neighboring Th1 and Th17 cells, controls antigen‐presenting cell activation, and creates a cytokine milieu conducive to resolution and repair [122].

Impact on RANKL Expression and Osteoclastogenic Signaling

The most direct osteoimmune link of this T‐cell shift involves the RANKL/RANK/OPG axis. Th17 cells are a significant cellular source of RANKL. By suppressing Th17 activity, MSC‐EVs reduce a key driver of osteoclastogenesis [117]. Conversely, the expanded Tregs secrete cytokines like TGF‐β, which inhibit osteoclast differentiation and may influence stromal cells to produce more OPG [123].

Furthermore, MSC‐EVs can be internalized by osteoblasts and stromal cells, directly modulating their gene expression to favor OPG production over RANKL [124].

Adaptive Immunity as a Regulator of Peri‐Implant Bone Homeostasis

Adaptive immunity is a central regulator of peri‐implant bone loss that can be therapeutically steered. The EV‐mediated reprogramming from a Th1/Th17‐dominant to a Treg‐dominant landscape transforms the peri‐implant microenvironment. This shift reduces the production of osteoclastogenic cytokines, increases anti‐inflammatory factors, and restores the RANKL/OPG balance.

The net effect is suppression of bone resorption and enhancement of osteoblast differentiation and matrix mineralization [125]. In this way, MSC‐EVs leverage the adaptive immune system to establish stable homeostasis that supports bone regeneration and long‐term implant integration [126].

Active Resolution of Inflammation: Pro‐Resolving Immune Pathways Induced by MSC‐EVs

Resolution Versus Immunosuppression: Conceptual Framework

A critical distinction exists between immunosuppression and active resolution. Immunosuppression broadly dampens immune cell activity, which can leave inflammatory stimuli unresolved, impair microbial clearance, and may result in fibrosis or incomplete resolution [127].

In contrast, active resolution is a coordinated, energy‐dependent process that involves the termination of neutrophil recruitment, the non‐inflammatory clearance of apoptotic cells and debris, and the active reprogramming of the immune milieu toward repair [128]. MSC‐EVs promote this outcome. They act as signal conductors that initiate a pre‐programmed sequence of events, shutting down pro‐inflammatory gene programs while simultaneously activating the biosynthesis of specialized pro‐resolving mediators (SPMs) and reparative cytokines, thereby guiding the inflammatory response to a controlled resolution [129].

Induction of Anti‐Inflammatory Cytokines (IL‐10, TGF‐β)

MSC‐EVs contribute to resolution by inducing the sustained production of key anti‐inflammatory and pro‐reparative cytokines, notably IL‐10 and TGF‐β [66]. IL‐10 strongly inhibits the production of Th1/Th17 cytokines and enhances macrophage efferocytic capacity [130]. TGF‐β is a key regulator of tissue repair, driving the differentiation of fibroblasts into matrix‐producing myofibroblasts and inducing the generation of Tregs [131].

EVs deliver molecular cargos that instruct macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells to shift their secretory profile toward these resolution‐phase cytokines, establishing a self‐reinforcing healing loop [132].

Promotion of Pro‐Resolving Macrophage Phenotypes

Building on the M2 polarization discussed earlier, the resolution phase involves specialization of macrophages into a pro‐resolving phenotype. These macrophages are distinguished by their high efferocytic efficiency and their ability to produce SPMs like lipoxins, resolvins, and protectins [133]. MSC‐EVs are potent inducers of this phenotype.

By providing signals that upregulate receptors for apoptotic cells (e.g., MerTK) and enzymes involved in SPM biosynthesis, EVs transform macrophages into resolution‐signaling hubs [134]. These macrophages engulf dead neutrophils and cellular debris without releasing additional pro‐inflammatory factors and instead secrete SPMs that actively limit neutrophil infiltration and enhance vascular integrity and stem cell recruitment, thereby facilitating completion of inflammation [135].

Transition From Inflammatory to Regenerative Microenvironments

The concerted actions described above, including cytokine shift, pro‐resolving macrophage induction, and SPM production, drive a major transition in the peri‐implant microenvironment. The biochemical landscape changes from one dominated by proteases, ROS, and pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐6) to one enriched in anti‐inflammatory signals, growth factors (VEGF, PDGF, TGF‐β), and resolving mediators [136].

This new microenvironment supports regeneration by dampening osteoclast activity, promoting MSC migration and osteogenic differentiation, stimulating angiogenic sprouting from local vessels, and facilitating organized deposition of new collagenous matrix [137]. In essence, MSC‐EVs act as a biological “switch”, transforming a hostile, catabolic environment into a regenerative environment supporting re‐osteointegration and functional bone healing.

Direct Osteogenic Effects: Activation of Progenitors and Intracellular Signaling

MSC‐EVs promote bone regeneration by activating resident osteoprogenitor populations, including preosteoblasts, periodontal ligament cells, and alveolar crest cells [138].

The osteogenic efficacy of MSC‐derived EVs has been quantitatively validated in multiple preclinical animal models. In a rat critical‐sized calvarial defect model (a general bone regeneration model not specific to implants), implantation of chitosan/hydroxyapatite scaffolds loaded with human umbilical cord MSC‐derived EVs resulted in significantly higher new bone volume (1.83 ± 0.54 mm3) compared to empty controls (1.06 ± 0.10 mm3), demonstrating the direct osteogenic potential of EVs even in challenging defect environments [139].

The source and preconditioning of MSCs significantly influence EV osteogenicity. EVs harvested from osteogenically committed human umbilical cord MSCs (cultured in osteogenic induction medium) outperformed those from proliferating MSCs in promoting bone repair in critical‐sized calvarial defects, suggesting that preconditioning strategies can enhance EV therapeutic efficacy [140]. Furthermore, stimulation of MSCs with mesoporous bioactive glass ionic products yielded EVs with superior osteogenic properties; on day 7 of in vitro culture, osteopontin (OPN) gene expression in osteoblasts treated with Exo‐mBG was 3.97‐fold higher than in untreated controls [141].

In a rabbit mandibular defect model, dental pulp stem cell‐derived EVs combined with bovine xenograft significantly improved new bone formation scores (4.87 ± 0.78) compared to xenograft alone (2.87 ± 0.41, p < 0.05). Although micro‐CT analysis did not show statistically significant differences in bone volume, the experimental group demonstrated greater bone mineral density and trabecular thickness, supporting the osteogenic potential of EVs in craniofacial bone regeneration [142].

One of the most relevant mechanisms is the transfer of pro‐osteogenic miRNAs. MSC‐EVs are enriched in miRNAs that regulate osteoblastic differentiation. Among the most studied is miR‐21, which promotes osteogenesis by suppressing Sprouty 1 (SPRY1), an inhibitor of the RAS/MAPK pathway, thereby facilitating activation of RUNX2 [143, 144], the main transcription factor involved in osteoblastogenesis. Complementarily, miR‐26a directs stem cell differentiation toward the osteoblastic lineage by modulating the BMP pathway, inhibiting SMAD1 and SMAD4 [145], while also enhancing BMP2 expression [146, 147].

Other miRNAs, such as miR‐15b‐5p, have demonstrated a critical role in metabolically compromised conditions; in a type 2 diabetes model, Wang et al. showed that replenishment via engineered EVs restored bone parameters, including bone mineral density and bone volume, supporting their therapeutic under adverse systemic conditions [148].

In addition to miRNAs, MSC‐EVs can carry osteoinductive proteins and their corresponding mRNAs, further enhancing their regenerative potential. For example, Zhujun Yang et al. showed that EVs enriched with BMP2 mRNA promoted osteogenesis and mineralization in vitro and significantly improved bone formation and bone–implant contact in vivo [149].

An in vitro study by Ahmad et al. provides a direct comparative analysis between periodontal‐specific EVs and rhBMP‐2—the current gold standard for bone regeneration in dentistry. Using EVs derived from periodontal ligament stem cells, the authors demonstrated that these vesicles promote osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells with efficacy comparable to rhBMP‐2, as evidenced by alkaline phosphatase activity, mineralization nodule formation, and expression of osteogenic markers including RUNX2, OCN, and OPN. Notably, under the specific in vitro conditions tested, periodontal ligament stem cell‐derived EVs induced a more sustained osteogenic response over time, whereas rhBMP‐2 triggered rapid but transient effects [150]. These findings are promising but require validation in relevant in vivo and implant‐specific models before conclusions about relative therapeutic superiority can be drawn.

It is worth noting that not all combinations yield positive outcomes. A study evaluating the combination of UC‐MSC EVs with platelet‐rich fibrin (PRF) in a rat critical femoral defect model found no synergistic effect; histomorphometric analysis for ossification area showed no statistical significance (p > 0.05), suggesting that EV combinations require careful optimization [151].

Finally, internalization of MSC‐EVs activates multiple intracellular signaling pathways critical for osteogenesis, including BMP/SMAD, WNT/β‐catenin, and PI3K/AKT [152]. Activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway is particularly relevant, as it promotes survival, proliferation, and differentiation of osteoprogenitor cells [153]. This pathway has also been described in migrasomes induced by titanium nanotubes, suggesting convergent vesicular signaling mechanisms involved in bone regeneration on implant surfaces [154].

Anti‐Osteoclastogenic Effect: Inhibition of Bone Resorption

One of the key mechanisms through which MSC‐EVs exert this effect is the modulation of the RANKL/OPG axis. MSC‐EVs have been shown to increase OPG expression and/or decrease RANKL expression in osteoblasts and stromal cells, creating a microenvironment less favorable for osteoclastogenesis [155]. Part of this effect may be mediated by the transfer of miRNAs, such as miR‐21, which also participates in osteogenesis [156].

Beyond this indirect effect on osteoclasts, MSC‐EVs can directly inhibit the differentiation of osteoclast precursors. In vitro studies have shown that EVs are internalized by monocytes and macrophages, where they interfere with critical intracellular signaling pathways, such as NF‐κB and MAPK, which are essential for RANKL response and, consequently, osteoclastogenesis [58].

The relevance of these findings is further underscored when considering the influence of local pathological factors within the peri‐implant environment, such as implant wear. Wanderson de Souza et al. demonstrated that TiO2 particles released from surface wear can be internalized by immature osteoblasts and accumulate in multivesicular bodies, leading to up to a 17‐fold increase in EV secretion. These so‐called “nanoparticle‐stimulated EVs” exhibit an altered molecular profile and exert negative effects on healthy mesenchymal cells, impairing their osteogenic differentiation and indirectly promoting a pro‐osteoclastogenic microenvironment [157]. These findings suggest that implant wear directly influences EV‐mediated intercellular communication and may contribute to the establishment of a peri‐implant microenvironment. In this context, MSC‐derived EV‐based approaches represent a potential strategy to restore more physiological signaling patterns and support peri‐implant bone homeostasis [58].

EVs and Neovascularization

Vascularization is crucial for implant osseointegration and survival. Areas such as the posterior mandible, particularly in elderly and edentulous patients, represent poorly vascularized regions, sometimes defined as “ischemic zones,” which can undoubtedly influence implant failure [158]. In this context, the effects of MSC‐EVs are particularly promising, as they promote angiogenesis‐related pathways through the expression of VEGF. Furthermore, increased expression of CD31 and endomucin—key markers of angiogenesis and wound healing—has been observed in rats with rotator cuff injuries treated with MSC‐EVs [159, 160].

Complementarily, titanium surfaces have been reported to enhance this process by modulating macrophage responses, inactivating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, and inducing an M2 phenotype, which increases endothelial cell migration and fosters a proangiogenic microenvironment. This interaction is accompanied by additional increases in VEGF, α‐smooth muscle actin, and type I collagen, contributing to vascular stability and facilitating the supply of calcium and phosphorus necessary for mineralization under ischemic conditions [161].

Effective EV administration must overcome the challenge of their short half‐life and local washout. To address this, surface engineering and biomaterial‐based systems have been developed, transforming the implant–tissue interface into a controlled, bioactive release platform. Targeted Delivery Systems and Dosing

Conventional administration of EVs in aqueous solution faces significant limitations, including rapid washout by tissue fluids and dilution at the target site, which substantially restricts their bioavailability and efficacy. To overcome this issue, surface engineering approaches have been developed to physically anchor EVs to the implant–tissue interface, creating a localized reservoir of bioactive signals.

In a rat femoral defect model (an osseointegration model), Li et al. demonstrated the impact of this type of anchoring. EVs immobilized on titanium via the PEP3 peptide promoted the formation of an almost continuous bone‐implant interface and achieved a mechanical failure load of 90.61 N, compared with only 16.22 N in the group treated with free EVs. Sustained retention of the PEP3 peptide for over 36 h suggests that this strategy maintains active osteogenic signaling during the critical early healing phase, resulting in structurally competent osseointegration [138]. Beyond surface engineering strategies, biologically derived matrices offer distinct advantages for EV delivery. Ivica et al. demonstrated that liquid fibrinogen formulations can effectively stabilize EVs and enhance their recruitment of host cells. Fibrinogen‐based systems, which polymerize in situ to form fibrin scaffolds, provide a biocompatible and resorbable matrix that mimics the natural wound healing environment. When combined with EVs, these systems have been shown to prolong local retention, support sustained release, and improve the recruitment and differentiation of reparative cell populations [162]. For peri‐implant applications, such fibrinogen‐based vehicles could be particularly valuable following implant surface decontamination, enabling localized immunomodulation and osteogenic stimulation during the critical early healing phase.

An even more sophisticated approach has been developed by Yang et al. combining physical anchoring via mussel adhesive protein (MAP) with genetic engineering of EV content. In this case, EVs were enriched with BMP2 mRNA, transforming the implant surface into a sustained‐release platform for BMP2. This strategy effectively turns the implant into a “bioactive factory,” capable of directing host cells to execute a targeted regenerative program [149].

Integration With Biomaterials and Scaffolds

The study by Jin et al. demonstrated that M2 macrophage‐derived EVs, when immobilized on scaffolds with specifically designed topography, possess the unique ability to induce the formation of specialized Type H blood vessels [163]. These vessels, identified by the high co‐expression of the markers CD31 and Emcn, constitute a coupled angiogenic–osteogenic niche that recruits and maintains osteoprogenitors, directly regulating bone formation and remodeling [164]. In a critical‐sized calvarial defect model (a non‐implant bone regeneration model), this strategy led to complete regeneration with a trabecular architecture closely resembling native bone within 10 weeks [163].

For peri‐implantitis therapy, this finding is particularly relevant, as it suggests that the ultimate objective is not only to achieve radiographic bone fill but to restore the integrated functional unit of vessel and bone that ensures long‐term viability and stability of the regenerated tissue. EVs, in synergy with an appropriate scaffold, thus act as conductors of a regenerative “symphony” that goes beyond isolated osteoinduction [165].

Li et al. demonstrated that a titanium implant surface coated with titanium nanotubes stimulates host cells to produce and deposit specialized vesicles called migrasomes in situ. At the implant interface, the generated migrasomes activated the pro‐survival and pro‐osteogenic PI3K/AKT pathway in surrounding stem cells, resulting in the formation of a thicker transitional tissue layer and denser, more mature cortical bone compared with conventional titanium surfaces [154]. This approach transforms the implant from a bioinert substrate into a bioactive, inductive platform, capable of actively modulating cellular behavior without the need for complex external biological loading.

Future Directions: Integrating EV‐Based Therapies With Current Clinical Protocols

EV‐based strategies are unlikely to replace current surgical and non‐surgical protocols; rather, their greatest potential lies in functioning as biologically active adjuncts capable of enhancing regenerative outcomes and modulating the peri‐implant inflammatory microenvironment within existing clinical workflows [166].

Recent human studies have provided early proof‐of‐concept for EV‐mediated regeneration in dentistry. In a landmark case report, Estrin et al. documented the first human application of EVs for alveolar ridge augmentation [167]. Using a combination of bone allograft, platelet‐rich fibrin, and a specialized EV subset (Periosomes), the authors achieved progressive bone growth confirmed by serial CBCT analysis at 1, 2, 3, and 6 months, with subsequent histology from core biopsy samples validating new bone formation. Separately, Froum et al. evaluated EV‐enriched solid platelet‐rich fibrin (Exos‐solid‐PRF) for the treatment of gingival recessions in a case series of 27 patients (125 teeth), reporting statistically significant improvements in clinical attachment gain, recession depth, and root coverage [168]. While both studies demonstrate that EV‐based approaches are clinically feasible and can yield promising results in selected indications, they are limited by their design (case report and case series) and do not specifically address peri‐implantitis. Therefore, they should be interpreted as initial feasibility signals that provide a rationale for rigorous, controlled clinical trials, rather than as evidence for routine clinical adoption in implant dentistry.

One of the most compelling integration points is within surgical regenerative therapy. Contemporary GBR protocols rely on bone graft materials and barrier membranes to provide structural support and compartmentalization. However, these approaches are primarily passive. The incorporation of MSC‐EVs into graft matrices or injectable hydrogel systems could transform GBR into a bioactive regenerative platform. By promoting M2 macrophage polarization, attenuating pro‐inflammatory signaling, and stimulating osteoprogenitor cell recruitment and differentiation, EVs may amplify the host regenerative response [169, 170]. Thermosensitive chitosan‐based hydrogels designed for sustained EV release exemplify this strategy, enabling localized immunomodulation and prolonged biologic activity within peri‐implant defects [171].

Another emerging application concerns titanium particle–mediated inflammation. Growing evidence suggests that titanium wear particles and corrosion byproducts contribute to chronic peri‐implant inflammation through innate immune activation [172, 173]. EVs enriched with anti‐inflammatory microRNAs, such as miR‐182‐5p, could theoretically mitigate particle‐induced NF‐κB signaling and excessive macrophage activation [92]. In this context, EV‐based therapies would complement—not substitute—efforts to minimize iatrogenic surface damage during debridement, addressing both mechanical and biologic drivers of disease progression.

Looking further ahead, personalized immunomodulation may represent the most transformative application. Risk stratification based on smoking status, metabolic control, or history of periodontitis could be combined with tailored EV preparations derived from preconditioned MSCs (e.g., hypoxia‐ or cytokine‐primed cells) [174]. Such strategies may enable the generation of EV populations enriched in specific anti‐inflammatory or pro‐osteogenic cargo, matched to individual patient inflammatory phenotypes. While still conceptual, this approach aligns with broader trends toward precision medicine in regenerative dentistry.

Preclinical evidence already supports this integrative vision. In experimental peri‐implantitis models, EV‐loaded composite hydrogels have demonstrated simultaneous suppression of inflammatory pathways and promotion of bone regeneration [92]. Similarly, modification of EV surface molecules to enhance macrophage interaction has been shown to improve therapeutic efficacy in implant‐related inflammatory environments [175]. These proof‐of‐concept studies suggest that EV‐based adjuncts can potentiate both immunomodulation and osseous repair in implant‐specific disease contexts.

Future research should therefore prioritize combination designs that reflect real clinical practice. Comparative preclinical and clinical trials evaluating xenograft ± EVs, membrane ± EVs, or implantoplasty ± EV‐based adjuncts will be critical. Standardized endpoints—including bone‐to‐implant contact, bone volume fraction, and quantifiable inflammatory biomarkers—should be incorporated to allow meaningful cross‐study comparisons. If successfully translated, the integration of EV technology into evidence‐based surgical protocols may shift peri‐implantitis management from a paradigm centered on disease control toward one focused on biologically driven regeneration.

Conclusion

Available evidence suggests that MSC‐EVs represent a promising strategy for bone regeneration in peri‐implantitis, simultaneously addressing the osteogenic, immunoinflammatory, and resorptive components of this pathology. Unlike conventional regenerative approaches, MSC‐EVs act as active modulators of the peri‐implant microenvironment, promoting osseointegration through the activation of bone progenitors, immune polarization toward a reparative phenotype, and inhibition of osteoclastogenesis. Advances in local delivery systems and implant surface engineering further strengthen their translational potential. However, clinical evidence remains limited, and future studies should focus on standardization, dosing, and clinical validation of these acellular therapies before their routine clinical application in peri‐implantitis.
