A major stakeholder in the lobbying efforts: the role of people who use drugs in the past and the current opioid crisis
Authors: Ehsan Jozaghi, Vancouver Area Network of Drug Users
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMass media campaigns have been effectively used in the past to shape public opinion on health. As the synthetic opioid epidemic sadly continues to take the lives of people across the globe, there is little information available on the power of mass media influenced by the advocacy and engagement of people who use drugs (PWUDs).MethodsUsing one of the oldest and potentially most significant drug user advocacy groups in North America, the Vancouver Area Network of Drug Users (VANDU), as a case study, this study reports on the relationship between overdose-related deaths and the level of community action taken by PWUDs. The level of community action is measured annually via news items attributed to VANDU members or the organization's engagement with mass media, as well as overdose death numbers and rates. SPSS version 30 is used for analyzing correlation and simple regression.ResultsAfter conducting Kendall’s correlation, the results indicate a weak but statistically significant relationship between community action and the level of overdose deaths (r = − 0.023; p < 0.001) and overdose death rates (r = − 0.034; p < 0.001). Additionally, a simple linear regression model was estimated. Based on the regression equation, there was a significant effect between the community action level (frequency of news items shaped by VANDU membership), rate and number of deaths, where increasing drug overdose death numbers (F (1, 25) = 6.096, p < 0.021, R2 = 0.196) and rates (F (1, 25) = 6.467, p < 0.018, R2 = 0.206) predicted the level of community action by PWUDs.ConclusionPWUDs’ role as significant stakeholders is often overlooked; however, this research has highlighted that the PWUDs have the capacity and potential to influence, shape, and change public opinion through engagement with news media. Therefore, government, cities, health agencies, and policymakers must engage with PWUDs and establish similar organizations, such as VANDU, to promote harm reduction, human rights, and treatment during an overdose crisis to facilitate shaping public opinion around addiction as a relapsing medical issue.

Introduction

Fentanyl and its related functional groups are powerful opioid medications used primarily to relieve pain. Still, its proliferation has been a significant factor in drug overdose deaths during the past few years, including a reduction in life expectancy in some Western countries [1–5]. Thousands of people have died of drug overdose deaths across the world since the start of the synthetic opioid crisis, compelling some researchers to compare it to the Ebola outbreak in West Africa, or the Vietnam War casualties [1–6]. The World Health Organization estimated that over 600,000 deaths were linked to drug use in 2019 (80% related to opioids) alone worldwide [7]. Similarly, the United States (US) life expectancy rate has reduced in recent years, linked to dramatically increased overdose deaths [5]. At the same time, some estimated that every kilogram of synthetic opioid consumption is linked to 500 deaths in the US alone [5, 7, 8].

Many jurisdictions in the US and Canada, including in British Columbia (BC), have identified the current overdose crisis as a public health emergency to respond to the exponential growth in the number of mortalities linked to illegal drug overdose cases [9–11]. In BC, for example, local, Indigenous, and provincial governments have implemented various policies and innovations to contain the epidemic, including mass media engagement, which has been used previously to address issues such as cholesterol, blood pressure, and smoking [12]. Moreover, in some Canadian jurisdictions, the establishment of more overdose prevention sites (OPS), the distribution of naloxone kits, decriminalization (e.g., Province of BC), safe supply, compassionate clubs, legalization (e.g., Marijuana federally), and addiction nursing education and fellowships have been implemented [6, 13–19].

Many of the above-noted innovations in harm reduction policy have been implemented in Vancouver’s Downtown Eastside (DTES) neighbourhood with a large population of people who use drugs (PWUD) [20, 21]. The DTES neighbourhood has also been home to North America’s largest and longest-running peer-based drug user advocacy group, known as the Vancouver Area Network of Drug Users (VANDU) [22, 23]. VANDU’s formation is tied to the lack of government action in response to illegal drug-related morbidity and mortality in the late 1990s [5, 23, 24]. A board of directors oversees VANDU, which is elected annually by more than 2,000 members [25]. VANDU membership is comprised mainly of people with lived or current experiences of illegal drug use [23]. The implementation and integration of peer-based programs in the harm reduction healthcare model, such as VANDU, reduce risky behavior and connect at-risk populations with appropriate services [20–29].

VANDU’s peer-based program is unique because it is funded directly by Vancouver Coastal Health (a local health authority), with an annual budget exceeding CAN$200,000 [21, 26]. VANDU’s storefront office is currently located in the DTES [21, 26]. In addition, VANDU operated a peer-run overdose prevention site (OPS) during the height of the overdose crisis, one of 14 in the small ten-block area of the DTES [30]. Finally, VANDU is home to many peer-based programs, including but not limited to Tuesday’s education group, the Western Aboriginal Harm Reduction Society (WAHRS), the Eastside Illicit Drinkers Group for Education (EIDGE), the British Columbia Association for People on Opiate Maintenance (BCAPOM), the BC-Yukon Association of Drug War Survivors (BC-YADWS), and the SALOME/NAOMI Association of Patients (SNAP) [31–33].

Rationale and research questions

While VANDU has been actively advocating for the health, human rights, and well-being of at-risk and disenfranchised PWUDs over the past two decades through acts of health civil disobedience, government officials and policymakers have historically not considered PWUDs a significant stakeholder [5, 34]. PWUDs have not been viewed as stakeholders due to stigma, the legal and social framework that historically has viewed addiction as moral relapse, rather than a legitimate medical condition that can be treated or managed with appropriate medical or social program interventions, rather than criminal justice or legal frameworks [5, 35].

In addition to the current public health emergency in BC linked to overdose cases of synthetic drugs, a similar public health emergency linked to illegal drug use was declared in the city of Vancouver in the late 1990s, related to outbreaks of infections, such as hepatitis C and HIV, attributed to syringe sharing [29]. Based on Rhodes’ [36] risk environment and harm reduction work, the risks of the current synthetic opioid crisis or Vancouver’s previous health emergency are not solely a result of PWUDs’ choices, but instead are socially, contextually, legally, and physically constructed.

The risk of the current opioid crisis for PWUDs is attributed to the broader legal and social fabric of “space—whether social, physical, economic, or policy—in which a variety of factors interact to increase or reduce the chances of drug-related harm” [36]. Therefore, this article hypothesizes that the facilitation of the alliances between the harm reduction community (e.g., news reports and coverage), concerned citizens (e.g., activism) and other broader social movements (e.g., potential of mass media changing public opinion), has enabled VANDU to promote issues, laws, and polices that reduce vulnerability of PWUDs, who are ‘steps away from overdosing’, through greater mass media engagement during increased overdose numbers and rates in support of greater harm reduction policy implementation.

Specifically, the following research questions are investigated:Does VANDU mass media engagement and community activism increase as more members of their community, friends, and family members lose their lives due to the overdose crisis (e.g., correlation between mass media engagement, number and rates of overdose deaths)?Are the drug overdose death numbers and rates a good predictor of community and mass media activism by VANDU and PWUDs (e.g., regression relationship between mass media engagement, number, and rates of overdose deaths)?

Methods

Data source

This research did not require ethical approval from a university or institution because it utilized publicly available data on the World Wide Web to obtain the necessary information about VANDU. For example, this research relied on publicly available sources for data: (1) Canadian Newsstream, (2) overdose death numbers, and (3) overdose rates. For overdose mortalities, this research relied on British Columbia Coroners’ Service annual drug overdose death reports [2]. However, a peer-reviewed Study Protocol published in 2022 describes the objectives and goals of this study's quantitative analysis [12].

The ProQuest Canadian Newsstream was used as the source for measuring activism and community action [12]. The critical indicator of interest was the frequency of news publications per year [37]. The Canadian Newsstream was selected over other news sources, such as Factiva, Google News, JSTOR, LexisNexis, or Online Periodicals Archive, due to its potential for archiving various sources, including magazines, videos, and newspapers, dating back to the 1970s [12, 37]. Moreover, the Newsstream via ProQuest Canadian interface provides the most significant Canadian and International database with over 300 unique primary national news sources (e.g., National Post, the Times, le Monde, and le Parisien) as well as some local ones (e.g., the Winnipeg Sun, the Windsor Star, the Vancouver Sun and Daily Gleaner) [12, 37].

Data collection

The primary objective behind using Canadian Newsstream is to summarize the frequency of news publications that VANDU, or its members, have shaped, influenced, or contributed to, as reported by media reporters captured in the dataset [12, 37]. The inclusion criteria for the news items pertained to all news articles written about VANDU, its members, or instances where VANDU members commented on a topic of interest as experts, commentators, or initiators of events, demonstrations, or conferences. At the same time, all the articles and news items from the formation of VANDU in the late 1990s (e.g., 1997) until the end of 2023 were included in the analysis [12]. The search query for the database involved the phrase “Vancouver area network of drug users” to obtain items, articles, and news sources [12]. Moreover, only items written in English were considered [12]. The selected items were reviewed, and each item’s publication date was noted during the final review [12].

Materials and data analysis

The quantitative analysis focused on the frequency of yearly publications on Canadian Newsstream, as well as overdose numbers and rates annually. The data gathered on the frequency of publication and news, as well as the British Columbia Coroners’ Service numbers and rates per year, were entered into SPSS version 30 (IBM Corp). Discussion

The hypotheses of this article were based on the premise that not only is there a correlation between the rate and number of overdoses when compared to community action/activism in mass media by PWUDs, but the annual number of overdose deaths and rates also predicted the level of activism by PWUDs. The results confirmed the hypothesis. For example, there was not only a correlation (weak, negative, and statistically significant), but the number and rate of overdose deaths also predicted the level of community activism. Therefore, mass media activism by PWUDs is directly tied to levels of drug overdose mortalities in the province of BC over the past decades. Moreover, the mass media advocacy and engagement by PWUDs has the potential to affect the risk environment space that Rhodes [36] identified where the risk of harm, which is socially situated and contextually produced, is minimized via greater alliances and advocacy between the harm reduction community (e.g., news reports and coverage), sympathetic citizens via greater mass media coverage.

In effect, Rhodes [36] claims that “risk environment– whether social or physical– in which a variety of factors interact to increase the chances of harm occurring” suggests that drug related harms (e.g., Morbidity and mortality) are influenced not only by physical but increasingly by public opinions that affect the social, legal and funding policies by all levels of governments. It is not surprising that BC, specifically Vancouver, has had the most progressive and science-driven policies toward addiction and substance use disorders over the past decades in North America [41]. For instance, Vancouver not only established the first supervised injection facility in North America but the first heroin-assisted program, the first addiction nursing fellowship in North America, the first province to allow Registered Nurses to prescribe opioid substitutes to unregulated street drugs, the first province in Canada to appoint Minister of Mental Health and Addictions, and the first province to decriminalize small drug position for personal use, the first province to experiment with safe-supply program, and the first jurisdiction where compassionate clubs were experimented [15, 34, 42–46]. New overdose death data from British Columbia Coroners’ Service reports indicate that overdose deaths have decreased in the province in 2024 for the first time in history [47].

Limitation

As discussed earlier, VANDU have developed various activities and organizations on different harm reduction issues over the past two decades and would have been more effective to explore and discuss the harm reduction activities that garnered more mass media attention and should be prioritized or could be implemented in nations or cities that either have strict laws prohibiting sanctioned harm reduction or lack funds and capacities. In addition, the assertion that mass media activism leads to an influence on opinion and facilitates alliances between various bodies may not be fully deduced from this study because it can be argued that the rate of overdose deaths and activism are usually correlated. Therefore, it is to be expected that activism will rise in tandem with overdose cases or rates. In addition, the correlation results could have been potentially more substantial and more positive (e.g., greater r value) if the significant spikes in 2002–2003 and 2008 had not occurred. Moreover, this study neglected to measure the social media engagement of the VANDU organization and its membership across various platforms, including X (formerly Twitter), YouTube, Facebook (Meta), TV, radio, and podcast [48, 49]. Previous research has demonstrated the significant potential of social media in shaping public opinion [37, 50].

Conclusion

Over the past few decades, the science-driven policy changes in health and criminal justice in BC were achieved not in a vacuum, but rather in tandem with the mass and social media activism of VANDU’s members [5, 12, 41]. For example, as many of the above health and criminal justice policies were revised, legislated, and re-introduced, VANDU was actively organizing demonstrations, local/national activism, mass and social media engagements (e.g., X, Facebook, podcast) and scientific collaboration to change public perception, opinions and views of British Columbians [12, 51]. The creation and establishment of WAHRS, EIDGE, BCAPOM, SNAP, BC-YADWS, and numerous scientific collaborations with academic researchers have incrementally changed public opinion, government policy, laws, and funding [50]. Such unique goals were only achieved via local health agencies funding small stipends to hold meetings, community organizing, educational activities, conferences, presentations, and demonstrations in a peer-based, low-barrier model [21, 26]. This research once again highlights the power of mass and social media in changing societal perceptions, norms, values, and public opinions as seen in previous successful mass media campaigns, such as those aimed at reducing cholesterol, smoking, and blood pressure [12].

As the rhetoric, fake news, disinformation, and misinformation against the effectiveness of harm reduction and the scientific evidence backing a harm-reduction-evidence-based approach has gained momentum in social and mass media, funding drug advocacy groups by health agencies and city administrations is the most prudent approach to promote human dignity, human rights and science [52–55]. In effect, organizing PWUDS or establishing drug peer-based advocacy groups, such as VANDU, can be active components of cities, health agencies, or states in combating the current opioid crisis while reducing opioid related harms (e.g., morbidity and mortality) [5]. Finally, the success of VANDU in shaping public opinion about drug policy can serve as an inspiration for other regions around the world (e.g., South and Central America, Africa, the Middle East, East and South Asia, Eastern Europe and central Asia), where PWUDs are stigmatized and thus are completely absent or poorly represented in public or political domains or discussion. Investment in peer-based programs, such as VANDU, has the potential to change public perception, norms, opinions, and values around addiction as a relapsing medical issue, rather than a moral relapse contextualized through the criminal justice system [53].
