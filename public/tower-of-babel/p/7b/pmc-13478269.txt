SNORD50A/B disassemble ASC-1 complex to silence NF-κB and boost PD-1 blockade in non-small-cell lung cancer
Authors: Jingjing Ma, Qingyuan He, Jun Pu, Yan Liu, Xiao Cui, Guobin Wang, Quan Chen, Le Ren, Mengdan Li, Peng Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small nucleolar RNA SNORD50A and SNORD50B (SNORD50A/B) have increasingly been linked to tumorigenesis due to their high frequency of deletion across multiple cancer types. However, their biological roles in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remain to be systematically investigated. In this study, we observed that SNORD50A/B were frequently deleted in lung adenocarcinomas, and this molecular event was strongly linked to poor patient survival. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated the tumor-suppressive role of SNORD50A/B in NSCLC. Mechanistically, SNORD50A/B directly bound to activating signal cointegrator 1 complex subunit 1 (ASCC1), preventing its interaction with activating signal cointegrator 1 (ASC-1) and disrupting the integrity of ASC-1 complex, which attenuates NF-κB transcriptional activity and down-regulates PD-L1 expression. Synthetic Snord50a/b-loaded cationic liposomes showed potent anti-tumor effects and sensitized NSCLC cells to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. Taken together, SNORD50A/B exert tumor-suppressive effects by disrupting the ASC-1 complex to silence NF-κB, highlighting its therapeutic potential via cationic liposomes for treating SNORD50A/B-deficient tumors.

Introduction

As a leading global health challenge, lung cancer has the highest mortality rate among all tumors, imposing substantial disease burden across healthcare systems1. NSCLC accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases, with adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and large cell carcinoma being the most common subtypes2,3. Currently, the therapeutic paradigm for advanced NSCLC has evolved from conventional platinum-based cytotoxic regimens toward precision medicine strategies incorporating molecularly targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)4. For the past few years, immunotherapy has emerged as an integral part of the treatment landscape for lung cancer5. ICIs have been incorporated into adjuvant therapy following surgery and chemotherapy, and approved as a first-line therapeutic option for metastatic lung cancer6. However, the clinical efficacy of these regimens is severely limited by intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms, along with suboptimal response rates, restricting durable therapeutic benefits in advanced-stage populations7,8. Therefore, the identification of novel therapeutic targets and the development of rational combination strategies are the top priorities for advancing NSCLC treatment paradigms9.

Small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) are a group of noncoding RNAs, which can form unique small nucleolar ribonucleoproteins (snoRNPs) through association with characteristic protein partners and play a crucial role in the maturation and function of ribosomes10–12. In recent years, emerging evidence indicates that dysregulated snoRNAs contribute significantly to tumorigenesis across multiple cancer types, extending beyond their traditional roles in ribosome biogenesis13. SNORD50A and SNORD50B (termed “SNORD50A/B” hereafter) are a pair of small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) located at the 6q15 chromosomal breakpoint of the t (3; 6) (q27; q15) translocation, which has been implicated in the pathogenesis of B-cell lymphoma14. Although earlier studies established the role of SNORD50A/B in rRNA 2’-O-methylation, subsequent studies showed that SNORD50A/B were down-regulated in prostate cancer and breast cancer, which was strongly correlated with adverse clinical outcomes14–16. Moreover, a pan-cancer study found that frequent deletion of SNORD50A/B in multiple cancers accelerated tumor progression through constitutive activation of the KRAS signaling cascade17. However, another study indicated that SNORD50A/B deletion inhibited the malignant phenotypes of p53 wild-type breast cancer cells by stabilizing p5318. Collectively, emerging data establish SNORD50A/B as a pleiotropic regulator in tumorigenesis, capable of orchestrating divergent biological outcomes through tissue-specific interaction networks in different cancer types.

The NF-κB transcription factor family, comprising Rel A (p65), Rel B, c-Rel, NF-κB1 (p105/p50), and NF-κB2 (p100/p52), functions through homo- or heterodimers that bind to κB sites in gene regulatory regions to differentially modulate transcriptional outputs19,20. Extensive studies have established NF-κB as a pivotal regulator in tumorigenesis and progression in various cancers, including lung cancer21,22. In addition to affecting the efficacy of chemoradiotherapy, NF-κB furthermore affects the efficacy of immunotherapy, and in many ways, as it orchestrates anti-tumor immunity by activating immune cells, while simultaneously inhibiting the killing effect of immune cells, mainly T cells, by up-regulating PD-L1 in tumor cells23–26. With the increasingly prominent role of immunotherapy in the treatment of NSCLC, the strategic combination of NF-κB pathway inhibitors with ICIs shows promise in overcoming treatment resistance and improving objective response rate in clinical practice.

The comprehensive activation of NF-κB involves intricate crosstalk with multiple oncogenic signaling pathways such as EGFR, MEKK1/ERK, and PI3K/AKT, and is further reinforced through extensive cooperativity with transcriptional factors including C/EBPβ, AP-1, Sp1, and STAT327–30. In addition, multiple molecules can form transcriptional co-activator/repressor complexes with NF-κB, including ASC-131. ASC-1 has a unique transcription activation domain and zinc finger structural element, which can form a functional ASC-1 complex with activating signal cointegrator 1 complex subunit 1, 2, and 3 (ASCC1, ASCC2 and ASCC3), potentiating AP-1, SRF, and NF-κB-dependent gene expression32.

In the present study, we found that SNORD50A/B had a prevalent genomic deletion in lung adenocarcinomas, with this molecular event demonstrating a significant association with poor patient survival. Our mechanistic investigation revealed that SNORD50A/B exerted tumor-suppressive effects in NSCLC by disrupting the assembly of the ASC-1 complex to attenuate NF-κB-mediated oncogenic signaling. Based on the molecular basis that SNORD50A/B downregulates PD-L1 by inhibiting NF-κB transcriptional activity, we developed SNORD50A/B-loaded cationic liposomes for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy.

Results

SNORD50A/B deletion is linked to poor prognosis of lung adenocarcinoma patients

We first analyzed the deletion rate of SNORD50A/B in various tumors using TCGA database, including lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, gastric cancer and colon adenocarcinoma, and found that SNORD50A/B was frequently deleted in multiple types of tumors (Fig. 1a). In comparison to other malignancies, SNORD50A/B exhibited a higher rate of deletion in NSCLC, especially in lung adenocarcinoma, with the deletion rate of 26.62%. We further analyzed the deletion rate of SNORD50A/B in several common pathological types of lung adenocarcinoma. The results showed that, in comparison to the less malignant colloid adenocarcinoma (18.75%) and non-mucinous bronchioloalveolar carcinoma (21.05%), the deletion rate of SNORD50A/B is higher in more malignant invasive adenocarcinoma, such as acinar cell carcinoma (33.33%) and papillary adenocarcinoma (40.19%) (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, our analysis showed an increased rate of SNORD50A/B deletion in advanced-stage tumors as compared with early-stage tumors (Fig. 1c).

To further elucidate the clinical significance of SNORD50A/B deletion in NSCLC, we examined the correlation between SNORD50A/B deletion and prognosis in patients with lung adenocarcinoma or lung squamous cell carcinoma. The results demonstrated a strong link between SNORD50A/B deletion and poor prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma patients, but not in lung squamous cell carcinoma patients (Figs. 1d; S1), suggesting that SNORD50A/B may play a more substantial role in the genesis and development of lung adenocarcinoma.

To further define the role of SNORD50A/B in vivo, SNORD50A/B stably overexpressed H460 cells were constructed using a lentivirus system and verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 2g). Subcutaneous xenograft tumor models were then established in nude mice using SNORD50A/B-overexpressing cells and their control cells. The results showed that SNORD50A/B-overexpressed tumors grew slower and smaller than that in control groups (Fig. 2h, i). IHC analysis showed a significant reduction in Ki-67 expression in SNORD50A/B-overexpressed tumors compared to control tumors (Fig. 2j), which was consistent with the observed growth suppression. These data, taken together, support the tumor-suppressive role of SNORD50A/B in NSCLC.

SNORD50A/B inhibits the formation of ASC-1 complex and subsequent transcriptional activation of NF-κB by directly binding to ASCC1

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that differential genes were enriched in immune-inflammatory responses and NF-κB regulation (Fig. S6). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed that differential genes were mainly enriched in cytokine-related pathways, among which NF-κB signaling pathway attracted our attention (Fig. 3b). To validate the above hypothesis, we systematically examined the impact of SNORD50A/B on the key downstream effectors of NF-κB cascade. The results showed that SNORD50A/B overexpression led to a significant down-regulation in mRNA and protein levels of Cyclin D1, c-Myc, BCL2, EGFR and IL6 (Fig. 3c, d), which was also supported by IHC staining of these molecules in SNORD50A/B-overexpressed xenograft tumors and control tumors (Fig. 3e). The dual-luciferase reporter assays were then employed to further characterize the effect of SNORD50A/B on the transcription activity of NF-κB. The results showed that, compared to the control, SNORD50A/B overexpression significantly suppressed NF-κB transcription activity in A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 3f). Conversely, SNORD50A/B knockdown induced a marked enhancement in NF-κB activity (Fig. 3g). Western blotting results also confirmed that SNORD50A/B overexpression profoundly reduced the level of phosphorylated p65 (p-p65), while the levels of phosphorylated IκBα (p-IκBα) and total IκBα remained largely unchanged (Fig. 3h). In parallel, IF assays revealed a marked reduction in nuclear accumulation of p65 in SNORD50A/B-overexpressing A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 3i). Notably, the observed decrease in p-p65 levels was also consistently detected in subcutaneous xenograft tumor tissues (Fig. 3j), further supporting western blotting results.

Canonically, snoRNAs exert their biological functions through specific protein interactions11. ASCC1, a component of the ASC-1 complex, was previously identified as an SNORD50A/B-interacting protein through systematic screening, implicating its involvement in SNORD50A/B-mediated biological processes17. Direct RNA-protein interaction between SNORD50A/B and ASCC1 was biochemically validated by EMSA, establishing the structural basis for their functional relationship in NF-κB regulation (Fig. 4a). Next, we knocked down ASCC1 in A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 4b), and demonstrated that ASCC1 depletion inhibited the proliferation and colony formation capabilities of these cells (Fig. 4c, d). Subsequently, we performed the dual-luciferase reporter assays to evaluate its effect on the transcriptional activity of NF-κB. The results indicated that ASCC1 knockdown attenuated NF-κB transcriptional activity compared to the control in A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 4e). Consistent with this, ASCC1 knockdown also down-regulated the expression of NF-κB cascade effectors in the above cells (Fig. 4f). To determine the essential role of ASCC1 in SNORD50A/B-mediated suppression of NF-κB activity, we first knocked down ASCC1 on the basis of SNORD50A/B depletion in A549 and H460 cells. The results showed that ASCC1 knockdown essentially abolished the promoting effect of SNORD50A/B knockdown on NF-κB transcriptional activity (Fig. 4g). Similarly, ASCC1 knockdown eliminated the inhibitory effect of SNORD50A/B overexpression on NF-κB transcriptional activity (Fig. 4h). We next sought to explore how SNORD50A/B regulates NF-κB transcriptional activity via its interaction with ASCC1. Given that ASCC1 has been reported to modulate NF-κB activity via interaction with p6532, we first employed Co-IP assays to investigate whether SNORD50A/B overexpression or ASCC1 knockdown interfered with the interaction of ASC-1 with p65. The results indicated that neither SNORD50A/B overexpression nor ASCC1 knockdown obviously altered the interaction between ASC-1 and p65 (Fig. S7a, b), suggesting that SNORD50A/B do not directly interfere with the interaction between ASC-1 and p65 to regulate NF-κB transcriptional activity.

Considering that ASCC1 can form ASC-1 complex with ASC-1, ASCC2, and ASCC3, and there is direct interaction with ASCC1 and ASC-1, we thus hypothesized that SNORD50A/B might regulate NF-κB transcriptional activity by interfering with the interaction with ASCC1 and ASC-1. Using Co-IP assays, we demonstrated that SNORD50A/B overexpression attenuated the interaction between ASCC1 and ASC-1 (Fig. 4i), and vice versa (Fig. 4j). To further confirm the importance of ASC-1 complex integrity in the regulation of NF-κB transcriptional activity, we knocked down ASC-1, ASCC2, and ASCC3 in A549 and PC-9 cells, respectively (Fig. 4k), and evaluated their effect on NF-κB transcriptional activity using the dual-luciferase reporter assays. The results showed that knockdown of ASC-1, ASCC2, and ASCC3 inhibited NF-κB transcriptional activity to varying degrees (Fig. 4l), indicating that the integrity of the ASC-1 complex is vital for the regulation of NF-κB transcriptional activity. Collectively, our findings reveal that SNORD50A/B disrupts the formation of the ASC-1 complex by directly binding to ASCC1 to repress transcription activity of NF-κB.

SNORD50A/B enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy by down-regulating PD-L1

Given the established role of NF-κB in promoting immune evasion by up-regulating PD-L126, we supposed that SNORD50A/B-mediated NF-κB suppression could potentiate immunotherapy efficacy in NSCLC. We first validated that SNORD50A/B overexpression significantly reduced both mRNA and protein levels of PD-L1 in A549, PC-9 and H460 cells (Fig. 5a, b). Then, we treated SNORD50A/B-overexpressing PC-9 and H460 cells and their control cells with NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082, and found that the pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB with BAY 11-7082 essentially abolished SNORD50A/B knockdown-mediated PD-L1 up-regulation (Fig. 5c) and SNORD50A/B overexpression-mediated PD-L1 suppression (Fig. 5d). The rapid evolution of RNA therapeutics has established a transformative approach to anti-tumor drug development, offering patients expanded therapeutic options33. Leveraging this advancement, we designed cationic liposomes encapsulating synthetic mouse Snord50a/b as a potential immunotherapeutic agent for NSCLC. Quantitative analysis via standard curve methodology revealed 65% encapsulation efficiency for Snord50a/b within the liposomal formulation (Fig. S8). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the presence of monodisperse nanoparticles with uniform spherical morphology (Fig. S9a). The comprehensive characterization showed that the physical and chemical properties of Lipo-Snord50a/b were optimal, with an average particle diameter of 223.56 ± 5.23 nm (Fig. S9b) and zeta potential of 48.41 ± 0.63 mV (Fig. S9c), collectively verifying robust colloidal stability for biomedical applications.

Prior to investigating the immunotherapeutic effect of Lipo-Snord50a/b, we first assessed the in vivo targeting efficiency of our liposomal delivery system. Tumor-bearing mice were intravenously administered either PEGylated (Lipo-DiR-PEG) or non-PEGylated (Lipo-DiR) liposomes, followed by the living animal imager to monitor their biodistribution. Strikingly, Lipo-DiR-PEG exhibited robust tumor accumulation, whereas Lipo-DiR failed to achieve significant enrichment (Fig. 6a). Complementary ex vivo analysis confirmed the in vivo observations, with Lipo-DiR-PEG showing significantly greater tumor accumulation than unmodified liposomes, while both were primarily localized in the liver (Fig. 6b). Discussion

SNORD50A/B, a pair of C/D box snoRNAs, have been implicated in cancer progression through its dual regulatory roles. While initial studies established SNORD50A/B as tumor suppressors by inhibiting KRAS pathway in multiple malignancies, emerging evidence reveals its paradoxical oncogenic function in p53-competent breast cancer17,18. Current evidence underscores the remarkable functional plasticity of SNORD50A/B, with its role in malignant progression varying significantly between different tumor entities and even among molecular subtypes within the same cancer classification. However, the precise biological function of SNORD50A/B in NSCLC and its mechanistic underpinnings remain incompletely characterized.

In this study, we found that SNORD50A/B was frequently deleted across multiple tumor types, especially in lung adenocarcinoma. Prognostic stratification analysis demonstrated that SNORD50A/B deletion was significantly correlated with poor prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma, but not in lung squamous cell carcinoma. The distinct cellular origins and genetic landscapes of lung adenocarcinoma versus lung squamous cell carcinoma likely contribute to the observed differential SNORD50A/B deletion frequencies, potentially through lineage-specific regulatory networks involving multiple oncogenic pathways. Owing to the limited cohort size of large cell carcinoma cases in our study population, comprehensive statistical analysis for this histological subtype was precluded, potentially warranting future investigation with expanded sample collections. Notably, we observed a positive correlation between SNORD50A/B deletion frequency and both advancing tumor grade and histopathological subtypes, suggesting its potential involvement in lung adenocarcinoma progression and morphological differentiation. Our further functional investigations established the tumor-suppressive role of SNORD50A/B in NSCLC.

To delineate the tumor-suppressive mechanism of SNORD50A/B in NSCLC, we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis and identified NF-κB as a potential target of SNORD50A/B. The NF-κB signaling pathway is crucial for NSCLC tumorigenesis and progression, which is involved in the regulation of multiple cellular functions, including proliferation, anti-apoptosis, metastasis and therapeutic resistance22. Subsequent experiments revealed that SNORD50A/B significantly attenuated the output of NF-κB signaling in NSCLC cells, as evidenced by marked down-regulation of canonical NF-κB target genes and decreased transcriptional activity, suggesting the inhibitory effect of SNORD50A/B on the NF-κB signaling pathway. The observed reduction in p-p65 levels and diminished nuclear accumulation of p65 upon SNORD50A/B overexpression further confirm its inhibitory effect on the NF-κB signaling pathway. Notably, however, the level of p-IκBα, a key upstream regulator of p65 activation, remained largely unchanged. These results collectively indicate that SNORD50A/B likely functions downstream of, or in parallel to, IκBα phosphorylation. This suggests a potential mechanism involving the influence of SNORD50A/B on post‑translational modifications of p65 or the modulation of NF‑κB activity via a non‑canonical signaling axis. Recognizing the complexity of NF-κB regulation through multiple intersecting signaling cascades, we sought to delineate the specific pathways by which SNORD50A/B attenuate its transcription activity. Previous studies have demonstrated that snoRNAs exert their function by interacting with other proteins, and a series of proteins have been identified to bind to SNORD50A/B, among which ASCC1 exhibits a strong binding affinity for both SNORD50A and SNORD50B17. ASCC1 contains an RNA-binding motif (KH domain), which may serve as the structural basis for its interaction with SNORD50A/B34. When the KH motif is mutated, ASCC1 failed to regulate NF-κB transcriptional activity, suggesting that SNORD50A/B may suppress the transcriptional activity of NF-κB by modulating the function of ASCC1.

ASCC1 constitutes the ASC-1 complex, together with ASC-1, ASCC2 and ASCC3. ASC-1 has a trans-activation domain that can interact with specific components of the RNA polymerase II complex to initiate transcription35. Additionally, through its zinc finger domain, ASC-1 coordinates transcriptional networks by recruiting coactivators such as SRC-1 and CBP/p300 to modulate downstream gene expression programs36. Our experimental data demonstrated that ASCC1 knockdown in NSCLC cells significantly attenuated NF-κB transcriptional activity and down-regulated canonical NF-κB target genes. Importantly, ASCC1 depletion abrogated SNORD50A/B-mediated NF-κB suppression, establishing ASCC1 as an essential mediator of SNORD50A/B’s tumor-suppressive function.

Accumulating evidence indicates that SNORD50A/B exerts dual regulatory effects on oncogenic pathways mainly by regulating key protein-protein interactions, including disrupting the KRAS-farnesyltransferase association to impair KRAS activation while promoting TRIM21-GMPS complex formation to destabilize p5317,18. It is tentatively hypothesized that SNORD50A/B may regulate the transcriptional activity of NF-κB by interfering with the ASC-1-p65 interaction. However, Co-IP assays demonstrated that SNORD50A/B knockdown or overexpression had no measurable impact on the dynamics of ASC-1-p65 interaction, necessitating alternative mechanistic explanations for the observed transcriptional regulation. Subsequent mechanistic studies established that SNORD50A/B functionally impairs ASC-1 complex assembly by specifically disrupting the interaction of ASCC1 with ASC-1. Furthermore, systematic depletion of individual ASC-1 complex components showed that each subunit contributes to NF-κB transcriptional activation, with varying but consistently inhibitory effects observed upon knockdown, demonstrating the functional dependence on intact complex architecture. While our present findings elucidate specific aspects of SNORD50A/B’s tumor-suppressive mechanisms in NSCLC, the complete molecular landscape of their pleiotropic functions remains to be fully characterized, warranting extensive future investigations across diverse cancer types and biological contexts.

Immunotherapy has revolutionized the management of advanced NSCLCs by harnessing and amplifying the host immune system’s inherent capacity to identify and eliminate malignant cells, thereby expanding the therapeutic arsenal for this challenging disease37. To address the clinical challenges such as delayed onset, limited responders, and therapeutic resistance, rational combination strategies have emerged as a promising therapeutic paradigm. Among them, inhibiting PD-L1 expression on the surface of tumor cells is one of the important strategies to enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of immunotherapy38–41. While high PD-L1 expression is a biomarker for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICBs), augmenting therapy by downregulating PD-L1 remains a valid strategy. SNORD50A/B-mediated PD-L1 reduction complements anti-PD-1 monotherapy, which often incompletely reverses immune evasion, by enabling a dual-targeting approach that may enhance efficacy and allow dose de-escalation to improve safety. This approach is mechanistically grounded in the pivotal role of NF-κB in fostering immunotherapy resistance. NF-κB contributes to PD-L1 upregulation both by directly transactivating the CD274 promoter and by facilitating post-translational protein stabilization, thereby establishing a robust immune-suppressive axis26,42–44. Collectively, we demonstrate that SNORD50A/B potentiates anti-PD-1 therapy by suppressing NF-κB-driven PD-L1 expression, leading to enhanced T-cell activity. These results delineate a clear mechanistic basis for advancing SNORD50A/B as an immunomodulatory agent in combination regimens.‌

The past decade has witnessed remarkable clinical translation of RNA-based therapeutics, with siRNA, ASO, and mRNA platforms emerging as the most extensively validated and rapidly advancing modalities in pharmaceutical development45. Currently, commercial RNA drugs mainly target infectious pathogens and monogenic diseases, and many investigational RNA drugs for the treatment of malignant tumors are in clinical trials, which marks a strategic expansion of therapy. While RNA therapeutics hold transformative potential, their delivery remains a rate-limiting step. Liposomal carriers, though clinically validated, require engineering strategies to improve their stability profiles, reduce immunogenicity, and enhance tissue penetration46. To elucidate the potential of SNORD50A/B in the treatment of NSCLC, we developed an optimized cationic liposome formulation (termed as Lipo-Snord50a/b), and demonstrated its excellent homogeneity and stability. Subsequent in vivo experiments showed that Lipo-Snord50a/b substantially enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody in NSCLC-bearing murine models. The observed down-regulation of PD-L1 and enhanced infiltration of T cells provide a mechanistic basis for the improved response to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.

In summary, building on our previous validation of SNORD50A/B’s tumor-suppressive roles in NSCLC and preliminary elucidation of its molecular mechanisms, we further reveal the potential role of SNORD50A/B in immunotherapy (Fig. 8). Specifically, SNORD50A/B prevents the formation of the ASC-1 complex and its transcriptional activation of NF-κB by directly binding to ASCC1, thereby suppressing tumor progression and sensitizing NSCLC cells to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. Methods

Copy number variation and survival curve analysis

We analyzed the copy number variation on chromosome 6 85677294-85677659, in patients with different tumors from the TCGA database, and defined the copy number change < -0.25 (log2) as SNORD50A/B deletion. Based on this criterion, we stratified lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma cohorts into deletion versus non-deletion (wild type) subgroups. Survival outcomes were compared between groups using Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank (Mantel-Cox) testing.

Cell culture

Human NSCLC cell lines A549, PC-9, and H460, as well as mouse Lewis lung carcinoma 1 (LLC1) cells, were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Jukat T cells were obtained from Zhejiang Meisen Cell Technology. Of them, PC-9, H460, and Jukat T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), A549 cells were cultured in F-12K medium containing 10% FBS, and LLC1 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS. All cells were maintained under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

Transfection of plasmid, lentiviruses, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs)

SNORD50A/B overexpression plasmid (pSPARTA-HGU50AB-DSRedExpr2 N3) and control plasmid were generously provided by Drs. Zurab Siprashvili and Paul A Khavari (Stanford University, USA). For transient transfection, plasmids (1 μg/well in 12-well plates) were delivered using X-treme GENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Stable overexpression cell lines were generated using lentiviral particles (Hanheng Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to transduce H460 cells, followed by antibiotic selection. For gene silencing, ASOs targeting SNORD50A/B and siRNAs against ASCC1, ASC-1, ASCC2, and ASCC3 (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) were transfected at 80 nM using X-tremeGENE siRNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The specific sequence information was detailed in Table S1.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

The RNAs were isolated from cells with different treatments, and then reverse transcription of cDNA, and qRT-PCR were conducted as described in a previous study47. The expression of SNORD50A/B was normalized using U6 as a reference gene, whereas β-Actin served as a reference gene for normalizing mRNA expression of the other genes indicated. Every sample was tested three times. The primers were obtained from Qinke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China), and their sequences are provided in Table S2.

In vitro functional studies

A series of in vitro assays was conducted to characterize the functional role of SNORD50A/B in NSCLC, including MTT for evaluating cell viability, colony formation for evaluating clonogenic potential, Transwell assays for evaluating cell migration/invasion capacity, and flow cytometry for apoptosis and cell cycle profiling, as previously established methodologies48. MTT and colony formation assays were also performed to characterize the functional role of ASCC1 in NSCLC cells.

Western blotting analysis

Protein extracts from cells with different treatments were subjected to western blotting following standard protocols, with β-Actin as loading control49. The information on all antibodies was presented in Table S3.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA-seq was performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). In brief, RNA was extracted from SNORD50A/B-overexpressing H460 cells and control cells (n = 3/group) using Trizol reagent (Life Technologies). RNA quality was verified by Nanodrop and Bioanalyzer. Libraries were then prepared with VAHTSTM mRNA-seq V2 Kit (Illumina) and sequenced on NovaSeq. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FoldChange | > 0.5, Padj < 0.05), and visualized by heatmaps. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted for the top 30 significantly enriched terms using the clusterProfiler (Padj < 0.05).

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The protein extracts were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the specified antibodies or the matching IgG, followed by 4 h incubation with 20 µL protein A/G agarose beads at 4 °C for with rotary agitation. Next, the beads were washed with PIPA buffer, and the eluted proteins were subjected to western blotting analysis. The information on related antibodies was also presented in Table S3.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

After corresponding treatments, the pGL3-NF-κB-Luc reporter plasmids (Miaoling Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) were co-transfected into cells with pGL3-basic negative control and pRL-TK normalization vector. After 36-48 h incubation, the luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each sample. Each experiment was carried out in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight with a primary antibody against p65. Subsequently, a fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody was applied for 1 h to visualize the target protein. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min. Finally, the images were acquired using a Leica fluorescence microscope. Details of antibodies used in IF assays were provided in Table S3.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

ASCC1 proteins were purchased from Cusabio (Wuhan, China). Biotin-labeled SNORD50A and SNORD50B, as well as unlabeled SNORD50A and SNORD50B were purchased from Gene Pharma (Shanghai, China). The assays were performed according to the instructions of the LightShift Chemiluminescent RNA EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

In vitro tumor cell killing assay

Prior to co-culture, Jukat T cells were activated with IL-2 (20 ng/mL) and CD3/CD28/CD2 T cell activator (2:100 ratio) for 48 h. Activated T cells were then incubated with target tumor cells at a 10:1 effector-to-target ratio for 72 h at 37 °C/5% CO₂. Tumor cell viability was then quantified using both crystal violet staining and MTT assay.

The preparation and characterization of Snord50a/b-loaded cationic liposomes

Mouse Snord50a and Snord50b RNAs were synthesized from Gene Pharma (Shanghai, China). To prepare snord50a/b-loaded cationic liposomes (termed “Lipo-Snord50a/b” hereafter), DOTAP, DOPE, CHOL, and DSPE-PEG2000 were dissolved in chloroform to achieve a final concentration of 10 mg/mL. The above components were then mixed at a volume ratio of 14:3:1:1. Next, the mixture was evaporated by rotary evaporation at 37 °C for 2 h in a water bath. After the liposome film hydration in ddH2O, vortex mixing and bath sonication were used to obtain an adequately cloudy liposome suspension. To obtain a uniform liposome, the liposome solutions were successively extruded using 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm millipore membrane. Snord50a/b loading was achieved by 30 min incubation at 37°C, followed by dialysis and lyophilization.

To evaluate the targeting effects of liposomes, DiR-loaded liposomes and DiR-loaded PEGylated liposomes were prepared in the similar way. The entrapment efficiency of liposomes was calculated by a standard curve, which was drawn using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. The particle size and zeta potential of liposomes were assessed using a particle size meter. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to observe the morphology of liposomes.

Animal studies

The animal experiments were performed in strict compliance with protocols reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Health Science Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTUAE2023-1052). All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle), with ad libitum access to standard laboratory chow and sterile water. The mice were caged 3–5 per group (same sex) and allowed to acclimatize for 7 days prior to any procedures. For each animal model, sample sizes were determined based on prior experience, which were sufficient to detect meaningful biological differences with minimal animal usage, and then mice were randomly allocated into experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence. The individual mouse was considered to be an experimental unit. The investigator was not blinded to group allocation during animal experiments and data collection due to the nature of the interventions. However, subsequent data analysis was performed by an independent researcher who was blinded to the group assignments. For surgical and imaging procedures, mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at a flow rate of 0.5–1 L/min, maintained with a nose cone delivering 1.5% isoflurane throughout the procedure. During anesthesia, body temperature was maintained using a heating pad. Mice were monitored continuously for respiratory rate and depth to ensure adequate anesthetic depth. Following completion of surgical or imaging procedures, mice were allowed to recover from anesthesia in a warm, clean cage and observed until fully conscious and mobile. At the experimental endpoints, all mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation, and this procedure was carried out by trained personnel to minimize pain and distress.

Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Gempharmatech, Chengdu, China) were randomized into two groups (n = 5/group) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Stable SNORD50A/B-overexpressing H460 cells (5×10⁶ cells) or vector controls were subcutaneously injected into the right inguinal region. Tumor dimensions (length/width) and body weights were measured every 2 days. Tumor volume was calculated as (length×width2)/2. After 14 days post-inoculation, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were excised for further analysis.

To validate the tumor-targeting capability of Lipo-Snord50a/b, we established xenograft models by subcutaneously inoculating H460 cells into the bilateral groin regions of four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice, and mice bearing tumors were divided into two groups (n = 3/group). DiR-loaded liposomes and DiR-loaded PEGylated liposomes were injected into mice via the tail vein. After 24 hours of injection, we performed the in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence imaging with a small animal in vivo imager and analyzed the fluorescence intensity of the tumors and organs in each group.

Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Gempharmatech, Chengdu, China) bearing established LLC1 tumors (5 × 10⁶ cells in PBS, right groin) were randomized into four groups (n = 6/group) when the tumor diameter reached about 4-5 mm: control group, Lipo-Snord50a/b group, anti-PD-1 group, and Lipo-Snord50a/b + anti-PD-1 group. Lipo-Snord50a/b was administered at a dose of 1 mg/kg via the tail vein once every 3 days, while the control group was treated with an equal volume of PBS. When the tumor diameter reached to about 10 mm, the anti-PD-1 antibody was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 100 μg per mouse every 2 days for a total of 3 times. Finally, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were then excised for further analysis.

An orthotopic lung cancer model was established by injecting 5 × 105 LLC1 cells into the right lung parenchyma of six- to eight -week-old male C57BL/6 mice. Tumor formation was monitored using an in vivo imager. When tumors were detectable, mice were randomly divided into four treatment groups (n = 5 /group): control (PBS), Lipo-Snord50a/b (100 μg per mouse, intravenous), anti-PD-1 (1 mg/kg, intraperitoneal), and Lipo- Snord50a/b + anti-PD-1. All treatments were administered every 3 days for 4 cycles. Tumor growth was tracked weekly by in vivo fluorescence imager. Finally, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were then excised for further analysis.

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

The IHC assay was carried out to analyze the expression of Ki-67, p-p65, Cyclin D1, c-Myc, BCL2, EGFR, IL6, CD8α, and CD4 in mouse tumor tissues. The experimental details were performed as described previously50. Information on antibodies was summarized in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was constructed utilizing GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software. Unpaired Student’s t-test and two-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni correction were used to assess differences between groups. All values were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.
