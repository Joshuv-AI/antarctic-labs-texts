Changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate after percutaneous nephrolithotomy in patients with stage 2–4 chronic kidney disease
Authors: Amr H. Abou Faddan, Mohamed Abdelghany, Adel Kurkar, Ahmed M. Moeen, Mohammed A. Gadalla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Objective:Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is the gold standard technique used for the treatment of large renal stones ≥ 2 cm. However, in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), the effect of PCNL remains underreported. We aimed to prospectively evaluate the changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) after PCNL in patients with stage 2–4 CKD.Subjects and Methods:This prospective study included patients with stage 2–4 CKD who underwent PCNL in the period from December 2020 to December 2023. eGFR was measured using the Cockroft–Gault formula preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively. Patients were classified as either improved/stable or deteriorated. Perioperative complications and stone-free rate (SFR) were also evaluated.Results:The study included 50 patients (male/female = 33/17) with CKD who underwent PCNL. The mean operative time was 84.8 ± 27.4 min. A total number of 49 complications were reported in 21 patients. At a follow-up of 3 months, eGFR was improved/stable in 40 (80%) patients, and it deteriorated in 10 (20%) patients. The SFR was 76% at 3 months postoperatively. Factors that significantly affected the eGFR were bleeding, access number, incidence of perioperative complications, stone burden, and operative time.Conclusions:PCNL is an effective and safe treatment option for patients with stage 2–4 CKD with renal stones ≥2 cm. It is advisable to avoid long operative time, excessive bleeding, and multiple accesses as possible in these patients to minimize the risk of renal function deterioration.

INTRODUCTION

Nephrolithiasis affects up to 10%–12% of males and 5%–6% of females, and the number is still rising.[1] It is linked to a higher risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD)[2] and end-stage renal disease.[3] About 3.2% of the patients who began maintenance dialysis therapy had CKD associated with nephrolithiasis.[4]

Causes of renal impairment in patients with renal calculi include renal obstruction, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), repeated surgical procedures, and associated medical disorders.[5]

Nephrolithiasis in CKD patients is challenging to treat. Watchful waiting, shockwave lithotripsy (SWL), an endourologic strategy (percutaneous nephrolithotomy [PCNL] or retrograde intrarenal surgery), laparoscopic surgery, or traditional open surgery are options for maintaining kidney function.[6]

With the highest stone-free rates (SFRs), PCNL is the most successful treatment for big renal stones ≥2 cm. However, bleeding, plural and visceral injury, and sepsis are all potentially serious side effects. As a result, the best treatment must be customized for each patient.[7]

In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), the serum creatinine level has been used as an approximate measure of renal function despite its unreliability. The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) should be used instead. The most reliable method for estimating renal function for medical professionals is GFR. It is economical to estimate GFR using prediction equations.[8]

This study aimed to prospectively evaluate the changes in estimated GFR (eGFR) after PCNL in patients with stages 2–4 chronic kidney disease.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This is a prospective study that was conducted at Assiut University Urology Hospital, Asyut, Egypt, in the period from December 2020 to December 2023.

Patients with the age group of ≥18 years old with renal stones ≥2 cm with stage 2, 3, or 4 chronic kidney disease were included in our study.

Exclusion criteria included uncorrected coagulopathy, active UTI, stones in ectopic pelvic and transplanted kidneys, patients with chronic renal failure on regular dialysis, pregnancy, and patients who refused PCNL.

Informed written consent was obtained from each participant of the study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Scientific Research in the Faculty of Medicine, Assiut University (IRB No. 17200604).

Preoperative assessment included detailed history, clinical examination, estimation of body mass index, urinalysis, urine culture and sensitivity test, complete blood count, serum urea and creatinine, prothrombin time and concentration, plain Kidney, Ureter and Bladder (KUB), abdominal ultrasound, and noncontrast computed tomography (CT) KUB. Analysis of stone distribution and location was done using Guy’s stone score.[9]

Each patient’s eGFR was determined using the Cockroft–Gault (CG) formula. The National Kidney Foundation’s Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) classification method was used to categorize CKD.[10]

CG = [Weight in kilograms × (140 − age) × (0.85 if female)]/(72 × serum creatinine in mg/dl)

This calculation assumes that creatinine is measured in milligrams per deciliter, and weight is measured in kilograms. Serum creatinine and eGFR were measured 1 day before PCNL.

Patients with positive urine culture were treated with antibiotic treatment until documentation of sterile urine.

Surgical technique

General anesthesia was used, followed by placement of a ureteral catheter 6 or 7 Fr in the lithotomy position using a cystoscope or Karl Storz 8 Fr ureteroscope. Then, the patient was then repositioned into a prone position. An 18G needle was inserted into the desired renal calyx using bull’s eye or triangulation techniques under fluoroscopic guidance. The access tract was dilated up to 22–30 Fr using Amplatz dilators.

A rigid nephroscope (Karl Storz 18 or 26 Fr nephroscope) is then introduced through the access sheath. A pneumatic intracorporeal lithotripter was then used to fragment the stones, and the fragments were removed using the atraumatic grasper or nitinol zero-tip stone retrieval basket. Before the end of the procedure, a flexible nephroscope was used in some cases for stones that were not accessible by the rigid nephroscope. At the end of the procedure, a nephrostomy tube 20 or 22 Fr was placed when indicated or left tubeless when there was no indication for nephrostomy tube placement. A double-J stent was placed in selected cases.

Operative time was defined as the time elapsed from the time of puncture till termination of procedure. Site of puncture, number of punctures, access sheath size, and perioperative complications were recorded for each patient.

Antibiotic prophylaxis was provided using third-generation cephalosporins. Plain KUB and abdominal ultrasound were done on day 1 after PCNL, for residual stones. Nephrostomy was clamped for 6–24 h postoperatively and removed after 48–72 h.

The second look PCNL was done 3 to 5 days postoperatively after the first look, when indicated.

The modified Clavien–Dindo classification system was used for assessment of intraoperative and postoperative complications.

Patients were studied 3 months postoperatively by urinalysis, serum creatinine, plain KUB, abdominal ultrasound, noncontrast CT in selected cases, and eGFR calculation using body surface area-based CG formula.

Statistical analysis

Data collected were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For quantitative data, an independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney test was used for not normally distributed data. For qualitative data, we used the Chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests (if one of the numerical values <5). For paired analysis between pre- and postoperative data, we used a paired samples t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test for normal and not normally distributed data, respectively. P value was considered significant if less than or equal to 0.05.

DISCUSSION

Renal calculus patients presenting with CKD are common in urologic practice.[11] Nowadays, there are several noninvasive and minimally invasive options for managing kidney stones, including SWL, flexible ureteroscopy, PCNL, and laparoscopic and robotic surgery.[12] Among these, PCNL is considered the gold standard for treating renal stones larger than 2 cm, with a well-established success rate supported by research. It also boasts the highest single-procedure success rate.[13]

Concerning the eGFR at 3 months postoperatively, improvement or stability occurred in 40 (80%) patients; however, deterioration occurred in 10 (20%) patients. Factors that significantly affected the eGFR were bleeding, access number, incidence of perioperative complications, stone burden, and operative time. Hence, it is advisable to perform PCNL in these patients using single renal access with the aid of a flexible nephroscope rather than multiple accesses. It is also advisable to avoid long operating time exceeding 100 min, and if it was necessary as in large stone burden, it could be performed in staged procedure.

Patel et al. found that at 6-month follow-up, eGFR improved or stabilized in 45 (75%) of the 60 patients in a prospective study, while it deteriorated in 15 (25%) patients post-PCNL. They reported that the main factors influencing the eGFR were diabetes, perioperative complications, and history of recurrent UTIs.[14]

Kurien et al. reported that 13.2% of patients had a decline in renal function following PCNL, whereas 86.8% of patients showed an improvement or stabilization in their renal function. According to their findings, eGFR improved more in patients with mild-to-moderate chronic kidney disease (CKD) than in those with severe CKD. They also found that postoperative complications and the peak eGFR recorded during follow-up were two indicators of a deterioration in renal function.[15]

According to Deka et al.’s retrospective study, which involved 50 patients, at a mean follow-up of 18 months, renal function improved in 24 (48%) patients, maintained in 13 (26%) patients, and decreased in 13 (26%) patients following PCNL. There was no statistically significant correlation between diabetes, hypertension, and postoperative decline in kidney function (P = 0.9).[16]

The mean ± SD operative time in our study was 84.8 ± 27.4 min. A single puncture was required in 36 (72%) patients; two punctures were required in 13 (26%) patients, while 1 (2%) patient required three punctures. As regards the puncture site in relation to the last rib, supracostal puncture was needed in 12 (24%) patients, infracostal puncture was needed in 27 (54%) patients, whereas combined supracostal and infracostal punctures were needed in 11 (22%) patients.

Patel et al. found that the mean ± SD operating time was 120.01 ± 38.24 min, with 44 renal units having a single puncture and 47 requiring multiple punctures. They reported that 25 renal units (27%) needed a supracostal puncture, whereas 66 renal units (73%) were accessed with an infracostal puncture. A total of 91 renal units in 60 patients underwent PCNL in this study.[14]

Despite its effectiveness, PCNL carries the risk of significant complications. These complications include bleeding, the need for blood transfusions, fever, UTIs, pelvicalyceal system perforation, urinoma, anesthesia-related issues, intensive care unit admissions, and potential injury to nearby organs.[17] For CKD patients, these risks are amplified because they often have other health conditions, such as diabetes, hypertension, or bleeding disorders. These factors make them more vulnerable during surgery compared to the general population. Another major concern with PCNL in CKD patients is the potential for kidney tissue damage, which can further harm already poorly functioning kidneys.[11]

As regards the complications and transfusion rates in our study, a total number of 49 complications were reported in 21 patients. Complications were recorded according to the modified Clavien–Dindo classification system. Postoperative fever was the most common complication; it occurred in 11 (22%) patients and resolved with antipyretics. Bleeding occurred in 6 (12%) patients and managed with blood transfusion. A transient rise in serum creatinine occurred in 11 (22%) patients. Prolonged urine leakage occurred in 3 (6%) patients (one of them necessitated JJ stent fixation), and perforation of pelvicalyceal system occurred in 2 (4%) patients. Three (6%) patients developed urinoma. Blood transfusion was needed in 13 (26%) patients. Seven patients received 1 unit of blood, four patients received 2 units, and two patients received 3 units.

Factors that significantly affected all complication rate were preoperative eGFR, stone burden, access number, and access site. The lower preoperative eGFR, the larger stone burden, and multitrack procedures were associated with significantly higher complication rate.

According to Akman et al., 27 (15.2%) patients experienced postoperative complications following PCNL, and blood transfusions were needed in 17 (9.6%) cases. One patient had a double-J stent placement because of a pelvicalyceal system perforation, and four patients (2.2%) had persistent urine leakage following the removal of the nephrostomy tube. Five (2.8%) patients had urosepsis, and intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics were effective in treating them. Six (3.4%) patients of transient fever were documented and managed with antipyretics. Three patients (1.7%) experienced acute renal failure; however, hemodialysis was not necessary. No patient experienced hemothorax or hydrothorax.[18]

Patel et al. reported 46 complications in 20 patients that were categorized as Clavien–Dindo classification. The most prevalent complication was bleeding that required a transfusion (26.6%). Seven (11.6%) patients experienced a fever, which was treated with antipyretics. Ten (16.67%) patients experienced a UTI that required antibiotic treatment 10. Three (5%) patients experienced convulsions in the immediate postoperative period and were managed with anticonvulsants. Urosepsis occurred in 3 (5%) patients and managed by fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, and antibiotics. Four (6.67%) patients required double-J stent placement for persistent urine leak. No deaths were documented in this study.[14]

In our study, there was a significant postoperative hemoglobin drop (P = 0.001). The mean hemoglobin drop was 1 g/dl. This was in accordance with that reported in literature.[1519] The stone burden in our study (4.7 ± 2.2 cm) was larger than reported in literature which may explain the significant postoperative hemoglobin drop as a result of intraoperative and postoperative bleeding.

The median hospital stay was 3 (2–9) days. Patients who needed a longer hospital stay were due to the incidence of perioperative complications or the second look. The hospital stay in our study was comparable with Kurien et al.,[15] Sairam et al.,[20] and Jones et al.[7] However, Bilen et al.[21] showed that the median hospital stay was longer, specifically 5.8 days (range: 1–36 days). Bilen et al. reported more severe complications like colonic injury (1 patient) and sepsis (3 patients). These complications led to more aggressive treatment, affecting nerve level care and the length of hospitalization.

Regarding stone clearance or SFR, we considered patients as clear of stones in the absence of any significant stone fragments more than 4 mm on postoperative KUB and abdominal ultrasound.

SFR in our study (at 3 months postoperatively) was 76% (38 patients). The remaining 12 patients who did not attain stone clearance, 4 have been subsequently treated by SWL (1 - 2 sessions), and 1 has been treated by flexible ureteroscopy with stone free rate post auxiliary procedures were 82% (41 patients), with no factors for stone clearance statistically significant.

In their study, which involved 60 patients, Patel et al. found that 49 (81.6%) patients had stone clearance following PCNL. Two patients underwent SWL, while four underwent ureteroscopy as an auxiliary treatment.[14]

Bilen et al. reported complete stone clearance in 81.1% of patients. Five patients underwent adjuvant SWL treatment, and five patients underwent ureteroscopy.[21]

According to Adiga et al., following one PCNL session, 67.1% of patients had attained stone-free status; this improved to 82% after relook PCNL (10 patients needed relook PCNL), and it improved to 94% after auxiliary treatments (8 patients needed SWL).[11] The SFR of our study was comparable with other series in the literature.

Limitations of the study

Small sample size that included 50 patients, short follow-up period that was 3 months postoperative, and our study was one-arm study; more insight can be achieved if it included control arm from patients with normal renal function.

CONCLUSIONS

PCNL is an effective and safe treatment option for patients with stage 2–4 CKD with renal stones ≥ 2 cm. It is advisable to avoid long operative time, excessive bleeding, and multiple accesses as possible in these patients to minimize the risk of renal function deterioration.
