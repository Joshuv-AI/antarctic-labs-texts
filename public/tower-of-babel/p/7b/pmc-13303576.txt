Comprehensive Chemical Profiling and Assessment of In-Vitro Antioxidant and Antidiabetic Activities of Polysaccharides Derived from Allium cepa and Allium sativum
Authors: Himangshu Deka, Ananta Choudhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPolysaccharides from diverse plant species have been consumed since ancient times to meet human energy needs. In recent years, plant-derived polysaccharides have gained scientific interest for their potential medicinal properties. Multiple studies indicate that these compounds exhibit a wide range of pharmacological activities. This study evaluated the antioxidant and antidiabetic properties of polysaccharides extracted from Allium sativum (ASP) and Allium cepa (ACP).MethodsPolysaccharides were isolated by hot-water extraction followed by ethanol precipitation. The extracts were chemically characterized through phytochemical tests and FT-IR analysis. Antioxidant and antidiabetic activities were assessed using DPPH radical scavenging, α-glucosidase and α-amylase inhibition assays, and an in vitro glucose uptake assay.ResultsPolysaccharides from both species were pale yellow in color. Total carbohydrate content was 76.12% for ASP and 72.93% for ACP. FT-IR analysis indicated the presence of reducing sugars and pectic polysaccharides. ASP showed stronger DPPH radical scavenging activity (IC50 = 85.7 ± 1.06 μg/mL) than ACP (IC50 = 99.38 ± 1.39 μg/mL), though both were less potent than ascorbic acid. ASP also demonstrated greater α-amylase inhibition (IC50 = 61.57 ± 0.86 μg/mL) compared with ACP (IC50 = 72.82 ± 2.51 μg/mL). α-Glucosidase inhibition was similar between ASP (IC50 = 69.56 ± 1.31 μg/mL) and ACP (IC50 = 68.61 ± 2.09 μg/mL). Both extracts enhanced glucose utilization in a dose-dependent manner, with ASP showing the highest glucose stimulation at 758.3 μg/mL, significantly increasing glucose uptake relative to untreated controls.ConclusionPolysaccharides from Allium species exhibit promising potential to be used in diabetes therapy. Further in vivo studies and clinical trials are required to confirm these findings.Keywords: Allium cepa, Allium sativum, anti-diabetic, antioxidant, polysaccharides, UV/Vis spectroscopy

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by high blood glucose levels, occurring when the body is unable to utilize glucose or when pancreatic beta cells produce insufficient insulin. Drugs such as biguanides, thiazolidinediones, and sulfonylureas are commonly used to manage hyperglycemia [1-3]. However, prolonged high-dose use of these agents can cause adverse effects, including hypoglycemia, gastrointestinal disturbances, and organ failure. These limitations highlight the need to explore alternative therapies that are free of side effects or associated with only minimal adverse reactions for the treatment and control of diabetes-related complications. Many research studies have also been conducted worldwide, both in vitro and in vivo, to identify new potential compounds from natural sources as alternative drugs for managing its complications. Over the past few decades, dietary polysaccharides have emerged as a promising area for treating and managing medical conditions. These polysaccharides can be derived from various natural sources, such as medicinal plants, edible fruits, vegetables, spices, and edible mushrooms [4-6]. Polysaccharides are complex carbohydrate molecules consisting of long chains of monosaccharide units that are attached through glycosidic linkages [7]. Polysaccharides are a diverse group of biological macromolecules that have been utilized for various medicinal purposes [8]. Among the numerous classes of polysaccharides, particularly the acidic polysaccharides, which are primarily composed of galactose, rhamnose, and galacturonic acid, are recognized as more bioactive than others. This biological activity may be attributed to the interaction of the polysaccharides with other biological macromolecules, such as proteins and phenolic acids [9-11]. Allium is one of the largest genera in the Liliaceae family, comprising more than 900 species worldwide. Numerous studies have highlighted the antioxidant and antidiabetic properties of different polysaccharides. Both garlic and onion polysaccharides scavenge reactive oxygen species in vitro and enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses in vivo, increasing activities of superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase while reducing lipid peroxidation markers. In animal models, these polysaccharides reduce oxidative stress-driven tissue damage in the liver, kidney, and pancreas, suggesting a role in chronic metabolic and inflammatory disorders [12, 13]. Garlic polysaccharide fractions also downregulate pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 while upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in cell and rodent models of LPS-induced inflammation and colitis. This anti-inflammatory activity is associated with inhibition of NF-κB and related signaling pathways [14]. Additional reported activities of polysaccharide-rich garlic and onion preparations include anti-microbial, hepatoprotective, cardioprotective, and neuroprotective effects, primarily demonstrated in preclinical work [15]. Recent reports further indicate that garlic-derived polysaccharides may modulate insulin signaling, promote GLUT4 translocation, stimulate AMPK activity, and enhance cellular glucose utilization. However, the inhibitory effects of garlic and onion polysaccharides on carbohydrate-digesting enzymes and their ability to further enhance glucose uptake at the cellular level remain poorly characterized. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the antioxidant and antidiabetic activities of polysaccharides from two commonly consumed Allium species.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Materials

The plants were collected from a local cultivator of Chandrapur, Assam, and authenticated at the Department of Botany, Gauhati University (Ref. No. Herb/GUBH/2024/059). DPPH, α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and p-nitrophenyl-d-glucopyranoside (pNPG) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. All solvents and chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade.

2. Methodology

1) Extraction of polysaccharides from Allium cepa and Allium sativum

The collected Allium cepa and Allium sativum were peeled and cleaned thoroughly. 1,000 g of each sample was weighed accurately and triturated well with a mortar and pestle. The meshed samples were then transferred to a beaker and defatted with petroleum ether under reflux (3 × 30 minutes), then dried. After that, the samples were immersed in distilled water (1:2 solid-to-solvent ratio) and heated at 80℃ for 2 hours before cooling to room temperature. The mixture was then centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 15 minutes to remove insoluble material, and the resulting supernatant was concentrated. Ethanol was added at a 2:1 ratio to precipitate the polysaccharides. The precipitates—Allium cepa polysaccharide (ACP) and Allium sativum polysaccharide (ASP)—were washed with ethanol, and deproteination was performed using the Sevag method. The deproteinized samples were freeze-dried at –40℃ to obtain powdered polysaccharides [16, 17]. Extraction yield (%) was calculated using the dried polysaccharide mass relative to the initial plant material.

2) Preliminary phytochemical screening

The ACP and ASP powders were subjected to phytochemical tests to identify major constituents. We performed Molish’s test (carbohydrates), Dragendorff’s test (alkaloids), Keller–Killani test (glycosides), ferric chloride test (tannins), ninhydrin test (proteins), Salkowski test (terpenoids), foam test (saponins), Benedict’s test (reducing sugars), and the iodine test (non-reducing sugars).

3) Total sugar content

We determined total sugar content using the phenol-sulfuric acid method. In this assay, glucose undergoes dehydration in a hot acidic environment to form hydroxymethylfurfural, which subsequently reacts with phenol to produce a yellow-brown compound with maximum absorbance at 490 nm [18].

4) Protein content

For the determination of protein concentration, Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 was used, and bovine serum albumin was used as the standard [19].

5) FT-IR analysis

We performed FT-IR analysis of ACP and ASP using a Bruker α II instrument (low amplitude, 0-4).

6) In vitro antioxidant activity

We evaluated antioxidant activity of natural products using the DPPH inhibition assay, following a previously reported method [20]. We prepared a 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol, producing a purple/violet-colored solution. A solution of DPPH was made by dissolving 394 mg of DPPH powder in 1,000 mL of methanol. We prepared ACP and ASP solutions at 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 µg/mL in methanol. We added 3 mL of DPPH solution to each concentration of extract and mixed thoroughly. We prepared ascorbic acid standards at the same concentrations. We incubated all samples in the dark for 30 minutes and measured absorbance of the final samples at 517 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. We performed each measurement in triplicate and calculated the average values. We calculated % inhibition using the formula:

% inhibition of DPPH = (Control Absorbance-Sample absorbance) / (Control absorbance) × 100

7) α-Glucosidase inhibition assay

The substrate solution was prepared by dissolving pNPG in 0.1 mol/L Na-phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). The reaction mixture consisted of 2 mL of 0.1 mol/L Na-phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), 20 µL of 5 mg/mL sample solution, 50 µL of 1 mg/mL reduced glutathione, and 20 µL of 1 U/µL -β-glucosidase. After mixing, the reaction mixture was incubated at 37.5℃ for 10 minutes. Saturated pNPG solution was then added to initiate the enzymatic reaction, followed by a second incubation at 37.5℃ for 30 minutes. This reaction was terminated by adding 10 mL of 0.1 mol/L Na2CO3 solution. The blank solution consisted of the reaction mixture without polysaccharides, while the background contained all components except α-glucosidase. The wavelength for determining the absorbance was set at 400 nm. Sodium-phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) was used as the zero-setting solution [21].

The % inhibition was calculated using:

Inhibition percentage (%) = A blank − (A sample – A background) / A blank × 100

8) Inhibition of α-amylase activity

A starch solution was prepared by adding 1 g of starch powder to 10 mL of distilled water, heating the mixture in a water bath, and adjusting the final volume to 100 mL with distilled water. Next, the α-amylase solution was prepared by diluting a concentrated α-amylase suspension with phosphate buffer 68. Test samples were prepared by mixing polysaccharide samples of varying concentrations with 0.3 mL of α-amylase solution and 0.6 mL of phosphate buffer. After mixing, the samples were incubated at 37℃ for 15 minutes. The final reaction mixture was then prepared by adding 4 mL of the sample solution, 3 mL of starch solution, and 2 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.5), followed by a second incubation at 37℃ for 45 minutes. After incubation, 1 mL of each reaction mixture was taken from each test tube, and 10 mL of iodine solution was added. Absorbance was recorded at 565 nm using a UV spectrophotometer [22].

The inhibitory activity was calculated using:

Inhibition percentage (%) = 100 × 1 − (A0 − At) sample / (A0 − At) background

where A0 and At represent absorbance values of standard and test sample at incubation time t = 0 min and t = 45 min, respectively.

9) MTT assay

The MTT assay was performed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of the extracted polysaccharides in L6 cells (rat skeletal myoblasts; NCCS Pune). Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 hours at 37℃ in DMEM medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium-AT149-1L) along with 1% antibiotic solution and 10% FBS under a 5% CO2 environment. After incubation, the cell culture medium was treated with MTT solution, and the plate was incubated for an additional 2 h. Untreated cells served as the controls, while wells without MTT served as the blank. After completion of the experiment, the culture supernatant was collected, and the in 100 µL of DMSO cell layer matrix was dissolved. Absorbance was recorded at 540 nm and 660 nm using an ELISA plate reader. The IC50 values were calculated using the GraphPad Prism-6 program. Images were captured using an AmScope digital camera mounted on an inverted microscope [23, 24].

10) In vitro glucose uptake assay

(1) Cell culture conditions

L6 myotubes (NCCS Pune) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 8 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h in standard culture medium. Before the uptake assay, cells were washed twice with KRPH buffer (5 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM CaCl2, 136 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl) and incubated in glucose-free DMEM for 1 h to induce glucose starvation. All incubations were performed at 37℃ in a humidified 5% CO2 environment.

(2) Treatment duration

After starvation, cells were incubated for 40 min in KRPH buffer containing 2% (v/v) bovine serum albumin. Test wells were then treated with 10 mM 2-DG for 20 min, while control wells received buffer without 2-DG. Following treatment, cells were washed three times with phosphate buffer to remove residual 2-DG. Cells were then lysed, frozen, and heated at 85℃ for 40 min to degrade NAD(P), followed by centrifugation at 500 rpm for 2 min.

(3) Normalization methods

Intracellular 2-deoxyglucose-6-phosphate (2-DG6P) in the supernatant was quantified using a GOD-POD assay, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm. Blank values were obtained from control cells not exposed to 2-DG. Glucose uptake was calculated using a standard curve prepared with authentic 2-DG6P and expressed as nmol of 2-DG uptake per well. Insulin (0.1 U/mL) served as the positive control, and metformin (1 mM) served as the negative control [25, 26].

RESULTS

1. Qualitative phytochemical and physiochemical analysis

The polysaccharides were obtained through a process involving hot water extraction followed by ethanol precipitation. The % yield increased with increasing ethanol concentration, particularly at 80%. The % yield of polysaccharides from Allium sativum and Allium cepa was 4.15% and 2.74%, respectively. Initial phytochemical investigations, as mentioned in Table 1, indicated the presence of carbohydrates and minimal amounts of protein in the extracted polysaccharides. In both of the extracted samples, reducing sugars were present. The total sugar and protein contents were found to be 72.83% and 1.1% in Allium cepa, and in Allium sativum, they were 76.12% and 1.9%, respectively. Notably, the Allium sativum extract shows higher total sugar content (76.12%) and protein content (1.90%) compared to the Allium cepa extract.

2. FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR analysis of ACP and ASP showed characteristic peaks that confirm the presence of functional groups related to carbohydrate. At around 3,270-3,280 cm–1, both spectra show a broad and intense O–H stretching vibration indicating the presence of abundant hydroxyl groups and extensive hydrogen bonding typical of polysaccharides, as shown in Fig. 1 and 2. The peaks at approximately 2,924-2,925 cm–1 in both spectra correspond to C–H stretching of aliphatic –CH2 groups, further supporting the polysaccharide backbone structure. In A. sativum, the peak at 1,622 cm–1 and in A. cepa at 1,628 cm–1 were attributed to –COO– asymmetric stretching or bound water, confirming the presence of uronic acid residues. Notably, A. cepa displayed a distinct absorption at 1,741 cm–1, representing C=O stretching of methyl-esterified uronic acids, suggesting a higher degree of esterification compared to A. sativum. Additional peaks between 1,384-1,403 cm–1 indicated –COO– symmetric stretching and C–H bending vibrations in both samples. The fingerprint region (1,200-900 cm–1) showed several strong absorptions corresponding to C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations associated with glycosidic linkages and pyranose/furanose ring structures (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the characteristic bands near 892-898 cm–1 in both polysaccharides confirmed the presence of β-glycosidic linkages. Overall, the FTIR profiles (Tables 2, 3) indicate that both ASP and ACPs predominantly consist of pectic-type polysaccharides (Fig. 4) with uronic acids and β-linked sugar residues.

3. In vitro antioxidant assay

The DPPH inhibition assay revealed that the scavenging activities of ASP and ACP increased progressively with increasing concentration, as described in Fig. 5. However, their antioxidant activities remained lower than that of the reference standard, ascorbic acid (IC50 = 24.16 ± 0.47 μg/mL), confirming the standard’s superior radical-quenching efficiency. Table 2 shows ASP at IC50 = 85.71 ± 1.06 μg/mL with stronger free-radical scavenging ability compared to ACP at IC50 = 99.38 ± 1.39 μg/mL. These differences are statistically significant, as supported by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, which showed highly significant variations between ascorbic acid vs. ASP (p < 0.0001), ascorbic acid vs. ACP (p < 0.0001), and ASP vs. ACP (p = 0.0002). The lower IC50 value for ASP indicates a higher concentration of hydrogen-donating constituents or phenolic-rich compounds than in ACP, which may contribute to its improved radical-scavenging efficiency. Overall, the results confirm that while both extracts demonstrate notable antioxidant potential, ASP shows greater activity, though it is still considerably lower than the standard antioxidant ascorbic acid.

4. In vitro α-glucosidase inhibition study

As demonstrated in Fig. 6, both the polysaccharides extracted from Allium sativum and Allium cepa showed a dose-dependent inhibition of α-glucosidase with IC50 values of 69.56 ± 1.31 μg/mL and 68.61 ± 2.09 μg/mL, indicating their potential to delay glucose metabolism.

5. In vitro α-amylase inhibition study

The α-amylase inhibitory assay showed marked differences in enzyme inhibition among Acarbose, ASP, and ACP. Acarbose, which was used as a reference drug, exerted the most potent inhibition with an IC50 value of 41.95 ± 0.76 μg/mL, while ASP (IC50 = 61.57 ± 0.86 μg/mL) and ACP (IC50 = 72.82 ± 2.51 μg/mL) exhibited relatively weaker inhibition. Tukey’s multiple-comparison test showed that these differences are statistically significant. Acarbose significantly differed from ASP (mean diff. = –19.63; p < 0.0001) and ACP (mean diff. = –30.88; p < 0.0001), indicating much lower inhibitory potency for the two extracts. Moreover, ASP was significantly better in the inhibition of α-amylase compared to ACP (mean diff. = –11.25; p = 0.0019), as evidenced by the lower IC50 value (Fig. 7).

7. In vitro glucose uptake assay

The in vitro glucose uptake assay using L6 myotubes revealed that polysaccharides from Allium sativum (ASP) and Allium cepa (ACP) significantly enhance glucose uptake in a dose-dependent manner. As shown in Fig. 10 both ASP and ACP demonstrated marked increases in glucose uptake compared to the untreated control, indicating their potential insulin-mimetic or insulin-sensitizing activity. Among the tested concentrations, ASP at 758.3 μg/mL elicited the highest glucose uptake response, with a 91.2% increase compared with the control, approaching the activity of insulin. ACP also showed increased glucose uptake, though its activity was slightly lower than ASP at the corresponding concentrations. ACP at 758.3 μg/mL demonstrated a clear stimulatory effect (58.2%), but to a lesser extent than ASP, indicating potential structural or compositional differences between the two polysaccharides that influence bioactivity.

DISCUSSION

The present study assessed the phytochemical characteristics, structural features, and in vitro bioactivities of ASPs and ACPs. The increased extraction yield at higher ethanol concentrations, especially at 80%, is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that ethanol effectively precipitates high-molecular-weight polysaccharides by reducing their solubility and increasing recovery efficiency [27]. Phytochemical qualitative assessment confirmed that the extracts were indeed rich in carbohydrates with minimal protein impurities, as previously reported for Allium polysaccharides extracted in hot water [28]. FTIR spectroscopy offered important information on the key structural features for ASP and ACP (Tables 2, 3). The broad O–H stretching vibrations at around 3,270-3,280 cm–1 are indicative of abundant hydroxyl groups and strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding, a common characteristic of polysaccharides with long-chain carbohydrate backbone structures [29]. Similarly, the C–H stretching vibrations around 2,924-2,925 cm–1 are typical of aliphatic methylene groups, which have been commonly reported in various plant heteropolysaccharides [30]. Absorption peaks between 1,622 and 1,628 cm–1 point out the presence of uronic acids, the hallmark components of pectic polysaccharides. The uronic acids, such as galacturonic acid, contribute to antioxidant activity and enzyme-binding potential through their carboxyl groups, which participate in electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions [31].

A more distinct absorption band at 1,741 cm–1 was observed in ACP, representing the C=O stretching of methyl-esterified uronic acids, and suggested that esterification was higher in ACP than ASP. Esterification is one of the most important structural parameters that can affect gelling behavior and solubility, hence biological functions. Higher esterification has also been associated with increased free radical-scavenging activity due to enhanced solubility and, therefore, interaction with reactive oxygen species [32]. The fingerprint region, which ranged between 1,200 and 900 cm–1, showed strong absorptions that were assigned to C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations characteristic of the glycosidic linkages of pyranose and furanose rings. In addition, β-glycosidic linkage–characteristic peaks at 892-898 cm–1 indicated that β-linkages predominated in both ASP and ACP, a structural feature commonly associated with improved physiological stability and bioactivity [33]. These FTIR results agreed with recent studies that reported that garlic and onion polysaccharides were primarily pectic polysaccharides rich in galacturonic acid and β-linked sugar residues [34, 35]. The FTIR analysis provided detailed information on functional groups and general chemical characteristics; however, this information is insufficient to elucidate the structure and monosaccharide composition. Further study with an advanced analytical tool is necessary to elucidate the structural characteristics of the polysaccharides fully.

The antioxidant potential, evaluated by DPPH scavenging activity, showed a dose-dependent relationship for both ASP and ACP. As shown in Table 5, though lower than the standard antioxidant ascorbic acid (IC50 = 24.16 µg/mL), the two extracts exhibited significant radical-scavenging capacities, with ASP displaying better activity (IC50 = 85.71 µg/mL) than ACP (IC50 = 99.38 µg/mL). These findings agree with the literature, which reports that the antioxidant capacity of polysaccharides depends considerably on the monosaccharide composition, the presence of uronic acids, the degree of branching, and the molecular weight [36]. Indeed, only moderate antioxidant activity was observed in ASP and ACP, probably due to their limited phenolic content. Nevertheless, uronic acids, along with possible conjugated glycoproteins or phenolic residues, could account for the hydrogen-donating capacity, as observed for pectic polysaccharides extracted from other vegetables [37].

The results obtained in the in vitro α-glucosidase and α-amylase inhibitory assays demonstrate the antidiabetic properties of ASP and ACP. ASP and ACP exhibited dose-dependent inhibition, although less potent than the synthetic reference drug Acarbose. The IC50 values of ASP and ACP for α-amylase inhibition were 61.57 µg/mL and 72.82 µg/mL, respectively, and for α-glucosidase inhibition were 69.56 µg/mL and 68.61 µg/mL, respectively, indicating promising potential to delay carbohydrate digestion and, subsequently, lower postprandial glucose levels. Similar ranges of IC50 values have been reported for polysaccharides from fruits and vegetables rich in pectin, further supporting the hypothesis that the structural features of the polysaccharides, especially uronic acid content and branching degree, are crucial for enzyme inhibition [38]. ASP again appeared to exhibit higher inhibitory activity against enzymes, which could be ascribed to its higher sugar content and possibly a higher degree of structural complexity, as reflected in the FTIR signals. Previous studies have shown that polysaccharides with high galacturonic acid content exhibit stronger binding to digestive enzymes via electrostatic or hydrogen-bond interactions [39].

The MTT test showed that ASP and ACP were nontoxic even at concentrations above 1,000 µg/mL, indicating excellent safety profiles. This agrees well with the general assertion that dietary polysaccharides from edible plants exhibit negligible toxicity at high doses [40]. Therefore, such a biocompatible nature could support their further application as nutraceutical or functional-food ingredients.

Further evidence of ASP and ACP hypoglycemic potential was demonstrated in the L6 myotubes glucose uptake assay. Both extracts increased glucose uptake dose-dependently, with ASP showing slightly higher activity. This stimulatory effect of polysaccharides on glucose uptake could be associated with their ability to promote either GLUT4 translocation or AMPK signaling, which are among the well-understood mechanisms underlying the antidiabetic activities of plant-derived polysaccharides [41]. For example, ASP may have superior activity due to structural factors, such as more β-linkages or a higher galacturonic acid content, which have been known to enhance receptor interaction and metabolic responses [42].

Collectively, the results show that both ASP and ACP exhibit remarkable antioxidant, enzyme-inhibitory, and glucose-uptake-enhancing activities, although ASP was slightly more biologically active in most assays. The FTIR analysis suggests that variations in esterification, uronic acid content, and β-glycosidic linkages are responsible for this variation. Overall, based on these results, Allium polysaccharides show potential as multifunctional bioactive agents suitable for developing therapeutic or functional food formulations targeting oxidative stress and metabolic disorders.

CONCLUSION

In the present study, polysaccharides extracted from Allium sativum and Allium cepa are shown to possess notable antioxidant and antidiabetic properties. ASP demonstrated higher biological activity than ACP, with lower IC50 values in the DPPH radical scavenging and α-amylase inhibition assays. Both polysaccharides showed similar α-glucosidase inhibitory activity and markedly increased glucose uptake in a dose-dependent manner.

Overall, this study’s findings regarding the combined antioxidant activity, inhibition of digestive enzymes, and enhancement of glucose uptake indicate that polysaccharides derived from Allium may serve as promising natural biomolecules for the development of functional foods or adjunct therapies targeting type 2 diabetes. To further establish their therapeutic potential, however, more research comprising thorough structural characterization, in vivo pharmacological evaluation, and clinical investigations is required.
