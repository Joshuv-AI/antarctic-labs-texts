Extensive Dysregulation of Phenylalanine Metabolism Is Associated With Stress Hyperphenylalaninemia and 30‐Day Death in Critically Ill Patients With Acute Decompensated Heart Failure
Authors: Wei‐Siang Chen, Min‐Hui Liu, Yi‐Liang Tsou, Huang‐Ping Wu, Hsuan‐Ching Lin, Chung‐Yu Liang, Chao‐Hung Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStress hyperphenylalaninemia predicts elevated mortality rates in patients with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF). This study investigated the metabolic pathways underlying this association and identified a unique metabolic phenotype underlying the association between stress hyperphenylalaninemia and adverse outcomes in ADHF.Methods and ResultsThis was a retrospective cohort study. We enrolled 120 patients with ADHF in an intensive care unit (60 with a phenylalanine level ≥112 μM, 60 with a phenylalanine level <112 μM), and 30 controls. Plasma phenylalanine‐derived metabolites were measured, and participants were evaluated for 30‐day death. Patients with ADHF had extensive activations of the alternative pathways for metabolizing phenylalanine, leading to the levels of phenylalanine‐derived downstream metabolites 1.5 to 6.1 times higher in patients with ADHF than in the controls (all P<0.001). Extensive dysregulation of these alternative pathways significantly increased phenylalanine levels and contributed to a distinct metabolic phenotype, characterized by increased phenylalanine, tyrosine, homogentisic acid, and succinylacetone levels but decreased benzoic acid and 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylalanine levels. Throughout the 30‐day follow‐up period, 47 (39.2%) patients died. This distinct metabolic phenotype was associated with an increased mortality rate (odds ratio, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.27–1.99]; P<0.001). A multivariable analysis confirmed the independent association of this metabolic phenotype, in addition to phenylalanine and tyrosine levels, with 30‐day death.ConclusionsIn patients with ADHF, extensive dysregulation of the alternative pathways for metabolizing phenylalanine was correlated with stress hyperphenylalaninemia and a distinct metabolic phenotype on the phenylalanine–tyrosine–homogentisic acid–succinylacetone axis. Both stress hyperphenylalaninemia and metabolic dysregulation on this axis were associated with poor outcomes.

In recent decades, substantial advances have been made in treating and managing heart failure (HF). Despite these advances, progress in addressing acute decompensated HF (ADHF) remains inadequate. Therefore, identifying novel mechanisms and biomarkers is critical for risk stratification and the development of targeted treatments. A remarkably elevated level of blood phenylalanine (>600 μM) is the primary diagnostic criterion for phenylketonuria, a congenital metabolic disorder resulting from a mutation in the phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) gene. 1 However, previous longitudinal studies showed that, in the community cohorts without phenylketonuria, mildly elevated phenylalanine is associated with the development of new diabetes 2 and cardiovascular diseases 3 and incidental HF hospitalization. 4 In addition, stress hyperphenylalaninemia (SHP) 5 with mild to moderate elevation of phenylalanine levels (from above normal to 600 μM) is observed in patients who do not have phenylketonuria but encounter stress such as severe HF, acute myocardial infarction, or sepsis. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Studies have demonstrated that SHP is associated with poor outcomes in patients with HF who require intensive care. 5 , 6 However, the mechanisms connecting SHP and poor outcomes remain poorly understood.

In adults not affected by phenylketonuria, the pathophysiology of HF, which involves multiple organs, results in elevated blood phenylalanine concentrations. SHP occurs due to tissue degradation, liver and kidney dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and anabolic resistance. 5 Unlike patients with phenylketonuria, patients with SHP can generate downstream metabolites through PAH‐mediated phenylalanine catabolism. However, the regulation of phenylalanine metabolism in SHP remains poorly understood, as does the influence of these downstream metabolites on outcomes in individuals with HF.

Examining all the metabolic pathways associated with SHP is vital to address these challenges. Furthermore, identifying new targets for therapeutic intervention is crucial. The objectives of our study were (1) to investigate the activation of alternative pathways in response to SHP, (2) to explore the relations between SHP and the concentrations of downstream metabolites and identify the dysregulated sites of phenylalanine metabolism in adults without phenylketonuria with ADHF, (3) to explore why mild to moderate elevation of phenylalanine is associated with poor outcomes and to assess the prognostic significance of all metabolites, and (4) to uncover novel pathways for targeted therapies.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patient Enrollment

This was a retrospective cohort study. Patients in this study were selected from the cohort of our previous study 5 (study flow diagram provided in Figure S1). Over a period from January 2020 to April 2021, patients were consecutively enrolled in the cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) of the study hospital under the following inclusion criteria: (1) requiring transfer to the cardiac ICU for ADHF (acute or acute on chronic HF); (2) exhibiting HF with reduced ejection fraction (EF <40%), HF with mildly reduced EF (EF 40%–49%), or HF with preserved EF (EF ≥50%) according to the 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure 11 ; (3) having NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels exceeding 900 pg/mL 12 , 13 ; (4) presenting with an Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score of ≥15 or requiring intubation due to respiratory failure; (5) requiring ICU admission for >48 hours; and (6) being aged >20 years. The exclusion criteria were (1) having comorbid conditions in addition to the primary reason for admission that could compromise survival within 3 months, such as terminal‐stage cancer, or (2) dying before the initial blood sample collection for phenylalanine measurement. Acute HF was identified at admission as a new diagnosis, encompassing a range of acute HF‐associated pathogeneses and acute myocardial infarction accompanied by acute pulmonary edema. The term acute on chronic HF applied to cases with a previous diagnosis of HF upon admission, including chronic HF with reduced ejection fraction, HF with mildly reduced EF, or HF with preserved EF. The identification of prior HF diagnosis involved a review of medical records from both the study institution and any referring institutions.

This study consecutively enrolled 60 patients exhibiting baseline phenylalanine levels ≥112 μM, 60 patients with baseline phenylalanine levels <112 μM, and 30 controls aged 45 to 72 years. These controls had American Heart Association HF stage B11 and received follow‐up care at the outpatient clinic. The cutoff value of phenylalanine set at 112 μM for SHP followed the procedures detailed in the authors' prior studies. 5 , 6 All participants provided informed consent. The investigation adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (institutional review board approval numbers: 201801514B0 and 201801513B0). This report follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Blood Sampling and Examination

Fasting blood samples were collected in EDTA‐containing tubes in the early morning, the day after obtaining informed consent. Plasma was separated and frozen immediately at −80 °C until analysis. Measurement of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and metabolites is described below. Measurement of other parameters, including alanine aminotransferase, albumin, estimated glomerular filtration rate, CRP (C‐reactive protein), hemoglobin, total creatine kinase, lactate, and NT‐proBNP was performed in the central laboratory.

Phenylalanine and Tyrosine Measurements

Plasma concentrations of phenylalanine and tyrosine were quantified by ultra‐performance liquid chromatography (UPLC). Plasma samples (100 μL) were precipitated with 10% sulfosalicylic acid. After protein precipitation and centrifugation, derivatization was initiated by 6‐aminoquinolyl‐N‐hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate in acetonitrile. Amino acids were then analyzed using the ACQUITY UPLC System (Waters Corp., Milford, MA), consisting of a Binary Solvent Manager, a Sample Manager, and a Tunable UV detector. We used Empower 2 software (Waters Corp.) to control the system and collect data. Separations were performed on a 2.1×100 mm ACQUITY BEH C18 column at a flow rate of 0.70 mL/min. The average intra‐assay coefficient of variation was 4.6% for phenylalanine and 4.3% for tyrosine. The total coefficient of variation was 3.7% for phenylalanine and 3.1% for tyrosine. The detection limit was 3.3 μM for phenylalanine and 2.0 μM for tyrosine. The linear range was 25 to 500 μM.

Analysis of Phenylalanine‐ and Tyrosine‐Derived Metabolites

Plasma concentrations of phenylalanine‐ and tyrosine‐derived metabolites were measured by UPLC–tandem mass spectrometry. Sample pretreatment was based on previous study with minor modification. 14 The UPLC–tandem mass spectrometry method was modified according to previous studies. 15 , 16 A Waters ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters Corp.) equipped with an API‐4000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA) was used for determining target analyst in plasma samples. Electrospray ionization was operated in positive polarity for ionizing phenylacetylglutamine, and the others were ionized in negative polarity of electrospray ionization. Scan mode of tandem mass spectrometry was multiple reaction monitoring with the nitrogen gas for collision induced dissociation. Metabolites were separated from the other matrix and interferences by a Hypersil Gold column (50×2.1 mm, 1.9 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) with the composition of formic acid (0.1%, v/v) as mobile phase A and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid as mobile phase B. Quality control samples were run with each batch of samples and interbatch variations expressed as coefficient of variation were <15%. For details, please see Data S1.

Follow‐Up Program

Patients received guideline‐based medical treatment for their diagnosed diseases. All patients were followed until death or for a maximum of 30 days. All‐cause death was the primary end point.

Statistical Analyses

Results are expressed as the mean±SD or SE for variables with normal distribution, and median (interquartile range) for variables with skewed distribution, and as the number (percentage) for categorical variables. Data were compared by t test (for variables with normal distribution) and Mann–Whitney U test (for variables with skewed distribution) when appropriate. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations between variables. Receiver operating characteristic curves and the areas under the curve were presented. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine the variables associated with SHP and death. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to adjust the prognostic value of metabolites by variables identified as significant in the univariate analysis. Variables with a P value <0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable analysis. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were calculated. All statistical analyses were 2‐sided and performed using SPSS software version 22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Laboratory Data

The average age of the study population was 73.5 years. Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores averaged 19.8 and 8.02, respectively. The median levels of NT‐proBNP were 5167 ng/mL. The baseline characteristics and laboratory data of patients are presented in Table 1. Compared with the control group, patients with ADHF with SHP were older; had lower levels of hemoglobin, albumin, and estimated glomerular filtration rate; and had higher levels of NT‐proBNP, white blood cell count, CRP, total creatine kinase, and lactate. Of the 120 patients admitted for ADHF, ischemic heart disease was identified as the cause in 52 (43.3%) cases, and infection exacerbated the condition in 24 (20%) cases.

In the patient group, patients exhibiting phenylalanine levels ≥112 μM had substantially higher Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II and SOFA scores, a higher frequency of ventilator and inotropic agent use, longer durations in the ICU, and higher concentrations of CRP, alanine aminotransferase, uric acid, total bilirubin, creatine kinase, and lactate, compared with those with phenylalanine <112 μM. By contrast, these individuals presented with lower left ventricular EF (LVEF) and albumin levels. In addition, a higher phenylalanine level was correlated with a lower LVEF (r=−0.24, P=0.011).

Metabolites in Patients With Different Phenylalanine Levels

Table 2 presents data for metabolites derived from phenylalanine and tyrosine across various groups. Although the patient group was older than the control group, age was not associated with metabolite levels in the control group (Table S1). In addition, the comparisons of metabolites between the control and patient groups were age adjusted. In the patient group, baseline phenylalanine concentrations varied from 38.1 to 534 μM, outside the normal range of 48.7 to 85.4 μM. 5 Relative to the control group, the study group exhibited markedly elevated levels of phenylalanine and tyrosine. The phenylalanine levels were significantly correlated with the tyrosine levels (r=0.795, P<0.001; the scatter plots were shown in Figure S2). The concentrations of nearly all metabolites derived from phenylalanine and tyrosine in the patient group were substantially higher than those in the control group (6.1 times for phenylpyruvate; 4.0 times for p‐hydroxyphenylacetate; 3.6 times for p‐hydroxyphenylpyruvate; 3.1 times for homogentisate; and 4.0 times for succinylacetone; Figure S3; Table 2). The sole exception to this trend was benzoic acid. By contrast, concentrations of hippuric acid were significantly lower in the patient group relative to the controls. A variety of ratios were calculated to represent the activity of enzymes responsible for the metabolism of phenylalanine and tyrosine. The details of the enzymes corresponding to these ratios were shown in Table S2. The ratios of phenylpyruvate to phenylalanine, p‐hydroxyphenylpyruvate to tyrosine, and tyramine to tyrosine were significantly higher in the patient group, whereas the ratio of tyrosine to phenylalanine was lower. Measurements of maleylacetoacetate and fumarylacetoacetate were not conducted due to the absence of commercialized internal standards.

Individuals with phenylalanine levels ≥112 μM in the patient group exhibited substantially elevated levels of phenylalanine and tyrosine compared with those with phenylalanine <112 μM. As for downstream metabolites, individuals with phenylalanine ≥112 μM had lower concentrations of cinnamic acid and 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) and higher concentrations of homogentisic acid and p‐hydroxyphenyllactate compared with those with phenylalanine <112 μM (Figure S4). No significant differences were observed in other metabolites between the cohorts. Furthermore, the study identified an association between lower levels of DOPA and an increased need for inotropic agent use (P=0.037). In terms of ratios, patients with a phenylalanine level ≥112 μM demonstrated significantly lower ratios of tyrosine to phenylalanine, phenylpyruvate to phenylalanine, phenylethylamine to phenylalanine, cinnamic acid to phenylalanine, DOPA to tyrosine, p‐hydroxyphenylpyruvate to tyrosine, and tyramine to tyrosine than those with phenylalanine <112 μM. In addition, lower ratios of phenylpyruvate to phenylalanine were associated with higher SOFA scores, CRP, total bilirubin, and lactate but lower LVEF (Table S3). Lower ratios of DOPA to tyrosine and p‐hydroxyphenylpyruvate to tyrosine were correlated with higher SOFA scores, total bilirubin, and lactate but lower LVEF. Detailed information is shown in Table S3.

Metabolites Between Patients of Survival and Death

Table 4 presents the data on metabolites derived from phenylalanine and tyrosine between patients who survived for 30 days and those who did not. Patients who did not survive had markedly higher levels of phenylalanine and tyrosine, lower concentrations of benzoic acid among phenylalanine‐derived metabolites, and lower DOPA levels coupled with higher homogentisic acid and succinylacetone levels in tyrosine‐derived metabolites (Figure 1). Moreover, all ratios were substantially lower in the patients who died within 30 days compared with those who survived, except for tyrosine to phenylalanine.

Metabolites Associated With 30‐Day Death

In the univariate logistic regression analysis, decreases in benzoic acid and DOPA levels and increases in homogentisic acid and succinylacetone levels were significantly associated with 30‐day death (Table 5). Subsequent multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed the independent significance of these 4 metabolites, which were subsequently incorporated into a “metabolite score.” Further examination in the univariate analysis indicated that the metabolic score (OR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.27–1.99]; P<0.001) and phenylalanine (OR, 1.13 [95% CI, 1.07–1.20]; P<0.001) and tyrosine levels (OR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.13–1.33]; P<0.001) were all significantly correlated with 30‐day death. In the multivariable analysis models, the metabolic score was associated with 30‐day death, independent of phenylalanine (model 1) and tyrosine levels (model 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that, in addition to the common pathway of metabolizing phenylalanine to tyrosine, alternative phenylalanine degradation pathways were extensively activated, leading to the clearance of phenylalanine accumulation in patients with ADHF. Dysregulation of these alternative phenylalanine degradation pathways was correlated with increased plasma phenylalanine levels and developed a distinct metabolic phenotype on the phenylalanine–tyrosine–homogentisic acid–succinylacetone axis. The metabolic dysregulation on this axis was also associated with adverse outcomes for patients critically ill with ADHF.

Consistent with the results of previous studies on patients with HF, 4 , 5 , 6 , 17 the present study suggested that SHP is associated with inflammatory status (increased CRP), tissue degradation owing to inadequate perfusion (increased total creatine kinase and lactate), and PAH dysfunction due to liver congestion (increased alanine aminotransferase and total bilirubin). Elevated phenylalanine levels predicted HF‐related hospitalization in community cohorts at cardiovascular risk in the PROSPER (Prospective Study of Pravastatin in the Elderly at Risk) and the National FINRISK Study cohorts. 4 Moreover, an increasing body of evidence indicates the detrimental effects of phenylalanine on cells and vital organs. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Czibik et al observed that PAH activity is downregulated in the liver but upregulated in the heart with age. 18 Elevated blood phenylalanine levels and ectopic uptake of phenylalanine by the heart resulted in cardiac senescence, redox, epigenetic modifications, and cardiac dysfunction. Wang et al reported that the accumulation of phenylalanine in endothelial cells exerted adverse effects through mitochondrial reactive oxygen species production, followed by activation of inflammasome and caspase‐1, secretion of interleukin‐1β and interleukin‐18 by endothelial cells, and pyroptosis. 19 Our previous study showed that higher phenylalanine levels were correlated with higher plasma proinflammatory, innate, and adaptive T lymphocyte immune cytokines such as interleukin‐8 and interleukin‐10. 6 Lv et al demonstrated that macrophages ingest overproduced phenylpyruvate in a manner dependent on the scavenger receptor CD36. This process increases the palmitoylation of the NOD‐like receptor protein 3, promoting inflammasome activation and the release of inflammatory factors, such as interleukin‐1β. 20 Moreover, Zhao et al observed that increased plasma levels of tyrosine were associated with cardiomyocyte apoptosis and the progression of HF. 21

The aforementioned studies have consistently demonstrated the benefits of diets with restriction of phenylalanine or tyrosine in animal models. 18 , 20 , 21 The results of these studies underscore the requirement for further investigation into the mechanisms connecting SHP with adverse outcomes. The present study observed that patients accumulated phenylalanine‐derived downstream metabolites in substantial quantities, even when phenylalanine levels were well below 112 μM or within the normal range. This observation suggests that the enzymes essential for metabolizing phenylalanine and tyrosine on the alternative pathways were activated early to compensate for phenylalanine elevation in HF. Intriguingly, the ratios indicating the function of these enzymes were remarkably lower in patients with phenylalanine levels of ≥112 μM compared with those with levels below 112 μM and in patients who died in 30 days compared with surviving patients. The decreased ratio of tyrosine to phenylalanine has been used to represent the dysfunction of PAH. 22 In addition, as shown in Tables S2 and S3, most of these enzymes are mainly present in the liver, kidney, and autonomic nervous system, which are significantly influenced by HF and inflammation. These findings suggest that the function of these enzymes was conditionally rate limiting and extensively dysregulated in patients with SHP or adverse outcomes.

Previous studies showed that excessive generation of phenylalanine catabolites, including phenylpyruvate 20 , 23 and phenylacetate, 24 , 25 contributed to oxidative stress. In our patients, despite extensive increases in downstream metabolites observed, most metabolites were not significantly associated with 30‐day death. As suggested by previous reports, 26 the blood concentrations of these metabolites were considerably below the levels associated with toxicity. Our study identified a unique metabolic phenotype on the phenylalanine–tyrosine–homogentisic acid–succinylacetone axis. This phenotype, characterized by decreased DOPA and benzoic acid levels and increased homogentisic acid and succinylacetone levels, was correlated with higher 30‐day death. Although the enzymes on the alternative pathways were overall activated, extensive dysregulation of their function led to decreased DOPA production and accumulated homogentisic acid in patients who died. The lower DOPA concentration was associated with a higher incidence of inotropic agent use. Although the enzymes for metabolizing phenylalanine to produce DOPA and dopamine are mainly present in the autonomic nervous system, they also exist in the heart. 27 The clinical significance of plasma DOPA concentration needs further investigation. On the other hand, the pathway of metabolizing phenylalanine to cinnamic acid and benzoic acid is probably related to gut microbiota and is not well explored currently (Table S2).

Homogentisic acid and following metabolites further accumulated with nonenzymatic conversion to succinylacetone. 28 This metabolic pattern closely mirrors the metabolic signature observed in patients with congenital tyrosinemia type 1, 29 suggesting suppressed activity of fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase. Fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase predominantly locates in the liver 30 and is subject to liver congestion associated with HF. The production of succinylacetone appeared to be independent of phenylalanine levels but depended on increased homogentisic acid levels and fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase dysfunction and provided additional prognostic value to phenylalanine. These findings suggest that the prognostic value of phenylalanine metabolism stems from the severity of SHP‐induced pathophysiology, the direct detrimental effect of phenylalanine, and distinct metabolic dysregulation.

Studies have demonstrated the toxicity of succinylacetone in the phenylalanine–tyrosine–homogentisic acid–succinylacetone axis. 31 Succinylacetone impairs aminolevulinic acid dehydratase activity and heme synthesis, causing an accumulation of aminolevulinic acid and ferritin. The disruption in iron metabolism is associated with anemia, ferritinophagy, and ferroptosis in different types of cells including cardiomyocytes. 32 , 33 Excessive aminolevulinic acid can cause oxidative damage to proteins, DNA, mitochondria, and synaptosomes. 34 Furthermore, succinylacetone is toxic to the liver, kidneys, and nervous system. 31 , 35 For the screening of congenital tyrosinemia type 1 in numerous countries, the threshold for succinylacetone ranges between 1.29 and 3 μM. 36 The blood concentrations of succinylacetone in most patients exceeded the established threshold (Figure S5). Consequently, patients with ADHF and high succinylacetone levels can be treated as patients with tyrosinemia type 1 by inhibiting the metabolic pathway from p‐hydroxyphenylpyruvate to homogentisic acid or by implementing a phenylalanine‐free and tyrosine‐free diet. These suggested interventions require validation in future clinical trials.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study did not measure the levels of maleylacetoacetate and fumarylacetoacetate and did not clarify the underlying mechanisms of how succinylacetone elevated. Second, this study provided the association between phenylalanine catabolites and patient outcomes. However, to support causal impact of accumulating phenylalanine, homogentisic acid, and succinylacetone on HF decompensation, further in vitro studies by challenging these metabolites on cardiomyocytes are mandatory. Third, the activities of enzymes were indirectly estimated by the ratios in this study and need direct assessment in the future. Finally, the control group was younger than the study group due to challenges in enrolling older individuals as controls. Nevertheless, the results of the analysis indicated that the metabolism of phenylalanine to all downstream metabolites assessed in this study was not significantly associated with age. In addition, the comparisons of metabolites between the control and patient groups were age adjusted.

Conclusions

ADHF is associated with the initiation of comprehensive pathways to eliminate accumulated phenylalanine. Extensive dysregulation of these pathways is associated with SHP and a distinct metabolic phenotype on the phenylalanine–tyrosine–p‐hydroxyphenylpyruvate–homogentisic acid–succinylacetone axis. Both SHP and metabolic dysregulation on this axis were associated with adverse outcomes in patients with ADHF. The advantages of treatments for patients with ADHF on the dysregulated alternative pathways or this axis, particularly regarding the final steps of succinylacetone production, require investigation in future studies.
