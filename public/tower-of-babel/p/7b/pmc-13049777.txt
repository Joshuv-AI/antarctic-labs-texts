Dynamic changes in nutrient composition and gene expression during persimmon fruit development
Authors: Yunliang Yang, Chao Duan, Shimin Tian, Hongyi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Persimmon is a popular food around our country. The nutrient composition profiles and dynamics of gene expression in persimmon are poorly understood till now. Our research found total soluble sugar, fructose and glucose accumulated gradually, and tannin content decreased during the persimmon ripening. Furthermore, we analyzed the temporal transcriptome data and identified the transcriptional changes during persimmon fruit development. Incredibly, 232 WRKY, 143 bHLH, 112 MYB, and 102 C2H2 were explicitly differentially expressed in five typical development stages of persimmon. Specifically, 11 condensed tannins (procyanidins) biosynthesis-related gene expressions analysis, and 13 critical genes, involved in starch and sucrose metabolism were identified. Our results provide insights into gene networks during Persimmon fruit development and generate a valuable resource for further Persimmon breeding studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08419-8.

Introduction

The persimmon tree (Diospyros kaki) is a woody plant belonging to the Ebenaceae family, widely cultivated worldwide as an economic crop with a long cultivation history. China currently has over 1,000 cultivated varieties [41]. As an economically important fruit tree globally, its fruits possess both nutritional and medicinal value [18]. The fruits are rich in bioactive phenolic compounds, particularly their characteristically high content of proanthocyanidins (commonly called persimmon tannins) [25]. In 2021, China alone contributed 3.43 million t, accounting for 79% of world output [10]. Despite this agronomic and economic prominence, the molecular mechanisms that orchestrate persimmon fruit development, ripening and quality formation remain poorly understood compared with those of other major fruit crops such as tomato, apple or grape. Recent advances in multi-omics approaches, including transcriptomics and metabolomics, have shed light on the molecular mechanisms underlying fruit development, ripening, and astringency regulation in persimmon. Transcriptome analyses have identified key genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, ethylene biosynthesis, and secondary metabolite pathways, which collectively influence fruit quality traits such as sugar accumulation, softening, and pigment formation [24, 35]. For instance, gibberellin (GA) signaling pathways, mediated by GASA genes, play a critical role in prolonging fruit maturation and modulating ripening-related processes [36]. Additionally, transcription factors like DkBZR1 and DkBZR2 have been shown to act as repressors and activators of ripening, respectively, by regulating ethylene production and cell wall metabolism [12]. Meanwhile, metabolomic studies have further elucidated the dynamic changes in primary and secondary metabolites during persimmon fruit development. For example, non-astringent cultivars exhibit distinct metabolic profiles, including higher levels of salicylic acid, ferulic acid, and flavonols, which may contribute to enhanced resistance to pathogens [37]. Integrative transcriptome-metabolome analyses have revealed coordinated regulation of pathways such as glycerophospholipid metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis, and terpenoid production, which are crucial for fruit flavor, texture, and nutritional value [11].

Fruit development and ripening are well-coordinated and finely regulated biological processes [40]. Figuring out the physiological and molecular mechanisms behind fruit development and ripening will enlarge our insight into the biological process and provide helpful information for plantations. However, the Transcriptome dynamics and the relative regulatory networks during the development and ripening of persimmon are still unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to figure out how the physical and chemical properties, titratable acid content, soluble sugars, and tannins are changed during the persimmon fruit development cycle and investigate the underlying mechanism in transcriptional regulation. Transcriptomes from five typical development stages of persimmon were developed. Transcriptome data and physiological and biochemical analysis provided comprehensive information on the dynamics of nutritional ingredients and the underlying regulatory pathways. Our studies will reveal the metabolic regulatory processes of persimmon fruits, provide more comprehensive dietary values, and contribute to the high-value utilization of persimmon by-products.

Materials and methods

Diospyros kaki cv. Youhou from five development stages, including young fruit, growth, expansion, color, and maturity, were collected from a crop located at Yangbao Farm, Cotton Research Institute, Shanxi Agricultural University. For each stage, three biological replicates of the samples were prepared. The samples were named a-e (Supplemental Table 1) for the following analysis. Samples were frozen with liquid nitrogen and preserved at -80℃ before use.

Detection of ascorbic acid

Sample extraction

Ten grams of the well-mixed sample were weighed and transferred into a grinder. Ten grams of oxalic acid solution were added, and the mixture was rapidly ground into a homogenate. Ten grams of the homogenate (± 0.01 g) were accurately weighed into a beaker, rinsed into a 100 mL volumetric flask with oxalic acid solution, and brought to volume. The solution was shaken thoroughly and filtered. When the filtrate was colored, 0.4 g of kaolin per gram of sample was added, the mixture was shaken again, and filtration was repeated until a clear filtrate was obtained.

Standardization of 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol titrant

Ten millilitres of standard ascorbic acid solution were pipetted into a conical flask and titrated with 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol solution until a faint red colour that persisted for 15 s was observed. The mass of ascorbic acid equivalent to 1 mL of titrant was calculated from the volume consumed. The titration was performed in triplicate, and the average value was taken. A blank titration was carried out with oxalic acid solution under identical conditions.

Sample determination

Ten millilitres of the clear filtrate were transferred into a 100 mL Erlenmeyer flask and titrated with the standardized 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol solution until a faint red endpoint that remained stable for 15 s was reached. The volume of titrant used was recorded. A blank determination was performed in parallel by titrating 10 mL of 20 g/ L oxalic acid solution. Each determination was conducted in triplicate.

Result calculation

The ascorbic acid content in the sample was calculated from the net titrant volume and expressed as milligrams of ascorbic acid per 100 g of fresh sample (mg /100 g).

Determination of Titratable Acidity (TA) in fruits

Sample extraction

Ten grams (± 0.01 g) of the well-mixed persimmon sample were weighed and transferred to a mortar, then thoroughly ground. The ground material was quantitatively transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask. The mortar was rinsed with distilled water, and the rinsings were combined in the same flask. The volume was adjusted to the mark with distilled water, mixed thoroughly, and the suspension was allowed to stand for 30 min before filtration.

Standardization of sodium hydroxide titrant

Approximately 0.6000 g of potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP), previously dried to constant mass at 105 ℃, was accurately weighed and dissolved in 50 mL of water with gentle shaking. Two drops of 1% (w/v) phenolphthalein indicator were added, and the solution was titrated with the prepared sodium hydroxide solution until a faint pink colour that persisted for at least 30 s was observed. The concentration of the sodium hydroxide titrant was calculated from the volume consumed and the exact mass of KHP used, taking 20.42 mg KHP as equivalent to 1.00 mL of 0.1 mol/ L NaOH.

Sample determination

Twenty millilitres of the clear filtrate were pipetted into a 100 mL conical flask. Two drops of 1% (w/v) phenolphthalein indicator were added, and the solution was titrated with the standardized sodium hydroxide solution until a pink colour persisted for at least 0.5 min (pH 8.1–8.3). The volume of titrant used was recorded, and the titration was performed in triplicate. A blank titration was carried out by replacing the filtrate with an equal volume of distilled water.

Result calculation

The titratable acidity was calculated from the net volume of sodium hydroxide titrant consumed and expressed as grams of citric acid per 100 g fresh sample.

Detection of soluble sugars

Sample weighing

Exactly 1.00 g of the freshly prepared sample was weighed and transferred into a 50 mL graduated test tube with a small volume of distilled water.

Sucrose standard solution, 100 µg /mL.

Analytical-grade sucrose was dried to constant mass at 80 ℃. Exactly 1.000 g was weighed, dissolved in a small volume of distilled water, transferred into a 100-mL volumetric flask, supplemented with 0.5 mL concentrated sulfuric acid, and then brought to volume with distilled water to afford a 0.01 g mL⁻¹ sucrose stock solution.

An aliquot of 1 mL of this 0.01 g mL⁻¹ stock was pipetted into a 100-mL volumetric flask, diluted to the mark with distilled water, and homogenized to obtain the 100 µg /mL working standard.

Sample preparation

The collected sample was comminuted and ground to a uniform slurry.

Construction of the calibration curve

Aliquots of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mL of the 100 µg/L sucrose standard were transferred into separate tubes and brought to a total volume of 2.0 mL with the corresponding additions of 2.0, 1.8, 1.6, 1.4, 1.2, and 1.0 mL of distilled water. To each tube, 0.5 mL anthrone–ethyl acetate reagent and 5.0 mL concentrated sulfuric acid were added; the mixtures were vigorously shaken and heated in a boiling-water bath for 1 min. After cooling spontaneously to room temperature, absorbance was measured at 630 nm against the 0-tube blank. A calibration curve was plotted with absorbance on the ordinate and sucrose mass on the abscissa, from which the linear regression equation was derived.

Sample weighing

Exactly 1.00 g of freshly prepared sample was weighed, moistened with a minimal amount of distilled water, and transferred into a 50-mL graduated test tube.

Extraction of soluble sugars

Distilled water was added to the 25 mL mark, the tube was sealed with plastic film, and extraction was carried out in a boiling-water bath for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the extract was filtered directly into a 100 mL volumetric flask. The residue was returned to the test tube, 5–10 mL of distilled water was added, and a second extraction was performed by boiling for 10 min. The filtrate was combined in the volumetric flask, and the test tube and residue were rinsed several times with water; all rinsings were transferred to the same flask. The combined filtrate was brought to the 100 mL mark with distilled water and mixed thoroughly.

Color development and measurement

A 0.5 mL aliquot of the extract was pipetted into a 25 mL graduated test tube and diluted with 1.5 mL of distilled water. The color development reagents were added following the procedure employed for the standard curve, giving a final reaction volume of 7.5 mL. When the absorbance exceeded the linear range, the extract was appropriately diluted and the colorimetric measurement was repeated.

Detection of fructose and glucose

Sample preparation

At least 200 g of the samples were mixed thoroughly and transferred into a clean, sealable container, which was then labelled appropriately.

Zinc acetate solution

Zinc acetate (21.9 g) was weighed, combined with 3 mL of glacial acetic acid, and dissolved in water. The solution was diluted to 100 mL.

Potassium ferrocyanide solution

Potassium ferrocyanide (10.6 g) was dissolved in water and diluted to 100 mL.

Sample processing

Approximately 5.0 g (± 0.001 g) of the well-mixed sample was weighed into a 100 mL volumetric flask, and about 50 mL of water was added. Zinc acetate solution (5 mL) and potassium ferrocyanide solution (5 mL) were added slowly with swirling. The contents were diluted to the mark with water and sonicated for 30 min. The solution was filtered through dry filter paper; the first 5–10 mL of filtrate were discarded. The remaining filtrate was passed through a 0.45 μm membrane filter into an HPLC vial.

Chromatographic analysis

Fructose and glucose were separated on an HPLC column and detected with a refractive-index detector. Quantification was performed using the external standard method.

Detection of tannin

Sample preparation

The edible portion of the fruit was taken, surface contaminants were wiped off with clean gauze, and the sample was cut diagonally. One segment obtained by diagonal sectioning was selected, finely chopped, and mixed thoroughly. A representative subsample was taken by quartering, homogenised in a tissue homogeniser, and stored for subsequent analysis.

Extraction of tannins

Exactly 2.0 g of homogenate were transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask with 80 mL of water. The flask was immersed in a boiling-water bath for 30 min, removed, and cooled to room temperature. The volume was adjusted to the mark with water, mixed, and 2.0 mL of the extract was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 4 min. The supernatant was collected and kept for analysis.

Standard curve construction

Aliquots (1.0 mL) of gallic acid standard solutions (0.00, 10.0, 20.0, 30.0, 40.0, and 50.0 mg/L; final assay concentrations 0.0–5.0 mg/L) were pipetted into separate test tubes. To each tube, 5.0 mL of water, 1.0 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and 3.0 mL of sodium carbonate solution were added, mixed, and allowed to stand for 2 h. Absorbances were measured at 765 nm against the 0.0 mg/ L blank. A calibration curve was constructed by plotting gallic acid concentration (x-axis) against absorbance (y-axis).

Sample determination

One millilitre of the supernatant was treated identically to the standards. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm against the 0.0 mg/ L blank. The tannin concentration was derived from the calibration curve and expressed as gallic acid equivalents. When absorbance exceeded that of the 5.0 mg/L standard, the extract was appropriately diluted and re-analysed.

Total RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s procedure. The total RNA quantity and purity of Bioanalyzer 2100 and RNA 1000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent, CA, USA) with RIN number > 7.0 were analyzed. Poly(A) RNA is purified from total RNA(5ug) using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads using two rounds of purification. Following purification, the mRNA is fragmented into small pieces using divalent cations under elevated temperatures. Then, the cleaved RNA fragments were reverse-transcribed to create the final cDNA library following the protocol for the mRNASeq sample preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA); the average insert size for the paired-end libraries was 300 bp (± 50 bp). And then performed the paired-end sequencing on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000, following the vendor’s recommended protocol. Meanwhile, sequencing data records will be accessible with the following link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1138712.

De novo assembly, unigene annotation, and functional classification

Firstly, Cutadapt [16] and Perl scripts in-house were used to remove the reads containing adaptor contamination, low-quality and undetermined bases. The sequence quality was verified using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), including the clean data’s Q20, Q30 and GC-content. All downstream analyses were based on clean, high-quality data. De novo assembly of the transcriptome was performed with Trinity 2.4.0 [8]. Trinity groups transcripts into clusters based on shared sequence content. Such a transcript cluster is loosely called a ‘gene.’ The most extended transcript in the cluster was chosen as the ‘gene’ sequence (aka Unigene).

All assembled Unigenes were aligned against the non-redundant (Nr) protein database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Gene ontology (GO) (http://www.geneontology.org), SwissProt (http://www.expasy.ch/sprot/), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)(http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and eggnog (http://eggnogdb.embl.de/) databases using DIAMOND [3] with a threshold of E value < 0.00001.

Differentially expressed gene analysis

Salmon [26] was used to perform expression levels for Unigenes by calculating TPM [23]. The differentially expressed genes were selected with log2 (fold change) > 1 or log2 (fold change) < -1 and with statistical significance (p-value < 0.05) by R package edgeR [27].

Results

Fruit morphology and detection of ascorbic acid, titratable acid, total soluble sugar, fructose, glucose, and tannin content at different development periods

With the development of fruits, the volume and weight of Persimmon increased continuously, and the persimmon color changed from green to yellow (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, the ascorbic acid, titratable acid, total soluble sugar, fructose, glucose, and tannin content were exhibited in Fig. 1B. During the developmental stages, a moderate increase of ascorbic acid was observed from the young fruit stage (a) to the maturity stage (e). By contrast, the titratable acid of fruit gradually decreased and reached a minimum value at the color stage (d). Our carbohydrate indicators profiling showed that fructose and glucose accumulated gradually during the persimmon ripening. However, we found a sharp decrease in total soluble sugar followed by a gradual increase. Tannin content decreased during ripening, with a sharp reduction from the young fruit (a) to the growth stage (b).

Global transcriptome analyses during fruit development and ripening with Illumina RNA‑seq

Fifteen libraries (three biological replicates of five development stages) were conducted, resulting in approximately 40–45 million clean reads; GC content accounted for 46–50% of these reads (Supplemental Table 2). Meanwhile, Trinity software assembled 101,427 unigenes (Supplemental Table 3), in which 47,359, 26,771, and 38,595 unigenes were annotated in GO, KEGG, and Pfam.

The gene expression levels were used to conduct a PCA for each biological replicate. Each replicate from the same group was clustered closely together, which suggested that the repeatability of each treatment was satisfactory. The samples from different time points of fruit development were clustered far from each other and the control groups, indicating that fruit ripening induced significant changes in gene expression (Fig. 2A). Specifically, a comparative transcriptome analysis of the different development periods was performed. A total of 3736, 2805, 2341, and 3260 DEGs were found in the b vs. a, c vs. b, d vs. c, and e vs.d groups (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, upregulated genes were significantly higher than down-regulated in b vs. a. The opposite was true for the other comparison groups. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis was used to explore the function of DEGs. The DEGs in the b vs. a groups protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, spliceosome, proteasome and flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 2C). In the c vs. b groups, DEGs were enriched in protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, phagosome and Endocytosis (Fig. 2D). The DEGs in the d vs. c groups were enriched in circadian rhythm-plant, starch and sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction and phenylalanine metabolism (Fig. 2E). In the e vs. d groups, the DEGs were enriched in circadian rhythm-plant, plant hormone signal transduction, photosynthesis-antenna proteins and flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 2F).

Identification of coexpressed gene networks by weighted gene co-expression network analysis

To further explore the regulatory mechanisms of ascorbic acid, titratable acid, total soluble sugar, fructose, glucose and tannin during fruit developmental stages, WGCNA was used to identify the coexpressed gene networks. Here, ascorbic acid, titratable acid, total soluble sugar, fructose, glucose and tannin at different stages were taken as phenotypic data for the analysis of gene module-trait correlations. A total of 29 distinct modules marked with different colors were produced according to the similar coexpressed patterns of each gene (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, the MEblack modules showed significant positive correlations with ascorbic acid, fructose and glucose, whereas the MEyellow modules presented significant positive correlations with titratable acidity and tannin. KEGG analysis revealed that the genes in MEblack modules were significantly enriched in galactose metabolism (map00052), amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (map00520), Starch and sucrose metabolism (map00500), Plant hormone signal transduction (map04075), and pentose and glucuronate interconversions (map00040) (Fig. 5B). the genes in MEyellow modules were significantly enriched in Ribosome (map03010), Protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (map04141), and spliceosome (map03040) (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, the MEdarkorange modules showed positive correlations with total soluble sugar. The genes in MEdarkorange modules were significantly enriched in Brassinosteroid biosynthesis (map00905), Peroxisome(map04146), Ascorbate and aldarate metabolism (map00053), and Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (map00520) (Fig. 5D). Thus, the genes in these modules might play a vital role in modulating ascorbic acid, titratable acid, total soluble sugar, fructose, glucose and tannin in the fruit developmental stages.

Identification of potential genes that regulate starch and sucrose metabolism

Carbohydrates, such as sucrose, are a key indicator of fruit ripening. Here, our expression profiling showed that sucrose accumulated gradually during the cashew ripening. Moreover, sucrose was identified as the most predominant fraction in the ripe persimmon. Then we investigated the potential key genes involved in starch and sucrose metabolism, in which 12 critical genes were identified, including three Alpha, alpha-trehalose-phosphate synthase genes (TPS), three sucrose synthase genes (SUSs), two invertase genes (INVs), three beta-glucosidase genes (BGLUs), and one Beta-amylase (BAM). All these 12 genes were upregulated gradually during fruit development and specifically highly upregulated during the ripening stage (Fig. 6A).

The expression levels of key genes involved in the biosynthesis pathways of condensed tannins were further analyzed to reveal the dynamic changes in gene expression at molecule level. Discussion

Persimmon is one of the most popular and economically significant fruits worldwide. It is pretty helpful for breeding practice and postharvest management to understand the physiological and molecular mechanisms behind Persimmon fruit development and ripening. During the ripening process of persimmon fruit, the changes in the content of ascorbic acid, titratable acid, total soluble sugar, fructose, glucose, and tannins have significant biological implications for fruit softening, flavor formation, and storability. The results indicated ascorbic acid rose modestly (a→e), titratable acid declined to a minimum at the colour stage (d), fructose and glucose accumulated steadily, total soluble sugar dipped then rose, and tannins dropped sharply from young fruit (a) to growth (b). Ascorbic acid is an important antioxidant in persimmon fruit, and its content gradually increases during ripening. Ascorbic acid not only helps maintain the redox balance of the fruit, reducing browning and oxidative damage, but also influences flavor and nutritional value. Additionally, the increase in ascorbic acid content may be related to enhanced ethylene synthesis during fruit ripening, thereby promoting the softening process [31]. The content of titratable acid gradually decreases during persimmon fruit ripening1, which is associated with the metabolic conversion of organic acids (such as citric acid and malic acid) [31]. Total soluble sugar, fructose, and glucose increase significantly during ripening [9]. The accumulation of these sugars is the primary source of sweetness in persimmon fruit. The increase in sugars not only enhances flavor but also provides energy substrates for respiratory metabolism. Additionally, sugar accumulation is associated with pectin degradation and cell wall loosening, promoting fruit softening [4]. Tannins are unique polyphenolic compounds in persimmon fruit, and their content and polymerization state directly affect astringency and texture. Highly polymerized tannins are abundant in immature fruit, causing strong astringency, whereas solubility and polymerization decrease during ripening, reducing astringency [19]. Previous research found that TFs are essential in regulating various plant processes, including fruit ripening [21]. WRKY transcription factors are one of the most prominent families of transcriptional regulators in plants that modulate many plant processes. The transcription of WRKY11 was upregulated during the cashew nut development [40]. ERFs are transcriptional regulators mediating ethylene-dependent gene expression in plants, and these TFs are associated with fruit ripening, especially in climacteric fruits [38]. The MYB family of proteins is large, functionally diverse, and represented in all eukaryotes. In plants, some members of this family also participate in fruit ripening [5]. In our research, 59 WRKY, 35 bHLH, 54 MYB, and 24 NAC were identified, and the TFs were specifically differentially expressed in five typical development stages of persimmon. For example, the three WRKY genes, including TRINITY_DN69081_c0_g3, TRINITY_DN60480_c1_g1, and TRINITY_DN54441_c1_g1, were only highly expressed in e stage. The two MYB genes, TRINITY_DN57589_c1_g1 and TRINITY_DN58130_c0_g4, were only highly expressed in a group, while TRINITY_DN65303_c5_g1 and TRINITY_DN53151_c3_g3 were significantly upregulated in c group. Some of the TFs were highly expressed during the early stages of fruit development, with a high proportion of genes being highly expressed at the stage of maturity. The TFs may perform different functions in the five developmental stages of fruits, and further study is needed.

Glucose and fructose are generally considered the main sugars, while sucrose is a minor component in mature persimmon fruits [1, 34]. Our results indicated that total soluble sugar, fructose, and glucose accumulated gradually (Fig. 1B) as the predominant fraction in persimmon during the late ripening stage. Meanwhile, transcriptome results also showed the 12 potential key genes involved in starch and sucrose metabolism, which were upregulated gradually during fruit development. Fan et al., found that the glucose content can be increased using alpha-trehalose-phosphate synthase genes (TPS) [20]. During fruit maturation, TPS genes show stage-specific expression patterns that correlate with carbohydrate dynamics. For instance, in melon fruits, different TPS isoforms exhibited distinct functions, participating in substrate synthesis for young fruit growth while regulating raffinose family oligosaccharides metabolism for sucrose accumulation in mature fruits [33]. Similarly, in loquats, key differentially expressed genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, including TPS-related pathways, were significantly regulated during cultivated fruit development [15]. In our study, three TPS genes were up-regulated in the fruit of the ripening stage, and fructose and glucose accumulated gradually during the persimmon ripening. SUS belongs to the glycosyltransferase-4 subfamily of the carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) repertoire, is a key enzyme involved in sucrose metabolism, which catalyzes the reversible transformation of sucrose into fructose and UDP-glucose, which plays essential roles in plant sugar metabolism [32]. For example, in potato, carrot taproots, and maize endosperm, starch content was reported to be decreased due to SuS activity reduction [43]. Many studies highlight the contribution of SuSy to the synthesis of both polysaccharides. Evidence of SuSy’s role in callose deposition was found in an Arabidopsis double mutant of phloem-specific SUS (sus5 sus6). The double mutant had less callose in its phloem plasmodesmata and in response to leaf wounding than wild-type or quadruple mutant (sus1, sus2, sus3, and sus4) plants [2]. The involvement of SuSy in callose deposition has also been suggested by co-distribution analysis [29]. Our research showed that three SUS genes were identified and highly expressed in the ripe stage of the fruit. These findings suggest that SUS genes play important roles in persimmon fruit ripening, likely through their involvement in sugar metabolism and fruit quality formation. The differential expression patterns of these SUS genes may reflect distinct mechanistic roles in persimmon ripening [6]. For instance, one SUS gene may primarily function in sucrose cleavage to provide substrates for glycolysis and energy production, while another could be more involved in starch-to-sugar conversion, a critical process for sweetness accumulation during ripening11. The third SUS gene might contribute to cell wall metabolism by supplying UDP-glucose for polysaccharide biosynthesis, thereby influencing fruit softening [36]. Invertase is responsible for the cleavage of sucrose into glucose and fructose and catalyzes the hydrolysis of sucrose in the vacuole, altering fruit sugar composition [28]. Silencing of a tomato vacInv gene resulted in higher sucrose and lower hexose concentrations in transgenic fruit than in wild-type fruit [17]. PbrAc-Inv1 could accelerate sucrose degradation during ‘Housui’ pear storage [22]. In our study, two invertase genes (INVs) were upregulated by fruit ripening. We speculated that these genes benefit carbohydrate accumulation and help fruit ripen.

Meanwhile, 11 condensed tannin biosynthesis-related genes were identified and down-regulated with fruit development. For example, four chalcone synthase genes were found, with low expression in the maturity of the fruit. Chalcone synthase (CHS) is a crucial rate-limiting enzyme in the plant flavonoid biosynthesis, which catalyzes the condensation of malonyl-CoA and ρ-coumaroyl-CoA to produce naringenin chalcone, which serves as the precursor of a variety of flavonoid derivatives and plays a vital role in regulating plant growth and development [13, 39]. Yu et al., found that the CHS genes were down-regulated during the fruit development of pepper (Capsicum baccatum). Consistent with our study, four chalcone synthase genes exhibited a similar trend of expression and evidently had lower expression with the maturity of the fruit. CHI is also a key enzyme in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway [30]. Research indicated that the overexpression of DcCHI1 or DcCHI4 enhanced the flavonoid production of D. cambodiana and tobacco [42]. In the present study, 4 CHI genes were identified and down-regulated with fruit development. Dihydroflavonol-4-reductase (DFR, EC1.1.1.219) catalyzes a key step late in the biosynthesis of anthocyanins, condensed tannins [14]. The DFR products, the leucoanthocyanidins, are substrates for the next step in the anthocyanin pathway and are also the substrates for the proanthocyanidin pathway [7]. One DFR gene had evidently low expression in the maturity of the fruit in our study. In short, the condensed tannin biosynthesis-related genes were evidently downregulated with fruit development, which explains reduced tannin content during ripening, especially with a sharp decrease from the young fruit stage to the growth stage. However, our research has certain limitations. Since all our samples have been used for the detection of substances such as sugars and tannins as well as transcriptome sequencing, the sample size is no longer sufficient to meet the requirements of further functional validation experiments.

In summary, fruit morphology and the detection of ascorbic acid, titratable acid, total soluble sugar, fructose, glucose, and tannin content were conducted during different development periods. On the other hand, we performed transcriptome analysis and explored the candidate pathway and genes involved in starch and sucrose metabolism and condensed tannins biosynthesis. Our data draws a temporal map of persimmon’s physiological indexes and transcriptomes during fruit development, provides insight into the transcriptional control and regulatory networks of carbohydrates and tannins, and offers guidance for future quality improvement and utilization of persimmon.
