Gut microbiota-derived taurolithocholic acid modulates myofiber-type switching via p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling underlying breed differences between Arbor Acres and Taoyuan chickens
Authors: F Peng, YY Zeng, L Chang, YX Huang, JT Deng, YX Liu, X He, ZH Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is well-established that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in skeletal muscle development and homeostasis. However, the contribution of the gut microbiome to the distinct meat quality phenotypes observed between fast-growing commercial broilers and slow-growing local chicken breeds remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate how the gut microbiota modulates pectoral muscle development by comparing muscle growth phenotypes and gut microbiome dynamics across these breeds. Using the fast-growing commercial Arbor Acres (AA) broiler and the slow-growing local breed Taoyuan (TY) chicken as models, we investigated how breed-specific gut microbiota modulate pectoral muscle fiber composition. AA broilers exhibited faster muscle growth but lower oxidative type I fiber proportion than TY chickens. While small intestinal microbiota succession was similar, cecal communities diverged markedly between breeds. Integrated metagenomic sequencing and metabolomics revealed that cecal Phocaeicola dorei abundance was strongly correlated with serum taurolithocholic acid (TLCA) levels and type I fiber content, especially in TY chickens, which prompted the selection of TLCA for functional validation. Reciprocal intestinal microbiota transplantation (IMT) shifted recipient muscle fiber phenotypes toward those of donors, confirming a causal role of the cecal microbiota. Furthermore, in vitro assays using AA-derived myoblasts demonstrated that TLCA promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and type I fiber formation by enhancing p38 MAPK phosphorylation and PGC-1α activation; this effect was abolished by the p38 inhibitor SB203580. Our study demonstrated that gut microbiota-derived TLCA modulates muscle fiber type transformation via the p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway. This finding reveals an intricate mechanism whereby the gut microbiota regulates host muscle development through a metabolite-signaling axis, providing critical insights into the gut microbe-myofiber relationship.Keywords: Taoyuan chicken, Muscle fiber, Gut microbiota, Intestinal microbiota transplantation, Taurolithocholic acid

Introduction

The establishment and succession of microbiota within the host intestinal tract have attracted widespread attention in recent years (Roswall et al., 2021; Stewart et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2021). The development of gut microbiota during the early stages of life has a profound impact on the host health, including stimulating the development of the immune system and the maturation of immune cells, and maintaining the homeostasis of the endocrine system (Abrahamsson et al., 2014; Raman et al., 2019; Robertson et al., 2019). Interventions on the gut microbiota during the early stages have long-term and far-reaching effects on later growth and development (Derrien et al., 2019; Macpherson et al., 2017). The close connection between the gut microbiota and the host in terms of nutrient metabolism, immunity, and diseases has become a consensus among researchers (De Vos et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Willson et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2020). Recent studies have shown that gut microbiota play an important role in promoting animal muscle growth and maintaining muscle function (Hanna et al., 2023; Xie and Huang, 2024). The bidirectional information exchange mechanism of the "gut-muscle axis" has emerged. The superior meat-quality traits of local livestock breeds are closely linked to their gut microbiota, and dissecting the development of gut microbiota has become a central focus for elucidating these advantageous characteristics (Ma et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018).

The Taoyuan (TY) chicken is one of the rare local large-sized meat-type chicken breeds in China. Compared with fast-growing white-feathered meat chickens, it has the advantages of delicious meat quality, tolerance to roughage, and strong adaptability. However, a critical but unresolved question is whether the distinctive muscle phenotype of TY chickens is linked to their breed-specific gut microbiota. Studies on pigs and mice have shown that transplanting the microbiota of obese Rongchang pigs to germ-free mice can transfer fiber characteristics and lipid metabolic profiles of skeletal muscle from pigs to germ-free mice (Yan et al., 2016). Another study has shown that succinic acid derived from intestinal microbiota can effectively induce the remodeling of mouse skeletal muscle fiber types through the SUCNR1 and CaN/NFAT signaling pathways (Wang et al., 2019). These findings collectively suggest that the gut microbiota can modulate host muscle development through microbe–metabolite–signaling axes. Yet, it remains unknown whether such a mechanism underlies the divergent meat quality between slow-growing indigenous chickens like TY and fast-growing commercial broilers—and, crucially, whether the “high-quality” microbial signature of TY chickens is transferable to improve muscle traits in production-oriented lines.

In this work, we selected fast-growing commercial broiler breed Arbor Acres (AA) and slow-growing local chicken TY chicken as the research subjects. We recorded the growth and development of AA and TY chickens, and compared the establishment and succession of microbiota in the four gut segments (duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum) of both types of chickens. Through correlation analysis, we established the association between intestinal microbial development and muscle fiber development. Finally, through intestinal microbiota transplantation (IMT) experiment and in vitro metabolite functional verification experiments, we clarified the influence and mechanism of gut microbiota on muscle fiber development.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Hunan Agricultural University Animal Ethics Committee (2021015).

Animal treatments and sampling

The fertilized eggs of commercial breeds AA broiler (fast-growing) and Chinese indigenous TY Chicken (slow-growing) were purchased from Henan FengYuan Poultry Breeding Co., Ltd. (Nanyang, China) and Hunan XiangJia Husbandry Co., Ltd. (Changde, China), respectively. Fertilized eggs were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37.5°C. Both of AA chickens (weighing 48.84 ± 3.80 g) and TY chickens (weighing 33.63 ± 3.34 g) were only retained male birds and then divided into 10 replicates, with 12 chickens in each replicate and provided with water and food ad libitum under identical conditions after hatching. AA and TY chickens were fed the same diet according the recommended standards of the Nutrient Requirements of Yellow Chickens, China (NY/T 3645-2020) and NRC (1994) (Table S1) (Peng et al., 2025). Six male broilers per breed were harvested at day 1 after hatching (D1), D7, D14, D21, D28, D42, and D56, respectively. In addition, six male broilers of TY were also executed at D77 and D105 (Fig. 1A). All broiler chickens were weighted before slaughter and the pectoral muscle weight (PMW) was accurately measured after slaughter. The right pectoral muscle tissue of all chickens was used for immunofluorescence analysis. The contents of duodenum, jejunum, ileum and cecum were collected into sterile precooled tubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at −80 °C for 16S rRNA sequencing analysis.

For IMT experiment (Fig. 3A), incubating and rearing a new batch of AA chickens and TY chickens following the above operating procedures. The AA chickens were slaughtered for sampling at D42, and the TY chickens were slaughtered for sampling at D56. The cecal contents were collected into sterile precooled tubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at −80 °C for the preparation of bacterial suspensions. Then, incubating the third batch of AA chickens and TY chickens following the same procedure, and retained only the male chicks upon hatching. A total of 96 1-day-old AA chickens (weighing 49.02 ± 4.11 g) and 96 1-day-old TY chickens (weighing 34.87 ± 3.62 g) were selected as recipients and randomly divided into the IMT group and control group, namely the AA chicken control group (AA_Ctrl), the AA chicken IMT group (AA_IMT), the TY chicken control group (TY_Ctrl), and the TY chicken IMT group (TY_IMT). Each group consisted of 6 replicates, with 8 chickens per replicate. The rearing conditions were maintained consistent with those of the first rearing trial. As described previously (Wang et al., 2023), the cecal contents from AA chickens (D42) and TY chickens (D56) were mixed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), respectively. The mixture was then filtered through sterile gauze to remove impurities. The microbial concentration was adjusted to 109 CFU/mL to prepare a 1 mL bacterial suspension. Birds in AA_IMT group were orally administrated with 1 mL bacterial suspension from TY chickens (AA←TY), while birds in TY_IMT group were orally administrated with 1 mL bacterial suspension from AA chickens (TY←AA). Both AA_Ctrl group and TY_Ctrl group were administrated equal volumes of PBS. The IMT trial was conducted from D1 to D21. The birds were weighed after a 12 h fast at D21 and D28, respectively. Subsequently, two chickens were randomly selected from each replicate of the four treatment groups for sampling. Blood samples were taken from the wing vein and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtained the serum for metabolomics analysis. Then, the birds were weighted before slaughter and the PMW was accurately measured after slaughter. The left pectoral muscle tissue was quickly collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at –80°C for RNA extraction. The right pectoral muscle tissue was used for histomorphological analysis. Cecal contents were collected and transferred into sterile precooled tubes, and then stored at −80°C for metagenomic analysis.

Cell culture

Chicken primary myoblasts (CPMs) were isolated from the pectoral muscles of 19-day-old embryonic AA broiler muscles according to the method previously described (Chen et al., 2024a). CPMs were maintained in complete DMEM/F-12 (1:1) (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) containing 15% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco) in a humidified incubator at 37°C under 5% CO2. When the cell density reached 90%, the culture medium was replaced with differentiation medium containing 2% horse serum (Gibco) without fetal bovine serum to achieve myotube differentiation.

Cell viability assay

CPMs were seeded in 96-well culture plates and grown to approximately 80% confluence. The cells were incubated with taurolithocholic acid (TLCA) (ST4440, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 0, 25, 50, 100, 150 and 200 μM in serum-free DMEM/F12 for 24 h, and their viabilities were determined by cell counting kit-8 assay (C0037, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The maximum concentration that had no effect on cell viability was chosen as the final concentration of TLCA for the following experiments.

p38 MAPK inhibition assay

The differentiation medium containing TLCA and p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580 (152121-47-6, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was added to CPMs after 4 days of differentiation, and the cells were collected after 24 h.

Histomorphological analysis

Muscle samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. The tissues were fixed and embedded in paraffin, sliced, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and the images were analyzed. The muscle fiber diameter (MDia) and muscle fiber density (MDen) were measured as described previously (Huo et al., 2021).

Immunofluorescence analysis

For immunofluorescence staining, muscle paraffin sections were left at room temperature for 10 min and then sequentially immersed in xylene, anhydrous ethanol, 85%, 75% ethanol and distilled water for dewaxing and hydration. The sections were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in antigen retrieval solution, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Following blocking with 1% BSA, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody MYH1A (1:100, F59, DSHB, USA) and the AF647-labeled goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:500, A0473, Beyotime), with the primary antibody MYH7B (1:100, S58, DSHB) and the FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:500, A0568, Beyotime). Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime). Finally, the sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and the fluorescence area was calculated using ImageJ software.

Real‑time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from pectoral muscle and CPMs using TRIZol Universal reagent (DP424, TIANGEN, Beijing, China) and transcribed into cDNA using the HisyGo RT Red SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA Wiper) kit (RT101-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The PCR primer sequences utilized for the determination of the gene expression were shown in Table S2. Gene expression was determined on a RT-qPCR system, using ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Q312-02, Vazyme). Relative quantification of the target gene expression was quantified using 2−△△CT method as previously described methods (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Metabolite and enzyme activity assay

Intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content and enzyme activity of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were determined by ATP assay kit (S0026, Beyotime), SDH activity detection kit (BC0955, Solarbio) and LDH activity detection kit (BC0685, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content analysis

Total cellular DNA was extracted from CPMs with TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0 (9765, Takara, Beijing, China) according to the instruction manual. The relative content of mtDNA was determined by RT-qPCR. The primers used for mtDNA amplification were shown in Table S2.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed according to our previous methods (Peng et al., 2023). The primary antibodies used were β-tubulin (1:5000, bsm-33034 M, Bioss, China), PGC-1α (1:2000, 6639-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), p38 MAPK (1:1000, 8690, CST, USA) and phospho-p38 MAPK (1:1000, 4511, CST). The secondary antibodies used were HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (1:1000, A0216, Beyotime) and HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:1000, A0208, Beyotime).

Microbiota genomic DNA extraction

The microbiota genomic DNA was extracted from the intestinal contents of AA and TY chickens using TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (DP328, TIANGEN). The quality and concentration of the obtained DNA was determined by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and Nanodrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA).

16S rRNA sequencing analysis

After the microbiota genomic DNA extracted from the contents of duodenum, jejunum, ileum and cecum have passed the quality inspection, the 16S rDNA gene’s V3–V4 region was amplified using the primer pairs (F: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′ and R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Raw fastq files were quality‐filtered and taxonomically analyzed using QIIME2 (v2024.10) (Bolyen et al., 2019). Sequences were concatenated into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using the DADA2 (v1.34.0) (Callahan et al., 2016). Taxonomy assignment was performed on the basis of the SILVA database (Release132, http://www.arb-silva.de). The alpha diversity and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-curtis dissimilarity were calculated using the R package vegan (v2.6-4) (Dixon, 2003). Statistical analyses for alpha diversity and beta diversity were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test/Wilcoxon rank-sum test and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA/Adonis), respectively. The Dirichlet polynomial mixture (Dirichlet multinomial mixtures, DMM) model was used to classify the sample into community types, and the samples were classified based on the relative abundance after logarithmic transformation (Feng et al., 2023). The appropriate cluster was determined according to the lowest Laplace approximation score.

Analysis of the contribution of the intestinal microbiota to the host phenotype

Since taxa at lower detection rates are less informative for association analysis, we retained only taxa that were presented in at least 5% of samples with a relative abundance greater than 0.1%. The association between qualified taxa and muscle development phenotypes (PMW) was analyzed using a two-part model with a customized R script (Fu et al., 2015). This model accounts for both binary (present and absent) and quantitative features, and its detailed calculation has been described previously (Wen et al., 2021). Based on the analysis results of the two-part model, we identified the taxa that were significantly associated with muscle development phenotypes in the four intestinal segments of AA and TY chickens. To quantitatively assess the potential impact of different taxa on muscle development, the results of the binary model and the quantitative model were combined to calculate the genus score for each sample. For the binary model, the estimated coefficients could be used to measure the direction and magnitude of the influence of the taxa on the phenotype. Only the relative abundance of the taxa > 0, the estimated coefficients were used as the genus score of the taxa (Table S3-10). For the quantitative model, the estimated coefficients reflected the extent to which the relative abundance of the taxa affects the phenotype. Therefore, the genus score of the taxa was obtained by multiplying the relative abundance of the taxa by the estimated coefficients from the linear regression (Table S11-18). Finally, the genus score of the taxa was the sum of the binary model and the quantitative model, and the cumulative score of all taxa for each sample was the total score.

The total score for each of the 384 samples was calculated as the sum of the products of the relative abundance of each microbial taxon and its corresponding regression coefficient from the taxon–phenotype association analyses. To compare the strength of association between the intestinal microbiota of different gut segments and muscle development phenotypes, linear regression models were fitted between the segment-specific total score and each phenotype (Table S19). The resulting coefficient of determination (R²) and P-value are reported for descriptive comparison across intestinal segments.

Metagenomic analysis

After the quality check, DNA from the contents of cecum was sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq/HiSeq platform by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The raw sequencing data was first quality-screened and filtered with fastp (v0.22.0) (Chen, 2023). Host sequences were then removed using minimap2 (v2.28) (Kalikar et al., 2022). The cleaned reads were assembled into contigs with MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) (Li et al., 2015), and genes were predicted from these contigs using Prodigal (v2.6.3) (Hyatt et al., 2010). Taxonomic annotation of the predicted genes was carried out with Kraken2 (v2.1.3) (Armstrong et al., 2023), and functional annotation with HUMAnN3 (v3.9) (Hua et al., 2024). Subsequent analyses were based on the resulting taxonomic and functional profiles.

Metabolomics analysis

We performed untargeted metabolomics to analyze the metabolites in chicken serum. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis was carried out by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The identification of metabolites was based on self-built library PSNGM Database constructed by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd, mzCloud online library (https://www.mzcloud.org/), LIPID MAPS (https://www.lipidmaps.org/), HMDB (https://hmdb.ca/), MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/) and NIST_2020_MSMS spectral library. The MS1 mass tolerance was set to 15 ppm and the MS2 Match Factor Threshold was set to 50.

The R package ropls (v1.24.0) was employed to separately conduct principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) for dimensionality reduction on sample data (Thévenot et al., 2015). The variable importance in projection (VIP) value of each variable in the OPLS-DA model was calculated to indicate its contribution to the classification. Metabolites with the VIP value > 1 were further applied to Student’s t-test at a univariate level to measure the significance of each metabolite, the P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

Data were shown as the mean ± SD, and analyzed using SPSS 27.0.1 software (SPSS, Chicago, USA). The comparison of two groups of data was conducted using the independent-sample t-tests. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

Results

Phenotypic characterization of pectoral muscle development in AA and TY Chickens

Body weight (BW) and PMW were recorded in AA chickens from D1 to D56 and in TY chickens fromD1 to D105, and compared between breeds at the same age. Although BW and PMW increased with age in both breeds, AA chickens exhibited significantly higher values than TY chickens at all seven time points (Fig. 1B and C). We compared the developmental trajectory of myofiber types in the pectoral muscle of AA and TY chickens. At hatching, both breeds possessed a large proportion of type I fibers, which were subsequently converted to type II fibers with age. However, this transition occurred markedly faster in AA chickens; type I fibers became undetectable by D7, whereas a substantial proportion persisted in TY chickens until D21, declining significantly only after D28 (Fig. 1D and E). These results demonstrated a substantial difference between the breeds, with AA chickens exhibiting significantly faster growth rates and a markedly accelerated transition from oxidative (type I) to glycolytic (type II) muscle fibers compared to TY chickens.

Cecal microbiota dynamics drive the growth and development of pectoral muscle in AA and TY chickens

The Shannon index changed significantly over time in the ileum (P = 0.02) and cecum (P < 0.02) of AA chickens (D1-D56) and in the duodenum (P = 0.02) and cecum (P < 0.01) of TY chickens (D1-D105) (Fig. 2A and B). Although microbial diversity changed with age in all segments, the cecal microbiota consistently exhibited the highest alpha diversity in both breeds. Furthermore, PCoA analysis revealed that the bacterial community structure in most gut segments at D1 was distinct from later time points. Notably, only the cecal microbiota demonstrated a clear, sequential temporal succession with age in both AA and TY chickens (Fig. 2C, Fig. S1).

Composition analysis revealed that all four gut segments of newly hatched (D1) AA and TY chickens were dominated by Proteobacteria at the phylum level. As the microbiota matured, the three anterior gut segments (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) of both breeds became predominantly colonized by Firmicutes. In contrast, the cecal microbiota, while also rich in Firmicutes, contained a substantial proportion of Bacteroidota. Notably, the relative abundance of Bacteroidota in the cecum decreased with age in AA chickens, whereas an opposite trend was observed in TY chickens (Fig. S2). At the genus level, Enterococcus and Escherichia-Shigella predominated in all gut segments of newly hatched chicks from both breeds. While Ligilactobacillus became the dominant genus in the three anterior intestinal segments after D7 in both breeds, it was notably absent from the cecum of TY chickens, though it was present in AA chickens. Furthermore, the number of bacterial genera in the cecum increased significantly after D1 in both breeds, with Faecalibacterium emerging as a dominant genus later in development. Notably, Faecalibacterium colonization occurred earlier in AA chickens (by D14) than in TY chickens (by D21) (Fig. 2D and E, Fig. S3).

To define the successional dynamics of the gut microbiota, we applied the DMM model to cluster community types across different gut segments in AA and TY chickens. Throughout development, the duodenum progressed through five distinct community types, dominated by Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, and Ligilactobacillus. (Fig. S4A). The jejunum, ileum and cecum of AA and TY chickens was exposed to four different community types. For the jejunum, Enterococcus, Limosilactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, and Staphylococcus dominated the community types (Fig. S5A). For the ileum, Enterococcus, Ligilactobacillus, and Candidatus_Arthromitus dominated the community types (Fig. S6A). For the cecum, Bacteroides and unclassified_Lachnospiraceae dominated the community types (Fig. 2F). These community types exhibited significant differences in alpha diversity and composition and displayed clear temporal succession (Fig. 2G-I, Fig. S4-6 B-D). Analysis of the successional patterns revealed a consistent transition from disordered to ordered community states after hatching in all segments. The time to reach a relatively stable state, however, was breed- and segment-specific: the cecum stabilized by D42 in AA chickens versus D56 in TY chickens, while the jejunum and ileum stabilized later, at D42 in AA and D77 in TY chickens. The duodenum did not reach a clearly defined stable state within the observation period.

We next assessed the contribution of gut microbiota colonization in four gut segments to PMW via correlation analysis. The analysis revealed that cecal microbiota colonization had the strongest effect on PMW in both AA chickens (R² = 0.77, P < 0.001) and TY chickens (R² = 0.70, P < 0.001). In contrast, the contributions of the duodenal, jejunal, and ileal microbiota were significantly smaller and often non-significant in both breeds (Fig. 2J and K). These results demonstrated that the cecum is the primary gut site influencing muscle development, with a much greater impact than the anterior intestinal segments.

The alteration of the microbial community in the cecum affects the pectoral muscle development of chickens

We investigated the effects of IMT on BW, pectoral muscle development, and cecal microbiota in recipient chickens. As shown in Fig. 3B, no significant differences in BW were observed between the AA_Ctrl and AA_IMT groups, as well as between the TY_Ctrl and TY_IMT groups at both D21 and D28 (P > 0.05). However, compared with the AA_Ctrl group, the PMW in the AA_IMT group significantly increased at D21 and D28 (P = 0.003 & P = 0.043). Compared with the TY_Ctrl group, the PMW in the TY_IMT group only significantly increased at D21 (P = 0.017) (Fig. 3C). The histomorphological examination revealed significant differences between the control and IMT groups both in AA and TY chickens (Fig. 3D and E). At D21 and D28, IMT significantly reduced the MDia but significantly increased the MDen of the pectoral muscles of AA chickens when compared to the AA_Ctrl group (P < 0.001). On the contrary, IMT significantly increased the MDia but significantly reduced the MDen of the pectoral muscles of TY chickens when compared to the TY_Ctrl group at D21, and IMT significantly resulted in a reduction in MDen of the pectoral muscles of TY chickens at D28 (P < 0.001). However, there were no significant changes in MDia between the TY_Ctrl and TY_IMT groups at D28 (P > 0.05). The changes in muscle fiber types induced by IMT were shown here (Fig. 3F and G). At D21 and D28, the AA_IMT group displayed a significant up-regulation of MYH7B (P < 0.001 & P = 0.002) and a marked down-regulation of MYH1A (P = 0.043 & P = 0.040) relative to the AA_Ctrl group. In contrast, at D21, the TY_IMT group showed a pronounced decrease in MYH7B (P = 0.011) and a significant increase in MYH1A (P = 0.039) compared with the TY_Ctrl group. At D28, the TY_IMT group maintained its significant elevation of MYH1A (P = 0.030), whereas the reduction in MYH7B (P = 0.064) was only evident as a downward trend.

We subsequently investigated whether IMT alters the composition and function of the cecal microbiota in IMT-recipient chickens. At D21, IMT exerted no discernible effect on cecal microbiota alpha diversity in either the AA or TY chickens compared with the AA_Ctrl or TY_Ctrl groups (Fig. 4A); however, it elicited a pronounced increase in the Shannon index of the AA_IMT group at D28 compared to the AA_Ctrl group (P = 0.016) (Fig. S7A). The PCoA results revealed significant divergence in cecal microbiota structure between the control and IMT groups within both the AA and TY chickens at D21 and D28 (Fig. 4B, Fig. S7B). At the phylum level, both the AA_IMT and TY_IMT groups exhibited a significant increase in the relative abundance of Bacteroidota and a concomitant decrease in Firmicutes and Firmicutes A compared with their respective controls at D21(Fig. S8A). At D28, the modulatory effects of IMT on the Bacteroidota had markedly attenuated in both AA and TY chickens (Fig. S8B). At the species level, IMT altered the relative abundances of several cecal taxa in both AA and TY chickens relative to their respective controls at D21 and D28. At D21, the affected taxa included Alistipes excrementavium, A. excrementigallinarum, Phocaeicola dorei, Barnesiella merdigallinarum, Parabacteroides faecavium, and Bacteroides togonis (Fig. 4C). At D28, shifts were evident in B. merdigallinarum, P. dorei, B. ndongoniae, and P. plebeius A (Fig. S7C). In particular, P. dorei was consistently enriched in the AA_IMT group at both D21 and D28 compared with the AA_Ctrl group, whereas its relative abundance declined in the TY_IMT group at both time points compared with the TY_Ctrl group. The Mann-Whitney U test revealed that at D21 and D28, the relative abundance of P. dorei showed a significant difference between the TY_Ctrl and TY_IMT groups (Table S20).

The LEfSe analysis based on the functional level of the intestinal microbiota indicated that at D21, the functional enrichment results of the AA_IMT group were more similar to those of the TY_Ctrl group, while the functional enrichment results of the TY_IMT group were more similar to those of the AA_Ctrl group (Fig. 4D). More importantly, the secondary bile acid biosynthesis pathway was significantly enriched in both the AA_IMT and the TY_Ctrl groups. However, this pattern of reciprocal similarity had disappeared at D28 (Fig. S7D). Collectively, these data indicated that IMT could restructure the cecal microbial composition and function of recipient chickens, ultimately modulating pectoral muscle development.

TLCA as a DEM may act as a key regulator of pectoral muscle fiber development

We further performed untargeted metabolomics to understand the alterations in the metabolites in the chicken serum after IMT. First, OPLS-DA was used to cluster the samples, and two separated groups were found in control and IMT groups both in AA and TY chickens at D21 and D28 (Fig. 5A-D). To identify the differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) that between the control and IMT groups of AA and TY chickens, we selected the metabolites with VIP value > 1 and P-value < 0.05 both in the positive and negative ion modes. At D21 and D28, 100 and 188 DEMs met this criterion in the AA_Ctrl and AA_IMT groups (Fig. 5E), respectively, and 55 and 40 DEMs met the same threshold in the TY_Ctrl and TY_IMT groups (Fig. 5F). To refine the list of DEMs, we constructed the Venn diagrams intersecting the metabolites significantly up-regulated by IMT in AA chickens with those significantly down-regulated by IMT in TY chickens at both D21 and D28. Ultimately, TLCA was identified as the sole DEM common to the four intersecting sets (Fig. 5G). Through the correlation analysis between the serum TLCA and phenotypic traits, it was found that TLCA was significantly negatively correlated with the gene expression levels of MYH1A (r = −0.691, P < 0.01), PMW (r = −0.742, P < 0.01), and MDia (r = −0.821, P < 0.01), and was significantly positively correlated with the gene expression level of MYH7B (r = 0.846, P < 0.01) and MDen (r = 0.854, P < 0.01) (Fig. 5H). These data suggested that the levels of serum TLCA may substantially influence pectoral muscle fiber development.

TLCA regulated mitochondrial biogenesis and altered myofiber type composition through the p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway

To verify our hypothesis that TLCA might affect the development of muscle fibers, we induced the differentiation of CPMs to form myotubes and added TLCA to determine whether it had an impact on the composition of myofiber types. The results of the CCK8 assay indicated that no evident cellular cytotoxicity was observed at TLCA concentrations ≤ 50 μM after cells were treated for 24 h (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6A). Thus, we incubated cells with 50 μM TLCA for 24 h in following experiment. Subsequent experimental results revealed that, in comparison with the CON group, the TLCA group elicited a significant augmentation in the mRNA expression levels of MYH7B (P < 0.001), while concurrently inducing a marked suppression of the mRNA expression levels of MYH1A (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). We further scrutinized the expression profiles of genes and enzymes associated with cellular oxidative metabolism and glycolysis. The results indicated that, relative to the CON group, the TLCA group significantly enhanced the activity of SDH (P = 0.007) while attenuating the activity of LDH (P = 0.020), and markedly elevated the intracellular ATP content (P = 0.011) and mtDNA content (P = 0.017) (Fig. 6C). Then, we assessed the mRNA expression levels of glycolysis-related genes and discovered that TLCA significantly downregulated the mRNA expression levels of HK1 (P = 0.003), GPI (P = 0.001), PGAM1 (P = 0.005), PGK1 (P = 0.030), and PYGL (P = 0.009) (Fig. 6D). In contrast, TLCA significantly upregulated the mRNA expression levels related to oxidative metabolism such as PGC-1α (P < 0.001), NRF1 (P = 0.008), and TFAM (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6D), and increased PGC-1α protein levels (P = 0.026) (Fig. 6E). Meanwhile, we observed that TLCA significantly promoted the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK (P = 0.005) (Fig. 6E). These data suggested that TLCA regulated mitochondrial biogenesis and altered myofiber type composition through the p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway. To determine whether TLCA drives myofiber type transition via the p38 MAPK/PGC-1α axis, we co-treated cells with TLCA and the p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580. As expected, the effects of TLCA on the above phenotypes were significantly weakened by SB203580 (Fig. 6F-I). Together, our data showed that TLCA regulated mitochondrial biogenesis and altered myofiber type composition through the p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway.

Discussion

In recent years, although the importance of gut microbiota is widely accepted, how to leverage the microbial resources of elite local breeds and employ microbiota interventions to improve host metabolism and overall broiler performance remains an urgent challenge (Hu et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024). Therefore, it is necessary to deeply understand the composition characteristics and succession patterns of poultry gut microbiota across physiological stages, enabling optimal interventions and maximal utilization of the microbial resources harbored by elite local breeds. In this study, we used the fast-growing commercial broiler AA as a control and tracked in detail the establishment and succession of microbiota in four gut segments, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum, of both AA and TY chickens. While previous studies have focused predominantly on the hindgut, our comparative analysis of both the foregut and hindgut revealed a consistent pattern that the cecal microbiota exhibited significantly higher alpha diversity than the duodenal, jejunal, and ileal communities in both AA and TY chickens. This finding aligns with the general principle that microbial abundance and diversity increase along the digestive tract, with the cecum—as a site of prolonged retention and fermentation—supporting the most complex and abundant microbial ecosystem (Yadav and Jha, 2019).

The succession of gut microbiota is driven by multiple factors, resulting in pronounced spatiotemporal variation (Feng et al., 2023). In both AA and TY chickens, the microbial profile of each gut segment at D1 was distinct from later time points. However, only the cecal community exhibited a clear chronological succession with age. This dynamic turnover reflects a more complex and competitive microbial environment, leading us to postulate that the cecum is the intestinal segment most strongly influenced by host growth and development. Previous studies have established the regulatory role of gut microbiota on the host; more recently, deepening research has revealed that the host can actively shape its microbial community through epigenetic modifications and altered gene expression (Pepke et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2020). In contrast to the foregut, which is primarily dedicated to nutrient digestion and absorption, the cecum serves far more complex functions. Its substantial microbial community engages in a two-way, or bidirectional, interaction with the host—both influencing and being influenced by host physiology. This dynamic renders the development of the cecal microbiota more intricate and distinctive than that of the foregut. (Godoy-Vitorino et al., 2012; Mu et al., 2017; Munn et al., 2022).

Following hatching (D1), the microbiota across all four gut segments in both AA and TY chickens was dominated by Firmicutes and Proteobacteria at the phylum level, with Escherichia-Shigella and Enterococcus being the predominant genera. From D7 onward, while differences among segments intensified, the three foregut sections maintained remarkable similarity, contrasting sharply with the substantial divergence observed between the foregut and the cecum. This divergence can be attributed to key environmental factors within the gut lumen, such as oxygen concentration, pH, and nutrient availability, which govern microbial colonization (Berry et al., 2013; Espey, 2013; O'May et al., 2005). The progressive decline in oxygen concentration along the digestive tract results in a corresponding shift in the microbial community from aerobes to strict anaerobes (De Vos et al., 2022). Consequently, the foregut is predominantly colonized by Firmicutes, while the oxygen-depleted cecum creates an ideal niche for strict anaerobic Bacteroidota. While our findings delineate a shared landscape of gut microbiota colonization in AA and TY chickens, the most pronounced distinction lies in the assembly of their cecal microbiota. Strikingly, the relative abundance of Bacteroidota decreases with a concomitant rise in Firmicutes in AA chickens, whereas TY chickens exhibit the opposite trend. This divergence is critical as the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio reflects the host's energy-harvesting capacity, with a higher ratio promoting greater weight gain (Magne et al., 2020; Mariat et al., 2009). The rapid weight gain of AA chickens, a product of intensive artificial selection, is associated with their elevated Firmicutes levels and higher F/B ratio. In contrast, the unselected TY chickens, with their higher abundance of Bacteroidota and lower F/B ratio, exhibit slower growth. We further characterized microbial community dynamics using DMM models, which revealed that TY chickens achieved a relatively stable community state in the jejunum, ileum, and cecum at later ages than AA chickens. This delay in microbial stabilization may contribute to their slower growth trajectory. The duodenum was an exception, exhibiting frequent community-state transitions in both breeds, likely due to its simpler and more dynamic microbial community (McCallum and Tropini, 2024). Our research revealed that prolonged artificial selection in the commercial AA line has imposed a "precocious" effect on intestinal microbiota maturation, accelerating its developmental timeline, despite underlying shared similarities in colonization with the indigenous TY breed.

In the present work, time-sequential monitoring revealed both shared and divergent patterns of pectoral muscle development between AA and TY chickens. In both breeds, myofiber types underwent a conversion from type I to type II, an obligatory trend during pectoral muscle development. Notably, we found that the abundance of type I fibers in TY chickens was significantly higher than in AA birds, and the transition from type I to type II fibers was markedly delayed in TY chickens compared with AA chickens. Suspecting that the phenotypic divergence between these two broiler breeds might be driven by their respective gut microbiota, we linked microbial colonization to host phenotypes through correlation analysis. We found that, in both AA and TY chickens, the cecal microbiota was significantly correlated with host pectoral muscle fiber development. To verify whether the cecal microbiota affects fiber development, we conducted an IMT experiment. Here, IMT markedly altered the muscle fiber phenotype in both AA and TY chickens, shifting the phenotype of the recipient birds toward that of the donors. Some of the phenotypic changes were already pronounced at D21 and persisted for at least one week after transplantation ceased. This result is also supported by previous results showing that early-life IMT-mediated manipulation of the gut microbiota can effectively modulate muscle fiber development (Chen et al., 2024b; Lahiri et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2023; Raymo et al., 2024). To clarify the link between altered muscle fiber phenotypes and the gut microbiota, we collected cecal contents for metagenomic sequencing. The results revealed that IMT significantly reshaped both the structure and composition of the microbiota in AA and TY chickens at D21 and D28, with the impact being more pronounced in AA chickens. We observed that the change in the relative abundance of P. dorei, the most distinctive signature in the IMT groups, was characterized by increases in AA_IMT groups at D21 and D28 and by a marked decrease in TY_IMT groups. This indicates that microbiota derived from the TY cecum promoted the colonization of P. dorei in the AA cecum, whereas microbiota from the AA cecum suppressed its colonization in the TY cecum. Interestingly, we also identified serum DEM TLCA exhibiting similar characteristics, which was significantly up-regulated in the AA_IMT groups and down-regulated in the TY_IMT groups at both D21 and D28. As a secondary bile acid, TLCA can only be produced with the participation of gut microbes (Perino and Schoonjans, 2022). Therefore, it is undeniably a metabolite derived from gut microbiota. We also found that both the AA_IMT and TY_Ctrl groups were significantly enriched for secondary bile acid biosynthesis at D21. Previous studies have shown that P. dorei can indirectly influence secondary bile acid production by regulating bile-salt hydrolase activity (Choi et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2023). Thus, we hypothesize that P. dorei might be the key bacterium controlling TLCA generation, and TLCA is the critical metabolite driving transformation of muscle fiber types.

In this study, we found that TLCA treatment significantly up-regulated the expression of oxidative-fiber marker genes and down-regulated that of glycolytic-fiber marker genes. In addition, TLCA treatment markedly increased cellular mitochondrial content, oxidative phosphorylation capacity, and mitochondrial respiratory function. We also specifically noted that PGC-1α responded robustly to TLCA treatment. As a transcriptional coactivator, PGC-1α orchestrates mitochondrial biogenesis and modulates diverse energy-metabolic processes, including glucose and lipid metabolism (Li et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). The composition of muscle fiber types is intimately tied to their metabolic patterns, and a series of evidences have demonstrated that PGC-1α can modulate fiber-type composition by orchestrating cellular metabolism, while PGC-1α activation and expression are in turn governed by upstream p38 MAPK signaling (Song et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). In our study, we likewise observed that TLCA markedly enhanced p38 MAPK phosphorylation. However, this regulatory effect was sharply attenuated when a p38 MAPK inhibitor (SB203580) was applied, and the phenotypic alterations induced by TLCA treatment were significantly blunted, thereby demonstrating that TLCA modulates the transformation of muscle fiber type via the p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway.

Notably, IMT altered PMW and fiber type without affecting final BW, indicating that the gut microbiota influences muscle quality through a pathway distinct from general somatic growth regulation. Our in vitro experiments demonstrate that TLCA directly activates p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling in CPMs to drive oxidative fiber gene expression, supporting a cell-autonomous mechanism. Given that the pectoralis major in commercial broilers is predominantly composed of glycolytic fibers, it may be particularly responsive to microbial metabolites that promote oxidative remodeling. Whether similar effects occur in other muscles remains to be determined. Importantly, while P. dorei and TLCA were strongly correlated, direct evidence that P. dorei produces TLCA in chickens is lacking. Future studies employing gnotobiotic models or in vitro anaerobic cultures with bile acid substrates will be needed to establish causality in this bacterium–metabolite relationship.

Conclusion

In summary, our results unveiled the co-variation patterns between intestinal microbiota colonization and pectoral myofiber characteristics in fast-growing commercial broilers (AA) versus slow-growing indigenous chickens (TY), highlighting the predominant contribution of the cecal microbiota to myofiber development. Subsequent IMT experiments and in vitro metabolite assays further demonstrated that the gut microbiota modulates myofiber development via the p38 MAPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway through specific metabolites. The present findings revealed the intricate mechanism by which the gut microbiota modulates host muscle development via a metabolite- signaling pathway axis, providing critical evidence for understanding the relationship between gut microbes and myofiber development.
