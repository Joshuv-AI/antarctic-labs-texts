Progressive spinal cord involvement in Leigh syndrome due to an NDUFV1 variant
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord involvement in Leigh syndrome (LS) due to an NDUFV1 variant has rarely been reported. The patient is a 4-month-old girl who developed generalized weakness, decreased vigilance, lethargy, cyanosis and unresponsiveness. Investigation revealed lactic acidosis, mild hyponatremia, normocytic anemia, elevated troponin, elevated pro-brain natriuretic peptide, Mobitz II block, systolic dysfunction, and pulmonary hypertension. Cerebral MRI showed symmetrical T2 hyperintensities in the brainstem and supra-tentorial, suggestive of LS, as well as T2 and STIR hyperintensities of the fasciculus gracilis and posterior and lateral gray matter horns, which were hypointense at T1. Seventeen days later, these lesions were more pronounced on T2 and STIR than before. Genetic testing revealed the compound heterozygous variants c.1162+4A>C and c.1138G>C in NDUFV1. In summary, mutations in NDUFV1 can also manifest phenotypically in the spinal cord and present clinically with sensory disturbances, spasticity and spinal ataxia. In patients with LS due to NDUFV1 variants, not only imaging of the brain but also of the spinal cord should be performed.Keywords: Complex-I deficiency, Mitochondrial DNA, NDUFV1, Respiratory chain, Leigh syndrome

Introduction

Leigh syndrome (LS), also known as subacute necrotizing encephalomyelopathy, is a rare, multisystemic mitochondrial disorder (MID, #OMIM 256000) first described in 1951 and caused by mutations in either nDNA (two-thirds of cases) or mtDNA genes (one-third of cases) [1]. Mutations in more than 100 genes are currently thought to be responsible for the disease [1]. The composition and function of complex I of the respiratory chain is most frequently affected by these mutations [1]. Phenotypically, LS is a multisystem disease, with the central nervous system (CNS) being the most commonly clinically affected organ. Radiologically, LS is characterized by symmetrical lesions that typically extend from the basal ganglia and thalamus through brainstem structures to the cerebellum and posterior columns [1]. Histologically, the CNS lesions present as capillary proliferation, gliosis, severe neuronal loss and relative preservation of astrocytes [1].

One of the nDNA genes mutated in LS is NDUFV1, which encodes a subunit of complex I [2]. Phenotypically, these patients show global developmental delay, epilepsy, cerebellar ataxia, dystonia, cystic leukoencephalopathy, spasticity, generalized hypotonia, progressive external ophthalmoplegia, nystagmus, failure to thrive, cardiomyopathy and arterial hypertension [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. In a study of 44 patients with LS due to NDUFV1 variants, 3 patterns were described on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): a Leigh type with involvement of the thalamus, putamen, basal ganglia and brainstem, a leukodystrophy type with cysts in the white matter, and a mixture of both [2]. Exon 8 was most frequently mutated in this cohort [2]. Spinal cord involvement in NDUFV1 mutation carriers has rarely been reported [5,7]. Spinal cord involvement in LS due to the compound heterozygous variants c.1162A>C and c.1138G>C has not yet been reported.

Case presentation

The patient is a 4-month-old girl who was born by caesarean section with an inguinal and umbilical hernia, an open foramen ovale, a deviated subclavian vein and horizontal nystagmus. The family history revealed no genetic or acquired diseases. There was no consanguinity between the parents. At the age of 4 months, she successively developed generalized weakness, decreased alertness, lethargy, cyanosis and unresponsiveness. Blood tests revealed lactic acidosis of 4.8 mmol/l (n <2.2 mmol/l), hyponatremia of 125 mmol/l (n, 135-145 mmol/l) and normocytic anemia. The patient was intubated and mechanically ventilated. She was treated with a bolus of hypertonic saline, hydrocortisone, ceftriaxone and a bolus of levetiracetam. The course in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) was complicated by arterial hypotension, which is why norepinephrine was administered. The ECG showed a Mobitz II block, and echocardiography revealed right systolic dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension. Troponin was elevated to 9.8 ng/ml (n, <2 ng/ml) and pro-brain natriuretic peptide to 1554 ng/l (n, <300 ng/l), and lactate was further elevated to 12.04 mmol/l. Milrinone and nitric oxide were administered. Follow-up echocardiography revealed right ventricular hypertrophy and a slight enlargement of the right ventricle, which had not been detected during the initial examination.

Cerebral MRI showed symmetrical T2 hyperintensities in the brainstem and supra-tentorial, suggestive of LS (Fig. 1). MRI of the spinal cord showed T2 and STIR hyperintensities of the gracilis fascicle and posterior and lateral gray matter horns, which were hypointense at T1 (Fig. 2). She was extubated on hospital day 11, but had to be intubated again 1 day later due to apnea and cyanosis. Glucocorticoids and intravenous immunoglobulins were administered. The sedation was then reduced, but the CO2 level remained high. Seventeen days after the first MRI, the previously found lesions were more clearly visible on T2 and STIR than before (Fig. 3). Genetic testing revealed the heterozygous variants c.1162+4A>C and c.1138G>C in NDUFV1. Despite maximal metabolic, cardiac and pulmonary therapy, the patient died of intractable heart failure 5 weeks after the onset of the disease.

MRI of the spinal cord showed T2 and STIR hyperintensity of the gracilis fascicle and the dorsal and lateral horn of grey matter (panels A to C, white arrows), which were hypointense on T1 (panel D, white arrow).

Spinal cord MRI 17 days later showed that these lesions were more pronounced on T2 and STIR than before (panels A to C, white arrows). Hypointensity on T1 has remained unchanged (panel D, white arrow).

Discussion

The presented patient with LS due to an NDUFV1 variant, which phenotypically manifested not only in the brain but also in the entire spinal cord, is interesting. Spinal cord involvement in NDUFV1 mutation carriers is rare and has only been reported in a few patients [7]. In 1 of these patients, a 13-year-old girl, spinal cord lesions involved the lateral corticospinal tract and dorsal columns since the age of 3 years [7]. In addition, T2-hyperintense, patchy signals were found in the upper cervical spinal cord [7]. At the age of 13 years, T2 axial images of the upper cervical spinal cord showed a high-intensity area in the central part of the spinal cord [7]. Another NDUFV1 mutation carrier, a 4-year-old boy, was found to have focal T1 and T2 hypointense lesions in the cervical spinal cord and symmetrical lesions in the midbrain, pons and bulb [8]. In a male in his mid-forties carrying an NDUFV1 variant, T2 imaging of the spinal cord showed a hyperintensity signal in the posterior two-thirds of the spinal cord over the entire extent, but predominantly in the cervical region [9]. Axial cervical MRI images showed that these lesions were inhomogeneous and also involved the anterior horn [9]. Three weeks later, the spinal cord abnormalities had only partially regressed [9]. At autopsy, hematoxylin-eosin and Luxol staining revealed extensive damage to the upper spinal cord in the anterior, lateral, and posterior tracts [9]. Partial or complete spinal cord involvement was also reported in a 24-year-old female NDUFV1 carrier [10]. MRI of the cervical spine in this patient showed a long T2-weighted signal abnormality of the spinal cord extending from C1 to T2 [10]. Postmortem hematoxylin-eosin staining of the spinal cord at all levels showed CD163-positive macrophage infiltration and evidence of myelin within the macrophages, with the dorsal columns severely affected while the corticospinal tracts were spared [10]. Spinal cord impairments have also been reported in other NDUFV1 mutation carriers, without specifying which pathways were specifically affected and which MRI modalities were used to detect these lesions [2].

Spinal cord involvement has been also demonstrated in patients with LS due to either mtDNA variants [11,12] or due to nDNA mutations in genes other than NDUFV1 [13]. In a 7-month-old child with complex IV deficiency, MRI of the spinal cord was normal, but postmortem examination of the spinal cord revealed symmetrical lesions throughout the spinal cord involving both gray and white matter [11]. The lesions in the spinal cord involved the ventral and lateral funiculi around the gray matter as well as parts of the ventral horns and fasciculi cuneatus and gracilis [11]. In an 11-year-old girl with complex IV deficiency, spinal cord imaging showed diffuse signal changes within the cervical spinal cord [1]. In a 2-month-old boy with the homoplasmic variant m.3275C>G in tRNA(Leu), MRI of the cervical spinal cord showed abnormal signal intensity and restricted diffusion [12]. There are even syndromic MIDs with exclusive or predominant involvement of the spinal cord, such as leukoencephalopathy with brainstem and spinal cord involvement and lactic acidosis (LBSL) [13].

Conclusions

This case shows that mutations in NDUFV1 can also manifest phenotypically in the spinal cord. Spinal cord involvement may be responsible for sensory disturbances, spasticity and spinal ataxia. In patients with LS due to variants in NDUFV1, not only brain but also spinal cord imaging should be performed to accurately explain the phenotype in these patients.
