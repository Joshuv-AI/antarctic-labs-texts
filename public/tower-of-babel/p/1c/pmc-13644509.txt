Effects of early-life tactile experience enrichment on autistic-like behaviors and synaptic alterations in Shank3 mutant mice
Authors: Mingyue Liu, Guaiguai Ma, Yangming Zhang, Kezi Li, Fei Li, Tao Peng, Congcong Qin, Violeta G López-Huerta, Mario A Arias-Garcia, Wenting Wang, Dayun Feng, Shengxi Wu, Baolin Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) presents a significant therapeutic challenge due to limited treatment options. While non-pharmacological approaches based on sensory stimulation have shown promise in clinical practice, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study explored the therapeutic potential of tactile experience enrichment (TEE) during specific developmental windows in Shank3 KO mice, a model of ASD. We found that pre-weaning TEE robustly rescued core behavioral deficits, including social interaction and repetitive grooming, whereas juvenile TEE only improved social behavior. RNA sequencing of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and dorsolateral striatum (DLS) revealed TEE-induced alterations in synaptic gene expression, including the upregulation of the Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK-1) pathway, which may modulate synaptic maturation. Furthermore, pre-weaning TEE restored dendritic spine density and morphology, along with synaptic transmission, in both the ACC and DLS. These findings indicate that early tactile enrichment, particularly during the pre-weaning period, can effectively alleviate core ASD-related behaviors in Shank3 KO mice by modulating synaptic structure and function, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic intervention for ASD.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Tactile experience enrichment, Social behaviors, Repetitive behaviors, Synapse, Spine

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental condition arising from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental influences [1,2]. Its diverse symptoms primarily manifest as deficits in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities [3]. The rising global prevalence of ASD over recent decades has emerged as a critical public health challenge. ASD epidemiological studies indicate a median worldwide prevalence of 1.0 % [4], 0.70 % in Chinese pediatric populations [5] and approximately 1 % in Europe [6]. Despite this substantial disease burden, effective pharmacological interventions for core symptoms remain severely limited, primarily due to the paucity of identified druggable targets. Consequently, non-pharmacological approaches, particularly behavioral interventions, have shown promise in mitigating autistic symptoms [7].

The neurodevelopmental nature of ASD underscores the importance of considering the precise timing of developmental processes when designing interventions [8,9]. These processes, including neuronal proliferation and migration, axon guidance, synaptogenesis, and activity-dependent refinement, unfold within specific time windows and vary across different brain circuits [10]. Clinical evidence demonstrates that the efficacy of non-pharmacological interventions is often linked to critical developmental periods, reinforcing the importance of timing in treatment strategies [11]. Therefore, identifying non-invasive therapies and optimizing their application during development is essential for maximizing clinical outcomes.

Large-scale genomic studies have identified numerous ASD risk genes, including SH3 and multiple ankyrin repeat domains protein 3 (SHANK3) [12]. SHANK3 encodes a scaffolding protein located at the postsynaptic density (PSD) of excitatory glutamatergic synapses [13]. Disruptions in SHANK3 expression in mouse models produce autistic-like behaviors, including impaired social interaction, repetitive behaviors, and other abnormalities [14]. These Shank3 mutant mouse models provide valuable tools for investigating the neural underpinnings of ASD. Research using these models has revealed a range of brain regions and circuits implicated in autistic behaviors [[15], [16], [17], [18]], providing a foundation for exploring how non-pharmacological interventions might modulate these key areas to improve behavioral manifestations.

Many autism-associated genes encode proteins localized at synapses, highlighting the importance of synaptic function in ASD [19]. Synaptic dysfunction, including immature dendritic morphology, altered synaptic transmission, and plasticity, has been observed in various autism models [[20], [21], [22]]. These models also exhibit significant changes in the molecular composition of synapses, leading to morphological and functional defects with potential neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric consequences [23]. Synaptic restoration, therefore, represents a promising therapeutic avenue. A pioneering study using a mouse model of Rett syndrome demonstrated that presymptomatic training improved motor performance and memory, along with rescuing hippocampal synaptic morphology and electrophysiology [24]. Given the neurodevelopmental deficits associated with ASD, interventions during critical developmental windows are likely to be highly impactful. In rodent models, postnatal day 21 (P21) roughly corresponds to the transition into early adolescence in humans, which is a period marked by synaptic maturation, heightened critical period plasticity, and the emergence of complex social behaviors post-weaning [25,26]. However, it remains to be determined whether the therapeutic efficacy of non-pharmacological interventions differs between early developmental stages and adolescence, and whether such interventions can effectively ameliorate synaptic deficits in autism-relevant brain circuits to improve core symptoms.

Environmental enrichment, involving exposure to diverse sensory stimuli, has emerged as a potential adjunctive therapy for various neurological disorders, including neurodevelopmental conditions [27]. Within the spectrum of sensory experiences, tactile stimulation holds particular relevance for ASD, given the frequent occurrence of tactile hypo- or hypersensitivity in individuals with this diagnosis [28]. This tactile abnormality has officially been incorporated as a diagnostic criterion for ASD in DSM-5. Promisingly, studies in both humans and rodents suggest that tactile stimulation may exert beneficial effects in ASD [29,30]. Therefore, this study investigated the therapeutic potential of a well-established tactile experience enrichment (TEE) environment [[31], [32], [33]] in Shank3 mutant mice. We aimed to assess the effectiveness of TEE at different developmental stages in mitigating autistic-like behaviors, determine the critical developmental period for intervention, and elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects.

Materials and methods

Animals

Mice were housed in ventilated cages under controlled temperature and humidity with a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 08:00). Food and water were available ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (FMMU) and adhered to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The approval number is FMMULL-20240830. Shank3 KO mice (Jackson lab, Strain # 017688, RRID: IMSR_JAX:017688) were bred from heterozygous Shank3 parents. The genotypes were determined by PCR of mouse tail DNA as previously reported [34]: primer F1b (GAGCTCTACTCCCTTAGGACTT) and R1b (TCCCCCTTTCACTGGACACCC) for the WT allele, and F1b and R2 (TCAGGGTTATTGTCTCATGAGC) for the mutant allele. WT littermates served as controls. Both male and female mice were used in this study, and data from male and female mice were pooled for all analyses after confirming no significant sex differences in preliminary assessments. We used separate cohorts and multiple litters of mice receiving the same treatment for behavioral, molecular, and electrophysiological experiments. The experimenters were blinded to the genotypes and groups of animals.

Tactile enrichment

For the TEE, mice were housed in cages containing suspended strings of plastic beads (8 mm diameter). The bead density and height were configured to ensure full contact with the whiskers, beard, and back hair during normal activity, providing harmless tactile stimulation. The plastic beads were thoroughly cleaned with 70 % ethanol and air-dried before use to eliminate residual odors. The presence of the beads did not impede normal cage activity. Control mice were housed in a normal environment. Both NE and TEE groups received food and water ad libitum. For pre-weaning TEE, dams were individually housed 3–5 days pre-parturition to minimize stress. Subsequently, the dams were maintained in cages with their pups throughout the pre-weaning TEE phase (Fig. S1). For the adolescent TEE treatment, the adolescent mice were group-housed (4–6 mice per cage) for all experimental groups.

Sparse labeling and morphological analysis

The plasmid of AAVPhP.eB-hSyn-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf (provided by Professor Guoping Feng, McGovern Institute for Brain Research, MIT) was packaged by PackGene Biotech in China and AAVPhP.eB- hSyn-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf with a titer of 1.3E + 12 gc/mL was delivered via retro-orbital injection. Mice were deeply anesthetized with 3 % isoflurane and positioned horizontally to facilitate injection. A total of 50 µL of virus was slowly injected into the orbital vein using a 1 mL insulin syringe. Following the injection, a sterile cotton ball was applied to the injection site for approximately 2 min. Mice were then returned to their cages and monitored until recovery from anesthesia. Tissue samples were collected by perfusion 3 weeks post-injection.

For sample collection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with 0.01 M phosphate buffer saline (PBS). Brains were extracted and sectioned into 200 µm coronal slices using a vibratome. Neurons within the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and dorsolateral striatum (DLS) were imaged using an FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan). Whole-neuron images were acquired at 20 × magnification, and dendritic spines were imaged at 60 ×. Neuronal morphology was reconstructed from confocal images using NeuronJ (ImageJ). Apical and basal dendrite length, number, and branching were quantified using the Sholl Analysis plugin. Dendritic spines were analyzed using Imaris software (version 7.7.1, Bitplane). The experimenters were blinded to the groups when analyzing the spine morphology.

Behavioral tests

Mice were handled for 5–10 min daily for 5 days prior to behavioral testing to acclimate them to the experimenter. Mice were habituated to the testing room for at least 2 h before each experiment. The behavioral testing was conducted over two consecutive days following an optimized sequence designed to minimize stress interference: on Day 1, we performed the three-chamber social test followed by grooming assessment after a 2-hour interval, and on Day 2, we conducted the open field test followed by the elevated plus maze after another 2-hour interval. The experimenters were blinded to the genotypes and groups when conducting the behavioral tests and analyses.

Open field test

The open field test was conducted in an opaque acrylic arena (50 × 50 × 40 cm) with a defined central zone (20 × 20 cm). Each mouse was placed at a consistent starting location within the open field, and their behavior was recorded. The arena was uniformly illuminated to eliminate dark corners, with a light intensity of ∼10 lx. Data were analyzed using SMART v.3.0 software.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze test was conducted using a maze consisting of two open arms (30 × 5 × 30 cm3) and two enclosed arms (30 × 5 × 30 cm3) extending from a central platform (5 × 5 cm2). Mice were placed in the center, facing an open arm, and allowed to explore for 10 min. The light intensity was ∼10 lx. Data were analyzed using SMART v.3.0 software.

Three-chamber social interaction test

Sociability was assessed in a three-chambered apparatus consisting of a central chamber (40 × 20 cm2) and two side chambers (40 × 20 cm2). A same-sex, unfamiliar conspecific was enclosed in a cylinder within one side chamber (social chamber), while an identical but empty cylinder was placed in the opposite side chamber (non-social chamber). The test mouse was initially placed in the central chamber and, following removal of the dividing walls, allowed to freely explore all three chambers for 10 min. The light intensity was ∼10 lx. Data were analyzed using SMART v.3.0 software.

Grooming test

Self-grooming behavior was assessed in a cage (20 × 20 × 25 cm3) for 1 hour. Grooming was defined as licking paws, washing nose/face/head, and grooming body, tail, or genitals.

In vitro electrophysiology

Brain slice preparation

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and transcardially perfused with 15–20 mL of ice-cold, carbogenated (95 % O2, 5 % CO2) cutting solution (194 mM sucrose, 30 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 0.2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 26 mM NaHCO3, and 10 mM d-(+)-glucose, 340–350 mOsm). Brains were extracted, placed in ice-cold cutting solution, and 300 μm coronal slices were prepared. Slices were incubated in carbogenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) at 32 °C for 10–15 min and then at room temperature for at least 1 hour in aCSF (119 mM NaCl, 2.3 mM KCl, 1.0 mM NaH2PO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM glucose, 1.3 mM MgSO4, and 2.5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.4, 295–305 mOsm).

sEPSCs recordings

For spontaneous synaptic activity measurements, slices were continuously perfused with carbogenated aCSF at room temperature (22–24 °C). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed using IR-DIC visualization. Pipettes for sEPSCs contained: 110 CsOH (50 % wt), D-gluconic acid (49 %–53 % wt), 4 mM NaCl, 15 mM KCl, 5 mM TEA-Cl, 20 mM HEPES, 0.2 mM EGTA, 5 mM lidocaine N-ethyl chloride, 4 mM ATP magnesium salt, and 0.3 mM GTP sodium salt (pH 7.2–7.4 adjusted with CsOH, osmolarity 298–300 mOsm adjusted with ∼8–10 mM K2SO4). Spontaneous synaptic event recording began 5 min after establishing whole-cell configuration to allow Cs+ dialysis and potassium channel blockage in both ACC pyramidal neurons and striatal medium spiny neurons (MSNs). Cells with series resistance (Rs) changes > 20 % (typically 8–12 MΩ) or Rs > 20 MΩ during recording were excluded. Cells were held at –70 mV (unless otherwise noted) using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices). Signals were filtered at 2 kHz, sampled at 10 kHz (Digidata 1440A, Molecular Devices), and stored for offline analysis. mEPSCs and mIPSCs were analyzed using MiniAnalysis (Synaptosoft) by experimenters blinded to the groups of cells.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane. Using pre-treated RNase-free and autoclaved instruments to extract the brain and place it on ice to prevent RNA degradation. The ACC and DLS tissues were collected via coronal dissection under RNase-free conditions. RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and quality was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). mRNA was purified with oligo (dT) beads, fragmented, and reverse transcribed to generate cDNA using random hexamer primers. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was carried out with DNA polymerase I, RNase H, dNTPs, and reaction buffer. The resulting cDNA fragments were purified with a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), end-repaired, adenylated, and adapter-ligated for Illumina sequencing. Following size selection via agarose gel electrophoresis and PCR amplification, the libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Bioinformatics analysis

The raw sequencing data were stored in FASTQ format. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), which normalizes the read counts (Count) for transcript length and sequencing depth. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the ACC and DLS following TEE exposure, we conducted comparative gene expression analysis. DEGs were screened using DESeq2 with the following thresholds: |log₂FoldChange| ≥ 0.5 and adjusted P value < 0.05. For functional enrichment analysis, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the R package fgsea in combination with the C2 pathway gene sets from the MSigDB database (Mus musculus category). Significantly enriched pathways were selected based on P value < 0.05, aiming to uncover potential associations of DEGs with cellular components, biological processes, and molecular functions.

Cell culture and immunocytochemistry

The Neuro-2a mouse neuroblastoma cell line used in this study was obtained from the Shanghai Cell Resource Center, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Corning) supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; vazyme) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). All cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5 % CO₂ atmosphere to ensure optimal growth conditions.

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from 16-day-old C57BL/6 J mouse fetuses. Following euthanasia of the pregnant mouse, the fetuses were extracted and immediately placed in ice-cold Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; Sigma). The cerebral cortices were carefully dissected from the fetal brains and collected. Cortical tissues were then mechanically dissociated through repeated trituration using a Pasteur pipette and 0.125 % trypsin without EDTA for 10 min at 37 °C. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 10 min, the resulting cell pellets were resuspended in neurobasal medium (Gibco) supplemented with 2 % B27 (Gibco), 2 mM glutamine (Gibco), and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. The cell suspension was plated onto poly-l-lysine-coated 35-mm dishes or coverslips, with half-medium changes performed every 3 days to maintain optimal culture conditions. At 16 days in vitro, we treated cultures with Ro3280 (100 nM, MCE) and performed morphological and functional assays after 24 h.

For immunocytochemistry, primary cortical neurons were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 for 15 min. The fixed cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-MAP2 (1:400, Abcam), guinea pig anti-Homer 1 (1:800, Alomone Labs), and rabbit anti-GluA1 (1:200, Abcam). After washing, the cells were incubated with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. For Dil (MCE) staining, neurons were incubated with 5 μM Dil working solution for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by fixation with 4 % paraformaldehyde. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the ACC and DLS tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596018). Reverse transcription was performed with Transcriptor First cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche) following the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR amplification was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (vazyme) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche Applied Science, Germany) with the following primer sets: 5′-CTGCCCAGAACATCATCCCT-3′ (forward) and 5′-TGAAGTCGCAGGAGACAACC-3′ (reverse) for Gapdh; 5′-CCAACCAGTCCTCTTTTGACAA-3′ (forward) and 5′- GCCTAGAAGCATAATCACCACG-3′ (reverse) for Rab37; 5′-GGTAAGTGCCGAGCTGAGATG′ (forward) and 5′-CGACCTGTGCAACCCTTTC-3′ (reverse) for Arc; 5′-TTGCCTGACAATCTGTACCCC′ (forward) and 5′- TAATGGGCTACCGAGTCGCT-3′ (reverse) for Egr3; 5′-TCACGACGACTCTTACGCAG′ (forward) and 5′-CCTTGAGACCCCGATAGGGA-3′ (reverse) for Junb; 5′-TGAGAAAAGGATTCGGCGACT′ (forward) and 5′-CTGGCACAGGTTCGATCACT-3′ (reverse) for Sytl4; 5′-CTTCCTGGGCAAGGAGGAC′ (forward) and 5′-CGTTTGATCCAAAGTTTCAGCTC-3′ (reverse) for Rcn1; 5′-CACAGTGGCGTTGACTAC-3′ (forward) and 5′-AGATGAGAGTTGAGCTGGTGA-3′ (reverse) for Cav2; 5′-CCGGGATTGGTTGGCTTCAA-3′ (forward) and 5′- AATGCTGCGAAACTTTGGTTTT-3′ (reverse) for Sytl4; 5′-ACAAGAGCATCCACCTGAGC-3′ (forward) and 5′- TTCTCTGCCTTGGACTCACG-3′ (reverse) for Grin1; 5′-ACTGCAAGCTGTGCTGGATT-3′ (forward) and 5′-GTTTTGAGCTCCATGATGTGAATG-3′ (reverse) for Gria2; 5′-AGGAAGTGGCAGTGGAGAGA-3′ (forward) and 5′-GGCTCATGCAGGGAATGGAT-3′ (reverse) for Gria1; 5′-GCTACCAAGATGAAGACACGCC-3′ (forward) and 5′-GGGGAGAATTGGCCTGGTTG-3′ (reverse) for Psd95; 5′-ACCAGTACCCCTTCACAGGA-3′ (forward) and 5′-TTGCTGAATTGAACGTGTACC-3′ (reverse) for Gria1; 5′-ATGCGACAGTATGACACCCG-3′ (forward) and 5′-CAGTGTAGTGGCGGAAGAGA-3′ (reverse) for Shank3; 5′-TGCCAACAAGACGGAGAGTG-3′ (forward) and 5′-TAGTGCCCCCTTTAACGCAG-3′ (reverse) for Syp.

Statistical analysis

All statistics are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and SPSS 21.0. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test, and normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Parametric tests were used for data meeting assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance: two-tailed unpaired t-tests, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, two-tailed paired t-tests, and two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Non-parametric tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. See Table S1 for statistical details.

Results

Effects of pre-weaning TEE on behavioral phenotypes in Shank3 KO mice

While environmental enrichment demonstrates positive effects on neural modulation and potential efficacy as an intervention for ASD, it involves highly augmented stimuli encompassing complex sensory and motor inputs. For easier future translational applications, we aim to isolate a single stimulus. Previous research indicates tactile input as a dominant sensory source within multimodal enriched environments [32], particularly impacting cognition and emotion. Given the atypical tactile sensation observed in autistic individuals, we introduced an environment saturated with tactile stimuli for our animal model. Recognizing ASD as a neurodevelopmental disorder, we applied this TEE to mice during early postnatal development (P0-P21, until weaning). Our TEE paradigm employed active stimulus reception, using multiple hanging bead curtains composed of multiple bead strings. Animals aged P0-P21 received stimulation upon initiating autonomous activity within their home cages (Fig. 1a, Fig. S1a-b). TEE intervention did not significantly affect maternal behaviors in dams that were maintained in cages with their pups throughout the pre-weaning TEE phase (Fig. S1c-e).

To assess long-term TEE effects, 4–6 animals were housed for an additional three weeks post-weaning before behavioral testing in adulthood. We performed tests focused on core autistic symptoms, social deficits and repetitive behaviors, alongside other abnormalities like anxiety and locomotor hypoactivity. We compared TEE-exposed Shank3 KO mice with WT and KO mice housed in standard environments. In the three-chamber test, WT and standard-housed KO mice exhibited distinct social preferences: WT mice spent more time in the compartment with a stranger mouse than in the empty compartment, while KO mice lost this preference, even displaying a preference for the empty compartment. However, early postnatally TEE-exposed KO mice demonstrated a social preference comparable to WT mice, spending more time with the social cue (Fig. 1b). No significant sex-based differences were observed across the groups (Fig. S1f-h). Regarding repetitive grooming, standard-housed KO mice showed significantly increased grooming duration, but early TEE exposure reduced grooming time in KO mice (Fig. 1c). We also used open field and elevated plus maze tests to assess anxiety levels. We observed that KO animals exhibited increased anxiety. While TEE-exposed KO mice showed a trend towards increased average time in the central area of the open field and the open arms of the elevated plus maze, no significant difference was observed compared to standard-housed KO mice (Fig. 1d). For locomotor abnormalities, KO mice travelled shorter distances in the open field, with no significant difference between standard-housed and TEE-exposed KO mice (Fig. 1e). These results suggest that pre-weaning TEE can ameliorate core symptoms like social deficits and repetitive grooming but shows limited impact on anxiety and locomotor defects in Shank3 KO mice.

Effects of TEE during the juvenile stage on behavioral phenotypes in Shank3 KO mice

Given the observed improvements in social deficits and repetitive grooming behaviors following pre-weaning TEE in KO mice, we investigated whether similar rescue effects could be achieved when TEE exposure occurred at the post-weaning stage, specifically during the juvenile period (P21-P42). To test it, animals were housed in standard cages until weaning, then transferred to either TEE or standard environment cages. The TEE apparatus was adjusted (interval and height of bead curtains) to accommodate the size of juvenile mice and ensure sufficient tactile stimulation during cage exploration. TEE exposure ceased at P42, and animals returned to standard housing for three weeks, mirroring the interval used with the postnatal TEE group. Behavioral assays were conducted after P63 (Fig. 2a).

The three-chamber test demonstrated similar rescue effects in the juvenile TEE group as those observed in the pre-weaning TEE group. KO mice housed in TEE cages during the juvenile period exhibited a preference for the social object, in contrast to their standard-housed counterparts (Fig. 2b). However, while standard-housed KO mice showed increased grooming duration, juvenile TEE exposure, unlike pre-weaning exposure, failed to suppress this behavior (Fig. 2c). A significant increase in grooming duration was observed in juvenile TEE-exposed KO mice compared to WT mice. Furthermore, similar limited rescue effects were observed for anxiety levels in KO mice, with decreased time spent in the central area of the open field and the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Fig. 2d). Likewise, no significant improvement in locomotor defects was observed following juvenile TEE intervention (Fig. 2e). These results suggest that TEE exposure during the juvenile stage only improves social performance but not repetitive behaviors, anxiety, or locomotor defects in Shank3 KO mice.

Taken together, considering the behavioral findings from both pre-weaning and juvenile TEE interventions, early tactile enrichment, particularly during the pre-weaning developmental period, appears to provide the most robust beneficial effects in Shank3 KO mice.

Pre-weaning TEE exposure influences gene expression related to synaptic function

To understand the neural substrates underlying the beneficial effects of pre-weaning TEE on the Shank3 KO mice, we performed bulk RNA-seq on two brain regions, the ACC and DLS, previously linked by our studies to social deficits [15] and repetitive behaviors [17], respectively, to investigate the changes led by TEE on the transcriptomes.

In the ACC, RNA sequencing analysis revealed 234 DEGs. Of these, 109 exhibited up-regulation and 125 showed down-regulation (Fig. 3a). Notably, several of the most significantly altered DEGs encode proteins that are located at synaptic sites or play a role in regulating synaptic functions (Fig. 3b), including Rab37, involved in presynaptic vesicle trafficking, and Arc, which influences postsynaptic plasticity via modulation of glutamatergic receptors. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, using a combined score metric, revealed significant enrichment of several biological processes, most notably “Asymmetric, Glutamatergic, Excitatory Synapse” (Fig. 3c, Fig. S2a-d). This finding suggests that pre-weaning TEE may modulate the behavioral phenotype in P42 Shank3 KO mice by altering glutamatergic excitatory synapse-related gene expression in the ACC. In the DLS, we identified 59 DEGs, with 37 up-regulated and 22 down-regulated (Fig. 3d). Like the ACC, several synaptic genes were also differentially expressed in the DLS (Fig. 3e), including Sytl4, which inhibits synaptic vesicle exocytosis, and Cav2, which initiates vesicular neurotransmitter release at synapses. GO enrichment analysis highlighted “neurotransmitter receptor complex” as one of the prominently affected processes (Fig. 3f, Fig. S2e-h). Collectively, these findings implicate alterations in synaptic changes as a key mechanism by which pre-weaning TEE exerts its beneficial effects in Shank3 KO mice.

To better understand how pre-weaning TEE influences synaptic function at the mechanistic level, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on our RNA sequencing data. This analysis identified the Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK-1) pathway as being significantly upregulated following TEE intervention (Fig. 4a-b). While PLK-1 is well-established as a key regulator of cell mitosis [35], its potential role in synaptic development and function remained completely unexplored. To investigate whether this TEE-upregulated PLK-1 pathway plays a critical role in synaptic regulation, we performed a series of experiments. First, in Neuro-2a cell lines, we administered 100 nM Ro3280 (a highly selective PLK-1 inhibitor) for 24 h and then examined mRNA expression changes of key synaptic genes (Fig. 4c-d). Quantitative analysis revealed that PLK-1 inhibition significantly decreased mRNA levels of excitatory synaptic components including glutamate receptors (Gria1, Grin1), the scaffold protein Homer1, and the presynaptic marker Syp, while increasing expression of Gria2 and Shank3 (Fig. 4e-j). These findings suggested that PLK-1 activity may be essential for maintaining normal excitatory synaptic function.

To more accurately model neuronal conditions, we extended our investigation to primary cultured cortical neurons. At 16 days in vitro (DIV16), we treated cultures with Ro3280 and performed comprehensive analyses after 24 h. Using Dil labeling to visualize neuronal morphology, we observed that PLK-1 inhibition led to a significant reduction in spine density (Fig. 4k-m). Subsequent immunofluorescence staining of MAP2-labeled dendrites showed decreased density of Homer1-positive postsynaptic densities and reduced colocalization of Homer1 with the AMPAR subunit GluA1, indicating impaired excitatory synapse formation (Fig. 4n-q). Functional assessment through whole-cell patch clamp recordings provided further evidence, demonstrating that PLK-1 inhibition substantially decreased synaptic activity in treated neurons (Fig. 4r-u). Taken together, these findings strongly suggest that the beneficial effects of pre-weaning TEE could be achieved through modulation of excitatory synaptic maturation, mediated at least partially through activation of the PLK-1 signaling pathway.

Effects of pre-weaning TEE on spine morphology and synaptic transmission in the ACC in Shank3 KO mice

Given our RNA-seq and in vitro findings, along with the known role of Shank3 in regulating spine morphology and its functional implications [36], we investigated the in vivo impact of TEE during early development on dendritic spine density and morphology in the ACC.

The spines were visualized via retroorbital injection of AAV-hSyn-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf (farnesylated enhanced green fluorescent protein), a viral tool used for unbiased sparse neuronal labeling, enabling visualization of fine neuronal structure. Using sparsely labeled GFP-positive neurons, we acquired high-resolution Z-stack images of apical dendritic segments within ACC layer II/III pyramidal neurons, quantifying and classifying dendritic spines (Fig. 5a-c). As expected, Shank3 KO mice exhibited decreased total apical spine density and spine length. Pre-weaning TEE exposure restored total apical spine density in KO mice but not spine length (Fig. 5d-e). Then, we further classified apical spines into four categories (filopodia, stubby, mushroom, and long thin) based on established criteria [37,38]. We found that Shank3 KO mice had significantly decreased mushroom spine density and increased filopodial-like spine density in the ACC (Fig. 5f-i). TEE exposure restored mushroom spine density with limited impact on other spine types.

Because spine morphology correlates with the maturity of glutamatergic synaptic transmission, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings on layer II/III ACC pyramidal neurons to functionally assess TEE’s impact on synaptic transmission. Recording spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) as a measure of excitatory synaptic activity (Fig. 6a), we observed significantly decreased sEPSC frequency and amplitude in KO mice compared to WT. Interestingly, TEE exposure partially restored the sEPSC frequency and fully corrected the amplitude (Fig. 6b-c), suggesting functional rescue of glutamatergic synaptic transmission. These morphological and functional findings together indicate that TEE may ameliorate behavioral abnormalities by modulating synaptic maturation in the ACC during development.

Effects of pre-weaning TEE on spine morphology and synaptic transmission in the DLS in Shank3 KO mice

We also examined spine morphology in the DLS, reconstructing and classifying MSN spines into the same four categories used for ACC analysis (Fig. 7a-c). In MSNs, Shank3 KO mice displayed decreased total spine density and increased spine length compared to WT mice. TEE exposure reversed these trends in KO mice (Fig. 7d-e). While no statistically significant differences were observed in the density of individual spine categories, KO mice showed a trend towards decreased stubby spine density and increased filopodial-like spine density. TEE exposure significantly increased stubby spine density and showed a trend, though not statistically significant, towards decreased filopodial-like spine density (Fig. 7f-i).

Effects of TEE during the juvenile stage on spine morphology and synaptic transmission in Shank3 KO mice

Given the distinct behavioral effects of juvenile-stage TEE, we next examined whether spine morphology and synaptic function exhibited differential responses compared to pre-weaning TEE. Brain tissues were collected at P63, corresponding with behavioral testing timepoints. In ACC analysis, Shank3 KO mice again showed reduced total spine density, but the distribution of spine subtypes differed markedly from pre-weaning TEE mice, which were tested at P42, revealing highly dynamic spine regulation. Notably, juvenile-stage TEE demonstrated limited efficacy in modulating either spine density or morphology in the ACC (Fig. S3). Parallel observations in the DLS revealed that while Shank3 KO mice exhibited characteristic spine deficits, juvenile TEE failed to normalize these abnormalities (Fig. S3). At the functional level, juvenile TEE partially restored synaptic transmission, normalizing sEPSC frequency but not amplitude (Fig. S4). The above rescue suggests a presynaptic mechanism that may contrast fundamentally with the synaptic correction mechanisms engaged by pre-weaning TEE.

In the DLS, Shank3 KO mice exhibited significant synaptic alterations, including reduced total spine density, increased spine length, and abnormal spine morphology compared to WT controls. Juvenile-stage TEE failed to rescue these structural deficits in KO mice (Fig. S5). Electrophysiological analysis revealed that juvenile TEE produced a non-significant trend toward increased sEPSC frequency without affecting amplitude in DLS neurons (Fig. S6). These results demonstrate that juvenile TEE enhanced presynaptic release in the ACC but showed limited efficacy in the striatum. These differential effects on synapses in the ACC and DLS may underlie the behavioral improvements in social behaviors without broader phenotypic rescue when the animals receive TEE during the juvenile stage.

Discussion

While pharmacological treatments for inherited neurological diseases are emerging [39], their specificity and effectiveness remain limited. This underscores the importance of exploring non-pharmacological interventions, particularly during critical developmental periods [24]. To explore this issue, we investigated the therapeutic potential of TEE in a Shank3 mouse model of autism. We hypothesized that early intervention, targeting the dynamic period of synapse formation, could significantly ameliorate autistic-like behaviors. Our findings demonstrate that pre-weaning TEE exposure effectively improved core autism-related symptoms, including social deficits and repetitive grooming behaviors, while having a limited impact on anxiety and locomotor defects. In contrast, TEE administered during the juvenile period, a stage associated with synaptic pruning [40], only showed improvement in social performance, with no discernible effect on other behavioral domains. Notably, for the pre-weaning TEE exposure, the dams were present in the cages. This means that while TEE may directly affect the pre-weaning animals, it could also indirectly influence the pups through its effects on the dams. This introduces a potential methodological limitation of our TEE treatment. Future studies employing alternative approaches will be essential to further elucidate this issue. Nevertheless, regardless of the presence of indirect effects, the stage-dependent efficacy of TEE raises a crucial question of why earlier intervention yields more robust benefits. This question touches upon the fundamental principles underlying neurodevelopmental disorders.

To explore the underlying mechanisms, we conducted high-throughput RNA sequencing, which revealed that TEE primarily influences synaptic processes. This finding aligns with critical periods in synapse development, where early experiences strongly influence synaptogenesis, which peaks in early development and persists into late childhood [41], followed by adolescent synaptic pruning that refines neural connections [42,43]. With the screening of molecular pathways that TEE modulated, PLK-1 is one of the most robust upregulated pathways in our analyses. PLK-1 is an evolutionarily conserved Ser/Thr kinase best known for its pivotal role in cell cycle regulation, particularly during the G2/S and M phases, where it phosphorylates substrates critical for mitosis, centrosome maturation, spindle assembly, and cytokinesis [35]. However, its function in postmitotic neurons or synaptic development has remained unexplored. Our GSEA analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the PLK-1 signaling pathway following TEE, prompting further investigation into its neuronal role. Strikingly, inhibition of PLK-1 in developing neurons led to impaired synaptogenesis, accompanied by reduced expression of key presynaptic and postsynaptic components, including diminished AMPA receptor levels at the postsynaptic membrane. These findings suggest that PLK-1, beyond its canonical cell cycle functions, may serve as a novel regulator of synaptic maturation. Notably, these experiments were conducted in glia-free primary neuronal cultures, indicating that PLK-1′s synaptogenic effects are neuron-intrinsic and likely operate during the synapse formation phase rather than later pruning stages. Similar to the potential of early intensive training to restore synapse density in other neurodevelopmental disorders [42,44], our findings highlight the therapeutic promise of early non-pharmacological interventions like TEE in shaping neural synapses and mitigating the behavioral manifestations of autism.

It is worth noting that ASD-related spine abnormalities present in two primary forms [45]: insufficient spine genesis and impaired spine pruning. Several autism models, including Mecp2 KO, 15q11–13 paternal duplication, and Shank3 KO model, exhibit reduced spine density, potentially leading to underdeveloped neural circuitry. Conversely, models like MeCP2 duplication and Fmr1 KO demonstrate reduced pruning and over-connectivity, resulting in less efficient adult neural circuitry. While the genetic diversity of neurodevelopmental disorders complicates the development of targeted therapies, early interventions focused on optimizing synaptic development in specific critical periods may offer a valuable and broadly applicable therapeutic approach.

Gentle tactile stimuli, conveyed by C-tactile (CT) afferents, promote rewarding behaviors [46,47]. Modifying access to gentle touch, through either deprivation or targeted stimulation, influences social behaviors by modulating specific neural circuits [48,49]. Individuals with ASD often exhibit atypical sensory processing, ranging from aversion to hyposensitivity to gentle tactile stimulation [50]. Our previous work demonstrated tactile hypersensitivity in adult Shank3 KO mice [16]. Moreover, studies targeting peripheral somatosensory neurons to correct tactile abnormalities in various ASD mouse models have shown improvements in other ASD-related phenotypes, including social deficits [51]. This suggests a crucial intersection between sensory processing and other behavioral symptoms in ASD, highlighting modulation of the sensory system as a target for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

To provide sustained gentle tactile stimulation, we drew inspiration from environmental enrichment paradigms, known for their long-term impact. We employed hanging bead curtains, composed of rows of bead strings. This approach allows mice to actively engage with tactile stimuli, mimicking naturalistic exploration and minimizing potential stress associated with passive, experimenter-delivered stimulation. While designed for tactile enrichment, this TEE may also provide incidental visual and other sensory stimulation, which cannot be ruled out. Therefore, caution is needed when interpreting these results. Early-life exposure to TEE rescued two core ASD symptoms but had a limited impact on anxiety-related behaviors. A previous study using similar paradigms reported improvements in affective states in adult mice [32]. These differing outcomes depending on the developmental stage at the time of intervention underscore the importance of the intervention window in determining TEE efficacy. This difference also suggests that TEE may influence specific behaviors in neurotypical animals; its effects likely differ from those observed in ASD mouse models. Further systematic comparisons across different ages, including a more fine-grained analysis of pre-weaning TEE exposure within the first three weeks of life, are needed to fully characterize TEE effects and fuel the potential clinical translation. In addition, it should also be noted that age-related differences in learning and sensory processing capabilities between pre-weaning and juvenile animals may influence the behavioral outcomes of TEE treatment. While our study controlled for environmental factors, developmental changes could partially confound direct comparisons across age groups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified that pre-weaning TEE ameliorates core behavioral deficits in Shank3 KO mice. These behavioral changes correlate with restoration of synaptic function and spine morphology in key brain regions. These findings underscore the importance of early sensory-driven interventions, such as tactile enrichment, during a critical developmental window, and their potential to ameliorate core symptoms of ASD.
