Comparative Outcomes of Direct Anastomosis Versus Patch Repair in Neonatal Aortic Arch Reconstruction: Freedom From Restenosis and Reintervention
Authors: Aybala Tongut, Zeynep Bengi Eren, Mahmut Ozturk, Alyssia Venna, Mitchell Haverty, Arif Selcuk, Manan Desai, Andrew Waberski, Can Yerebakan, Yves d’Udekem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study compares outcomes of patch repair vs direct anastomosis in neonatal aortic arch repair with the goal of identifying any long-term differences in restenosis, reoperation, and reintervention rates.MethodsNeonates who underwent aortic arch repair via median sternotomy between 2004 and 2021, using either patch repair or direct anastomosis techniques, were included in this study. Patch materials were glutaraldehyde-treated pericardium and femoral vein homograft. Statistical comparisons included descriptive and bivariable analyses to evaluate patient characteristics, reintervention, reoperation, and restenosis at a 20 mm Hg threshold.ResultsSeventy-five neonates, who underwent neonatal arch repair using patch technique (n = 53) or direct anastomosis (n = 22), were included in the study. No significant differences in reintervention, reoperation, or restenosis were found between surgical approaches at 20 mm Hg threshold (P > .13). Patient characteristics such as younger age (P = .06), shorter height (P = .1), and lower weight (P = .2), at the time of surgery were not significant predictors of restenosis. Cardiopulmonary bypass and circulatory arrest times were shorter with direct anastomosis. Ten-year mortality was 11% (n = 8).ConclusionsThere is no clear advantage of either patch or direct anastomosis for neonatal aortic arch repair regarding early- to mid-term outcomes. Patient-specific factors did not appear to be stronger predictors of restenosis risk than the surgical technique employed.Keywords: coarctation of the aorta, neonatal aortic arch repair, direct anastomosis, end-to-end repair, restenosis, reintervention

INTRODUCTION

Neonates with coarctation of the aorta (CoA), particularly those with distal arch hypoplasia, face high mortality risks if left untreated.1 Early surgical intervention is essential to improve survival, yet postoperative challenges remain, including early mortality, restenosis, and systemic hypertension.2 The choice of optimal repair strategy is still debated, with 2 primary techniques in focus: direct anastomosis and patch augmentation of the aortic arch. Direct anastomosis, involving the removal of the narrowed segment and a tension-free reconnection, has been suggested to reduce restenosis rates.3 However, it has also been proposed that patch repair offers greater adaptability for addressing extensive stenosis, even in the face of worse restenosis outcomes, and some centres continue to prefer it due to acceptably low mortality and re-coarctation rates.4,5

In this paper, we investigated a single-centre’s long-term outcomes after either direct anastomosis repair or patch repair of CoA. We aimed to determine which technique yields a lower prevalence of restenosis post-operatively and a lesser need for reintervention for recurrent coarctation in the subsequent years post-repair. We hypothesize that direct anastomosis offers more favourable outcomes in respect to the chosen parameters of evaluation in comparison to patch repair of aortic arch coarctation.

METHODS

The design of the study was approved, and a waiver of consent was obtained by the Children’s National Hospital Institutional Review Board (Pro00015566). The study was designed as a comparative investigation of patients who underwent aortic arch repairs by either direct anastomosis or patch repair technique.

The study included neonates who underwent aortic arch repair via sternotomy at the Children’s National Hospital in Washington, DC, United States, between January 2004 and February 2021 (Figure 1). Patients who had repairs via left thoracotomy were excluded from the study. Two types of patches were used in the repairs: a glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patch and a homograft femoral vein patch. The choice between a pericardial patch and a femoral vein homograft was made based on specific surgical considerations for each patient.

Ethics statement

No biological materials or data were collected for storage in a biobank. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the principles outlined in the WMA Declaration of Taipei.

Statistical analysis

To assess structural restenosis of the aorta, the echocardiographically calculated maximum pressure gradient across the aortic arch at the last follow-up was used. Restenosis was defined as peak Doppler gradient ≥20 mm Hg across the aortic arch at the last follow-up echocardiography.

Reintervention was defined as any catheter procedure performed after the patient’s discharge that was related to aortic arch coarctation. Reoperation was defined as any surgical intervention for aortic arch coarctation after discharge. Any reintervention performed during the initial hospitalization with the intention of managing postoperative complications, rather than addressing failure of the initial repair, was excluded from the analysis.

The 2 surgical repair techniques were compared with the primary outcome of interest defined as the occurrence of reoperation, catheter-based reintervention, or echocardiographic evidence of restenosis at the last follow-up.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the survival and descriptive analysis included in Table 1. A 2-sided significance level of α = 0.05 was used throughout. Due to minimal missing data, a complete-case analysis was conducted. P-values are reported to 3 decimal places, and 95% CIs are provided when appropriate. A separate bivariable analysis was conducted for 20 mm Hg threshold, and the incidence of restenosis was evaluated for both surgical techniques: direct anastomosis and patch repair. Cox regression was conducted with time to restenosis as the outcome. Multivariable regression and propensity score analyses were not performed owing to limitations in sample size and event counts.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 75 patients were included in the study: 36 (48%) males and 39 (52%) females. Patient demographic and operative characteristics are summarized in Table 1. At the time of operation, the median age was 8 days (95% CI, 6-17) and the median weight was 3.3 kg (95% CI, 2.80-3.60). Sixty percent (n = 45) of the patients were prenatally diagnosed with aortic arch coarctation. Fifteen patients (20%) had a known diagnosis of a genetic syndrome, and 23 patients (31%) had an accompanying non-cardiac anomaly at the time of repair. Among the cohort, 23 patients had arch hypoplasia, 44 had ventricular septal defects, 5 had atrial septal defects, and 1 had partial anomalous pulmonary venous return as accompanying cardiac conditions. Only 2 patients had isolated aortic arch coarctation.

A total of 22 patients underwent repair using direct anastomosis techniques, while the remaining 53 received the patch repair. Of the 53 patients who underwent patch repair, 21 received glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patches and 32 received homograft femoral vein patches. Among the 22 patients who underwent direct anastomosis, 16 were repaired using extended end-to-end anastomosis, and 6 with end-to-side anastomosis. The median cross-clamp time was 63 min (IQR, 50-79), the median cardiopulmonary bypass time was 126 min (IQR, 108-150), and the deep hypothermic circulatory arrest time was 34 min (IQR, 24-44). Patients who underwent direct anastomosis repair had shorter cardiopulmonary bypass times compared to patch repair patients [median: 110 min (IQR, 100-119) vs 137 (IQR, 113-153); P < .001] and shorter deep hypothermic circulatory arrest times [median: 22 (IQR, 19-26) vs 39 (IQR, 28-48); P < .001]. The median length of stay at the hospital for the surgical repair was 21 days (IQR, 14-66). Sixty-four percent of the patients (n = 48) had a postoperative complication, and the overall mortality was 11% (n = 8). Other than cardiopulmonary bypass times and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest times, there were no statistically significant differences in the clinical or demographic characteristics between those who received direct anastomosis and those who received a patch repair.

Bivariable analysis assessed the likelihood of reintervention, reoperation, or restenosis at the most recent follow-up. A separate analysis was conducted for restenosis based on the maximum pressure gradients of the descending aorta at 20 mm Hg. The risk of reintervention, reoperation, or restenosis as a time-dependent variable did not significantly differ based on the surgical approach used for coarctation repair (20 mm Hg: 0.0886). This is further illustrated by the Kaplan-Meier analysis showing no significant difference in freedom from reintervention (Figure 2). Similarly, freedom from reoperation was comparable between groups (Figure 3), although reoperation events were infrequent.

DISCUSSION

The central finding of this study is that neither direct anastomosis nor patch for aortic arch repair presents a significantly greater risk of reintervention, reoperation, or restenosis. Our results align with multiple studies suggesting no significant long-term advantage of one technique over the other regarding freedom from recoarctation or reoperation.6,7 The choice of technique may, therefore, depend on the surgeon’s expertise and comfort level. While some studies suggest that end-to-end anastomosis may be associated with a slightly lower risk of long-term complications, such as aneurysm, other evidence indicates that technique choice alone is not a major determinant of mortality in coarctation repair and that instead patient factors such as age at time of surgery are more important.4,6,8

The Kaplan-Meier analysis in our study indicates that direct anastomosis and patch repair yield comparable outcomes (P-value = 0.53) during the early years of follow-up. However, as follow-up duration increases, subtle differences may emerge (Figure 3). While direct anastomosis appears to demonstrate marginally higher freedom from reintervention, reoperation, and restenosis with extended follow-up, the limited follow-up duration restricts statistical significance, preventing definitive conclusions on this observed trend. Patients who underwent direct anastomosis exhibited a stable clinical course beyond the first postoperative year, suggesting that the early postoperative period is the highest-risk window for reintervention or reoperation (Figure 4). In contrast, patients in the patch group demonstrated a more prolonged vulnerability, with events occurring later during follow-up. Additionally, a maximum descending aortic gradient ≥20 mm Hg was a significant predictor of restenosis (OR, 7.19; P < .01), emphasizing its utility in postoperative surveillance and decision-making regarding reintervention.

In addition to the surgical approach, the choice of patch material may also influence long-term outcomes. In neonatal aortic arch repair, particularly in the paediatric population, patch material selection plays a critical role in long-term surgical outcomes. Emerging literature supports the superiority of biologic materials over synthetic alternatives in minimizing postoperative complications and enhancing tissue remodelling capacity.9 Notably, a recent study reported significantly lower rates of restenosis in neonates repaired with homograft tissue compared to CardioCel bovine pericardium, highlighting the advantages of biologic tissue integration.10

In our study, 2 patch materials were utilized: glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patches and homograft femoral vein. Pericardial patch, widely adopted globally, is favoured for its availability, ease of handling, and established medium-term durability, with several studies supporting its safety and efficacy in aortic arch reconstruction.11 Although no direct comparative studies exist between pericardial patches and femoral vein homografts, mid-term outcomes for the latter are also encouraging. Homograft femoral vein is particularly suitable in settings requiring conduit-like reconstruction, rather than simple patch augmentation.12

In our cohort, reintervention rates were comparable between the femoral vein homograft (n = 6) and pericardial patch (n = 5) groups, with no clear advantage of one material over the other. It is important to recognize that both the choice of patch material and the surgical technique likely influence long-term outcomes.6 Therefore, patch selection should be guided by surgeon’s experience and intraoperative handling characteristics. At present, no strong evidence favours one biologic material over the other in terms of restenosis risk.

Additionally, neonatal aortic tissue is inherently more elastic and compliant, allowing for tension-free end-to-end anastomosis, which supports segmental growth and reduces the risk of restenosis.13,14 Achieving durable outcomes with this technique requires thorough mobilization of the descending aorta to ensure sufficient length and minimize anastomotic tension. As the child grows, the aorta stiffens, and mobilization becomes more challenging, increasing the risk of restenosis or aneurysm formation.15,16 In such cases, patch augmentation with autologous or homograft tissue provides improved luminal geometry and reduces wall stress, supporting long-term patency.17,18 Sliding arch aortoplasty may be a valuable alternative in selected anatomies, preserving native tissue continuity, promoting growth potential, and maintaining low restenosis rates.19,20 Ultimately, the surgical approach should be tailored based on patient age, arch anatomy, and tissue compliance to optimize repair durability.

Limitations

This study is limited by a relatively small sample size (N = 75) and a low number of reintervention events (n = 17), which precluded the use of multivariable or propensity score analyses. Due to the limited event rate, 95% CIs around Kaplan-Meier estimates were not reported, as they would have been excessively wide and potentially misleading. These constraints reduce the precision of survival estimates and should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Consequently, analyses were restricted to unadjusted (bivariable) comparisons, and results are presented descriptively rather than with inferential intent. Confounding by indication—arising from surgeon discretion in repair technique—remains a recognized limitation. Moreover, the long study period (2004-2021) likely reflects evolving surgical techniques, patch materials, and perioperative management, which may have influenced outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Statistical analysis suggests that neither end-to-end anastomosis nor patch repair technique has demonstrated superiority over the other in terms of freedom from reintervention, reoperation and restenosis, even in the strictest definition of restenosis. Hence, the surgeons’ expertise, preferences, and the centre’s discretion should be the determining factors in the choice of surgical technique of repair. This study underscores the importance of extended follow-up to capture potential late differences in outcomes between the 2 techniques.
