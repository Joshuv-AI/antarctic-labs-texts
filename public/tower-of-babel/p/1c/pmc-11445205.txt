Advancements in wound management: integrating nanotechnology and smart materials for enhanced therapeutic interventions
Authors: Simran Nasra, Sanjali Pramanik, Vidhi Oza, Krupa Kansara, Ashutosh Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Wound management spans various techniques and materials tailored to address acute and chronic non-healing wounds, with the primary objective of achieving successful wound closure. Chronic wounds pose additional challenges, often necessitating dressings to prepare the wound bed for subsequent surgical procedures like skin grafting. Ideal dressing materials should not only expedite wound healing but also mitigate protein, electrolyte, and fluid loss while minimizing pain and infection risk. Nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative tool in wound care, revolutionizing the landscape of biomedical dressings. Its application offers remarkable efficacy in accelerating wound healing and combating bacterial infections, representing a significant advancement in wound care practices. Integration of nanotechnology into dressings has resulted in enhanced properties, including improved mechanical strength and controlled drug release, facilitating tailored therapeutic interventions. This review article comprehensively explores recent breakthroughs in wound healing therapies, with a focus on innovative medical dressings such as nano-enzymes. Additionally, the utilization of smart materials, like hydrogels and electroactive polymers, in wound dressings offers dynamic functionalities to promote tissue regeneration. Emerging concepts such as bio-fabrication, microfluidic systems, bio-responsive scaffolds, and personalized therapeutics show promise in expediting wound healing and minimizing scarring. Through an in-depth exploration of these advancements, this review aims to catalyze a paradigm shift in wound care strategies, promoting a patient-centric approach to therapeutic interventions.

Introduction

Laid around a surface area of about 2 m2 and occupying about 15% of the total body mass, skin acts as the body’s primary and largest single organ [1], playing an impeccable role in crucial functions like regulation of temperature, protection from external agents and moderating functions of the sensory and immunologic systems, etc. [3]. The integrity of the skin remains continually at risk of compromise by numerous external factors such as the occurrence of injuries, burns, contusions, cuts, traumas, diseases, and surgical incisions. When the skin sustains acute damage, it can be life-threatening. Consequently, prompt regeneration and healing are essential to re-establish its functions and restore the body’s homeostasis. The self-healing trait of the body promotes complete wound healing within three months, depending on the injury’s severity. However, if an infection occurs and persists without proper management, a chronic condition can develop that endures for months or even years. Diabetic individuals face a 15–25% risk of developing diabetic chronic abscesses [2]. The hypodermic tissue of chronic and non-healing wounds is often exposed to the external environment for prolonged periods, making patients susceptible to bleeding and osteomyelitis, posing an extreme risk of fatality [4]. These wounds are vulnerable to complications such as cellulitis and abscess formation and if not effectively managed, can escalate to Bloodstream infections such as Bacteraemia and Sepsis [5]. Chronic wounds can also lead to haemorrhage, tissue decay, and amputations, presenting socioeconomic burdens and gravely impacting quality of life.

Wound dressings are crucial for creating an optimal environment for promoting wound healing and protection from further harm and infections. Traditional dressings such as gauzes, bandages and Plasters are often used widely as primary and secondary dressings for prevention of microbial infections [6]. However, these traditional forms of dressings have some major drawbacks given their high moisture absorption properties, as they can cause the wound to dry out and adhere to the dressing, leading to scab formations, ultimately reversing, and impeding the natural healing process [7]. Moreover, they tend to lack the presence of bioactive components such as antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, causing increased susception to bacterial invasion. Studies indicate that both traditional dressings as well as modern day dressings (Hydrogels, hydrocolloids, foams, and polymeric films) can effectively promote wound closure and healing, however bioactive dressings with incorporation of antimicrobial agents are much more effective in combating infections and accelerating repair[8–10]. The ideal dressing for wound healing should know the extracellular matrix (ECM), maintain a moist environment, possess antimicrobial properties, and promote cell proliferation and angiogenesis [11].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become a significant global public health threat responsible for at least 1.27 million deaths annually [13]. The demand and need for "smart dressings" that combine physical functions with additional properties such as anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial etc. has been on the rise and to address this problem, nanotechnology and nanoparticles have been developed to combat bacterial resistance and bacterial biofilms, alongside their effectiveness in wound care owing to their better adsorption capacity, tunable shape, and drug loading properties. Nanoparticles are engineered structures with sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm [12]. Nanotechnology has fuelled tremendous progress in the field of wound management, providing creative answers to persistent problems in wound care. Because of its capacity to work with materials at the nanoscale, nanotechnology has made it possible to create sophisticated wound dressings with improved features. These dressings address specific issues like infection management, moisture regulation, and scar reduction in addition to hastening the healing of wounds. The purpose of this review is to examine the latest developments in wound healing therapies, with an emphasis on the application of cutting-edge and emerging nanotechnology concepts to medical dressings. Through a comprehensive analysis of these advancements, this review endeavours to advocate for a paradigm shift towards patient-centric wound care strategies.

Physiology of wound healing

Chronic wounds including pressure sores, surgical wounds that don’t heal, and arterial ulcers can result from disturbances in the regular healing process [26]. Cell senescence/apoptosis, extended inflammation, matrix degradation, infection, and systemic circumstances such as chronic diseases and inadequate nutrition are among the factors that lead to aberrant wound healing [27, 28]. The imbalance of cellular and molecular processes in chronic wounds causes poor healing, the creation of biofilms, and tissue damage that extends beyond the original wound site [29–31]. As shown in Fig. 1, a few of the variables that affect wound healing are inadequate blood flow, foreign particle presence, microbial infection, poor venous drainage, and changed matrix metalloproteinase levels. Numerous cell types, chemokines, and growth factors coordinate a complex set of physiological systems that facilitate the healing of wounds [15, 16]. It includes primary and secondary hemostasis as well as vasoconstriction. In this stage, biomolecules are essential because they promote the deposition of collagen by fibroblasts and epithelial cells [17, 18].ii.Inflammatory phase After an accident, inflammation usually starts right away and lasts for three days. Vasodilation, heightened vascular permeability, and the release of inflammatory mediators are all brought on by platelet degranulation. After that, neutrophils are eliminated by macrophages, which reduces inflammation and stops tissue deterioration [19].iii.Proliferation phase Neovascularization and re-epithelialization take place over a few weeks during the wound healing period. Angiogenesis, the process by which new blood vessels grow from pre-existing arteries, and vasculogenesis, the process by which endothelial progenitor cells develop new arteries to guarantee tissue nutrition uptake, are both components of neovascularization. By creating a thin layer of epithelial cells that progressively thickens, epithelialization shrinks the size of wounds. M2 phenotype macrophages release anti-inflammatory mediators that encourage the deposition of collagen, the proliferation of fibroblasts, and the promotion of angiogenesis. The wound enlarges and fibroplasia progresses alongside revascularization as collagen fibres take the place of the fibrin mixture. Capillary buds proliferate and migrate into the wound, which is triggered by cytokines from platelets, macrophages, and lymphocytes as well as low oxygen tension, lactic acid, and biogenic amines. The milieu (low pH, low oxygen, high lactate) and growth factors (FGF, TGF-β, VEGF) promote endothelial cell adhesion to the extracellular matrix and promote healing, which in turn stimulates the development of new blood vessels [20–23].iv.Remodelling phase Remodelling is the last stage of wound healing, during which the original collagen structure of healthy tissue is restored. Fibroblasts are responsible for the synthesis of extracellular matrix and matrix metalloproteinases, which facilitate wound contraction, decrease epithelialization, and inhibit the formation of scars. An ordered extracellular matrix replaces the granulation tissue to generate scar tissue that has mechanical characteristics akin to that of the tissue before the damage. Granulation tissue’s blood vessel density returns to normal. Collagen is broken down by fibroblasts using matrix metalloproteinases to create a structure that is more robust. The ratio of type I to type III collagen gradually reaches the level of normal skin, indicating that the arrangement of collagen affects healing. Collagen fibrils get bigger with time and start to resemble a healthy dermis. Applications of different nanomaterials in wound healing

Nanoparticles exert their therapeutic effects in wound healing by combining antimicrobial activity, drug delivery capabilities, modulation of cellular responses, and promotion of tissue regeneration. When it comes to treatments for wounds, nanoparticles are quite advantageous. By increasing keratinocyte proliferation, silver nanoparticles facilitate healing and lessen scarring [53, 54]. At low concentrations, gold nanoparticles promote keratinocyte growth and inhibit microbial colonization [55]. Hydrogel-based zinc oxide nanoparticles promote re-epithelialization and behave as potent antibacterial [56]. Because of their high-water absorption, biopolymeric nanoparticles promote healing by creating a humid atmosphere [57, 58]. Cell adhesion, proliferation, and angiogenesis are all improved by hyaluronic acid. Multidrug-resistant bacteria can be effectively combated by nanoparticles as well.

Nanoparticles against anti-bacterial resistance in wound healing

Antibacterial resistance is a major obstacle in the field of wound healing because it makes some bacterial strains resistant to the effects of traditional antimicrobial therapies. This resistance can worsen infections, raise the chance of complications, and delay the healing of wounds. Bacterial resistance can result in chronic or non-healing wounds in the setting of infectious wound healing, necessitating more aggressive treatment methods. In the field of wound care, dressings based on nanoparticles present a promising way to overcome antibiotic resistance. These dressings are able to target and eradicate resistant bacteria because they take advantage of the special qualities of nanoparticles, which include their capacity to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), break down cell walls and membranes, and enter biofilms. Furthermore, antimicrobial medicines can be more effectively delivered to wound areas with the help of nanoparticles, all while reducing systemic side effects. Nanoparticles used in wound healing for their antimicrobial properties

Silver nanoparticles

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) play an antimicrobial role in the wound-healing process. They possess multiple mechanisms that disrupt bacterial cells and inhibit biofilm formation [226]. Due to their nanometric size and increased surface area, AgNPs can destroy bacterial membranes, penetrate cells, and induce intracellular damage. They also induce oxidative stress, release metal ions, and interact with biological molecules such as DNA and enzymes (as shown in Figure 3). AgNPs tend to aggregate on bacterial membranes, interfering with key biological processes and disrupting enzyme activity involved in energy metabolism and oxidative defense. Additionally, AgNPs inhibit the synthesis of exopolysaccharides [227], essential for biofilm formation, and interfere with quorum sensing, a bacterial communication system regulating biofilm development and virulence factor expression [228]. Flexible cloths with silver nanoparticle coating are used for dressing of the wounds [59]. Studies also suggest that the use of AgNPs may have a role in reducing inflammation in wound healing. Silver-induced neutrophil apoptosis and decreased MMP (Matrix Metalloproteinase) activity are mentioned as potential mechanisms contributing to a decrease in the inflammatory response. Neutrophil apoptosis refers to the programmed cell death of neutrophils, which are a type of white blood cell involved in the inflammatory response. By inducing apoptosis in neutrophils, silver may help regulate and reduce the inflammatory response. Additionally, decreased MMP activity could lead to reduced degradation of extracellular matrix components, which are often associated with inflammation. Therefore, by decreasing MMP activity, silver may help mitigate inflammation in wounds, potentially contributing to an increased rate of wound healing [60]. Clinical trials have shown that silver nanoparticle (AgNP) wound dressings, such as Acticoat and PolyMem Silver, are effective in treating burn wounds and diabetic ulcers by reducing bacterial colonization, preventing sepsis, and promoting faster healing. Comparative studies indicate that AgNP dressings are generally superior or comparable to other treatments like silver nitrate solutions and plain gauze in terms of infection control, pain reduction, and healing time. However, concerns about potential toxicity, environmental impact, and bacterial resistance persist, necessitating further extensive clinical trials to assess the long-term safety and potential adverse effects of AgNPs in wound care. Gold nanoparticles

AuNPs play anti-inflammatory and antioxidative [61], roles in wound healing process. In addition to having promising antibacterial activity against gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria. AuNPs are also found to possess antifungal properties against unicellular and multicellular fungi both independently [229], and when integrated with biopolymers/materials like chitosan. Gold nanocomposites [185] like Chi/AuNPs have been demonstrated to be safe to use due to them not affecting the normal human skin cell line. AuNPs have also been used for improvement of antimicrobial resistance by their addition into chitosan hydrogels, which lowers bacterial infection and improves wound healing [62]. Nonetheless, Gold nanoparticles are less commonly used in wound healing compared to silver nanoparticles due to factors such as cost, lower antibacterial efficacy, concerns about biocompatibility and regulatory approval, and differences in their mechanisms of action. Silver nanoparticles have a longer history of use, stronger antimicrobial activity, and established regulatory approval, making them a preferred choice for wound healing applications. However, research into gold nanoparticles continues for their potential in wound healing and other biomedical applications.

ZnO-nanoparticles

Zinc oxide nanoparticles [202] have shown potential in wound healing due to the creation of electron-hole pairs, which generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and exhibits antimicrobial activity. It has been shown to help in re-epithelialization process of wound healing [63].

PLGA nanoparticles

Poly-(lactic-co-glycolic acid)(PLGA) nanoparticles, in conjugation with various biological materials such as antimicrobial peptides, alkaloids(analgesic, anti-microbial, anti-inflammatory properties), synthetic forms of vitamins, etc have been studied for their wound healing properties, granulation tissue formation and immunomodulation [64]. PLGA supplies lactate that accelerates neovascularization and promotes wound healing [64]. Silver has been historically used in wound healing for centuries, with AgNPs having a well-established safety profile that contributed to their clinical approval. In contrast, treatments based on gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), or poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles (PLGA NPs) have not been clinically approved due to concerns regarding biocompatibility and toxicity.

Nanofibrous scaffolds

Nanofibers in wound healing are ultrafine fibres with diameters typically in the nanometre range. These nanofibrous scaffolds are produced from natural or synthetic based polymers and are carefully engineered to mimic the extracellular matrix of tissues, thus acting as a scaffold for adherence, proliferation, and differentiation of cells [32]. They possess high water absorption quality, moisture permeability, high interconnected porosity, and breathability. These factors promote hemostasis, prevent exogenous infections, and create an optimal environment for facilitation of cellular respiration [32]. They exhibit more advantages over block materials like hydrogels given their high porosity and surface area which has beneficial effects on cell activity [33, 36]. The physicochemical characteristics of engineered nanofibers renders them excellent vehicles for drug delivery [34]. Loading nanofibrous scaffolds with biologically active agents such as growth factors, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial drugs, cytokines etc. suppress scar formation [38], combat bacterial infections, and foster healthy wound healing [39, 40].

Polymers used to make nanofibrous scaffolds

Nanofibers utilized in wound dressings or implantable scaffolds must exhibit essential characteristics such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low cytotoxicity for both the fibres and their degradation products. Natural polymers

Natural polymers, include various proteins and polysaccharides which resemble the components of the natural extracellular matrix (ECM), while possessing inherent structural and biological properties [37, 41]. These natural components provide excellent biocompatibility, promote cell adhesion and proliferation, enhance epithelialization, vascularization and are low in cytotoxicity. They are fully biodegradable as well as provide haemostatic and antibacterial properties for enhancing wound healing.

Synthetic polymers

Synthetic polymers, such as polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), polyethylene glycol (PEG), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and polycaprolactone (PCL), provide several advantages such as engineered scaffold fibres with finer diameters, higher porosity, tuneable mechanical strength etc. [33]. However, synthetic polymers often lack certain biological properties. Polymers used to make these scaffolds degrade over time into smaller molecules. Synthetic Polymers unlike Natural Biocompatible material, can sometimes produce components which are toxic to cells and tissues after degradation. If not appropriately handled, these acidic breakdown products have the potential to cause inflammation, irritation, or cytotoxicity by lowering the pH in the surrounding tissue environment. The breakdown products must be either non-toxic or effectively removed from the wound site in order to lessen these problems. If not, the presence of harmful by-products may impede the healing process and can result in issues including delayed tissue regeneration or persistent inflammation.

The rate at which a nanofiber scaffold degrades is critical for wound healing. Ideally, the scaffold should degrade at a rate that matches the natural healing process of the wound [42]. If the degradation rate is mismatched, such as scaffold degradation occurs faster than the formation of new tissue, it may not provide sufficient structural support for the newly formed tissue. This can result in the wound collapsing or not healing properly. Conversely, if the scaffold degrades too slowly, it can obstruct tissue regeneration. In order to minimise toxicity and obtain the appropriate degradation profile, researchers often try to build hybrid polymers by blending natural and synthetic polymers. Scaffolds are tailored to break down in a way that promotes and enhances tissue regeneration and growth while avoiding accumulation of toxic degradation products.

Hybrid polymers

By blending natural and synthetic polymers, it is possible to create composites that harness the best attributes of both types of materials. Natural polymers contribute biocompatibility, biodegradability, and bioactivity, while synthetic polymers provide enhanced mechanical strength and consistent quality [43]. This synergistic approach aims to produce more effective and reliable materials for tissue engineering applications. Electrospinning is a common technique to create nanofibrous scaffolds, but some natural polymers like chitosan, cannot be spun into standalone electrospun films [45]. To address this, natural polymers are blended in combination with synthetic fibres or other natural fibres to get maximum advantages. [44, 46]

Methods for drug-loading on nanofibrous scaffolds

Physical adsorption method

As shown in Figure 5, the physical adsorption method involves loading drugs onto the scaffold surface by non-chemical interactions such as electrostatic interactions and van der Waals interactions [47]. In this method, the drug is dissolved in a solvent and the nanofibrous scaffolds are immersed in the solution, allowing the drug to attach to the surface of the scaffold. The main advantage of the physical adsorption method is its simplicity, as it does not alter the drug’s chemical structure or activity. As there is no chemical bonding between the drug and the scaffold, the drug’s chemical structure and activity remain unaffected [48]. This method maintains the drug’s physical activity but results in fast drug release rates due to limited loading capacity and weak interactions [47].Fig. 5(1) Physical adsorption method: a The physical adsorption method involves loading drugs onto the scaffold surface through nonchemical interactions like van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions. Chemical conjugation method

The Chemical Conjugation method involves drug loading onto the scaffolds via stable chemical bonds such as covalent bonds, ester bonds, amide bonds etc. This method requires reactive functional groups on either the drug or scaffold, or their modification to introduce such groups [49, 50]. This method ensures stability of the drug-scaffold binding, permitting slow and prolonged release rate, along with the ability to load multiple drugs, However, it may alter the drug’s innate chemical structure and requires stringent reaction conditions as well as complex preparation processes [51, 52].

Coating method

The coating method involves directly covering the surface of the scaffold with the drug or the drug-carrier composite. Similar to the physical adsorption method the drug is dissolved into a solvent [73] and then the scaffold is either immersed in or coated with this solution using spraying, roll-coating, or brush-coating methods [74]. This method also results in weak interactions between the scaffold and the drug and thus fast release rate, even though it provides the benefit of simplicity and loading of multiple drugs. This method is suitable for small molecules (physisorption) or large molecules (encapsulation) [75, 76].

Co-blending electrospinning method

In this method, the drug and scaffold materials are electrospun together to create Drug-Loaded nanofiber scaffolds [35, 77]. The process includes dissolving the drug and scaffold materials in appropriate solvents, mixing these solutions, and electrospinning the blend to form nanofibers with uniform drug distribution [78]. Slow and sustained release of the drug can be achieved via this method, and mechanical properties of the scaffold are also improved. However, controlling the release rates and achieving drug stability might be hampered during electrospinning due to temperature change or light exposure. Scaling up production is also affected due to the time-consuming procedure [47].

Application of nanofibrous scaffolds in wound healing

Promotion of hemostasis

Bioactive substances like thrombin, clotting factors, and hemostatic peptides are added to nanofiber scaffolds to enable hemostatic actions [79]. Additionally, several natural polymers, such as chitosan and gelatin, have innate hemostatic qualities [80, 81]. The enzyme thronging acts directly on fibrinogen, converting it into fibrin and accelerating the blood clotting process. Pandey et al. created a polyvinyl alcohol/gelatin/poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanofiber scaffold rich in thrombin (TMB) and vancomycin (VCM), to prevent excessive bleeding, stop the growth of bacteria, and aid in the treatment of wounds. Rat models showed that the thrombin-loaded Nanofiber scaffolds demonstrated superior wound closure, quick clotting, and reduced bleeding time [82]. Some ions, such as calcium and aluminium ions, are crucial to the blood clotting process because they can activate several of the enzymes needed for blood coagulation. A gelatin–calcium chloride electrospun nanofiber was created by Xinrong Yu et al. for quick hemostasis [83]. In comparison to the gauze-bandage group, Sara Nasser et al.’s PLLA nanofiber mat loaded with aluminium chloride demonstrated optimal hemostatic efficacy at 30% w/w, resulting in an 80% reduction in blood coagulation time and a 178% increase in blood absorption capacity [78].

Reduction in chronic inflammation

Reactive oxygen species, bacterial infections, and proinflammatory chemokines are some of the causes that cause chronic inflammation, which impedes wound healing. Herbal extracts with anti-inflammatory qualities, such as propolis, aloe vera, and cinnamon, can speed up the healing of wounds [84–86]. Novel nanofiber technologies have been developed by researchers to further improve healing. Wang et al. developed DQHP-n nanofibers containing dihydromyricetin to encourage cell migration and reduce inflammation [87]. Furthermore, Sun et al. demonstrated that by promoting cell migration and angiogenesis, crossed nanofibrous scaffolds promote optimum repair in diabetic rats [88].

Enhancement of cellular proliferation, secretion and migration

Fibroblasts are essential for wound healing because they multiply, migrate, and secrete matrix elements like collagen that assist tissue repair and give structural integrity. Growth factors that stimulate these processes include VEGF, TGF-β, FGF, and EGF. In a mouse model, Wang et al. created a scaffold containing dual growth factors (EGF/bFGF), which improved collagen deposition and encouraged angiogenesis [89]. Additionally, vitamins A, C, E, B12, and B2 are necessary for wound healing, collagen synthesis, fibroblast proliferation, and inflammation reduction [90–93]. A poly(caprolactone)/gelatin nanofiber scaffold containing vitamin B12 was created by Sayeed Farzanfar et al. At one and three days after seeding, L929 cell proliferation significantly increased, according to cell studies. After 14 days, compared to the group without vitamin B12, the dressings containing B12 greatly improved wound closure (92.27 ± 6.84% vs. 64.62 ± 2.96%) [92].Curcumin, due to its bioactivity [175] is an excellent organic material which encourages the growth of fibroblasts and the deposition of collagen. Using curcumin-loaded cellulose nanofibers, Jose et al. demonstrated improved fibroblast migration in models of chronic wounds [94].

Neovascularization support

Neovascularization is an essential component in wound healing, and nanofiber scaffolds are essential in promoting this process. Through the delivery of angiogenic-promoting substances such as collagen proteins, growth factors, and cytokines, these scaffolds accelerate the healing process by improving the blood supply to the wound. To encourage angiogenesis, VEGF, FGF, PDGF, and TGF-β are frequently added to nanofiber scaffolds [48, 75, 95, 96]. Furthermore, endothelial cell migration and proliferation are induced by cytokines such as IL-8 and MCP-1 [97, 98], which accelerate angiogenesis further. Because of their biocompatibility, collagen proteins help to create the ideal conditions for the development of new blood vessels [99]. Zhan and colleagues created a multipurpose nanofiber with antibacterial and angiogenic qualities, loaded with chitosan, copper, and decellularized Wharton’s jelly matrix [100]. These investigations demonstrate how nanofiber scaffolds can promote neovascularization and quicken the healing process after wounds.

The goal of this localised and targeted drug release is to increase therapy safety and efficacy. pH-responsive nanofibrous scaffolds

Drug delivery systems made of pH-responsive nanofibers are intended to release medication in reaction to variations in the surrounding pH. These systems make use of polymers that have functional groups that ionise or protonate in response to pH variations, such as carboxyl and amine groups [103]. The polymer chains expand or contract as a result of this ionisation or protonation, changing the pore shape of the scaffold [103]. For example, polyethyleneimine (PEI) compresses owing to deprotonation in alkaline settings and expands due to protonation in acidic settings [104]. Furthermore, certain polymers, such as N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM), change from hydrophobic to hydrophilic depending on pH, which affects how drugs are released. pH variations can also cause the breakdown of polymer scaffolds or the breaking of chemical bonds that are sensitive to acids and bases, further facilitating drug release. These methods improve treatment efficiency and minimise negative effects by enabling tailored drug delivery, which is especially useful in environments like tumour tissues or inflammatory regions.

Koohzad et al. created a nanofibrous framework that adsorbs drugs by electrostatic action, allowing for the gradual release of medications at alkaline wound sites [105]. A pH-responsive nanofiber scaffold made by Altinbasak et al. employing an electrospinning polymer made of a main chain of acrylate that is protected by a trimethoxybenzaldehyde sidechain, which is hydrolyzable, and acid sensitive. The scaffold changes from a hydrophobic to a hydrophilic state at a physiologically relevant pH of 6.5 in the extracellular tumour environment. This causes the side chains to hydrolyze and the fibre diameter to roughly triple, which releases the medication contained within the nanofiber scaffold [106].

Temperature-responsive nanofibrous scaffolds

Temperature-responsive nanofiber scaffolds are made to release medication in response to variations in temperature by means of phase transitions, expansion, breakdown, or weakening of the bonds that hold the drug and scaffold materials together. Temperature-sensitive polymers, which go through phase transitions close to their upper critical solution temperature (UCST) or lower critical solution temperature (LCST), are used in these scaffolds. For example, the LCST of poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) is around 32 °C, at which point it becomes hydrophobic instead than hydrophilic, allowing the scaffold to contract and release the medication. Temperature increases can also result in the polymer scaffold thermally expanding in addition to phase changes [107]. The pore structure of the scaffold is altered by this expansion, creating more channels for drug diffusion, and accelerating drug release. Increased temperatures may also cause the scaffold to thermally degrade. This is especially true for biodegradable polymers like polylactic acid (PLA) and polycaprolactone (PCL), which release the pharmaceuticals they contain when they degrade at high temperatures. Moreover, higher temperatures have the ability to lessen interactions between the drug and the polymer, such as hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, which can facilitate drug release [108].

Wang Xiaocheng et al. utilised the temperature-responsive properties of PNIPAM hydrogels in conjunction with the photothermal conversion capabilities of MXene nanosheets. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) was delivered under controlled conditions, resulting in the proliferation, migration, and proangiogenic effects of endothelial cells [109]. Liang Yuting et al. by grafting the pH- and temperature-responsive polymer polyethyleneimine-N-isopropyl acrylamide (PEI-NIPAM) onto cellulose nanofibers (CNF-COOH), created a dual-responsive nanofiber. Photoresponsive nanofibrous scaffolds

Drug delivery systems with photoresponsive nanofibers are engineered to release medication in response to light through a variety of methods, including charge transfer, photothermal effects, photoinduced isomerization, and photocleavage of photosensitive bonds [110, 111]. These systems contain polymers that have chemical linkages (such as amide or photocleavable ester bonds) that are photosensitive [110]. When exposed to light, these bonds break, causing the scaffold to collapse and releasing the medication. When exposed to light, some materials go through a process called cis-trans isomerization, which modifies the scaffold’s structure and characteristics and affects drug release [112]. In some polymers, exposure to light can also cause charge transfer, which can change how the drug and polymer interact and make release easier. Furthermore, localised heating of the scaffold due to light irradiation may result in drug release by thermal expansion, degradation, or modifications in thermally induced interactions [108, 113] . For instance, surface plasmon resonance is used by nanofiber scaffolds containing gold nanoparticles to produce localised heat that initiates drug release when exposed to light [114]. Magnetic-responsive nanofiber drug delivery systems

Copolymerization or physical mixing are common methods used to integrate magnetic nanoparticles with magnetic-responsive polymers. These magnetic-responsive scaffolds display a range of behaviours in response to a magnetic field, including magnetostriction, magnetothermal reactions, magnetomotive effects, and chemical bond breakage triggered by the magnetic field [118–121]. Under the influence of the magnetic field, these effects cause changes in the polymer’s structure, chemical makeup, or physical characteristics. Yonggang Zhang et al., for example, created a magnetically reactive nanofiber ceramic scaffold with drug delivery and motility on demand. Their scaffold allowed for magnetically controlled drug release by releasing medications when exposed to an alternating magnetic field. It was made of a sandwich structure comprising hydroxyapatite (HA) and Fe3O4 [122]. Die Dong et al. developed a drug delivery method using cellulose nanofiber that is multi-responsive and loaded with Fe3O4 nanoparticles [108]. This allows for magnetic control over drug release processes. These magnetically responsive devices may find use in targeted medication delivery, where the exact regulation of therapeutic agent release through the use of external magnetic fields might improve treatment outcomes and minimise side effects.

Hydrogel based Nanofibrous Scaffolds are extensively being used for clinical trials at present. A cellulose-based nanofiber dressing i.e., the CE marked FibDex (NB0044) from the UPM-Kymmene Corporation has been used in clinical applications, which promotes wound healing efficiency by maintaining appropriate moisture balance. It detaches itself from the lesion after epitheliazation [235]. There are currently no established products for drug-carrying nanofiber dressings, making it a viable area for future development [47].

Another type of cellulose-based wound dressing is made from bacterial cellulose, which is produced in a manner akin to 3D printing by certain bacteria. This type of dressing has a very porous texture and can function as a scaffold for new tissue growth and wound healing. In both pre-clinical and clinical investigations, cellulose-based bandages have been used to treat burns, chronic ulcers, and surgical incisions and have been deemed safe and effective [236–238].

Nanozymes

Nanozymes are artificial enzymes that mimic the activity of a natural enzyme and can substitute for natural enzymes by mimicking the coordination environment of the catalytic site. Bacteria inhabit human skin without causing infection under healthy conditions, but when the skin is damaged, invasion of bacteria can lead to acute or chronic infection wounds. Enzymes are highly efficient, environmentally friendly, and specific catalysts [127]. During bacterial infections, human neutrophils utilise the action of the enzyme myeloperoxidase to produce certain highly toxic Reactive Oxygen Species from Hydrogen Peroxide to attack bacteria, however these natural enzymes hold limitations in practical applications [128]. Inspired by these enzymes, researchers are developing artificial Nanozymes with enzyme-like activity to attack microbes and disrupt biofilms [129, 130]. Nanozymes have several advantages, including high surface energy, excellent photoelectron transport, variable catalytic efficiency, and increased stability. They can be surface-modified for increased biocompatibility and are inexpensive, stable, and appropriate for mass production. Nanozymes show promise for treating bacterial infections and promoting wound healing.

Nanozymes are upcoming materials with numerous advantages over traditional antibacterial agents. Nanozymes, unlike antibiotics which attack bacterial cellular functions and can be rendered ineffective by occurrence of mutations and development of resistance mechanisms, function by generating highly toxic Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) through their oxidoreductase activity [131]. Bacteria struggle to combat ROS damage, making Nanozymes less susceptible to resistance. Nanozymes can be engineered for targeted antibacterial effects by integrating them with Targeted Molecules. For example, aptamers that can bind to specific targets can be conjugated to Nanozymes, for enhancing their selectivity. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) or cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) can be attached to the surface of Nanozymes which can target and penetrate bacterial cells effectively. Nucleic Acid Probes and Antibodies can also be used for targeted attack on microbes via Nanozymes. For example, Photoacid Molecules that change their charge state upon exposure to UV light and Molybdenum Disulfide (MoS2) when integrated into Nanozymes, can help Nanozymes preferentially bind to the targeted bacterial type depending on whether it’s gram positive or gram negative [132].

Nanozymes are produced through simple chemical synthesis methods making them cost-effective and suitable for mass production. Nanozymes can be of various types such as metal-based, metal oxide-based, carbon-based, and metal–organic framework (MOF) or covalent-organic framework (COF)-based nanozymes [203]. Nanozymes use their inherent enzyme-like capabilities, such as peroxidase (POD) and oxidase (OXD) functions, to rapidly generate hazardous free radicals and regulate ROS levels, resulting in antibacterial action [133]. Their principal antibacterial actions are based on their catalytic capabilities and capacity to break biofilms and bacteria directly. To improve efficacy, glucose oxidase can be encapsulated in metal organic frameworks, resulting in a pH regulated glucose-H202-hydroxyl radical cascade reaction with antibacterial activity [134]. Some nanozymes, including TiO2 nanotubes and black phosphorus, can be photoactivated to increase ROS production and antibacterial activity [135, 136]. Nanozymes with deoxyribonuclease (DNase)-like activity can break down environmental DNA in biofilms, whereas hydroperoxidase-like nanozymes can impair bacterial communication (quorum sensing) and limit biofilm formation [137, 138]. Furthermore, designs that take advantage of the biofilm microenvironment’s properties (negative charge, low pH, and high glutathione) increase antibacterial efficacy.

Gold nanozymes

Gold Nanoparticles offer stable catalytic, optical, electrical, supramolecular, and biological capabilities in a variety of shapes [143]. Smaller gold Nanoparticles have better catalytic activity due to more angular sites [144], albeit this varies with the shape and size. Gold Nanozymes catalyse ROS generation via peroxidase (POD) activity, making them useful in treating infected wounds.

Graphitic carbon nitride based nanozymes

Graphitic Carbon Nitride (gC3N4) is a nontoxic polymer with excellent thermal and chemical durability with enzyme-like catalytic activity [145, 146]. While not directly applicable to wound infections, combining gC3N4 with other antibacterial compounds shows prospective [131]. For example, Wang et al. prepared a g-C3N4@AuNPs nanocomposite (CNA), which can catalyse production of highly toxic -OH production at low H2O2 concentrations, effectively disintegrating biofilms and killing bacteria while avoiding tissue damage [147]. CNAs maintain high activity in the wound pH environment and are effective against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as reduce inflammation of lung infections caused by methicillin-resistant S. aureus MRSA [131]. Although there hasn’t been much research studying various forms of nanozymes in in vitro applications, higher surface-to-volume ratios are typically expected to indicate improved catalytic effectiveness [131].

The fundamental problem of metal nanomaterials, particularly silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), is their intrinsic toxicity and tendency to agglomerate when exposed to bacteria, reducing their antibacterial efficacy and biocompatibility [148, 149]. To solve this problem, Liang et al. created a hybrid material by integrating AgNPs into ultrathin black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNs), taking use of BPNs’ tunable bandgaps, high carrier mobility, and in-plane anisotropy. This hybrid decreases toxicity by slowing the release of Ag ions while increasing photocatalytic activity by inhibiting electron–hole recombination and broadening light absorption [135]. Dressings, namely hydrogels, are being investigated as stable platforms for metal nanozymes. Hydrogels are three-dimensional hydrophilic polymer networks that keep wounds wet and promote healing. Recent research has focused on developing nanobiocomposites using hydrogels such as carboxymethyl cellulose, lignin-agarose, and sodium alginate [150–155]. Jia et al., for example, used tannic acid on Ag nanoparticles to create a self-coagulating hydrogel nanozyme that improved mechanical characteristics, electrical conductivity, and antibacterial efficiency against E. coli by producing hydroxyl radicals from H2O2 [156].

Metal oxide nanoymes

Metal oxide nanomaterials, possess unique redox and optoelectronic properties, offer remarkable catalytic characteristics that resemble those of natural enzymes [157]. The breakdown of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into reactive oxygen species (ROS), just like other types of nanozymes, can be efficiently catalysed by these metal oxide nanomaterials [158]. These extremely reactive ROS have the ability to cause oxidative stress, which can destroy proteins, nucleic acids, bacterial cell membranes, and other essential biological components. Additionally, the pH dependent nature of their enzymatic activity presents opportunities for tailored applications in specific environments, such as infected wounds.

Various metal oxide nanozymes have been investigated for their antibacterial properties, such as Fe3O4 [159], CuO [160], CeO2 [161–163], V2O5 [164], Tb4O7 [165], and ZnO [166] etc. The utilisation of metal oxides in combination with specific drug carriers characterised to provide the most benefits has advantages such as enhanced antibacterial efficiency, controlled activation, pH dependent activity, controlled internal environment and multifunctionality.

Bacterial biofilms can be efficiently broken down into their constituent proteins, polysaccharides, and nucleic acids by the ROS that catalyses the production of H2O2 [128]. Fe3O4 metal nanoparticles (MNPs) have been shown to have POD-like action, which can break down H2O2 [167]. However, ROS have a relatively short survival cycle and are not specific to bacteria in practical applications. Consequently, reducing the amount of time that ROS need to reach bacteria is a feasible way to raise sterilisation effectiveness. Using Hyaluronic Acid-encapsulated graphene-mesoporous silica nanosheets (GS) as drug carriers and Fe3O4 MNPs as catalysts, Ji et al. created a targeted drug delivery system to deliver ascorbic acid (AA) as prodrug. The light-absorbing capacity of GS was utilised to work in tandem with the ROS that Fe3O4 nanoparticles catalyse in order to solve the issue of huge ROS inactivation during the transfer process, efficiently destroy the biofilm in situ, and drastically reduce the action distance of ROS [168].

Nanozymes can be further activated by adjusting external factors such as pH and light. For example, visible light can activate CuO nanorods (NRs) with peroxidase (POD) -like activity, increasing the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and improving their antibacterial efficacy [160]. The pH-dependent nature of nanozymes can restrict their use in wounds that are infected. Under neutral pH circumstances, modulators such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) can increase the enzymatic activity of citrate-modified Fe3O4 nanozymes, enhancing their antibacterial efficiency [160]. Drug-resistant bacteria are effectively combated by dual-enzyme active vanadium oxide nanodots (VOxNDs), which demonstrate synergistic POD-like and oxidase-like activity [169]. Additionally Increasing the antibacterial effects and accelerating wound healing can be achieved by combining photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) with nanozyme therapy [163].

Chalcogenide-based nanozymes

Metal sulfide-based nanomaterials provide significant benefits in terms of electron optics, physicochemistry, functional structure, and cost of manufacture. They also exhibit excellent photodynamic properties when utilised as enzyme-like active materials, making them suitable for effective and safe antibacterial applications in wound healing. 2D nanomaterials composed of metal sulfides render more environmentally friendly than their metal or metal oxide counterparts. However, there are safety concerns about using these materials since they can dissolve and lead to the creation of harmful heavy metals. Hence, it’s important to rectify the stability and safety of these materials to treat infected wounds [170].

Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), copper sulfide (CuS), and iron disulfide (FeS2) are widely studied due to their intrinsic enzyme-like catalytic activity [171, 172]. Chalcogenides possess intrinsic enzyme-like catalytic activity, but it is insufficient on its own for antibacterial treatments. Most research focuses on enhancing catalytic performance or integrating it with other techniques. Metal Sulfides also possess inherent optical properties which provide excellent photocatalytic and photothermal properties for in situ photodynamic sterilization [135]. Yi et al. developed a PEG functionalised molybdenum Disulfide nanoflowers (PEG-MoS2 NFs) using a one-pot hydrothermal method. The combination of its POD activity and PTT activity shortens treatment time and it has better affinity for H2O2 than horseradish peroxidase. This formulation demonstrated ability to kill ampicillin-resistant E. Coli and B. Subtilis Endospores [135]. Yu et all utilised PEG- MoS2 NFs to serve as a coating for TiO2 nanotubes with high specific surface area and excellent electron transport ability. By coating TiO2 nanotubes, MoS2’s POD-like activity increases and TiO2’s bandgap decreases, which thus enhances the photoresponse range and results in copious amounts of ROS production [173].

Although natural organosulfur compounds have long been used to combat bacterial infections, their limited water solubility and challenging mass synthesis make them unsuitable for use in biomedicine [174]. Using a solvothermal technique, Xu et al. transformed natural organosulfur compounds into inorganic sulphur compounds, and the resulting nanomaterial has antibacterial capacity 500 more than organosulfur compounds generated from garlic [172]. By heating an alkaline solution containing Cu2 + and bovine serum albumin (BSA), Nain et al. produced copper sulphide nanocrystals (BSA-CuS NCs). This simple process eliminates the need for the addition of an extra sulphur source. BSA-CuS NCs demonstrated a more than 60-fold increased antibacterial response in comparison to non-irradiated settings, when exposed to near infrared (NIR) laser irradiation [176].

Carbon-based nanozymes

Carbon-based nanomaterials, including carbon dots (CDs), carbon nanotubes, carbon nitride, fullerenes, and graphene, have been widely reported for nanozyme catalytic applications [131]. Carbon-based nanomaterials undergo surface functionalization and have great biocompatibility and catalytic capabilities; hence they are being used more and more for nanozyme catalytic applications. Examples of these materials include carbon dots (CDs), carbon nanotubes (CNTs), carbon nitride, fullerenes, and graphene. These materials have oxygen-containing functional groups which give rise to intrinsic peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), hydrolase, and superoxide dismutase activities, which are frequently seen in them. Therefore, they act as feasible options for both treating and avoiding infectious wounds.

Carbon dots (CDs)

Made of zero-dimensional carbon, CDs are stable, biocompatible, and have excellent optical qualities [177, 178]. They are effective against E. coli in light-induced sterilization, producing superoxide ions in the presence of visible light to attain over 100% bactericidal effectiveness. Different precursors, such as S. cerevisiae and ampicillin, can be used to create CDs that selectively stain and measure the survival of bacteria, damage cell membranes, and inhibit a variety of bacteria, including S. aureus and Listeria monocytogenes [179]. Langlin et Al. developed a Fe/N-doped chitosan-chelated carbon dot-based nanozyme which showed multiple advantages such as highly efficient antibacterial activity, excellent peroxidase-like activity, high stability, and high biocompatibility, shortening the wound healing time [180, 181].

Carbon nanotubes

Because of their low toxicity and prospective applications in biomedicine, CNTs can be altered to increase catalytic efficiency. Surface groups functioning as active or inhibitory sites are revealed when CNTs are oxidised to form oxide-rich CNTs (o-CNTs). Enhancing POD action using modification techniques such as 2-bromo-1-acetophenone treatment permits the production of ROS, lowers bacterial inflammation, and inhibits bacterial growth [131].

Graphene-based nanozymes

The antibacterial qualities of graphene materials rely on a number of variables, including layers, shape, and electron transport capability [181]. When coupled with low-concentration H2O2, graphene quantum dots (GQDs), which are less toxic than graphene oxide (GO), transform it into hydroxyl radicals (-OH) to improve antibacterial characteristics [182, 183]. Under light, functionalized GQDs show synergistic sterilisation through photothermal, photodynamic, and multivalent interactions.

MOF-based nanozymes

Coordination chemistry is used to assemble organic ligands and metal ions/clusters to generate Metal–Organic Frameworks (MOFs), which are highly permeable and crystalline porous coordination polymers [184]. MOFs with enzyme-like activity can be produced by designing specific metal nodes and organic ligands [185].

MOFs can be tailored for specific applications due to their flexible coordination chemistry, tunable porosity, mesoporous structure, customisable cavities, and channels allowing for specific interactions within the framework [186, 187], slow and continuous release of ROS, high loading capacity, biocompatibility, biodegradability, dispersibility, beneficial photocatalytic activity and antibacterial properties.

Applications of MOFs include sensing, biomedicine, gas adsorption/separation, and catalysis [188–192]. They have recently been investigated as antibacterial agents, especially for the treatment of wounds. By encasing natural enzymes in their exoskeletons, MOFs protect them from external stimuli and keep them activated. However, the pH of the optimal reaction environment for nanozymes is generally 3–4, and it is often troublesome to optimize performance in the microenvironment of infected wounds, which severely limits their application [193].

In general, 2D MOFs have better catalytic activity than 3D MOFs due to their higher specific surface area. Cu-TCPP(Fe)-encapsulated glucose oxidase (GOx) is one example of a 2D MOF developed by Liu et al., that can form self-activating cascades. By converting glucose to gluconic acid and H2O2, GOx lowers pH and increases catalytic activity in wound settings. Li et al. in their research, utilised a coprecipitation technique to create a multilayer film with GOx and Hn on the surface of MnCO3 and then eliminated MnCo3 to create Microreactors (MRs). These MRs have strong antibacterial activity and enhanced glucose affinity in mildly acidic environments [194].Although metal-based nanozymes are better at catalysing reactions, they still have drawbacks such as aggregation and biosafety difficulties. By combining metal nanoparticles and MOFs, stability and reaction kinetics can be improved and aggregation can be reduced [195]. Hu et al. generated ultrasmall gold Nanoparticles (UsAuNPs) on ultrathin 2D MOFS via in situ reduction. MOFs provide an excellent reaction platform for UsAuNPs as they are prone to aggregation despite their advantages. UsAuNPs/MOFs have superior stability which can help reduce mass transfer resistance and increase reaction speed [196].

COF-based nanozymes

COFs are emerging porous crystalline materials that are tunable, stable, eco-friendly, and non-toxic [204–206]. In the field of medicine, COF-based nanomaterials are currently in investigation for a variety of applications, such as serving as ligands or carriers for antibiotics, photodetectors, aptasensors, photodynamics, and photocatalysis [134, 141, 197–200]. Zhang et al. created a COF-incorporated photosensitizer (TPDA) that improved light-converting capacity and showed strong antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus [200]. Using an ionic COF nanozyme (GFeF) with glucose oxidase (GOx) for indirect H2O2 production, Li et al. demonstrated potent antibacterial activity while minimising harm to normal cells [201]. Additionally, Zhang et al. (2023) invented the MOF@COF nanozyme (NMCTP-TTA), in which enzyme-binding pockets were aided by surface COF, producing remarkable antibacterial effects through the formation of ROS [202].

Nanozymes hold tremendous potential in wound healing due to their ability to generate ROS for antibacterial action and tissue regeneration. The use of Nanozymes for clinical trials in the burgeoning area of exploring nanomaterials in wound healing is still in its infancy. Initial trials are aimed at determining the efficacy, safety, and biocompatibility of nanozymes for wound healing. The key issues include providing sustained and regulated release at wound sites, reducing toxicity, and improving biocompatibility [231]. The in vivo strategies explored in this field still require further study for targeting unwanted degradation of Nanozymes inside the body [232]. Prussian Blue Nanoparticles that can act as artificial nanozymes are recently approved by the US FDA for their ROS scavenging properties [230]. Studies have indicated that most of the Nanozymes made via utilising FDA- approved nanoparticles are safe to use in animals [233]. Iron-oxide-based nanoparticles have previously been employed as clinical MR contrast agents and hence iron-oxide-based nanozymes can be considered attractive candidates in this regard [234]. Other nanozymes based on biocompatible nanoparticles, such as graphene oxide, metal–organic frameworks, single-atom nanozymes, and so on, are also being thoroughly investigated [234].

Electro active polymers

The use of intrinsically electroactive/conducting polymers in wound care and skin tissue engineering presents a novel opportunity for accelerated wound healing by electrical stimulation that promotes cell migration, proliferation, collagen synthesis, and angiogenesis which are crucial for tissue repair [216]. The way they function is based on the fact that the human epidermis usually sustains a transepithelial potential, similar to an ‘internal/endogenous battery [65]. When the skin’s structural integrity is compromised, this potential is disrupted, creating a current at the wound’s edge. This current directs cell migration towards the wound centre, facilitating the healing process. It also controls the orientation and frequency of cell division [212]. Researchers have found that by applying an external electric field to the wound area in a similar manner, the rate of wound healing can be further accelerated [213–215].They have also demonstrated enhanced antibacterial activity and the potential for controlled drug delivery [217]. Through their increased electrical conductivity, EAPs can enable the direct application of electrical stimulation to the wound area. The delivery of drugs or biological agents to the wound site can also be controlled using external electrical stimuli. Integrating EAPs into wound dressings leverages their unique physicochemical properties to synergistically combine antimicrobial, antioxidant, and electroactive functionalities, offering a multifaceted approach to accelerate the wound healing cascade. To construct a dressing with the maximum number of ideal factors, several synthetic and natural polymers can be utilized [218]. The toxicity and biocompatibility of electroactive polymers (EAPs) are under ongoing clinical investigation. Consequently, regulatory guidelines for their application in treatments are still developing and undergoing refinement, resulting in a current absence of clinically approved EAP-based treatments.

PEDOT

Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene), commonly known as PEDOT, is a conducting polymer, valued for its excellent electrical conductivity. PEDOT,while has not been used independently, has been used in conjugation with other materials for wound healing. Studies have demonstrated preparation of conductive hydrogels by incorporation of PEDOT, which possess properties of both hydrogels and electroactive polymers. Such gels have antioxidant properties that maintain an intracellular redox balance, are biocompatible and the conductive properties of the polymer help significantly accelerate wound healing [66].

PEDOT:PSS

As mentioned before PEDOT is a widely studied composite material with significant potential in wound healing. The conductive properties of PEDOT in combination with the processability and mechanical flexibility provided by PSS, make it particularly suitable for biomedical applications [68]. This enhanced healing is primarily attributed to the polymer’s ability to promote angiogenesis, as evidenced in studies by the significant upregulation of VEGF and a slight increase in TGF-β1 expression in experimental subjects [207, 208]. Importantly, PEDOT:PSS have been found to show negligible cytotoxic effects on body cells like macrophages, and not induce anti-inflammatory skin effects, underscoring its biocompatibility [68]. The hydrogel formulation of PEDOT:PSS has been proven effective in both in vivo and in vitro settings, emphasizing its clinical potential [220–225]. Given its high electrical conductivity, water dispersibility, and cytocompatibility [208–211], PEDOT:PSS emerges as a promising candidate for developing advanced wound dressings and medical devices, offering a non-toxic and effective solution for enhancing wound healing without compromising skin integrity [219]. These findings pave the way for further exploration of PEDOT:PSS in clinical wound care applications, particularly for chronic wounds [67]. Finally, it is demonstrated that PEDOT:PSS hydrogels and hydrogel fibers have potential to be used for the development of soft and self-healable hydrogel bioelectronic devices [68, 69].

Polypyrrole

Polypyrrole (PPy) is used in hydrogels, electrospun fibres and composites with biopolymers like chitosan/collagen for wound dressing and healing. Studies display that nanofibers composing 10% polypyrrole possess better cell adhesion, growth and proliferation properties comparing to other compositions [70].Composite nanofibres containing PPy with other suitable polymers give the wound dressing enhanced flexibility, breathability, and wettability. Electrical stimulations by harvesting mechanical motions and positive charges on the polypyrrole surface have been shown to kill over 96% of bacteria due to their synergistic effects on cell membrane disruption. Studies also suggest that electrical stimulation enhances gene expression of growth factors for accelerated wound healing. This provides new insights into the design of wearable and multifunctional electrotherapy devices for chronic wound treatment [71].

Polyaniline(PANI)

PANI is a conducting polymer that can be synthesized in various forms, such as nanofibers and films or incorporated into other materials such as hydrogels. Studies have demonstrated better healing and intensification in collagen and granulation at the site of the wound, along with reduced inflammation.

Natural polymers and synthetic polymers

Natural polymers like chitosan, gelatin, collage, alginate, and silk fibroin are combined with synthetic polymers to combine electrical conductivity as well as biocompatibility [72].

Microfluidics in wound healing

Microfluidic devices enhance wound healing through precise drug delivery, allowing controlled release of therapeutic agents in response to specific triggers. These systems can simulate wound environments for research, providing insights into healing processes and aiding the development of new treatments. Microfluidics also supports cell culture and tissue engineering by supplying necessary nutrients and growth factors in a controlled manner. Integrated sensors in microfluidic devices enable real-time monitoring of wound conditions, facilitating timely adjustments to treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes. The synergy between personalized therapeutics and microfluidics offers a powerful combination for developing precise, effective, and patient-specific wound care solutions. Microfluidics show promise for enhancing wound healing in the future, but there are no approved clinical treatments using this technology currently. This is due to challenges like standardization, scalability, and limited human clinical trials to prove safety and effectiveness.

Personalized therapeutics in wound healing

Personalized therapeutics involves creating customized treatment plans based on an individual’s genetic makeup, lifestyle, and specific wound conditions, ensuring more effective and targeted healing. This approach utilizes genomic and proteomic profiling to identify biomarkers and develop tailored therapies. Personalized drug delivery systems release therapeutic agents precisely when needed, while autologous stem cell therapy uses a patient’s own cells to promote tissue regeneration, minimizing immune rejection. Advanced monitoring systems provide real-time feedback, allowing for continuous adjustment of treatments to optimize healing conditions. While personalized therapeutics have achieved significant progress in oncology and pharmacogenomics, in the realm of wound healing they still remain in the early stages of development and largely limited to experimental trials, with widespread clinical adoption yet to be achieved.

Conclusion

The incorporation of nanotechnology and smart materials into wound treatment offers a significant improvement in therapeutic interventions. Nanotechnology has exceptional promise in wound care, owing to nanoparticles’ unique features, such as a high surface area to volume ratio, which enhances their interaction with biological systems. Silver, gold, zinc oxide, and PLGA nanoparticles provide particular benefits such as antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory properties, and tissue regeneration. Smart materials, such as nanofibrous scaffolds, hydrogels, and electroactive polymers, add dynamic features that aid in healing and medication delivery. These materials resemble the extracellular matrix, provide a moist environment, and respond to electrical stimuli, so promoting cell growth and migration. Biofabrication, microfluidic systems, bioresponsive scaffolds, and personalised therapies are all emerging concepts that hold promise for further advancement.

These sophisticated dressings have antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and pro-regenerative qualities that help to speed up healing and reduce consequences from chronic wounds. Future research should focus on optimising the manufacture and functionalization of these materials to improve their biocompatibility, efficacy, and patient-specific adaptability. This paradigm change towards patient-centered wound care solutions has the potential to transform therapeutic interventions, enhance patient outcomes, and lessen the socioeconomic burden associated with chronic wounds. To summarise, the use of innovative materials and technologies in wound healing offers promising opportunities for improving therapy efficacy and patient outcomes. Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs) and Covalent Organic Frameworks (COFs) provide customisable platforms with tunable porosity and enzyme-like activities, whereas electroactive polymers like PEDOT and its derivatives have significant potential for promoting angiogenesis and speeding up wound healing.

Nanozyme-based methods, including metal- and carbon-based nanozymes, show promise for overcoming antibacterial resistance and enhancing therapeutic outcomes in infected wounds. The mix of natural and synthetic polymers improves the electrical conductivity and biocompatibility of wound dressings, while microfluidic devices allow for accurate drug delivery and real-time monitoring of wound parameters. Personalised medicines, which are informed by genetic and proteomic analysis, customise treatments to particular patient needs, hence optimising healing outcomes. These breakthroughs highlight a multidimensional approach to wound care that combines materials science, biotechnology, and personalised medicine to speed up healing and improve clinical results. Future research should focus on improving advanced material production procedures, clarifying the underlying mechanisms of their therapeutic benefits, and performing large-scale clinical trials to validate their efficacy and safety in a wide range of patient groups. Furthermore, efforts should be made to build integrated systems that incorporate several features, such as medication administration, sensing, and feedback mechanisms, in order to construct smart wound dressings that can respond to changing wound conditions in real time. By addressing these issues and capitalising on the synergistic benefits of modern materials and technologies, the field of wound healing stands to make major advances, ultimately enhancing the quality of life for millions of patients worldwide. Clinical experiments with nanomaterials in wound healing are in their early phases, but the results are encouraging for a variety of applications. Nanozymes, particularly Prussian Blue Nanoparticles and iron-oxide-based nanozymes, have promising ROS scavenging and biocompatibility qualities. Hydrogel-based nanofibrous scaffolds, particularly cellulose-based dressings, have shown efficacy and safety in clinical trials. Although silver nanoparticle treatments are efficacious and approved, other nanoparticles, such as gold and zinc oxide, confront biocompatibility and toxicity issues. Emerging technologies such as microfluidics and personalised therapies are still in development, necessitating more comprehensive testing to determine their clinical feasibility.
