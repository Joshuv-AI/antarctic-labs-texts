Short- and long-term outcomes of totally robotic versus robotic-assisted total gastrectomy for advanced gastric cancer: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Dongning Liu, Feng Xiao, Penghui He, Yahang Liang, Jinyuan Duan, Shanping Ye, Wenjun Hu, Ruichen Zhou, Taiyuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis research conducts a comparison of Totally Robotic Total Gastrectomy (TRTG) and Robot-Assisted Total Gastrectomy (RATG) to evaluate their relative outcomes in patients with advanced gastric cancer, providing evidence to guide individualized surgical strategy selection.MethodsWe retrospectively gathered clinical data from patients who underwent either TRTG or RATG between January 2015 and December 2021, totaling 204 participants. After applying Propensity Score Matching (PSM), the matched cohort consisted of 118 patients, with 59 individuals in each of the TRTG and RATG groups. The study then compared the short-term and long-term outcomes between these two groups.ResultsPost-matching, there were no significant baseline differences between the groups. Surgery duration and anastomosis duration were both longer in the TRTG group than in the RATG group. However, TRTG was associated with significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss, shorter times to postoperative mobilization, flatus, and eating, decreased hospital stay, smaller incision sizes, and lower postoperative stress responses compared to RATG. The RATG group harvested a higher number of lymph nodes. No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding postoperative complications, proximal and distal margins, nerve invasion, vascular cancer thrombus, postoperative drainage output, and hospitalization costs. The three-year overall survival and disease-free survival rates were similar between both groups.ConclusionsTRTG offers a safer and more advantageous surgical option regarding short-term outcomes compared to RATG and may be considered a preferable choice for total gastrectomy.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide. Statistics from 2022 indicate approximately 968,000 new cases globally, representing around 4.9% of all cancer diagnoses and ranking fifth in incidence. It accounts for about 659,000 deaths, approximately 6.8% of cancer-related mortality, also placing fifth in death rates [1]. In China, cancer data for 2022 reported roughly 358,700 newly diagnosed gastric cancer cases, making up 7.4% of all cancer diagnoses for the Year, with mortality reaching around 260,400 [2].

Since Kitano et al. first introduced laparoscopic-assisted gastrectomy in 1991 [3], minimally invasive surgical techniques have undergone substantial advancements. Laparoscopic radical D2 lymphadenectomy has emerged as a primary therapeutic approach for cancer treatment, garnering international consensus and establishing itself as the standard surgical method [4, 5]. Laparoscopic Total gastrectomy can be performed via two primary methods: Laparoscopic-Assisted Total Gastrectomy (LATG) and Totally Laparoscopic Total Gastrectomy (TLTG). However, the widespread adoption of TLTG is hindered by the technical complexities involved in gastrointestinal reconstruction and the requisite accumulation of laparoscopic expertise among surgeons [6, 7]. Compared to traditional laparoscopy, robotic systems provide three-dimensional imaging and high-definition visualization. These features expand the surgical field, minimize physiological tremors, and enable multi-arm operations. Consequently, robotic systems address many limitations associated with laparoscopic techniques. Robotic surgery offers numerous advantages over laparoscopic procedures, including: reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, faster recovery of gastrointestinal function, quicker time to first flatus, and improved cosmetic outcomes of anastomoses [8–10], Importantly, these benefits are achieved while maintaining robust oncological safety.

With the assistance of robotic technology, total gastrectomy can be performed through Robot-Assisted Total Gastrectomy (RATG) or Totally Robotic Total Gastrectomy (TRTG). The intracorporeal-dominant approach of Totally Robotic Total Gastrectomy (TRTG)—analogous to Totally Laparoscopic Total Gastrectomy (TLTG)—confers distinct advantages over Robotic-Assisted Total Gastrectomy (RATG), including: reduced intraoperative blood loss, accelerated recovery of bowel function [11]. Despite these benefits, several questions remain unanswered, particularly regarding which surgical approach provides greater patient benefits. Moreover, there is a paucity of Literature reporting on the short-term and long-term outcomes of RATG and TRTG. To address this gap, we collected data from 204 patients and utilized propensity score matching to minimize allocation bias between the two study groups. This study aims to compare the short-term and long-term outcomes following RATG and TRTG, thereby offering new insights and evidence to enhance practices in robotic total gastrectomy.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study analyzed data from patients diagnosed with resectable gastric cancer without distant metastasis, confirmed through imaging and histological examinations, who underwent surgery between January 2015 and December 2021 at our gastroenterological surgical center. A total of 204 patients underwent robot-assisted total gastrectomy, categorized into RATG (140 cases) and TRTG (64 cases). All procedures complied with our center's ethical standards and the Declaration of Helsinki, with informed consent obtained from each patient. Clinical data were sourced from a prospectively maintained database at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, and ethical approval was granted by the hospital’s ethics committee.

Propensity score matching, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

Prior to PSM, significant differences existed between RATG and TRTG groups concerning clinical-pathological characteristics (Table 1). To mitigate these biases, PSM was performed using a 1:1 matching ratio with a caliper of 0.05, considering variables such as gender, age, BMI, ASA score, T stage, N stage, TNM classification, histological type, Lauren classification, and tumor size. Surgeon experience

All procedures were exclusively performed by Dr. Taiyuan Li, whose cumulative experience exceeds 4,500 robotic gastrointestinal procedures since January 2015. Prior to the index case of this study, 50 completed robotic gastrostomies established technical proficiency, effectively mitigating potential learning curve bias.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. Statistical significance was assessed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, analyzed via χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Two-factor repeated measures ANOVA was used for post-operative WBC, CRP, PCT, IL-6 and VAS scores. Survival analysis utilized the Kaplan–Meier method, with log-rank tests comparing TRTG and RATG groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Surgical technique

All operations were performed by the same surgeon, adhering to standard robotic D2 lymphadenectomy and Roux-en-Y anastomosis in both TRTG and RATG groups. There were no differences in pelvic dissection, vessel ligation, or tumor resection between the two groups; the primary distinction lay in the method of gastrointestinal reconstruction.

Gastrointestinal reconstruction methods

TRTG Group (Totally Robotic Reconstruction): After esophageal mobilization, a Linear stapler was used to transect the esophagus 20 cm from the ligament of Treitz. The distal jejunum was positioned laterally to the esophagus, with small punctures made in the mesenteric border for the linear stapler jaws, completing a side-to-side anastomosis. The specimen was placed in a retrieval bag, the abdomen was irrigated with warm distilled water, a drain was inserted, and an appropriate incision was made for specimen removal.

RATG Group (Assisted Small Incision Reconstruction): Following lymphadenectomy, a small incision (5–7 cm) was made below the xiphoid process, through which a circular stapler was introduced to transect the esophagus. The jejunum was transected 15–25 cm distal to the Ligament of Treitz, and an anastomosis was performed using the stapler. An end-to-end anastomosis was then achieved with Linear staplers, followed by a side-to-side anastomosis 40–60 cm from the esophagojejunostomy. The abdomen was irrigated, a drain was placed, and the incision was closed.

Adjuvant treatment

Patients with locally advanced gastric cancer received fluorouracil-based combination therapy as per guidelines to alleviate symptoms and improve survival and quality of life.

Results

Before applying propensity score matching, notable differences existed between the RATG and TRTG groups in the overall cohort, particularly regarding age and maximum tumor diameter (64 [58–69] Years vs. 59 [55–66] years; p = 0.015). Since age significantly affects long-term survival, PSM was employed to balance intergroup differences, resulting in 118 matched patients. Detailed outcomes of the matched cohort are presented in Table 1, showing no significant differences in gender, age, BMI, ASA score, T stage, N stage, clinical TNM stage, histological type, Lauren classification, or tumor size between the two groups.

Short-term outcomes

Perioperative metrics (Table 2) indicated that the TRTG group had longer surgical (243.3 ± 40.3 min vs. 227.4 ± 46.5 min; p = 0.044) and anastomosis times (102.5 ± 9.8 min vs. 91.2 ± 8.3 min; p < 0.001) compared to the RATG group. However, intra-abdominal blood loss was significantly less in the TRTG group (120 [90–250] ml vs. 200 [100–250] ml; p = 0.031). Regarding gastrointestinal recovery, recovery times were significantly shorter in the TRTG group than in the RATG group: time to first flatus (42.6 ± 3.8 h vs. 57.0 ± 7.6 h; p < 0.001), time to first eating (63.6 ± 5.5 h vs. 70.8 ± 8.7 h; p < 0.001), and time to hospital discharge (7.9 ± 1.2 days vs. 9.8 ± 4.6 days; p = 0.003). Additionally, time to post operative activity was also shorter in the TRTG group (33 [28–38] vs. 41 [35–47]; p < 0.001). Incision length was significantly shorter in the TRTG group (3.6 ± 0.5 cm) compared to the RATG group (6.4 ± 0.6 cm; p < 0.001), nearly halving the average size. Postoperative analgesic requirements were significantly lower in the TRTG group (5.3 ± 1.5) than in the RATG group (8.9 ± 1.9; p < 0.001). There were no significant differences in total hospitalization costs (p = 0.068) and drain output (p = 0.355). Pathologically, the RATG group had fewer harvested lymph nodes (25 [19–30] vs. 29 [21–35]; p = 0.032). No significant differences were observed between groups regarding proximal margin (p = 0.590), distal margin (p = 0.916), nerve invasion (p = 0.697), or vascular invasion (p = 0.712), and no positive margins were noted in either group. The TRTG group reported 6 complications (1 bowel obstruction, 2 intra-abdominal bleedings, 1 abdominal infection, 1 anastomotic bleeding, and 1 fistula), while the RATG group had 8 complications (2 bowel obstructions, 2 intra-abdominal bleedings, 1 pulmonary complication, 2 incision infections, and 1 anastomotic bleeding). The overall complication rates did not significantly differ between groups (p = 0.569). Notably, the TRTG group had no incision-related or pulmonary complications, whereas the RATG group had 2 and 1 cases, respectively. Postoperative inflammatory markers indicated lower CRP levels in the TRTG group on days 1, 3, and 5 post-surgery (p < 0.001, p < 0.001), while leukocyte counts (p = 0.139) and procalcitonin levels (p = 0.643) showed no significant differences. VAS scores were also significantly lower in the TRTG group (p < 0.001). Discussion

Laparoscopic total gastrectomy has established itself as a reliable and safe technique for managing gastric cancer [6, 7]. With advancements in robotic technology, there has been a growing focus on robotic total gastrectomy using systems like the Da Vinci robot. However, existing literature on TRTG remains sparse, primarily limited to case reports [12, 13] and comparisons with laparoscopic [14–16]. Few studies have thoroughly examined the short-term and long-term outcomes of TRTG compared to RATG. In our clinical practice, we observed enhanced short-term postoperative outcomes in certain TRTG patients, prompting this study. Our findings support these observations.

Perioperative data are commonly used to assess the advantages of each surgical approach. In this study, both total operative time and anastomosis time were significantly longer in the TRTG group compared to the RATG group (p = 0.044; mean difference: 16 min for operative time; p < 0.001; mean difference: 11 min for anastomosis time). These differences are clinically negligible in actual practice, yet the accelerated postoperative recovery they facilitate is clinically justified. Concurrently, TRTG demonstrated significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss (120 [90–250] mL vs. 200 [100–250] mL; p = 0.031). Gastrointestinal recovery was notably better in the TRTG group, with shorter times to flatus and eating (42.6 ± 3.8 h vs. 57.0 ± 7.6 h; p < 0.001; 63.6 ± 5.5 h vs. 70.8 ± 8.7 h; p < 0.001). This improvement may be attributed to reduced extraluminal bowel manipulation in the TRTG group, thereby diminishing stimulation and preserving gastrointestinal function. Additionally, the TRTG group exhibited: smaller incision length (3.6 ± 0.5 cm vs. 6.4 ± 0.6 cm; p < 0.001), shorter time to post operative activity (33 [28–38] vs. 41 [35–47]; p < 0.001), reduced number of additional analgesics used (5.3 ± 1.5 doses vs. 8.9 ± 1.9 doses; p < 0.001). While hospitalization costs showed no significant difference (p = 0.068), the TRTG group demonstrated significantly shorter hospital stay (7.9 ± 1.2 days vs. 9.8 ± 4.6 days; p = 0.003), aligning with enhanced recovery goals [17, 18].

From an oncological perspective, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding proximal resection margin, distal resection margin, perineural invasion, or lymph vascular invasion (p = 0.590, p = 0.916, p = 0.697, p = 0.712, respectively). Although the total lymph node yield was statistically higher in the TRTG group compared to the RATG group, this numerical difference is clinically negligible. Crucially, our survival analysis demonstrated no significant difference in long-term oncological outcomes between the two surgical approaches. Consequently, the observed disparity in lymph node retrieval did not confer a meaningful clinical impact and is unlikely to affect patient prognosis. Safety assessments indicated no significant differences in complication rates (p = 0.569), with the TRTG group reporting no incision or major pulmonary complications, potentially due to the smaller incision size. Surgical stress response significantly impacts outcomes, with greater trauma leading to elevated postoperative inflammation [19, 20]. Our study found that TRTG resulted in lower CRP and IL-6 levels on postoperative days 1, 3, and 5 compared to RATG (p < 0.001), while leukocyte counts (p = 0.139) and procalcitonin levels (p = 0.643) showed no significant differences, our results were consistent with previous studies [21, 22]. Lower inflammatory levels in the TRTG group may positively influence long-term prognosis by reducing cancer recurrence and metastasis risks [23, 24]. Additionally, lower VAS pain scores in the TRTG group (p < 0.001) indicate better postoperative comfort. Clinical evidence supports the benefits of robotic-assisted total gastrectomy in promoting rapid recovery, minimizing complications, and enhancing lymphadenectomy [25–27], although open abdominal techniques do not fully exploit robotic minimally invasive advantages. In this study, TRTG demonstrated advantages in reduced blood loss, expedited gastrointestinal recovery, diminished inflammation, less pain, and absence of incision complications, reinforcing the superiority of robotic-assisted intraluminal anastomosis.

Given the frequent diagnosis of gastric cancer at advanced stages (II and III) due to limited early detection, achieving curative resection remains paramount. Concurrently, rising patient demand for improved cosmetic outcomes has driven the adoption of minimally invasive surgical techniques like robotic gastrectomy. However, the dominance of Intuitive Surgical Inc. in the surgical robot market has led to increased hospitalization costs. In response, China has committed to developing its own surgical robot industry, introducing various domestic robotic systems such as MicroHand S [28], KanDuo SR1000 [29], Toumai MT1000 [30], and Edge MP1000 [31].

To our knowledge, this represents the first comparative analysis of long-term oncological outcomes between Totally Robotic Total Gastrectomy (TRTG) and Robotic-Assisted Total Gastrectomy (RATG). Our study provides initial evidence, demonstrating comparable 3-year overall survival (OS; p = 0.766) and disease-free survival (DFS; p = 0.784) rates between the two approaches, as illustrated by the overlapping survival curves. These findings suggest comparable oncological safety and efficacy for TRTG and RATG in the treatment of advanced gastric cancer over the medium term. However, the 3-year follow-up period in our study likely represents an insufficient duration to fully capture the long-term trajectory of gastric cancer outcomes. Consequently, future studies with extended follow-up are essential to definitively confirm the long-term oncological equivalence of these surgical techniques.

Nevertheless, this study is subject to several limitations. First, its retrospective nature, despite employing propensity score matching (PSM) to mitigate confounding factors, could not eliminate the potential for selection bias. Second, the single-center design and limited cohort size may compromise the generalizability (external validity) of the findings. Consequently, future validation through multi-center randomized controlled trials (RCTs) is warranted to corroborate these results.

Based on the empirical insights from this study, we propose three recommendations for future research: Sample size calculations powered for rare complications (e.g., anastomotic leakage), Follow-up designs covering the 5-year survival plateau, Implementation of multi-center studies.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that TRTG is a safe and viable surgical approach that offers clinically meaningful advantages in key short-term recovery metrics compared to RATG, including reduced physiological stress, accelerated functional recovery, and comparable oncological safety. TRTG represents a technologically advanced option for radical D2 lymphadenectomy in total gastrectomy.
