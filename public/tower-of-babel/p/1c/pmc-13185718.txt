Advanced Stage Hodgkin Lymphoma: A Review on Frontline Treatment Advances
Authors: Kelsey Baron, Boyu Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Before 1970, Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) was an often-fatal lymphoma treated with radiation and/or single-agent chemotherapy. In contrast, HL is now a largely curable disease treated with multiagent chemotherapy regimens. In the intervening half-century, the goal of HL treatment has evolved from improving cure rates to developing novel treatment paradigms that maintain historical efficacy while minimizing the short- and long-term side effects of intensive chemo- and radiation therapies. The development of PET-adapted escalation and de-escalation strategies has played a critical role in balancing the desired efficacy with toxicity. More recently, novel therapies including brentuximab vedotin and nivolumab have resulted in dramatic improvements in outcomes and a necessary reduction in toxicity in both pediatric and adult patients. Future efforts to improve short- and long-term toxicities through novel therapies and collaborative efforts between adult and pediatric groups will be essential as we seek to improve outcomes in patients with advanced-stage HL.

Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) is a unique lymphoid neoplasm characterized by the presence of Hodgkin Reed-Sternberg cells within an inflammatory tumor microenvironment. With a bimodal age distribution, HL accounts for ∼10% of all newly diagnosed lymphomas within the United States1 and represents the most common lymphoma in adolescent and young adult (AYA) populations (15-39 y old).2 In the United States alone, there are over 8000 new cases per year with >1000 related deaths.1 Advanced-stage HL encompasses stage III and IV disease, defined by the presence of involved lymph nodes above and below the diaphragm and/or extranodal involvement as per the Ann Arbor staging criteria.3 Despite generally high cure rates for HL, historical outcomes for patients with advanced-stage HL were suboptimal, with 15% to 30% of patients failing to respond to or relapsing after frontline treatment.4 Over the last 50 years, significant advancements in the treatment of advanced-stage HL have steadily improved survival outcomes for these patients. Response-adapted approaches have balanced efficacy with toxicity, while novel immunotherapies and their combination treatments have improved outcomes in the frontline treatment setting and beyond. To better understand our current treatment landscape, we aim to review the historical evolution of HL treatment and the unique cellular and molecular features of HL, which underpin the scientific rationale of modern treatment approaches for advanced-stage HL.

HISTORICAL TREATMENT OF ADVANCED-STAGE HL IN ADULTS

Modern day HL treatment has evolved from 2 doxorubicin-based regimens, ABVD (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) and escalated (e) BEACOPP (bleomycin, etoposide, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, procarbazine, prednisone). Developed by the Milan Cancer Institute, ABVD became the preferred backbone of HL treatment in North America in the 1990s after demonstrating improved efficacy and decreased myelo- and gonado-toxicity compared with MOPP (mechlorethamine, vincristine, procarbazine, prednisone), the standard of care at that time.5–7 In a phase III trial conducted by the Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB), 5-year failure-free survival in patients 16 years and older treated with ABVD versus alternating MOPP/ABVD versus MOPP was 61%, 65%, and 50%, respectively. ABVD alone was associated with decreased myelotoxicity and dose reductions compared with the MOPP-containing regimens, positioning ABVD as the new standard of care.8

Around the same time, the German Hodgkin Study Group (GHSG) developed eBEACOPP based on the notion that increased doses of standard chemotherapy given over a shorter period of time would improve survival outcomes.9 The GHSG HD9 clinical trial served to test this hypothesis by randomizing patients to 8 cycles of ABVD alternating with COPP (ABVD/COPP) or eBEACOPP followed by radiotherapy (XRT) to sites of initial bulk or posttreatment residual disease, which was the standard approach at the time.10,11 Compared with ABVD/COPP, eBEACOPP significantly improved freedom-from-treatment-failure, early progression, and overall survival (OS), making it the first regimen to show an OS advantage over an ABVD-based regimen after more than a decade of randomized clinical trials.12–17 While these findings were significant, the survival benefit of eBEACOPP was lost in patients 60 to 65 years old, limiting the utility of this regimen in older HL patients due to the severe toxicities experienced in this age group. Of note, eBEACOPP was associated with increased toxicity compared with ABVD, including mucositis, grade 4 cytopenias, infections, and infertility. Most concerning, the cumulative 10-year incidence of secondary acute myeloid leukemia/myelodysplastic syndrome (sAML/MDS) was 3.2% for eBEACOPP versus 0.4% in those patients treated with ABVD/COPP (P=0.03). Nevertheless, HD9 pushed the boundaries of dose modifications and intensifications while highlighting the potential for increased toxicity, paving the way for future de-escalation and PET-adapted treatment approaches.

In the years that followed, multiple studies contributed to the growing pool of evidence that eBEACOPP resulted in improved tumor control over ABVD, with ~10% to 15% progression-free survival (PFS) advantage across trials. However, this was at the cost of high rates of short- and long-term toxicity, including cytopenias, infection, infertility, and secondary malignancies in up to 7% of patients. Furthermore, eBEACOPP never demonstrated superior OS compared with ABVD, likely owing to the ability to successfully salvage patients after relapse from ABVD and increased mortality from toxic effects and secondary malignancies associated with eBEACOPP.18–22 As a result, ABVD remained the preferred standard in North America and in many countries outside of Europe. To reconcile this discrepancy, investigators sought reliable methods of treatment escalation and de-escalation. Risk-adapted approaches utilizing a PET scan after an initial 2 cycles of chemotherapy (PET2) were designed to differentiate high-risk patients that would benefit from the enhanced tumor control provided by eBEACOPP from patients in whom a cure was feasible with ABVD or de-escalation of eBEACOPP, thus minimizing overtreatment and toxicity. The predictive value of a negative PET2 (PET2−) was based on prior studies in which patients with a complete remission on PET2 had a significantly improved 2-year PFS of 95% compared with 0% to 12.8% in patients with a positive interim PET2 (PET2+).23,24

Notable PET2-adapted clinical trials for advanced-stage HL are shown in Table 1. Two trials supported the de-escalation of therapy in PET2− advanced-stage HL patients who were treated with an initial 2 cycles of eBEACOPP.25–27 The GHSG HD18 trial demonstrated noninferior PFS, decreased toxicity, and a significant OS benefit in PET2− patients treated with a total of 4 cycles of eBEACOPP compared with the previous standard of 6 to 8 cycles.25,26 The Lymphoma Study Association AHL2011 trial demonstrated noninferiority and decreased toxicity of a PET-adapted treatment approach compared with 6 cycles of eBEACOPP without interval PET. In the AHL2011 study, patients who were PET2− after an initial 2 cycles of eBEACOPP went on to receive 4 cycles of ABVD, whereas those who were PET2+ received an additional 4 cycles of eBEACOPP.27 Both studies confirmed that the implementation of a risk-adapted treatment strategy through interim PET response could be successful.

Other PET-adapted trials for advanced-stage HL utilized upfront ABVD, reflecting the lack of universal support of frontline eBEACOPP advocated by the GHSG. Both the Southwest Oncology Group (SWOG) in the United States and the Italian Gruppo Italiano Terapie Innovative nei Linfomi/Fondazione Italiana Linfomi (GITIL/FIL) conducted trials in which patients received an initial 2 cycles of ABVD followed by interim PET2.28–31 PET2+ patients received 6 additional cycles of eBEACOPP, while PET2− patients continued with 4 additional cycles of ABVD. The 3-year PFS for the PET2+ patients in the SWOG study was 60%,28,29 and the 5-year PFS in the Italian study was 66%.30,31 These results were dramatic improvements compared with a 2-year PFS of 0% to 12% observed in PET2+ patients who continued on ABVD,23,24 suggesting that the majority of PET2+ patients could be successfully salvaged from their poor prognosis by an escalation in treatment. Perhaps most impactful among the PET-adapted trials was the UK-based RATHL (Response Adapted Therapy for Hodgkin Lymphoma) study.32,33 The design of this study was additionally informed by the morbidity and mortality of bleomycin pulmonary toxicity, which is observed in approximately a quarter of HL patients and disproportionately affects patients aged above 40 years old.34,35 RATHL randomized PET2− patients who were treated with an initial 2 cycles of ABVD to 4 additional cycles of either ABVD or AVD. Omission of bleomycin in the PET2− cohorts did not impact PFS or OS and was associated with significantly less bleomycin pulmonary toxicity. PET2+ patients had an escalation of their therapy to 4 additional cycles of eBEACOPP, resulting in a 3-year PFS of 67.5%, comparable to the SWOG and GITIL/FIL studies.

While comparison across these studies is limited due to varying study designs and response definitions, they demonstrated that a PET-adapted approach could successfully predict which patients would benefit from escalation versus de-escalation of therapy. The success of response-adapted strategies, coupled with the lack of an OS benefit with eBEACOPP over ABVD, positioned ABVD-based approaches, including RATHL, as the favored treatment approach for adult patients in the majority of the world.

HISTORICAL TREATMENT OF PEDIATRIC HL

Similar to adult HL treatment efforts, pediatric HL trials have focused on balancing efficacy with long-term side effects through response-adapted protocols (Table 2). These efforts were particularly important in pediatric patients, given their expected life span of 50 to 70 years beyond their HL diagnosis and treatments. To achieve this balance, dose-dense regimens incorporating etoposide and cyclophosphamide were utilized with the goal of decreasing cumulative doses of toxic chemotherapy by enabling early response and subsequent de-escalation of therapy. Priority was placed on reducing exposure to alkylating agents and doxorubicin, given the associated risk of secondary malignancies and gonadal- and cardio-toxicity.36–38 Minimizing XRT-induced toxicity was also a central aim of these trials. Pediatric protocols at the time included XRT to all involved sites at diagnosis, resulting in extensive radiation exposure. Compared with the general population, pediatric HL patients had a 75-fold increased risk of breast cancer, which was independently associated with increased XRT doses.36

While the Ann Arbor staging system is also used to define advanced-stage disease in pediatric patients, HL risk stratification differs from adults, lacks uniformity across study groups, and is divided into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk. Importantly, both early-stage and advanced-stage HL can be deemed intermediate and high-risk diseases. In many pediatric studies that inform the treatment of advanced-stage disease, both intermediate and high-risk patients are included.

In North America and Europe, parallel studies demonstrated the feasibility of response-adapted protocols in reducing XRT exposure.39–41 In AHOD00031, conducted by The Children’s Oncology Group (COG), patients with a rapid early response (defined as PET2−) after induction with 2 cycles of ABVE-PC (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vincristine, etoposide, prednisone, cyclophosphamide) were treated with 2 additional cycles of ABVE-PC. Slow early responding (defined as PET2+) patients received an additional 2 cycles of ABVE-PC versus 2 cycles of DECA (dexamethasone, etoposide, cisplatin, cytarabine) followed by 2 cycles of ABVE-PC. Rapid early responders who remained in a complete response (CR) at the end of treatment were randomized to involved-field radiotherapy (IFRT) of all previously involved sites versus no further treatment. Omission of XRT in this cohort did not impact 4-year event-free survival (EFS; 87.9% vs. 84.3%), supporting the use of PET-guided radiation therapy.39 AHOD0831 used a similar design but instead utilized vinorelbine/ifosfamide in addition to ABVE-PC for slow early responders and limited consolidative IFRT to sites of initial bulk in rapid early responders. Five-year EFS was 83.5% in rapid early responders and 73.2% in slow early responders, further demonstrating the predictive value of interim response and suggesting that IFRT volumes could be safely reduced.40 In Europe, the European Network for Pediatric Hodgkin Lymphoma conducted the EuroNET-PHL-C1 trial. Patients received induction with OEPA (vincristine, etoposide, prednisone, and doxorubicin) followed by COPP versus COPDAC (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, dacarbazine). Patients with an inadequate response to induction OEPA received IFRT to all involved sites, whereas radiation was omitted in patients with an adequate response to OEPA. Survival outcomes between patients treated with chemotherapy only and chemotherapy plus IFRT were comparable (5 y EFS 90.1% vs. 87.1%), further supporting the use of PET-guided omission of consolidative IFRT.41

Response-adapted trials successfully lead to a substantial reduction in radiation therapy exposure, effectively changing the treatment landscape for pediatric patients. However, these studies also highlighted that despite parallel efforts to optimize treatment of HL between adult and pediatric groups, the difference in adult and pediatric therapies was based on tolerance and toxicities, rather than inherent differences in disease biology. AYA patients were candidates for both pediatric and adult regimens, with the choice of therapy often coming down to where the patient first sought care (pediatric vs. adult institutions). This emphasized the need for future collaborative efforts between adult and pediatric study groups.

NOVEL THERAPIES

Brentuximab Vedotin

CD30 is a transmembrane protein of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily variably expressed on T, B, and NK cells and universally expressed in HRS cells. Binding of CD30 leads to downstream activation of NFκ-B, resulting in T-cell stimulation and B-cell proliferation and immunoglobulin production.42 Successful targeting of CD30 within HL has been led by the antibody-drug conjugate therapy, brentuximab vedotin (BV). BV is an anti-CD30 monoclonal antibody (mAb) conjugated to the microtubule-disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E. Upon binding to CD30 on Hodgkin Reed-Sternberg cells, BV is internalized, and monomethyl auristatin E is cleaved from the mAb by lysosomal proteases. Monomethyl auristatin E binds and disrupts microtubules, leading to cellular arrest and apoptosis of the malignant cell. Early studies of BV led to its approval in 2011 for the treatment of R/R HL after autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) and in 2015 as maintenance therapy after ASCT.43–45 Subsequent studies of BV demonstrated its efficacy in both the salvage setting and the frontline setting when combined with chemotherapy.46–51

Novel Regimens Utilizing BV in Adults

Trials incorporating novel immunotherapies are summarized in Table 3. The phase III ECHELON-1 study was conducted to compare BV-AVD to ABVD in newly diagnosed, previously untreated, adult patients with advanced-stage HL.52–54 After 5 years of follow-up, BV-AVD significantly improved modified PFS (82.2% vs. 75.3%) and OS with a 41% reduction in risk of death (6 y OS 93.9% vs. 89.4%, HR 0.59; 95% CI: 0.40-0.88, P=0.009), marking BV-AVD as the first regimen since eBEACOPP to demonstrate an OS advantage over ABVD. Neutropenia and febrile neutropenia were more common with BV-AVD, especially among patients aged above 60 years old (febrile neutropenia 37% vs. 17%). A protocol amendment partway through the study necessitated granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (GCSF) support for all patients treated with BV-AVD, resulting in a more acceptable rate of febrile neutropenia (11% with GCSF vs. 21% without GCSF) with rates more closely resembling the ABVD cohort. Peripheral neuropathy (PN) was also more common with BV-AVD (67% vs. 43%). While most patients with PN reported resolution (71.85%) or improvement (13.8%) in extended follow-up, neuropathy persisted in 19% versus 9% of patients with PN treated with BV-AVD versus ABVD, respectively, after 6 years. The survival advantage and relatively manageable side effect profile of BV-AVD established this regimen as a new standard of care for advanced-stage HL in younger, adult patients outside of Europe.

The GHSG investigated BV in patients aged above 60 years old with stage IIB, III, and IV HL in the HD21 trial comparing eBEACOPP to BrECADD (BV, etoposide, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, dacarbazine, and dexamethasone) with the aim of maintaining efficacy while decreasing toxicity by omitting vincristine and exchanging bleomycin for BV, procarbazine for dacarbazine, and prednisone for a shorter course of dexamethasone.55 Interim PET2 guided the duration of treatment, building upon the previous results from HD18, in which PET2− and PET2+ patients received a total of 4 and 6 cycles of the assigned treatment, respectively.25,26 BrECADD significantly improved 4-year PFS (94.3% vs. 90.9%, HR: 0.66, P=0.035) and resulted in significantly less treatment-related morbidity, a finding largely driven by decreased grade ≥3 anemia (72% vs. 55%) and thrombocytopenia (59% vs. 30%), leading to fewer dose reductions with BrECADD. Despite this, the rate of febrile neutropenia was higher with BrECADD (28% vs. 21%). PET2 remained predictive of PFS in patients treated with BrECADD with an unprecedented 4-year PFS of 96.8% in PET2− and 90.3% in PET2+ patients. Long-term toxicities, including gonadal toxicity as measured by recovery of follicle-stimulating hormone and number of births by patients or partners of patients, and incidence of sAML/MDS, were lower with BrECADD as compared with eBEACOPP, mostly owing to the substitution of dacarbazine in BrECADD for the procarbazine in eBEACOPP.56 The results of HD21 established a new European standard for younger patients with advanced-stage HL; however, applicability in North America is limited due to the lack of utilization of BEACOPP-based regimens at North American institutions.

Novel Regimens Utilizing BV in Pediatrics

The COG led AHOD1331 trial similarly sought to improve upon outcomes in high-risk patients treated with ABVE-PC by exchanging bleomycin for BV and further minimizing radiation exposure.57 Patients enrolled in this randomized phase III study were assigned to BV-AVE-PC or standard ABVE-PC treatment (Table 3). IFRT was administered to all sites of initial bulk, areas of disease with a Deauville score of 4 to 5 after PET2, and residual disease at the end of treatment. BV-ABVE-PC resulted in improved 3-year EFS over ABVE-PC (92.1% vs. 82.5%). A little over half (53%) of patients received radiotherapy, which compared favorably to prior PET-adapted trials in which 60% to 76% of patients required treatment with XRT.39–41 Adverse events, including febrile neutropenia and PN, were comparable between the 2 treatment groups, with PN being reported less frequently than in adult BV trials and in only 19% of the BV-AVE-PC cohort. Importantly, a post hoc analysis revealed a benefit in adding BV for all prespecified subgroups except for those patients with stage IVB disease for unclear reasons. While this study demonstrated improved efficacy with BV, over half the patients still required XRT, and stage IVB patients saw no added benefit, collectively highlighting the need for ongoing improvement in frontline treatment.

PDL1 Blockade

Hodgkin lymphoma is characterized by relatively few Hodgkin Reed-Sternberg cells surrounded by an inflammatory tumor microenvironment composed of various immune cells, such as cytotoxic T-cells that comprise the bulk of the tumor mass. Critical to the survival of the Hodgkin Reed-Sternberg cell is the overexpression of programmed cell death ligand 1 and 2 (PD-L1, PD-L2), which bind the programmed cell death receptor on T-cells, resulting in T-cell inhibition and immune evasion.58 PD-L1 expression is more common in advanced-stage HL and likely contributes to tumor spread, providing the biological rationale for PD-1 blockade in advanced-stage HL.58–60 Further informing immunotherapy regimens in advanced-stage HL is the success of PD-1 blockade in combination with chemotherapy in solid tumors. Preclinical models have shown that chemotherapy-induced tumor cell death is a complex mechanism that stimulates the immune system to recognize the malignant cells, depletes tumor-supporting immune cells, and promotes cytotoxic T-cell expansion. Immunotherapy synergizes with chemotherapy to maintain this activated immune response, resulting in improved outcomes in solid tumors such as non-small cell lung cancer.61

The efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy given concurrently with chemotherapy for previously untreated advanced-stage HL was initially demonstrated in the phase II CHECKMATE 205 study,62 in which 51 patients were treated with nivolumab (N) monotherapy followed by N-AVD, resulting in an ORR of 84%, including responses in all 6 patients aged above 60 years old, and a 9-month PFS of 92%.

On the basis of these findings, a collaborative effort between COG and the adult NCTN groups, led by SWOG, resulted in the development of the randomized phase III S1826 clinical trial assessing the efficacy of N-AVD for the frontline treatment of advanced-stage HL.63 Patients aged 12 years and older throughout the United States and Canada were randomized to receive 6 cycles of BV-AVD versus N-AVD. After a median of 2.1 years of follow-up, N-AVD resulted in improved 2-year PFS (92% vs. 83%) and exhibited an improved side effect profile. Nivolumab was discontinued less frequently than BV (9.4% vs. 22%), and fewer patients treated with N-AVD discontinued all treatment early. In subgroup analysis, PFS was improved in all age groups who were treated with N-AVD, including those patients aged less than 18 and above 60 years old. In a subset analysis of the 99 patients aged 60 years or older, 2-year PFS and OS were significantly improved in the N-AVD cohort (2 y PFS: 89% vs. 64%, HR: 0.24, P=0.001; 2-year OS 96% vs. 85%, HR: 0.16, P=0.005), mostly due to the 10% of on-therapy deaths in the above 60-year-old patients treated in the BV-AVD arm.64 This was an important finding as patients aged 60 years or older did not derive any OS benefit from BV in the ECHELON-1 trial and had more febrile neutropenia, limiting the applicability of BV-AVD in this patient population.52–54 The findings from this subset analysis marked N-AVD as the first regimen to significantly improve OS in patients aged 60 years or older and established N-AVD as the new treatment standard in this older advanced-stage HL population.

While the overall percentage of adverse events was equal across cohorts, the majority of adverse events were more common with BV-AVD. Among these, peripheral sensory neuropathy was reported in 59% of patients versus 29% treated with N-AVD (grade 3: 8% vs. 1%). Conversely, neutropenia was more common with N-AVD (56% vs. 34%) due to the study-mandated use of G-CSF for all patients in the BV-AVD arm. Despite the higher rates of neutropenia associated with N-AVD, the rates of febrile neutropenia and infection were similar between the cohorts and were even more frequent in patients aged above 60 years old who received BV-AVD. Except for thyroid dysfunction, the incidence of immune-related adverse events was not increased with N-AVD, an important consideration given the known associated risk of checkpoint inhibitor-induced autoimmune-like complications.

Finally, S1826 is the only contemporary trial in advanced-stage HL to include patients with HIV (n=16), allowing for formal, prospective documentation of feasibility and safety in a historically underrepresented patient population. Along the same lines, S1826 enrolled a racially and ethnically diverse patient population with 12% Black, 13% Hispanic, and a small portion of Native American patients, reflecting the racial distribution of HL incidence in North America.63

While direct comparison of S1826 to HD21 is limited, a few key differences should be noted. Unlike S1826, HD21 excluded patients with HIV and enrolled only 2 Black patients.55 HD21 limited enrollment to patients 18 to 60 years as opposed to S1826, which enrolled patients 12 years and older with nearly 10% of the study population ≥60 years. Lastly, consolidative radiation was utilized in 17% of patients in HD21 compared with <1% in S1826.

The improved efficacy and side effect profile of N-AVD over BV-AVD has established it as the preferred treatment for adolescent, adult, and elderly patients with AS HL in North America. The unique collaboration between adult NCTN and COG resulted in rapid accrual of the study, effectively allowing for earlier access to immunotherapy in pediatric patients. This unprecedented joint effort has set a new standard for all future HL studies.

CONCLUSIONS

The treatment landscape for advanced-stage HL has dramatically evolved over the last 5 decades. The use of interim PET-adapted escalation and de-escalation treatment strategies and the more recent integration of novel immunotherapies have led to unprecedented survival outcomes while limiting treatment-related toxicities, including the omission of radiotherapy for AYA patients. N-AVD treatment is now the North American standard for advanced-stage HL patients aged 12 years or older, with the S1826 study serving as a model for future successful collaborative efforts between pediatric and adult groups. Moving forward, innovation in novel therapies, collaborative efforts, and a focus on reducing long-term toxicity and improving quality of life will be imperative to discovering new treatment paradigms in advanced-stage HL.
