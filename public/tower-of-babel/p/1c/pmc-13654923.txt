Inhibition of DLX6 sensitizes lung adenocarcinoma to cisplatin via GPX4-dependent ferroptosis
Authors: Zedong Sun, Minmin Zhou, Can Wang, Mingrui Liu, Ningdan Song, Simian He, Feifan Ji, Jiayu Chen, Lin Xu, Xiufen Zheng, Binhui Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with limited treatment options due to inherent chemotherapy resistance. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, has recently emerged as a promising strategy to overcome this resistance. Inducing ferroptosis may sensitize LUAD cells to chemotherapy, but its molecular regulators and therapeutic potential remain largely unexplored. This study aims to investigate the role of Distal-less homeobox 6 (DLX6) in regulating ferroptosis and its contribution to LUAD progression.MethodsTo elucidate the function of DLX6 in LUAD, we employed a multi-omics approach, integrating TCGA data analysis, in vitro cell culture models, and in vivo xenograft experiments. Transcriptome sequencing, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays, and dual-luciferase reporter assays were utilized to uncover the underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsWe found that DLX6 is significantly upregulated in LUAD and associated with poor patient prognosis. Functional studies showed that silencing DLX6 suppressed cell proliferation, triggered ferroptosis, and sensitized LUAD cells to chemotherapy. Mechanistically, DLX6 acted as a transcriptional activator of Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a central regulator of ferroptosis, by directly binding to its promoter region. Inhibition of DLX6 triggered ferroptosis in LUAD cells, enhancing treatment sensitivity to cisplatin.ConclusionsOur findings highlight DLX6 as a key regulator of ferroptosis and cisplatin sensitivity in LUAD. By promoting GPX4 expression and inhibiting ferroptosis, DLX6 plays a crucial role in LUAD progression. This study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms driving LUAD and offers a potential therapeutic target for sensitizing cisplatin treatment and improving chemotherapy resistance.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13402-026-01263-w.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. LUAD is the most common histological subtype, accounting for approximately 40% of all lung cancers [2]. Despite significant advancements in diagnosing and treating LUAD, overcoming its resistance to therapy remains a major challenge [3]. Chemotherapy, one of the most widely used cancer treatments, has shown effectiveness but is hindered by the development of resistance in tumor cells [4]. Apoptosis induction has been a primary strategy in cancer treatment [5]. However, tumor cells often develop resistance to apoptosis, limiting the efficacy of apoptosis-based therapies [6]. Therefore, exploring alternative non-apoptotic pathways such as ferroptosis [7–9], necroptosis [10], and autophagy [11] is crucial for the development of new therapeutic strategies to effectively eliminate tumors [12].

Ferroptosis, a recently identified form of programmed cell death, is iron-dependent and distinct from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy [13]. Preclinical studies have indicated that targeting ferroptosis could serve as a promising approach to treat lung cancer [14]. Inducing ferroptosis in LUAD has been shown to inhibit tumor progression both in vitro and in vivo [15]. The primary mechanism driving ferroptosis involves the inhibition of GPX4 activity, leading to depletion of glutathione (GSH) and subsequent lipid peroxidation [16]. GPX4 functions both as a structural protein and an antioxidant, effectively preventing lipid oxidation. In recent years, GPX4 has emerged as a critical regulator of ferroptosis, influencing lipid and amino acid metabolism and playing key roles in cellular aging, oncogenesis, and cell death [17]. Increasing evidence suggests that targeting GPX4-mediated ferroptosis holds significant therapeutic potential, particularly in cancer treatment [16]. Ferroptosis is closely associated with amino acid, iron, and lipid metabolism, as well as with GSH and reactive oxygen species (ROS) biosynthesis. Additionally, initial studies have shown that ferroptosis is characterized by abnormal mitochondrial morphology, including reduced size, increased membrane density, and diminished cristae [18].

DLX6 belongs to the homeobox gene family of transcription factors, and the DLX gene family plays a crucial role in embryonic development and cellular differentiation [19]. Studies had confirmed the presence of disturbed DLX gene expression in tumors and its association with tumor progression [20, 21]. Recent studies have suggested that DLX6 acts as an oncogene in glioma and oral squamous carcinoma, with DLX6 knockdown inducing cell death [22]. Meanwhile, a genome-wide analysis of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has also identified DLX6 as a potential oncogenic transcription factor [23]. However, whether DLX6 influences cell death in LUAD remains unexplored.

In this study, we reveal that knockdown of DLX6 enhances sensitivity to ferroptosis in LUAD. Bioinformatics analysis indicates significant alterations in GSH-related metabolism and cellular lipid ROS production in DLX6-knockdown cells. The results revealed that the DLX6 knockdown contributes to increased cell death and lipid peroxidation, subsequently inducing ferroptosis in LUAD cells. Acting as a transcription factor, we demonstrated that DLX6 transcriptionally activates GPX4, which attenuates lipid peroxidation and thereby suppresses ferroptosis. Moreover, inhibition of DLX6 significantly enhanced sensitivity to cisplatin both in vitro and in vivo by triggering ferroptosis. Importantly, we elucidated the underlying mechanism through which DLX6 governed ferroptosis to promote resistance against tumor chemotherapy. Our results highlight ferroptosis as a potential new therapeutic strategy for lung cancer and emphasize the therapeutic value of DLX6 inhibition in overcoming chemotherapy resistance.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Lung adenocarcinoma cells (PC9 and A549), human embryonic kidney 293T cells (293T), and human bronchial epithelial cells (HBE) were purchased from the Shanghai Institutes for Biological Science, China. PC9 and A549 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (KeyGene, Nanjing, China), HBE and 293T cells were cultured in DMEM medium (KeyGene, Nanjing, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Animal studies

Balb/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of China Pharmaceutical University (Nanjing, China) under protocol number IACUC-202,307,012. No preference for mouse sex was given in any of the studies. DLX6-sh PC9 cells (5 × 106) and control cells were subcutaneously injected into 24 nude mice, respectively. On day 10 after injection and when palpable tumors were present, the control group or DLX6-sh group mice were divided into two groups randomly by weight. These tumor-bearing mice were intraperitoneally injected with either cisplatin (5 mg/kg) or saline every 4 days. Tumor growth was monitored every 7 days and the mice were culled after 26 days.

Reagents and antibodies

Erastin (S7242), Ferrostatin-1 (S7243), Z-VAD-FMK (S7023), Necrostatin-1 (S8037), Chloroquine (S4157), and Cisplatin (S1166) were obtained from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX). NAC (S0077), GSH (S0073), and CCK-8 Assay Kit (C0037) were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, PR China). The Cell Apoptosis detection kit and ROS probe were purchased from KeyGene (Nanjing, China). The Liperfluo probe was obtained from DoJinDo (Japan). Anti-DLX6 (ab137079, 1:1000), anti-GPX4 (ab125066, 1:2000), anti-xCT (ab307601, 1:1000), anti-TFRC (ab214039, 1:1000), anti-GAPDH (ab8245, 1:10000), and anti-Ki67 (ab15580, 1:10000) and anti-4-HNE (ab46545, 1:100) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Anti-SLC1A5 (20350-1-A, 1:2000) was purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Cell proliferation assays

All the cell proliferation assays were performed 24 h after transfection. For the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells/100 µL and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured every 24 h. For the Real-Time xCELLigence analysis system (RTCA), 5000 cells/100 µL were seeded in E-plates, and the plates were locked into the RTCA DP device in the incubator to calculate the “cell index” value. For the 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay (EdU Apollo® 488 In Vitro Imaging Kit, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China), 10,000 cells/100 µL were plated in 96-well plates. Then the cells were incubated with 100 µL 50 µM EdU solution for 2 h and fixed with 50 µL 4% paraformaldehyde. 50 µL 2 mg/mL glycine was added to neutralize paraformaldehyde and the cells were washed with 100 µL 0.5% TritonX-100. After that, the cells were stained with 100 µL 1 × Apollo solution and 100 µL 1 × Hoechst 33,342 solution. After being washed with 100 µL PBS 3 times, images were obtained from a fluorescence microscope to further calculate proliferation rates. EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) incorporation assay was used to test the cell proliferation, which was performed with the BeyoClick EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) by the manufacturer’s instruction. Representative images were viewed and captured under a confocal microscope.

Analysis of cell death

The cell death ratio was analyzed using the propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (KeyGene, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1–5 × 105 cells were stained with PI for 10 min at room temperature and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. The apoptosis ratio was analyzed using the Annexin V fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) / propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (KeyGene, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1–5 × 105 cells were stained with Annexin V and PI for 10 min at room temperature and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. Experiments were repeated in triplicate.

MDA, GSH, GSH/GSSG, glutamate and glutamine assay

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the relative MDA concentration in cell or tumor lysates was assessed using a Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The MDA-TBA adduct can be quantified colorimetrically (OD = 532 nm). Glutathione redox state was measured by total and oxidized glutathione (GSH and GSSG) respectively using a commercially available kit (BC1170 and BC1185 Solarbio, China). The ratio of GSH/GSSG was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions (S0053, Beyotime, Shanghai, PR China). Glutamine and Glutamate kits purchased from Mlbio (ml403690 and ml076519, Shanghai).

Fluorescent probes staining

An equal number of cells were treated as designed and stained with the fluorescent probe of DHE-ROS, Liperfluo, which were used for detecting the level of cellular ROS and lipid peroxides, respectively. After incubation for 30 min at 37℃, cells were washed three times with HBSS and subsequently detected by flow cytometry.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

After the indicated treatments, the cells were fixed by 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated by a graded series of ethanol and eventually transferred to absolute acetone. After infiltration with absolute acetone and the final spurr resin mixture, the samples were embedded, ultrathin sectioned, and stained. Finally, the samples were observed in the Hitachi Model H-7650 TEM.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The concentration and quality of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop one spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An amount of 1 µg of RNA was used to synthesize cDNA using the RT reagent Kit gDNA Eraser (RR047A, Takara, Shiga, Japan). The corresponding cDNAs were subjected to quantitative PCR analysis using SYBR Green (RR820A, Takara). The used primers for qRT-PCR assays are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Western blotting

Cultured cells were lysed with lysis buffer (RIPA, KeyGene) containing protease inhibitors (PMSF, KeyGene) on ice, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA Kit (KeyGene). Western blotting was obtained utilizing 20–40 µg of lysate protein. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat: 3700; 1:1000), anti-DLX6 (Abcam; Cat: sc515294; 1:1000).

siRNA and plasmid construction and cell transfection

The siRNAs targeting DLX6 and GPX4 were constructed and purchased from KeyGene, China. The full-length cDNA of human DLX6 and GPX4 plasmids was prepared by cloning into the expression vector pENTER (KeyGene, Nanjing, China). The siRNAs were transfected into LUAD cells using Imax (Invitrogen), and the plasmid vectors were transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA, and shRNA sequences are shown in Supplemental Table S2.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

A549 cells were collected and incubated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature for crosslinking, followed by incubation with 10 ⋅ glycine for 5 min. After washing with PBS, precooled PBS was added to the cells, and the cells were scraped into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged for 10 min at 4 °C. Next, the cells were incubated with 500 µL of cell lysis buffer (containing 2.5 µL cocktail) on ice for 15 min. After centrifugation, the cell precipitates were resuspended in 500 µL of nucleus lysis buffer (containing 2.5 µL cocktail) and sheared by sonication. The 10 µg anti-DLX6 antibody or 5 µg mouse IgG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; cat: sc-2025) was added to 100 µL dilution buffer and 20 µL protein A/G Plus-agarose beads, followed by incubation on a rotating device at room temperature for 30 min. For immunoprecipitation, chromatin was added to the above bead-antibody complex and incubated at 4 °C overnight. For DNA purification, immunoprecipitation products were added to the ChIP elution buffer (containing proteinase K), followed by heating at 62 °C for 2 h and 95 °C for 10 min. Beads were wiped, and the supernatant was transferred into a new tube. The supernatant was added to 100 µL of Bind reagent A and placed on an absorption column to centrifuge at 12,000 × g for 30 s. Then, the column was washed with 500 µL of Elution buffer C. ChIP DNA samples were subjected to PCR amplification with primers specific to the GPX4 promoter region. The primer sequences are shown in Supplemental Table S1, and GAPDH was used as a control.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

Human GXP4 WT promotor fragments were synthesized by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China). The KODPlusMutagenesis Kit (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) was used to mutate the binding sites and the mutation was confirmed by DNA sequencing. The Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was used to measure luciferase reporter activity.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between groups were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test and paired t-test. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences were analyzed with the log-rank test. All the performed statistical analyses are described in each figure legend. Statistical p-values were obtained by application of the appropriate statistical tests using the GraphPad Prism 9. For all tests, p < 0.05 was considered significant (ns: not significant, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

DLX6 overexpression contributed to LUAD progression and poor prognosis in patients

We were intrigued by the potential association between DLX6 expression and various molecular biological characteristics of cancer, as well as its potential as a prognostic marker for tumor progression. Through pan-cancer analysis of the TCGA database, we observed that DLX6 was overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to paired adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, we found a marked reduction of DLX6 expression in normal tissues, whereas LUAD tissues exhibited a significant upregulation of DLX6 expression (Fig. 1B). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a strong correlation between high DLX6 expression levels and poorer overall survival rates in LUAD patients (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these findings suggest that DLX6 may play a pivotal role in the progression of LUAD.

Given the differential expression of DLX6 across LUAD cell lines and the inherent heterogeneity of EGFR mutations in LUAD, we selected A549 and PC9 cell lines for further investigation [24]. We utilized two siRNAs and an overexpression plasmid to knock down or overexpress DLX6 in LUAD cell lines (A549 and PC9). The knockdown of DLX6 significantly suppressed cell proliferation, as confirmed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8), Real-Time xCELLigence Analysis system (RTCA), and 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays (Fig. 1D-H). Conversely, overexpression of DLX6 promoted cell proliferation in both A549 and PC9 cells compared to the vector control group (Supplemental Fig. 1A-C). Additionally, fluorescence-based mitochondrial staining with PI and JC-1 showed that DLX6 knockdown not only inhibited cell proliferation but also induced cell death (Fig. 1I-N). Taken together, our findings underscore the critical role of DLX6 in the proliferation and death of LUAD cells.

DLX6 deletion-induced lipid peroxidation promoted cell death in LUAD

The functional role of DLX6 in LUAD remains unclear, prompting an investigation using si-DLX6 RNA-seq analysis to predict its involvement in PC9 cells. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were predominantly associated with the “regulation of cell proliferation”, “cellular lipid metabolic processes”, and “regulation of cholesterol biosynthetic processes” (Fig. 2A). Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified significant alterations in metabolites related to “alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism” and “D-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism” (Fig. 2B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further revealed a marked increase in pathways related to “Biological oxidations”, “cellular amino acid catabolic process”, “reactive oxygen spcies biosynthetic” and “glutamate metabolic process” (Fig. 2C and Supplemental Fig. 1D-F). Notably, several of these amino acids are key precursors for the synthesis of glutathione (GSH), a major intracellular antioxidant. Therefore, the knockdown of DLX6 may be strongly correlated with enhanced sensitivity to amino acid metabolism in LUAD cells.

To further explore this hypothesis, we measured GSH levels, the GSH/GSSG ratio, as well as glutamate and glutamine levels in A549 and PC9 cells. The results indicated that DLX6 knockdown led to a reduction in both GSH levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 2D-G), accompanied by a toxic accumulation of glutamate and glutamine (Supplemental Fig. 1G-H). Additionally, the ROS levels were assessed using a ROS-DHE probe, and the findings demonstrated a significant increase in ROS following DLX6 knockdown (Fig. 2H-K). These results indicate that DLX6 knockdown is closely associated with lipid peroxidation in LUAD cells, likely due to the depletion of GSH, which subsequently triggers compensatory shifts in glutamine metabolism. This metabolic reprogramming may contribute to the cell death observed following DLX6 knockdown.

DLX6 inhibition induced ferroptosis in LUAD cells through GPX4-mediated lipid peroxidation

Lipid peroxidation is a hallmark of ferroptosis [25]. To explore the relationship between DLX6 and ferroptosis in LUAD, we examined the impact of DLX6 knockdown on ferroptosis in LUAD cells following treatment with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that DLX6 knockdown significantly promoted ferroptosis induced by Erastin in both A549 and PC9 cells (Fig. 3A-C). Additionally, we assessed the potential for rescuing Erastin-induced cell death in DLX6-knockdown cells using various programmed cell death inhibitors. The results indicated that cell death was only rescued by the ferroptosis inhibitor FER-1, while inhibitors of apoptosis (Z-VAD-FMK), necroptosis (Necrostatin-1), and autophagy (chloroquine) had no protective effect (Fig. 3D-E). These findings suggest that the cell death caused by DLX6 knockdown is primarily due to ferroptosis, rather than other forms of programmed cell death.

Next, we utilized the Liperfluo probe to detect oxidized lipids and further analyzed the mean fluorescence intensity by flow cytometry. The results showed that DLX6 knockdown increased the fluorescence of Liperfluo in A549 and PC9 cells treated with the ferroptosis inducers RSL3 and Erastin, whereas DLX6 overexpression reduced lipid peroxidation compared to the vector group (Fig. 3F-G and Supplemental Fig. 1I-J). Furthermore, DLX6 knockdown led to an increased concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA), a byproduct of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 3H). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that DLX6 knockdown induced characteristic ferroptosis-like features, such as mitochondrial crumpling and cristae reduction, in PC9 cells (Fig. 3I). DLX6 knockdown also exacerbated Erastin-induced mitochondrial damage. Collectively, these data demonstrate that DLX6 inhibits ferroptosis in LUAD cells, and its inhibition enhances cell sensitivity to ferroptosis by promoting lipid peroxidation.

Interestingly, we observed that DLX6 knockdown significantly increased the lipid peroxidation response upon Erastin treatment, compared to control cells. In contrast, cells treated with RSL3 were less sensitive to ferroptosis than those treated with Erastin. Both Erastin and RSL3 can induce ferroptosis, but via distinct mechanisms. RSL3 directly interacts with GPX4 and inhibits its enzymatic activity to trigger ferroptosis [26, 27], while Erastin inhibits the System Xc- transporter (xCT), preventing extracellular cystine uptake, disrupting intracellular GSH synthesis, and ultimately leading to ferroptosis [28]. These findings further suggest that ferroptosis induced by DLX6 knockdown may be linked to GPX4 activity.

DLX6 suppressed ferroptosis in LUAD by transcriptionally regulating GPX4

As a transcriptional regulator, DLX6 modulates the expression of specific genes by binding to their promoters. In this study, we sought to identify the downstream transcriptional targets influenced by DLX6. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) identified 24,813 peak genes, while RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) revealed 3,155 significantly differentially expressed genes. Ferroptosis-related genes were selected using the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb). Integration of these datasets identified 8 genes, including key players in ferroptosis, and provided fold enrichments for these genes: GSH peroxidase 4 (GPX4, a key regulator of GSH metabolism), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11, part of the System Xc-), solute carrier family 3 member 2 (SLC3A2, also part of the System Xc-), solute carrier family 1 member 5 (SLC1A5, neutral amino acid transporter), acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ASCL4), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), transferrin receptor protein (TFRC), and Lys phosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) (Fig. 4A). We next investigated whether DLX6 could influence the mRNA and protein expression of these 8 genes. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis revealed that GPX4 and SLC7A11 were significantly downregulated in DLX6-knockdown A549 and PC9 cells (Fig. 4B-C). In contrast, GPX4 expression was significantly upregulated in DLX6-overexpressing A549 cells (Fig. 4D). Western blot analysis further confirmed that only GPX4 was markedly downregulated in DLX6-knockdown cells and upregulated in DLX6-overexpressed cells (Fig. 4E-F). These results suggest that GPX4 may function as a downstream effector in DLX6-mediated biological processes.

To explore the mechanism by which DLX6 regulates GPX4 transcription, we referred to the JASPAR database, which predicts that DLX6 enhances the transcription of target genes via the CTAATTAT binding motif (Fig. 4G). We identified this DLX6 binding motif within the promoter region of GPX4 and designed a mutant plasmid and primers accordingly. ChIP assays successfully demonstrated the direct binding of DLX6 to specific regions of the GPX4 promoter (Fig. 4H-I). Additionally, we constructed mutant luciferase reporter plasmids in which the DLX6 binding site was altered. Notably, mutation of the binding site in the reporter plasmid attenuated the regulatory effect of DLX6 knockdown on luciferase activity (Fig. 4J-K). Taken together, our data confirm that DLX6 suppresses ferroptosis in LUAD cells through the transcriptional activation of GPX4.

DLX6 overexpression counteracted GPX4 silencing-induced ferroptosis in LUAD cells

Analysis of the TCGA and GEO databases revealed that GPX4 was upregulated in tumor tissues compared to matched normal tissues (Supplemental Fig. 1K). Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that high GPX4 expression was associated with poorer overall survival in LUAD patients (Supplemental Fig. 1L). To investigate the functional role of GPX4, we efficiently knocked down GPX4 expression in LUAD cells (Supplemental Fig. 1M). To investigate the functional relationship between DLX6 and GPX4 in the regulation of ferroptosis, we overexpressed DLX6 in GPX4-knockdown cells. The goal of this rescue experiment was to determine whether DLX6 could mitigate the ferroptotic effects induced by GPX4 silencing. Subsequent assays measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, GSH levels, GSH/GSSG ratios, and Liperfluo fluorescence indicated that DLX6 overexpression significantly alleviated the lipid peroxidation induced by GPX4 silencing (Fig. 5A-C). Interestingly, we also observed that DLX6 overexpression partially rescued the cell death induced by GPX4 silencing (Fig. 5D-E). These findings suggest that DLX6 plays a protective role against ferroptosis in LUAD cells by modulating GPX4 expression and lipid peroxidation.

Inhibition of DLX6 induced ferroptosis to sensitize LUAD cells to cisplatin in vitro

Cisplatin-based chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for LUAD; however, the downregulation of cisplatin sensitivity severely limits the therapeutic efficacy in patients [29]. Previous studies have demonstrated that chemotherapy sensitivity can be enhanced by inducing ferroptosis, and some chemotherapeutic agents, including cisplatin, have been shown to trigger ferroptosis [30]. In line with these findings, our results indicate that the ferroptosis inhibitor FER-1 partially rescued cisplatin-induced cell death in LUAD cells (Fig. 6A). Moreover, we observed an increase in malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and a decrease in glutathione (GSH) levels upon cisplatin treatment (Fig. 6B-C), suggesting that cisplatin induces ferroptosis in LUAD cells. Next, we investigated whether DLX6 regulates the sensitivity of LUAD cells to cisplatin-induced ferroptosis. Our results showed that DLX6 knockdown enhanced the inhibitory effect of cisplatin on cell viability, a phenomenon that was reversed by DLX6 overexpression (Fig. 6D-E). Additionally, we assessed lipid peroxidation levels as an indicator of ferroptosis and found that cisplatin-induced cell death was indeed driven by ferroptosis. In the absence of DLX6, cisplatin-induced lipid peroxidation was further amplified (Fig. 6F-G). In summary, our study reveals that inhibition of DLX6 enhances the sensitivity of LUAD cells to cisplatin-induced ferroptosis, providing a potential strategy for overcoming cisplatin resistance in LUAD therapy.

Inhibition of DLX6 induced ferroptosis and increased LUAD sensitivity to cisplatin treatment in vivo

Next, we further explored in vivo if manipulating the activity of DLX6 could serve as a potential therapeutic approach against tumor growth. To investigate this, we injected PC9 cells with stably reduced DLX6 expression subcutaneously into male Balb/c nude mice aged 6 weeks. These mice, bearing the tumors, received cisplatin (5 mg/kg) or saline injections every 4 days from day 10. On the 26th day, we extracted the tumors and conducted an analysis (Fig. 7A). Our results showed that DLX6 knockdown not only inhibited tumor growth but also significantly increased the sensitivity of the tumors to cisplatin treatment (Fig. 7B-C). Notably, DLX6 knockdown had no effect on the body weight of the mice, indicating that the treatment did not cause systemic toxicity. (Fig. 7D). Further analysis revealed that the absence of DLX6 intensified the generation of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) induced by cisplatin, a marker of lipid peroxidation, while Ki-67 expression, a marker of cell proliferation, decreased significantly (Fig. 7E-G). Additionally, DLX6 knockdown led to reduced GPX4 expression, whereas cisplatin treatment alone did not affect GPX4 levels (Fig. 7H-I). In summary, our in vivo data highlight that DLX6 silencing promotes ferroptosis by enhancing lipid peroxidation and reducing tumor proliferation, thereby improving the therapeutic efficacy of cisplatin in LUAD treatment.

Discussion

Platinum-based chemotherapies remain the cornerstone of treatment for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, resistance to cisplatin has become a significant barrier to effective therapy [31–34]. Cancer cells often upregulate protective antioxidant pathways to escape oxidative stress, which limits the therapeutic efficacy of chemotherapy [29, 35]. Ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent programmed cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, has recently emerged as a potential mechanism to overcome this challenge [13, 36–38]. Studies have shown that upregulation of GPX4 in cancer cells protects against ferroptosis induced by radiotherapy [39, 40]. This suggests a critical need to explore the regulatory mechanisms of ferroptosis across multiple levels—transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, andpost-translational particularly following treatment, to improve tumor prevention and therapy strategies.

Recent studies have identified several transcription factors that influence susceptibility to ferroptosis, including TP53, NFE2L2, NRF2, ATF3, ATF4, YAP1, SP1, HIF1A, EPAS1, HIF2A, BACH1, TFEB, JUN, HIC1, and HNF4A. These transcription factors exert their effects through both transcriptionally-dependent and transcriptionally-independent mechanisms [18]. They modulate the activity of various target genes that regulate the different pathways involved in ferroptosis. Based on their roles, these transcription factors can be classified as ferroptosis drivers or suppressors. Some transcription factors, such as TP53, act in a dual capacity, promoting ferroptosis under certain conditions and inhibiting it under others. NRF2, for instance, is considered a critical pro-ferroptosis regulator. It modulates several ferroptosis-related genes, including those involved in GSH regulation, NADPH regeneration, and iron homeostasis, such as iron export, storage, and heme synthesis and catabolism [41]. Conversely, TP53 promotes ferroptosis through its regulation of genes like SLC7A11, GLS2, SAT1/ALOX15, ALOX12, PTGS2, and FDXR. At the same time, TP53 can inhibit ferroptosis by regulating genes such as p21, DPP4, and Parkin [42]. In the present study, we identify DLX6 as a transcription-dependent regulator of ferroptosis. As a transcription factor, DLX6 directly binds to the promoter region of GPX4 and acts as a transcriptional activator of its expression, thereby modulating lipid peroxidation and promoting ferroptosis. Our novel finding demonstrates that DLX6 enhances the sensitivity of LUAD cells to cisplatin through its interaction with GPX4, thereby inducing ferroptosis.

Our findings suggest that DLX6 may promote ferroptosis by modulating the expression of GPX4, which in turn sensitizes LUAD cells to cisplatin treatment. The DLX6/GPX4 signaling axis is upregulated in LUAD and correlates with poor treatment responses in patients. This suggests that it may serve as a potential predictive biomarker for cisplatin resistance, which could help guide personalized clinical treatment strategies. This study also has certain limitations. The relatively small sample size in these experiments represents a limitation that should be considered when interpreting the results. Besides, While our study demonstrates the role of DLX6 in regulating ferroptosis and its therapeutic potential, several key aspects remain unexplored. The impact of DLX6 post-translational modifications on ferroptosis, as well as the clinical feasibility of targeting DLX6 through nanoparticle delivery systems, warrants further investigation. Importantly, it should be noted that recent studies have also shed light on the various mechanisms by which different transcription factors regulate GPX4, and DLX6 represents just one piece of this regulatory puzzle. Whether DLX6 interacts synergistically with other ferroptosis targets remains unclear. Therefore, to fully comprehend the complexity of ferroptosis and its therapeutic implications, it is crucial to investigate the organization of this regulatory network, rather than focusing solely on individual transcription factors. Understanding the mechanisms governing the interplay between transcription factors and target genes will provide insights into ferroptosis regulation and advance its application in cancer therapy.
