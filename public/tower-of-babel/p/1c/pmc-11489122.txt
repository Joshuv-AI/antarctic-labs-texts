Ectopic thymus in the neonatal period: A case report
Authors: Junyan Wang, Hua Li, Xiaomei Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe thymus, a vital immune organ in humans, plays a crucial role in the differentiation and development of T cells. Its normal development commences around the fourth week of gestation, originating from the ectoderm of the third branchial cleft and the endoderm of the third parotid pouch. By the 7-8th week of gestation, the primary thymus migrates towards the central axis. However, various factors may disrupt this process, leading to congenital anomalies such as incomplete descent of thymus tissue. Persistence of residual thymus tissue can result in the formation of ectopic thymus. Although ectopic thymus is rare in clinical practice, sporadic cases have been reported in the neck or mediastinum, with fewer occurrences in the pericardium, chest wall, axilla, and thyroid gland. It is estimated that ectopic thymus accounts for approximately 0.4% of neck masses, and improper removal may predispose the child to immune deficiencies. This article focuses on a neonatal patient presenting with respiratory failure, where a foreign body was discovered in the oral cavity during weaning from mechanical ventilation. Subsequently, the tumor progressively enlarged postextubation. Surgical excision of the tumor revealed pathological abnormalities upon hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, confirming ectopic thymus tissue. B-ultrasound examination confirmed the presence of thymus tissue, and the child's condition improved, leading to hospital discharge. Follow-up assessments showed no evidence of immune deficiency in the child.Keywords: Case report, Ectopic thymus, Neonate, Immune deficiency

Introduction

The normal thymus has a bilobed structure. Each lobe has an independent fibrous capsule. The 2 are connected by the isthmus and have different shapes. During the neonatal period, the volume of the thymus increases relatively (relative to body weight), reaches its maximum size before puberty, and its main function is to produce hormones that stimulate the maturation of T cells. After puberty, it gradually degenerates and is replaced by adipocytes.

The normal position of the thymus is in front of and above the great blood vessels of the human mediastinum, adjacent to the thyroid gland superiorly, and close to the fourth costal cartilage inferiorly. In infancy, its color ranges from the level of the left brachiocephalic vein to the beginning of the large blood vessel caudally. Clinical information

The patient, a female, is G2P2, and was transferred to our department 1 day after “resuscitation following asphyxia”. She was delivered by emergency cesarean section at a local hospital due to “premature rupture of membranes” at a gestational age of 39 weeks. No abnormalities were observed in the amniotic fluid or placenta. Birth weight: 3.3 kg. After birth, the infant had weak crying, and the Apgar score was 9 at 1 minute (with a deduction of 1 point for breathing) and 8 at 5 minutes (with deductions of 1 point each for breathing and skin color). The infant exhibited obvious respiratory distress, cyanosis around the mouth, progressive cyanosis throughout the body, weak breathing, and decreased heart rate. Immediate resuscitation was initiated but provided no relief. The patient's condition continued to deteriorate with declining heart rate and blood oxygen levels. Subsequently, endotracheal intubation and chest compressions were performed, resulting in gradual recovery of heart rate and blood oxygen levels. Local blood gas analysis indicated: PH:6.55 with a normal reference range of 7.35-7.45, PCO2:74 mmHg with a normal reference range of 40-60, PO2:55 mmHg with a normal reference range of 80-100, Lac:11.3 mmol/L with a normal reference range of 0.5-2.2, BE:-31.6 mmol/L with a normal reference range of -3-3, HCO3-:-7.1 mmol/L with a normal reference range of 21-28. Due to “respiratory distress and unstable blood oxygen levels”, the patient was transferred to our department for further evaluation and management.

Upon admission, the following examinations were conducted: Abdominal ultrasonography (liver, gallbladder, pancreas, spleen): No abnormalities observed; Chest X-ray: Increased lung markings bilaterally; Cranial ultrasound: No abnormalities observed; Complete blood count: WBC:26.6*109 with a normal reference range of 15-20, HGB:186 g/L with a normal reference range of 99-160, PLT:211*109 with a normal reference range of 183-614; Blood gas analysis: PH:7.37 with a normal reference range of 7.35-7.45, PCO2:30 mmHg with a normal reference range of 40-60, PO2:46 mmHg with a normal reference range of 80-100, Lac:8.8 mmol/L with a normal reference range of 0.5-2.2, BE:-8.0 mmol/L with a normal reference range of -3-3, HCO3-:17.3 mmol/L with a normal reference range of 21-28. On the second day after admission, the patient's respiratory condition stabilized, and extubation was attempted. However, postextubation, progressive swelling of a mass was observed, leading to respiratory distress. Emergency neck CT scan revealed: “Soft tissue mass extending from the oral cavity to the exterior, unclear demarcation at the base of the tongue, slight narrowing of the oropharynx due to soft tissue posteriorly”. Due to progressive enlargement of the oral mass, anticipated difficulty in intubation, and postextubation respiratory distress, the patient was re-intubated 5 hours after extubation for ventilator support under CPAP mode. Subsequently, the patient underwent surgical excision of the mass by otolaryngologists. Intraoperatively, a mass extending from the pharynx to the exterior was observed, with some parts exhibiting branching morphology and smooth mucosal surface with visible blood vessel distribution. The base of the mass was thin and originated from the free margin of the right palatopharyngeal arch. Pathological examination postsurgery reported “mass in the right tonsillar fossa suspected to be ectopic thymus”. On the second day postsurgery, B-ultrasound re-examination revealed: “Thymus in front of the aortic arch measuring 2.8 × 1.7 × 1.3 cm, which falls within a typical range”. The patient's respiratory status improved, symptoms resolved, and she was discharged 3 days postsurgery. The patient underwent follow-up examinations at 6 months and 1-year postsurgery, with normal growth and development noted at present.

Discussion

The development of the immune system begins in early fetal stages and is not fully mature at birth. The immune system comprises innate (or natural) immunity and adaptive immunity. Innate immunity, which evolved over time, primarily consists of physical barriers such as the skin, blood-brain barrier, phagocytic cells, and various humoral factors. Adaptive immunity, on the other hand, is developed as the organism encounters antigens repeatedly and includes cell-mediated immunity mediated by T lymphocytes and humoral immunity mediated by B lymphocytes.

The thymus originates from primitive foregut epithelial cells and gradually migrates from the dorsal and ventral positions to the anterior mediastinum. It serves as the primary lymphoid organ of the immune system and facilitates the development of T cells. Various factors can lead to incomplete descent of thymic tissue during its descent from the pharynx to the mediastinum, resulting in partial or complete ectopic thymus. Ectopic thymus is most commonly found in the neck, mediastinum, or lungs, with rare occurrences in the pharynx. Due to its varied locations, ectopic thymus may be misdiagnosed as a tumor or mass, leading to erroneous resection and potential immunodeficiency.

There are relatively few reported cases of ectopic thymus, with neck ectopic thymus cases typically occurring between the ages of 3 and 12 years. Infants account for only 10% of ectopic thymus cases, with a male-to-female ratio close to 3:1 [1]. However, there have been reports of ectopic thymus lesions in the thyroid, with an average age of 6.4 years and a higher prevalence in girls than boys [2]. Ectopic thymus has also been found in the esophagus and thyroid [3], with fewer clinical cases identified in newborns, who often present with respiratory or swallowing difficulties [1,4]. The exact incidence of ectopic thymus remains unclear, but it has been reported to be as high as 2.3% [5], with a case of solid ectopic thymus found in the oropharynx of a 4-month-old infant [6]. Additionally, cases of ectopic thymus have been identified in 26-month-old boys and 52-month-old girls presenting with thyroid masses on pathological examination [3]. In this case, the patient presented with respiratory failure and was found to have an oropharyngeal foreign body. Imaging, surgery, and pathology confirmed the diagnosis of ectopic thymus. Specifically, in Fig. 1, a neck CT scan in the sagittal plane shows that the mass originates from the oral cavity, possibly from the pharynx, aiding in determining which secondary department (oral cavity or otolaryngology) should perform the surgery. Fig. 2, an axial CT scan of the neck, reveals soft tissue shadows behind the oral cavity causing slight narrowing of the oropharynx and unclear delineation from the tongue, providing preoperative imaging for assessment by the surgeon. Based on these findings, otolaryngology was chosen for the surgery. Intraoperatively, the presence of a pedunculated mass extending from the pharynx outside, with some parts appearing lobulated, smooth mucosa on the surface, vascular distribution in the submucosa, and a thin base originating from the free margin of the right palatopharyngeal arch was observed. Histological examination of the mass stained with hematoxylin-eosin at different high magnifications revealed both cortical and medullary components, with clear boundaries between them. The cortex mainly consisted of immature T lymphocytes, and Hassall's corpuscles were visible in the medulla, confirming the origin of the mass as the thymus as seen in Figs. 3 to Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6. Thus, the diagnosis of ectopic thymus was further confirmed.

The surface markers of T cells most commonly used in clinical practice are CD3, CD4, CD8, and NK cells. There is currently no accurate normal range for the CD4/CD8 ratio in neonates, but in general, the CD4/CD8 ratio should be greater than 1, indicating that CD3+CD4+ cell counts should be higher than CD3+CD8+ T cell counts under normal circumstances. The patient in this case exhibited this characteristic, with lymphocyte subsets within normal ranges during follow-up, indicating no occurrence of immunodeficiency later on. Given that this case involves a newborn infant, it is relatively rare in clinical practice for ectopic thymus presenting as tonsillar fossa ectopic thymus. Similar reports have described cases of newborns, with the youngest being a 1-day-old female infant who was found to have oropharyngeal ectopic thymus during extubation due to respiratory arrest 3 days later [7]. Conclusion

Ectopic thymus is rare in clinical practice, especially in the tonsillar fossa, where it is even less common. It can easily be mistakenly excised, potentially affecting the normal immune function of the child. In this case, the patient was very young at the time of onset. However, due to thorough preoperative evaluation and long-term postoperative follow-up, the immune system function remained normal, making this a successful clinical case.
