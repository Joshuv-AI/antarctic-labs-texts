The NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal malignancies: molecular mechanisms, regulatory networks, and therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Lei Cai, Wenzhi Deng, Bang Liu, Jin Wang, Xiangyu Chen, Ying Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe NLRP3 inflammasome is a key inflammatory sensing platform in the innate immune system. It integrates pathogen-associated molecular patterns, damage-associated molecular patterns, and diverse cellular stress signals. Once activated, it promotes caspase-1 activation, maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, and gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis. Through these processes, the NLRP3 inflammasome contributes to inflammatory responses and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis. Increasing evidence has shown that the NLRP3 inflammasome plays a critical but complex role in the initiation and progression of gastrointestinal malignancies.Main bodyIn esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer, NLRP3 activity can be regulated by microbial infection, bile acid stimulation, environmental carcinogens, gut dysbiosis, metabolic abnormalities, mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and treatment-induced stress. Sustained or aberrant NLRP3 activation may promote chronic inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, IL-1β-mediated myeloid cell recruitment, immunosuppressive microenvironment formation, tumor invasion and metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. In contrast, under specific therapeutic conditions, NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in tumor cells can enhance antigen release, activate antitumor immunity, and improve the efficacy of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Therefore, the function of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal tumors is highly dependent on cell type, disease stage, inflammatory intensity, immune composition, and treatment context. This review summarizes the molecular mechanisms of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, its tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive roles in gastrointestinal malignancies, its relationship with immune checkpoint inhibitor response, and emerging therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway.ConclusionsThe NLRP3 inflammasome is not simply a tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive factor in gastrointestinal malignancies. Instead, its biological effects are highly context dependent. Precise modulation of NLRP3, rather than simple inhibition or activation, may provide new opportunities for individualized treatment of gastrointestinal cancers. Future studies should define the cellular source, activation state, downstream effector profile, and immune context of NLRP3 signaling to guide rational therapeutic application.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal malignancies, including esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer, are major cancer types with high morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Their development is not only driven by genetic alterations and epigenetic abnormalities, but is also shaped by chronic inflammation, infection, gut microbiota dysbiosis, metabolic disorders, epithelial barrier injury, and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment [2]. Persistent inflammatory stimulation can induce tissue damage, oxidative stress, abnormal cell proliferation, and immune suppression [3]. These changes create a long-lasting tumor-promoting microenvironment that facilitates gastrointestinal tumorigenesis [3]. The NLRP3 inflammasome is one of the most extensively studied inflammatory sensing platforms in the innate immune system [4]. It is mainly composed of NLRP3, ASC, and pro-caspase-1. After activation, the NLRP3 inflammasome promotes caspase-1 activation, induces the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, and triggers gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis [4]. Unlike some other inflammasomes, NLRP3 does not recognize a single specific ligand. Instead, it senses a wide range of intracellular danger signals, including abnormal ion flux, mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, lysosomal damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, metabolic disturbance, and microbial stimuli [5]. Thus, NLRP3 is considered an important molecular hub that links cellular stress, inflammatory responses, and tissue homeostasis disruption [5]. In gastrointestinal malignancies, the function of the NLRP3 inflammasome is clearly bidirectional and context dependent [6]. On the one hand, sustained NLRP3 activation can promote IL-1β-mediated chronic inflammation, increase the infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages, disrupt the epithelial barrier, and support tumor proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [6]. On the other hand, under certain treatment conditions, NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis can enhance tumor antigen release and immune cell activation. This may strengthen antitumor immunity and improve the efficacy of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors [7].

In recent years, accumulating studies have shown that the NLRP3 inflammasome is involved in the development and progression of esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and [8–10]. For example, Helicobacter pylori, bile acids, Fusobacterium nucleatum, environmental carcinogens, and gut microbial metabolites can regulate NLRP3 activity through mechanisms involving ROS production, NF-κB signaling, mitochondrial injury, or immune cell remodeling [11, 12]. At the same time, several natural compounds, small-molecule agents, and combination treatment strategies may exert antitumor effects by suppressing pro-inflammatory NLRP3 signaling or by inducing NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis [12].

Therefore, it is important to systematically summarize the activation mechanisms, biological functions, and therapeutic significance of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal malignancies. This review focuses on the molecular regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, its roles in esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer, and its relationship with immune checkpoint inhibitors. We also discuss potential therapeutic strategies targeting NLRP3 and highlight key directions for future research. Importantly, the apparently contradictory roles of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal malignancies should not be interpreted as simple inconsistency among studies. Instead, they likely reflect a context-dependent biological model. The functional outcome of NLRP3 activation is determined by several interrelated factors, including the cellular source of activation, disease stage, duration and intensity of inflammasome signaling, downstream cytokine balance, and therapeutic context [13]. In tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, or chronically inflamed epithelial tissues, persistent NLRP3 activation tends to promote IL-1β-driven inflammation, myeloid cell recruitment, epithelial barrier disruption, angiogenesis, immune suppression, and tumor progression [13]. In contrast, transient or therapy-induced NLRP3 activation in tumor cells may promote GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, antigen release, dendritic cell activation, CD8⁺ T-cell priming, and enhanced sensitivity to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immune checkpoint inhibitors [14]. IL-18 may also support antitumor immunity in selected contexts by promoting IFN-γ production by NK cells and cytotoxic T cells [14]. Therefore, the biological role of NLRP3 is not intrinsically tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive, but is shaped by the immune composition of the tumor microenvironment, tumor evolutionary stage, inflammatory status, and treatment conditions.

Mechanisms linking NLRP3 inflammasome activation to gastrointestinal tumorigenesis

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a major innate immune sensing platform that converts pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and intracellular stress signals into inflammatory and cell-death responses [15, 16]. Because gastrointestinal tissues are continuously exposed to microbial products, dietary metabolites, bile acids, barrier injury, and treatment-induced tissue damage, NLRP3 activation is particularly relevant to gastrointestinal malignancies. Rather than providing an exhaustive overview of inflammasome biology, this section focuses on mechanisms that directly connect NLRP3 signaling to gastrointestinal tumor initiation, tumor immune remodeling, epithelial barrier disruption, pyroptosis, and therapeutic response. Core components and tumor-relevant downstream effects of the NLRP3 inflammasome

The canonical NLRP3 inflammasome consists mainly of NLRP3, the adaptor protein ASC/PYCARD, and pro-caspase-1 [17, 18]. Upon activation, NLRP3 recruits ASC and pro-caspase-1 to form a multiprotein inflammasome complex, resulting in caspase-1 activation. Activated caspase-1 cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into mature IL-1β and IL-18 and also cleaves GSDMD to generate the pore-forming GSDMD N-terminal fragment [19]. These events lead to inflammatory cytokine release and pyroptotic cell death, thereby linking danger sensing to both inflammation and immunogenic cell death. In gastrointestinal cancers, the biological meaning of this pathway depends strongly on the cellular source and inflammatory context. In tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, neutrophils, or chronically injured epithelial tissues, NLRP3 activation often promotes IL-1β-driven inflammation, vascular permeability, myeloid cell recruitment, angiogenesis, invasion, metastasis, and immune suppression [20]. In contrast, therapy-induced NLRP3 activation in tumor cells may trigger GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, increase tumor antigen release, and enhance immune cell activation [21]. IL-18 also has context-dependent functions: it may promote IFN-γ production by NK cells and T cells in antitumor immune responses, but may also contribute to chronic inflammation or immune escape in selected tumor settings [20]. Therefore, NLRP3 signaling should be interpreted according to cell type, activation intensity, disease stage, and treatment context rather than as uniformly tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive.

Two-signal activation model in gastrointestinal tumor contexts

Canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation generally requires a priming signal and an activation signal. The priming signal is usually mediated by Toll-like receptors, tumor necrosis factor receptors, interleukin-1 receptors, or other pattern recognition receptors, leading to activation of NF-κB, MAPK, AP-1, and related pathways and subsequent induction of NLRP3 and inflammatory cytokine precursors [22]. In gastrointestinal malignancies, chronic infection, gut microbiota dysbiosis, epithelial barrier disruption, bile acid stimulation, necrosis-associated DAMPs such as HMGB1, and chemotherapy- or radiotherapy-induced injury can all provide priming signals [23, 24]. This step is particularly important in chronically inflamed mucosa, where repeated priming may create a long-lasting tumor-promoting inflammatory field.

The second activation signal is induced by cellular stress events such as ATP release, potassium efflux, calcium influx, chloride flux, mitochondrial damage, ROS production, lysosomal rupture, and particulate or crystal deposition [25]. These stimuli converge on NLRP3 conformational activation, oligomerization, ASC recruitment, caspase-1 activation, IL-1β/IL-18 maturation, and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis [26]. In gastrointestinal tumors, the coexistence of microbial stimulation, metabolic stress, hypoxia, epithelial injury, and anticancer treatment can provide both priming and activation signals. This explains why NLRP3 may support chronic inflammation during tumorigenesis but may also contribute to immunogenic tumor cell death during therapy. Negative regulation of NLRP3 is equally important for maintaining tissue homeostasis. Autophagy and mitophagy can restrain NLRP3 activation by clearing damaged mitochondria, reducing mitochondrial DNA and ROS release, and degrading inflammasome components [27]. Antioxidant systems, including NRF2-mediated responses, also limit ROS accumulation and inflammasome activation [28]. In addition, regulatory proteins, immune checkpoint signals, anti-inflammatory cytokines, and metabolic inhibitory signals may suppress NLRP3 activity directly or indirectly [29, 30]. In cancer, this regulation has dual implications. Excessive NLRP3 suppression may reduce chronic tumor-promoting inflammation, but it may also weaken therapy-induced pyroptosis and antitumor immune activation. Therefore, NLRP3-targeted interventions should be designed according to disease stage, cellular source, and immune microenvironmental features [31].

GI cancer-relevant mechanisms of NLRP3 inflammasome activation

Ion flux disturbance

Disturbed ion homeostasis is one of the most common mechanisms of NLRP3 activation. Potassium efflux, often induced by extracellular ATP through P2X7 receptor signaling, is a central trigger that promotes NLRP3 conformational change and inflammasome assembly [32]. Calcium influx, chloride efflux, sodium influx, and osmotic changes can further cooperate with mitochondrial injury and membrane stress to amplify inflammasome activation [33]. In gastrointestinal malignancies, extracellular ATP released from necrotic tumor cells, acidic metabolic conditions, hypoxia-related membrane channel dysfunction, microbial toxins, and treatment-induced cell injury may all disturb ion balance [6]. Thus, ion flux links tissue damage and tumor metabolic stress to IL-1β/IL-18 release, inflammatory cell infiltration, and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production

Mitochondrial dysfunction is highly relevant to NLRP3 activation in gastrointestinal tumors. Loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, mitochondrial ROS production, oxidized mitochondrial DNA release, cardiolipin exposure, and abnormal mitochondrial dynamics can all promote NLRP3 relocalization, oligomerization, and inflammasome assembly [34]. Gastrointestinal cancer cells and tumor-associated immune cells often experience hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress, and metabolic reprogramming, all of which can disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis [6]. In chronic inflammation, sustained mitochondrial stress may promote tumor-supportive IL-1β/IL-18 signaling, angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and immune suppression. Under therapeutic stress, however, acute mitochondrial damage and ROS accumulation may facilitate NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis and enhance antitumor immunity. The mitochondrial-NLRP3 axis therefore represents a context-dependent bridge between tumor metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and treatment response.

Lysosomal damage and particulate stress

Lysosomal damage provides another route for NLRP3 activation. Crystals, particles, cholesterol deposits, microbial products, exogenous nanoparticles, or engulfed cellular debris can induce lysosomal membrane rupture and release cathepsins into the cytosol, thereby promoting inflammasome activation [35]. In gastrointestinal malignancies, bile acid stimulation, abnormal lipid metabolism, tumor necrosis, microbiota-derived metabolites, and uptake of treatment-generated tumor debris by macrophages may all induce lysosomal stress [35]. This mechanism is especially relevant to tumor-associated macrophages and other myeloid cells, in which lysosomal injury can drive IL-1β release, chronic inflammation, recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, and tumor progression.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress, Golgi remodeling, and spatial organization

NLRP3 activation is also shaped by organelle stress and spatial reorganization. Endoplasmic reticulum stress can promote NLRP3 activation through unfolded protein response signaling, calcium imbalance, and ROS production [36]. Tumor cells frequently undergo endoplasmic reticulum stress because of hypoxia, nutrient limitation, high protein synthesis, and therapeutic pressure, making this mechanism relevant to gastrointestinal cancer biology. Golgi remodeling may further provide membrane platforms for NLRP3 recruitment and oligomerization, and membrane contact sites among mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and the Golgi apparatus may coordinate inflammasome assembly [37]. These spatial mechanisms suggest that NLRP3 activation in tumors is not only controlled by soluble inflammatory signals, but also by the stressed organelle network of malignant and immune cells.

NEK7-mediated inflammasome assembly

NEK7 is an important auxiliary factor for NLRP3 inflammasome activation. After NLRP3-activating stimuli, NEK7 interacts with NLRP3 and promotes NLRP3 oligomerization and inflammasome assembly, usually downstream of potassium efflux [38]. Because NEK7 is also involved in cell-cycle regulation, it may connect inflammasome activation with proliferative states [21]. This is relevant to gastrointestinal tumors, where rapidly proliferating tumor cells and inflammatory immune cells coexist. Future studies should clarify whether NEK7-dependent NLRP3 activation differs between tumor cells and myeloid cells and whether this interaction can be selectively targeted to modulate tumor-associated inflammation without broadly suppressing host immunity.

Post-translational regulation of NLRP3

Post-translational modifications regulate NLRP3 protein stability, localization, conformation, oligomerization, and interaction with ASC or NEK7. These modifications include ubiquitination, deubiquitination, phosphorylation, acetylation, SUMOylation, and S-nitrosylation [39]. In gastrointestinal tumors, hypoxia, metabolic abnormalities, oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, microbial products, and therapeutic pressure may alter the enzymes that control these modifications [40]. As a result, post-translational regulation may help explain why NLRP3 activation differs across tumor types, disease stages, and immune microenvironments. Targeting specific enzymes that regulate NLRP3 modifications may provide a more precise approach than global inflammasome inhibition, especially in settings where myeloid inflammation should be suppressed but tumor cell pyroptosis should be preserved.

Metabolic reprogramming and immunometabolic regulation

Metabolic reprogramming is closely linked to NLRP3 inflammasome activity. Enhanced glycolysis, lactate accumulation, abnormal fatty acid metabolism, cholesterol accumulation, altered amino acid metabolism, and impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation can all influence inflammasome activation [41]. In gastrointestinal malignancies, both tumor cells and tumor-associated immune cells display strong metabolic plasticity. Hypoxia, acidic metabolites, lipid accumulation, and altered nutrient availability can regulate NLRP3 activity in macrophages, dendritic cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [42, 43]. For example, free fatty acids, oxidized lipids, and cholesterol crystals may induce mitochondrial or lysosomal stress, whereas lactate-rich acidic microenvironments may suppress antitumor immunity while reshaping inflammatory signaling. Thus, NLRP3 should be viewed not only as an inflammatory sensor, but also as an immunometabolic responder that links tumor metabolism to immune escape and therapeutic sensitivity.

Gut microbiota and microbial metabolites

The gastrointestinal tract contains a dense and diverse microbiota, making microbe-inflammasome interactions highly relevant to gastrointestinal tumorigenesis [44]. Microbiota-derived PAMPs, including lipopolysaccharide, peptidoglycan, bacterial DNA, and flagellin, can activate NF-κB-dependent priming, whereas bacterial toxins, epithelial injury, ion flux disturbance, mitochondrial stress, and microbial metabolites can provide activation signals [44]. Short-chain fatty acids, bile acid metabolites, tryptophan metabolites, and polyamines may either inhibit excessive inflammation and support barrier homeostasis or, under dysbiotic conditions, promote persistent NLRP3 activation and tissue injury [45]. During gastrointestinal tumorigenesis, dysbiosis may therefore promote an inflammatory tumor microenvironment through NLRP3-dependent IL-1β/IL-18 release, epithelial damage, immune remodeling, and altered treatment response [49]. The microbiota-NLRP3 axis is particularly important because it connects environmental exposure, mucosal inflammation, microbial metabolism, and cancer therapy response.

Translational implications of NLRP3 activation mechanisms

Taken together, these activation mechanisms should be interpreted in relation to gastrointestinal oncology rather than as isolated inflammatory pathways. In gastrointestinal tumors, NLRP3 can be activated by chronic infection, bile reflux, gut dysbiosis, epithelial barrier disruption, hypoxia, metabolic stress, and anticancer therapy-induced injury. Persistent activation in myeloid cells or chronically injured epithelium may promote IL-1β-driven inflammation, immunosuppressive cell recruitment, angiogenesis, and tumor progression. In contrast, acute activation in tumor cells under therapeutic stress may induce pyroptosis, antigen release, and antitumor immune activation. Therefore, the clinical challenge is not simply to inhibit or activate NLRP3, but to determine when, where, and in which cellular compartment NLRP3 modulation will provide therapeutic benefit. The role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in colorectal cancer

The role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in colorectal cancer (CRC) is clearly bidirectional and cell type dependent. In the context of chronic inflammation, metabolic disturbance, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment, NLRP3 can promote IL-1β release, myeloid inflammatory responses, and tumor progression. In contrast, under conditions involving tumor cell pyroptosis, antitumor immune activation, and enhanced cGAS/STING signaling, NLRP3 may exert tumor-suppressive effects [6, 46–48]. Spatial transcriptomic and tissue-mapping studies have identified distinct macrophage subsets in CRC tissues. Among them, NLRP3⁺ macrophages tend to colocalize with neutrophils and activate inflammasome responses within local tumor regions. This finding suggests that NLRP3-associated myeloid niches may be an important component of the inflammatory microenvironment in CRC and may influence tumor progression and patient prognosis [49]. Chronic inflammation-driven colorectal tumorigenesis is closely associated with NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Tumor cell-derived 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) can promote Ca²⁺ influx in macrophages through the HTR3A receptor. This activates CaMKIIα and induces NLRP3 phosphorylation at Ser198, thereby promoting inflammasome assembly. Subsequent maturation and release of IL-1β further increase TPH1 transcription and 5-HT synthesis in CRC cells. This forms a positive feedback loop between 5-HT, NLRP3, and IL-1β, which drives CRC progression [6]. Post-translational modification and protein stability of NLRP3 are also important mechanisms in inflammation-associated CRC. The deubiquitinase OTUD6A can directly bind to the NACHT domain of NLRP3 and remove K48-linked polyubiquitin chains from NLRP3 at K430 and K689. This stabilizes NLRP3, enhances IL-1β release, and aggravates intestinal inflammation. Loss of Otud6a attenuates DSS- or TNBS-induced colitis and suppresses AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated cancer. These findings suggest that the OTUD6A-NLRP3 axis may be a potential intervention target for IBD-associated CRC [7]. NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis is also important for antitumor therapy in CRC. In CRC cells, NLRP3 expression can be silenced, thereby limiting GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. HDAC2 suppresses NLRP3 transcription by removing H3K27ac and reducing chromatin accessibility. In contrast, HDAC2 knockout or treatment with the HDAC2 inhibitor Santacruzamate A promotes the recruitment of the BRD4-p-P65 complex to the NLRP3 promoter. This restores NLRP3 expression and enhances 5-fluorouracil- or regorafenib-induced pyroptosis and therapeutic efficacy [46].

RNA epigenetic modification can also regulate NLRP3 and affect CRC pyroptosis and malignant progression. WTAP is markedly upregulated in CRC tissues and cells. It mediates m⁶A modification of NLRP3 mRNA, suppresses NLRP3 expression, and weakens activation of the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD axis. As a result, pyroptosis is reduced, whereas CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion are enhanced and apoptosis is inhibited. These findings indicate that the WTAP-m⁶A-NLRP3 axis may represent a novel epitranscriptomic mechanism driving CRC progression [50]. The NLRP3 inflammasome also contributes to early antitumor immune surveillance in CRC. NCF4 can act as a sensor of ROS levels and cooperate with NCF1 and NCF2 to promote activation of both NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes. NCF4 deficiency leads to more severe colorectal tumorigenesis, increases transit-amplifying and precancerous cells, and reduces the frequency and activation of CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells. These findings suggest that NCF4 supports early antitumor immunity by maintaining the inflammasome-IL-18-IFN-γ axis [47]. Metabolic factors can promote CRC by affecting NLRP3 inflammasome activity and macrophage polarization. A modest amount of fructose promotes the interaction between hexokinase 2 and the endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ channel IP3R3. This effect is independent of obesity-related metabolic disorders. The interaction lowers cytosolic and mitochondrial Ca²⁺ levels, suppresses MAPK and STAT1 signaling, and inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation. As a result, M1-like macrophage polarization is impaired, which supports CRC initiation and growth [48]. Several natural compounds may exert anti-CRC effects by suppressing pro-inflammatory NLRP3 activation or by inducing tumor cell death. 2′,4′-DHC induces apoptosis and pyroptosis in CRC cells, causes G2/M cell-cycle arrest, and inhibits cell migration. It also suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation through inhibition of the NF-κB pathway. In parallel, 2′,4′-DHC enhances T-cell infiltration and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T-cell function, while reducing CD11b⁺CD206⁺ macrophage infiltration and activity. These effects help remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment and inhibit tumor growth [51]. Atractylenolide I inhibits colitis-associated CRC progression by reducing NLRP3 inflammasome activation through suppression of Drp1-mediated excessive mitochondrial fission. This compound decreases the viability of HCT116 and SW480 cells and induces apoptosis. It also suppresses colon tumor formation in the AOM/DSS model. However, Drp1 overexpression reverses the inhibitory effects of atractylenolide I on NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β release. These findings suggest that the Drp1-mitochondrial fission-NLRP3 axis is an important therapeutic target in inflammation-associated CRC [52].

Inorganic polyphosphate may also promote CRC progression by regulating communication between tumor cells and immune cells. The iPolyP/TRPM8 axis induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition in CRC cells. It also indirectly activates the NLRP3 inflammasome in immune cells and increases IL-1β levels in the tumor microenvironment. These events promote tumor growth, progression, and metastasis, suggesting that the iPolyP/TRPM8-NLRP3-IL-1β pathway may be therapeutically targetable [53]. It is worth noting that NLRP3 does not always function through the canonical inflammasome pathway. Specific activation of NLRP3 in CRC cells can enhance cGAS/STING signaling in both MSI and non-MSI CRCs and promote CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. This effect is NLRP3-specific and independent of classical inflammasome activity. Instead, it relies on early crosstalk between NLRP3 and cGAS/STING signaling mediators and on positive feedback regulation of gene expression [54]. Overall, the function of the NLRP3 inflammasome in CRC depends on the activated cell type, disease stage, and microenvironmental context. In settings of chronic inflammation, myeloid cell activation, metabolic disturbance, and immune suppression, the NLRP3/IL-1β axis can promote tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. In contrast, in the context of tumor cell pyroptosis, IL-18-IFN-γ-mediated antitumor immunity, and enhanced cGAS/STING signaling, NLRP3 may exert tumor-suppressive effects. Therefore, NLRP3-targeted therapy for CRC should not simply aim to inhibit or activate NLRP3. Instead, it should be guided by inflammatory status, tumor cell pyroptotic capacity, immune cell composition, and therapeutic purpose. These findings indicate that NLRP3 signaling in CRC cannot be evaluated solely by measuring total NLRP3 expression. Instead, the key analytical variables are the cellular compartment in which NLRP3 is activated, whether downstream signaling is dominated by IL-1β-mediated myeloid inflammation or IL-18/pyroptosis-mediated antitumor immunity, and whether inflammasome activation occurs during chronic tumor-promoting inflammation or acute therapy-induced immunogenic cell death.

The role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in esophageal cancer

The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a highly context-dependent role in esophageal cancer. On the one hand, aberrant NLRP3 activation may promote inflammatory responses, immunosuppressive microenvironment formation, and therapeutic resistance. On the other hand, under specific drug-induced conditions, NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis may serve as an important mechanism for suppressing the malignant behavior of esophageal cancer cells [55–57]. In the context of chemoresistance, the NLRP3 inflammasome may have a tumor-promoting role. 5-fluorouracil markedly activates NLRP3 and increases IL-1β levels. The natural flavonoid galangin can reverse this effect by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and increasing the sensitivity of esophageal cancer cells to 5-fluorouracil-induced apoptosis. In xenograft models, galangin combined with 5-fluorouracil further suppresses tumor growth. These findings suggest that inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome may be an effective strategy to improve chemotherapy sensitivity in esophageal cancer [55]. In contrast to its role in promoting therapeutic resistance, NLRP3 may also participate in antitumor pyroptosis. Piperlongumine inhibits the proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells. It also upregulates ASC, cleaved caspase-1, NLRP3, and GSDMD, thereby promoting ROS-TXNIP-NLRP3 axis-mediated pyroptosis. NRF2 activation, ROS scavenging, or TXNIP inhibition can weaken this effect. These findings indicate that piperlongumine exerts antitumor activity in esophageal cancer mainly by inhibiting NRF2, increasing ROS generation, and activating the TXNIP-NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD pathway [9].

Tumor-associated microbes can also promote esophageal cancer progression and therapeutic resistance through the NLRP3 inflammasome. Fusobacterium nucleatum infection is associated with high NLRP3 expression and myeloid-derived suppressor cell enrichment in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma tissues. It is also linked to shorter patient survival. In vitro and in vivo studies further show that F. nucleatum induces high NLRP3 expression in esophageal cancer cells, promotes MDSC recruitment, and reduces the sensitivity of esophageal cancer cells to cisplatin. These results suggest that the “microbial infection-NLRP3 activation-MDSC enrichment” axis may be an important mechanism underlying immune suppression and chemoresistance in esophageal cancer [56]. Environmental carcinogen-induced early inflammatory responses may also depend on the NLRP3 inflammasome. Nitrosamines, an important class of environmental carcinogens associated with digestive tract tumors, induce oxidative damage, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis in normal esophageal epithelial cells in a dose-dependent manner. The ROS scavenger NAC, especially the mitochondrial ROS scavenger Mito-TEMPO, effectively inhibits NLRP3 activation. These findings suggest that nitrosamines may drive NLRP3 inflammasome activation through mitochondrial ROS, thereby contributing to esophageal epithelial inflammatory injury and potential malignant transformation [57]. Taken together, the function of the NLRP3 inflammasome in esophageal cancer is not uniform. Sustained or aberrant NLRP3 activation may promote inflammatory cytokine release, MDSC enrichment, immune suppression, and chemoresistance. In contrast, drug-induced ROS-TXNIP-NLRP3 pyroptosis may directly restrict malignant tumor cell phenotypes. Therefore, NLRP3-related therapeutic strategies for esophageal cancer should distinguish between its pro-inflammatory, tumor-promoting effects and its pyroptosis-mediated antitumor effects. Such strategies should also consider the tumor microenvironment, microbial infection status, and treatment context. Thus, in esophageal cancer, NLRP3 appears to function as a double-edged regulator. Microbial infection- or carcinogen-driven chronic NLRP3 activation may promote inflammatory injury, MDSC recruitment, chemoresistance, and malignant transformation, whereas drug-induced ROS-TXNIP-NLRP3 activation may directly trigger tumor cell pyroptosis and suppress malignant phenotypes [58]. These differences emphasize that the upstream trigger and cellular target of NLRP3 activation are critical determinants of its biological outcome. Overall, compared with CRC, evidence regarding NLRP3 in esophageal cancer remains less extensive and is mainly concentrated on chemoresistance, microbial infection, carcinogen-induced epithelial injury, and drug-induced pyroptosis. These studies suggest that the biological outcome of NLRP3 activation in esophageal cancer depends strongly on the upstream trigger. Microbial infection- or carcinogen-induced NLRP3 activation may promote inflammatory injury, MDSC recruitment, immune suppression, and treatment resistance, whereas ROS-TXNIP-driven activation under specific pharmacological conditions may induce tumor cell pyroptosis and suppress malignant phenotypes. Future studies should further define the cellular source of NLRP3 activation, distinguish epithelial/tumor cell-intrinsic effects from myeloid cell-mediated effects, and determine whether NLRP3-related biomarkers can predict chemotherapy or immunotherapy response in esophageal cancer.

The role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastric cancer

The NLRP3 inflammasome has an important but complex regulatory role in gastric cancer. It can participate in tumor-promoting inflammatory responses driven by bile reflux, Helicobacter pylori infection, and chronic gastric mucosal injury. It can also induce gastric cancer cell pyroptosis under specific drug- or treatment-related conditions, thereby exerting antitumor effects [10, 59, 60]. Bile reflux-associated gastric carcinogenesis may involve USP50-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages. In active human bile reflux gastritis and mouse duodenogastric reflux models, USP50 is markedly increased and is mainly upregulated in macrophages. USP50 interacts with ASC and deubiquitinates ASC, thereby enhancing bile acid-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and HMGB1 release. HMGB1 further promotes gastric tumorigenesis through the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways. These findings suggest that the USP50-ASC-NLRP3-HMGB1 axis may be an important target for preventing and treating bile reflux-associated gastric cancer [10]. H. pylori infection, especially infection with CagA-positive strains, can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome through a ROS-dependent mechanism and promote gastric cancer cell invasion and migration. Hp/CagA⁺ infection or CagA overexpression induces intracellular ROS production, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and pyroptosis-related responses. The ROS scavenger NAC effectively blocks NLRP3 activation. In addition, NLRP3 silencing weakens CagA-mediated migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells. These findings indicate that the ROS-NLRP3 axis is one of the key mechanisms by which CagA promotes malignant progression in gastric cancer [60]. Further evidence suggests that the H. pylori CagA-related signaling pathway not only activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, but may also affect disease progression and treatment response by remodeling the gastric cancer immune microenvironment. NLRP3 expression is associated with clinicopathological features, prognosis, and immune cell infiltration. This suggests that the CagA-NLRP3 axis may participate in immune microenvironment regulation in gastric cancer and may have potential value as a prognostic marker and an indicator for immunotherapy stratification [61].

The NLRP3 inflammasome is also involved in chronic atrophic gastritis and spasmolytic polypeptide-expressing metaplasia (SPEM), which represent important premalignant stages of gastric cancer. GRIM-19 is an essential subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I and is progressively reduced during the malignant transformation of chronic atrophic gastritis. Loss of GRIM-19 activates NRF2-HO-1 and NF-κB signaling through ROS-mediated oxidative stress. This further induces NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-33 expression, promoting gastritis and SPEM formation. The NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 markedly attenuates GRIM-19 loss-induced gastritis and SPEM. These findings suggest that the ROS-NRF2-HO-1-NF-κB-NLRP3/IL-33 axis may be an important intervention pathway for the early prevention of intestinal-type gastric cancer [62].

On the other hand, NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis can also be exploited to suppress gastric cancer cell growth. THBS1 is highly expressed in gastric cancer cells. Its knockdown inhibits malignant cell behavior and induces G1-phase arrest and apoptosis. When THBS1 knockdown is combined with baicalin, LDH release and ROS levels are further increased. This combination promotes pyroptosis through the NF-κB-NLRP3-Caspase-1 axis and synergistically suppresses gastric cancer progression in vitro and in vivo. These results suggest that induction of NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis may represent a new therapeutic strategy for gastric cancer [59]. Kaempferol can also induce gastric cancer cell pyroptosis by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome. It promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation in AGS cells through NF-κB signaling, induces caspase-1 cleavage and IL-18 release, and triggers pyroptosis through mitochondrial damage. These findings suggest that natural flavonoids may exert anti-gastric cancer effects by regulating the NF-κB-NLRP3-Caspase-1 pathway [63]. Overall, the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastric cancer is highly stage- and context-dependent. In the setting of bile reflux, H. pylori infection, and chronic gastric mucosal injury, sustained NLRP3 activation can amplify inflammation, promote epithelial damage, support premalignant lesion formation, and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment. In contrast, under drug-induced conditions, NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD-related pyroptosis can directly inhibit gastric cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth. Therefore, future research and therapeutic strategies for gastric cancer should distinguish between the pro-inflammatory, tumor-promoting role of NLRP3 and its pyroptosis-mediated antitumor function.

Compared with CRC, current studies on NLRP3 in gastric cancer are more closely linked to chronic mucosal injury, Helicobacter pylori infection, bile reflux, and premalignant inflammatory lesions. These findings suggest that NLRP3 may be particularly important during the inflammation-metaplasia-carcinoma sequence of gastric tumorigenesis. However, its function in established gastric cancer remains context dependent. In macrophages or chronically injured gastric mucosa, NLRP3 activation may amplify IL-1β/HMGB1-mediated inflammation and promote tumor-supportive signaling [64]. In contrast, in gastric cancer cells exposed to specific natural compounds or therapeutic stress, activation of the NF-κB-NLRP3-caspase-1 axis may induce pyroptosis and suppress tumor growth [63]. Future work should clarify whether NLRP3 primarily acts during early inflammatory transformation, advanced tumor progression, or treatment response, and should integrate microbial, immune, and molecular subtype information.

The role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in inflammation-associated intestinal tumorigenesis

Compared with colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and esophageal cancer, direct evidence regarding the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in primary small intestinal cancer remains limited. Therefore, this section focuses more broadly on inflammation-associated intestinal tumorigenesis, particularly IBD-associated intestinal cancer and colitis-associated cancer models. This reframing is important because many available studies use DSS-induced colitis or AOM/DSS-induced tumorigenesis models, which more closely reflect chronic intestinal inflammation-driven carcinogenesis than primary small intestinal malignancy [65]. In these settings, the NLRP3 inflammasome acts as an important molecular link among chronic intestinal inflammation, epithelial barrier injury, myeloid cell activation, and inflammation-associated tumor development. These effects provide a persistent inflammatory microenvironment for tumor development [7]. The deubiquitinase OTUD6A directly binds to the NACHT domain of NLRP3 and removes K48-linked polyubiquitin chains from NLRP3 at K430 and K689. This enhances NLRP3 protein stability and promotes IL-1β production and intestinal inflammation. Otud6a deficiency reduces DSS- or TNBS-induced colitis and suppresses AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated cancer. These findings suggest that the OTUD6A-NLRP3 axis may be a potential therapeutic target for IBD and inflammation-associated intestinal tumors [7]. D-cycloserine shows dual activity by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and protecting the intestinal epithelial barrier. In LPS-stimulated intestinal epithelial cells, D-cycloserine suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and restores tight junction protein expression. In DSS-induced colitis and AOM/DSS-induced inflammation-associated colorectal cancer models, it reduces inflammation and tissue damage, maintains intestinal barrier integrity, and decreases tumor burden and Ki-67-positive cell proliferation. These findings suggest that D-cycloserine may exert anti-inflammatory and antitumor effects by inhibiting the NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD pathway [66].

The damage-associated molecular pattern Prdx1 may also serve as an upstream activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome during intestinal inflammation. Serum Prdx1 levels are elevated in patients with Crohn’s disease and in mice with experimental colitis. These levels positively correlate with the severity of intestinal inflammation. Prdx1 promotes macrophage infiltration and inflammatory cytokine production. After internalization by macrophages, it induces lysosomal damage and further activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby aggravating intestinal inflammation [67]. Natural or drug-derived small molecules may also exert antitumor effects by suppressing NLRP3-related inflammatory signaling. AST inhibits the proliferation of HCT116 and HT29 colorectal cancer cells, induces mitochondria-dependent apoptosis, and causes cell-cycle arrest. In AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated cancer models, AST markedly reduces tumor number and decreases the expression of NF-κB, NLRP3, ASC, IL-1β, COX-2, iNOS, and multiple inflammatory cytokines. These findings indicate that inhibition of the NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammatory axis may be an important mechanism by which AST alleviates inflammation and suppresses tumor progression [68]. MG53 may act as an endogenous protective factor that limits excessive NLRP3 inflammasome activation. MG53-deficient mice show more severe weight loss, higher disease activity scores, shortened colons, and aggravated inflammation after DSS treatment. Recombinant MG53 alleviates colitis and suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, MG53 interacts with NLRP3 and reduces ASC speck formation and NLRP3 oligomerization, without affecting upstream signaling or NLRP3 stability. These findings suggest that MG53 may exert anti-inflammatory protection by blocking NLRP3 inflammasome assembly [69]. Taken together, the NLRP3 inflammasome has a dual significance in the transition from intestinal inflammation to inflammation-associated cancer. On the one hand, sustained activation of NLRP3 amplifies IL-1β-mediated inflammation, disrupts the epithelial barrier, and promotes a tumor-supportive microenvironment. On the other hand, targeting NLRP3 stability, inflammasome assembly, macrophage activation, or the NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD pathway may provide new strategies for the prevention and treatment of IBD-related intestinal tumors. Thus, this body of evidence should be interpreted as reflecting inflammation-associated intestinal tumorigenesis rather than primary small intestinal cancer. The available studies support a model in which persistent NLRP3 activation contributes to chronic intestinal inflammation, barrier dysfunction, IL-1β production, and tumor-supportive immune remodeling. At the same time, inhibition of excessive NLRP3 activation may reduce colitis severity and tumor burden in inflammation-associated cancer models. Future studies specifically focused on primary small intestinal tumors are needed to determine whether the mechanisms identified in colitis-associated models are directly applicable to small intestinal malignancies.

The NLRP3 inflammasome and tumor immune checkpoint inhibitors

The relationship between the NLRP3 inflammasome and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is complex and bidirectional. On the one hand, NLRP3 activation can induce pyroptosis, enhance antigen presentation, and promote T-cell priming. These effects may convert immunologically “cold” tumors into more immunogenic “hot” tumors and improve ICI efficacy. On the other hand, sustained NLRP3-driven inflammation may upregulate PD-L1, promote the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, and contribute to immune-related toxicity during ICI treatment [30, 70]. In chemo-immunotherapy, NLRP3-mediated tumor cell pyroptosis may be an important mechanism for enhancing ICI responses. The endoplasmic reticulum stress sensor IRE1α suppresses the immunostimulatory effects of taxane chemotherapy through its RNase activity. It clears taxane-induced double-stranded RNA through regulated IRE1-dependent decay, thereby preventing ZBP1-mediated danger signal recognition and NLRP3-GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. Inhibition of IRE1α allows taxanes to induce extensive dsRNA accumulation, activate the ZBP1-NLRP3-GSDMD pyroptosis pathway, and convert PD-L1-negative, ICI-unresponsive triple-negative breast cancer into PD-L1-high immunogenic tumors that are highly sensitive to ICIs [30]. In ovarian cancer, the NLRP3 inflammasome appears to promote immune suppression and tumor progression. NLRP3 is highly expressed in human epithelial ovarian cancer tissues and is associated with poor prognosis. LPS- and ATP-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation promotes ovarian cancer cell proliferation and increases IL-1 and PD-L1 levels. The NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 reduces IL-1 and PD-L1 expression, decreases immunosuppressive cell populations such as MDSCs, TAMs, and PD-1⁺CD4⁺ T cells, and suppresses tumor growth. These findings suggest that NLRP3 blockade may help relieve immune suppression and improve immunotherapeutic responses [70]. The combination of radiotherapy and NLRP3 activation can enhance immune priming and overcome anti-PD-1 resistance. In both anti-PD-1-sensitive and anti-PD-1-resistant murine lung adenocarcinoma models, intratumoral injection of an NLRP3 agonist combined with stereotactic radiotherapy improves control of both primary and distant tumors and induces a clear abscopal response. Among the tested radiation schedules, 12 Gy × 3 fractions is more effective than 5 Gy × 3 fractions, whereas 1 Gy × 2 fractions does not enhance the NLRP3-related effect. This indicates that the antitumor immune effect of NLRP3 activation depends on appropriate radiation dose and treatment sequencing [71]. Furthermore, triple therapy with an NLRP3 agonist, radiotherapy, and anti-PD-1 treatment markedly increases several inflammatory and immune-activating cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-4, IL-12, IL-17, IFN-γ, and GM-CSF. It also enhances antigen presentation, innate immune function, and T-cell priming. As a result, this strategy can induce stronger systemic antitumor immune responses in immunologically “cold” tumors or tumors resistant to prior anti-PD-1 treatment [71].

However, the NLRP3 inflammasome also contributes to ICI-related immune-mediated liver injury. Anti-CTLA4 combined with anti-PD-L1 treatment induces hepatocyte apoptosis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Total liver knockdown of caspase-3 unexpectedly worsens liver injury and further increases inflammasome activation and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. In contrast, hepatocyte-specific caspase-3 knockdown reduces liver injury and NLRP3 activation. These findings suggest that hepatocyte apoptosis, myeloid NLRP3 inflammatory responses, and interactions among CD8⁺ T cells and macrophages jointly drive ICI-associated hepatotoxicity [72].

NLRP3 may also influence infection-related inflammatory responses by regulating the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. This provides an additional perspective for understanding ICI-related inflammation and immune adverse events. In a model of acute pneumonia induced by antibiotic-resistant Serratia marcescens, NLRP3 activation promotes GSDMD-mediated macrophage pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion. It also affects PD-L1/PD-1 expression in macrophages, alveolar macrophages, interstitial macrophages, and neutrophils. Nlrp3 deletion reduces lung injury, decreases bacterial burden, and improves survival. These findings suggest that the NLRP3 inflammasome not only regulates inflammatory injury, but may also influence PD-1/PD-L1-related immune states [73]. Overall, the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in ICI therapy depends on its activation site, cell type, and therapeutic context. In tumor cells, moderate NLRP3 activation can induce pyroptosis, increase danger signal release, enhance antigen presentation, and promote T-cell priming, thereby improving ICI sensitivity. However, in myeloid cells or chronic inflammatory environments, sustained NLRP3 activation may upregulate PD-L1, promote MDSC and TAM infiltration, and aggravate immune-related adverse events. Therefore, future strategies combining NLRP3 modulation with ICIs should emphasize precise regulation rather than simple activation or inhibition. Tumor immune status, chemo-radiotherapy sequencing, inflammatory toxicity risk, and immune microenvironment composition should all be carefully considered.

Future research directions

Although the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal malignancies has received increasing attention, its function remains complex and highly context dependent. The NLRP3 inflammasome may exert tumor-suppressive effects by promoting antitumor immunity, inducing inflammatory cell death, and maintaining epithelial barrier homeostasis [74]. However, it may also promote tumor progression by driving chronic inflammation, immune suppression, angiogenesis, tumor invasion and metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Therefore, future studies should define the precise functions of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal malignancies from multiple perspectives, including cell type, disease stage, tumor microenvironmental status, and treatment context. This will help translate NLRP3-related findings into clinically useful diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Although NLRP3 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target, its dual roles in cancer require precise and context-specific investigation. Conceptual framework explaining the dual roles of NLRP3 in gastrointestinal malignancies

A key challenge in interpreting the literature is that NLRP3 inflammasome activation can produce opposite biological outcomes in gastrointestinal malignancies [75]. This duality should not be viewed simply as inconsistency among studies, but rather as a reflection of the strong context dependence of this pathway. Several interrelated factors determine whether NLRP3 acts as a tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive regulator, including the cellular source of activation, tumor stage, duration and intensity of inflammasome signaling, downstream cytokine balance, immune composition of the tumor microenvironment, and treatment conditions [75]. First, the cellular source of NLRP3 activation is critical. When NLRP3 is persistently activated in chronically inflamed mucosa, tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, neutrophils, or injured epithelial tissues, it often amplifies IL-1β-driven inflammation, myeloid cell recruitment, immune suppression, epithelial barrier disruption, stromal remodeling, angiogenesis, and tumor progression [76]. This tumor-promoting pattern is particularly relevant to inflammation-associated gastrointestinal tumorigenesis, including colitis-associated colorectal cancer, bile reflux-related gastric injury, and microbiota dysbiosis-associated intestinal inflammation [14]. In these settings, NLRP3 functions mainly as an inflammatory amplifier that supports a tumor-permissive microenvironment.

By contrast, NLRP3 activation may exert tumor-suppressive effects when it occurs acutely in tumor cells under therapeutic stress. In this context, NLRP3 activation can promote caspase-1 activation, GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, antigen release, IL-18-dependent IFN-γ production, antigen presentation, and cytotoxic T-cell priming [77]. Therefore, during established tumor treatment, transient NLRP3 activation may enhance chemotherapy-, radiotherapy-, or immunotherapy-induced tumor cell death and strengthen antitumor immunity. This may also explain why NLRP3 activation can be advantageous when combined with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or immune checkpoint blockade, whereas NLRP3 inhibition may be more beneficial in tumors dominated by IL-1β-driven chronic inflammation and myeloid immunosuppression [78].

Second, tumor stage and activation dynamics strongly influence the biological outcome of NLRP3 signaling. During chronic gastritis, colitis, dysbiosis, or reflux-associated mucosal injury, low-grade and persistent inflammasome activation may promote epithelial damage, compensatory proliferation, stromal remodeling, and tumor initiation [79]. In contrast, strong and transient activation during anticancer therapy may induce immunogenic tumor cell death rather than long-term tumor-promoting inflammation. Third, the balance between downstream mediators is also important. IL-1β is frequently associated with angiogenesis, MDSC recruitment, TAM polarization, immune suppression, and tumor progression, whereas IL-18 may promote NK-cell and CD8⁺ T-cell activation in selected contexts [80]. Finally, the immune composition of the tumor microenvironment determines whether NLRP3-driven inflammation supports immune surveillance or immune escape. Therefore, future studies should move beyond evaluating total NLRP3 expression alone. Single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunofluorescence, and functional tumor-immune co-culture models should be used to define the cellular source, activation state, downstream effector profile, and immune context of NLRP3 signaling in gastrointestinal malignancies. Such analyses will be essential for determining when NLRP3 should be inhibited to suppress tumor-promoting inflammation and when it should be activated to enhance immunogenic cell death and antitumor immunity.

Complex interactions between the NLRP3 inflammasome and the tumor immune microenvironment

Future studies should further clarify how the NLRP3 inflammasome regulates the immune microenvironment of gastrointestinal tumors. After activation, the NLRP3 inflammasome promotes caspase-1 activation and induces the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18 [81]. These inflammatory cytokines can affect the recruitment, differentiation, and functional states of multiple immune cell populations. For example, the NLRP3-IL-1β axis may promote the infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and neutrophils, thereby creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment. In contrast, IL-18 can enhance IFN-γ production by NK cells and CD8⁺ T cells in certain contexts, which may support antitumor immunity. Thus, the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in tumor immune regulation is not unidirectional. It is shaped by tumor type, microenvironmental composition, and the intensity of inflammation. Several key questions remain to be answered. In which cell types is the NLRP3 inflammasome mainly activated: tumor cells, intestinal epithelial cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, or tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells? Does NLRP3 activation from different cellular sources produce distinct or even opposite effects? How do NLRP3-mediated IL-1β, IL-18, and pyroptotic signals influence T-cell exhaustion, immune checkpoint expression, antigen presentation, and response to immunotherapy? Addressing these questions is essential for understanding the true role of NLRP3 in the immune microenvironment of gastrointestinal tumors. Future studies should integrate single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunofluorescence, mass cytometry, and organoid-immune cell co-culture models. These approaches can help generate cell type-specific activation maps of the NLRP3 inflammasome in tumor tissues. By distinguishing NLRP3 activity across different cellular compartments, researchers can more accurately determine whether NLRP3 acts as a tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive factor and identify clearer cellular targets for therapy.

Crosstalk between the NLRP3 inflammasome and other forms of cell death

One of the classical downstream effects of the NLRP3 inflammasome is the induction of gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis. However, during tumor progression and treatment response, the NLRP3 inflammasome does not act in isolation. Instead, it interacts with several other forms of cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy-related cell death, cuproptosis, and PANoptosis. On the one hand, NLRP3 inflammasome activation can induce pyroptosis through the caspase-1-GSDMD axis. This process promotes inflammatory cytokine release and tumor antigen exposure, thereby enhancing immunogenic cell death. On the other hand, excessive or persistent pyroptosis may release large amounts of inflammatory mediators, which can promote chronic inflammation, tissue injury, and tumor progression. In parallel, signaling crosstalk may occur among apoptosis-associated caspases, the necroptosis-related RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway, and pyroptosis-related gasdermin family proteins. This crosstalk gives tumor cell death a high degree of plasticity. Future studies should determine how the NLRP3 inflammasome influences cell death decisions in gastrointestinal tumor cells and how different forms of cell death are interconverted. For example, during chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy, does NLRP3 activation promote tumor cell pyroptosis and antitumor immunity, or does it enhance therapeutic resistance through inflammatory cytokine release? Is NLRP3 functionally linked to ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial damage, and ROS accumulation? Does autophagy limit excessive NLRP3 activation by removing damaged mitochondria and inflammasome components? Answering these questions will improve our understanding of how cell death networks shape therapeutic outcomes. In addition, combined targeting of the NLRP3 inflammasome and other cell death pathways may become a new strategy to improve treatment sensitivity in gastrointestinal tumors. In specific tumor contexts, induction of immunogenic pyroptosis in tumor cells may enhance antitumor immunity. In contrast, in tumors driven by chronic inflammation, inhibition of NLRP3-mediated inflammatory cell death may help reduce a tumor-promoting inflammatory microenvironment. Therefore, NLRP3-related cell death should be precisely regulated according to disease stage and therapeutic goal.

NLRP3 inflammasome-activating signals in chronic inflammatory disease states

Gastrointestinal malignancies are often closely associated with long-term chronic inflammation. Representative examples include inflammatory bowel disease-associated colorectal cancer, Helicobacter pylori-associated gastric cancer, hepatobiliary inflammation-related tumors, and chronic pancreatitis-associated cancer. In these pathological settings, persistent tissue injury, epithelial barrier disruption, gut dysbiosis, oxidative stress, and abnormal immune cell infiltration can repeatedly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and promote the transition from inflammation to cancer. Future studies should dissect the key upstream signaling events that activate the NLRP3 inflammasome under chronic inflammatory conditions. These signals include PAMP- and DAMP-mediated TLR/NF-κB priming, ATP-P2X7-induced potassium efflux, mitochondrial ROS accumulation, mitochondrial DNA release, lysosomal damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, Golgi remodeling, metabolic abnormalities, and stimulation by bile acids or microbial metabolites. Different chronic inflammatory conditions may depend on distinct patterns of NLRP3 activation [82]. Therefore, NLRP3 should not be viewed as a single uniform inflammatory node. A particularly important point is that NLRP3 activation in chronic inflammation is often low-grade, persistent, and recurrent. This differs from the strong but transient activation observed during acute infection. Long-term NLRP3 activation may be more likely to induce DNA damage, abnormal epithelial proliferation, stromal remodeling, angiogenesis, and immune suppression, thereby creating a permissive environment for tumorigenesis. Future research should therefore establish models that better reflect clinical inflammation-to-cancer progression. These may include chronic colitis-associated cancer models, gastritis-intestinal metaplasia-gastric cancer progression models, and tumor organoid systems combined with microbial metabolite stimulation. Such models will help more accurately define the role of NLRP3 during chronic inflammation-associated malignant transformation.

Innovative therapeutic strategies targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome

Because the NLRP3 inflammasome lies at the intersection of inflammation, immunity, cell death, and tumor microenvironmental regulation, targeting NLRP3 has considerable therapeutic potential. However, NLRP3 has dual functions across different tumor stages and cell types. Therefore, future therapeutic strategies should not simply aim to completely inhibit NLRP3. Instead, they should focus on precise and context-dependent modulation. One important direction is the development of cell type-specific NLRP3 regulatory strategies. For example, inhibition of NLRP3-IL-1β signaling in tumor-associated macrophages or myeloid-derived suppressor cells may help relieve immune suppression and chronic inflammation. In contrast, induction of moderate pyroptosis in tumor cells may enhance antigen release and immune cell activation. Another direction is to combine NLRP3 modulation with existing treatment strategies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, gut microbiota intervention, and metabolic therapy.

Targeting key upstream or downstream nodes of NLRP3 is also feasible. Inhibition of P2X7 receptors, ROS generation, cathepsin B release, NEK7-NLRP3 interaction, or IL-1β/IL-18 signaling may reduce tumor-promoting inflammation at different levels. Conversely, in settings where stronger antitumor immunity is needed, induction of tumor cell pyroptosis or enhancement of inflammatory antigen release may improve the response to immunotherapy. The key challenge is to determine when, where, and in which cell types NLRP3 should be inhibited or activated. Nanodelivery systems, antibody-drug conjugates, tumor microenvironment-responsive drug release platforms, RNA interference, and CRISPR-based regulatory tools may provide new technical routes for precise NLRP3 targeting. For instance, nanoparticle-mediated delivery of NLRP3 inhibitors to tumor-associated macrophages may reduce systemic immune suppression and inflammatory side effects. In contrast, targeted induction of pyroptosis in tumor cells may increase therapeutic selectivity.

Current trends in the development of NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors

The development of NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors is shifting from traditional broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory agents toward more selective and mechanistically defined small-molecule inhibitors. Future studies need to further optimize the specificity, pharmacokinetic properties, tissue distribution, safety, and tumor indication selection of these inhibitors. A major trend is the direct targeting of the NLRP3 protein itself to inhibit its ATPase activity, conformational change, or oligomerization. Compared with blockade of downstream inflammatory cytokines, direct inhibition of NLRP3 may more effectively prevent inflammasome assembly and caspase-1 activation. Another strategy is to block the interaction between NLRP3 and key auxiliary factors, such as the NEK7-NLRP3 interaction, thereby interfering more precisely with inflammasome formation. Some agents also indirectly inhibit NLRP3 activation by regulating upstream signals, including ion channels, mitochondrial ROS, lysosomal damage, or NF-κB-mediated priming. However, several challenges remain when NLRP3 inhibitors are used in cancer therapy. First, NLRP3 inhibition may weaken host defense against infection and may also impair certain antitumor immune responses. Second, the function of NLRP3 may differ among gastrointestinal tumor types, meaning that the same inhibitor may not be suitable for all patients. Third, reliable biomarkers that predict the efficacy of NLRP3 inhibitors are still lacking. Future studies should integrate multiple markers to establish more reliable patient stratification systems. These markers may include NLRP3 expression, ASC speck formation, caspase-1 activity, IL-1β/IL-18 levels, pyroptosis markers, immune cell infiltration patterns, and gut microbiota features. In addition, combination strategies using NLRP3 inhibitors with immunotherapy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy deserve further investigation. In some tumors, NLRP3 inhibition may reduce myeloid inflammation and immune suppression, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. In other contexts, however, excessive inhibition of inflammasome activity may weaken therapy-induced immunogenic cell death. Therefore, preclinical models and clinical sample analyses are needed to define the optimal therapeutic window and combination regimens for NLRP3 inhibitors.

Development and optimization of individualized therapies targeting NLRP3

Because the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal malignancies is highly dependent on tumor type, disease stage, cellular source, immune status, and gut microbiota environment, individualized therapy will be an important direction for future development. Unlike traditional “one-size-fits-all” anti-inflammatory therapy, individualized NLRP3-targeted strategies should first define the activation status of the NLRP3 pathway in each patient’s tumor and clarify its functional consequences. In the future, multidimensional biomarker-based patient stratification systems could be established. For example, NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, GSDMD-N, IL-1β, and IL-18 levels could be measured in tumor tissues and peripheral blood. These markers should be combined with immune infiltration features, including the proportions of CD8⁺ T cells, Treg cells, tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and neutrophils. At the same time, gut microbiota composition, metabolite profiles, inflammatory cytokine signatures, and treatment response status should also be assessed. Together, these indicators may help classify patients into NLRP3 pro-inflammatory/tumor-promoting, NLRP3 immune-activating, or NLRP3-low activity subtypes. Different therapeutic strategies could then be selected accordingly. In terms of treatment optimization, future studies should focus on several key issues: identifying which patients are suitable for NLRP3 inhibitor therapy; defining the best timing for combining NLRP3 inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted agents; evaluating the impact of long-term NLRP3 inhibition on infection risk, intestinal homeostasis, and tissue repair; and developing delivery systems that selectively target specific cell populations within the tumor microenvironment to reduce systemic toxicity. In addition, patient-derived organoids, tumor-immune co-culture systems, humanized mouse models, and single-cell multi-omics analyses can be used to predict individual responses to NLRP3-targeted therapy. These platforms can more faithfully model patient-specific tumor architecture, immune microenvironment, and drug response. They may therefore provide an experimental basis for designing individualized therapeutic strategies.

Genetic alterations of NLRP3 inflammasome-related genes in gastrointestinal tumors

In addition to transcriptional, post-transcriptional, post-translational, metabolic, and microenvironmental regulation, genetic alterations in NLRP3 inflammasome-related genes represent an important but relatively underexplored area in human cancer. Public cancer genomics resources, such as TCGA and cBioPortal, provide useful platforms for evaluating somatic mutations, copy-number alterations, mRNA expression changes, and clinical correlations of inflammasome-related genes across tumor types [83]. In gastrointestinal malignancies, alterations in genes encoding inflammasome sensors, adaptor proteins, effector caspases, gasdermins, and downstream cytokines, including NLRP3, PYCARD, CASP1, GSDMD, IL1B, and IL18, may potentially influence inflammasome activity, inflammatory cytokine production, pyroptotic capacity, immune infiltration, and treatment response. Previous studies have suggested that somatic alterations in NLR family genes can be detected in gastric and colorectal cancers, although their frequency, functional significance, and clinical implications may vary among tumor types and molecular subgroups. Therefore, future studies should systematically integrate cancer genomic datasets with transcriptomic, proteomic, single-cell, spatial, and functional experimental analyses to determine whether genetic alterations in NLRP3 inflammasome-related genes contribute to gastrointestinal tumor initiation, progression, immune escape, or therapeutic sensitivity. Such analyses may also help identify patient subgroups that are more likely to benefit from NLRP3-targeted or inflammasome-modulating therapies.

Clinical trials and translational advances involving the NLRP3 inflammasome

Clinical translation of NLRP3 inflammasome modulation is still at an early stage, but increasing clinical and preclinical studies have begun to evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway. A current clinical trial, “NLRP3 Inflammasome and Physical Therapy in ICU-Acquired Weakness (NCT07017517), is designed to determine whether enhanced physical therapy can reduce NLRP3 inflammasome activation and skeletal muscle atrophy in patients with critical illness myopathy. Although this trial is not cancer-specific, it highlights the growing clinical interest in monitoring and modulating NLRP3 inflammasome activity in human disease. In parallel, several NLRP3 inhibitors have shown encouraging translational potential in inflammatory, metabolic, and neurological disease models. VTX3232, an oral, selective, and brain-penetrant NLRP3 inhibitor, suppresses the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-18, IL-1α, IL-6, and TNF, from macrophages and microglia exposed to metabolic stressors, and improves systemic and tissue-specific inflammation in diet-induced obesity models [84]. Similarly, the clinical-stage NLRP3 inhibitors NT-0249 and NT-0796 reverse high-fat diet-induced obesity, systemic inflammation, and hypothalamic gliosis in mice, with brain exposure appearing important for efficacy [85]. OLT1177/dapansutrile, a selective NLRP3 inhibitor reported to be safe in humans, also reduces IL-1β and IL-18 production, attenuates immune cell infiltration, and ameliorates disease severity in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, a mouse model of multiple sclerosis [86].

In oncology, clinical exploration of NLRP3 inflammasome modulation is also emerging. BMS-986,299, an NLRP3 inflammasome agonist, has been evaluated in a phase I clinical trial as intratumoral monotherapy and in combination with nivolumab and ipilimumab in patients with advanced solid tumors [87]. This strategy reflects a different therapeutic concept from NLRP3 inhibition, aiming to activate local innate immune signaling and enhance antitumor immunity. However, clinical trials specifically designed to target the NLRP3 inflammasome in gastrointestinal malignancies remain limited. Therefore, future studies should determine whether NLRP3 inhibition or activation can be rationally combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, microbiota-directed therapy, or anti-inflammatory strategies in colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, esophageal cancer, and other gastrointestinal tumors. Importantly, future clinical trial design should incorporate biomarker-based patient selection, including NLRP3 pathway activity, IL-1β/IL-18 levels, caspase-1 activation, GSDMD cleavage, immune cell infiltration, systemic inflammatory markers, and gut microbiota features, to identify patients most likely to benefit from NLRP3 inflammasome modulation.

Conclusion

The NLRP3 inflammasome is an important molecular platform that connects chronic inflammation, cell death, the tumor immune microenvironment, and therapeutic response. In gastrointestinal malignancies, its functions are highly complex and context dependent. On the one hand, sustained or aberrant NLRP3 activation can promote the initiation and progression of esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer by inducing IL-1β/IL-18 release, myeloid cell recruitment, epithelial barrier disruption, immune suppression, and tumor-associated inflammation. On the other hand, under specific therapeutic conditions, NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in tumor cells can enhance antigen release, promote immune activation, and improve the efficacy of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Current evidence indicates that the function of NLRP3 cannot be simply defined as tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive. Instead, it depends on tumor type, disease stage, activated cellular source, inflammatory intensity, and treatment context. In chronic inflammatory and myeloid immunosuppressive environments, inhibition of the NLRP3/IL-1β axis may help block inflammation-driven tumor progression. In contrast, in settings where antitumor immunity needs to be enhanced, moderate induction of NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis in tumor cells may increase tumor immunogenicity and therapeutic sensitivity. Therefore, future NLRP3-targeted strategies should emphasize precise modulation rather than simple inhibition or activation. Integration of single-cell omics, spatial transcriptomics, tumor organoids, immune co-culture models, and patient-derived samples will help clarify the real functions of NLRP3 in different cell populations and identify patients who may benefit from NLRP3-targeted therapy. Overall, the NLRP3 inflammasome is not only a key mechanistic node in inflammation-associated malignant transformation, but also a promising target for improving immunotherapy, overcoming therapeutic resistance, and developing individualized treatments for gastrointestinal malignancies.
