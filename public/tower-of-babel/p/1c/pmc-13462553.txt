A single-cell atlas of alternative wing development in two hemipteran species
Authors: Yi Wan, Hui-Jie Wu, Heng-Guang Huang, Zhuo-Qi Liu, Zhao-Xiang Sun, Zhang-Nv Yang, Hai-Jun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsect wing polyphenism enables a single genome to produce distinct wing morphs in response to environmental cues, yet its underlying cellular determinants remain elusive. Here, we perform single-cell RNA sequencing of long-winged- and short-winged-destined wing buds of Pyrrhocoris apterus and Nilaparvata lugens, identifying six conserved cell types with comparable proportions between the two morphs. RNA interference-mediated silencing of 51 marker genes indicates that wing-patterning genes En (epithelial-like cells) and bs (tracheal cells), and cell-cycle genes Anln, CycB3, and cdk1 (neuron cells), are essential for long-winged development, among which En exhibits a specific temporal requirement. Flow cytometry analysis shows that long-winged formation mainly relies on an extended duration of cell proliferation. Cross-species comparisons indicate shared wing cell identities. Our findings indicate that hemipteran short-winged morphs may evolve from ancestral long-winged forms via precise regulation of wing-patterning and cell-cycle gene expression in epithelial‑like, tracheal, and neuron cells. This provides insights into the developmental plasticity of insect tissues at single‑cell resolution.Subject terms: Evolutionary developmental biology, Entomology, Morphogenesis

Introduction

Phenotypic plasticity, the capacity of a single genotype to exhibit variable phenotypes in response to environmental cues, is common in insects1. Discrete morphological polyphenisms are well-known examples of this concept, where notable cases include the castes of social insects (e.g., honey bees, ants, and termites), the head horn plasticity of beetles, the seasonal forms of butterflies, and the alternative wing morphs in a range of insects. Wing polyphenism is one of the most extensively studied examples of phenotypic plasticity, where long-winged (LW), short-winged (SW), or wingless adults develop within the same species2,3. Evolutionarily, the SW and wingless morphs likely originated from an initially winged ancestor4–6. In insect populations exhibiting wing polyphenism, the LW morphs have fully developed wings, making them capable of long-distance migration to escape deteriorating environments and colonize new niches. By contrast, the SW or wingless morphs are obligate flightless because of their underdeveloped wings, but usually exhibit a fitness trade-off between flight capability and reproduction. Thus, wing polyphenism serves as a crucial determinant of adaptive success in heterogeneous environments, demonstrating how phenotypic plasticity resolves ecological paradoxes through context-dependent fitness maximization7.

Extensive research on wing polyphenism has predominantly focused on hemipteran insects, with planthoppers, true bugs, and aphids emerging as pivotal model systems for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying this adaptive polyphenism. The insulin/insulin-like signaling (IIS) pathway is assumed to positively modulate growth and development in response to nutrients across metazoans8–12. Emerging evidence indicates that IIS is co-opted to play a widespread role in regulating insect polyphenisms, including mandible growth in the soldiers of termites, and horn plasticity in males of rhinoceros beetles13–15. Genetic manipulations identified the IIS pathway as a key regulator in controlling the development of alternative wing morphs across Hemiptera16–20. In two representative hemipteran taxa from the Heteroptera and Auchenorrhyncha suborders—the European firebug Pyrrhocoris apterus and the brown planthopper Nilaparvata lugens, respectively (Fig. 1a) - nymphs bear bipotential wing buds that can develop into either SW or LW adults; however, these wing buds are indistinguishable externally during this nymph stage, becoming visible only once the adults emerge (Fig. 1b, c). In the firebug, RNA interference (RNAi)-mediated knockdown or CRISPR-Cas9-based knockout of either one of two insulin receptors (PaInR1a and PaInR2) enables bipotential wing buds to develop into long wings19,21. In the planthopper, dysfunction of genes acting positively (e.g., NlInR1) within the IIS pathway leads to short wings, whereas dysfunction of genes acting negatively (e.g., NlInR2 and NlFoxO) has the opposite effect, leading to long wings20,22. Further studies suggest that the IIS pathway might regulate wing growth through DNA replication and cell proliferation21,23,24. However, despite extensive research elucidating the genetic regulators of wing polyphenism in hemipteran insects, little is known about the cellular determinants governing the development of alternative wing morphs.

Most understanding of insect wing cellular composition originates from the model organism Drosophila melanogaster. In this species, the wing disc forms a sac-like structure and is commonly subdivided into the pouch/hinge region, the notum region, and the peripodial membrane region based on the expression of landmark genes25–27. Wing cells can be functionally categorized into several cell types, such as epithelial-like cells (ECs), myoblasts, tracheal cells (TCs), as well as a small subset of neurons (NCs) and glia (GCs) cells26,28–30. The cell types and their regional identities arise through progressively refined patterning during development30. Despite this complexity, systematic identification of wing cell types and the developmental trajectories that specify their regional fates remains limited. Recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has offered a powerful approach to decipher the cellular composition and gene expression programs of developing organs across insects, such as in the yellow fever mosquito Aedes aegypti31, silkworm Bombyx mori32–35, honeybee Apis mellifera36,37, and American cockroach Periplaneta americana38. In terms of wing development, only partial cellular landscapes have been captured in Drosophila wings. Specifically, an atlas of either muscle cells (MCs)27, MCs and ECs26,39, MCs, ECs, and TCs25, or MCs, NCs, GCs, and hemocyte (HEs)40 was constructed from Drosophila wings in each study. In addition, the genetic and developmental identities of wing scale cells were characterized in the butterfly Bicyclus anynana through scRNA-seq41. Despite the accumulated information, the molecular mechanisms governing cellular heterogeneity in LW and SW morphs in wing-polyphenic insects remain uncharacterized.

In this work, we performed scRNA‑seq on LW- and SW-destined wing buds from the firebug P. apterus and the planthopper N. lugens. We constructed high-resolution cellular atlases for both LW- and SW-destined wing buds, and systematically delineated major cell types as well as their associated marker genes. Through integrated analyses, including RNAi-mediated gene silencing and flow cytometry, we identified key cell types and regulatory genes essential for long-wing development. Cross-species comparative transcriptomic analysis indicated evolutionarily conserved cell type architectures between P. apterus and N. lugens. By integrating single-cell resolution with functional validation, our work suggests a foundational framework for understanding how genetic factors coordinate cell fate decisions to produce alternative wing morphs in insects.

Results

Single-cell atlas of SW- and LW-destined wing buds in the firebug

In the firebug, the fifth-instar stage (the last nymphal stage) is the key developmental window for bipotential wing buds to develop into either short wings or long wings21. To uncover the heterogeneity of SW- and LW-destined wing cells, we dissected wing buds from fifth-instar wild-type (Wt) and PaInR2-mutant (PaInR2De4) firebugs for scRNA-seq analysis using the 10× Genomics platform (Fig. 2a). The Wt is considered to be SW-destined because at least 99% of emerged adults within the firebug population have short wings. The PaInR2De4 mutants were generated by CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing of PaInR2, resulting in 100% LW morphs via redirecting the developmental trajectory from short to long wings21. Owing to the lack of an LW-destined Wt population, the PaInR2De4 mutants were used as a surrogate. In total, 51,381 and 55,417 high-quality cells were retained from three replicates of Wt and PaInR2De4 fifth-instar nymphs, respectively (Fig. 2b). After quality control, the single-cell datasets from Wt and PaInR2De4 wings were integrated to mitigate batch effects. Subsequent clustering analysis identified 23 distinct cell clusters, as visualized using the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithm (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

Cell composition of SW- and LW-destined wings

To identify cell types of SW- and LW-destined wing buds, we categorized the 23 distinct cell clusters by applying dimensionality reduction algorithms. The clusters were subsequently characterized by marker genes that were significantly enriched in each cluster (Supplementary Data 1). Single-cell profiles from Wt and PaInR2De4 wing buds were co-embedded in uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) space for joint visualization of wing cell types (Fig. 2c). In parallel, we generated separate UMAP spaces for Wt and PaInR2De4 wing buds to show the reliability of biological replicates in each sample, which indicated that Wt and PaInR2De4 wing buds had a similar cell atlas and shared six common cell types including ECs, TCs, NCs, GCs, HEs, and MCs (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The six cell types identified in the firebug encompassed all cell types that were previously identified from Drosophila wings26,27,40. Notably, Wt and PaInR2De4 wing buds shared an identical proportion of the ECs, TCs and NCs, which represent the top three major cell types, together accounting for more than 90% of the total wing cells (Fig. 2d). However, the proportion of GCs and HEs showed a small but significant difference between Wt and PaInR2De4 (Fig. 2d), together accounting for less than 10% of the total wing cells.

Next, we examined the expression of all 30 marker genes for each cell type (Fig. 2e). ECs were characterized by the expression of four genes (Nub, Cht2, Egfr, and Axin), among which Nub is a patterning gene that regulates the asymmetric division of cells during development42. TCs were marked by seven genes (trh, bs, dar1, Cad89D, Cyp301a1, NT1, and Sb-sbd). Both trh and bs have been reported to participate in TC fate determination in Drosophila, and bs also functions as a wing-patterning gene27,43,44. NCs exhibited expression of genes encoding key neuronal markers, such as hdc, FAS1, para, CAC, ACE, Rdl, Anln, CycB3, and cdk1, among which four genes (para, CAC, ACE, and Rdl) are well recognized as neuronal markers in insects40. In addition, three genes (CycB3, cdk1, and Anln) are involved in cell cycle processes45,46. GCs showed elevated expression of Hgsnat, Abcb1a, GUSB, and Spon1. In Drosophila, the deficiency of Hgsnat causes dysfunction in glial cells, resulting in impaired climbing ability and reduced activity47. HEs were marked by the expression of genes such as LanA, Npc2a, and gcm. Previous studies indicated that gcm is expressed in embryonic hemocyte precursors and is required for specifying plasmatocyte fate48. MCs were distinguished by three genes that were previously identified as MC markers: Mef2, a master transcription factor (TF) required for muscle differentiation49; Mlc2, a structural component contributing to sarcomere structure and muscle contraction50; and sing, an essential regulator of myoblast fusion51 (Supplementary Data 1). In all, these events reinforce the biological validity of the cell type classifications from firebug wings.

To further characterize the six cell types, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each cell type between PaInR2De4 and Wt samples. From the scRNA-seq dataset, 1960, 3026, 2944, 2091, 2169, and 2007 DEGs were identified in ECs, TCs, NCs, GCs, HEs, and MCs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Among these, DEGs in ECs, TCs, NCs, GCs, HEs, and MCs were predicted to be regulated by 78, 78, 79, 72, 75, and 72 TFs (Supplementary Data 2), with 71 TFs common to all six cell types. Transcriptional regulatory network (TRN) analysis of the top 10 TFs indicated that most DEGs are modulated by shared TFs (Supplementary Fig. 2b–g). To distinguish functional differences between cell types, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. Among the top 20 GO terms, DEGs were significantly enriched in 13 GO terms (e.g., membrane-bounded organelle, intracellular organelle, and biological regulation) that substantially overlapped across cell types (Supplementary Fig. 3). Different from GO analysis, no common KEGG pathways were found across the six cell types. In both ECs and TCs, the ribosome and the spliceosome were the two top-ranked pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). In NCs, DNA replication is the most enriched term, followed by ribosome and cellular senescence (Supplementary Fig. 4c). In GCs, top-ranked pathways included protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, N-glycan biosynthesis, and mitophagy (Supplementary Fig. 4d). In HEs, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, and mitophagy were significantly enriched in addition to ribosome and spliceosome (Supplementary Fig. 4e). In MCs, the top three pathways were oxidative phosphorylation, thermogenesis, and cell cycle (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Collectively, these findings suggest that wing plasticity in the firebug arises from subtle changes in transcriptional regulation.

To validate the reliability of the scRNA-seq data, we generated a bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) dataset from Wt and PaInR2De4 wing buds, and then conducted cross-platform comparison between bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq. We found a moderate-to-strong positive relationship (Pearson’s r = 0.66) between bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq (pseudo-bulk), indicating that bulk RNA-seq profiles demonstrated substantial concordance with the transcriptomic patterns observed in scRNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. 5a). In addition, all marker genes that were identified from scRNA-seq also exhibited robust expression in the bulk RNA-seq dataset (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Together, the consistency of gene expression profiles across the two sequencing platforms indicates that the scRNA-seq dataset accurately captures the overall transcriptional landscape of the wing buds.

Diversification of epithelial-like cells

Given that ECs represent the most abundant cell type of firebug wings, we categorized their internal heterogeneity by subdividing the EC population. We generated four transcriptionally distinct subsets (EC0–EC3) as visualized by UMAP (Fig. 3a). EC0 cells are characterized by the combined expression of eight genes (Rab32, Hlf, Shd, AkR1B1, HAO1, Trf, Chac1, and Tret1). EC1 cells were characterized by five highly-expressed genes (En, Hr38, Ugt2B17, ara and dpp4). EC2 cells showed combined expression of four genes (Unc-22, Wnt-1, Toll-7, and Fj), as did EC3 cells (Salm, Exd, Zfhx4, and Mab21) (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Data 3). The distinct expression pattern of the 21 marker genes for EC0–EC3 indicates the unique internal heterogeneity of the EC population.

Interestingly, a specific cluster was identified exclusively in EC0 of Wt (Fig. 3a). To examine whether this Wt-specific cluster arose from sample bias or batch effects, we generated individual UMAP projections for each of the three biological replicates within every sample. The Wt-specific cluster was identified in all three Wt replicates instead of in the PaInR2De4 samples (Supplementary Fig. 6a), indicating that these cells are unique to SW-destined wings. To determine whether the Wt-specific cluster represented a distinct EC subtype, we performed differential expression analysis to identify its marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Although the most highly expressed genes (such as Pgk, elF5, and ETFB) showed elevated average expression within this cluster, their expression percentages were notably low (mostly <20%; Supplementary Fig. 6b), falling below the established thresholds for defining a cell type in scRNA-seq studies52. Intriguingly, the Wt-specific cells were still present even if we raised the resolution for cell clustering (Supplementary Fig. 7). Collectively, these data support the cell-type classification, in which Wt-specific cells are categorized as the EC0 subtype.

To uncover the specific biological function of EC0–EC3, we conducted functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs across the individual subsets. As the dominant sub-cluster within the EC population, EC0 contained upregulated-genes that were functionally enriched in pigmentation, amino acid catabolic processes, and plasma membrane composition (Supplementary Fig. 8a). In EC1, upregulated DEGs were related to epithelial development and wing-disc morphogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 8b). In EC2, upregulated DEGs were involvement in cuticle development and chitin metabolic processes (Supplementary Fig. 8c). In EC3, upregulated DEGs were primarily associated with cell fate commitment, cell differentiation, and regulation of developmental process (Supplementary Fig. 8d). These events might underscore the crucial roles of each sub-cluster of ECs in the formation and patterning of the wing disc epithelium. We subsequently investigated the relative proportion of EC0–EC3 between Wt and PaInR2De4 wing buds. SW- and LW-destined wing buds exhibited similar proportions of EC0–EC3 subsets (Fig. 3c), suggesting that the proportion of EC subsets does not serve as a primary determinant in the development of alternative wing morphs.

Knockdown of an EC marker gene, En, affects the plastic development of wings

To investigate whether ECs contribute to the differentiation of wing morphs, we performed RNAi-mediated gene silencing of the 21 marker genes for EC0–EC3. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis showed that the transcript levels of all targeted genes were significantly reduced following RNAi treatment compared to the Gfp-RNAi control group, demonstrating highly effective gene silencing for subsequent phenotypic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9). Four distinct phenotypes were obtained from RNAi-based phenotypic analyses: no morphological changes, lethality, hypopigmentation, and alteration of wing development. We found that knockdown of 10 genes (HAO1, Trf, Chac1, Tret1, ara, Ugt2B17, dpp4, Wnt-1, Fj, and Mab21) had no effects on morphology, whereas knockdown of another eight genes (AkR1B1, Shd, Hr38, Unc-22, Toll-7, Salm, Exd, and Zfhx4) resulted in varying degrees of lethality (Supplementary Data 4). The hypopigmentation phenotype (the third phenotype) was attributed to two marker genes (Rab32 and Hlf). Knockdown of Rab32 or Hlf in the context of PaInR2De4 induced pronounced hypopigmentation in both the body integument and corium region of the forewings compared with Gfp-RNAi (Fig. 4a–c), indicating that the two genes are involved in wing color polymorphism in these insects. The Rab32-RNAi phenotype in the firebug is consistent with the previous finding in Drosophila, where mutations in Rab-RP1 (an ortholog of Rab32) led to a lightoid phenotype, manifesting as hypopigmentation of the eye53. Interestingly, Hlf-RNAi-treated nymphs had a prolonged fifth-instar nymphal stage but were unable to molt to adults and eventually died. Given that wing buds have already developed well but are folded under the cuticle at the late fifth-instar stage, we dissected wing buds from Hlf-RNAi-treated nymphs for morphological examination. The corium region of Hlf-RNAi-wing was transparent compared with the reddish wing buds of Gfp-RNAi-treated nymphs (Fig. 4c). Given the important role of Hlf in organogenesis, tissue development, and regulation of the PI3K-AKT pathway54, our findings suggest that this TF has additional functions to those previously reported. Next, we examined the expression pattern of Rab32 and Hlf in wing buds using in situ hybridization (ISH). Rab32 was ubiquitously expressed in the corium region and specifically expressed along with the vein position in the membrane region (Fig. 4d). Unlike Rab32, the distribution of Hlf was restricted to the corium region (Fig. 4d). Together, the expression pattern of Rab32 and Hlf is in line with their function in decoloration of the corium region of forewings.

The fourth distinct phenotype (alteration of wing development) was observed in En-RNAi-treated adults. The homeodomain-containing TF En was characterized as a nuclear regulator that specifies posterior compartment identity in Drosophila wing discs55,56. In the firebug, En-RNAi in PaInR2De4 nymphs (LW-destined) induced a complete metamorphic switch to SW adults (Fig. 4e), which contrasted sharply with Gfp-RNAi-treated LW adults (Fig. 4a). Quantification of wing area confirmed that En-RNAi significantly decreased both forewing and hindwing size in PaInR2De4 adults compared to Gfp-RNAi (Supplementary Fig. 10). To investigate whether En is also required for short wing development, Wt nymphs (SW-destined) were used for En-RNAi. This approach showed no apparent difference between En-RNAi and Gfp-RNAi in terms of wing size (Fig. 4f, g, Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore, ISH assays revealed that En was vein-specifically expressed in both corium and membrane regions of fifth-instar nymphs, with strong signals in veins of the posterior compartment (Fig. 4h). Overall, these findings imply that En shows a temporal requirement specifically for the plastic development of wings.

In addition to the 21 marker genes of EC0–EC3, we further investigated the phenotype resulted from knockdown of four marker genes (Nub, Egfr, Cht2 and Axin) in the EC cell type (Fig. 2e). Knockdown of Nub, but not the other three genes, caused a severe wing size reduction in PaInR2De4 (Supplementary Fig. 10), with the most pronounced shrinkage observed in the corium region compared with the Gfp-RNAi control (Fig. 4i). To investigate whether Nub affects the corium region in SW-destined nymphs, we conducted Nub-RNAi in Wt nymphs. Consistent with the phenotype from Nub-RNAi-treated PaInR2De4, Nub-RNAi also reduced the corium size in Wt adults (Fig. 4j, Supplementary Fig. 10c). The phenotype parallels that of the milkweed bug Oncopeltus fasciatus57. These results indicate that Nub differs from En in wing size control: the latter is more likely to regulate long wing development, while the former is required for the development of the corium region in both SW and LW morphs. In addition, ISH assays showed that Nub was predominantly expressed in the corium region of forewings (Fig. 4k). Given that the corium region is comparable in both SW and LW Wt firebugs, it is more likely that Nub is implicated in general corium development rather than specifically in the plastic development of LW wings.

TCs and NCs contribute to the development of alternative wings

We assessed the contribution of the other five cell types (TCs, NCs, GCs, HEs, and MCs; Fig. 2e) to wing-morph alteration via RNAi targeting corresponding marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 11). Except for a TC marker bs and three NCs marker genes (Anln, CycB3, and cdk1), knockdown of the remaining 22 marker genes either had no effects on wing morphology or led to lethality (Supplementary Data 4), suggesting that GCs, HEs, and MCs have a marginal role in the development of alternative wing morphs. By contrast, knockdown of bs, Anln, CycB3, and cdk1 in fifth-instar PaInR2De4 nymphs (LW-destined) led to SW morphs (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 10a, b) compared with LW morphs resulting from Gfp-RNAi treatments (Fig. 4a). In parallel, knockdown of bs, Anln, CycB3, and cdk1 in Wt nymphs (SW-destined) resulted in even smaller wings (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 10c, d) than the short wings in Gfp-RNAi controls (Fig. 4g), indicating that the four genes are required for normal wing development. This phenotype is distinct from the normal short wing observed in Wt nymphs upon En knockdown. Notably, bs-RNAi led to a high mortality during the nymph–adult transition and successfully emerged adults exhibited blisters on both their forewings and hindwings, which is coincident with the finding in Drosophila following bs knockdown58. Beyond the wing phenotypes, we observed that bs-RNAi also resulted in structural defects and blistering on the pronotum and scutellum (Supplementary Fig. 12), which is consistent with the phenotype derived from bs knockdown in the milkweed bug O. fasciatus57. Furthermore, ISH assays revealed that CycB3 and cdk1 were broadly expressed in wing cells (Fig. 5c). By contrast, bs was predominantly expressed in the intervein sector of the membrane region (Fig. 5c), which is in line with the shrunken membrane region in bs-RNAi firebug adults. Unfortunately, no specific Anln signals were detected in wing tissues despite using optimized anti-Anln probes in ISH assays. CycB3 and cdk1 are required for a crucial transition in cell division, where CycB3 functions to activate cdk1 during the G2/M transition phases, facilitating mitotic progression45,59. In Drosophila, Anln has been reported as a conserved component of the contractile ring essential for cytokinesis60, where bs is required for intervein cell differentiation61. Collectively, it is plausible that bipotential wing buds might use the mitotic machinery for long wing growth.

LW-destined wing buds prolong cell proliferation

Given that cell proliferation contributes significantly to long wing development21 and that three cell cycle genes (Anln, CycB3, and cdk1) were identified from scRNA-seq for a proper patterning of both long and short wings, we used flow cytometry analysis to analyze cell cycle distribution during the late fifth-instar stage of Wt and PaInR2De4 nymphs. After excluding debris and aggregated cells, we obtained intact wing cells (Fig. 6a), in which doublets and multiplets were further removed to obtain a clean single-cell population with ~10,000 events (Fig. 6b). The histogram of DNA content revealed a typical bimodal distribution of the clean single-cell population, with well-resolved peaks corresponding to the G0/G1 and G2/M populations, separated by an intermediate S-phase region. This profile enabled precise identification and quantification of each cell cycle phase using the Watson Pragmatic model, as exemplified by a representative PaInR2De4 sample (Fig. 6c). In both Wt and PaInR2De4, more than 60% wing cells were arrested at the G0/G1 phase (Fig. 6d). However, PaInR2De4 contained a significantly higher proportion of wing cells at the S and G2/M phases compared with Wt (Fig. 6d, e).

To confirm the cell proliferation activity of wings in late fifth-instar PaInR2De4 nymphs, we assessed cell numbers by measuring genomic DNA (gDNA) copy number using quantitative PCR (qPCR). PaInR2De4 and Wt nymphs had a comparable number of gDNA copies at the early fifth-instar stage (3 days after eclosion). However, at 5 days and 7 days after fifth-instar eclosion, PaInR2De4 nymphs contained a significantly higher number of gDNA copies compared with Wt (Fig. 6f). We then monitored DNA replication by EdU staining in PaInR2De4 and Wt wing buds. EdU is a thymidine analog that can be incorporated into cellular DNA to label proliferating cells. When 72–96 h fifth-instar nymphs were used for EdU microinjection, EdU signals were readily detected in both PaInR2De4 and Wt, implying that wing cell proliferation occurs in both SW- and LW-destined morphs during this developmental period (Fig. 6g). However, EdU signals were only detected in PaInR2De4 instead of Wt when 96–120 h fifth-instar nymphs were microinjected with EdU, indicating that wing cell proliferation terminates in SW-destined wing buds, but continues in LW-destined wing buds. To confirm the contribution of cell proliferation to long-wing development, we injected fifth-instar PaInR2De4 nymphs with Hydroxyurea, an agent that inhibits DNA synthesis by targeting ribonucleoside diphosphate reductase. In the Hydroxyurea-treated group, 70% (n = 10) of PaInR2De4 nymphs developed into adults with short wings (Fig. 6h), whereas all PaInR2De4 nymphs injected with the control solution developed long wings.

To determine whether the 30 marker genes for the six major cell types (Fig. 2e) contribute to the prolonged cell proliferation observed in LW-destined wing buds, we examined their expression levels in the scRNA-seq data from PaInR2De4 and Wt wing buds. We found that 28 of 30 marker genes were differentially expressed between PaInR2De4 and Wt wings (Supplementary Fig. 13). Especially, three cell cycle-associated genes (Anln, CycB3, and cdk1) were significantly upregulated in PaInR2De4 compared with Wt wings. This finding aligns with the extended wing cell proliferation in PaInR2De4 detected by flow cytometry analysis. Collectively, these findings indicate that long-wing development in the firebug is mainly the result of prolonged cell proliferation at the final nymphal stage, which is the key development window for bipotential wing buds to develop into either short or long wings.

Evolutionary conservation of cell types between the firebug and the planthopper

To investigate the potential evolutionary conservation of wing cell types underlying alternative wing development across Hemiptera, we performed scRNA-seq on the planthopper N. lugens. We dissected wing buds from fifth-instar Wt and NlInR2E4 nymphs for scRNA-seq analysis (Fig. 7a). Wt nymphs were considered to be SW-destined because more than 90% SW adults developed within the population. The NlInR2E4 nymphs were loss-of-function NlInR2 mutants, fated to develop into LW adults by redirecting the developmental trajectory from short to long wings24. Using similar approaches to those described for the firebug, we obtained 29,889 and 21,769 high-quality cells from Wt and NlInR2E4 planthoppers, respectively (Fig. 7b). In total, 17 distinct cell clusters were identified using the t-SNE algorithm (Fig. 7c). Cell composition was subsequently characterized and cluster-specific marker genes were identified (Fig. 7d, Supplementary Data 5). Identical to the firebug, six major cell types (ECs, TCs, NCs, GCs, HEs, and MCs) were identified in wing buds of the planthopper (Fig. 7e), among which ECs, TCs, and NCs were the three most abundant cell types in Wt and NlInR2E4 (Fig. 7f). The LW-destined NlInR2E4 shared an identical proportion of cell types with the Wt planthopper (SW-destined) except for the NC cluster (Fig. 7f), which showed a slight but significant difference between the two morphs.

We next investigated the conservation of cell types between the firebug and planthopper by aligning marker genes in the two species, revealing 18 marker genes common to both species (Fig. 7d, arrowheads): five of eight genes were in ECs, two of eight genes were in TCs, seven of eight genes occurred in NCs, one of three genes occurred in both GCs and HEs, and two of four genes occurred in MCs. Notably, similar to the firebug, the wing-patterning gene En and cell cycle gene cdk1 were identified as marker genes for ECs and NCs in the planthopper, respectively. To further validate the conservation of cell types between the firebug and planthopper, we conducted cross-species analysis based on the top 50 genes that are enriched in each cell type. The top 50 genes exhibited remarkably consistent correlation between the two species, with those in MCs exhibiting the highest consistency values (>0.95), followed by NCs and HEs (Fig. 7g, Supplementary Data 6). Taken together, the evolutionary stability of wing cell identities between the firebug and planthopper suggests that the two species evolved wing polyphenism by regulating the same cell types.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized the composition and cellular heterogeneity of LW- and SW-destined wing buds in two representative wing-polyphenic insects, the firebug P. apterus and the planthopper N. lugens. Wing buds of the firebug can be classified into six distinct cell types (ECs, TCs, NCs, GCs, HEs, and MCs), characterized by 51 cell type-specific marker genes. The LW- and SW-destined wing buds shared similar proportions of the six cell types, among which ECs, TCs, and NCs together accounted for more than 90% of the total cells. RNAi-mediated gene silencing assays demonstrated that one EC marker gene (En), one TC marker gene (bs), and three NC marker genes (Anln, CycB3, and cdk1) had a pivotal role in long-wing development. In addition, flow cytometry analysis indicated that extended cell proliferation duration contributed significantly to long-wing development, which was in agreement with the phenotype derived from knockdown of cell cycle genes (Anln, CycB3, and cdk1). Finally, cross-species comparative analysis revealed that the firebug and the planthopper shared conserved wing cell identity characteristics. These findings indicate that the developmental plasticity of wing buds, which determines the formation of LW or SW morphs, is primarily governed by differential proliferation of conserved ECs, TCs, and NCs. This process may involve the modulation of wing-patterning and cell cycle genes rather than the evolution of novel cell types or programs.

An intriguing finding in this study was that the cellular resolution achieved in characterizing firebug wings surpassed that of previous single-cell studies conducted on other insect wings, with identification of six wing cell types (ECs, TCs, NCs, GCs, HEs, and MCs). Earlier work on Drosophila and Lepidoptera wing discs often focused on specific biological questions, thus capturing only partial cellular landscapes25–27,39. An atlas of either MCs27, ECs and MCs26,39, or ECs, MCs, and TCs25 was constructed from Drosophila wings instead of profiling all wing cell types. Therefore, a comprehensively annotated and integrative cellular atlas of the firebug wing could serve as a unified reference framework, enabling robust comparisons in future research on wing development and regeneration.

Another intriguing finding was that the LW- and SW-destined wing buds of both the firebug and planthopper shared a similar proportion of each cell type, although the proportion of GCs and HEs in PaInR2De4 is slightly but significantly lower than Wt (Fig. 2d). This observation implies that the increased wing size in LW adults is not due to changes in cell type composition. Instead, we demonstrated that ECs, TCs, and NCs collectively account for more than 90% of the total cells in both LW- and SW-destined wing buds, which implies that the development of long wings mainly relies on the proliferation of the three dominant wing cell types. In Drosophila, the wing epithelium tissue establishes key patterning axes, the dorsoventral boundary via Apterous/Notch signaling and the anteroposterior axis via Hedgehog/Dpp, which collectively control growth, fate specification and vein/intervein patterning30,62. During wing-disc morphogenesis, ECs form a mechanically integrated sheet, where apical adherens junctions are anchored to the actin cytoskeleton to enable intercellular coupling, while also incorporating septate-junction barrier functions and subapical polarity-signaling modules. TCs contribute to wing patterning through fibroblast growth factor signaling, which positions and expands the air-sac primordium adjacent to the wing disc, establishing cytoneme-mediated exchanges with epithelium63. These processes heavily rely on the orchestration of TFs, including En, Nub, and bs. The homeodomain TF En controls Hedgehog production in posterior cells and the downstream Dpp response across the anteroposterior boundary to specify posterior compartment identity in Drosophila wing epithelium55. The homeodomain TF Nub is required for the formation of the proximal–distal axis, with dysfunction of Nub in wing imaginal epithelium resulting in the loss of wing structures and defects along the proximal–distal axis in Drosophila42. A similar phenotype was observed in the planthopper N. lugens upon Nub-RNAi64. The TF bs functions in linking tracheal terminal fate with intervein patterning43. In the firebug, Nub and En were identified as EC-specific markers, whereas bs was specifically expressed in TCs. Knockdown of Nub, En, or bs in the context of LW-destined firebugs significantly reduced the forewing and hindwing size. Notably, Nub-RNAi strongly affected the proximal wing region, while the distal region remained largely intact. This phenotype clearly differs from that of En-RNAi, which led to a pronounced reduction in the distal part of the wing. These results suggest that Nub is mainly required for the development of both long and short wings, rather than specifically for the development of long wings. Together, the findings indicate that long wings develop mainly via regulating wing patterning genes En and bs in ECs and TCs in the firebug.

In addition to wing pattern genes in the ECs and TCs, we identified three cell cycle genes (CycB3, cdk1, and Anln) as NC marker genes involved in long-wing development. However, we noticed that knockdown of the three cell cycle genes (Anln, CycB3, and cdk1) produced a phenotype distinct from that observed upon En knockdown. While knockdown of Anln, CycB3, or cdk1 reduced wing size in both PaInR2De4 and Wt backgrounds, En knockdown reduced wing size only in PaInR2De4. These results indicate that Anln, CycB3, and cdk1 are required for both normal and plastic development of wings, and further highlight the pivotal role of En in regulating developmental plasticity of the wing. RNAi-based gene silencing assays combined with flow cytometry analysis of LW- and SW-destined wing buds provided strong evidence that prolonged cell proliferation duration contributed significantly to long-wing development in the firebug. This finding supports previous studies conducted in both the firebug and the planthopper, in which long-wing development was also shown to result from prolonged cell proliferation21,23,24. These findings indicate that long wings develop in part via the regulation of cell cycle genes.

The most noteworthy finding emerges from our cross-species comparative analysis between the firebug and planthopper, which enabled the identification of homologous cell types and conserved gene expression across these species. The firebug and planthopper share identical cell types marked by overlapping marker genes such as wing-patterning genes for ECs and TCs, as well as cell cycle genes for NCs. Given that SW insects originated late in evolution from winged forms, we propose that a conserved genetic machinery has evolved to modulate the developmental plasticity of wings through the coordinated deployment of wing-patterning (e.g., En) and cell cycle (e.g., cdk1) genes. In this scenario, SW morphs might arise from a truncation of cellular proliferation of default long wing development by precisely controlling the expression of En and cdk1 in the firebug and the planthopper. However, in other wing-polyphenic hemipterans, it is possible that a second set of genes associated with wing-patterning and cell cycle could also have evolved to mediate this process. In this study, a two-species comparison is insufficient to definitively determine whether this scenario represents a shared ancestral state within the Hemiptera or emerged independently through convergent evolution. Future investigations involving a broader range of hemipteran lineages will be essential to reconstruct the ancestral state of wing plasticity and to elucidate how these conserved cellular programs have diversified or been modified across the evolutionary history of the order.

One cavity of this study is that we used the PaInR2De4 mutants as a surrogate of the LW-destined firebug because of the lack of an LW-destined Wt population. Thus, we could not rule out any pleiotropic effect of PaInR2 knockout on wing cell types, although PaInR2De4 mutants morphologically resemble LW morphs. Secondly, the identification of cell clusters in scRNA-seq is fundamentally based on statistical enrichment and differential expression analysis52 rather than the “absolute” exclusive expression of a gene. In this study, we concluded that the developmental plasticity of wings was primarily governed by differential proliferation of ECs, TCs, and NC according to the phenotype derived from knockdown of En, bs, Anln, CycB1, and cdk1. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that additional cell types with relatively low expression of marker genes would also contribute to this process. Thirdly, a cluster of ECs specific to Wt was identified in SW-destined morphs, but not in LW-destined PaInR2De4 mutants. This observation can be explained by two possible scenarios: (i) The Wt-specific ECs are also present in LW-destined morphs, but are absent in PaInR2De4 mutants due to pleiotropic effects caused by the PaInR2-knockout genetic background; or (ii) these ECs are unique to SW-destined morphs, with their function remaining uncharacterized. To completely resolve the character of Wt-specific cells, future studies should perform scRNA-seq at different developmental timepoints of fifth-instar nymphs in SW-destined morphs.

In summary, our findings provide insights into wing polymorphism at the cellular level and could facilitate future exploration of the genetic, developmental, and evolutionary foundations of insect wing diversification.

Methods

Insects

The SW-destined P. apterus population (SW ratio >99%) was collected from Urumqi, Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, China, in 2020. Firebugs were reared at 28 ± 0.5 °C under a photoperiod of 16:8 h (Light: Dark) and 50 ± 5% relative humidity on red clover seeds (Trifolium pratense L.). The LW-destined PaInR2De4 mutants (100% LW morphs) were constructed by CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing, which are loss-of-function PaInR2 mutants due to a 4-nt deletion in exon 521. The SW-destined N. lugens population (SW ratio > 90%) was originally collected from Hangzhou, China, in 2008, and kept in our laboratory for 17 years. The planthopper was reared at 26 ± 0.5 °C under a photoperiod of 16:8 h (Light: Dark) and 50 ± 5% relative humidity on rice seedlings (rice variety: Xiushui 11). The LW-destined NlInR2E4 homozygous mutants (100% LW morphs) were constructed by CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing, as our previous report24.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis was performed based on 175 single-copy orthologs from 14 insect species deposited in InsectBase 2.0 (www.insect-genome.com/): the Asian citrus psyllid Diaphorina citri (IBG_01777); the aphids Acyrthosiphon pisum (IBG_00009), Aphis gossypii (IBG_00078), and Myzus persicae (IBG_00561); the true bugs P. apterus, Gerris buenoi (IBG_00399), Oncopeltus fasciatus (IBG_00587), and Riptortus pedestris (IBG_00689); the planthoppers N. lugens (IBG_00572), N. muiri (IBG_02928), Laodelphax striatellus (IBG_00477), and Sogatella furcifera (IBG_00709); the tea green leafhopper Empoasca onukii; and the fruit fly D. melanogaster (IBG_00296). OrthoFinder (v2.5.5) with default parameters was used to determine the orthologs and paralogs65. All identified single-copy orthologs were aligned using MAFFT (v7.520) and trimmed utilizing trimAl (v1.4.1)66,67. The maximum likelihood tree was constructed using IQ-TREE (v2.2.3)68 with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates.

scRNA-seq

To conduct scRNA-seq of wing buds of the firebug, fifth-instar Wt (96–120 h after eclosion) and PaInR2De4 (120–144 after eclosion) nymphs were collected for wing bud dissection. To conduct scRNA-seq of wing buds of the planthopper, fifth-instar Wt (42–48 h after eclosion) and NlInR2E4 nymphs (42–48 h after eclosion) were collected for wing bud dissection. For each sample, three biological replicates were set. Wing buds were collected in cold Sf-900 II SFM medium (Cat#10902096, Gibco), and then digested in 0.25% trypsin solution (Cat#T4049, Sigma) at 37 °C for 5–10 min. Dissociated wing cells were pelleted at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in Sf-900 before filtration through 30 μm filters. Cell concentration and viability were assessed using LUNA-FL Dual Fluorescence Cell Counter (Cat#L20001, Logos Biosystems). Approximately 10,000 live cells (cell viability >90%) were obtained for 10× Genomics Chromium scRNA-seq following the manufacturer’s instructions. Standard library preparation was performed, followed by sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000, generating 150 bp paired-end reads (Annoroad Gene Tech, Beijing, China).

scRNA-seq data analysis

Single-cell analysis was performed on the OmicShare platform69. For read processing and quantification, sequencing data were processed with CellRanger (v6.1.0) (10× Genomics) for demultiplexing, alignment, barcode/UMI processing, and gene–cell count quantification. The fastq files were aligned to the P. apterus (NCBI Accession: GCA_039877355.1) and N. lugens (NCBI Accession: GCA_014356525.1) reference genomes using STAR RNA-seq aligner70. Low-quality or potential multiplet profiles were filtered out using the following criteria: for P. apterus, gene counts 320-4600 per cell, UMI counts <26,000 per cell, percentage of mitochondrial genes (10%); for N. lugens, gene counts 310-3500 per cell, UMI counts <18,000 per cell, percentage of mitochondrial genes (25%). Putative doublets were identified with DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) and excluded71. Violin plots were generated on the OmicShare platform.

For normalization and integration, libraries were normalized using Seurat’s “LogNormalize” method. To mitigate batch effects within each species, we used Seurat’s CCA-based workflow72 and Harmony73 to integrate the single-cell transcriptome expression matrices of P. apterus and N. lugens, respectively.

For dimensionality reduction, clustering, and visualization, we conducted principal components analysis and constructed a shared nearest-neighbor graph (Seurat “FindNeighbors”). Principal components were then used for graph-based clustering (Seurat “FindClusters,” resolution = 0.2). Low-dimensional embeddings were obtained using t-SNE and UMAP for visualization on the OmicShare platform.

For marker gene identification, the Seurat “FindAllMarkers” function with the default setting was utilized to identify DEGs for each cluster. Marker genes were identified as those upregulated in the cluster and meeting the following criteria: overexpression level >1.28-fold, minimum percentage >0.25, and p value < 0.01 in the target cluster. Dot plots of the marker gene were generated on the OmicShare platform.

Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the online OmicShare tool (www.omicshare.com/tools/home/report/goenrich.html). DEGs were annotated and classified based on the GO and KEGG databases for functional annotation and classification. p values were calculated and adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and GO terms and KEGG pathways with FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Correlation analysis of bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq

For bulk RNA-seq, wing buds from three biological replicates of LW- and SW-destined P. apterus were dissected. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Cat#9108, Takara) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were constructed using a NEBNext ultra RNA library prep kit (Cat#E7770, NEB) and then sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. For alignment and quantification, clean reads were mapped to the same reference genome used for scRNA-seq analysis. Gene expression was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using the RESM software. For differential expression in bulk data, raw count matrices were analyzed using DESeq2 (v1.20.0). Genes with a p value < 0.01 and an absolute fold change >1.28 were considered to be DEGs.

To assess concordance between bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq, pseudo-bulk profiles were generated by averaging normalized scRNA-seq expression values across all cells within each scRNA-seq sample. Bulk RNA-seq expression values were averaged across experimental replicates. The bulk RNA-seq and pseudo-bulk datasets were transformed as log2(expression +1). Only genes present in both datasets were retained. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the degree of concordance between the two datasets. A scatter plot with a linear regression line was constructed using the ggplot2 package to visualize the relationship. To assess whether cell-type marker signatures from scRNA-seq were recapitulated in bulk RNA-seq, bulk expression of marker sets was displayed in a heatmap.

TRN analysis

To reconstruct the TRN and identify cell-type-specific TF activities, we performed SCENIC analysis using the pySCENIC (v0.12.1) pipeline74,75. Due to the lack of dedicated motif databases for P. apterus, we constructed species-specific databases by mapping their gene sets to D. melanogaster orthologs. Orthology assignments were established via BLASTp-like searches using DIAMOND (v0.8.36.98), allowing the utilization of well-annotated D. melanogaster motif collections for subsequent regulatory analysis. Based on these orthology assignments, we retrieved 19,892 motifs associated with 645 D. melanogaster TFs from the SCENIC database (https://resources.aertslab.org/cistarget/motif2tf/). These were then mapped to the orthologous TFs identified in our target species, P. apterus. We identified 289 orthologous TFs associated with 2,917 Position Weight Matrix (PWM) models. Regulatory regions were defined as 5 kb upstream and 2 kb downstream of the transcription start site, and PWMs were scored to establish gene-motif rankings.

The TRN inference followed a sequential workflow: initially, candidate regulatory modules were inferred from co-expression patterns between TFs and potential target genes using GRNBoost2; these modules were subsequently refined using cisTarget to eliminate indirect targets, retaining only motifs with a Normalized Enrichment Score > 2.0; finally, the AUCell algorithm was employed to assess the activity of each resulting regulon within individual cells by calculating the Area Under the recovery Curve across gene expression rankings. The resulting files (.loom) containing regulon activity scores were integrated and processed in R using SCopeLoomR (v0.13.0) and SCENIC (v1.3.1) to facilitate downstream analysis. Specifically, the top 10-ranked regulons and their corresponding target genes, identified through the SCENIC pipeline, were imported into Cytoscape (v3.10.4)76 to construct a directed graph illustrating the relationships between TFs and DEGs. In these networks, node size was scaled according to the number of targeted DEGs to highlight the relative importance of each TF within its respective cell-type-specific TRN.

RNAi

Double-strand RNA (dsRNA) synthesis and injection were performed as previously described20. Briefly, dsRNA was synthesized with T7 primers (Supplementary Data 7) using a T7 high-yield RNA transcription kit (Cat#TR101-02, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Synthesized dsRNAs were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Before injection, fourth- or fifth-instar nymphs were anesthetized with CO2. A FemtoJet microinjector (Eppendorf) was used for dsRNA injection with a borosilicate glass capillary needle. Nymphs were anesthetized with CO2 for 10–15 s. Each fourth- or fifth-instar P. apterus nymph was microinjected with about 1 μg of dsRNAs (Supplementary Data 4). After microinjection, all insects were maintained in transparent polycarbonate boxes and fed with red clover seeds.

RNAi efficiency validation via qRT-PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from Wt nymphs at 48 h post-dsRNA injection using RNAiso Plus (Cat#9108, Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA using HiScript QRT Super Mix (Cat#R123-01, Vazyme). The resulting cDNAs were fivefold diluted and used as templates for qRT-PCR with primers specific to each gene (Supplementary Data 7). qRT-PCR was subsequently performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) under the following conditions: denaturation for 3 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, and then 60 °C for 30 s. The ribosomal protein 49 (rp49) gene served as the internal reference genes for P. apterus. The 2−∆∆Ct method was used to determine relative expression levels.

ISH assay

ISH hybridization was performed as previously described with minor modifications77. Digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled RNA probes (Supplementary Data 7) were generated using the DIG RNA labeling Kit (Cat#11277073910, Roche) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In situ probes were stored in prehybridization buffer (1 μg/mL) at −80 °C until further use. Wing buds were dissected from fifth-instar nymphs (6 days post-eclosion) and fixed in 10% formaldehyde overnight at room temperature. Fixed samples were washed three times in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (Cat#ST825, Beyotime) (PBST) for 10 min each, and then dehydrated with methanol (Cat#676780, Sigma) for 30 min. Samples were rehydrated in gradient solutions (75%, 50%, and 25% methanol/PBST) for 5 min each. Following rehydration, samples were washed twice with PBST for 10 min each, and treated with 20 μg/mL proteinase K (Cat#03450384103, Roche) for 6 min, followed by a brief rinse in PBST for 5 min. After proteinase K treatment, wing buds were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Cat#SL18301, Coolaber) for 20 min and washed twice with PBST for 10 min each.

For hybridization, wing buds were prehybridized in prehybridization buffer at 68 °C for 2 h. Following prehybridization, samples were incubated with DIG-labeled probes in prehybridization buffer at 68 °C overnight. The following day, samples were washed through serial washing steps in washing solution (2× SSCT: formamide solutions, 50:50 and 75:25, v/v) at 70 °C twice for 20 min each. After washing, samples were rinsed and washed four times with maleic acid buffer (MABT: 1× maleic acid (Cat#M0375, Sigma), 0.1% Tween 20) for 10 min each at RT. Samples were then blocked in 1× DIG Block Buffer for 2 h at RT and incubated in 1 mL anti-DIG-AP antibody (Cat#11093274910, LOT#32871924, Roche, 1:2000 dilution in 1× DIG Block Buffer) overnight at 4 °C. The color reaction was then developed with the NBT/BCIP kit (Cat#C3206, Beyotime) in the dark at RT. Post-staining, samples were fixed again with 4% PFA for 1 h at RT and washed through a series of methanol/PBST gradients (0%, 25%, 50%, and 75%) for 10 min each in the dark at RT. Samples were then rinsed and washed with 100% methanol for 3 min and soaked in 100% glycerol for imaging. A Leica DVM6 microscope was used to take pictures with Leica Application Suite X (LAS X) software.

Quantification of wing cell number by qPCR

Forewing buds were dissected from fifth-instar Wt and PaInR2De4 P. apterus nymphs at 3 (n = 40), 5 (n = 30), and 7 (n = 30) days post-eclosion. gDNA was extracted from wing buds using the DNA Purification Kit (Cat#A1120, Promega), following the manufacturer’s protocol. To estimate the total cell number per wing bud, we employed qPCR to measure the genomic copy number of the single-copy Rdl gene. To set up a standard curve, a recombinant plasmid containing a 277 bp genomic fragment of the Rdl gene was constructed. The absolute copy number of the plasmid stock was determined based on its concentration (108.4 ng/μL) and sequence length. A ten-fold serial dilution of the plasmid was used to establish the standard curve. The qPCR reactions were conducted on a CFX96 Real-time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) with the following conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Three independent biological replicates, each with three technical replicates, were performed for each sample. The absolute Rdl copy number was interpolated from the standard curve.

Microinjection of EdU and Hydroxyurea

Fourth-instar Wt P. apterus nymphs were microinjected with dsRNAs targeting PaInR2 or Gfp to generate LW- and SW-destined morphs, respectively. At 72–96 h and 96–120 h post-eclosion, fifth-instar nymphs were microinjected with EdU (1 mM)24. At 24 h after microinjection, forewings were dissected and fixed in FAA (formalin-acetic acid-alcohol solution: 5 mL 37% formaldehyde, 5 mL acetic acid, 81 mL ethanol, and 9 mL H2O). EdU incorporation was detected using the Click-iT EdU assay (Cat#C10337, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hoechst 33342 solution (Cat#C10337, Invitrogen) was used for nuclei staining. Fluorescent images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 810 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen).

To inhibit cell proliferation in wing buds, an individual fifth-instar PaInR2De4 nymph at 0-120 h post-eclosion (n = 10) was microinjected with approximately 1 μL of 0.1 mM Hydroxyurea (Cat#HY-B0313, MedChemExpress). Control groups were injected with an equivalent volume of 10% DMSO (Cat#A430113, Sangon Biotech).

Flow cytometric analysis of wing cells

Wing buds from six biological replicates were dissected from fifth-instar Wt (96–120 h post-eclosion) and PaInR2De4 (120–144 h post-eclosion) nymphs. Wing cells were suspended in cold Sf-900 II SFM medium (Cat#10902096, Gibco), and then centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. After removing the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in an ethanol: PBS (7:3, v/v) mixture, followed by incubation for 2 h on ice. Cells were then pelleted again by centrifugation (500 × g, 5 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in Sf-900 II SFM medium containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Cat#TB0198, BIO BASIC) for permeabilization. Cells were incubated at 4 °C for 10 min and subsequently resuspended in Sf-900 II SFM medium containing 2 μg/mL DAPI (Cat#D9542, Sigma) for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. Suspensions were passed through a 200-mesh nylon filter to remove aggregates.

Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD FACSDiscover S8 instrument. Debris aggregates were excluded using SSC-H vs LightLoss-H gating; singlets were defined by pulse-geometry gating on the DNA channel (UV5(460)W vs A and W vs H). After hierarchical gating, ~10,000 singlet events were acquired per sample. DNA content (DAPI) was modeled in FlowJo v.10.10.0 (FlowJo, LLC) using the Watson pragmatic algorithm to estimate G0/G1, S, and G2/M fractions. Images of gated populations were generated with BD CellView (v.1.3.6). Data are reported as the mean ± SEM, and statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2) (GraphPad Software, La Jolla).

Transcriptional similarity assay across species

We assessed the transcriptional similarity of wing cell types between P. apterus and N. lugens. In brief, we retained one-to-one orthologous genes among the top 50 DEGs for each cell cluster in both species, which were then used for MetaNeighbor (v1.20.0) analysis78. The results were visualized as a heatmap generated by the function of the R package “gplots.” The similarity among cell types was quantified using the average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) score.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses except in the scRNA-seq analysis, in which low-quality cells and putative doublets were removed during scRNA-seq quality control. Insect samples of the same developmental stage were randomly allocated into experimental and control groups (e.g., for RNAi and pharmacological treatments). The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2) or R as appropriate. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± SEM or mean ± SD, as indicated in the figure legends. Biological replicates, sample sizes and exact p values are provided in the methods section, corresponding figure legends, and the Source data file.
