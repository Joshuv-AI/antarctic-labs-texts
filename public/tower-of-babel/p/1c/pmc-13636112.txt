The 5R's of large language model‐assisted diagnosis: A practical framework for hospitalists
Authors: Peter Barish, Andrew D Auerbach, Sumant R Ranji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnostic error remains a major patient safety challenge in hospital medicine. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used by clinicians to aid in diagnosis, yet most lack a structured approach for doing so safely and effectively. In this piece, we propose a practical, clinician‐centered framework for LLM‐assisted diagnosis. The 5R's Framework promotes a human‐in‐the‐loop approach that harnesses LLMs to reduce diagnostic error while preserving critical reasoning skills.

A hospitalist admits a 70‐year‐old woman with metastatic gastric cancer (on active treatment with paclitaxel) and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, initially attributed bacterial pneumonia and heart failure exacerbation. Despite antibiotics and diuresis, on hospital day 3 she has escalating oxygen requirements. The hospitalist is uncertain about the diagnosis and wonders if artificial intelligence could assist, but she has not used AI before and is unsure how to proceed.

INTRODUCTION

Diagnostic errors occur frequently in hospital medicine, 1 often resulting from delays in considering or prioritizing a diagnosis, failure to consider complications, or problems with test ordering and interpretation. 2

Large language models (LLMs) have tremendous promise for improving the diagnostic process. In simulated studies, LLMs have solved complex clinical cases at rates equal to or surpassing expert clinicians. 3 , 4 , 5 Many clinicians are using LLMs to aid in diagnosis; in a 2024 survey, 12% of physicians reported using AI as a diagnostic tool, 6 a proportion likely increasing due to the availability of free, point‐of‐care applications.

However, there are few studies of LLMs in real‐world clinical care, and LLM use itself has risks. 7 , 8 Hospitalists who use LLMs for diagnosis therefore need to be familiar with the strengths and weaknesses of these tools and how to use them as a decision support tool at the bedside.

The “5 rights” framework for clinical decision support systems (CDS) outline principles 9 to ensure that clinicians receive actionable, evidence‐based information to provide better care. An effective CDSS should deliver the Right information to the Right person in the Right format, using the Right channel and at the Right time in the clinician's workflow. In the same vein, we propose the 5R's of LLM‐assisted diagnosis, a pragmatic framework grounded in clinical reasoning science. This approach should help hospitalists use LLMs to improve diagnosis while limiting the potential for errors or unintended consequences.

IMPORTANCE OF A HUMAN‐IN‐THE‐LOOP APPROACH

LLMs are capable, confident, and fast. However, these features can obscure important limitations when applied to clinical care: they can generate plausible sounding but incorrect information (“hallucinations”), inherit biases from their training data, and may lack the nuanced contextual factors that clinicians weigh subconsciously. 7 , 8

A primary concern accompanying increasing use of AI, particularly for trainees, is the risk of cognitive deskilling. 10 If an LLM can instantly generate a thorough differential diagnosis, will the next generation of physicians lose the skill of clinical reasoning?

As AI tools become integrated into diagnosis, we must rigorously reaffirm the clinician's role. LLMs should function as diagnostic assistants, not autonomous diagnosticians. When using an LLM, the cognitive work does not vanish; it shifts. The physician must still perform the critical tasks of data gathering and synthesis to summarize the clinical presentation for the AI and acting on LLM outputs requires a sophisticated understanding of limits and bias. As with all clinical decision support tools, the physician is ultimately responsible for their medical decision making.

Thus, an effective “human‐in‐the‐loop” approach is the best way to ensure patient safety, limit liability, and enhance (rather than degrade) clinical reasoning skills.

THE 5R'S FRAMEWORK

To optimize LLM‐assisted diagnosis, we propose the 5R's framework. This model guides hospitalists through the safe and effective application of LLMs at the bedside (Figure 1).

They can be particularly helpful for expanding a differential when clinicians feel “stuck” or during a diagnostic time‐out. Physicians must start with their own diagnostic thinking (to avoid deskilling), but LLMs can aid in identifying rare diagnoses that might otherwise be overlooked or organize thinking around complex, multi‐system presentations.LLMs should be avoided in high‐stakes situations where the risk of model hallucination is unacceptable. Not all LLMs are created equal, and using the wrong architecture for a diagnostic task can lead to suboptimal results. The landscape of high‐performing LLMs (often called “frontier” models) changes rapidly, and hospitalists should be able to distinguish between three broad categories:

1. Standard models for simple tasks: For tasks like creating patient education materials, communication scripts, or drafting administrative documents, standard models (e.g., ChatGPT “Instant” or Gemini 2.5 “Fast”) are sufficient, and often free to use.

Standard models for simple tasks: For tasks like creating patient education materials, communication scripts, or drafting administrative documents, standard models (e.g., ChatGPT “Instant” or Gemini 2.5 “Fast”) are sufficient, and often free to use.

2. Reasoning models for logic tasks: For complex differential diagnosis and multi‐step clinical logic, newer “reasoning” models (e.g., ChatGPT or Gemini with “Thinking” selected) are superior. Reasoning models use “chain‐of‐thought” processing to solve complex problems, mirroring human clinical reasoning. 10 Emerging evidence also suggests these models are better at medical benchmarks and may be less susceptible to cognitive biases than standard models. 11 , 12

3. De‐identification: Strict adherence to institutional policies for protected health information (PHI) is paramount. Clinicians should remove all PHI identifiers (i.e., dates) and generalize ages, unless their institution offers access to a secure LLM that is approved for use with PHI. Information should never be copied from the EHR into any public LLM.

5.Reflect on Reasoning—Reinforce and calibrate diagnostic skills.

Reflect on Reasoning—Reinforce and calibrate diagnostic skills.

The final “R” ensures that the use of LLMs contributes to the clinician's long‐term growth. 15 Close each interaction with a distinct 60‐s pause for deliberate reflection, comparing the LLM's output to your working differential diagnosis.

Identify discrepancies and calibrate: If the LLM suggested diagnosis you missed, reflect on why you didn't include it. Was it a knowledge gap? Premature closure? This step turns the consult into a learning event (deliberate practice).

Team‐based learning: LLM‐assisted diagnosis can also be used for teaching; teams can compare and contrast their differential against the AI's, making the reasoning process visible and collaborative.

CONCLUSIONS

In the scenario, the hospitalist discussed the case with a colleague; together, they queried an LLM using a deidentified problem representation and structured prompt. The LLM suggested a diagnosis which had not been considered previously: paclitaxel‐induced pulmonary toxicity. After consulting the pulmonary service, the hospitalist initiated treatment with oral prednisone. The patient's symptoms and oxygen requirement improved quickly, and she was discharged on hospital day 7.

This case illustrates the potential of LLMs to improve diagnosis in complex hospitalized patients by offering a “second opinion” on demand. These powerful tools have immense potential for improving diagnosis, but the introduction of AI into the diagnostic process is not without risk. The 5R's Framework aims to promote effective and safe LLM use anchored in the principles of clinical reasoning and deliberate practice. Appropriate LLM use can harness the power of these models to reduce diagnostic error while simultaneously sharpening hospitalists’ own clinical acumen.
