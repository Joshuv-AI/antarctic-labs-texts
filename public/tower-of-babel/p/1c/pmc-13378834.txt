Levels of stress, anxiety, and depression during pregnancy after bariatric surgery: A comparative study with women with obesity
Authors: Maryam Navaee, Maryam Kashanian, Mehrdad Vossoughi, Arash Mohazzab, Fatemeh Salmani, Negar Zamaninour, Ali Kabir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Obesity can lead to stress, anxiety, and depression (DAS). Bariatric surgery is often used to treat severe obesity, but it may also increase anxiety and depression. It’s unclear if pregnant women with bariatric surgery experience more depression and anxiety than pregnant women with obesity and without surgery history. This study intends to compare the levels of DAS during pregnancy for women with bariatric surgery and those who have obesity.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This study measured DAS using the DASS-21questionnaire (Depression, Stress, and Anxiety Scale) in the first and third trimesters of pregnancy in 76 women who experience bariatric surgery and 73 women with obesity between May 2022 and January 2024. Data analysis was conducted using independent and paired t-tests, Mann-Whitney, Chi-square, and multiple linear regression with SPSS-22 software.RESULTS:No significant differences were observed in the average scores of DAS among the two groups (P > 0.05). However, there were significant associations among maternal occupation (inversely related) and anxiety (P = 0.014), as well as unwanted pregnancy (directly related) and depression (P = 0.030) during the first trimester. Additionally, in the third trimester, there were significant associations among monthly family income (directly related) and hospitalization (inversely related) with the two variables of stress and anxiety (P < 0.05).CONCLUSION:Pregnancy following bariatric surgery does not necessarily accompany an increase in DAS level compared to women without a surgical history. However, occupation, unplanned pregnancy, income level, and hospitalization could be influential factors in DAS.

Introduction

Ten percent of pregnant women globally experience mental disorders,[1] mainly experiencing stress, anxiety, and depression.[2] The occurrence of depression during pregnancy varies from 7 to 20%.[3] In Iran, 25% of expectant mothers report indications of depression, with half experiencing anxiety.[4] A meta-analysis discovered that prenatal anxiety affects around 18.2%, 24.6%, and 12.8% of women in each trimester, respectively.[5]

Stress is the most apparent symptom in pregnant women with mental disorders, negatively impacting fetal growth and development.[6] This can lead to an increased risk of problems such as abortion, placental and fetal abnormalities, premature birth, and low birth weight, along with reduced adherence to prenatal care.[78] These effects can have lasting impacts on children’s growth.[9] Anxiety and stress are also associated with depression in women before and after childbirth,[10] which can negatively impact mother-child relationships and increase behavioral-emotional problems, such as hyperactivity disorder in children.[9]

Early identification and management of DAS (Depression, Anxiety, and Stress) during pregnancy are critical to prevent adverse impacts on maternal and fetal health.[11] Maternal mental health during pregnancy can be influenced by factors such as poverty, education level, income, age, and obesity.[12] Obesity is a significant concern in this century[13] and has been extensively studied in psychopathology. However, there are some controversies surrounding this association. According to a study by Seyedamini et al.,[14] individuals who are obese are more prone to experiencing increased levels of depression, anxiety, and physical ailments compared to individuals with a normal weight. Studies in Iran have found that the prevalence of depression is 2–3 times higher in obese individuals compared to those who are slightly overweight.[1516] International studies have indicated that individuals with obesity are five times more likely to encounter symptoms of depression than those with a normal weight.[1117] However, some research has not found a strong connection between body mass index (BMI) and levels of depression in women.[18]

The problem of obesity in pregnant women has risen worldwide, even in countries like Iran, which is among the top 20 countries with high rates of overweight individuals.[19] In Iran, over half of women are overweight or obese.[20] Pregnant women with obesity may experience physical and mental health issues, such as pre-eclampsia, diabetes, and pregnancy/postpartum depression. Research has shown that obese pregnant women tend to experience higher levels of DAS compared to women of normal weight.[21] One potential solution for weight loss is bariatric surgery (BS), which can significantly impact obesity treatment.[22] BS works by altering the digestive system, leading to weight loss.[23] Studies have shown that most people experience improved psychosocial functioning after bariatric surgery. For example, a study on 69 patients who underwent bariatric surgery found that ten months after surgery, the prevalence of major psychosocial stressors, including DAS, relationships, and overeating, decreased from 70% to 12%.[24] Another study found that bariatric surgery resulted in a greater reduction in psychiatric issues, such as DAS, compared to non-surgical treatment.[25] Nevertheless, rapid changes after BS can also lead to psychological disorders such as depression and anxiety.[11]

Factors such as gut microbiota changes, alterations in nutrient absorption, and hormone production can cause mood changes, intestinal inflammation, and higher isobutyric acid levels after bariatric surgery.[262728] During pregnancy, these issues may be related to changes in food intake and difficulties adapting to pregnancy due to the physical and mental changes caused by bariatric surgery.[29] These changes, combined with the challenges of adapting to pregnancy, can increase the risk of mental disorders throughout pregnancy, particularly in the first and third trimesters and within one month after delivery.[11] Research suggests that women with a history of bariatric surgery may experience elevated rates of depression and anxiety throughout pregnancy compared to obese women who have not had this surgery.[30] Prospective studies in this field are limited, but Jans et al.[31] reported a potential increase in the risk of anxiety and depression during pregnancy, with 57% of women in the surgery group exhibiting elevated anxiety levels. Walter et al.[32] found that 15.2% of pregnant women with a history of bariatric surgery were treated with psychotherapeutic drugs during pregnancy, and only 7.6% of postpartum depressed women had given birth. They concluded that bariatric surgery may reduce postpartum depression.

However, there are limited prospective studies in this area, and existing research has shown mixed results with small sample sizes.[3132] The results of bariatric surgery on the risk of depression and anxiety during pregnancy compared to women with severe obesity who have not undergone the surgery remain unclear.[30] Mental health issues during pregnancy can significantly impact both the mother and her child, affecting pregnancy outcomes and the child’s development.[33] There is a limited amount of research on Depression, Anxiety, and Stress in pregnant women with history of bariatric surgery in other countries Additionally, there are only a few prospective studies that do not include a comparison to a control group. Considering the impact of the mental state of pregnant women on fetal health and pregnancy, an extensive search on various scientific websites did not yield any articles that examined the effects of bariatric surgery on stress, anxiety, and depression in Iranian pregnant women. Therefore, based on the aforementioned points, the researcher conducted a comparative study of stress, anxiety, and depression levels among Iranian pregnant women with and without a history of bariatric surgery.

Objective

This study was conducted to “Levels of stress, anxiety, and depression during pregnancy after bariatric surgery; a cohort study”.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The research was conducted as a prospective study involving 149 pregnant women in Tehran from May 2022 to January 2024. There were 76 participants in the surgery group and 73 in the obesity group. The sample size was determined based on the stress outcome in the third trimester, with a Type I error rate of 0.05 and a power of 0.2, resulting in 72 participants in each group, as per the information from the Goele Jans article.[31]

Study participants and sampling

The researchers selected a group of pregnant women with a history of bariatric surgery (the exposure group) using the National Obesity Surgery Database (NOSD), which provided a list of women with a history of excess weight and weight-loss surgery. They contacted these women to determine if they were pregnant. If they were under 14 weeks pregnant, the researchers explained the study’s objectives and invited them to participate, providing their phone numbers for interested women to express their interest via SMS without pressure.

For the comparison group, the researchers chose 15 primary healthcare clinics in Tehran to identify pregnant women with obesity (BMI ≥ 30) who had no history of bariatric surgery. They obtained a list of these women from maternal care registers or the Sina system and contacted them to gauge their willingness to participate after a brief description of the study’s goals. In both groups, the researchers inquired about any history of mental illnesses, psychiatric hospitalizations, or the use of mental health medications. Only women with negative responses to these inquiries were invited to participate. A detailed description of the sampling method and study procedure can be found in the protocol study by Navaee et al.[34]

Inclusion criteria included willingness to participate in the study, being in the first 14 weeks of pregnancy calculated from the first-trimester ultrasound, being aged between 18 and 40, and having a body mass index (BMI ≥ 30) if obese and without a history of surgery. Participants must not have certain health conditions such as uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes. Furthermore, individuals should not have a history of mental illnesses such as severe depression (scoring >20 on the DASS-21 questionnaire) or dementia, should not have experienced severe stressful situations in the past six months, and should not currently be taking psychotherapeutic drugs to be eligible for the study. Exclusion criteria included termination of pregnancy (abortion) or childbirth before completing the DASS-21 questionnaire (related to the third trimester of pregnancy), unwillingness to continue participating, and being single, widowed, or separated

Data collection tool and technique

The survey consisted of two parts, with its validity and reliability assessed before use. The first part collected individual information, while the second part utilized the DASS-21 questionnaire (DASS Lavibond and Lavibond, 1995), which includes 21 questions measuring stress, depression, and anxiety (7 questions for each). Responses were scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3, yielding a total DAS score range of 0–21. In Iran, the validity and reliability of the DASS-21 were studied by Samani and Jokar,[35] reporting Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.81, 0.74, and 0.78 for the DAS scales. In this study, the reliability was confirmed through internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.90, 0.81, and 0.78.

After obtaining verbal consent from the pregnant women, participants in the surgery group were asked to visit the obesity clinic, while those in the non-surgical group were directed to their healthcare center. Eligible participants provided written consent and personal information. The researcher calculated each participant’s questionnaire score, omitting individuals with a depression score higher than 20. The DASS-21 questionnaire was then administered again during the third trimester (T3) of pregnancy (32–36 weeks of pregnancy) via telephone. If any participants were removed during the study, replacements were made.

To investigate the factors affecting DAS in pregnant women, several variables were considered, including age, job, education, gravidity (primigravida or multigravida), monthly family income, pre-pregnancy BMI, unwanted pregnancy, interval pregnancy (previous pregnancy), history of miscarriage and infertility, history of previous pregnancy complications (hypertension, diabetes, IUGR, miscarriage, preeclampsia, or IUFD), hypertensive disorders (pregnancy-induced hypertension and preeclampsia), and diabetes in the current pregnancy (measured in the second trimester through glucose overload of 75 g, with glycemia levels ≥92 mg/dL for fasting, ≥180 mg/dL for 60 minutes after overload, and ≥153 mg/dL for 120 minutes after overload), COVID-19 during pregnancy, bleeding during pregnancy, anemia (hemoglobin <11 g/dL), hyperemesis gravidarum, maternal hospitalization for any reason, progesterone consumption, maternal serum TSH levels, and physical activity (assessed with the Physical Activity Questionnaire). The Physical Activity Questionnaire is a validated instrument.[36] The power of physical activity was determined using Met. This questionnaire was completed through a telephone in the T1 and T3. The Persian version has good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.69.[37] In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85.

Ethical considerations

The research was endorsed by the Research Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran, with the reference code IR.IUMS.REC.1401.048. Participants were informed about the study’s goals, their voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw at any time. They were assured that their information would be kept confidential. All participants provided written informed consent, and ethical standards were upheld in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis method

SPSS-22 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was employed to analyze the data. Means, percentages, frequencies, and standard deviations were calculated to describe the characteristics and preferences of the participants. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the quantitative data. The independent t-test was utilized to compare the means between the surgery and non-surgical groups. A paired t-test was employed within each group to compare average scores from the third trimester to the first trimester. The Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare the frequencies of categorical and nominal variables between the two groups. Multiple linear regression was applied to evaluate whether DAS were associated with each of the influencing factors. Variables included in the models were age, job, gravidity, history of infertility and abortion, complications in previous pregnancies, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in the current pregnancy, unwanted pregnancy, COVID-19 during pregnancy, progesterone consumption, vaginal bleeding during pregnancy, anemia in the third trimester, hospitalization during pregnancy, and monthly family income. These factors were included in the models based on a literature review, with a significance level of less than 0.25 in univariate analysis. Pseudo R² statistics were used to assess model fit, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The results found that the surgery and Non-surgical groups had significant differences in maternal age (P = 0.006), GDM in current pregnancy (P = 0.019), Vaginal bleeding in T1(first trimester) (P = 0.035), Anemia (P = 0.001), Vaginal bleeding during pregnancy (The sum of the T1 and T3((P = 0.015), Progesterone consumption (P = 0.004), and body mass index in pre-pregnancy (P ≤ 0.001). Women in the surgery group were older (33.30 ± 4.37 years) contrasted with those in the Non-surgical group (31.14 ± 5.10 years), and had lower pre-pregnancy BMI due to bariatric surgery (28.34 ± 4.70 vs. 33.35 ± 3.64, respectively). The incidence of gestational diabetes was higher in the Non-surgical group (37%) compared to the surgery group (19.7%). The two groups showed no notable differences in other characteristics. Discussion

This is the first study in Iran to inspect DAS in pregnant women with bariatric surgery history, comparing it with women with obesity without bariatric surgery. The results show no significant difference in the average DAS scores among the two groups in the T1 and T3, but the average DAS scores were elevated in the surgical group than in the obese group.

The findings of the present study did not align with the study by Kim et al.,[30] which showed significant differences in depression and anxiety scores between the two groups. One difference between this study and Kim’s was the sample size—Kim’s study included 2,616 participants in the surgical group and 4,984 in the obese group, which is considerably larger. Kim’s study was a retrospective analysis using a registry system, while the present study employed a reliable and valid questionnaire at two intervals. Another difference is in the study design and method of measuring variables; Kim’s research assessed anxiety and depression based solely on a yes/no response regarding whether the mother had experienced depression and/or anxiety during pregnancy as recorded in the pregnant women registry system. In contrast, in the present study, each variable was assessed through seven questions using a validated questionnaire at two intervals (first and third trimesters). A similarity in both studies is the higher average scores in the surgical group. This increase in the surgical group may be due to natural pregnancy, which typically involves systemic and physiological changes that can elevate the risk of anxiety and depression.[38] Additionally, after bariatric surgery, alterations in intestinal hormones and adipokines can affect brain structure and function.[39] Consequently, the combined effect of pregnancy and bariatric surgery may increase DAS scores during pregnancy.

The current study has similarities and differences with Jans et al.’s[31] study (2018). Both studies were prospective cohorts; however, Jans’ study had a smaller sample size (54 women with a history of obesity surgery and 25 women with obesity) and utilized different questionnaires. Jans’ research found that anxiety scores were significantly higher in the surgery group compared to women with obesity in T1, but depression scores did not differ significantly. In T3, both anxiety and depression scores were higher in the surgery group, with the difference in anxiety being significant. The increased anxiety and depression scores in women with a history of surgery may be attributed to concerns about weight regain during pregnancy and nutritional deficiencies resulting from surgery.[11]

In Jans’ obesity study group, the average state anxiety score during pregnancy increased significantly from 34 ± 9 to 38 ± 9, while the average depression score decreased from 8 ± 5 to 7 ± 4.[31] In the current obesity study group, there was a significant increase in anxiety from 4.9 ± 4.6 to 6.7 ± 5.8 (P = 0.001), and in depression from 5.2 ± 5.7 to 6.4 ± 7.0 (P = 0.055). In the Jans study in the surgery group, the average anxiety score during pregnancy remained almost the same (43 ± 11 in T1 and 43 ± 12 in T3), but the depression score decreased (from 6 ± 9 to 5 ± 8). In the current surgery group, however, the average anxiety score increased from 6.7 ± 5.1 to 9.0 ± 8.0, and the depression score increased from 6.37 ± 6.5 to 7.2 ± 8.3, which was significant for anxiety but not for depression. The increase in average anxiety and depression scores in both groups in this study could be attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic and influenza. Although no significant association was found between COVID-19 and DAS, the average DAS scores in women who had COVID during pregnancy were higher than in those who did not. Researchers found that the lives of pregnant women who were infected, or had relatives who were infected, were more adversely affected, leading to psychological effects, including DAS.[4041] Studies conducted in Canada and Turkey also confirmed higher levels of DAS compared to the period before the spread of COVID-19.[4243] Reasons for this may include quarantine, loss of social and family support, and concerns about fetal health.[4445]

In the analysis of factors affecting DAS in T1, it was found that unwanted pregnancy significantly increased the average depression score. The rate of unwanted pregnancy is very high; according to studies, out of 175 million pregnancies annually, 75 million are unwanted.[46] In Iran, the prevalence of unwanted pregnancy is also reported to be high (27.8%).[47] Mothers with unwanted pregnancies may experience high levels of anxiety and depression for reasons such as delays in seeking prenatal care, the use of various medications for abortion, and limited support, especially from their spouses.[48] In this study, women with unwanted pregnancies generally had higher average scores for each of the dependent variables in both T1 and T3. Specifically, the average depression score in the first three months for women with unwanted pregnancies was 2.43 points higher than for women who intended to become pregnant. The results of this study were not in line with those of Shobeiri et al.[49] In Shobeiri’s research, there was a notable contrast in the average anxiety ratings between the two groups of planned and unplanned pregnancies. However, there was no significant difference in depression scores, as they were nearly equal. Nonetheless, our findings align with those of Khajehpoor et al. and Zangeneh et al.,[4850] where women with unwanted pregnancies exhibited elevated levels of depression.

In this study, a significant link was found between job status and average anxiety scores, identifying job status as a factor influencing pregnant women’s anxiety in T1. The research revealed that employed women had lower anxiety scores than housewife mothers. These findings contrast with those of Dawood et al. and Adhikari et al.[5152] Dawood’s study, which involved 324 pregnant Egyptian women, indicated that employed women had notably higher anxiety and depression scores based on the Beck questionnaire compared to housewife mothers. The variation in cultural backgrounds and types of questionnaires used may contribute to the discrepancies among these studies. Dawood’s research was conducted in Egypt, where, according to him, a male-dominated society often places the burden of household chores and childcare solely on working women when they return home.[52] Conversely, Fall et al., Gurudatt et al., and Fatima et al.[535455] reported lower anxiety and depression levels among employed pregnant women compared to housewife mothers, which aligns with our findings. This difference could be attributed to employed women’s higher education levels, their ability to lead healthier lifestyles, and their better coping mechanisms for life’s stresses. Additionally, their financial independence and increased opportunities for socializing and leisure may contribute to reduced anxiety and depression.[5455]

Regarding factors affecting stress and anxiety in T3, a significant connection was identified among maternal hospitalization, monthly income, and levels of stress and anxiety. Most studies typically describe income levels as good, moderate, or bad, making it challenging to compare findings accurately. Effati-Daryani et al.[56] found that the prevalence of these disorders was higher in women who perceived their income as completely good or relatively good compared to those with poor income status. This finding aligns with our study’s results. Our study showed that as monthly income (measured in one million tomans) increased, stress and anxiety levels rose significantly. Similar outcomes were observed in Khouj et al.’s study,[57] where higher income levels were associated with increased stress and depression. This pattern may result from higher-income individuals spending more time working and being less supported at home. However, studies by Ceulemans et al., Colli et al., Wang et al., and Ghaffar et al.[58596061] did not find a significant relationship between monthly income and DAS among pregnant women.

Being hospitalized can add stress for women,[62] especially for those hospitalized due to high-risk pregnancies, who may be at higher risk for DAS. This could be due to reasons such as reduced contact with family and friends during hospitalization, concerns about family management, and hospitalization costs.[636465] For example, Thiagayson et al.[66] showed anxiety disorders in 12.5% of women hospitalized for high-risk pregnancies. In the present study, the average stress score for women hospitalized in T3 was 5.77 points higher, and the anxiety score was 3.34 points higher than that of non-hospitalized women. These results were not consistent with the findings of Hosseini et al.,[67] where the average anxiety score of hospitalized pregnant women in the third trimester did not differ from that of non-hospitalized women, but were consistent with the results of the study by Smorti et al.[68]

The interesting point observed in this study was that there was a reverse relationship among the previous pregnancy complications and the average score of anxiety and depression in the T1, and the DAS in the T3 of pregnancy, although this relationship was not significant. That means that women who had complications in their previous pregnancy had lower average DAS scores compared to women without complications, while we would expect that the presence of a history of complications in the previous pregnancy would lead to higher levels of these disorders. Perhaps one reason for this is that these individuals receive specialized care and thorough examinations from women’s health experts and healthcare providers before their new pregnancy, and usually have planned pregnancies, which reduces their psychological disorders. In this respect, our study’s conclusion did not match with that of Keramat et al.[69] study. In Keramat’s study, the average anxiety score for women with a history of complications in the previous pregnancy was 1.11 higher than those without a history of complications, although this difference was not significant (P = 0.406). Perhaps one reason for this inconsistency is the small sample size of the current study compared to the Keramat study (295 subjects vs. 149 subjects).

Limitations and recommendation

One of the strong points of the current study is the examination of the three DAS variables in the T1 and T3 of pregnancy, which could indicate changes in mental health over time. Additionally, pregnant women were investigated for the influence of some factors affecting DAS throughout their entire pregnancy. One of the factors affecting DAS is the level of physical activity, which was assessed through a reliable questionnaire in the present study. Some of the study’s restrictions include the small sample size and the lack of a third group with normal weight, and it is suggested that future studies pay attention to and address these issues.

Conclusion

The consequences of our study indicate the lack of a significant effect of bariatric surgery on DAS in pregnant women compared to non-surgically women. Though, additional research with a larger sample size are needed. Regarding factors affecting the average DAS score in the T1 of pregnancy, there was only a significant relationship among maternal occupation and anxiety, and unwanted pregnancy with depression, with an inverse relationship with occupation and a direct one with unwanted pregnancy. In terms of factors affecting the T3, only a significant relationship among hospitalization and family income with stress and anxiety was observed. Studies on the impact of bariatric surgery on psychological disorders in pregnant women are limited, and concerns about the mental health of pregnant women undergoing surgery persist. However, it’s essential to prioritize future research on the mental health of women who have undergone bariatric surgery, as this may significantly impact the health of future generations. It is also necessary in future studies to include pregnant women with a normal body mass index alongside women with a history of bariatric surgery and women with obesity. This study data was collected from a nationwide database that includes specialized information from patients referred from across the country. As a result, the current findings are applicable to the broader population.
