Validation of the Hearing Utility Measure (HUM) in Cochlear Implant Candidates and Users
Authors: Peter R. Dixon, Anuja H. Shah, Theodore R. McRackan, David Feeny, Sharon L. Cushing, George Tomlinson, Joseph M. Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo validate the Hearing Utility Measure (HUM), a tool designed to quantify hearing loss impact on health and quality of life for use in economic evaluation.Study DesignProspective cohort.SettingTertiary academic center.MethodsCochlear implant (CI) candidates were enrolled between June 25, 2020, and March 17, 2024. Participants completed HUM alongside other assessments, including Health Utilities Index, Mark‐3 (HUI‐3), Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing Scale (SSQ‐49), Cochlear Implant Quality of Life Profile (CIQOL‐35), and Tinnitus Handicap Inventory (THI) at the time of CI evaluation and 12 months post‐activation. A subgroup with stable hearing status completed HUM twice, 6 months apart, to assess test‐retest reliability.ResultsAmong 1315 participants, HUM showed strong correlations with CIQOL‐global (r = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.66‐0.72) and SSQ‐overall (r = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.67‐0.73), supporting construct validity. HUM Tinnitus domain correlated negatively with THI (−0.75; −0.77, −0.73), as expected. Test‐retest reliability was excellent (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient [ICC] 0.90, 95% CI: 0.83‐0.94). HUM hearing utility was higher for candidates with single‐sided deafness (SSD) (0.76) than with traditional and asymmetric hearing loss (0.58, P < .001). Median HUM improved from 0.70 to 0.78 for SSD recipients (P = .002) and 0.57 to 0.75 for traditional recipients (P < .001).ConclusionHUM is a reliable, valid, and responsive measure of hearing‐related health in adults with advanced hearing loss. Unlike traditional instruments such as SSQ and CIQOL, which capture patient‐reported outcomes but are not designed for economic analysis, HUM is a preference‐based utility instrument that enables cost‐effectiveness evaluation. It outperforms the available HUI‐3 by better reflecting hearing loss impact and complements existing nonutility instruments used in clinical and health policy decision‐making.

Hearing loss significantly affects quality of life across a multitude of domains. 1 To inform decisions about resource allocation, such as whether hearing interventions like cochlear implants (CIs) or other hearing devices are cost‐effective, we need tools that quantify how much value patients and society place on improvements in hearing.

Health utility instruments serve this purpose. They convert complex health states into a single number, typically ranging from 0 (representing the state of being dead) to 1 (perfect health), based on how a population values different levels of functioning. These scores are used in cost‐effectiveness analyses to compare the value of different treatments across diseases. However, generic utility instruments, which are designed to be applicable to all health conditions and interventions, often fail to capture aspects of hearing loss that matter to patients. These limitations have important implications. Accurate utility estimates are essential for drawing valid conclusions from cost‐utility analyses, especially given the high sensitivity of hearing intervention cost‐effectiveness models to changes in utility estimates. 2 , 3 , 4 The risk of failing to measure important differences in hearing‐related quality of life (HRQoL) is that resulting policy and funding decisions may withhold beneficial hearing interventions. Conversely, without appropriate and accurate measurement tools, policy and funding decisions may also risk supporting interventions that are ultimately not beneficial, leading to inefficient allocation of resources.

The Health Utilities Index, Mark 3 (HUI‐3) is commonly used in hearing research because it includes a hearing domain. 5 Other health utility instruments, such as the EuroQol‐5D (EQ‐5D) and Short‐Form 6D (SF‐6D), do not explicitly assess hearing. However, the HUI‐3 defines hearing narrowly as the ability to understand speech with or without hearing aids, and overlooks key dimensions such as sound localization, tinnitus, and music appreciation. 1 , 6 These gaps may explain why the HUI‐3 fails to detect utility gain after interventions like bilateral CIs, 7 or bone‐conduction devices for single‐sided deafness (SSD). 8 Its focus on hearing aids may also confuse respondents who use other types of devices. 9

To address these limitations, we developed the Hearing Utility Measure (HUM), a new instrument that assesses hearing across seven domains: speech understanding, environmental sounds, sound localization, listening effort, tinnitus, music, and assistive hearing devices. 10 Each combination of responses defines a unique hearing state, which is then assigned a utility score based on preferences from the general population. 11 The HUM can be used on its own to estimate hearing utility on a scale from 0 (unable to hear at all) to 1 (perfect hearing) or as a replacement for the HUI‐3 hearing domain to estimate health utility on a scale from 0 (dead) to 1 (perfect health). Health utility estimates derived using the HUM measure hearing function more comprehensively than the HUI‐3.

In this study, we evaluated the psychometric properties of the HUM in adult CI candidates and users. Specifically, we asked (1) Is the HUM reliable over time in individuals with stable hearing? (2) Does the HUM demonstrate construct validity, as evidenced by expected correlations with other patient‐reported and audiometric measures? (3) Is the HUM responsive to changes in HRQoL following cochlear implantation? A secondary research question was to assess whether the HUM outperforms the HUI‐3 in construct validity and responsiveness in this population.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Adults (≥18 years) referred for a CI evaluation at a tertiary academic center in Ontario, Canada, between June 25, 2020, and March 17, 2024, were considered for eligibility. Patients referred for revision CI surgery due to complications or device failures were excluded. All participants were proficient in English, and all assessments were conducted in English to ensure standardized administration and comprehension. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre and is reported in accordance with the COSMIN (Consensus‐based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments) reporting guidelines. 12

Reliability

To assess the stability of the HUM over time, test‐retest reliability was evaluated in a consecutive subset of participants who completed the HUM at two time points 6 months apart. These participants did not undergo any hearing‐related interventions during that interval. At the second administration, a transitional index was administered in which participants rated perceived changes in their hearing on a 7‐point Likert scale (“Much worse” to “Much better”). Those who indicated “No change” were included in the test‐retest reliability analysis.

Validity

Construct validity was assessed using the multitrait, multimethod (MTMM) approach. 13 All participants were assessed at the time of CI evaluation, and CI recipients were assessed again at 12 months after activation. At these time points, participants completed the HUM along with several established measures of HRQoL: the Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing Scale (SSQ‐49), the Cochlear Implant Quality of Life Profile (CIQOL‐35), the Tinnitus Handicap Inventory (THI), and the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI). Audiometric testing included pure‐tone audiometry and best‐aided speech recognition testing with consonant‐nucleus‐consonant (CNC) words and phonemes, and sentence recognition using the Hearing in Noise Test (HINT) and/or AzBio sentences in quiet and in noise at +10 and +5 dB signal‐to‐noise ratios, when performance in quiet exceeded 60% correct. All participants were assessed in their best‐aided condition, which for many included hearing aids or other assistive listening devices. Device use and any changes in amplification during the study period were recorded. As all participants were either CI candidates or recipients, the validation sample represents adults with advanced hearing loss rather than the general hearing‐impaired population.

We examined the pattern, strength, and direction of pairwise correlations between HUM scores and these clinical and patient‐reported measures to evaluate convergent and discriminant validity. For overall construct validity, the HUM‐derived hearing utility score was compared to global scores from the CIQOL‐35 and SSQ‐49. For domain‐specific construct validity, each HUM domain score was compared to conceptually related domains from the other instruments. For example, we expected the HUM speech understanding domain to correlate strongly with the CIQOL Communication domain and to have the highest correlations with audiometric measures. The HUM Tinnitus domain was expected to correlate strongly and negatively with the THI, reflecting tinnitus burden. We did not anticipate strong correlations between the HUM domains and the DHI, as the HUM is not intended to measure dizziness‐related constructs; a finding that would support divergent construct validity according to the MTMM approach.

Known‐groups validity was assessed by comparing HUM scores among CI candidacy groups. SSD was defined by CNC ≤ 5% in the implanted ear and contralateral 4‐frequency (0.1, 1, 2, and 4 kHz) pure‐tone average (PTA) ≤ 30 dB HL (strict criteria) or by CNC ≤ 10% in the implanted ear and contralateral PTA ≤ 40 dB HL (expanded criteria). Traditional candidates were defined by bilateral moderate or worse PTA thresholds with sentence scores ≤ 50% in the implanted ear and ≤60% in the contralateral ear or binaural condition. Asymmetric hearing loss (AHL) candidates included those who met traditional criteria in the ear to be implanted, but better than 60% sentence scores contralaterally or binaurally without meeting SSD criteria. Noncandidates had open‐set sentence scores > 60% in all tested conditions in both ears. For the purposes of known‐groups analysis, traditional and AHL candidates were combined into a single group due to their similar clinical presentation and presence of bilateral hearing loss. In contrast, individuals with SSD represent a distinct clinical subgroup with markedly different hearing configurations and patterns of functional impairment, warranting separate analysis.

Responsiveness

For participants who received a CI, responsiveness to HRQoL changes associated with receipt of a CI was assessed by readministering the HUM at 1‐year post‐activation. Within subject, pre‐CI and post‐CI HUM hearing utilities were compared.

Statistical Analysis

Given that utility scores are bounded between 0 and 1 and that the HUI‐3 utility scores are highly discretized (with only six possible values), we did not assume a normal distribution and used nonparametric methods.

Test‐retest reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), calculated with a two‐way mixed‐effects model treating subjects as random effects and time points as fixed effects (ICC [3, 1]). ICC values between 0.50 and 0.75 were interpreted as moderate reliability, between 0.75 and 0.90 as good, and above 0.90 as excellent. 14

Construct validity was evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients to assess the strength and direction of associations between HUM domain scores and scores from related domains on other instruments (SSQ‐49, CIQOL‐35, THI, and DHI), as well as audiometric measures. The strength of Spearman's rho (ρ) was interpreted as follows: 0.00 to 0.19 indicated no correlation, 0.20 to 0.39 weak correlation, 0.40 to 0.59 moderate correlation, 0.60 to 0.79 strong correlation, and 0.80 to 1.00 very strong correlation. Fisher's r‐to‐z transformation was used to test whether the strength of correlations between HUM and external measures differed from those observed for the HUI‐3. For known‐groups validity, differences in HUM‐derived hearing utility scores across CI candidacy groups were first assessed using the Kruskal‐Wallis test. When the omnibus test was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn's test with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance for pairwise tests was determined based on the Bonferroni‐adjusted P‐value relative to the corrected alpha threshold, and reported using conventional thresholds (P < .05, P < .01, and P < .001) applied after adjustment.

Responsiveness to change following cochlear implantation was assessed using the paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank test to compare preimplant and postimplant HUM utility scores within individuals.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Participants

Characteristics of the 1315 patients who responded to the initial referral survey during the study period (median age 64 years [interquartile range (IQR) 51, 75], 687 [52%] female) are summarized in Table 1. Of those, 1218 (93%) were referred for their first CI, and the remainder were referred to establish care or for consideration of a revision or second implant. Candidacy determination was made for 1084 (82.4%) with traditional candidates comprising the most common candidacy group (636/1084, 58.7%), followed by SSD (242/1084, 22.3%), and asymmetric hearing (149/1084, 13.7%). Candidacy could not be determined for 231 (21.3%) participants due to absent or incomplete audiometric data at the time of evaluation. Median hearing utility at CI evaluation for the whole cohort (n = 1315) was 0.55 (IQR 0.31, 0.73).

Reliability

A subsample of 136 consecutive patients participated in the test‐retest reliability study by completing the HUI‐3 and HUM at 6‐month intervals without having undergone interval surgery (median age 63 [IQR 54, 73] years, 54% female [73/136]). Among those 55/136 (40.4%) who indicated “No change” in hearing‐related ability or disability on the transitional index, the HUM exhibited excellent reliability across the 6‐month interval with ICC (3, 1) of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.83, 0.94).

Construct Validity

Hearing utility estimated with the HUM showed strong overall construct validity, correlating well with global scores from both the CIQOL‐35 (ρ = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.66‐0.72) and SSQ‐49 (ρ = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.67‐0.73; Figure 1).

Correlation matrix comparing Hearing Utility Measure domains with other measures of hearing‐related quality of life and ability. The color scale is based on Pearson's correlation coefficient demonstrating strong positive (yellow) or strong negative (purple) correlations. CIQOL, Cochlear Implant Quality of Life; CNC, consonant‐nucleus‐consonant; DHI, Dizziness Handicap Inventory; HUM, Hearing Utility Measure; PTA, pure‐tone average; SSQ, Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing Scale; THI, Tinnitus Handicap Inventory.

The speech understanding domain of the HUM demonstrated strong correlation with the CIQOL communication domain (ρ = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.65‐0.70), and with the SSQ speech domain (ρ = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.61‐0.68). Speech understanding also showed the strongest correlation of any HUM domain with audiometric measures, ranging from ρ = 0.42 (95% CI: 0.35‐0.48) for better‐ear CNC words to ρ = 0.49 (95% CI: 0.42‐0.55) for binaural AzBio sentences in quiet, although the strength of these correlations was low.

The environmental sounds domain was most strongly associated with the CIQOL environment domain (ρ = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.59‐0.66), supporting its convergent validity.

The music appreciation domain showed a strong correlation with the CIQOL entertainment domain (ρ = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.75‐0.80) and correlated strongly with the SSQ quality domain (ρ = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.59‐0.65).

The HUM listening effort domain exhibited moderate correlations with all CIQOL domains, including the CIQOL listening effort domain (ρ = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.45‐0.53), indicating that this construct is broadly associated with perceived hearing‐related challenges.

The sound localization domain was moderately correlated with the SSQ spatial domain (ρ = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.50‐0.57), but showed weak or no correlations with CIQOL domains or audiometric outcomes.

The tinnitus domain demonstrated a strong negative correlation with the THI total score (ρ = −0.75, 95% CI: −0.77 to −0.73). The negative correlation was expected given that the HUM tinnitus domain measures tinnitus‐related quality of life and THI measures burden of tinnitus.

The assistive device reliance domain exhibited weak or no correlations with external PROMs or audiometric outcomes, suggesting it may represent a distinct construct related to device usage rather than hearing performance.

Finally, no meaningful correlations were observed between any HUM domains and the DHI. This finding was expected and supports appropriate divergent construct validity, given that the HUM is not designed to assess vestibular or balance‐related constructs.

Known‐Groups Validity

HUM‐derived hearing utility estimates differed according to candidacy group (Figure 2). Among known‐candidacy groups, the median HUM hearing utility estimate was highest for the SSD group (0.76, IQR 0.62, 0.83), followed by noncandidates (0.64, IQR 0.56, 0.74), and lowest for traditional/AHL candidates (0.58; IQR 0.45, 0.69). All pairwise comparisons of medians were significantly different (adjusted P < .01 for all). The unknown candidacy group had a median HUM hearing utility of 0.67 (IQR 0.56, 0.80).

Comparing Construct Validity of HUM and HUI‐3

Hearing utility estimates derived using the HUM exhibited stronger correlations with other hearing PROMs than those derived using the HUI‐3 (Supplemental Table S1, available online). CIQOL global and all but one CIQOL sub‐score (effort, P = .33) were significantly more strongly correlated with HUM than HUI‐3 hearing utility. SSQ spatial and quality domains were more strongly associated with HUM than HUI‐3 hearing utility. THI had a weak negative correlation (−0.28, 95% CI: −0.33, −0.23) with HUM hearing utility and no correlation with HUI‐3 hearing utility (−0.01, 95% CI: 0.07, 0.04).

Responsiveness

The HUM detected significant improvements in hearing utility after cochlear implantation (Figure 3). Among SSD recipients, median HUM hearing utility improved from 0.70 at CI evaluation to 0.78 at 12 months post‐activation (∆0.08; P = .002). Traditional and AHL recipients improved from a median hearing utility of 0.57 at evaluation to 0.75 at 12 months post‐activation (∆0.18; P < .001). In contrast, the HUI‐3 detected significant improvements in hearing utility after cochlear implantation only in traditional and AHL recipients (0.32‐0.71, ∆0.32; P < .001) and not in SSD recipients (1.0‐0.71, ∆−0.29; P = .9).

Discussion

In this validation study of adult CI candidates and users with advanced hearing loss and benefit from cochlear implantation, the HUM demonstrated excellent test‐retest reliability, strong construct validity, effective discrimination of known groups, and expected responsiveness to change after cochlear implantation. Its construct validity, as determined by correlation with other measures of hearing‐related QoL, was higher than that of the HUI‐3. Among SSD recipients, the HUM was more responsive to HRQoL changes after implantation than the HUI‐3.

Support for the HUM's validity comes from the expected strength and duration of correlations between its domain scores and established measures of target constructs. For example, the HUM speech recognition domain correlated strongly with the CIQOL communication and the SSQ speech domains, and the HUM sound localization domain correlated strongly with the SSQ spatial domain. Conversely, the HUM domains for sound localization, tinnitus, and assistive devices did not correlate strongly with any CIQOL domains, suggesting that these constructs are not captured by the CIQOL. This is expected in the case of assistive device reliance, as the CIQOL assumes all respondents use at least one device. DHI was not strongly correlated with any HUM domain scores, consistent with the HUM's design, which does not include dizziness‐related quality of life.

Historically, utility estimates in CI populations have relied on generic instruments, most commonly the HUI‐3. 5 , 7 In this study, HUM‐derived hearing utility estimates showed stronger associations with HRQoL than those from the HUI‐3. Constructs such as tinnitus, spatial sound awareness, and music enjoyment were better captured by the HUM, supporting its use in place of the HUI‐3 hearing attribute, especially when broader aspects of hearing health are relevant.

The HUM detected a significant hearing utility gain from CI in SSD patients that were not captured by the HUI‐3. This likely reflects the HUM's ability to measure nonspeech benefits of CI in SSD. These findings again align with prior studies showing that the HUI‐3 underestimates utility gain when transitioning from unilateral to bilateral hearing. 7 , 15 This underestimation has also been observed through direct utility elicitation methods, including time trade‐off (TTO) and visual analog scale (VAS). 15 Further study is needed to determine whether utility elicited via standard gamble, TTO, or discrete choice experiments more closely align with HUM‐derived utility estimates than those from generic instruments.

Condition‐specific utility instruments are not a novel concept. Similar concerns about the limited specificity of generic utility instruments have led to the development of the Bladder Utility Symptom Scale (BUSS) 16 and the Patient‐Oriented Prostate Utility Scale (PORPUS) 17 designed to capture health utility differences in bladder and prostate cancer patients that generic instruments could not detect. Our findings support the ongoing development of condition‐specific utility instruments to improve validity and discrimination for constructs poorly measured by generic instruments.

This study has limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single tertiary CI center, which may limit generalizability. However, CI care in Ontario is highly regionalized, and patients were selected based on geographic catchment areas, reducing the risk of selection bias. Referral survey completion was near 100%. Additionally, because the validation cohort was limited to CI candidates and recipients with advanced hearing loss, the psychometric properties reported here may not generalize to individuals with milder hearing loss or those using only hearing aids. Further validation in these populations is warranted before applying the HUM in broader clinical or research contexts. Second, the patient‐reported and audiometric outcome measures used for construct validation do not represent the full range of available instruments; inclusion of generic HRQoL instruments and directly elicited utility could provide additional insight. Lastly, some measurement properties including internal consistency and structural validity were not evaluated in this study. However, in multi‐attribute health utility instruments like the HUM, items and domains are intentionally designed to be structurally independent. 6 Internal consistency, which assesses item intercorrelation, is therefore not a relevant reliability metric. Structural validity, which examines how item groupings represent constructs, is also less applicable since each domain uses a single set of response levels. Still, further study could explore whether certain HUM domains are structurally redundant.

Conclusions

The HUM is a reliable, valid, and responsive measure of hearing utility that outperforms the HUI‐3 in capturing the impact of advanced hearing loss and the benefit from cochlear implantation. By encompassing a broader range of hearing constructs beyond speech recognition, the HUM addresses key gaps in existing utility instruments. As a preference‐based tool, it enables cost‐effectiveness analyses and supports health policy decisions. The HUM is not intended to replace existing nonutility instruments, like the CIQOL, but to complement them, ensuring that the full value of hearing rehabilitation is accurately reflected in both clinical research and economic evaluations.
