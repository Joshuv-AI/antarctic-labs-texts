Genome-wide characterization and seasonal modulation of Hsp60 expression in juvenile Hilsa Shad across distinct upstream habitats
Authors: Md.Toasin Hossain Aunkor, Afroza Pervin, Md.Nazmul Hasan, Zobada Kanak Khan, Rakibul Islam Akanda, Mohammad Mehedi Hasan Khan, Kazi Mohammad Ali Zinnah, Md.Faruque Miah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

The national fish Hilsa Shad (Tenualosa ilisha) is the geographical indicator and silver pride of Bangladesh. Even though there are much bigger fish than Hilsa Shad (maximum size ≤ 63 cm), it is locally known as “King of all fish”. This teleost species has great market demand for its taste, smell and culinary properties. It also contains high levels of proteins, minerals, vitamins and omega-three fatty acids.1 Bangladesh is ranked fifth in aquaculture production and third in inland open-water fish capture, but it holds the top position in Hilsa production worldwide.2 As a single species, Hilsa contributes the highest share, approximately 12 percent, of the country's total fish production.2 According to recent data, the total Hilsa catch reached 550,428 metric tons and accounted for approximately 86 % of the global harvest among leading Hilsa-producing countries.3 Anadromous fish are ancestrally marine, but adult individuals migrate to riverine habitats to spawn. After spawning, the development from larva to fry and from fry to juvenile takes place in freshwater, after which the juvenile migrates back to the sea for further growth and maturation.4 According to the gonadosomatic index (GSI) values, February is recognized as part of the second breeding period of anadromous Hilsa shad.5 During this time, the fish migrate from the Bay of Bengal into the rivers of Bangladesh, especially the Meghna river, which serves as one of their major spawning grounds.6 After spawning, Hilsa juveniles develop in the Meghna river, which is also acknowledged as their largest nursery ground. Subsequently, they begin to migrate downstream toward the Bay of Bengal through the estuary, guided by their intrinsic migratory behavior.7 Winter in Bangladesh typically begins in November. However, in 2024 the season was unusually prolonged, with mild cold waves continuing through the end of February.8 At this stage, the average total length of juveniles in the Meghna river was around 9 cm, where the water temperature remains around 27 °C.9, 10 Notably, the Meghna river is formed within Bangladesh by the confluence of two northeastern rivers, the Surma and the Kushiyara. Over the past couple of decades, it has been observed that water levels in the Kushiyara river rise during the monsoon period (June–August), often triggering flash floods. These floods in the northeastern region flow downstream into the Meghna river via the Kushiyara. In response, Hilsa juveniles swim against the current and migrate from the upper Meghna into the northeastern riverine system, including the Surma river.11 The migration of juvenile Hilsa from the Meghna river in the southeastern part to the Surma river in the northeastern part of Bangladesh is referred to as upstream migration.12, 13 Furthermore, autumn in Bangladesh feels quite different from what is typically experienced in temperate regions such as the American Northeast. In August and September, the season remains warm and humid, with rainfall continuing well into this period. In 2024, September experienced record rainfall and record-high temperatures, making it the most unusually hostile September in the last 50 years.14 According to a report by the Bangladesh Meteorological Department, the Sylhet region where the Surma river is located recorded a maximum temperature of 38.3 °C in the middle of this month.15 This anomaly was likely a consequence of the intense South Asian heatwave that began in April 2024 and continued to affect regional weather patterns into September.16, 17 Recently, Zaman et al.18 investigated monthly variations in water temperature of the Surma river and reported the highest value in September (30.00 ± 1.29 °C). It should be emphasized that river water temperature is correlated with air temperature.19 Therefore, the temperature of the Surma river may have increased further in 2024 due to the impacts of climate change.20 Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are a family of highly conserved molecular chaperones that are upregulated in response to environmental stressors such as temperature fluctuations.21 As a key member of the HSP family, Hsp60 plays a crucial role in the cellular stress response and serves as a reliable molecular signature of thermal stress in fish.22 It is found in mitochondrial matrix of eukaryotic cells which major contribution is to assist in the folding of newly synthesized proteins as well as the refolding of denatured or misfolded proteins.23 It is important to mention that the first full genome of Hilsa Shad was sequenced in 2019, and three assemblies are currently available in the NCBI database.24 Surprisingly, despite its significant contribution to the economy and food security, the genomic information of Hilsa Shad remains insufficiently updated. Remarkably, it still lacks comprehensive genomic and proteomic data for Hsp60 and other heat shock protein family members in major biotechnological databases, including NCBI. To date, Hsp60 genes have been studied in teleosts such as Danio rerio and Oreochromis niloticus in relation to environmental stress responses.25 However, unlike other heat shock proteins, Hsp60 has not been widely studied as a molecular indicator. Nevertheless, its role remains considerably underexplored in fish, particularly in anadromous species like Hilsa shad.26 Furthermore, no genome-wide investigation has yet been undertaken to identify and characterize Hsp60 gene in Hilsa Shad, nor to examine its expression patterns under environmental stress conditions. This lack of information limits our understanding of the species’ molecular stress response and adaptive capacity in the face of changing aquatic environments. In addition to, temperature is a major abiotic stressor to which fish are continually exposed.27 For Hilsa, this is particularly critical, as their migratory journey between habitats exposes them to a wide range of temperature conditions.28 Significantly, as of yet, no studies have investigated the expression of thermo-responsive genes in Hilsa Shad during their migration across different habitats. Thus, to address these important gaps, this study employed in-depth computational and experimental approaches to identify, characterize, and determine the expression of Hsp60 genes in Hilsa Shad during their upstream migration from the Meghna to the Surma river across two different seasons. During the investigation, these genes were first identified and characterized using computer-aided analyses of their physico-chemical properties, gene structures, functional domains, conserved motifs, sequence similarity, evolutionary relationships, structural features, and predicted functions. Later, the results of all the aforementioned in silico analyses were compared with the Hsp60 sequence of the three-spined stickleback (Gasterosteus aculeatus) for validation. Like Hilsa Shad, it is also an anadromous fish and has been a leading model system for studying adaptation and addressing a wide range of biological questions for several decades.29 Moreover, it has a well-assembled genome and high-quality genomic resources, including genetic maps and sequenced genomes from diverse populations.30, 31 Finally, the relative expression levels of the identified Hsp60 paralogs were quantified in various tissue specimens of Hilsa shad juveniles collected from two different upstream habitats. This analysis was performed during winter and autumn to assess their potential role as molecular indicators of the seasonal heat stress response. The results of this study will serve as a valuable reference for the functional genomics of Hilsa Shad. It is noteworthy that, according to a recent report by the Department of Fisheries, Hilsa production in Bangladesh declined to its lowest level in seven years during the 2023–2024 season.32 This alarming trend highlights the urgent need for an improved understanding of the genetic basis of Hilsa’s adaptive responses to freshwater habitats under different environmental challenges. Such knowledge is essential for developing novel aquaculture practices aimed at boosting production.33 It also underscores the need to implement strategies for their stress-resistance breeding.34 By focusing on the Hsp60 gene and its expression patterns across two rivers with distinct season, this research offers crucial insights to inform sustainable aquaculture development in freshwater environments. Additionally, it elucidates the influence of habitat temperature on the adaptive responses of juvenile Hilsa shad in riverine environments. This understanding is important for the continued development of effective conservation strategies.

Materials and methods

Genome wide identification of Hilsa Shad Hsp60

As query sequence, the Hsp60 gene sequence of three-spined stickleback (Gasterosteus aculeatus) was retrieved from the ENSEMBL (ID: ENSGACG00000008860). Then, this sequence was used in NCBI Genome-BLAST program against the whole genome sequence of Tenualosa ilisha where we optimized the search program mega-blast. The genome blast search yielded three contigs that potentially may contain the heat shock 60 genes. Afterwards, the FASTA format of the identified contig sequences were employed to predict the gene using FGENESH, an HMM-based gene sequence prediction tool. Three-spine stickleback was selected as the organism-specific gene-finding parameter. FGENESH results predicted the position of different gene specific coding sequence/mRNA (CDS) together with their encoded proteins. Further, the existence of heat shock 60 CDS and protein sequences were confirmed by checking the functional domain of all predicted genes suggested by FGESESH gene finder. We found that the CDS and protein sequences of the Hilsa Shad Hsp60 completely matched with the functional domain of the query sequence in NCBI's conserved domain search. At the end, the three Hilsa Hsp60 gene sequences were obtained in FASTA format from the contig sequences by selecting the specific regions indicated by the CDS. The identified Hsp60 gene copies (paralogs) were then named hspd1.1, hspd1.2, and hspd1.3, following the ortholog (hspd1) from the three-spined stickleback, and the corresponding proteins were abbreviated as hspd1.1, hspd1.2 and hspd1.3.

In silico characterization of Hilsa Shad Hsp60

The protein characterization was started by measuring the physico-chemical properties with the help of ProtParam. The subcellular localization of the encoded proteins was determined by Deeploc1. The presence of cysteine residues and disulfide bridges was determined using the SoftBerry CYS_REC server, while signal peptides were predicted with SignalP 5.0. Gene structures were visualized using Gene Structure Display Server 2.0, and functional domains were illustrated with TB tools. Lastly, the motif sequences in the studied proteins were identified using MEME Suite version 5.5.7.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

For multiple sequence alignment, Hsp60 protein sequences from eight economically important anadromous fish species and three model fish were aligned with the Hilsa Shad hspd1.1–1.3 sequences using Clustal Omega. The names of the organisms and their corresponding NCBI gene IDs are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The aligned sequences were then visualized in Jalview to highlight conserved regions by color. Pairwise alignment between the query sequence and Hilsa Shad hspd1.1–1.3 was conducted using EMBOSS Needle. The same set of organisms was also used for phylogenetic tree construction in MEGA X, using default parameters and 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Protein structure modelling and functional annotation

Secondary structure prediction was carried out using two widely recognized web-based tools, PSIPRED and SOPMA, to gain deeper insights into the structural characteristics of the Hilsa Shad hspd1.1–1.3 proteins. For tertiary structure construction, homology modeling was performed for query and hspd1.1–1.3 of T. ilisha by SWISS-MODEL server. Besides that, TM-align was applied to superimpose the predicted 3D structures and identify conserved spatial arrangements between the query and the Hilsa hspd1.1–1.3 proteins. Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) annotations for the predicted proteins were retrieved via InterProScan. Corresponding PANTHER IDs were extracted and analyzed using the g: Profiler web tool to perform GO enrichment analysis, and the results were visualized as a bubble plot.35

Sample collection

Live juvenile Hilsa Shad samples were collected using traditional gill nets during daylight hours directly from fishermen’s catch sites. The winter samples were collected from the Meghna river of Chandpur district in mid-February, while the autumn samples were obtained from the Surma river of Sylhet district in mid-September of 2024 (Fig. 1). Morphometric characterization was conducted immediately after the catch, with the average size of the Meghna and Surma samples recorded as 9.05 ± 0.15 cm and 18.83 ± 0.35 cm, respectively. Later, the gill, kidney, liver and skeletal muscle tissues were isolated in situ by using sterile surgical instruments. The tissues were immediately transferred to liquid nitrogen tank and brought to the department of Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology, Shahjalal University of Science and Technology, Sylhet. However, all samples were then kept at –80 °C until RNA isolation. A proper cold chain was strictly maintained during sample transport from the collection site to the laboratory to minimize RNA degradation. This precaution ensured the integrity of the samples for downstream molecular analyses. Careful labeling and thorough documentation of all samples were maintained throughout the process to prevent misidentification and cross-contamination. These rigorous measures ensure that the genetic material extracted from the tissues reliably reflects the true biological condition of the fish in its natural habitat. RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, primer construction, and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from gill, kidney, liver, and muscle tissues using the RNAsimple Total RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, Shuangying West Road, Changping District, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with five biological replicates (5x) for each tissue. The overall yield of RNA obtained from the aforementioned tissues and their quality were assessed spectrophotometrically using the Thermo Scientific NanoDrop One device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Only the RNA samples possessing high integrity and purity (A260/A280 ratio ≥ 1.9) were utilized for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis. The first-strand cDNA synthesis was executed using the PrimeScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Nojihigashi 7-4-38, Kusatsu, Shiga 525-0058, Japan) following the supplier’s recommendations. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted in 10 μL reaction volumes containing no more than 100 ng of cDNA as template, 0.3 μL of both forward and reverse primers, and 5 μL of TR Green premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH plus) SYBR master mix (Takara Bio Inc., Nojihigashi 7-4-38, Kusatsu, Shiga 525-0058, Japan). Results and discussion

Genome wide identification of Hilsa Shad Hsp60

Genome-BLAST analysis of Tenualosa ilisha revealed three Hsp60-related contigs. The Hsp60 gene sequence of the three-spined stickleback (Gasterosteus aculeatus) was used as the query in this analysis. Each contig sequence was analyzed with a eukaryotic gene prediction server. There is a region within each contig that contains sequences similar to the query, located at different positions in the genome, as shown in Table 2. The predicted Hilsa Hsp60 gene sequences extracted from contig numbers 89, 496, and 2737 were designated hspd1.1, hspd1.2, and hspd1.3, respectively. The Genome-BLAST results showed that the contigs containing hspd1.1 and hspd1.2 had identical query coverage (7 %) and identity percentages (81.20 %). In contrast, the contig corresponding to hspd1.3 exhibited a slightly higher identity percentage (83.15 %) but lower query coverage (2 %). This pattern suggests that hspd1.3 shares greater local similarity with the query sequence but aligns over a shorter region, possibly due to partial or divergent genomic organization. Notably, all three contigs had very low E-values (ranging from 7e-49 to 9e-38), indicating highly significant matches. The differences in gene length and exon–intron structure among hspd1.1, hspd1.2, and hspd1.3 are the result of post-duplication divergence. Such divergence is a common outcome of gene duplication events during evolution.37 Furthermore, the pI values for hspd1.1–1.3 were predicted to be below 7, classifying them as acidophilic proteins similar to the query. All proteins exhibited negative GRAVY values, suggesting their hydrophilic nature. They were also predicted to localize in the mitochondria, where they play a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial protein homeostasis.38

It is worth noting that, there is minor sequence variability among the Hsp60 paralogs, which further determined in the multiple sequence alignment section. This could contribute to the slight differences observed in several physico-chemical characteristics among hspd1.1-hspd1.3.39 Closely alike the query sequence, we further predicted four cystine residues in different positions of the hspd1.1–1.3 proteins which are essential in protein stabilization and translocation.40 However, disulfide bond formation was not predicted in hspd1.1–1.3. This is because heat shock protein 60 functions in the cytoplasm, which contains strong NADPH-dependent reducing systems. These pathways maintain proteins in a reduced form, preventing disulfide bond formation.41 Thereafter, the absence of signal peptides in hspd1.1–1.3 suggests that they are secreted through unconventional or non-classical pathways. These pathways may involve mechanisms such as inhibition of lipid raft dynamics or exosome-mediated transport, which are often triggered by cellular stress.42 Moreover, the percentage of constituent amino acids of the predicted hspd1.1–1.3 proteins was calculated and compared with that of the query protein to assess their similarity. The amino acid composition revealed a high amount of valine (∼10 %), glycine (∼10 %), alanine (∼10 %), leucine (∼8%), lysine (∼7%), and glutamic acid (∼7%) in all of the heat shock 60 sequences of Hilsa Shad, resembling the query sequence obtained from the three-spine stickleback (Fig. 2). Gene structure, functional domain and motif analysis

The query sequence and hspd1.3 each consist of 10 exons, while hspd1.1 and hspd1.2 contain 11 exons. Despite this, the overall gene structure of hspd1.3 differs in exon positions compared to hspd1.1 and hspd1.2, as illustrated in Fig. 3. This finding suggested that hspd 1.3 gene of Hilsa Shad might be structured and function more similarly to the query sequence.43 On the other hand, the similar exon–intron boundary patterns of hspd1.1 and hspd1.2 genes recommended that they were highly conserved in Hilsa Shad.44 Importantly, the structural differences between hspd1.1/hspd1.2 and hspd1.3 are likely the result of gene duplication events that give rise to paralogs over evolutionary time.45Fig. 3The gene structures of Hsp60 paralogs are illustrated. Functional domain refers to a specific region or sequence of a protein that possesses a distinct biological function. Our functional domain analysis revealed that all Hilsa Shad Hsp60 protein paralogs contain chaperon like superfamily or molecular chaperone GroEL like superfamily domain (Fig. 4a), which plays an essential role in assisting protein folding.46 Besides that, Supplementary Fig. S1 displayed an illustrative representation of all the functional domains of the hspd1.1–1.3 proteins, where we found that all of them have a length of 520 amino acids. The additional amino acids outside the annotated functional domain may serve regulatory or structural roles.47 They could correspond to intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) previously observed within the equatorial domain of human Hsp60.48 However, further investigation is required to study these IDRs in Hilsa Shad hspd1.1–1.3. Afterwards, the hspd1.1–1.3 proteins found in Hilsa Shad had identical protein motif patterns or consensus sequence distribution analogous to the query sequence. Motif consensus sequences were typically 10–20 amino acid long conserved sequences that are highly significant to characterize predicted proteins. They also act as a binding site of different transcriptional factors to regulate gene expression and function in different cellular processes.49 In Fig. 4b, the appearance of same motif consensus sequences in the Hilsa Shad hspd1.1–1.3 together with the query sequence stated the probability of same regulatory mechanisms.50Fig. 4(a). Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree analysis

Protein conserved regions and sequence variations were identified through multiple sequence alignment. The analysis included three model fish species: Zebrafish (Dr), Goldfish (Ca), and Stickleback (Ga), along with eight economically important species including Japanese Medaka (Ol), Rainbow Trout (Om), Atlantic Salmon (Ss), American Shad (As), Allis Shad (Aa), Milkfish (Cc), Butterfish (Sa), and Atlantic Herring (Ch). These sequences were used for both multiple alignment and phylogenetic analysis.

The multiple sequence alignment results as shown in Fig. 5 indicated higher degree of conservation and less variation in Hsp60 of Hilsa Shad with the comparing species. Here, the degree of conservation was higher in the N-terminal region compared to the C-terminal region. This is because the N-terminal region may contain a mitochondrial import signal (MIS) sequence necessary for its import into mitochondria.51 However, some conserved and semi-conserved substitutions of amino acid residues with similar physicochemical properties were observed within the protein sequences of hspd1.1-hspd1.3. These substitutions typically arise through evolutionary processes that preserve protein structure and function over time despite sequence variation.52Fig. 5Comparative alignment of Hilsa Hsp60 paralogs with other fish species.

Comparative alignment of Hilsa Hsp60 paralogs with other fish species.

Moreover, some regions of the alignment were incomplete and partially truncated which attributes to the genetic diversity in Hsp60.53 Subsequently, we conducted sequence alignment of hspd1.1–1.3 to assess their internal divergence and compared each sequence with the query for similarity assessment. The intra-species alignment (Fig. 6) of Hilsa Shad Hsp60 paralogs revealed that hspd1.1 and hspd1.2 were identical, while hspd1.3 showed divergence from both hspd1.1 and hspd1.2.Fig. 6The alignment of Hsp60 paralogs with the query sequence.

The alignment of Hsp60 paralogs with the query sequence.

This sequence variation can be explained by the evolutionary dynamics of gene duplication, where one copy may remain conserved under purifying selection to maintain the original function. In contrast, the other copies can gradually accumulate mutations due to weaker selective pressure. Alternatively, they may undergo sub-functionalization, which enables them to assume distinct regulatory or functional roles.54 In the alignment result, the query Hsp60 sequence starts with serine (S) and contains several additional residues at the N-terminus. On the other hand, the Hilsa Hsp60 paralogs begin with methionine (M) and lack those N-terminal extensions. These extra residues in the query sequence (STRSTLQ) likely represent a mitochondrial targeting peptide, which constitutes the precursor form of the stickleback Hsp60. The absence of this sequence in Hilsa Hsp60 is due to its cleavage by mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP) upon import. As a result, the protein exists in a mature and processed form within Hilsa mitochondria.55 TargetP analysis, however, predicted MTSs in both stickleback and Hilsa Hsp60, but with different cleavage positions. This suggests that both paralogs are synthesized as precursors and subsequently processed upon import into mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. S2). Furthermore, pairwise alignment was carried out between the query sequence and the Hilsa Shad hspd1.1–1.3 sequences to evaluate one-to-one gaps observed in the previous alignment. Here, all Hilsa Shad Hsp60 protein paralogs showed around 88 % identity, 93 % similarity and only 2 % gaps in comparison with the query sequence of stickleback (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. S3). This high similarity and low gap percentage between the predicted Hilsa Shad Hsp60 paralogs and the well-characterized stickleback Hsp60 sequence suggest a strong degree of functional conservation.56Fig. 7Pairwise alignment of Hsp60 paralogs with query sequence.

Pairwise alignment of Hsp60 paralogs with query sequence.

We constructed phylogeny tree to see the evolutionary relationships and ancestral lineage of species with Hilsa Shad (Fig. 8). The three Hilsa paralogs (hspd1.1, hspd1.2, and hspd1.3) were grouped into two distinct clades. The clade contained Hilsa Shad hspd1.3 consisting of Atlantic herring, American Shad and Allis Shad which are also anadromous in behavior. Here, the bootstrap value of this branch was 100, indicating strong confidence in the evolutionary relation of the hspd1.3 sequence of Hilsa Shad with the aforementioned species.57, 58 The clade comprising hspd1.1 and hspd1.2 (bootstrap value = 100) shares a common ancestor with the clade that includes hspd1.3 together with orthologs from Atlantic herring, American shad, and Allis shad. This relationship indicates evolutionary divergence among Hilsa Shad Hsp60 paralogs. Hither, the placement of hspd1.1/1.2 and hspd1.3 into separate clades corresponded to distinct selective pressures caused by diverse environmental conditions and ecological niches. Secondary and tertiary structure evaluation

The secondary structure of a protein involves the local folding of the polypeptide chain into alpha helices, beta sheets, or random coils. In contrast, the tertiary structure describes the overall three-dimensional conformation of the protein. As illustrated by both servers, PSIPRED (Fig. 9) and SOPMA (Supplementary Table S2), three major types of secondary structures were identified in the proteins. These included approximately 53–56 % alpha helix, 7–9 % beta turn, 23–26 % random coil, and 11 % extended strand. The high percentage of alpha helices suggested that these proteins may have had stable and well-defined structural regions.61 In addition to, the presence of a significant number of random coils indicated that these proteins also had flexible regions. This flexibility is important for accommodating structural changes during protein function or interactions.62Fig. 9Graphical representation of secondary structure prediction for (a) query sequence and (b-d) Hilsa Shad Hsp60 protein paralogs.

All of our sequences yielded approximately 50 predicted templates each, which were used to construct the structural models. These templates exhibited high sequence identity and elevated Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE) values, indicating strong model reliability.63 The 3D model of our query and Hilsa Shad hspd1.1–1.3 sequences showed GMQE score 0.83 and 0.87,0.82, 0.83, respectively. These scores close to 1 claimed a high-quality model with a good level of accuracy in structure prediction. It also evaluated that the hspd1.1–1.3 models were consistent with the query model. Moving to the next, the Ramachandran plots above (Fig. 10e–h) depicted the phi (ϕ) and psi (ψ) dihedral angles of the amino acid residues. These angles are essential for understanding the conformational landscape of our protein structures. In these plots, the red regions represent the most favored (core) regions where the ϕ and ψ angles are typically found in a well-defined α-helix or β-sheet. The yellow regions were considered as less common but still acceptable for stable conformations and black dots represented disallowed regions. These results suggested that the predicted models of Hilsa Shad heat shock 60 protein paralogs were in energetically favorable conformations. Expression of Hilsa Shad Hsp60 in seasonal variation

Hilsa juveniles develop during winter in the Meghna river, which represents the minor breeding season of adult individuals. They are found in the Surma river in autumn after their upstream migration triggered by the mid-year flush floods in northeastern Bangladesh. The relative expression levels of hspd1.1-hspd1.3 across various tissue samples of juvenile Hilsa Shad under two aforementioned seasonal conditions in two distinct river environments are illustrated in Fig. 13. During autumn in the Surma river, hspd1.1 expression increased by approximately 15-fold (p < 0.0001) in the gill and 12-fold in the liver (p < 0.0001) compared to winter control group obtained from the Meghna river. Additionally, it exhibited moderate expression in muscle (∼3.7-fold, p < 0.0001), while no significant upregulation was observed in kidney. Similarly, hspd1.2 exhibited elevated expression in the gill and liver (both ∼ 7-fold, p < 0.0001), followed by the muscle (∼4.6-fold, p < 0.0001), with a modest upregulation (∼2-fold, p = 0.0090) also observed in the kidney of the autumn samples. However, hspd1.3 showed a marked upregulation in the gill (∼12-fold, p < 0.0001), whereas only modest increases (∼1.6–2.6-fold, p = 0.0012–0.3625) were observed in other tissues compared to the winter samples from the Meghna river. In the gills, all three paralogs showed a sharp and significant upregulation during autumn (hspd1.1 > hspd1.3 > hspd1.2). This pattern demonstrated that gill tissue was highly temperature-sensitive during the warmer months, when increased Hsp60 levels likely provided enhanced HSP-mediated protection against temperature-induced oxidative damage in Surma river.74, 75 Thereafter, the upregulation of Hsp60 (hspd1.1 > hspd1.2 > hspd1.3) in the liver indicated that thermal stress is also higher in the hepatic tissue of Hilsa Shad juveniles at elevated autumn temperature in Surma river.76, 77 It also suggested that, in autumn the level of protein damage and subsequent requirement of Hsp60 is possibly greater in liver than the other tissues.78 Furthermore, autumn muscle samples from the Surma river exhibited a moderate level of Hsp60 expression compared to those from the Meghna river juveniles. This suggests that Hsp60 may serve as an indicator of mild thermal stress in skeletal muscle, which may moderately affect the muscle physiology of juvenile Hilsa Shad.79Fig. 13Relative expression levels of Hsp60 gene paralogs in juvenile Hilsa Shad collected during different seasons from two upstream river sites. The modest increase in Hsp60 mRNA transcripts in muscle tissue may indicate enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis. It could also support the folding of nuclear-encoded proteins within mitochondria compared to the control samples.80 Moreover, in response to environmental stress, the harmful accumulation of unfolded proteins triggers the induction of Hsp60 in tissues.81 In the current study, the slight upregulation of renal Hsp60 (hspd1.2 > hspd1.3 > hspd1.1) suggests a low level of misfolded or unfolded protein accumulation. This finding aligns with the relatively mild thermal stress experienced by the kidneys of juvenile Hilsa Shad during the warm autumn conditions in the Surma river. However, it is significant to recognize that the expression of Hilsa Hsp60 exhibited tissue specificity in response to seasonal changes in river temperatures.82 Due to organ-specific differences in energy expenditure and metabolic activity, energy metabolism has been acknowledged as a crucial driver of HSP expression. Heat stress impacts energy production by altering glycolysis. Therefore, like other chaperones, the expression of Hsp60 may vary across different tissues under heat stress as a result of differences in thermal sensitivity in their respective organs.83 Such tissue-specific expression differences among Hsp60 paralogs are also consistent with the positive association between gene expression and sequence divergence described by Lian et al.84 In addition to, the upregulation of Hsp60 paralogs across all examined tissues in autumn samples strongly indicates activation of the stress response in juvenile Hilsa shad living in environment with elevated temperature.85 Overall, these findings confirmed that seasonal changes play a vital role in modulating Hsp60 expression in juvenile Hilsa shad across the diverse habitats encountered during their upstream migration. It is important to acknowledge that the consistent upregulation of Hsp60 paralogs during autumn strongly supports temperature as a key driver of their expression. However, additional habitat-specific factors such as dissolved oxygen, nutrient availability, pollutants, and salinity fluctuations may also influence the observed patterns. Since Hilsa aquaculture has not yet been developed, establishing controlled in vivo experimental conditions using live fish remains a significant challenge. Therefore, future studies under in vitro conditions using a Hilsa shad cell line are planned to disentangle the specific effects of temperature from other environmental variables.

Conclusion

This study provides the first detailed investigation of the Hsp60 in Hilsa Shad. Three Hsp60 paralogs were identified across the genome, followed by comprehensive in silico characterization. The results revealed strong similarity to the Hsp60 of a well-established model fish species, suggesting a high degree of evolutionary conservation. This conservation supports the functional reliability of the predicted sequences and strengthens the validity of the comparative approach. Furthermore, the expression analysis revealed that these genes respond to seasonal temperature variations across different habitats. During autumn, when temperatures tend to soar, gene expression was significantly upregulated in thermally sensitive tissues of fish from the Surma river compared to those from the Meghna river in winter. The highest expression was observed in the gill, followed by the liver, muscle, and kidney, indicating their tissue-wise variation in heat stress response. These findings confirm the contribution of Hsp60 as molecular indicator of thermal stress in this migratory fish. This research deepens our understanding of stress adaptation in juvenile Hilsa Shad and provides valuable insights for the development of sustainable aquaculture practices in riverine environments. Moreover, the outcomes of this study provide a molecular framework that can guide future investigations on climate resilience, conservation strategies, and species management in fluctuating environmental conditions.
