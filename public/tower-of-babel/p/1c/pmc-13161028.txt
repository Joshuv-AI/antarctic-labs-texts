Giant Cell Arteritis Following COVID-19 Vaccination: A Consumer-Stimulated Analysis
Authors: Aishwarya N Shetty, John H Mallard, Daneeta Hennessy, Nicholas Wood, Hazel J Clothier, Jim P Buttery, the SAFESIG–GP Primary Health Network (PHN) Collaboration Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGiant cell arteritis (GCA) is an immune-mediated vasculitis of large and medium arteries, mainly affecting older adults. Prompted by a consumer concern, a few spontaneous reports after coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccination and Australian regulator signal, we investigated a possible association between GCA and vaccination.MethodsThis study analysed multiple data sources from January 2021 to September 2024. We reviewed spontaneous GCA reports submitted to the statewide vaccine safety service, Surveillance of Adverse Events following Vaccination in the Community (SAEFVIC), and applied self-controlled case series (SCCS) to two large Australian healthcare datasets [general practice (GP) and linked hospital data]. SCCS used a 1-42-day risk window, stratified by vaccine type, age, and sex. Incident GCA cases were identified via keyword matching and diagnostic codes [Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED-CT)/International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM)].ResultsSix cases of GCA were spontaneously reported, four with onset within 42 days postvaccination, all following the adenoviral vectored Vaxzevria® {reporting rate 0.10 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.03–0.27]}. The proportional reporting ratio signal detection method did not indicate a safety signal.Of 2700 incident cases of GCA identified across primary and hospital care datasets, 293 occurred within 42 days of COVID-19 vaccination.There was no increased risk of GCA following COVID-19 vaccination in either dataset [GP relative incidence (RI): 0.96 (95% CI 0.76, 1.21); hospital RI: 0.79 (95% CI 0.68, 0.91)], including by vaccine type, age, or sex.ConclusionsUsing three Australian data sources, this study found no increase in GCA presentations within 6 weeks of COVID-19 vaccination. These consumer-stimulated findings support informed decisions, strengthen vaccine safety evidence, and help clinicians counsel patients.

Key Points

1. Consumer reports triggered investigation into GCA following COVID-19 vaccines.2. No increased GCA risk was found within 6 weeks of COVID-19 vaccination.3. This study reinforces vaccine safety and public confidence.

Introduction

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) or temporal arteritis is an immune-mediated vasculitis of large and medium arteries of the head and neck. The pooled global incidence of GCA is 10 per 100,000 adults over 50 years of age [1], with incidence increasing with age and cases rarely observed in persons aged under 50 years [2]. Clinical presentation includes severe headaches, scalp tenderness and jaw and tongue pain and visual problems. In severe cases it can cause sudden vison loss in one or both eyes, stroke and aortic aneurysm [3, 4].

In June 2022 Victoria’s statewide vaccine safety service, Surveillance of Adverse Events following Vaccination in the Community (SAEFVIC), was contacted by a 60–70-year-old man suffering loss of left eyesight, diagnosed as GCA, a few weeks following vaccination with a messenger RNA (mRNA) coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) booster dose (mRNA-1273). The consumer raised concern that vaccination had triggered their GCA onset.

Consumer concerns, a small number of spontaneous GCA reports following COVID-19 vaccination identified through routine vaccine safety surveillance, ongoing investigation by the Australian regulator and the existing evidence base, largely limited to case reports advocating for larger studies [5, 6], enabled this question to be prioritised for further investigation. Additionally, the immune-driven nature of GCA provides a biological rationale for investigating whether immune stimuli, such as vaccination, could rarely trigger the condition. Accordingly, we used three large independent Australian healthcare datasets: spontaneous reporting, primary care, and linked hospital data.

Methods

Data Sources

Spontaneous Reporting

SAEFVIC is the central vaccine safety surveillance service for Victoria, Australia’s second largest state, with an estimated population of 7 million in 2024 [7]. SAEFVIC operates a comprehensive surveillance model integrated with clinical follow-up and referral of individuals with significant adverse events or ongoing concerns for future vaccination to specialist immunisation clinics when required [8, 9]. During the COVID-19 vaccination program, adverse event reports were received through spontaneous reporting by healthcare professionals or vaccine recipients, as well as Victorian reports through AusVaxSafety, a federal government-funded cohort event monitoring system that issued follow-up text message surveys on day 8 and day 42 after vaccination (Fig. 1) [10].

Characteristics of data sources used in the study

General Practice Presentations

General practice (GP) data were routinely collected and curated through the Population Level Analysis and Reporting (POLAR) platform, with approval and permission from participating Primary Health Networks (PHNs) across Victoria and New South Wales (NSW), Australia. These included Central and Eastern Sydney, Eastern Melbourne, Western Victoria, Gippsland, South-Eastern Melbourne, and South-Western Sydney PHNs. The resulting dataset comprises approximately 12 million routinely collected electronic health records, which are provided de-identified within a secure data environment for vaccine safety investigations [11, 12]. The GP dataset included all vaccinations given in the participating practices but may not include all vaccinations given in other settings (Fig. 1).

Hospital Presentations

The Vaccine Safety Health Link (VSHL) is a repository of linked statewide administrative health datasets in Victoria, Australia [13]. VSHL includes the Australian Immunisation Register (AIR) records of Victorian residents, hospital records of emergency department presentations from the Victorian Emergency Minimum Dataset (VEMD) and hospital admissions from the Victorian Admitted Episodes Dataset (VAED), notifiable condition and pathology records from the Public Health Event Surveillance System (PHESS) and Transmission and Response Epidemiology Victoria (TREVI), and death records from the Victorian Deaths Index (VDI) [14]. Data linkage is conducted by the Centre for Victorian Data Linkage and linked, de-identified data analysed within the secure data environment (Fig. 1).

Identification of GCA Diagnoses

Analysis of spontaneous reporting used a consensus list of keywords derived from the reports’ free-text fields describing GCA events. The identified keywords were “giant cell arteritis” and “temporal arteritis”. In GP data, GCA cases were defined by Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine (SNOMED)-coded diagnoses of giant cell arteritis (414341000), temporal arteritis (400130008), Takayasu's disease (359789008), and aortic arch syndrome (359789008). In hospital data, cases were identified by admissions with International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM) codes M31.4 [Aortic Arch Syndrome (Takayasu)], M31.5 (Giant Cell Arteritis with Polymyalgia Rheumatica) and M31.6 [Other Giant Cell Arteritis (including temporal arteritis)] (Fig. 1).

Methods

The study period was from 1 January 2021 to 30 September 2024. As the COVID-19 vaccine rollout in Australia began on 21 February 2021, we included the preceding days to provide an adequate pre-vaccination control period for individuals vaccinated early in the program.

Spontaneous reports submitted to SAEFVIC were screened for keywords and filtered to include only cases with GCA onset within 42 days of COVID-19 vaccination. Symptom onset was based on the self-reported date or, if unavailable, the report submission date. Data analysed included age, sex, vaccination date, vaccine brand, and time to symptom onset. Adverse event counts and reporting rates (RR) were calculated per 100,000 doses administered, using AIR data, with 95% Poisson confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values. We also conducted a signal detection analysis using the proportional reporting ratio (PRR) disproportionality method. This involved comparing the frequency of GCA reports following COVID-19 vaccination with those following all other vaccines. A PRR greater than 2, with a corresponding chi-squared value greater than 4, was considered to indicate a potential safety signal [15].

In the GP and linked dataset, we conducted a self-controlled case series (SCCS) analysis and included people who had a GCA diagnosis and at least one COVID-19 vaccination. The relative incidence (RI) of GCA was assessed for the 42-day risk window compared with the control period (all periods outside the risk window and in study period) (Fig. 2) [16–18]. We chose a 42-day post-vaccination risk window on the basis of multiple considerations, including clinical advice; the timing of adaptive immune responses, which typically develop within 6 weeks; and prior vaccine safety analyses of autoimmune and inflammatory conditions [19–22]. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted using 1–7, 1–14 and 1–28-day post-vaccination risk windows. To capture incident cases, we only included individuals who had a GCA diagnosis in the study period and no prior history of the condition back to 2019. A washout of 365 days was applied between two diagnoses in the study period. Analysis was stratified by COVID-19 vaccine type [adenoviral vector ChAdOx1-S, mRNA (BNT162b2, mRNA-1273) or protein-subunit NVX-CoV2373]. Analysis was further stratified by age (< 65 and ≥ 65 years of age) and sex. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.3) using the SCCS package, with significance set at p < 0.01.

Risk window, control period and observation period of self-controlled case series analysis

Results

Spontaneous reporting data had six GCA cases reported during the observation period, with four having an onset date within 42 days. All four cases were post-Vaxzevria® with 75% reported following dose 1. Reports were received from both healthcare professionals and members of the public, including reports submitted by a family member or local health authority. Median days to onset was 11 days (range 1–28 days). The reporting rate for all vaccine types was 0.02 per 100,000 (95% CI 0.01, 0.05) and for ChAdOx1-S 0.10 per 100,000 (95% CI 0.03, 0.27) doses (Table 1). There was no difference in reporting rate by age, sex or dose (Table 2). A signal detection analysis using the PRR method did not indicate a safety signal, with a PRR of 0.05 and a chi-squared value of 2.75. Additionally, clinical follow-up of the reports did not indicate a safety concern, consistent with the quantitative PRR results.

In both GP and linked hospital SCCS datasets, the majority of cases were in persons aged ≥ 65 years (90% in GP and 84% in hospital) (Fig. 3) and in female patients (67% in GP and 66% in hospital), with most having received at least two vaccine doses (85% in GP and 99% in hospital). Vaccines administered were predominantly BNT162b2, followed by ChAdOx1-S, mRNA-1273 and NVX-CoV2373. Approximately 11% of individuals in each dataset experienced a new GCA presentation within 6 weeks of vaccination (Table 3). In both cohorts, the number of GCA presentations during the 6-week risk window did not exceed those observed in the pre- and post-vaccination control periods (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This consumer-flagged investigation led to the interrogation of large Australian healthcare datasets, which found no increase of GCA in the 6 weeks following a COVID-19 vaccination compared with other times in the study period.

Our study is the first to investigate this relationship using large real-world datasets. So far published evidence rely upon case reports of GCA occurrences following COVID-19 vaccinations. The majority of reported episodes occurred following mRNA vaccines, with viral vector vaccines the next most frequently described. Cases were reported in both male and female patients, with most cases occurring in older individuals with a median age of 74 years [5, 6, 23–34].

There are several theories as to why GCA may occur following vaccination. As an antigen-driven disease, GCA has previously been reported after influenza and varicella-zoster vaccines [35, 36]. This association is thought to result from an interplay of genetic predisposition and environmental triggers, such as infections or immune activation. COVID-19 vaccines may stimulate a strong immune response via the spike protein, which may, in rare cases, trigger vascular inflammation and contribute to GCA onset [37]. In addition, the lipid nanoparticles in COVID-19 mRNA vaccines can trigger strong inflammation, including the release of immune signals such as IL-1β and IL-6, which may enhance the vaccine’s immune-stimulating (adjuvant) effect [38].

In contrast to our findings, a global pharmacovigilance study by Mettler et al. reported a disproportionality signal for GCA following COVID-19 vaccination using data from VigiBase, the World Health Organization (WHO) global safety database [39]. However, spontaneous reporting systems such as VigiBase (and SAEFVIC) are prone to key limitations, including underreporting, lack of denominator data and susceptibility to reporting and selection bias. Importantly, the analysis was conducted early in the COVID-19 vaccine rollout (up to August 2021), a period characterised by heightened clinical vigilance, media attention, and stimulated reporting. This may have contributed to inflated signal detection, especially in the absence of appropriate comparison groups. In contrast, our study used routinely collected Australian healthcare data with clearly defined denominators and applied a self-controlled case series design to minimise confounding and temporal bias, providing a more robust estimate of any vaccine-associated risk [39].

This study exemplifies how vaccine safety investigations can be initiated in response to consumer-reported concerns. In this instance, the investigation was prompted by multiple credible signals: a direct query from a consumer, investigation from the Australian regulator, relevant cases identified in an Australian jurisdictional spontaneous reporting system (SAEFVIC) and numerous published case reports in international literature. Together, these factors provided sufficient grounds for a formal investigation. Investigating a well-substantiated signal is essential to maintaining public trust, especially when there is a plausible biological mechanism and consistent temporal association.

Our study has some limitations. The spontaneous reporting data are subject to reporting bias; however, the use of GP and linked datasets helps mitigate this, as individuals with GCA symptoms are more likely to present to their health professional as the first point of contact than make a report to spontaneous reporting. A limitation of the GP SCCS dataset is that it does not capture all COVID-19 vaccinations, since individuals may have been vaccinated in healthcare settings not registered on the POLAR platform. The consistent findings across both GP and linked data sources along with the linked hospital dataset providing a complete immunisation history during the observation period helps overcome this limitation.

Consumer-led reports complement traditional surveillance by highlighting rare or unexpected events. Transparent reporting of findings whether or not there is a significant association is found to reinforce vaccine safety and helps counter misinformation. It also signals that the system takes consumer concerns seriously and responds with scientific scrutiny. Such work also informs clinical practice by equipping health professionals with timely, evidence-based insights to guide patient conversations, particularly around vaccine hesitancy. These findings can support updates to clinical guidance and contribute to responsive, evidence-based vaccine policy.

Conclusions

This study, using large Australian datasets, found no increased risk of incident GCA presentations within 6 weeks of COVID-19 vaccination. As a consumer-flagged investigation, this study helps maintain public confidence in the vaccination program while contributing to the growing safety evidence base. We hope these findings support consumers in making informed health decisions and assist clinicians in confidently counselling their patients.
