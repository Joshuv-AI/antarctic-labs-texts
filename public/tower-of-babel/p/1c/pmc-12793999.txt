Paving the Way to a Radiation-Free Era
Authors: Eue-Keun Choi, Hyunho Ryu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: atrial fibrillation, catheter ablation, intracardiac echocardiography, zero fluoroscopy

Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most prevalent sustained cardiac arrhythmia globally, imposes formidable clinical and socioeconomic burdens. Catheter ablation has become a cornerstone of rhythm control strategies for AF, offering symptom relief and improved outcomes for appropriately selected patients. Historically, fluoroscopy-guided catheter ablation has been regarded as the gold standard. However, its inherent reliance on ionizing radiation has garnered heightened scrutiny, given its well-documented risks in modern electrophysiological practice.

Despite its utility, fluoroscopy exposes both patients and health care professionals to cumulative doses of radiation, which are not without consequence. Patients are at risk for stochastic effects such as malignancy, as well as deterministic injuries, including radiation-induced skin changes and cataracts. Operators and laboratory personnel also face occupational hazards, with prolonged exposure linked to elevated rates of cancer, as well as orthopedic complications related to the use of heavy lead aprons required for radiation protection. Recognizing these multifaceted hazards, there is a pressing imperative to adopt procedural strategies that uphold safety and efficacy while reducing radiation exposure for all stakeholders.1 Accordingly, the principle of “as low as reasonably achievable,” now foundational to interventional cardiology, serves as the ethical and practical benchmark for minimizing occupational and patient radiation dose and is increasingly emphasized in current guidelines and consensus statements.

With the rising incidence of AF and the parallel increase in ablation procedures, intensive investigation has focused on techniques to reduce or eliminate fluoroscopic imaging during AF ablation. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE)–guided nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy (also known as zero or near-zero fluoroscopy) AF ablation is one such approach. Several studies have shown that ICE-guided nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy ablation achieves procedural success and safety profiles comparable with conventional fluoroscopy-guided methods (Table 1).2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Recent meta-analyses consistently show that nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy ablation is feasible and safe and achieves acute and long-term efficacy comparable with conventional methods.7,8 Specifically, the OR for 12-month AF recurrence with nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy ablation was 1.34 (95% CI: 0.77-2.34; P = 0.297), while fluoroscopy time was shorter by 8.8 minutes (95% CI: 5.9-11.9 minutes; P < 0.001) compared with conventional methods.7 Furthermore, major guidelines, including statements from the Heart Rhythm Society, the European Heart Rhythm Association, and the Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society, increasingly advocate for the adoption of these strategies, especially in populations more vulnerable to the effects of radiation.9,10 Despite these advances, it is important to note that most published studies have been conducted at single centers, which may restrict the broader applicability of their outcomes. This limitation highlights the critical need for expansive, multicenter research efforts to validate these promising techniques across diverse clinical settings.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Wang et al11 report the PAF-ICE trial, a prospective, multicenter, noninferiority randomized study comparing nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy ablation with conventional fluoroscopy-guided ablation for AF. A total of 448 patients with paroxysmal AF were enrolled across 15 centers in China and randomized equally to the nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy arm (n = 223) or the conventional fluoroscopy arm (n = 225). In the nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy group, procedures were performed under intracardiac echocardiographic guidance to avoid or minimize x-ray use, although fluoroscopy was permitted when deemed necessary. Both groups underwent pulmonary vein isolation as the procedural backbone, and patients were scheduled to be followed at 3-month intervals through 12 months and every 6 months thereafter. However, this schedule was disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic in China.

Over a median follow-up period of 12.2 months, the HR for recurrent AF in the nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy arm vs the conventional arm was 0.949 (95% CI: 0.774-1.164; P = 0.858), thereby meeting the predefined noninferiority margin for efficacy. The primary safety endpoint (a composite of all-cause mortality, stroke or transient ischemic attack, and other serious adverse events) occurred at similar rates in both groups (RR: 1.055; P = 0.975). Notably, true nonfluoroscopy procedures were achieved in 125 of 223 patients (56.1%) in the nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy group, and operators wore radiation protection equipment for significantly less time during nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy ablation (P < 0.001). These results demonstrate that nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy AF ablation achieves efficacy and safety outcomes comparable with those of conventional methods, supporting broader adoption of radiation-sparing workflows in electrophysiology practice.

Although several prior trials demonstrated the feasibility of ICE-guided nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy AF ablation, only a few adopted a multicenter design, which is a key strength of the PAF-ICE study. Multicenter assessments are critical for demonstrating the generalizability of novel approaches across institutions with differing sizes, procedural volumes, and resource capacities. By enrolling patients from 15 centers, the PAF-ICE trial provides compelling evidence that nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy ablation is not only feasible but also effective and scalable in a broad array of real-world hospital settings. This enhances the external validity of the findings and supports broader adoption of this radiation-sparing technique.

A notable contribution of this study is the quantification of lead apron wear time, a metric rarely addressed in previous reports focused on procedure or fluoroscopy duration. The significantly longer wear time in the conventional group highlights the potential musculoskeletal benefits of minimal-fluoroscopy approaches. The study’s rigorous methodology, encompassing detailed complication assessments and clear procedural definitions, enhances its validity, while the inclusion of operators with varying experience levels supports the real-world applicability of these workflows beyond specialized centers.

Despite the study’s many strengths, several limitations warrant discussion. First, some centers enrolled only a few patients (the 2 centers recruiting the fewest enrolled just 2 and 6 patients), which can compromise generalizability. Second, fluoroscopy use was left to the operator’s discretion, introducing procedural heterogeneity. Although prior studies reported the need for minimal “rescue” fluoroscopy in only 6% to 28% of cases, this trial observed a considerably higher rate of 44%, reflecting variability in operator comfort with minimal-fluoroscopy workflows and differing levels of ICE proficiency.7,8 Third, follow-up schedules were adapted during the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in variable visit intervals. However, time-to-event analyses helped mitigate bias; complete uniformity of surveillance could not be ensured. Last, the median fluoroscopy time in the conventional group was already limited to 5 minutes, and only one-half of the minimized-fluoroscopy group underwent a truly zero-fluoroscopy procedure, suggesting technical and practical challenges. The clinical value of reducing such minimal radiation exposure remains a matter of individual physician judgment, especially when balanced against the demands and risks of adopting a zero-fluoroscopy approach. Importantly, successful implementation requires substantial experience with intracardiac echocardiographic catheter manipulation, which is a nontrivial skill. These factors collectively limit the generalizability of the findings and suggest that the observed statistical reduction in fluoroscopy time alone may not be sufficient to support widespread adoption without careful consideration of procedural complexity and operator expertise.

Looking forward, several avenues warrant further investigation. Comparative trials involving alternative energy sources such as cryoballoon or pulsed-field ablation could identify modality-specific benefits for radiation-sparing workflows. Additionally, cost-effectiveness analyses will be essential to weigh the upfront investment in operator training against potential long-term savings from reduced radiation-related health risks and work-related musculoskeletal disorders. Such evaluations will be critical to inform broader adoption and policy decisions regarding minimal- or zero-fluoroscopy strategies in electrophysiology practice.

In summary, the PAF-ICE trial establishes ICE-guided nonminimized or minimized fluoroscopy AF ablation as a safe, effective, and broadly feasible alternative to conventional fluoroscopy-guided procedures across varied clinical settings. By achieving noninferior arrhythmia outcomes while significantly reducing radiation exposure time, this multicenter study paves the way toward a radiation-free era in AF management. Widespread adoption of these techniques promises to protect both patient and operator health without compromising procedural success and may soon redefine standard practice in electrophysiology.
