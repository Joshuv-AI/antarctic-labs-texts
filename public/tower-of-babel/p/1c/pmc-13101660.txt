Subspecialty interests

When I first finished fellowship, I did not think of myself as subspecialized. Now, 10 years into community practice, I see community pediatric orthopaedics as a discipline: families should be able to show up with any musculoskeletal problem and receive thoughtful, high-level care close to home. While I am the only pediatric orthopaedic surgeon in my group, my close connections with academic mentors and colleagues ensure that even patients with complex diagnoses receive the right level of care.

An important part of my current practice centers on a weekly (Wednesday) spine clinic focused on adolescent idiopathic scoliosis and nontraumatic back pain. Early in my training, I was influenced by growing evidence including the BrAIST trial (NEJM 2013) demonstrating that bracing can meaningfully alter the natural history of scoliosis. During fellowship at Children's Hospital Los Angeles, working with David Skaggs and learning from leaders like Michael Vitale, I saw how thoughtful nonoperative management could meaningfully influence outcomes.

When I entered community practice in 2016, I set a simple goal: build a clinic where routine scoliosis care could be delivered locally at a high level, with the expectation that many patients could avoid surgery and, when possible, halt progression or even reduce the curve over time. Over the past 10 years, through close partnership with orthotists, an emphasis on patient initiative over compliance, and systems that support families, Wednesdays have become my favorite day of the week.

Education and early influences

I grew up as a competitive swimmer and entered college with a strong interest in research. At the University of Minnesota, I studied chemistry and biochemistry and initially planned to pursue a PhD in chemistry. I was drawn to the structure and logic of science. But during a developmental biology course in 1998, when I first encountered C. elegans as a model organism, something shifted. I became fascinated not just with molecules, but with how systems develop and organize themselves.

At the same time, after finishing my swimming eligibility, I stayed on as a fifth-year student coach. That experience shaped me in a different way. I realized how much more rewarding it was to work directly with people than to focus solely on experimentation. Coaching forced me to think about growth, feedback, and long-term development in a very human way.

I ultimately applied to MD/PhD programs and completed my training at Stanford. My doctoral work was in developmental biology, and that perspective has never left me. Even now, I think about growth and healing as biological processes that unfold over time. We are not imposing change—we are guiding it.

Training and finding my direction

I completed my orthopaedic surgery residency through the Harvard Combined Orthopaedic Residency Program, including 6 months at Boston Children's Hospital. Like many residents, those years were intense and often a blur. What has stayed with me, though, was a question I asked during morning trauma rounds. I remember wondering aloud why so many pediatric injuries were being transferred into Boston rather than managed locally in surrounding communities. The answer was simple: historically, community surgeons had cared for many of these children, but that pattern had diminished over time. Dr. Kasser commented that a well-trained pediatric orthopaedist could build a meaningful practice in the community, serving patients locally and building a fulfilling professional life.

At the time, I had planned a largely academic career. I expected to be a general pediatric orthopaedist at an academic center, dedicating most of my time to basic science research. But fellowship changed that trajectory. The fellows functioned with significant autonomy: we were credentialed as attending physicians, took independent call, managed inpatient consults, and ran our own clinic sessions on Fridays. That responsibility rekindled something in me. Caring for patients and their parents—directly, longitudinally, and with ownership—felt similar to coaching years earlier. It clarified that what mattered most to me was not dividing my time between clinic and the laboratory, but being fully present in the care of children and families. I began to see community practice not as a compromise, but as an opportunity.

Becoming a reviewer

When I first volunteered to review for JPOSNA®, I was unsure whether I truly belonged in that role. I was not actively writing manuscripts, leading a funded research project, or working within an academic center. I wondered whether my evaluations would be sufficiently rigorous and whether I could critically assess study design and methodology at the level expected.

After completing my first review, I read the other reviewer's comments and realized that, despite stylistic differences, we had identified the same core strengths and concerns. That pattern continued, reinforcing the idea that my instincts aligned with those of my peers.

I read manuscripts asking myself a simple question: Does the data support the conclusions being drawn? If someone were to read only the abstract, could they trust that it accurately reflects the full paper? Would this study meaningfully influence how I care for patients? Peer review is, at its core, a conversation among peers. Community surgeons are peers. If we do not participate in that process, we are not fully serving the patients who ultimately benefit from careful and balanced scholarship.

Outside the hospital

Outside of the clinic and the operating room, most of my time revolves around my family. With children in middle and high school and my wife in academics, much of our schedule centers on their practices and activities. In recent years, I have taken up trail running while they are at their practices. I prefer trails—both to stay away from traffic (so I do not become a patient myself) and to maintain strong movement patterns and reflexes as I grow older.

Looking forward

Practicing in the community has reinforced that most patients do not need my surgical skill, but instead my expertise from many years of training and practice. My early interest in developmental biology continues to shape how I think. I now see biology as the healer, and myself as its assistant.

That perspective also informs how I approach emerging tools such as artificial intelligence. In preparing this profile, I used artificial intelligence (AI) to help organize and refine my thoughts and prepare this text. Physicians remain responsible for judgment and interpretation. “AI assists. We think.” As our patients increasingly interact with these tools, we have a responsibility to engage with them ourselves to understand their capabilities and limits, and to help shape their use in medicine.

I continue to value the ability to move between academic and community environments. Reviewing for JPOSNA® has allowed me to reconnect with scholarship while remaining grounded in day-to-day patient care. I hope more community surgeons choose to participate in that process. Our role is not different, and it is not secondary. It is simply part of the shared responsibility: advancing knowledge in a way that ultimately serves children and their families.
