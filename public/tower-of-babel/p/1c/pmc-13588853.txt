Associations between waist-to-height ratio and hormonal–metabolic profiles in midlife women: evidence from a population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Miao Deng, Zhifen Zhang, Nan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCentral adiposity increases during the menopausal transition, yet its hormonal signatures are not well defined. This study examined whether the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) reflects coordinated variation in sex-steroid, thyroid, and metabolic pathways in midlife women, and evaluated the discriminative value of key endocrine markers for identifying elevated WHtR.MethodsWe conducted a population-based cross-sectional analysis of 17,379 women aged 40–60 years who underwent standardized WHtR measurement and serum assessment of sex-steroid hormones (estradiol, testosterone, progesterone, FSH, LH), thyroid hormones (free T4, free T3, TSH), and metabolic markers (lipid profile, HbA1c). Associations were tested using univariate correlations and multivariable regressions adjusted for age and BMI. Age-adjusted WHtR percentiles were estimated using quantile regression. Hormonal–metabolic clustering was mapped using correlation matrices. Logistic regression and ROC analyses evaluated the performance of estradiol, free T4, and their combination for identifying WHtR ≥ 0.50.ResultsWHtR values were in the healthy range for 68.4% of women, while 29.3% exceeded the ≥ 0.50 threshold. WHtR showed weak negative correlations with estradiol (r = −0.024, P = 0.0019) and free T4 (r = −0.017, P = 0.026), with small effect sizes, and these markers remained independently associated with WHtR after adjusting for age and BMI. ROC analyses demonstrated moderate discrimination by FT4 (AUC 0.67), compared with estradiol (AUC 0.61), with improved accuracy when combined (AUC 0.76).ConclusionLower estradiol and FT4 levels were associated with higher central adiposity in midlife women. WHtR showed weak, but significant associations with both hormones, yet the small effect sizes and low explanatory power render it clinically unsuitable for individual-level assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00404-026-08518-9.

What does this study adds to the clinical work

Waist-to-height ratio is associated with subtle but significant changes in endocrine and metabolic parameters in midlife women, including lower estradiol and FT4 levels. The associations are modest, indicating limited clinical utility at the individual level, and are more appropriate for population-level risk assessment.

Introduction

The menopausal transition is a critical period of physiological recalibration in which the progressive decline in ovarian function intersects with age-related changes in metabolic, vascular, and inflammatory pathways [1]. Although menopause is a universal biological transition, the lived experience varies widely. Women in East Asia consistently report a heavier burden of vasomotor symptoms, sexual difficulties, sleep disturbances, and lower urinary tract complaints—symptom clusters that often disrupt daily functioning and diminish overall well-being [2, 3]. These clinical manifestations reflect more than the simple withdrawal of ovarian steroids; they arise from a broader reconfiguration of thermoregulatory circuits, autonomic balance, tissue sensitivity to hormonal fluctuations, and systemic metabolic tone. As such, understanding why symptom intensity varies so widely among women of similar chronological age remains a central challenge in menopause research [4].

Growing evidence suggests that central adiposity as an important modifier of menopausal physiology [5]. Visceral fat is not merely a passive energy store, but a hormonally active and immunologically dynamic tissue, capable of influencing aromatase activity, sex-steroid balance, and low-grade inflammation [5, 6]. Through cytokine release, adipokine signaling, and altered vascular reactivity, excess abdominal fat has been associated with changes in thermoregulatory function and urogenital tissue integrity. These processes may interact with neuroendocrine networks that are already sensitive during the menopausal transition, suggesting that women with greater central adiposity may experience increased endocrine variability and symptom burden [7]. However, these mechanisms are inferred from prior studies and were not directly assessed in the present analysis. Our earlier work in a clinically verified cohort of Chinese midlife women underscored this pattern: overall adiposity showed only modest links with selected menopausal symptoms. However, traditional anthropometric measures, especially BMI and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), did not adequately capture the biologically meaningful aspects of fat distribution [8]. BMI could not distinguish metabolically active visceral fat from peripheral stores, while the WHR only partially reflected central adiposity [9]. These limitations underscored the need for a more precise and physiologically grounded index. Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), which normalizes waist circumference to body stature, offers a direct reflection of abdominal fat loading and has emerged as a robust indicator of cardiometabolic, inflammatory, and endocrine dysfunction. However, its potential to identify early hormonal destabilization during the menopausal transition has received remarkably little empirical attention [10, 11].

Large cross-sectional and population studies reinforce this gap, as inconsistent staging methods, non-standardized symptom measures, and unverified community samples have produced variable regional findings and left key mechanistic pathways unclear [1, 12, 13]. Importantly, few investigations have examined whether central adiposity precedes or predicts endocrine instability, and none to date have leveraged large-scale clinical cohorts to test whether WHtR captures the coordinated hormonal deviations that accompany menopausal symptom expression [14]. Establishing such a link would advance understanding of the metabolic–endocrine interface that shapes menopausal health and would offer a practical, clinic-ready tool for early risk stratification [15].

In addition to circulating hormone levels, interindividual variability in cardiometabolic risk during the menopausal transition may also be influenced by genetic determinants of steroid metabolism [16, 17]. For example, polymorphisms in enzymes involved in estrogen biotransformation, such as cytochrome P450 1B1 (CYP1B1), have been associated with differences in cardiovascular risk profiles in midlife women [16, 18]. Variation in estradiol hydroxylation capacity may contribute to heterogeneity in hypertension risk, particularly in the context of central adiposity and environmental exposures [19, 20]. These findings indicate that endocrine–metabolic variability may reflect both circulating hormone concentrations and genetically determined differences in hormone metabolism.

In this context, our study was designed to determine whether WHtR reflects underlying hormonal alterations and symptom burden in midlife women. Using cross-sectional data from 17,380 clinically assessed women in Eastern China, we characterized metabolic, thyroid, and sex-steroid profiles across established WHtR categories and evaluated their independent contributions to central adiposity. Higher WHtR was consistently associated with lower free thyroxine (FT4) and estradiol (E2) levels, alongside adverse lipid and glucose profiles, even after adjusting for age and BMI. Domain-specific models identified FT4 and E2 as the most robust hormonal correlates of WHtR, and discriminative analyses showed that combining thyroid and estrogen markers improved identification of women with high WHtR, albeit with modest overall predictive power. Women with elevated WHtR also exhibited greater menopausal symptom burden and a more unfavorable metabolic–inflammatory profile. In this study, WHtR is considered not only as an anthropometric index, but also as a simple surrogate reflecting overall endocrine–metabolic status, especially adiposity-related hormonal and metabolic changes. Taken together, our findings suggest that WHtR is associated with patterns of hormonal imbalance and symptom severity during the menopausal transition, and may serve as a practical marker to help identify women at heightened metabolic–endocrine risk.

Methods

Study population

As outlined in Fig. 1, participants were drawn from a large clinical cohort of women who attended the Perimenopausal Health Evaluation Program at Hangzhou Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital between June 2022 and June 2025. The program serves as a regional referral center in Eastern China and provides standardized endocrine testing, metabolic profiling, and symptom assessment for midlife women [8]. A total of 20,462 women underwent preliminary screening. Eligible participants were aged 40–65 years and had lived in Hangzhou long enough to ensure stable environmental and lifestyle exposure. Reproductive stages were assigned according to the STRAW + 10 framework using menstrual characteristics, follicle-stimulating hormone concentrations, and physician assessment [21]. All anthropometric, hormonal, metabolic, and symptom evaluations were performed during a single clinical visit to minimize diurnal variability and enhance internal consistency. After applying clinical, menopausal, and data-quality exclusions, 17,379 women remained with complete datasets suitable for endocrine–adiposity analyses [22]. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women were eligible for inclusion if they were classified within the menopausal transition or natural postmenopause according to STRAW + 10 staging [21], had completed all required anthropometric, hormonal, metabolic, and symptom assessments, and had provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were applied in a stepwise manner to ensure physiologically interpretable endocrine profiles. Women with surgical menopause, specifically hysterectomy or bilateral oophorectomy, were excluded at the outset, along with those presenting with major systemic illness, such as decompensated hepatic or renal impairment, uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, or active malignancy. Additional exclusions targeted untreated endocrine disorders affecting the hypothalamic, pituitary axis, including thyroid dysfunction, pituitary disease, and hyperprolactinemia. Women undergoing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or systemic corticosteroid treatment, as well as those who had recently used menopausal hormone therapy, selective estrogen receptor modulators, or phytoestrogen supplements, were removed to avoid pharmacologic disruption of endogenous hormonal patterns. Pregnancy, lactation, and menstrual patterns inconsistent with STRAW + 10 category definitions also resulted in exclusion. A final data-quality review eliminated participants with missing anthropometric values, incomplete hormone or metabolic panels, missing symptom scores, or biologically inconsistent laboratory measurements. These processes yielded a cohort with clean and interpretable endocrine and adiposity characteristics.

Data collection and clinical characterization

All participants underwent a structured, clinician-administered interview that captured demographic information, reproductive and menstrual history, lifestyle behaviors, comorbid conditions, and medication use. Interviewers were trained to follow standardized protocols to minimize inter-observer variability. The clinical evaluation included a comprehensive physical examination and anthropometric measurements of height, weight, and waist circumference, all performed using standardized instruments. Venous blood samples were obtained in the morning following an overnight fast and were processed in a single certified laboratory to ensure analytic uniformity. Hormonal assays included estradiol, testosterone, progesterone, follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, free thyroxine, free triiodothyronine, and thyroid-stimulating hormone. Metabolic profiling comprised fasting glucose, insulin, triglycerides, high-density and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol, and glycated hemoglobin. Menopausal symptom burden was assessed using the validated modified Kupperman Index (mKI), which quantifies vasomotor, psychosocial, somatic, sexual, and urinary symptoms. Educational attainment was also recorded during the structured interview using a standardized form aligned with the Chinese National Education System. Participants were categorized into five levels: primary/below, junior high, senior high/technical, college, and university or above, to enable evaluation of socioeconomic gradients in central adiposity. Together, these standardized procedures yielded a rigorously characterized cohort suitable for examining the relationship between central adiposity, hormonal regulation, and menopausal symptom experience.

Menopausal symptom assessment

Menopausal symptoms were assessed using the validated Chinese adaptation of the mKI, following the standardized procedures established in our previous study, where the full operational workflow is described in detail [24, 25]. The instrument evaluates 13 symptoms covering vasomotor disturbances, mood and sleep alterations, somatic complaints, sexual dysfunction, and urinary symptoms. Each item carries a predetermined clinical weight and is rated for severity from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe). Weighted scores are summed to generate both domain-specific indices and an overall mKI score that reflects global symptom burden. All assessments were administered by trained clinicians using uniform interviewing procedures, ensuring consistency in symptom elicitation and minimizing inter-observer variability. The mKI has been widely applied in Chinese clinical research and demonstrates strong psychometric reliability, making it well-suited for quantifying symptom patterns across large midlife populations.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were obtained by certified nursing staff using standardized techniques in accordance with international anthropometric guidelines and the Chinese national standards for clinical measurement. Height was measured to the nearest millimeter using a calibrated stadiometer, and weight was recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg with a routinely calibrated digital scale, with participants wearing light indoor clothing and no shoes. Waist circumference was measured with a non-stretch measuring tape at the midpoint between the lower costal margin and the iliac crest, and hip circumference at the level of maximal gluteal prominence, following the recommended procedures of the World Health Organization (WHO) STEPS protocol [26]. All measurements were taken with participants standing upright, feet shoulder-width apart, and breathing normally. Duplicate measurements were performed, and a third measurement was taken when discrepancies exceeded prespecified quality-control thresholds; the median value was used for analysis. These procedures adhered to the same standardized anthropometric protocol implemented in our previous study, ensuring continuity and methodological rigor. WHtR was calculated as waist circumference (cm) divided by height (cm) and served as the primary index of central adiposity. WHtR categories were defined according to internationally accepted cardiometabolic risk cut-offs endorsed by the European Association for the Study of Obesity (< 0.40, 0.40–0.49, 0.50–0.59, ≥ 0.60) [23, 27]. Although BMI was recorded using the conventional formula of weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), it was used solely as an adjustment variable given the superior physiological relevance of WHtR for evaluating visceral adiposity. WHR was intentionally excluded from multivariable models to avoid mathematical collinearity and because WHtR is a more robust indicator of endocrine-metabolic risk in midlife women.

Hormonal and metabolic assessments

Fasting venous blood samples were collected in the early morning after an overnight fast to minimize circadian variation and nutrient-related fluctuations in metabolic and endocrine markers. All analyses were performed in a single accredited central laboratory at Hangzhou Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, following standardized operating procedures and the national clinical laboratory quality-control framework. Samples were processed promptly after collection and analyzed using automated chemiluminescent immunoassay platforms routinely used in tertiary hospitals for endocrine evaluation. Serum sex-steroid hormones, including estradiol (E2), testosterone, progesterone, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and prolactin were quantified using automated chemiluminescence analyzers with both internal and external quality-control verification. Thyroid function was assessed through free thyroxine (FT4), free triiodothyronine (FT3), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), measured using assays calibrated against national reference standards and compliant with performance specifications recommended by the Chinese Society of Endocrinology. Calibration curves, analytical sensitivity, and intra-/inter-assay coefficients of variation were monitored routinely throughout the study period. Metabolic biomarkers were determined using standardized enzymatic and immunoturbidimetric assays on fully automated biochemistry analyzers. These markers included fasting glucose, fasting insulin, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), apolipoproteins A1 and B, lipoprotein(a), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). All analyses adhered to national and international clinical chemistry guidelines. Batch–to–batch consistency was evaluated continuously, and laboratory values outside predefined biological plausibility thresholds underwent re-analysis to ensure accuracy. Analytical precision for all endocrine and metabolic assays remained within internationally accepted limits throughout the study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp.) and R version 4.3 for confirmatory modeling. Continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviations; categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences across WHtR categories were examined using ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing. Associations between WHtR and hormonal or metabolic variables were first explored via Pearson correlation. Variables with P < 0.10 were entered into multivariable linear regression models to identify independent determinants of WHtR. Domain-specific models (hormonal, thyroid, and metabolic) were constructed, followed by a fully adjusted model including age, BMI (where not collinear with WHtR), menopausal status, and relevant lifestyle factors. To evaluate the discriminative ability of hormonal markers for elevated central adiposity (WHtR ≥ 0.50), logistic regression models and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) were generated. Model performance was summarized using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity, and AUCs were compared using the DeLong test. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with values < 5 considered indicative of acceptable collinearity for all models. Model fit and explanatory power were evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) for linear regression models. An exploratory mediation analysis was performed to assess whether triglycerides may partially mediate the association between hormonal variables and WHtR. All P values were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic, clinical, and endocrine–metabolic characteristics across WHtR categories

Table 1 presents the full distribution of demographic, hormonal, thyroid, lipid, and glycemic parameters across the four WHtR categories in the analytic cohort of 17,379 women. The majority of participants were classified within the healthy WHtR range (0.40–0.49; n = 11,788; 68.4%), with substantially fewer women in the very lean (< 0.40; n = 544; 3.2%) or high-risk (≥ 0.60; n = 273; 1.6%) groups. Age differed modestly across categories (P < 0.001), rising slightly in the healthy group (53.07 ± 10.51 years) relative to the others. In contrast, BMI showed a non-monotonic pattern across WHtR categories (P < 0.001), decreasing from 24.15 ± 3.13 kg/m2 in very lean women to 22.20 ± 7.03 kg/m2 in the healthy range and then increasing to 24.27 ± 3.15 and 24.00 ± 2.90 kg/m2 in the increased-risk and high-risk groups, respectively. Notably, the relatively large standard deviation in the healthy group suggests greater heterogeneity within this category. Other menopausal symptom measures, inflammatory and hemorheological parameters, and bone-health indices, listed in Supplementary Table S1, also reinforced this multi-system pattern. CRP levels nearly tripled across the WHtR continuum (P < 0.001), and homocysteine, whole-blood viscosity, and erythrocyte aggregation indices all shifted toward less favorable profiles with increasing WHtR, consistent with heightened inflammatory tone and microcirculatory strain. These additional parameters will be reported in forthcoming analyses. Mean mKMI scores across WHtR categories are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Average symptom scores ranged from approximately 17–18 points in the Very Lean and Healthy groups to 19–20 points in the Increased-Risk and High-Risk groups, with wide within-group variability (approximately 10–30 points). These findings indicate that higher WHtR is associated with a small, but measurable increase in menopausal symptom burden. However, socioeconomic patterns, examined in Supplementary Table S2, showed that women with primary or junior-high education constituted the majority of the increased-risk and high-risk WHtR groups, whereas university-educated women were disproportionately represented in the very lean and healthy categories. This gradient reflects well-established associations between lower educational attainment, higher adiposity, and heightened cardiometabolic vulnerability. Hormonal regulation demonstrated marked variation across WHtR strata. FSH was highest in the healthy category (32.90 ± 11.18 IU/L) and lower at both extremes (P < 0.001), indicating a nonlinear pituitary–gonadal response to adiposity. LH showed minimal differences (P = 0.134). Prolactin differed significantly (P < 0.001), with higher values in the healthy group (15.07 ± 7.31 ng/mL) compared with the other categories. Sex-steroid hormones exhibited clear downward trends with increasing WHtR: estradiol declined from 58.80 ± 23.12 pg/mL in the very lean group to 46.94 ± 23.64 pg/mL in healthy women, 44.71 ± 23.22 pg/mL in the increased-risk group, and 43.97 ± 23.22 pg/mL in high-risk women (P < 0.001). Progesterone showed a similar gradient (P < 0.001), and testosterone displayed structured variation—highest in the healthy group (0.68 ± 0.22 ng/mL) and lower in the higher WHtR categories (P < 0.001). Together, these patterns reflect coordinated reductions in ovarian steroid availability as central adiposity increases.

The thyroid axis showed comparably ordered shifts. FT4 decreased steadily from 1.21 ± 0.25 ng/dL in very lean women to 1.09 ± 0.18 ng/dL in the high-risk category (P < 0.001), while TSH rose reciprocally from 2.08 ± 1.90 to 2.37 ± 1.93 µIU/mL across the same strata (P < 0.001). FT3 showed statistically significant, but clinically modest variation (P = 0.008). This inverse FT4–TSH pattern is consistent with reduced peripheral thyroid hormone availability and increased pituitary drive in women with greater central adiposity. However, not all lipid parameters followed a strictly monotonic trend across WHtR categories. For example, LDL-C and total cholesterol showed some variability between groups, suggesting that lipid profiles may be influenced by factors beyond central adiposity alone. Triglycerides increased from 1.67 ± 0.64 mmol/L in very lean women to 1.98 ± 0.68 mmol/L in the high-risk group (P < 0.001). Total cholesterol and ApoB rose sharply with WHtR (both P < 0.001), reflecting a progressively more atherogenic lipid phenotype. HDL-C showed modest reductions (P < 0.001), while LDL-C demonstrated a structured pattern (P < 0.001), underscoring WHtR’s strong alignment with lipid dysregulation. ApoA1 and Lp(a) also differed significantly across categories.

Glucose-regulation markers followed a similar upward trajectory. Fasting blood glucose increased from 5.36 ± 0.84 to 5.45 ± 0.82 mmol/L across WHtR categories (P < 0.001), paralleled by fasting insulin (P < 0.001) and HbA1c (P < 0.001). These findings indicate early deterioration in glycemic control beginning in the ≥ 0.50 WHtR range.

Together, these demographic, endocrine, metabolic, inflammatory, socioeconomic, and technical findings demonstrate that WHtR reflects not merely central adiposity, but a multi-system physiological profile. Increasing WHtR is generally associated with lower sex-steroid and thyroid hormone availability and less favorable metabolic profiles, although not all parameters showed strictly progressive trends across categories.

Endocrine and metabolic signatures of central adiposity

To clarify which physiological pathways are most closely linked to central adiposity, we examined the univariate and multivariable associations between key hormonal and metabolic indices and WHtR. This analysis was undertaken to determine whether specific endocrine or metabolic patterns, independent of age and BMI, serve as meaningful correlates of WHtR in midlife women.

In univariate analyses, only three biomarkers exhibited statistically significant, but modest associations with WHtR. Estradiol showed a small inverse correlation (r = –0.024, P = 0.0019), consistent with the downward estrogen gradient observed across WHtR categories. Free T4 demonstrated a similarly weak negative association (r = −0.017, P = 0.026), suggesting that women with higher WHtR display reduced peripheral thyroid hormone availability. In contrast, triglycerides were positively correlated with WHtR (r = + 0.017, P = 0.021), reflecting the early emergence of a more atherogenic lipid milieu with increasing central adiposity. Independent predictors of WHtR in the fully adjusted model

A fully adjusted model was constructed to examine which physiological variables were associated with WHtR after accounting for age, BMI, and shared metabolic variation. This approach allows assessment of whether endocrine or metabolic markers retain independent association with central adiposity beyond demographic and anthropometric factors.

Within this multivariable framework, estradiol remained a significant negative predictor of WHtR (β = −0.00005; 95% CI −0.000076 to −0.000018; P = 0.001), indicating that lower estrogen concentrations continue to characterize women with higher WHtR values even after extensive adjustment. FT4 also preserved a significant inverse association (β = −0.00295; 95% CI −0.005724 to −0.000174; P = 0.037), suggesting a potential link between thyroid function and central adiposity as captured by WHtR.

In contrast, triglycerides were not independently associated with WHtR in the fully adjusted model (β = + 0.00075; P = 0.168), implying that their univariate association may be partly explained by other covariates. Age (P = 0.113) and BMI (P = 0.560) were also not independently associated with WHtR in this model. All variables demonstrated acceptable variance inflation factor (VIF) values (< 5), indicating no substantial multicollinearity. To further explore the attenuation of triglycerides in the fully adjusted model, an exploratory mediation analysis was performed. The results suggested that triglycerides may partially mediate the association between estradiol and WHtR, as well as between FT4 and WHtR, although the magnitude of the indirect effects was small. These findings should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional design.

Overall, these findings suggest that, after adjustment for key demographic and metabolic factors, estradiol and FT4 remain independently associated with WHtR. Predicted WHtR across the FT4–E2 hormonal spectrum

Discriminative performance of hormonal predictors for high WHtR

We next assessed whether these hormonal markers can discriminate women with clinically elevated central adiposity, defined as WHtR ≥ 0.50. ROC analyses based on logistic regression models (Fig. 5 and Table 5) showed that estradiol and FT4 alone had limited discriminative ability. Estradiol achieved an AUC of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.59–0.63), with 54% sensitivity and 62% specificity, while FT4 showed modestly better performance with an AUC of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.65–0.69), 61% sensitivity, and 68% specificity. This agrees with the linear-model findings, where effect sizes were statistically significant, but modest. Combining E2 and FT4 increased the AUC to 0.72 (95% CI: 0.70–0.74), with balanced sensitivity and specificity (70% and 71%, respectively), significantly outperforming FT4 alone (P = 0.008). The highest discrimination was observed in the fully adjusted model—including FT4, E2, triglycerides, age, and BMI, which achieved an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.74–0.78), with 74% sensitivity and 74% specificity. This represents a modest improvement compared with models including hormonal markers alone (P < 0.001 vs. FT4). Overall, these findings indicate that while estradiol and FT4 capture relevant physiological signals, their individual discriminative ability is limited, and improved classification of elevated WHtR requires integration of hormonal, metabolic, and anthropometric variables. Discussion

In this large population-based cross-sectional cohort of midlife women, the WHtR emerged as a sensitive physiological index that integrates multiple layers of endocrine and metabolic regulation. Although routinely used as a simple anthropometric marker, WHtR in this study reflected a broader biological landscape involving ovarian steroid decline, altered thyroid hormone availability, triglyceride-rich dyslipidemia, impaired glycemic control, and heightened inflammatory and hemorheological strain. The coherence of these patterns across independent analytical approaches indicates that central adiposity during the menopausal transition is not an isolated physical attribute, but a visible manifestation of deeper endocrine destabilization. Alternatively, endocrine changes and central adiposity may develop in parallel through shared metabolic and inflammatory pathways.

A key finding was the consistent decline in estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone across rising WHtR strata, with parallel alterations in gonadotropin profiles [28, 29]. These differences align with the well-characterized reorganization of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis during the menopausal transition, yet our data indicate that adiposity meaningfully shapes this trajectory [30]. Visceral adipose tissue is a source of inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α and leptin, that can blunt hypothalamic GnRH plasticity, thereby altering FSH and LH secretion [31]. Consistent with our findings, Guo et al. reported that greater visceral fat area was associated with lower circulating estradiol concentrations in overweight and obese women, supporting the notion that central adiposity can influence reproductive hormone dynamics [32]. At a cellular level, diminished estradiol availability may be linked to visceral fat accumulation by impairing mitochondrial efficiency, suppressing AMPK-driven lipid oxidation, and weakening ERα-dependent control of adipocyte growth [33, 34]. Together, these mechanisms provide a coherent physiological basis for the hormonal gradients observed across WHtR categories in the present cohort.

Equally notable was the decline in FT4 and the reciprocal rise in TSH with increasing WHtR. This pattern persisted in fully adjusted models and in prediction surfaces across the FT4–E2 spectrum, pointing to an independent and physiologically coherent association between central adiposity and reduced peripheral thyroid hormone availability [14]. Inflammatory processes have been proposed to influence type-2 deiodinase activity, cellular transport of thyroid hormones, and hepatic T4 clearance, which may be associated with this “relative hypothyroxinemia” pattern. However, given the cross-sectional design, these relationships should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Thyroid hormones regulate basal metabolic rate, lipid turnover, brown-adipose thermogenesis, and hepatic VLDL secretion; even modest reductions in FT4 may impair metabolic flexibility and favor triglyceride accumulation [35]. The observation that low FT4 and low estradiol jointly predicted the highest WHtR values underscores the interdependence of thyroid and ovarian axes in maintaining metabolic homeostasis during midlife [36, 37]. The metabolic profiles accompanying elevated WHtR further support this integrative model [38, 39]. Triglycerides, LDL-C, ApoB, fasting glucose, and fasting insulin increased steadily across WHtR categories, marking a transition toward insulin-resistant, triglyceride-enriched physiology beginning around WHtR ≥ 0.50. Similar patterns have been reported in population-based studies, with evidence indicating that visceral adiposity, rather than BMI, is more closely associated with impaired glucose tolerance and adverse metabolic outcomes [40]. In addition, evidence based on WHR supports the role of central fat distribution in lipid remodeling. For example, stepwise regression analysis has shown that WHR is independently associated with non-HDL-C levels, even after adjustment for age, BMI, smoking status, and genetic factors, suggesting that central adiposity contributes to atherogenic lipid profiles beyond general adiposity [41, 42]. These findings provide complementary support for the present WHtR-based results. Central adiposity indices have also been linked to hepatic enzyme activity. Previous studies have reported that both WHR and WHtR are positively associated with gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) levels, with WHR showing an independent association after adjustment for metabolic covariates [43]. These findings suggest that visceral fat accumulation may be related to subclinical hepatic dysfunction, further supporting the systemic metabolic impact of central adiposity. Potential mechanisms proposed in previous studies include inflammation-related changes in adipose tissue function, altered adipokine profiles, and downstream effects on glucose and lipid metabolism. These processes may help explain the parallel changes observed in metabolic and hormonal profiles [44]. Notably, BMI was not independently associated with WHtR in the fully adjusted model. This likely reflects shared variance between general and central adiposity measures, as both indices incorporate aspects of body fat distribution. Such collinearity may attenuate the independent contribution of BMI when WHtR is included in the same model.

The domain-specific models confirmed that central adiposity reflects the convergence of thyroid, sex-steroid, and metabolic pathways. Reduced FT4, elevated triglycerides, and lower estradiol were the clearest physiological determinants of WHtR, with FT4 showing the strongest independent effect. Comparable multi-axis interactions have been documented by Fröhlich et al. [45], who showed that coordinated modulation of thyroid hormones and sex steroids shapes energy homeostasis, adipose distribution, and metabolic risk, reinforcing the integrated framework proposed in our study. This modest classification accuracy is unsurprising, given that visceral fat distribution reflects a complex network of behavioral, inflammatory, hepatic, and autonomic influences, not merely endocrine levels. Indeed, recent frameworks emphasize that chronic inflammation, adipose dysfunction, and insulin resistance mediate the metabolic complications of obesity rather than single biomarkers alone [46]. These findings mirror previous analyses of metabolic syndrome prediction, where composite profiles consistently outperform isolated endocrine markers.

This study has several strengths, including its large sample size, detailed hormonal and metabolic phenotyping, and the integration of descriptive, multivariable, and predictive modeling approaches. The convergence of trends across independent analytic frameworks strengthens confidence in the biological relevance of the findings [47]. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes determination of temporal ordering; longitudinal sampling is needed to identify whether hormonal shifts precede changes in WHtR or occur as a consequence of increasing adiposity. Estradiol was measured once per participant, and cycle-related variability may introduce random noise in early perimenopause. Moreover, unmeasured determinants, including diet, sleep patterns, vasomotor symptom frequency, physical activity, and stress physiology, could also influence both endocrine function and adiposity [48]. Finally, the relatively small number of very lean and high-risk participants may limit precision at WHtR extremes.

Future studies would benefit from longitudinal hormone profiling, with repeated measures of FT4, estradiol, deiodinase activity, thyroid hormone metabolites, and detailed lipid subfractions to clarify the trajectories of endocrine change leading to central adiposity. Multi-omics analyses, including metabolomics, lipidomics, steroid metabolite profiling, and inflammatory cytokine panels, could help delineate molecular signatures that distinguish benign from maladaptive adiposity trajectories. Imaging-based quantification of visceral and hepatic fat, combined with assessments of brown-adipose activity and skeletal-muscle mitochondrial function, may further illuminate mechanisms linking thyroid–estrogen cross-talk to adipose remodeling [49]. Such work will be essential for determining whether early detection of FT4–E2 imbalance can guide targeted interventions aimed at preserving metabolic stability across the menopausal transition.

Beyond circulating hormone levels, genetic variability in steroid metabolism may also contribute to the heterogeneity of cardiometabolic risk observed in midlife women [16, 18–20]. Evidence from population-based studies indicates that polymorphisms in estrogen-metabolizing enzymes, such as CYP1B1, are associated with differential cardiovascular risk, particularly in the presence of central adiposity and environmental exposures [18, 50]. This suggests that the associations observed in the present study may reflect not only endocrine status, but also underlying differences in hormone biotransformation capacity, which were not assessed in this analysis.

Overall, the present findings suggest that WHtR reflects more than an anthropometric marker and may capture an integrated physiological signal related to endocrine–metabolic changes in midlife women [51]. The coordinated declines in estradiol and FT4, together with the increases in triglycerides and glycemic markers, indicate a convergent hormonal–metabolic profile that may be associated with increased cardiometabolic risk [52]. These patterns highlight the importance of early detection and provide a framework for future studies targeting the endocrine aspects of central adiposity.

Importantly, the absolute change in predicted WHtR across the FT4 range was modest (approximately 0.005 units). Although statistically significant, this small effect size suggests limited clinical relevance at the individual level and indicates that the observed associations reflect subtle physiological variation rather than strong effects.

The strengths of this study include its large sample size, clinical verification of menopausal status, and standardized anthropometric and symptom assessments [53]. In addition, exploratory analysis suggests that lipid metabolism may represent one pathway linking hormonal changes to central adiposity, although this requires confirmation in longitudinal studies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; prospective data are required to determine whether adiposity drives hormonal changes or vice versa. Second, the observed effect sizes were very small, with correlation coefficients below 0.03 and an R2 of 0.04. This indicates that the model explains negligible individual-level variance. Thus, despite statistical significance driven by the large sample size, WHtR lacks clinical utility for assessing hormonal status in individual women. Third, the study population was recruited from a single urban center in Eastern China, which limits generalizability to other regions or ethnic groups. Fourth, symptom assessment relies on self-report, which may introduce recall bias and be influenced by cultural factors. Fifth, we lacked data on several potentially relevant variables, including diet, physical activity, phytoestrogen intake, and psychosocial stress [54, 55]. These unmeasured confounders may bias the observed associations between adiposity and hormonal profiles. Sixth, we did not measure detailed inflammatory markers or additional hormones that could clarify underlying mechanisms [56]. Finally, hormone levels were assessed only once. In perimenopausal women, hormonal fluctuations are substantial, so a single blood draw may not accurately represent an individual’s true endocrine status.

Conclusion

In summary, WHtR shows statistically significant, but weak associations with declining estradiol and FT4 during the menopausal transition. Effect sizes are small, and individual-level variation remains largely unexplained. At the population level, WHtR may still capture broad physiological patterns in midlife women. Longitudinal studies with larger effect magnitudes are needed before clinical application can be considered.
