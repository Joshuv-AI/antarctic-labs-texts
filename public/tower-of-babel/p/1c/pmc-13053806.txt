Self-righting strategies of wingless stick insects on a smooth substrate
Authors: Weijia Zong, Zhouyi Wang, Zhendong Dai, Xiaohui Yin, Yueyue Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySelf-righting enables terrestrial animals to recover from inversion, with strategies shaped by body morphology. While extensively studied in compact-bodied insects, the mechanisms in wingless, elongated stick insects remain unclear. Here, we quantified the self-righting biomechanics of Medauroidea extradentata on smooth horizontal substrates using motion capture. Based on body posture and limb configuration, we identified three self-righting patterns—rolling, semi-pitching, and pitching—with maximum trunk pitch angles of 21.7°, 34.2°, and 58.5°, respectively. Rolling dominated (69%) due to its energetic and temporal efficiency. This strategy relied on coordinated limb-abdomen interaction, with active abdominal bending playing a pivotal role—validated by biomechanical simulations where rigid-abdomen models failed to right. These findings show that stick insects adopt quasi-static, morphology-constrained strategies, in contrast to the momentum-based tactics of compact-bodied species. This work highlights how extreme body shape dictates adaptive self-righting and offers principles for designing bioinspired robots capable of recovering under geometric constraints.

Introduction

Self-righting from a surface—the autonomous recovery from an overturned position—is essential for the survival of terrestrial animals. Such inversion is often triggered by destabilizing pitch or roll moments during locomotion on slopes, low-friction surfaces,1,2 or complex obstacles.3,4 It also frequently occurs during dynamic behaviors such as jumping and climbing.5,6 Regardless of the cause, being overturned increases vulnerability to predation, dehydration, and excessive energy loss.7 Consequently, the inability to self-right rapidly impairs critical biological functions, including foraging, escape, and reproduction.8 To mitigate these risks, animals have evolved a variety of morphology-dependent ground self-righting strategies that represent key adaptations for ecological success.9 Deciphering these strategies offer not only insights into biomechanical evolution but also principles for the design of bioinspired robotic.10

Effective self-righting from a surface universally requires overcoming gravitational potential barriers and frictional resistance through morphology-specific mechanisms.9 Springtails, for instance, employ abdominal arching to create rotational pivots,7 whereas beetles and cockroaches generate angular momentum via coordinated limb and/or wing strokes.8,9 Click beetles utilize a unique latch-mediated spring-actuation mechanism, storing and rapidly releasing elastic energy to execute ballistic jumps to achieve reorientation,11,12 while high domed-shelled turtles rely on geometric stability to passively roll upright.13,14,15 However, these strategies depend critically on specialized anatomical features—such as wings,9 highly deformable body segments,9,16 or monostatic shells8,13—that are absent in wingless stick insects characterized by extreme morphological elongation.

Stick insects (Medauroidea extradentata) exemplify such constrained morphologies, characterized by exceptionally slender bodies and limbs (body length-to-width ratio ≈28:1; cf. locust ≈14:2,10 cockroach ≈5:29), rigid segmented trunk and limbs, and a flexible abdomen. This configuration creates significant biomechanical challenges for ground self-righting: the elongated body and limbs result in an expansive support polygon, while long moment arms necessitate high joint torques.17 Furthermore, structural rigidity of the trunk may impede force application, and the relatively heavy abdomen is often perceived as a passive inertial load. Despite documented righting capability in the air18 and sophisticated limb coordination during complex locomotion,19,20,21 the appendage-body coordination and the functional integration of rigid and flexible segments during ground self-righting remain uncharacterized. This knowledge gap is particularly significant for bioinspired robotics, where rigid-flexible hybrids are increasingly employed for mobility in complex terrain.22,23,24 Deciphering the biological principles of ground righting in such structures can provide essential guidance for the design of adaptive self-righting robots operating under severe morphological constraints.

We hypothesized that stick insects would not employ dynamic self-righting strategies comparable to those of compact-bodied insects (e.g., cockroaches9 and beetles8), which typically rely on rapid limb and wing movements. Furthermore, given their exceptionally elongated body plan, we postulated a behavioral preference for rolling over pitching, as the former minimizes the gravitational potential energy barrier.

To test these hypotheses, we first quantified biological kinematics to derive motion parameters, which were subsequently used to drive a biomechanical simulation model of Medauroidea extradentata. A motion capture system was utilized to characterize self-righting on a smooth horizontal surface, enabling the classification of distinct self-righting patterns based on body posture and limb configuration. We further identified the functional roles of the abdomen, trunk, and limb pairs during both the searching (preparation) and righting stages. Building upon these biological data, we constructed comparative biomechanical models with flexible and rigid abdomens to validate the mechanical feasibility and transferability of the identified self-righting strategy while demonstrating the functional necessity of abdominal flexibility. This integrative experimental-robotic approach enabled systematic classification and mechanical validation of self-righting strategies within a controlled biomechanical framework.25,26 By uncovering the self-righting mechanics of wingless, elongated stick insects, this study enhances our understanding of how morphological constraints and limb-abdomen coordination shape behavioral strategies. These findings also offer foundational biological principles for designing slender, limb-driven robotic systems capable of operating effectively under similar morphological limitations.

Three distinct kinematic patterns were identified (Figure 3 and Videos S1, S2, and S3): Rolling (pattern 1), characterized by rotation about the longitudinal axis; pitching (pattern 2), involving pronounced trunk elevation and aerial swinging of the fore- and midlimbs; and semi-pitching (pattern 3), representing an intermediate strategy between the two. Temporal analysis revealed significant differences in the total self-righting duration among patterns (Kruskal-Wallis: df = 2, χ2 = 17.5, p < 0.001) (Figure 2C). Pairwise comparisons showed the total self-righting duration of pattern 2 was significantly longer than that of the other two patterns (pattern 1 vs. 2, p < 0.001; pattern 3 vs. 2, p = 0.003). Specifically, pattern 1 exhibited the shortest righting duration (1.2 ± 0.98 s), which was significantly shorter than those of Pattern 3 (2.2 ± 0.72 s) and pattern 2, with the latter showing the most prolonged duration (3.6 ± 2.1 s) (Figure 2C). Significant variation was also observed in searching duration (Kruskal-Wallis: df = 2, χ2 = 30.21, p < 0.001), which ranged from 0.7 ± 0.6 s in pattern 3 to 3.3 ± 1.2 s in pattern 2, with pattern 1 averaging 1.5 ± 1.0 s. Consistent with its superior temporal efficiency, Rolling was the dominant self-righting pattern (∼69%), whereas pitching (∼17%) and semi-pitching (∼14%) were employed less frequently (Figure 2D).

Preliminary analysis confirmed that due to the bilateral symmetry of the stick insect, leftward and rightward maneuvers are kinematically equivalent and occur with comparable probability across all patterns. Accordingly, for clarity and consistency, subsequent kinematic analyses focus on rightward self-righting attempts as a representative dataset.

Stick insects exhibited a stereotyped sequence of motor events during the self-righting process. This conserved motor pattern consistently comprises two primary stages: (1) a searching stage, characterized by trunk elevation and periodic oscillatory movements of the aerial limbs to maximize spatial exploration; and (2) a self-righting stage, driven by synergistic limb–abdomen coordination, in which the abdomen and supporting limbs generate the necessary rolling torque, followed by body rotation and the re-establishment of ground contact by the swing-side limbs (Figure 3B). While this framework is shared, kinematic analysis (Figure 3, also see Figures S1 and S2) reveals that the transition between these stages is governed by pattern-specific initiation mechanisms: rolling and semi-pitching are driven by rolling-based triggers (condition 1), whereas pitching is triggered by pitching-based mechanisms (condition 2) (Figure 3B(a)).

Rolling (pattern 1): during the searching stage, the forelimbs and abdomen maintained ground contact with the trunk slightly arched (Figure 3B). The forelimbs exhibited relatively stable femur-tibia angles (θLF, θRF), whereas the midlimbs and left hindlimb displayed periodic aerial fluctuations (Figure S2A(a)). The trunk was prominently elevated, with the forelimbs and abdomen supporting on the ground until the trunk pitch angle reached the threshold (β1∗) (Figure 3B(b)). The righting maneuver was preceded by a rapid drop in the right hindlimb angle (θRH) to 60 ± 18° (∼40% of sequence duration; ∼1.1 s). Subsequently, body rotation was initiated as the right hindlimb retracted to a threshold angle (αRH1∗) of 6.4 ± 4°. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant bilateral asymmetry in minimum hindlimb α angles (df = 1, F = 33.88, p < 0.001; Figure 3A), which facilitated longitudinal rolling. Following this trigger (∼57% duration), the left forelimb initiated the movement with sharp decreases in θLF and αLF (ranges: 98 ± 18° and 75 ± 25°, respectively). In contrast, the right forelimb showed a delayed response, with marked angular reductions occurring near the end of the righting stage (∼80%). Concurrently, the midlimb angle (αRM) decreased to a minimum of 25 ± 5° to assist rotation, while αRH gradually recovered until ground contact was re-established.

Pitching (pattern 2): during the searching stage, the fore- and midlimbs remained suspended, performing searching movements that generated periodic, symmetrical fluctuations in femur-tibia angles (Figure S2A(b)). The trunk was significantly elevated to a threshold angle (β2∗) while maintaining a concave posture, laterally supported by the abdomen and hindlimbs (Figure 3B(c)). Both hindlimbs extended outward but exhibited distinct peak timings and magnitudes: αLH peaked first (109 ± 10°) at ∼33% of sequence duration, followed by αRH (126 ± 9°) at ∼42%. The latter defined the threshold (αRH2∗) for the transition into the righting stage. A significant difference was observed between these maximum extension angles (ANOVA: df = 1, F = 17.19, p = 0.004; Figure 4A, Table S7). Following maximum extension, the left hindlimb detached from the substrate and rotated around the trunk, initiating the righting stage. Semi-pitching (pattern 3): This pattern often functioned as a transitional strategy following failed rolling attempts. During the searching stage, the trunk was elevated and supported primarily by the right lateral limbs (Figure 3B(d)), while θRH remained relatively stable (Figure S2A(c)). In contrast, the suspended left fore- and midlimbs exhibited pronounced periodic fluctuations in θ, with angular excursions of 126 ± 20° and 133 ± 29°, respectively. Similar to pattern 1, the righting maneuver was triggered when trunk elevation reached a threshold (β3∗) and the supporting hindlimbs retracted to a value (αRH3∗) of 3.5 ± 1.5°. As the maneuver progressed, the angles of the left hindlimb (θLH and αLH) decreased to minima of 52 ± 13° and 16 ± 7°, respectively. Simultaneously, the supporting right hindlimb exhibited a coordinated decrease in θRH, reaching its minimum value of 30 ± 10°.

Body deformation and movement

Significant differences in the peak femur extension angle (α) across fore-, mid-, and hindlimbs were observed across different patterns (Figure 4A, Table S8). While no statistical difference in α was detected between the rolling and semi-pitching patterns (ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test: p = 1.00), the pitching pattern showed significantly higher αRH threshold than the semi-pitching (ANOVA with the Tukey: p = 0.006).

Significant differences were observed in the abdomen bending angle (η) and the trunk pitch angle (β) across different self-righting patterns (minimum η: df = 2, F = 11.247, p < 0.001; maximum β: df = 2, F = 37.17, p < 0.001; Figures 4B and 4D, Table S9). However, no significant difference was found in the trunk-abdomen angle (ε) among these patterns (maximum ε: df = 2, F = 0.516, p = 0.601; Figure 4C).

In the rolling pattern, the trunk underwent slight elevation (maximum β = 21.7 ± 6.5°) with minimal rotation (range of trunk rotation angle ζ = 14.6 ± 9.0°). Conversely, the pitching pattern exhibited substantial trunk elevation (maximum β = 58.5 ± 6.2°) accompanied by significant rotation (range ζ = 44.7 ± 18.0°). The semi-pitching pattern demonstrated intermediate trunk elevation (maximum β = 34.2 ± 3.7°, differing significantly from other patterns, p < 0.001) with pronounced rotation (range ζ = 34.5 ± 13.4°) (Figures 4D and 4E). However, there was no significant difference in the average rotational angular speed among the three patterns (Figure 4F; Kruskal-Wallis: df = 2, χ2 = 3.83, p = 0.15).

Additionally, the position and degree of abdominal curvature varied across patterns. In the rolling pattern, the primary support position of the body was at the distal end of the abdomen, which curved into an arch shape with the abdomen bending angle (η) fluctuating between 160° and 180°. In contrast, the abdominal support positions in the pitching and semi-pitching patterns were located near the middle of the abdomen, resulting in a concave curvature. The fluctuation range of η (120°–180°) in these patterns was significantly greater than that observed in the rolling pattern, as indicated by the green-shaded area (Figure S3).

The overall body rolling dynamics were consistent across different patterns. Initially, the insect rested in a dorsal (overturned) position, with the theoretical rolling angle (γ) approximating 180°. During the searching stage (Figure S3C, pink shaded region), as the insect initiated righting attempts, partial body rolling occurred, resulting in a gradual decrease in γ. Upon successful righting, the insect either remained stationary or transitioned into walking (light-yellow shaded region), with its dorsum oriented upward and γ approaching 0°. The actual righting event, demarcated by the green-shaded region, was characterized by a rapid and pronounced decrease in the rolling angle γ. In addition, no significant difference was observed in the average rolling angular speed among the different patterns (Figure 4G; Kruskal-Wallis: df = 2, χ2 = 5.1, p = 0.8).

Self-righting simulations of biomechanical model

Biomechanical simulations confirmed the mechanical feasibility of the self-righting pattern and assessed the contribution of abdominal flexibility (Figure 1D). Ten repeated trials were conducted under two conditions: a biomechanical model with a flexible versus a fixed abdomen, achieved via articulated and locked abdominal joints, respectively. Given its predominance and high success rate in biological observations, the rolling pattern was selected for implementation. The simulation parameters—including specific self-righting steps, limbs joint angles, and body angles—were directly informed by the biological data. Comparative analysis of the simulation sequences (Figures 6A and 6B, Video S4) and the associated body rolling angle change (Figure 6C(a)) demonstrates that the flexible model with active abdominal joints successfully achieved self-righting. To elucidate the failure mechanism, we analyzed the dynamics of the simulation trials. Notably, the maximum CoM height showed negligible differences between the successful and failed cases (Figure 6C(b)). However, the rolling torque profiles exhibited significant difference. The rolling torque—determined by the ground reaction force and the corresponding pivot point—came into play only at the onset of the self-righting process. Consequently, significant rolling torque was primarily observed during the mid-righting phase (approximately between 11 and 14.5 s). The model with active abdominal joints generated a continuous torque consistent with the self-righting direction, whereas the locked-joint model produced an antagonistic torque (Figure 6C(d)).

Force sensor data from the distal tip of the abdomen revealed that the flexible abdomen generated a significantly higher ground reaction force compared to the rigid abdomen (Figure 6C(c)). This confirms that active abdominal bending facilitates leveraging the ground reaction forces to generate efficient rolling torque (consistent with the self-righting direction), effectively overcoming the antagonistic torque to complete the self-righting process. Furthermore, the influence of limb searching movements on self-righting success was evaluated using the simulation model with abdominal bending joints. Comparative analysis of the simulation trials with and without searching movements (Video S5) revealed that successful self-righting was achieved in both scenarios, suggesting that the mechanical advantage provided by abdominal flexibility is sufficient for successful maneuvers regardless of searching limb actions.

Discussion

Pattern classification and transitions strategy in ground self-righting behavior

Unlike stick insect nymphs that utilize aerodynamic mechanisms for self-righting,6,18 adults rely exclusively on ground reaction forces, exhibiting a purely mechanical terrestrial righting process. Three distinct righting patterns—rolling, semi-pitching, and pitching—are identified based on body posture and limb configuration. Our observations reveal an energy-based decision hierarchy underlying the selection of self-righting patterns. Rolling is the dominant pattern (69%) and serves as the primary pattern for reorientation. Its prevalence can be explained by both temporal efficiency and energetic optimality: rolling exhibits the shortest righting time (Figure 2C) and, according to our geometric energy landscape model, requires surmounting the lowest gravitational potential energy barrier (Figures 5A and 7B). In contrast, pitching necessitates elevating the trunk to a significantly higher position, incurring a markedly greater energetic cost (Figure 5). Consequently, stick insects prioritize the low-cost rolling strategy and adaptively transition to the more energetically demanding semi-pitching or pitching patterns only when the initial efforts fail, despite the relatively high first-attempt success rate of the pitching maneuver (Figure 7A). A key determinant of successful self-righting is the precise adjustment of body posture and limb arrangement, which enables the insect to reach critical kinematic thresholds required to initiate the righting maneuver (Figure 3B). Specifically, all three patterns require elevating the trunk to a critical decision angle. The hierarchical relationship β1∗ < β3∗ < β2∗ suggests that strategies requiring higher trunk elevation pose greater mechanical challenges. Furthermore, while pattern 1 (rolling) and pattern 3 (semi-pitching) rely on reaching a minimized hindlimb extension threshold, pattern 2 (pitching) necessitates maximizing hindlimb extension (α2∗ > α1∗ > α3∗) (Figure 3B). These findings indicate that rolling is the most kinematically accessible strategy, explaining its higher success probability compared to alternative patterns (Figures 2 and 7A). Trials involving multiple attempts demonstrate that failure often results from an inability to attain these critical configurations (Figure 7A). In such cases, insects frequently exhibit corrective adjustments in subsequent attempts, progressively improving postural alignment to satisfy the required kinematic conditions. Semi-pitching acts as a functional intermediate in this landscape: its limb arrangement during the searching stage resembles the early stages of rolling (Figures 3B(b) and 3B(d)), but it additionally recruits pitching torque when rolling torque alone is insufficient. This observation suggests that Semi-pitching commonly emerges as a compensatory kinematic adjustment following failed rolling initiation (Figures 7A and 7C).

Comparative analysis reveals a clear trade-off between righting speed and stability. Stick insects exhibit markedly slower rotation speeds (∼20°/s in yaw; ∼85°/s in roll) than cockroaches (∼180°/s), beetles (90–360°/s), and locusts (>90°/s),8,9,10 employing a quasi-static strategy that prioritizes stability over speed. Despite these slower dynamics, stick insects achieve a high overall success rate (∼85%), with most successful events occurring on the first attempt (Figures 2A and 2B). In contrast, cockroaches and beetles, which employ momentum-based strategies, show lower success rates (60%–80%) and frequently require multiple attempts.4,8 While dynamic strategies prioritize speed to escape predation, the stick insect’s slow, deliberate, and highly reliable righting behavior aligns with its cryptic lifestyle.27 In an ecological context where camouflage constitutes the primary defensive strategy, a controlled, high-probability recovery is evolutionarily favored over rapid, chaotic inversion that might attract visual attention from predators.9,28

Morphology-dependent self-righting strategies for wingless stick insects

Body morphology critically constrains self-righting strategies. Organisms with compact, discoidal, or hemispherical bodies—such as beetles and turtles—are often characterized by limited limb reach and high rotational inertia, necessitating dynamic body oscillations to accumulate sufficient kinetic energy (Ek) for righting8,13 (Figure 8D). Our study provides a detailed biomechanical characterization of ground self-righting in the stick insect Medauroidea extradentata, revealing a strong association between body morphology and strategy selection. The elongated stick insects (body diameter-to-length ratio ≈ 0.04) exhibit mechanical characteristics more closely aligned with those of other slender insects, such as locusts,10 and adopt quasi-static, limb-driven self-righting strategies (Figures 8A and 8B). This elongated morphology facilitates a distinct control mechanism predicated on extensive limb reach and kinematic manipulability,20 rather than the inertia-dominated mechanisms required by compact-bodied species. Although the periodic aerial limb motions observed during self-righting might superficially resemble the kinetic energy accumulation seen in discoidal species, our simulations reveal a fundamentally different functional role (Video S5). Comparative simulations with and without active limb searching demonstrate that these motions are primarily exploratory—serving to locate an optimal foothold—rather than propulsive. Once stable contact is established, the insect forms a closed kinematic chain and exploits its body geometry to complete the self-righting maneuver. Consequently, for stick insects, self-righting success depends fundamentally on the effective spatial acquisition of footholds.

The abdomen as an active torque generator and stability modulator

Integrating empirical kinematics with biomechanical model simulations elucidates the role of the abdomen. While our experimental results revealed prominent abdominal bending during the righting sequence, our subsequent comparative simulation isolated the underlying mechanical necessity of this movement. Crucially, the failure of the biomechanical model without active abdominal joints was not due to insufficient CoM elevation—as both models reached similar peak heights—but rather to the inadequate rolling torque aligned with the self-righting direction (Figure 6). By selectively enabling abdominal flexibility, we demonstrated that the abdomen functions as an active mechanical lever rather than a passive inertial load. In the rolling pattern, simultaneous forelimb anchoring and abdominal contact create an arched posture (Figure 8E). In this configuration, the abdomen establishes contact with the substrate, generating substantial ground reaction forces that, synergistically with the supporting limbs, produce a rolling torque consistent with the self-righting direction (Figure 6, Video S4). In the pitching pattern, ventral abdominal depression provides the essential fulcrum (Figure 8F). These behavioral observations and quantitative analyses suggest that a key strategy for stick insects with such slender morphology to achieve successful righting relies on the synergistic integration of limb anchoring and abdomen-based torque generation.

The configuration of the projected support area is a critical determinant of self-righting performance. Generally, minimizing the support area reduces postural stability, thereby lowering the energetic threshold required to initiate a rolling maneuver. Our cross-species analysis indicates that larger insects do not exhibit proportionally larger support areas (Figure 9A), implying the use of active postural adjustments to modulate stability. Unlike soft-bodied organisms capable of extensive shape deformation,31 rigid-exoskeleton insects achieve reconfiguration through articular adjustments.9,16 Our analysis reveals a convergent mechanical strategy across all righting patterns (Figures 9B–9D): stick insects actively shift their CoM to the periphery of, or slightly beyond, the boundary of the support polygon. Collectively, the detailed biomechanical characterization presented in this study offers valuable insights for designing bioinspired self-righting robots, particularly those required to operate under severe morphological constraints (e.g., highly elongated bodies) and on low-friction surfaces. By understanding how stick insects utilize specific righting patterns, precise limb arrangements, and active abdominal torque generation, we can inform the development of more energy-efficient and adaptive righting mechanisms in legged robots,32,33 thereby enhancing their autonomy and resilience in unpredictable environments. Moving forward, future research should investigate how various environmental factors—such as surface texture, compliance, and slope—influence righting efficacy.26,34,35 Direct measurements of ground reaction forces during the righting process will be critical for quantifying exactly how posture and limb-ground interactions generate sufficient torque to overcome gravitational energy barriers. Furthermore, the specific contribution of tarsal adhesion mechanisms (e.g., pads and claws) to ground stability during righting maneuvers warrants deeper exploration.36,37 Ultimately, integrating these force measurements with neurophysiological techniques, such as electromyography (EMG) and neural recordings, may elucidate how motor control is distributed and coordinated across body segments during these complex righting behaviors.15,38

Limitations of the study

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, experiments were conducted under highly controlled laboratory conditions on smooth, rigid platforms, whereas natural surfaces are often irregular and varied. Consequently, the ecological applicability of our findings may be constrained, as these simplified settings do not fully account for the challenges posed by complex natural terrains or fluctuating environmental factors (e.g., substrate texture and terrain irregularity). Furthermore, the biomechanical model employed here was built on simplified assumptions, which may limit its ability to replicate the full complexity of a stick insect’s self-righting locomotion.

Experimental model and study participant details

Six adult female stick insects (Medauroidea extradentata) of similar body size were used in this study. The mean body length, body width, and whole-body mass were 70.63 ± 2.42 mm, 2.48 ± 0.16 mm, and 0.48 ± 0.04 g, respectively. The insects were housed individually in ventilated plastic containers maintained at 27°C–29 °C under a 12:12 h light–dark cycle. They were provided with fresh rose leaves for food and periodic water spraying to maintain optimal humidity.

All experimental procedures were performed using live animals in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Association for the Study of Animal Behavior (ASAB). As the subjects are invertebrates, no specific ethical approval was required under local or institutional regulations; however, standard animal care and welfare protocols were strictly followed throughout the study.

Method details

Experimental setup and protocol

Self-righting behavior was recorded using a customized multi-camera motion capture system optimized for small animal locomotion. The setup included four motion capture cameras (Prime17.0, Optitrack, USA) arranged in a semicircular configuration,39 a data processing workstation, and a horizontal, smooth acrylic platform (Figure 1A).

For each trial, the stick insect was manually overturned onto its dorsum (abdomen facing upward) and gently placed on the platform. Video data were captured using the motion capture system at a sampling rate of 50 frames per second with a resolution of 800 × 600 pixels. The intensity level of the integrated infrared LEDs (850 nm) on the cameras was set to 10. The exposure time was fixed at 1000 μs to mitigate motion blur. The recorded sequences facilitated subsequent 3D tracking using the Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) method.40 In cases where the insect remained motionless, gentle stimulation was applied to the tip of the abdomen using a small brush. Preliminary tests confirmed no significant behavioral differences between spontaneous and stimulated self-righting. If the insect failed to respond after stimulation, it was returned to its container for recovery.

Sample size and attempts definition

We conducted between 20 and 40 trials per insect. After excluding trials in which the insects remained stationary throughout the observation period, a total of 208 valid trials were analyzed. Within these trials, insects occasionally required multiple attempts to achieve a successful righting maneuver. Across all trials, we identified 245 distinct attempts, comprising 208 successful and 37 failed attempts. An “attempt” was defined as any coordinated body and/or limb movement involving pitching and/or rolling aimed at restoring an upright posture. If the insect returned to its dorsal position without completing the righting maneuver, the attempt was classified as failed. Each trial consisted of one successful attempt and, in some cases, multiple failed attempts.

Performance analysis

For each attempt, the self-righting process was partitioned into two sequential stages: a searching stage and a righting stage.8 To accurately quantify the temporal dynamics of the successful self-righting while mitigating variability introduced by failed attempts, we analyzed the effective searching duration. This metric was defined as the duration of the searching stage immediately preceding the successful righting attempt, explicitly excluding time intervals associated with prior failed attempts. Consequently, the total successful self-righting duration was calculated as the sum of the effective searching duration and the righting time of the successful righting stage.

Trials were not terminated after a predetermined number of failed attempts. Instead, observations continued as long as the insect remained active and initiated subsequent searching phases immediately following a failed attempt. A trial was deemed complete upon successful righting or when the insect ceased all movement and entered a quiescent state (i.e., cessation of behavioral response). We recorded whether righting was achieved on the first attempt and the total number of attempts per trial, thereby quantifying the number of failed attempts preceding the final success.

Construction of biomechanical model

A biomechanical model of Medauroidea extradentata was developed within the CoppeliaSim simulation environment, utilizing the Bullet 2.73 physics engine to evaluate the contribution of abdominal flexibility during ground self-righting (Figure 1D). All body segments were modeled as uniform rigid cylinders, with dimensions scaled up proportionally from biological measurements (Tables S1–S3) and interconnected by three joints (MMe, MAb, and ABe). Each leg comprised two segments (the femur and a combined tibia–tarsus segment) articulated by three joints (TC, CTr, and FTi). The degrees of freedom were implemented as revolute joints located at the rotation pivots of the bar links. The simulation assumed standard Earth gravity (9.81 m/s2). The simulation and dynamics computation time-steps were set to 0.02 s and 0.001 s, respectively, ensuring stable contact dynamics and high-precision control.

Crucially, self-righting motions in the simulation were directly driven by the joint angle trajectories derived from the biological experiments. These joint angles were computed from 3D marker coordinates obtained via the motion capture system (Figure 1A), allowing the model to faithfully reproduce the kinematics observed in real insects. Two model variants were constructed: (i) the flexible model, featuring active abdominal joints (MAb and ABe), and (ii) the rigid model, with a fixed abdomen (locked abdominal joints) (Figures 1D(b) and 1D(c)). A trial was considered successful when the model restored its dorsoventral axis to an upright orientation. Comparative testing between these two variants isolated the mechanical role of abdominal flexibility in successful righting.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction was performed from high-quality OptiTrack video recordings using the DLTdv8 digitizing software40 (Hedrick, 2008; Hedrick Lab, UNC Chapel Hill). Statistical analyses were performed utilizing SPSS software (SPSS 22.0, IBM Inc., NY, USA). One-way ANOVA, complemented by the Tukey’s post hoc test, was employed to assess significant differences in kinematic and energetic variables among different self-righting patterns. The analyzed variables included abdomen bending angle, trunk pitch angle, trunk rotation angle, trunk-abdomen angle, trunk rotation angular speed, body rolling angular speed, and potential/kinetic energy barriers. This analytical approach was also extended to compare angles between bilateral limbs and among fore-, mid-, and hindlimbs. Prior to parametric testing, data were evaluated for normality and homogeneity of variance. In instances where data deviated from a normal distribution, non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test) were implemented. When the homogeneity of variance assumption was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was performed, with the Games–Howell test serving as the post hoc analysis. A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was applied across all statistical evaluations. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on n total attempts from N individuals, unless otherwise stated. In all boxplots, the central horizontal line represents the median, the box boundaries indicate the 25th and 75th percentiles, and the “x” marker indicates the mean. The whiskers denote the range excluding outliers, while individual points above and below the whiskers represent outliers.
