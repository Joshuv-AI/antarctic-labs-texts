Beyond Medication: A Cross-Sectional Study of How Sex Influences Overall Chronic Disease Adherence Behaviour Using the French Generic Adherence for Chronic Diseases Profile (GACID-P)
Authors: Mary A De Vera, Marc Soudant, Christine Rotonda, Vicki Cheng, Alexander J Lin, Cyril Tarquinio, Francis Guillemin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdherence research has historically focused on medication-taking behaviors. While evidence suggests that males are generally more adherent to medication regimens than females, the influence of sex on overall adherence behaviors remains unclear. We explored how sex influences a multidimensional profile of adherence beyond medication using the Generic French Adherence in Chronic Diseases Profile (GACID-P).MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study using data from the validation of the GACID-P. Participants were adults (>18 years) with chronic conditions attending specialist care across the Grand-Est region of France. The 25-item self-report GACID-P assesses four domains of adherence – Intention to comply with treatment, Forgetting to take medication, Healthy lifestyle, and Limitation of risk-related consumer habits – with scores ranging from 0 (worst) to 10 (best), except for forgetting, which is reverse scored. Analyses included sex-stratified comparisons, multivariable linear regression models, and multivariate analysis of variance to examine sex-based adherence patterns within and across domains.ResultsAmong 397 participants (53% male; mean age: males 57.1 ± 10.7, females 59.3 ± 11.7), domain scores indicated generally good adherence. Within domains, males had higher scores than females for Intention to comply (9.18 ± 1.15 vs 8.96 ± 1.51), less Forgetting to take medication (1.35 ± 2.42 vs 1.82 ± 2.85), more Healthy lifestyle (6.92 ± 1.95 vs 6.61 ± 2.00), while females scored higher for Limitation of risk-related consumer habits (6.15 ± 2.14 vs 5.24 ± 2.14); however, none of these differences reached statistical significance. Across domains, a multivariate analysis suggested a sex effect (Wilks’ Lambda = 0.84, F(4,73) = 3.58, p=0.010), driven primarily by differences in Forgetting to take medication where males reported less forgetting than females, when adherence patterns are considered jointly across domains (F(1,76) = 10.39, p = 0.0019).ConclusionDespite the absence of statistically significant sex-based differences within individual GACID-P domains, a multivariate analysis suggested potential differences when adherence behaviors were considered jointly. While these findings should be interpreted cautiously, they support consideration of a sex-based lens to better understand adherence.Keywords: adherence, sex and gender-based analysis, survey, health measurement

Background

Effective chronic disease management depends on adherence, which the World Health Organization defines as “the extent to which a person’s behaviour – taking medication, following a diet, and/or executing lifestyle changes – corresponds with agreed recommendations from a health care provider”.1 Previous research on adherence measurement has primarily focused on medication-taking. In a systematic review of 60 articles capturing 43 self-report adherence tools, Nguyen et al (2014) classified tools by the type of information assessed and found that the majority primarily measured medication-taking behavior.2 Consequently, authors emphasize the need for more balanced tools that capture the full complexity of adherence behavior;2 this is particularly important given that adherence has commonly been measured using self-report tools, which may be subject to biases such as social desirability and recall error.

Rather than relying on existing tools that are either limited to medication adherence2 or tailored to specific diseases,3–5 a generic adherence tool may provide a more comprehensive view by capturing overall patient engagement in chronic disease management. The Generic Adherence for Chronic Diseases Profile (GACID-P) is a recently developed and validated self-reported generic questionnaire designed to assess adherence across multiple disease areas, including cardiology, rheumatology, diabetes, cancer, and infectiology.6 In a sample of 397 patients, factor analyses revealed four dimensions of adherence: Intention to comply with treatment, Forgetting to take medication, Healthy lifestyle, and Limitation of risk-related consumer habits. The optimized version of the GACID-P, which includes 25 items across these dimensions, demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties and calibration, confirming its validity.6

This study extends the evaluation of the GACID-P by examining how sex may influence adherence. In this context, sex refers to biological attributes, which is often conflated with gender, which refers to sociocultural roles, identities, and norms.7 To highlight prior evidence of potential sex and gender disparities in medication-taking behavior, a 2023 scoping review of 937 pharmacoepidemiologic studies examined how sex and gender are considered in medication adherence research, finding that the two terms were often conflated.8 While 49% of included studies reported no statistical difference in adherence between cisgender participants, among those that did, poorer adherence was more often observed in females/women (31%) than in males/men (20%).8 Nonetheless, no studies have conducted an in-depth exploration of how sex influences medication adherence. Most studies included in the scoping review relied on group comparisons or treated sex as a covariate without examining sex-specific patterns in greater depth.8 This gap extends to the broader study of adherence overall, where—to our knowledge—sex has rarely been explicitly examined. Indeed, evaluating how sex may impact medication adherence and adherence overall is crucial, given potential influences from a variety of biological factors. For example, with respect to medication taking, females have up to 1.7-fold risk of adverse drug reactions due to sex differences in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics,9–11 which could be an important factor influencing adherence to medications. Sex differences in adherence may also be explained by psychosocial, individual, and systemic factors related to the patient (eg, health literacy, religious/cultural beliefs, social support), their health (eg, alcohol/substance use, physical/cognitive impairments), and the healthcare system (eg, patient–provider relationship, lack of insurance coverage), all of which are influenced by gender.12

Our objective was to leverage the multidimensional GACID-P to examine how sex influences adherence behaviors within and across domains, as an exploratory secondary analysis. Accordingly, our study is guided by a Sex- and Gender-Based Analysis (SGBA) framework, first developed in Canada in the 2000s.13 This framework outlines several approaches for consideration of sex and gender throughout the research process, including the option most relevant to our study: revisiting existing data and retroactively applying sex-based secondary analysis.13 We also draw on more recent guidance, including the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines,14 along with others15,16 – which collectively emphasize the importance of exploring sex and gender differences, explaining them through underlying mechanisms, and contextualizing findings into real-world applications.

Methods

Study Design and Study Sample

We conducted a cross-sectional study as a secondary analysis of data from the validation and optimization of the GACID-P. As previously described, the study sample in the original study included adults with chronic conditions attending routine consultations at two university hospitals and three private practices in France’s Grand-Est region, where patients were approached and invited to participate during their visits.6 Eligible participants were over 18, able to complete the questionnaire, and under specialist care for one of the following: cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, rheumatic disease, cancer treated with oral non-hormonal drugs, or HIV. As part of the original validation study, the GACID-P was voluntarily completed prior to routine physician consultations.6 Informed consent was obtained from study participants prior to study commencement. This study received approval from the Advisory Committee on Information Processing in Health Research (Comité Consultatif sur le Traitement de l’Information en matière de Recherche dans le domaine de la Santé, CCTIRS; File Reference No. 14.161) and the National Commission for Information Technology and Freedoms (Commission Nationale de l’informatique et des Libertés, CNIL; Reference No. MMS/CV/AR149307).

Description of the GACID-P

The GACID-P is a 25-item generic adherence self-report questionnaire that captures both medication-taking behaviors and adherence to lifestyle recommendations across four domains (see Supplementary Figure 1) with items assessed using a 4-point Likert-type format (1 = never to 4 = all the time). It was developed and refined through classical test theory and item response theory to improve measurement precision and domain structure.6 The psychometric properties of the GACID-P, including internal consistency and test–retest reliability, have been previously established and are reported in the original validation study, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.62 to 0.85 and intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.49 to 0.66.6 The first two domains relate to medication-taking behavior but reflect distinct underlying processes. Intention to comply with treatment (10 items) captures deliberate and planned management of therapy, including medication use (eg, timing, dosage, duration) and engagement in follow-up care (eg, appointments, tests). In contrast, Forgetting to take medication (7 items) reflects unintentional lapses, such as missing specific doses or forgetting during certain times, including those associated with routine disruptions (eg, weekends, holidays). Healthy lifestyle (3 items) assesses self-directed behaviors that support overall well-being—namely physical activity, balanced diet, and sufficient rest. Limitation of risk-related consumer habits (5 items) measures efforts to reduce harmful intake, such as fat, sugar, salt, alcohol, and smoking. The original validation study found no evidence of differential item functioning by sex,6 supporting the validity of the GACID-P for examining how sex influences adherence behaviors.

Covariates

Socio-demographic variables, collected at the administration of the GACID-P questionnaire, included sex (male, female), age, marital status (single, married/cohabitating, divorced, in a partnership, or widowed), level of education (ranging from certificate of studies to post-baccalaureate degree), employment status (working full-time, working part-time, and not working), household (living alone, living with a partner, living with children, living with parents, other), and disease group (oncology, infectiology, diabetes, rheumatology and cardiology).

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized as frequencies and proportions for categorical variables, and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Descriptive analyses were conducted for the full sample and stratified by sex (male, female).

For within-domain analyses, scoring procedures followed the original GACID-P validation study and its scoring manual.6 Higher scores indicated better adherence for all domains except the Forgetting to take medication domain, where higher scores reflected poorer adherence. Descriptive statistics were computed, and independent samples t-tests were used to compare mean domain scores by sex. Multivariable linear regression models were fitted for each domain score (dependent variable), with sex as the primary independent variable, to examine associations while adjusting for potential covariates. Covariates that showed potential sex-based differences (p<0.10) were considered as candidates for inclusion, with the final model specification guided by model fit (adjusted R-squared) to maintain parsimony given the limited number of available covariates in this exploratory analysis.

For across-domain analyses, we used multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) to examine the overall effect of sex on GACID-P domain scores (dependent variable), with sex as the independent variable, allowing assessment of patterns across multiple adherence domains simultaneously. For this analysis only, scores for the Forgetting to take medication domain were reverse-coded to ensure consistent interpretation across domains, such that higher scores reflected better adherence. Subsequently, a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was conducted with covariates considered based on potential differences between males and females (p < 0.10) and inclusion further supported by assessing model fit using Wilk’s Lambda, to ensure parsimony given the limited covariates in this exploratory analysis. Finally, we conducted sex-stratified correlation analyses to explore relationships between domains within male and female subgroups.

Given the exploratory nature of the analyses using secondary data, adjustments for multiple testing were not applied, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Parametric tests were used under standard assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, which were considered appropriate given the sample size. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R version 4.3.1 within the RStudio environment (Posit Software, Boston, MA).

Influence of Sex Within GACID-P Domains

Univariate analyses of individual GACID-P domain scores, both overall and stratified by sex, are presented in Table 2. For the Intention to comply with treatment domain, the overall score was 9.09 ± 1.33, indicating generally better adherence. Among males, the score was 9.18 ± 1.15 and among females 8.96 ± 1.51; no statistically significant difference was observed. In the Forgetting to take medication domain, where higher scores indicate poorer adherence, the overall mean was 1.56 ± 2.63 also suggested better adherence in the study sample. Sex-stratified scores for males (1.35 ± 2.42) and females (1.82 ± 2.85) did not differ significantly (p = 0.11). For Healthy lifestyle, the score for males was 6.92 ± 1.95 and for females, 6.61 ± 2.00 (p = 0.14). For Limitation of risk-related consumer habits, males scored lower than females (5.24 ± 2.14 vs 6.15 ± 2.14; p = 0.068), though this difference was also not statistically significant. Overall, these findings indicate that sex-based differences within individual domains were small and not statistically significant.

Influence of Sex Across GACID-P Domains

Results of MANOVA revealed a significant multivariate effect of sex on combined GACID-P domains (Wilks’ Lambda = 0.84, F (4, 73) = 3.58, p=0.010), suggesting sex-based differences observed across domains when considered jointly. Univariate ANOVAs suggested that this effect was primarily driven by significant differences in the Forgetting to take medication domain (F [1, 76] = 10.39, p=0.0019), with males demonstrating higher adherence in this domain compared to females. No significant sex differences were observed for the other three domains: Intention to comply with treatment (F [1,76] = 0.20, p=0.66), Healthy lifestyle (F [1,76] = 0.84, p=0.36), and Limitation of risk-related consumer habits (F [1,76] = 2.89, p=0.093). A subsequent MANCOVA including covariates (age, marital status) also showed a significant multivariate effect of sex across GACID-P domains (Wilks’ Lambda = 0.82, F (4, 65) = 3.56, p=0.011), that was driven by significant differences in Forgetting to take medication.

Correlation matrices for the four GACID-P domains, both for the overall study sample and stratified by sex are shown in Figure 2. We observed statistically significant negative correlations between Intention to comply with treatment and Forgetting to take medication in the overall sample (r = −0.37; p<0.0001) that remained consistent for males (r = −0.37; p<0.0001) and females (r = −0.36; p<0.0001). We observed positive correlations between Intention to comply with treatment and Healthy lifestyle, as well as between Healthy lifestyle and Limitation of risk-related consumer habits, which also remained consistent for both sexes. Finally, we observed a negative correlation between Forgetting to take medication and Healthy lifestyle in the overall study sample (r= −0.23; p<0.0001), which we also observed among males (r= −0.30; p<0.0001) but not among females (r= −0.14; p=0.08).

Discussion

Leveraging validation data of the GACID-P, a generic multidimensional questionnaire capturing adherence across four domains, our study suggests the relevance of considering sex in the context of adherence, as it may shape behaviors in meaningful but often subtle ways. At the individual domain level, although none of the observed differences reached statistical significance and were generally small, findings suggest sex-based patterns in adherence: males reported higher intention to comply with treatment, less forgetting, and healthier lifestyles, whereas females reported higher limitation of risk-related consumer habits. In contrast, analysis across GACID-P domains suggested a multivariate effect of sex, driven primarily by differences in the Forgetting to take medication domain, though this should be interpreted cautiously in the context of aforementioned non-significant differences at the domain level. Taken together, these findings highlight the complex nature of adherence and the value of assessing it using multidimensional tools like the GACID-P. This multidimensional view aligns with the World Health Organization’s Five Dimensions of Adherence framework, which conceptualizes adherence as being shaped by a dynamic interplay of factors related to the patient, social and economic circumstances, the condition, the therapy, and the health care system (eg, models of care).1 Sex as a biological factor intersects with these dimensions of adherence by influencing condition-related experiences (eg, symptom severity and disease burden) and therapy-related responses (eg, treatment efficacy and side effects).

To our knowledge, there are no prior studies that have specifically evaluated the influence of sex on adherence as a general behaviour. Domain-specific findings from the GACID-P—particularly those related to medication-taking—may be contextualized within prior medication adherence research, though they should be interpreted cautiously given the lack of statistical significance. Findings with Intention to comply with treatment may be considered in relation to prior studies that have reported medication adherence for males and females.8 In the aforementioned 2023 scoping review of 937 pharmacoepidemiologic studies on medication adherence, 49% of studies reported no significant sex differences, while among studies that did, poorer adherence was more frequently observed among females/women (31%) than males/men (20%).8 Behaviors such as stopping medications—which this scoping review found to be more frequently reported among cisgender women than men8—reflect intentional nonadherence, defined as the active decision to forego prescribed therapy.17 This behavior is often shaped by a patient’s willingness17 and aligns with the Intention to comply with treatment domain of the GACID-P. The other medication-taking domain—Forgetting to take medication—reflects a more routine-based aspect of adherence and aligns with unintentional nonadherence, which has been described as a passive process in which patients fail to follow prescribing instructions due to factors such as forgetfulness or circumstances beyond their control.17 Notably, this GACID-P domain directly captures forgetfulness as a mechanism of nonadherence. Unintentional nonadherence has primarily been assessed in small, disease-specific samples, with an exception being a 2012 US study of 24,017 adults with chronic diseases in which 70% reported at least one unintentional nonadherence behavior—most commonly forgetting18—though sex differences were not examined. A 2012 nationwide survey in Sweden examined gender differences in medication adherence, finding no overall difference between men and women; though, men were more likely to report forgetting or adjusting their dose, while women were more likely to report filling a prescription but not taking the medication.19 However, comparisons with our study findings are cautioned given that the Swedish study examined gender and based their findings on single items of the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS) (eg, “do you ever forget to take your medicine”),19 which may be subject to recent validity concerns with the MMAS.20 Altogether, the mixed alignment of our findings with prior literature on medication adherence reflects variability in how adherence is defined and measured, and the distinct roles that sex and gender may play—underscoring the need for future research to better understand adherence as a context-dependent behavior.

In contrast to within-domain findings, examination across GACID-P domains suggested a multivariate effect of sex, indicating that adherence differences between males and females may extend beyond isolated domains. Follow-up univariate ANOVA revealed that the domain Forgetting to take medication contributed to the overall pattern, with males reporting higher adherence (ie, being less likely to forget) than females. While not statistically significant at the individual domain level, the contribution of this domain at the multivariate level highlights the relevance of forgetting—not only as a cognitively driven, routine-based form of unintentional nonadherence, but also as a potentially fundamental challenge in medication-taking behavior. This may represent a notable finding of our study, particularly when viewed in a cross-domain context where factors such as mental load, daily routines, and competing demands may contribute to differential experiences of forgetting. Arguably, it is not “just forgetting”; what may appear to be a simple lapse in memory likely reflects deeper, intersecting social, cognitive, and structural pressures—reinforcing the need for a sex-informed lens on even seemingly simple adherence behaviors.

Exploring how sex influences relationships across GACID-P domains also provides greater insight into the structure and function of the questionnaire itself. The consistency of inter-domain correlations between males and females suggests that the questionnaire captures adherence behaviors in a conceptually coherent and comparable way by sex. For example, a negative correlation between intention and forgetting was observed in both groups, as were positive correlations between intention and healthy lifestyle, and between healthy lifestyle and limitation of risk-related habits. The only divergence was a negative correlation between forgetting and healthy lifestyle, which was evident in males but not in females. One possible explanation is that, among males, medication-taking may be more closely integrated into broader health routines, aligning healthier lifestyles with more consistent use. In contrast, among females, competing demands and mental load may weaken this alignment – though these interpretations should be considered cautiously. Nonetheless, in this context, while this exception warrants further exploration, it also adds to a pattern in our findings suggesting that Forgetting to take medication may be a particularly important domain for understanding sex differences in adherence. This is especially noteworthy given limited prior literature,19 which contrasts with our findings, thus highlighting an important direction for future research. These insights have practical implications, as even subtle sex-related differences in unintentional nonadherence—particularly forgetting to take medication, as suggested by our findings—could inform the design of targeted interventions. Tailoring adherence strategies to address the specific, everyday adherence challenges faced by different patient groups may enhance their effectiveness and relevance.

Strengths and limitations of this study merit consideration. By leveraging data from the original validation of the GACID-P, we addressed our objective of examining sex-based patterns in adherence and, in doing so, also demonstrated the broader utility of the questionnaire for examining the complex ways in which factors such as sex influence adherence within and across domains. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, causal relationships between variables cannot be inferred. As a secondary analysis, the current study inherits limitations of the original study. For example, social desirability bias may be a relevant limitation, as participants completed the GACID-P immediately before their clinical consultations—potentially prompting them to overreport adherence in order to align with perceived expectations from their physicians.6 This may also help explain the generally high adherence observed in our study, as reflected in mean scores across all GACID-P domains—particularly in the Intention to comply with treatment and Forgetting to take medication domains, potentially reflecting ceiling effects in the measurement of adherence based on self-reports. It is also possible that participants’ experiences living with chronic conditions contributed to the high adherence scores, as established routines and sustained engagement with treatment may support consistent behaviors. Another consideration is the context in which the data were collected: all participants were patients receiving care in a single region of France. Given that social and economic circumstances and the health care system are among the World Health Organization’s Five Dimensions,1 caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other countries or care settings. Furthermore, as a secondary analysis, we were also limited to the variables and measurement approaches available in the original dataset, which restricted our ability to examine additional factors or refine measures in line with the specific objectives of the present study. Notably, an important limitation of the current study is the absence of a gender measure, which restricts our ability to differentiate between biological (sex-based) and sociocultural (gender-based) influences on adherence. While sex was assessed, it is unclear whether the question sufficiently distinguished sex assigned at birth from gender identity, raising concerns about how participants may have interpreted and responded to the item. Unfortunately, sex and gender are often conflated in health research, yet they represent distinct dimensions of identity with different implications for health outcomes.21 Indeed, sociological factors related to gender are also considerations within the World Health Organization’s domains of adherence, as they influence the “patient”-related beliefs and attitudes, “social/economic” access to resources, and interactions with the “health system”. However, the mere inclusion of a categorical gender identity variable may not be sufficient. Understanding how gender shapes adherence may require attention to gendered norms, roles, and expressions—many of which cannot be adequately captured by a single-item gender identity question. Future examinations of how sex and gender influence adherence should consider adopting more rigorous approaches—such as the two-step method, which separately captures sex assigned at birth and current gender identity—and incorporating measures that reflect gendered roles, norms, and access to resources.21

We also highlight several directions for future research. While the present analysis focused on sex-based patterns in adherence, other sample characteristics (eg, employment status, disease group) may intersect with sex in shaping adherence behaviors and warrant further investigation. In some cases, such questions may be more appropriately addressed in studies designed to examine adherence within specific disease populations, as adherence is often studied within single-condition contexts. In addition, examining these intersections would likely require larger samples to ensure sufficient statistical power. Future work should also continue to refine the measurement of sex, as well as gender, in adherence research to better capture their distinct and interacting influences.

In conclusion, this study contributes to the understanding of adherence as a multidimensional behavior shaped by biological factors, including sex. The unique multi-domain structure of the GACID-P facilitated the exploration of sex-based adherence patterns both within and across domains. In doing so, this study suggests how applying a sex-based lens to existing data may help identify differences between males and females, while also offering insight into the structure and function of questionnaire itself. As the World Health Organization has emphasized that improving adherence could have greater impact on health outcomes than developing new treatments,1 we suggest that refining and more effectively utilizing existing adherence tools—such as the GACID-P—may yield more value than creating additional tools, particularly in a field already populated by many tools, many of which have not undergone rigorous evaluation or demonstrate known limitations in validity. Moving forward, integrating SGBA into adherence research—alongside the thoughtful application of existing tools—may support the development of more equitable, responsive and patient-centered approaches across diverse patient populations.
