A survey of explainable AI applications in infectious disease diagnosis
Authors: Hossein Valishirin, Fatemeh Inanloo S A, Kelly A Brayton, Shira L Broschat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, has shown significant promise in accelerating the diagnosis of infectious diseases. However, the inherent “black box” nature of deep learning is a major barrier to clinical adoption, preventing trust and accountability. Explainable AI (XAI) tries to address this problem by adding transparency to model decision-making. This paper presents a comprehensive scoping review of XAI applications in infectious disease diagnosis based on literature published between 2023 and 2025. We review studies of major diseases, including COVID-19, tuberculosis, malaria, monkeypox, and dengue, categorizing them by data modality (e.g., imaging, tabular) and the XAI techniques employed (e.g., SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAM). We conclude that XAI methods are effective in identifying clinically relevant features, such as key biomarkers from lab results and pathological regions in medical images, thereby aligning model reasoning with established clinical knowledge. Despite this progress, significant challenges persist, including poor generalizability from small, single-center datasets, the computational overhead of post-hoc methods, and a gap between technical explanations and clinical utility. We outline future directions that should be taken, including the need for standardized evaluation metrics, multi-center validation, and clinician-centered XAI design to facilitate the responsible integration of AI into clinical practice. Introduction

Infectious diseases remain a major global health challenge, causing millions of deaths each year and placing disproportionate burdens on low-resource settings [1, 2]. Recent outbreaks, including SARS-CoV-2, monkeypox, and rising antibiotic resistance, highlight the urgent need for rapid diagnosis and timely public health decision making [3–5]. Early and accurate detection is especially critical for children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become increasingly important in infectious disease diagnosis. Machine learning and deep learning systems show strong performance across diverse data sources, such as medical images, laboratory results, and point-of-care biosensor measurements [6–8]. These models have been used for tasks such as detecting COVID-19 in chest CT scans, identifying malaria in blood smears, and analyzing bacterial infection through electrical biosensing [1, 2, 9].

Despite strong predictive accuracy, many AI systems operate as opaque models with limited insight into how decisions are produced. In clinical environments where decisions must be fast, reliable, and accountable, this lack of transparency reduces trust and slows adoption [10, 11]. For infectious disease diagnosis, interpretability is essential for safe and effective use. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) seeks to address this problem by making model behavior more transparent and easier for clinicians to understand. Evidence shows that explanations help healthcare professionals assess the reliability of predictions and integrate AI output into diagnostic reasoning [2, 12, 13]. Recent reviews highlight interpretability as a necessary requirement for safe and ethical medical AI systems [5, 14, 15].

This scoping review examines how XAI has been applied to infectious disease diagnosis across viral, bacterial, and vector-borne diseases. We summarize the interpretability techniques used in this domain, describe how they support clinical decision making, and outline the key challenges that motivate the need for transparent and trustworthy medical AI.

It is also worth distinguishing two distinct deployment contexts in which XAI for infectious disease may be applied, as they differ in their requirements and constraints. The first is individual clinical diagnosis support, where a model assists a clinician in reaching a diagnosis for a specific patient, and where explanation fidelity, uncertainty quantification, and real-time responsiveness are paramount. The second is public health decision support, where models inform population-level decisions such as outbreak surveillance, resource allocation, or triage prioritization across facilities, and where the relevant stakeholders, timescales, and accountability structures differ substantially from those of the clinical encounter. The reviewed literature addresses primarily the former context; studies explicitly targeting public health decision support remain limited, and this distinction should be borne in mind when interpreting the scope of the evidence presented.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section “Materials and methods” describes the materials and methods used in selecting, reviewing, and analyzing the studies included in this work. Section “AI in infectious disease diagnosis” provides an overview of AI and machine learning models used in infectious disease diagnosis and summarizes the role of Generative AI, along with the common data types and model families employed in the literature. Section “Explainable AI methods” reviews key explainable AI techniques and interpretability approaches that support transparency in medical decision making. Section “Applications of XAI in infectious disease diagnosis” presents real-world applications of these XAI techniques in clinical and diagnostic contexts. Section “Challenges and limitations” discusses current challenges and limitations related to the use of XAI in medical settings. Finally, section “Conclusion” concludes the paper with insights and potential future directions for advancing trustworthy and interpretable medical AI systems.

Materials and methods

This article is a scoping review of recent applications of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in infectious disease diagnosis. A scoping review design was selected because the literature in this area is broad and heterogeneous, spanning multiple infectious diseases, data modalities, model families, and explanation strategies. Rather than addressing a narrowly defined question suitable for quantitative meta-analysis, the goal of this review is to map the current landscape of XAI in infectious disease diagnosis, identify recurring methodological patterns, and examine common strengths, limitations, and open challenges across studies.

The review focuses on studies involving infectious diseases such as COVID-19, tuberculosis, pneumonia, dengue, malaria, monkeypox, and other viral, bacterial, and vector-borne conditions. The selected studies cover a wide range of diagnostic and clinical decision-support tasks, including medical imaging analysis, symptom-based prediction, laboratory data interpretation, biosensor analysis, and multimodal diagnostic modeling.

These disease categories were selected because they were the infectious disease areas with sufficient recent peer-reviewed literature combining diagnostic AI with explicit XAI methods during the review period. They also represent a broad range of clinically important infectious disease contexts, including viral respiratory disease, bacterial disease, vector-borne disease, parasitic infection, emerging zoonotic infection, and radiology-based respiratory infection. Therefore, the scope was not intended to cover all infectious diseases exhaustively, but to provide a representative scoping review of disease areas in which XAI-based diagnostic research has recently been most active and methodologically informative.

For each study included, information was extracted for the infectious disease addressed, data modality, dataset characteristics, machine learning or deep learning model, explainability method, reported evaluation results, and major methodological limitations. Additional attention was given, when available, to factors such as dataset scale, use of synthetic or augmented data, presence or absence of external validation, and the extent to which XAI output was clinically interpretable or reviewed by domain experts. These extracted elements were used not only to summarize individual studies, but also to support comparative analysis across diseases, model types, and XAI approaches.

A structured literature search was conducted using major scientific databases, including IEEE Xplore, PubMed, and Google Scholar. The search covered studies published between January 2023 and August 2025. Search queries combined terms related to explainable artificial intelligence with terms related to infectious disease diagnosis. Representative search concepts included combinations of terms such as “explainable AI,” “interpretable machine learning,” “SHAP,” “LIME,” and “Grad-CAM” together with disease-related terms such as “infectious disease,” “COVID-19,” “tuberculosis,” “malaria,” “dengue,” “monkeypox,” and “pneumonia.” The search emphasized peer-reviewed journal and conference papers in venues relevant to medical AI, including IEEE Access, Scientific Reports, BMC Infectious Diseases, Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, Artificial Intelligence in Medicine, Computers in Biology and Medicine, and Expert Systems with Applications.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were chosen to keep the review focused on recent, peer-reviewed diagnostic studies in which XAI was explicitly applied and sufficiently described. The January 2023–August 2025 window was selected to capture the most recent post-pandemic developments in explainable AI for infectious disease diagnosis, including newer applications of SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAM, transformer-based models, and generative AI. Although COVID-19 received substantial attention before 2023, this review emphasizes more recent work to avoid duplicating earlier COVID-19-focused reviews and to provide a current cross-disease view that also includes infections such as tuberculosis, malaria, dengue, monkeypox, and pneumonia.

Study selection and synthesis process

The study selection process was conducted in several stages. First, records were identified through database searches using combinations of XAI-related and infectious-disease-related keywords. Next, duplicate records were removed. The remaining articles then underwent title and abstract screening to exclude studies unrelated to infectious disease applications or unrelated to explainable AI. Potentially relevant studies were subsequently reviewed in full text using the eligibility criteria described above. During full-text assessment, particular attention was given to methodological clarity, relevance to infectious disease tasks, explicit use of XAI, and adequacy of reporting. Finally, the studies included were analyzed according to disease category, data modality, AI/ML model type, and XAI method, and these extracted attributes were used to support the comparative synthesis presented in subsection “Cross-disease synthesis and comparative analysis”.

Data extraction was performed using a predefined extraction template to improve consistency across reviewers. The extracted variables were operationally defined before extraction. The infectious disease variable referred to the target disease or infection addressed by the study. The data modality variable referred to the primary input type used by the diagnostic model, such as imaging, tabular clinical or laboratory data, biosensor data, audio, omics, or multimodal data. Dataset characteristics included dataset name, source, sample size, public or private status, single- or multi-source design, and any reported augmentation or synthetic-data procedures. The model variable referred to the main machine learning or deep learning architecture evaluated. The XAI method variable referred to the explanation technique used to interpret the model output. Reported evaluation results referred to the main predictive metrics reported by the authors, and methodological limitations referred to issues affecting interpretation, such as limited sample size, class imbalance, internal-only validation, lack of external validation, unclear preprocessing, or limited assessment of explanation quality. Two of the authors independently extracted information from the included studies and then compared entries for consistency. Disagreements or unclear classifications were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Formal inter-rater agreement statistics were not calculated because the review was designed as a scoping synthesis rather than a quantitative systematic review; however, consistency was assessed through independent duplicate extraction and consensus checking.

The synthesis followed a comparative thematic approach. After extraction, studies were compared across five analytical dimensions: infectious disease category, data modality, model family, XAI method, and validation design. Recurring patterns were then grouped into cross-cutting themes, including modality-dependent explanation strategies, differences between visual and feature-attribution explanations, strength of validation evidence, explanation-quality assessment, and clinical actionability of the explanations. Simple descriptive counts were also calculated from the disease-specific summary tables to support the main patterns reported in the cross-disease synthesis.

To improve methodological transparency, the number of records at each stage of screening was documented. A total of 642 records were initially identified through database searching. After removal of 157 duplicate entries, 485 records remained for title and abstract screening. Following this stage, 373 records were excluded, leaving 112 articles for full-text eligibility assessment. Of these, 54 full-text articles were excluded for reasons such as lack of an explicit XAI component, insufficient methodological detail, non-infectious disease diagnosis focus, non-peer-reviewed publication status, or language mismatch. Ultimately, 58 studies were included in the final review. A PRISMA-style flow diagram summarizing the study identification and selection process is provided in Fig. 1.

Quality and risk-of-bias assessment

Because the reviewed studies were heterogeneous in disease focus, data modality, model architecture, dataset size, and explanation method, a simplified quality and risk-of-bias assessment was used rather than a single tool designed for one clinical study type. Each included study was assessed descriptively across five domains: (1) data source and sampling, including whether the study used single-center, public, convenience, augmented, or synthetic data; (2) dataset size and class balance, including whether the sample size was limited or whether balancing procedures could affect generalizability; (3) validation design, including whether evaluation was internal only, based on a held-out internal test set, cross-dataset, or performed on an external cohort; (4) model and reporting transparency, including whether the model architecture, preprocessing steps, evaluation metrics, and experimental design were sufficiently described; and (5) explanation-quality assessment, including whether XAI outputs were evaluated for clinical plausibility, clinician review, faithfulness, stability, localization quality, or other explanation-specific criteria.

For each domain, studies were categorized as having low, moderate, or high concern. Low concern indicated that the study provided adequate methodological detail and used stronger validation or explanation-assessment procedures. Moderate concern indicated that the study was methodologically usable but had limitations such as internal-only validation, limited dataset diversity, or incomplete explanation evaluation. High concern indicated major limitations such as very small or highly augmented datasets, unclear sampling, lack of external validation, insufficient methodological reporting, or explanation results presented only as qualitative visual examples without formal assessment. These ratings were not used to exclude studies from the review; instead, they were used to contextualize the strength of evidence and to support the comparative synthesis of limitations across disease categories.

AI in infectious disease diagnosis

This section provides an overview of the AI model families, data modalities, and generative AI approaches that appear most frequently in the studies reviewed in this paper. Rather than offering a broad tutorial on machine learning, the aim is to establish only the technical context needed to follow the disease-specific discussions in section “Applications of XAI in infectious disease diagnosis”. Readers looking for definitions of individual AI and XAI terms are directed to Appendix A which provides non-technical explanations of general AI/ML terminology, neural network and deep learning terminology, common AI/ML models, and common evaluation metrics.

Overview of AI/ML models in infectious disease diagnosis

Infectious disease diagnosis draws on a wide range of data types, including medical images, structured clinical and laboratory records, free text, biosensor signals, and time-series measurements. This diversity matters because the structure of the input data is one of the strongest determinants of which model families are appropriate and, in turn, which explainability strategies are feasible. Imaging studies predominantly use convolutional neural networks (CNNs), vision transformers (ViTs), or related deep architectures. Structured clinical datasets are more often handled with logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting methods, support vector machines, or multilayer perceptrons. Temporal or sequential data involve recurrent neural networks (RNNs), long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), gated recurrent units (GRUs), or transformer-based models [16, 17].

Interpretability considerations run parallel to this model taxonomy. Simpler models such as logistic regression, decision trees, and explainable boosting machines are generally regarded as intrinsically transparent because their decision logic can be inspected directly. In contrast, deep and transformer-based architectures typically require post-hoc explanation methods to make their predictions understandable. In the context of infectious disease diagnosis, where clinical adoption depends on trust and accountability, this distinction is not just technical; it has direct implications for whether and how a model can be deployed in real clinical settings [18, 19].

Major model families

The reviewed studies span a wide spectrum of approaches. At one end, traditional machine learning models, including logistic regression, support vector machines, k-nearest neighbors, naïve Bayes, random forests, extra trees, and gradient boosting variants, remain common for tabular clinical and laboratory data. These methods are favored when datasets are relatively small or when the ability to inspect feature contributions is an explicit design goal [20–22].

At the other end, deep learning models dominate imaging, text, and multimodal applications. CNN-based architectures are the most widely used choice for classifying or detecting disease from chest radiographs, CT scans, skin-lesion photographs, and microscopy images. Other deep architectures encountered in the literature include RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, capsule networks, ViTs, and large language models (LLMs). These models can capture intricate patterns across high-dimensional inputs, but their complexity means that understanding why they produce a particular output often requires a dedicated XAI layer [16, 17, 23, 24].

Model evaluation considerations

Across the reviewed literature, the most commonly reported metrics are accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, sensitivity, and specificity. Some imaging studies additionally report intersection over union (IoU) or perturbation-based localization scores when assessing how well a model identifies the correct spatial region. These metrics can be informative, but they need to be interpreted carefully when the underlying data are small, single-center, heavily augmented, or otherwise constrained, and especially when no external validation has been performed [25–29].

A parallel set of criteria applies to explanations. Qualities such as faithfulness, completeness, stability, and correctness are increasingly discussed in the XAI research community as standards for evaluating whether an explanation genuinely reflects the decision process of the model. In practice, however, these criteria are inconsistently reported in infectious disease studies, and many papers present explanations that look clinically plausible without formally testing whether they are robust or reliable [30, 31].

The role of generative AI in infectious disease diagnosis

Generative AI is increasingly present in infectious disease research, but it contributes in two quite different ways that are worth distinguishing clearly. The first is data augmentation and synthetic image generation. Methods such as generative adversarial networks (GANs), variational autoencoders (VAEs), and diffusion models are used to produce synthetic medical images or expand datasets for rare or emerging infections where annotated clinical data are difficult to obtain [32–35]. The second role is language and explanation support. Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) are applied to tasks such as summarizing clinical notes, supporting triage decisions, and generating human-readable interpretations of model outputs for clinicians [24, 36, 37]. Although both fall under the generative AI umbrella, their functions, limitations, and transparency implications are fundamentally different, and conflating them can obscure important distinctions in how each should be validated.

Both types of generative AI introduce challenges for trustworthy deployment. GAN-generated images may not fully capture the distributional complexity of real clinical data, which risks inflating model performance when augmented samples end up in both training and evaluation sets. LLM outputs, despite their fluency, can be factually incomplete or internally inconsistent in ways that may not be immediately obvious to the reader. For these reasons, the growing use of generative AI in this domain makes rigorous explainability and external validation even more important. When LLM-generated explanations are deployed in clinical or public health contexts, several risk-mitigation principles apply. First, hallucination risk must be acknowledged: LLMs can generate fluent but factually unsupported statements, which is particularly dangerous when the output is intended to inform a diagnostic decision. Second, explanations should be grounded in structured, verifiable evidence rather than generated freely from model weights alone; retrieval-augmented or constrained output formats reduce the risk of unverifiable claims. Third, structured output formats such as templated summaries tied to specific model predictions are preferable to open-ended natural language generation, as they limit the degrees of freedom available for confabulation. Fourth, human oversight remains essential: LLM-generated explanations should be reviewed by a qualified clinician before influencing any clinical action, and should be clearly labelled as AI-generated interpretations rather than clinical conclusions [19, 38].

Explainable AI methods

Explainable AI (XAI) refers broadly to methods that make the reasoning of AI systems interpretable to human users. In healthcare, this is not a minor concern; clinicians need to be able to evaluate the credibility of a model’s prediction, recognize when something may have gone wrong, and integrate AI output into their broader clinical reasoning in a way that they can justify to colleagues and patients [19]. The aspiration to make automated reasoning transparent predates modern deep learning (early medical expert systems of the 1970s were built around explicit inference rules precisely so their conclusions could be audited), but the proliferation of high-capacity neural networks has made the problem considerably more difficult [31, 39].

XAI methods are generally organized into two different approaches. The first distinguishes attribution-based methods, which estimate how much each input feature or region contributed to a given prediction, from non-attribution-based methods, which explain model behavior through higher-level abstractions such as concept activations, counterfactual examples, or learned prototypes [40–42]. The second approach separates intrinsic (ante-hoc) interpretability, where the model is designed to be transparent from the outset (logistic regression, decision trees, prototype networks), from post-hoc interpretability, where a separate explanation is derived after the model has been trained [18].

In practice, the choice of XAI method is also shaped by the data modality. Image-based studies call for spatially resolved explanations that can be overlaid on the original scan and evaluated against known anatomical or pathological landmarks. Tabular and laboratory studies benefit more from feature-attribution scores that rank the contribution of specific clinical variables and can be connected directly to clinical reasoning. Text and time-series data each bring their own requirements. The subsections below describe the methods that appear most often in the infectious disease literature reviewed in this paper, grouped by their methodological character.

Feature-attribution methods

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) attributes a contribution score to each input feature for a given prediction by applying Shapley values from cooperative game theory [43]. Concretely, the Shapley value for a feature is the average marginal contribution of that feature across all possible subsets of features, which gives it a well-defined and theoretically grounded interpretation. SHAP supports both local explanations (why did the model produce this particular output for this instance) and global explanations (which features matter most on average across the dataset). In infectious disease applications it has been used to identify influential biomarkers in laboratory panels, explain severity predictions, and support triage decisions [44–46]. Its main practical limitation is computational cost: exact Shapley value computation is exponential in the number of features, and approximations can be slow for large deep networks.

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) takes a different approach; rather than examining the model globally, it focuses on a single prediction and constructs a locally faithful linear surrogate by generating perturbed versions of the input, querying the black-box model on each, and fitting a simple interpretable model to the resulting input-output pairs [47]. Because it treats the underlying model as a black box, LIME is applicable to any architecture and any data type. In infectious disease studies it has been used across tabular clinical records, image classifiers, and multimodal systems [44, 45, 48, 49]. A known limitation is instability; the local approximation can vary across repeated runs, particularly for complex or highly non-linear decision boundaries.

Gradient- and activation-based visual explanation methods

The methods in this group are designed for CNN-based image classifiers and generate spatially resolved maps that highlight which regions of an image most influenced the model’s prediction. They differ primarily in how they estimate the importance of each spatial location.

Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) computes a localization map by taking the gradient of the class score with respect to the feature maps of the final convolutional layer, spatially pooling the gradients to obtain per-channel importance weights, and projecting the weighted combination back onto the input image as a heatmap [49, 50]. It requires no modification to the model architecture and produces coarse but interpretable visualizations that can be compared against known pathological regions. In the infectious disease literature it is one of the most widely applied explanation methods [51].

Grad-CAM++ refines this by replacing the global average pooling of gradients with a weighted combination of higher-order partial derivatives of the class activation. This yields better spatial coverage and improved localization in cases where multiple discriminative regions are present in the same image [52].

Score-CAM takes a gradient-free approach. Rather than backpropagating class scores, it masks the input with each feature map in turn and measures the resulting change in the model’s confidence for the target class [53]. This perturbation-based strategy avoids gradient saturation effects and tends to produce more reliable maps in settings where gradients carry limited information.

CAM (Class Activation Map) is the original formulation from which the other CAM methods were derived. It computes a discriminative map as a weighted sum of the feature maps from the final convolutional layer, with weights taken from the classification layer [54]. CAM requires a specific architectural constraint (global average pooling before the classifier), but when this constraint is met, it provides a clean and readily interpretable spatial summary of class-relevant image regions.

Guided Grad-CAM combines the class-discriminative localization of Grad-CAM with guided backpropagation, which zeroes out negative gradients during the backward pass [55]. The result is a high-resolution map that retains both the region-level coarseness of Grad-CAM and the pixel-level detail of guided backpropagation, making it useful when fine-grained anatomical correspondence is important.

Saliency Maps are one of the earliest gradient-based explanation methods. The gradient of the class score with respect to each input pixel indicates which pixels, if perturbed slightly, would most change the prediction [56]. Compared to CAM-based methods, saliency maps are higher resolution but typically noisier and more sensitive to gradient artifacts.

Occlusion Sensitivity estimates the importance of image regions by systematically masking each region and recording how the confidence of the model changes [57]. Regions whose occlusion produces large drops in confidence are inferred to be highly discriminative. The method is straightforward and model-agnostic but can be computationally expensive for fine-grained patch sizes.

Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) propagates the model’s output score backward through the network layer by layer, distributing relevance according to conservation rules, until a per-input-feature relevance score is obtained [58]. LRP produces dense attribution maps with resolution comparable to saliency maps but is grounded in a more principled redistribution framework and is applicable to a broad range of neural architectures.

Captum is an open-source interpretability library for PyTorch that exposes a unified programming interface for a wide range of attribution methods (gradient-based, perturbation-based, and layer-attribution approaches), making it straightforward to compare multiple explanation strategies on the same model within a single experimental setup [59].

Rule-based and decomposition methods

Anchors produces sufficient-condition rules of the form “if conditions, then prediction holds with probability ≥ p.” These rules are derived by searching for the minimal set of feature conditions that locally stabilizes the model output, and they read directly as clinical decision heuristics [60].

QLattice searches the space of compact symbolic mathematical expressions to find one that approximates the input-output mapping of the model over a given region of the feature space [61]. Because the resulting expression is algebraically explicit, it can be inspected and evaluated against domain knowledge in a way that most black-box explanation methods do not permit.

ELI5 is a Python library that provides feature-attribution explanations for a range of scikit-learn estimators and wraps the results in a readable reporting format [62]. It is particularly well-suited to rapid inspection of tree-based or linear models on tabular clinical data.

DALEX (Descriptive mAchine Learning EXplanations) is a model-agnostic toolkit that brings together several local and global explanation strategies, including SHAP, LIME, partial dependence profiles, accumulated local effects, and breakdown plots, under a common interface, making it convenient to diagnose and compare models across different pipelines [63].

Specialized and emerging methods

GNNExplainer addresses the specific case of graph neural networks. It identifies the minimal subgraph and the subset of node features that are most important for a given prediction, making it applicable when patient interaction networks, protein-protein interaction graphs, or similar relational structures are part of the input [64].

PES (Patch-wise Evaluation of Saliency) is an evaluation protocol for assessing how faithful a saliency map is. It works by occluding image patches that a given XAI method has marked as important and measuring the resulting change in model confidence [65]. Because it yields a quantitative score rather than a qualitative visual judgment, PES provides a basis for objectively comparing competing explanation methods on the same model and dataset, a capability that is still underused in infectious disease studies.

LLaMA and related instruction-tuned language models have been applied in a small number of studies to generate free-text, clinician-readable descriptions of model predictions derived from structured data or image-based results [66]. LLaMA sits at the intersection of XAI and clinical natural language generation; while promising, it remains to be seen whether the explanations it generates are reliable.

Applications of XAI in infectious disease diagnosis

Section “Explainable AI methods” highlights the practical use of XAI in infectious disease diagnosis. Building on the conceptual groundwork and technical overview from earlier sections, we now examine how specific explainability methods such as SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM have been implemented in recent studies. By surveying a range of infectious diseases, including dengue, malaria, monkeypox, tuberculosis, COVID-19, and pneumonia, we demonstrate the real-world impact, challenges, and insights that XAI brings to clinical practice.

Dengue

The dengue literature reviewed here shows a clear modality-dependent pattern in both model design and explainability strategy. Most studies rely on tabular symptom, laboratory, or biosensor-derived data, whereas only one study uses blood smear imaging. This difference is reflected in the choice of XAI methods; tabular studies primarily use feature-attribution approaches such as SHAP and LIME, while the image-based study uses Grad-CAM to localize visually important regions. Across all four studies, the reported results are promising, but they should be interpreted cautiously because the datasets are relatively small, come from limited sources, and do not provide strong external validation [2, 67–69].

Among the tabular studies, a recurring theme is that XAI methods identify clinically plausible dengue-related predictors. Kumar et al. [67] used symptom-based data in a dual-level framework combining a lightweight multilayer perceptron with rule-based inference, and SHAP and LIME highlighted features such as skin rash and high fever. Riya et al. [69] used complete blood count data in a stacking ensemble model and reported that platelet count, neutrophil percentage, and eosinophil levels were the most influential variables according to LIME. Vaiyapuri [2] analyzed a biosensor-derived dengue dataset and found that SHAP emphasized hemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelet count. Taken together, these studies suggest that feature-attribution methods can generate explanations that align with clinically relevant symptom and hematological patterns. However, the supporting evidence remains limited by dataset size, with one study using only 77 records and another using 320 samples, and by the absence of broader validation across institutions or populations [2, 69].

The image-based study by Mayrose et al. [68] provides a useful contrast. Using 888 labeled peripheral blood smear images from a single medical center and transfer learning with pretrained convolutional neural networks, the authors reported the highest internal accuracy in this subsection, with MobileNetV3Small achieving 98.2%. Grad-CAM heatmaps highlighted lymphocyte-related image regions, offering visual localization rather than feature-level attribution. This study illustrates an important difference across modalities; image-based dengue diagnosis tends to rely on saliency-style XAI, whereas tabular studies more often use SHAP or LIME to rank influential variables. At the same time, the strong reported performance should be interpreted carefully because the dataset was single-center and the explanations were presented mainly as plausible visual overlays rather than being quantitatively validated for faithfulness or stability [68].

Overall, the dengue studies suggest that XAI can improve interpretability across both tabular and image-based diagnostic models, but the current literature remains closer to promising early evidence than to clinically validated deployment. High internal accuracies are reported in several studies, yet these results are frequently derived from small, limited-source datasets and are not supported by strong external validation. In addition, although SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM produce explanations that appear clinically reasonable, the studies in this subsection do not rigorously evaluate explanation reliability through measures such as faithfulness, stability, or clinician agreement. Therefore, the main contribution of the current dengue literature is to show that interpretable AI is feasible in this domain, while stronger validation and more systematic assessment of explanation quality are still needed before these systems can be considered robust for clinical use [2, 67–69].

Malaria

The malaria studies reviewed here span both microscopy-based image classification and tabular clinical diagnosis, and they show a clear modality-dependent pattern in both predictive performance and explainability strategy. The highest reported results generally come from blood-smear image studies using deep learning, whereas tabular studies based on symptoms or clinical records report more modest accuracy but offer more direct feature-level explanations. This difference is also reflected in the XAI methods used; image-based studies mainly rely on visual explanation tools such as Grad-CAM, LIME, feature maps, and attention-based visualizations, while tabular studies more often use SHAP and LIME to identify influential symptoms or clinical variables [70–75].

Among the microscopy studies, several report very strong internal performance on large blood-smear image datasets. Mmileng et al. [70] used 27,558 original images from the Lister Hill malaria collection and expanded them through augmentation to 606,276 images. Their best model, ConvNeXt V2 Tiny Remod, achieved 98.1% accuracy, with LIME heatmaps and large-language-model-generated text used to support interpretability. However, the authors also note that no additional external testing data were included, which limits conclusions about generalizability beyond the study dataset. Gaouar et al. [73] used a large NIH–NLM smear-image dataset from Bangladesh and Thailand and reported the best performance for a stacked-LSTM with attention, achieving 99.12% accuracy. Grad-CAM and LIME highlighted parasite-relevant regions and local image features. Ahamed et al. [74] reported the strongest performance for this approach. They used customized CNN architectures on a reannotated NIH malaria dataset of 26,161 images, with SPCNN achieving 99.37% accuracy and very high AUC. Their XAI analysis combined feature maps, Grad-CAM, and SHAP, and they also evaluated the models on additional datasets and malaria species, making this one of the stronger validation efforts. At the same time, the paper still notes that broader geographic and population diversity remains a limitation.

The tabular studies provide a more clinically mixed and, in some ways, more realistic picture. Awe et al. [71] analyzed records from 337 Nigerian patients aged 3–77 and found that random forest performed best among several ensemble models with an accuracy of 81.95% and ROC AUC of approximately 0.87. SHAP, LIME, and permutation-based analyses highlighted clinically meaningful predictors such as age, headache, coca-cola urine, prostration, convulsion, diarrhea, and hyperpyrexia, supporting the value of feature-attribution methods for symptom-based malaria diagnosis. Attai et al. [72] studied malaria and typhoid jointly using 3,914 patient records from the Niger Delta after preprocessing, feature selection, and SMOTE balancing. Random forest again performed best, but with lower accuracy (71.99%), reflecting the harder task of differential diagnosis in the presence of overlapping symptoms. LIME identified key symptoms, and natural-language explanation support was used to make the model output more accessible in a clinical setting.

A separate but related line of work is represented by Udoh et al. [75], who developed the MLAHD system for automated hemoparasite detection, including malaria, using a portable Raspberry Pi–oriented CNN trained on 10,000 labeled blood-smear images. The paper reports strong performance and pilot use in resource-limited settings together with Grad-CAM, SHAP, and LIME presented through a clinician-oriented dashboard. However, because this study targets hemoparasites more broadly rather than malaria alone, its results are best interpreted as supportive evidence for deployment-oriented XAI rather than as a directly comparable malaria-only benchmark.

Overall, the malaria literature presents some of the strongest promising early results in this review, especially for microscopy-based screening. However, the evidence is still uneven. The highest reported accuracies mostly come from image-based studies that use curated smear-image datasets, extensive preprocessing, or heavy augmentation, so these results should be interpreted with caution when considering broader clinical use. The type of explanation also differs by data modality; microscopy studies mainly rely on visual localization, whereas tabular studies focus more on symptom ranking and, in some cases, natural-language explanation support. Although these explanations are often described as clinically meaningful, most studies do not evaluate their quality in a rigorous way, for example through measures of faithfulness, stability, or clinician agreement. Overall, the findings suggest that XAI-enhanced malaria diagnosis is promising, but stronger external validation and more careful assessment of explanation quality are still needed before these systems can be considered ready for routine clinical deployment [70–75].

Monkeypox

The monkeypox literature reviewed here follows two main directions: image-based diagnosis from skin-lesion photographs and symptom-based diagnosis from tabular clinical data. Most studies fall into the first category and use deep learning models on publicly available or curated lesion-image datasets, whereas a smaller group focuses on symptom-driven machine learning models designed for settings where imaging may be limited or unavailable. This distinction influences not only predictive performance but also the type of explanation provided. Image-based studies mainly rely on Grad-CAM and LIME to localize lesion regions associated with model decisions, while symptom-based studies more often use SHAP, LIME, ELI5, QLattice, and counterfactual reasoning to identify influential clinical features [76–87].

Across the image-based studies, a consistent pattern is the use of transfer learning, ensemble CNNs, attention mechanisms, or transformer-inspired architectures on relatively small or medium-sized lesion-image datasets. Reported results are often very strong, with accuracies commonly exceeding 98%, as seen in studies based on MSID, MSLD, curated six-class lesion datasets, close-up public image collections, and augmented monkeypox skin-lesion datasets [77–83, 85–87]. These studies collectively show that deep learning can separate monkeypox lesions from visually similar conditions with high internal performance. At the same time, their strongest results are frequently obtained on public, curated, augmented, or otherwise constrained datasets, including binary or simplified classification settings. This makes the reported performance encouraging, but not automatically representative of broader clinical generalizability. Another common feature of this group is that explanations are usually presented through lesion-centered heatmaps, with Grad-CAM and LIME serving as the dominant tools for showing whether the network attended to visually plausible disease regions [77–79, 81–83, 85–87].

The symptom-based studies provide a different but clinically important perspective. Rather than relying on lesion images, these studies use structured symptom records derived from WHO or publicly curated monkeypox case collections. In this setting, gradient boosting and ensemble tree methods appear particularly effective, with XGBoost and LGBMClassifier emerging as strong performers on relatively small datasets [76, 84]. Compared with the image-based studies, these models generally report lower or more variable performance, but they offer more direct interpretability because the explanations are tied to explicit clinical variables such as fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, oral or genital ulcers, fatigue, and sore throat. This makes the reasoning process easier to connect to clinical understanding, especially when SHAP, LIME, ELI5, QLattice, and counterfactual explanations are used together to compare feature importance from multiple perspectives [76, 84]. These studies are also more immediately relevant to low-resource settings where image capture and dermatological expertise may not be consistently available, although their small sample sizes remain an important limitation.

A further distinction across the monkeypox literature lies in how explanation quality is assessed. In most image-based studies, interpretability is demonstrated visually, and the resulting heatmaps are treated as plausible if they align with lesion regions that appear clinically relevant. Only a small number of studies move beyond this qualitative approach. Among the studies reviewed here, the hybrid/transformer work stands out for reporting quantitative evaluation of Grad-CAM explanations through measures such as mask overlap, feature relevance, and consistency error [78]. By contrast, most other studies do not formally evaluate whether the explanations are faithful, stable, or reliable across cases. The symptom-based studies are somewhat stronger in this respect conceptually because they compare multiple explanation tools and present feature-level reasoning more directly, but they still rely mainly on interpretability by plausibility rather than on systematic explanation validation [76, 84].

Overall, the monkeypox literature provides strong early evidence that XAI-enhanced diagnosis is feasible across both image-based and symptom-based workflows. However, the current evidence base remains uneven. The strongest reported accuracies are concentrated in image-based studies that rely on curated or public datasets, often with augmentation or limited-source sampling, which makes it difficult to infer broad clinical generalizability from internal performance alone. In addition, explanation strategies differ substantially by modality; image-based studies mainly emphasize lesion localization, whereas symptom-based studies provide feature-level and, in some cases, counterfactual reasoning that may be more directly interpretable in clinical settings. Although many studies present their explanations as clinically meaningful, formal evaluation of explanation quality remains limited in most cases. Overall, these findings show that XAI-supported monkeypox diagnosis is promising, but stronger validation across more heterogeneous patient populations and clinical environments is still needed before routine deployment can be justified [76–87].

Tuberculosis (TB)

Across the reviewed tuberculosis (TB) studies, two main patterns emerged. First, most XAI applications focused on chest x-ray analysis, and explainability was primarily used to localize radiographic abnormalities and support visual interpretation of model predictions. Second, a smaller group of studies used structured clinical or laboratory data, and feature-attribution methods were applied to identify important predictors and improve transparency in risk estimation. Overall, the TB literature shows that XAI has been applied both to imaging-based screening and to clinical decision support, although the strength of validation and the robustness of reported explanations varied substantially across studies.

In imaging-based studies, chest x-rays were the dominant data modality, and most models relied on convolutional neural networks or transformer-based architectures. Özkurt [88] developed a CNN model using a dataset composed of 700 publicly available TB-positive chest x-rays, 2800 additional TB-positive images from the NIAID TB portal [89], and 3500 normal images. Their model achieved strong classification performance, and SHAP and LIME were used to explain predictions, with SHAP generally producing more detailed and reliable visual explanations than LIME by highlighting both positively and negatively contributing image regions. Similarly, Vanitha et al. [90] proposed a Vision Transformer (ViT) integrated with Grad-CAM for TB detection from chest x-rays. Their results showed high predictive performance, while Grad-CAM heatmaps highlighted pathological regions such as nodules and infiltrates, helping link model decisions to clinically relevant image features.

Other studies emphasized the balance between interpretability, model complexity, and practical deployment. Maheswari et al. [91] introduced an explainable shallow CNN for TB screening and showed that a less complex architecture could achieve competitive performance while producing more localized and interpretable visual explanations through CAM and LIME. The highlighted regions corresponded to clinically meaningful abnormalities such as granuloma formations and infiltrates in the upper lung zones. Khan et al. [92] also explored lightweight CNN architectures for multi-class pulmonary disease classification, including TB, and used Grad-CAM and t-SNE visualizations to highlight discriminative lung regions while maintaining computational efficiency. These studies suggest that, in TB imaging tasks, explainability is often presented not only as a transparency tool but also as a practical component for supporting low-resource or real-time screening settings.

Some studies further strengthened imaging pipelines by improving the focus of both prediction and explanation. Rahman et al. [93, 94] showed that lung segmentation before classification could improve TB detection performance and reduce the influence of irrelevant image regions. Their work supports the idea that preprocessing steps such as segmentation may improve both predictive reliability and the clinical plausibility of subsequent visual explanations. However, despite these promising findings, many TB imaging studies relied on public, merged, preprocessed, or augmented datasets, which may have inflated performance estimates. It also made it difficult to assess how well the models and their explanations would generalize to new hospitals, imaging devices, or patient populations.

In addition to imaging applications, XAI was used in structured-data TB studies. Peng et al. [95] used electronic medical records from 429 patients with tuberculosis–diabetes comorbidity to predict treatment failure risk. Among the evaluated machine learning models, XGBoost performed best, and SHAP was used to identify influential predictors such as drug-resistance type, activated partial thromboplastin time, thrombin time, platelet distribution width, HbA1c, and other laboratory variables. In a different clinical setting, Yao et al. [96] developed an interpretable light gradient boosting machine (LGBM) model for noninvasive diagnosis of tuberculous pleural effusion using routine laboratory data from 963 patients. SHAP was applied at both the global and individual levels to explain model predictions. Notably, this study included an external validation cohort, which was uncommon among the reviewed TB studies.

Taken together, the tuberculosis literature shows broader methodological diversity than some other infectious disease categories, spanning chest x-ray screening, treatment-risk prediction, and pleural effusion diagnosis. Across these applications, SHAP, LIME, CAM, and Grad-CAM were the most common XAI methods. In imaging studies, these tools were mainly used to localize suspicious lung regions, whereas in structured-data studies they were used to rank influential clinical features. Nevertheless, several common limitations remain. Many studies relied on retrospective, single-center, augmented, or otherwise constrained datasets, and external validation was still limited beyond a few exceptions. In addition, although many papers presented visually plausible heatmaps or feature-attribution results, formal evaluation of explanation quality was rarely reported. These patterns suggest that XAI for TB is promising, but stronger validation and more systematic assessment of explanation reliability are still needed before it is routinely used in clinical settings.

COVID-19

The COVID-19 literature reviewed in this paper spans several distinct data modalities, including routine clinical and laboratory markers, chest x-ray and CT imaging, molecular and omics-style measurements, mass spectrometry profiles, and respiratory audio signals. Altogether, these studies show that XAI has been used across a broader methodological range for COVID-19 than for many other infectious diseases. At the same time, the strength of evidence varies substantially across modalities, especially with respect to dataset composition, external validation, and how explanation quality is assessed [98–110].

A substantial portion of the COVID-19 XAI literature is based on imaging, particularly chest x-rays and CT scans. These studies generally report very strong internal classification performance and use post hoc visual explanation methods to highlight infection-relevant regions. In chest x-ray studies, transfer-learning and ensemble CNN frameworks have been used not only to distinguish COVID-19 from normal images but also to separate it from related thoracic conditions such as lung opacity and non-COVID viral pneumonia [104, 107]. In CT-based studies, models built on DenseNet-style or hybrid deep architectures have similarly reported high performance, and explanation methods such as LIME, Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, and SHAP were used to emphasize regions consistent with ground-glass opacities, consolidation, and other lung abnormalities associated with COVID-19 [105, 106, 108]. These results support the potential of XAI-enhanced imaging systems as transparent screening tools. However, many of the strongest results come from public or curated datasets, often with augmentation, class balancing, or limited-source sampling, so high internal accuracy should be interpreted more cautiously than raw performance numbers alone might suggest [104, 105, 107, 108].

Tabular and laboratory-based studies provide a different kind of interpretability. Rather than focusing on lesion localization, these models identify feature-level patterns in blood markers, inflammatory indicators, and other routine clinical variables. In this group of studies, albumin, total white blood cell count, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, CRP, D-dimer, LDH, ferritin, sodium, and related markers repeatedly emerged as influential for diagnosis, severity assessment, or ICU triage [98–100]. This style of explanation is often easier to connect to conventional clinical reasoning because the models can show how particular biomarker values shift predictions toward a more severe or more likely COVID-19 diagnosis. At the same time, these studies are usually based on retrospective hospital data from a limited number of institutions, and most do not include external validation cohorts, which constrains how confidently the reported models can be generalized beyond their local settings [98–100].

Beyond imaging and routine blood data, some studies have explored more specialized biological signals. Yagin et al. [101] used metagenomic next-generation sequencing data to identify gene-level biomarkers, with XGBoost combined with SHAP and LIME highlighting genes such as IFI27, LGR6, and FAM83A as particularly important. Seethi et al. [102] used MALDI-ToF mass spectrometry from gargle samples and showed that explainable machine learning can also be applied to protein-profile based diagnosis, with interpretable feature importance helping connect predictions to biologically meaningful signal ranges. These studies are important because they move explanation beyond image saliency and toward molecular or biochemical interpretation. However, they also rely on comparatively small datasets and still need broader validation before such approaches can be viewed as mature diagnostic solutions [101, 102].

Respiratory audio represents another approach in the COVID-19 literature. Using breathing recordings from the Coswara dataset, Sultana et al. [103] showed that explainable ensemble learning can identify discriminative acoustic features from breathing signals, with SHAP highlighting higher-frequency Mel-spectrogram components, chroma-based features, and RMS energy as important contributors. This line of work is appealing because it is inexpensive, non-invasive, and potentially deployable through smartphones. Still, these studies often require substantial preprocessing, filtering, balancing, and augmentation before classification, which means the reported performance should be viewed in the context of carefully prepared research settings rather than immediate real-world deployment [103].

An important difference across the COVID-19 literature is not only what gets explained, but also how explanation quality itself is examined. In most studies, explanation methods are used mainly to produce plausible visual or feature-based interpretations, but the explanations are not rigorously validated for faithfulness, stability, or consistency. One noteworthy exception is the work of Sun et al. [111], who proposed a patch-perturbation-based evaluation framework to test how well different XAI methods could recover deliberately injected trigger regions in chest x-ray classification. Their study went beyond visual inspection by comparing methods for correctness-, consistency-, and efficiency-oriented metrics. In contrast, most other COVID-19 studies stop at showing heatmaps, ranked features, or local explanations that appear clinically reasonable, without formally testing whether those explanations reliably reflect the actual decision process of the model [98–107, 109].

Overall, the COVID-19 literature provides strong evidence that XAI can be integrated into a wide range of diagnostic pipelines, from radiology and routine laboratory medicine to molecular profiling and respiratory acoustics. The literature is especially rich in transparent proof-of-concept systems and high-performing retrospective models. However, the evidence is still uneven in terms of external validation, dataset diversity, and rigorous assessment of explanation quality. For that reason, the current body of work is best viewed as showing that explainable COVID-19 diagnosis is technically feasible and often clinically promising, while broader validation and stronger evaluation of the explanations themselves remain important next steps before these approaches can be considered robust for routine deployment [98–111].

Pneumonia

For the pneumonia studies that were reviewed, two main patterns emerged. First, most XAI applications focused on chest x-ray analysis, where explainability was mainly used to localize radiographic abnormalities and support visual interpretation of model predictions. Second, a smaller group of studies used structured clinical and laboratory data to predict disease severity or intensive care needs, where feature-attribution methods were used to identify influential predictors and improve model transparency. Overall, the pneumonia literature shows that XAI has been applied both to imaging-based diagnosis and to clinical risk stratification, although performance, validation design, and explanation depth varied considerably across studies.

In structured-data studies, explainability was primarily used to identify clinically meaningful predictors rather than to visualize disease regions. Huang et al. [114] developed a machine learning model to predict intensive care unit admission among patients with community-acquired pneumonia and connective tissue disease using retrospective clinical data from 1,529 patients. Among nine machine learning algorithms, XGBoost showed the best predictive performance, and SHAP and LIME were used to interpret the model. The most influential predictors included higher N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide and C-reactive protein, lower CD4+ T cell and lymphocyte levels, lower serum sodium, and a positive serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan test. This study illustrates how XAI in pneumonia can support early risk stratification in addition to diagnosis, although its single-center retrospective design and lack of external validation are important limitations.

In imaging-based studies, chest x-rays were the dominant modality, but the reviewed works differed substantially in modeling philosophy. Hsu et al. [115] developed deep learning models to support the diagnosis of ventilator-associated pneumonia in mechanically ventilated neonates using 900 chest x-ray images from 670 patients in a prospective single-center intensive care unit study. Their ensemble model combining ResNet50 and RegNetX080 achieved the best overall performance, and Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, and Score-CAM were used to generate heatmaps. These visualizations consistently emphasized the lower-left lung region, providing image-based support for clinical interpretation. The study focused only on imaging data without integrating laboratory or clinical features, and external validation was not performed.

Other imaging studies placed greater emphasis on interpretability by design rather than post hoc visualization alone. Yazdani et al. [116] applied a deformable prototypical part network (D-ProtoPNet) to pediatric chest x-rays for classifying normal, viral, and bacterial pneumonia. Unlike standard black-box classifiers, this ante-hoc model explained its predictions by matching image regions to learned class-representative prototypes. Although its performance remained below that of some black-box models, it improved on the original ProtoPNet and, importantly, included a radiologist’s review of the learned prototypes and activated patches. This made it one of the stronger pneumonia studies in terms of clinical interpretability because explanation quality was not assessed only visually by the authors but was also judged by an expert radiologist. At the same time, the study still relied on a public retrospective dataset and internal cross-validation rather than a truly external clinical cohort.

The question of how clinicians actually perceive and interact with XAI outputs was addressed directly by Ihongbe et al. [117], who conducted a human-centered user study evaluating Grad-CAM and LIME applied to deep learning models for pneumonia and COVID-19 diagnosis from chest x-rays and CT scans, achieving 90 and 98% accuracy, respectively. Participants generally expressed a positive perception of XAI in chest radiology, but a notable lack of awareness of its practical value was observed. Grad-CAM was preferred over LIME in terms of coherency and trust, though concerns about its clinical usability were raised. The study identified multi-modal explainability and inclusive design as key requirements for XAI systems to be genuinely useful to medical practitioners. This work complements the findings of Yazdani et al. by shifting the evaluation lens from technical explanation quality to end-user acceptance, and it underscores that even technically sound XAI outputs may fall short of clinical adoption without deliberate attention to usability.

Biswas et al. [118] proposed FLPneXAINet, a federated deep learning and XAI framework for pneumonia detection from chest x-rays. Their approach combined federated learning, CycleGAN-based augmentation, feature optimization, and an ensemble deep learning pipeline, with LIME and Grad-CAM used for explanation. The framework achieved high performance on a public pediatric chest x-ray dataset and was presented as a privacy-preserving alternative to centralized training. This study is notable because it links explainability with privacy-aware deployment, but its strong results should still be interpreted cautiously given the heavy reliance on GAN-augmented data and the absence of external multicenter validation. As in several other pneumonia imaging studies, the explanation outputs were visually plausible, but formal evaluation of explanation quality was limited.

In summary, the pneumonia literature shows substantial methodological diversity, spanning intensive care risk prediction, neonatal ventilator-associated pneumonia detection, pediatric multi-class classification, privacy-preserving federated diagnosis, and human-centered XAI evaluation. Across these settings, SHAP and LIME were more common in structured-data studies, whereas Grad-CAM and related saliency methods dominated imaging-based studies. An exception was the prototype-based work of Yazdani et al., which provided ante-hoc interpretability and included a radiologist’s review of the model’s explanations. The user study of Ihongbe et al. added a further dimension by demonstrating that clinician acceptance of XAI outputs is not guaranteed even when technical performance is high. Nevertheless, several limitations were shared across studies; many relied on single-center or public retrospective datasets, some used augmentation or synthetic data generation, and external validation was missing. Overall, XAI for pneumonia appears promising across both clinical and imaging applications, but stronger validation, more systematic assessment of explanation reliability, and greater attention to end-user usability are still needed.

Other infectious diseases

The studies grouped in this category differ from the disease-specific sections above because they address broader infectious disease classification problems rather than focusing on a single infection. The studies reviewed can be divided into two categories. First, some targeted multiple infectious skin diseases using image-based deep learning models to distinguish between conditions with visually similar dermatological features. Second, others addressed multiple respiratory infections simultaneously, typically using lung ultrasound or chest x-ray images to classify different infectious and non-infectious conditions. For both types of studies, XAI was mainly used to show that model predictions were based on clinically meaningful image regions, although validation rigor and formal evaluation of explanation quality were limited.

For skin-disease classification, Abbas et al. [119] proposed a transfer-learning framework based on a pre-trained VGG16 model to classify chickenpox, measles, monkeypox, and normal skin images. The study used an augmented open-source skin-image dataset [120] and reported strong internal performance with an accuracy of 93.29%, precision of 88.72%, specificity of 97.99%, sensitivity of 93.19%, and an F1-score of 0.91. For interpretability, the authors applied layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP), which generated heatmaps showing the image regions that contributed most to the model’s predictions. Of note is that this study explicitly framed explainability as a way to improve trust in automated diagnosis. The dataset, however, was moderate in size, was derived from open-source images rather than a prospective clinical cohort, and did not include external validation, limiting the generalizability of the reported results.

For respiratory-infection classification, Hasan et al. [121] developed FP-CNN, a fuzzy pooling-based deep learning framework for classifying lung ultrasound images into COVID-19, pneumonia, and normal classes. Unlike many studies that relied on chest x-rays, this work focused on lung ultrasound which is portable and radiation-free. The dataset included more than 4,000 ultrasound images from 166 patients, and the best-performing configuration achieved 97.2% accuracy. To explain predictions, SHAP was applied to intermediate CNN layers, and the resulting heatmaps highlighted clinically meaningful ultrasound features such as B-lines, pleural irregularities, and lung consolidations. This correspondence between model explanations and known radiological signs improved the interpretability of the approach. Nevertheless, the study relied on internal data splits from a relatively limited patient cohort, and no external validation was reported.

Sarkar et al. [122] adopted a broader cross-disease perspective by developing a multi-scale CNN for chest x-ray classification across several lung-related conditions, including COVID-19, bacterial pneumonia, viral pneumonia, tuberculosis, fibrosis, lung opacity, and normal cases. The model was trained on a seven-class dataset assembled from four public chest x-ray sources and achieved high performance across several classification settings, including 96.05% accuracy in the full seven-class task. For explainability, SHAP was used to quantify influential pixel-level contributions, and Grad-CAM generated heatmaps highlighting clinically meaningful lung abnormalities such as opacities and lesions. Although these results suggest strong predictive capability and useful visual explanations, the study relied on an internally split dataset constructed from multiple public sources, and the balanced design may not reflect real-world disease prevalence or inter-site variability.

The studies reviewed in this section demonstrate that XAI is also being applied successfully in multi-disease settings. For skin, ultrasound, and chest x-ray applications, explainability was mainly used to visualize disease-relevant regions and to improve transparency. The studies shared common limitations; they relied largely on public or open-source datasets, internal validation protocols, or augmented and curated image collections. Formal evaluation of explanation quality using criteria such as faithfulness, completeness, or stability was not reported, and true external validation was not reported. These patterns suggest that XAI can support broader infectious-disease screening frameworks, but stronger validation and more systematic assessment of explanation reliability are still needed before such models can be considered robust for routine clinical use.

Cross-disease synthesis and comparative analysis

To strengthen the cross-disease synthesis, the studies summarized in the disease-specific tables were compared using the analytical framework described in section “Study selection and synthesis process”. Across the 54 study entries summarized in and Tables 2–8, imaging-based studies were the largest group (37/54, 68.5%), followed by tabular clinical, laboratory, or biosensor studies (14/54, 25.9%). The remaining studies used omics, mass spectrometry, or audio data (3/54, 5.6%). Validation was generally limited: 42 of the 54 summarized study entries (77.8%) used internal-only validation, 5 (9.3%) used held-out internal testing, 6 (11.1%) reported cross-dataset or cross-validation designs, and only 1 (1.9%) reported an external cohort. In terms of XAI methods, LIME appeared in 30 study entries and SHAP in 26, making feature-attribution methods the most common explanation strategy overall. Grad-CAM or Grad-CAM++ appeared in 23 and 5 study entries, respectively, reflecting the dominance of heatmap-based explanations in imaging studies.

The comparative thematic synthesis revealed four main cross-cutting patterns: modality-dependent selection of XAI methods, stronger reported performance in image-based studies but weaker generalizability evidence, limited formal evaluation of explanation quality, and a gap between technically interpretable outputs and clinically actionable explanations.

One consistent observation is that the choice of XAI method more often than not depended on the type of data being analyzed. For image-based studies, especially those using chest x-rays, CT scans, skin-lesion images, blood-smear images, and ultrasound, explanation was most commonly provided through visual methods such as Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, CAM, and related saliency-based approaches. In contrast, studies based on tabular clinical, laboratory, or biosensor data usually used feature-attribution methods such as SHAP and LIME to identify influential predictors and explain individual predictions [2, 67–69, 71, 72, 76, 84, 95, 96, 98–100, 114]. This pattern appeared across dengue, malaria, monkeypox, tuberculosis, COVID-19, and pneumonia, suggesting that the structure of the data plays a major role in shaping how interpretability is implemented.

Another recurring pattern was that the strongest predictive results were usually found in image-based studies, particularly those using curated public datasets, transfer learning, augmentation, or retrospective single-center data. In several malaria, monkeypox, tuberculosis, COVID-19, and pneumonia studies, the reported internal accuracies were very high. However, these results were frequently accompanied by limited external validation, modest dataset diversity, or study designs that make generalizability uncertain [68, 70, 73, 74, 77–79, 87, 90, 97, 104–106, 108, 118]. For this reason, strong internal performance should be interpreted carefully, especially when it is reported from small, augmented, or internally evaluated datasets.

Most studies presented their explanations as clinically plausible, but relatively few formally evaluated explanation quality. In many papers, explanations were considered useful when highlighted image regions or ranked features appeared to agree with known clinical signs or biomarkers. Although this kind of alignment is important, it is not the same as showing that an explanation is faithful, stable, complete, or otherwise reliable. Only a limited number of studies went beyond qualitative plausibility by including more rigorous evaluation of explanation behavior or stronger validation strategies [78, 96, 111, 116]. Overall, the literature suggests that explanation generation has become common in infectious disease AI studies, whereas explanation validation remains much less developed [111].

The reviewed studies also suggest a difference between technically interpretable output and clinically actionable explanation. In tabular studies, feature-attribution methods often produced explanations that could be directly linked to symptoms, biomarkers, or routine laboratory findings, making them easier to connect to conventional clinical reasoning [2, 69, 71, 76, 84, 95, 96, 98–100, 114]. In contrast, many imaging studies relied on heatmaps that showed where the model focused, but not always why those regions were clinically meaningful or how the explanation should be interpreted in practice [68, 73, 77, 79, 88, 90, 104, 105, 115, 118]. This does not reduce the value of image-based XAI, but it does suggest that visual explanations may need stronger clinical grounding, quantitative evaluation, or expert review to become more actionable in real-world settings.

A small number of recent studies have begun to examine multimodal infectious-disease systems, particularly for COVID-19, where imaging data can be combined with clinical or laboratory variables. For example, Tur [123] developed a multimodal COVID-19 diagnostic model that combined blood biomarkers such as CRP, ferritin, NLR, and albumin with chest x-ray images. In that system, the image model and biomarker model were integrated through late fusion, and SHAP and LIME were used to interpret both the clinical and imaging contributions. The explanations identified inflammatory markers such as CRP and ferritin as important predictors while also highlighting lung regions that contributed to the imaging-based prediction. Olar et al. [124] similarly studied COVID-19 severity prediction using chest x-rays together with clinical metadata. Their explainable models used SHAP to assess the contribution of clinical and radiographic severity features and used attention-based visual maps to connect metadata features with image regions. These examples show that multimodal XAI can provide a more complete explanation than single-modality approaches by indicating not only which image regions influenced the prediction but also which clinical or laboratory variables supported the same decision. However, such studies remain relatively limited, and future work should more systematically evaluate how explanations from different modalities are fused, weighted, and validated.

As a whole, these findings show that XAI in infectious disease diagnosis is promising in multiple disease settings, but the current evidence remains uneven in strength. The reviewed literature shows that XAI can help identify influential biomarkers, highlight disease-relevant regions in medical images, and improve transparency across a wide range of diagnostic tasks. At the same time, the field remains limited by weak external validation, frequent reliance on constrained datasets, inconsistent reporting of explanation-quality measures, and a continuing gap between plausible explanations and clinically validated trustworthiness [96, 111, 116]. These shared patterns suggest that future work should place greater emphasis on multicenter validation, stronger assessment of explanation reliability, and closer alignment between XAI outputs and real clinical needs.

Challenges and limitations

While this survey highlights the significant potential and rapid expansion of explainable AI (XAI) in infectious disease diagnosis, several important challenges and limitations remain. Addressing these issues will be necessary if XAI is to move from promising research prototypes to reliable and clinically useful tools.

Selection and Publication Bias: This review may be affected by selection bias arising from the databases searched, the English-language restriction, and the focus on peer-reviewed journal and conference publications. Although PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar provide broad coverage of biomedical, engineering, and interdisciplinary AI literature, relevant studies indexed in other databases or published in other languages may have been missed. In addition, excluding non-peer-reviewed preprints, theses, editorials, and gray literature may have reduced methodological heterogeneity but may also have excluded emerging work, negative results, or studies reporting unsuccessful applications. Publication bias is also possible because studies with strong predictive performance, visually compelling explanations, or positive conclusions are more likely to be published than studies with weak, null, or negative findings. Therefore, the patterns identified in this review reflect the peer-reviewed English-language literature indexed in the selected databases and should not be considered a comprehensive account of all XAI research in infectious disease diagnosis.

Dataset and Generalizability Constraints: A major limitation across the reviewed literature is the frequent reliance on small-scale, single-center, geographically limited, or otherwise constrained datasets. Such limitations reduce confidence in model generalizability. A model trained on data from one hospital, imaging device, or patient population may not perform reliably in a different clinical setting, especially in the presence of domain shift. In addition, many public datasets are affected by class imbalance, source bias, augmentation-heavy workflows, or limited external validation, all of which can inflate reported performance and weaken confidence in the resulting explanations.A further quality concern is the frequent underreporting of the reference standard used to establish ground truth labels. In imaging studies, labels were often inherited from the source dataset without specifying whether they were derived from PCR confirmation, radiologist consensus, or clinical diagnosis. In tabular studies, the labeling process was similarly underspecified in many cases. The absence of a clearly documented reference standard makes it difficult to assess whether a model is learning to predict a clinically meaningful outcome or an artifact of the labeling procedure.A third concern, and the most critical for interpreting reported performance figures, is the lack of explicit data leakage controls. Patient-level splitting, that is, ensuring that all images or records from a given patient appear exclusively in either the training or the test partition, is necessary to prevent artificially inflated performance estimates. In imaging studies where multiple images per patient are common, image-level random splitting without patient-level separation can result in near-duplicate images appearing in both training and test sets, producing accuracy figures that substantially overestimate true generalization. Among the studies reviewed, few explicitly stated that patient-level splitting was applied, and fewer still reported leakage controls as a formal methodological criterion.Taken together, these study quality dimensions, data source type, reference standard documentation, and leakage controls, directly constrain the strength of conclusions that can be drawn from reported results. Where studies report high accuracy from single-center, publicly curated, or image-level-split datasets without external validation, the evidence supports proof-of-concept feasibility rather than clinical readiness. The conclusions drawn throughout this review are calibrated accordingly: stronger claims are reserved for studies that include external validation, clearly documented labeling, and explicit leakage controls, while studies lacking these features are described in terms of internal performance only.

Computational Overhead and the Accuracy–Interpretability Trade-off: Another recurring challenge is the trade-off between model complexity and interpretability. Deep learning models, including complex convolutional architectures, transformers, and ensemble systems, often achieve the strongest predictive performance, but they are also among the least transparent. Explaining these models commonly requires post hoc XAI techniques that can be computationally expensive. For example, SHAP is attractive because of its theoretical grounding and flexibility, but it may be too slow for real-time clinical use, especially in resource-limited environments. Conclusion

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into medical diagnostics has created new opportunities for improving infectious disease diagnosis. As shown throughout this survey, AI models, especially deep learning approaches, have demonstrated strong potential across a wide range of data types, including medical images, laboratory measurements, biosensor data, respiratory signals, and molecular profiles. These methods have been applied to important infectious diseases such as dengue, malaria, monkeypox, tuberculosis, COVID-19, and pneumonia, often with promising predictive performance. At the same time, the black-box nature of many AI systems remains a major obstacle to clinical adoption, particularly in settings where trust, accountability, and transparent reasoning are essential.

This paper presented a comprehensive scoping review of explainable AI (XAI) applications in infectious disease diagnosis for literature published between 2023 and 2025. We reviewed widely used XAI methods, including feature-attribution approaches such as SHAP and LIME, visual explanation methods such as Grad-CAM and its variants, and other techniques aimed at improving model transparency. Through the disease-specific and cross-disease analyses, this survey found that, within the peer-reviewed English-language studies captured by the search strategy, XAI was commonly used to identify influential biomarkers, localize disease-relevant regions in medical images, and improve the interpretability of AI-assisted diagnostic systems across a variety of clinical tasks.

The simplified quality and risk-of-bias assessment showed that most included studies had at least moderate concern in one or more domains. The most common sources of concern were limited dataset diversity, internal-only validation, reliance on public or augmented datasets, and lack of formal explanation-quality assessment. Studies that used external cohorts, cross-dataset evaluation, clinician review, or quantitative explanation assessment were considered stronger, but these were less common across the reviewed literature. Therefore, the overall evidence base supports the technical feasibility of XAI for infectious disease diagnosis more strongly than it supports broad clinical generalizability or deployment readiness.

At the same time, the reviewed literature also revealed several important limitations. Many studies reported strong internal performance, but relied on small, retrospective, single-center, public, or augmented datasets, making generalizability uncertain. External validation remained limited in many disease areas. In addition, although explanations were frequently presented as clinically plausible, formal evaluation of explanation quality was uncommon. Metrics such as faithfulness, stability, completeness, and correctness were rarely assessed systematically, and clinician-centered validation was also limited in most studies. As a result, the current evidence supports the technical feasibility of XAI in infectious disease diagnosis more strongly than its readiness for routine clinical deployment.

Future work should address these limitations directly. More robust evaluation protocols are needed to assess not only predictive performance, but also explanation reliability and clinical usefulness. There is also a need for computationally efficient XAI methods that are suitable for real-time or resource-constrained settings. A clinician-centered design perspective will be important to ensure that explanations align with diagnostic workflows and support decision-making in practice. In addition, larger multicenter studies using more diverse datasets are needed to improve generalizability, and advances in multimodal XAI will be important for reflecting the complexity of real clinical reasoning. Continued collaboration among AI researchers, clinicians, ethicists, and regulatory bodies will be necessary to establish standards for responsible and effective use.

In summary, the reviewed evidence suggests that explainable AI holds promise for improving the transparency, trustworthiness, and potential clinical utility of AI systems in infectious disease diagnosis, but this conclusion should be interpreted in light of the search restrictions described earlier, possible publication bias, and the limited external validation of the included studies.

Logistic Regression: Logistic regression is a widely used and straightforward AI method for making predictions, especially when the outcome is a simple yes/no question, such as “Does this person have the disease?” Imagine filling out a health questionnaire in which each answer, such as age, temperature, or blood test result, is assigned a certain weight based on how important it is for the diagnosis. Logistic regression takes all these weighted answers, adds them up, and then uses a special formula to turn that total into a probability between 0 and 1. If the probability is high enough, the model predicts “yes”; if it is low, the prediction is “no.” It is a bit like a points system for making decisions: easy to use, quick to run, and generally easy to interpret. However, it works best when the relationship between the inputs and the outcome is fairly simple and straightforward [20].
