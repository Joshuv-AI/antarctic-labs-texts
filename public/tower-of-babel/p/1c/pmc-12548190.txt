Multivariate screening of upland cotton genotypes reveals key traits for salt tolerance at the seedling stage
Authors: Zunaira Anwar, Allah Ditta, Muhammad Kashif Riaz Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoil salinity poses a serious threat to cotton production worldwide by impairing growth, yield, and fiber quality. Salt stress disrupts key morphological, physiological, and biochemical processes in cotton plants, leading to considerable reductions in productivity. Therefore, identifying salt-tolerant cotton genotypes is essential for improving crop performance in saline environments.MethodsIn this study, fifty-one cotton genotypes were evaluated for their response to salinity stress at the seedling stage. Plants were grown in hydroponic culture under controlled glasshouse conditions and subjected to 200 mM NaCl to simulate salt stress. The experiment followed a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications, and data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and multivariate approaches, including principal component analysis (PCA), heatmap analysis, and the multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI).ResultsANOVA showed significant variation among genotypes for all traits. Salt stress caused significant reductions in growth traits, including shoot and root length, fresh and dry biomass, water relation traits, gaseous exchange traits and photosynthetic pigments. In contrast, excised leaf water loss (ELWL), sodium (Na+ )accumulation in roots and shoots, oxidative stress markers like hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA), osmolytes including proline, glycine betaine (GB), and saponin, and antioxidant enzyme activities like superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT) increased, while potassium contents (K+) and sodium to potassium ratio (K⁺/Na+) decreased. Under control conditions, PCA showed little variation, whereas under salt stress, it explained 64.8% of the variance and separated growth- from stress-related traits. Heatmap analysis confirmed these patterns and grouped genotypes into three clusters based on ion homeostasis and oxidative stress traits. MGIDI index integrated all traits into a single score and identified superior genotypes like G2 (NIAB-868), G22 (NIA-Noori), G32 (FH-530), G3 (NIAB-878-B), G49 (FH-911), G28 (FH-416), G33 (FH-534), and G39 (FH-546).ConclusionThese findings suggest that multivariate and multi-trait screening at the seedling stage is a useful method for identifying cotton germplasm with salt tolerance, providing a foundation for breeding programs and further field evaluation that may contribute to stable yields under saline conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07354-4.

Background

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is a crucial global cash crop and the leading natural fiber for the textile industry, supporting the livelihoods of millions of farmers worldwide. Its economic importance is especially significant in arid and semi-arid regions, where it serves as a vital source of income [1, 2]. However, increasing soil salinity directly reduces cotton yield and fiber quality, posing a serious threat to both the productivity and sustainability of cotton farming. During the past 30 years, global soil salinization has intensified, with remote sensing revealing an increase in the world’s saline land from 915.5 million hectares in 1986 to 1,069.3 million hectares in 2016 [3, 4]. In major cotton-producing regions, salinity currently affects up to 17% of irrigated lands in India, nearly 30% in Pakistan [5], and exactly 37.72% of cotton-growing areas in China [6].The problem is expected to worsen with ongoing climate change, water shortages, and poor irrigation practices [7], all of which contribute to rising salt levels in the soil surrounding plant roots [8].

Salt stress causes immediate osmotic and ionic imbalances in cotton plants, leading to reduced water and nutrient uptake. These primary stresses inhibit root elongation, limit shoot growth, and trigger stomatal closure to prevent water loss [9]. As a result, photosynthetic efficiency declines, metabolic processes are disrupted, and overall plant vigor is compromised [10]. Prolonged or severe exposure can cause leaf necrosis, decreased fiber quality, and substantial yield loss. In addition to these physiological effects, salinity also induces significant biochemical and molecular responses [11]. Cotton plants attempt to minimize damage by limiting uptake and transport of toxic ions such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻, sequestering excess salts into vacuoles, regulating proton gradients, and adapting tissue structure

[12]. Osmotic adjustment is achieved through the accumulation of compatible solutes like proline and glycine betaine, which help retain water and preserve cell turgor [13, 14]. Salt stress also triggers a surge in reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide and superoxide, which damage membranes, proteins, and DNA [15]. To protect cells from oxidative injury, cotton activates antioxidant defense systems comprising enzymatic antioxidants such as SOD, CAT, and POD, as well as non-enzymatic antioxidants like ascorbate and phenolics [16]. Therefore, identifying cotton genotypes with efficient antioxidant systems, effective osmotic adjustment, enhanced nutrient regulation, and sustained growth is essential for breeding cultivars capable of thriving in salt-affected environments [9, 17].

Among the available strategies for sustainable agriculture on saline soils, developing and selecting salt-tolerant varieties is recognized as the most practical and long-term solution [17]. Regular screening for salt tolerance forms the foundation of breeding resilient cultivars and maintaining stable productivity in affected regions [10, 18]. While field-based screening provides valuable information, it is frequently constrained by environmental variability and high resource demands [19]. In contrast, controlled hydroponic screening at the seedling stage enables precise and reproducible evaluation of genotypic responses to salinity, making it widely adopted for early-stage selection in breeding programs [20, 21]. However, relatively few studies have used hydroponic systems to assess genotypic responses to salinity in cotton [21–23].

To extract more meaningful insights from salt stress screening, recent studies have increasingly employed multivariate tools such as PCA and MGIDI to evaluate crop responses [24, 25]. PCA summarizes variation and relationships among morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits, helping to identify the most informative variables. MGIDI integrates these multiple trait performances into a single index, accounting for correlations among traits and reducing the impact of multicollinearity. This approach enables objective ranking of genotypes, with top performers typically identified based on the lowest MGIDI values under defined selection intensity [24].

In this study, 51 upland cotton genotypes were evaluated at the seedling stage under hydroponic salt stress. PCA identified key morpho-physiological and biochemical traits associated with tolerance, while the MGIDI ranked genotypes by overall performance. This integrative approach enabled the identification of promising genotypes and clarified important trait associations, particularly those involving ion homeostasis, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant activity. These findings suggest practical approaches for breeding cotton varieties with enhanced salt tolerance.

Materials and methods

Plant material, experimental design, and growth conditions

The experiment was conducted on February 28, 2024, in a controlled glasshouse at the Plant Breeding and Genetics Division (PBGD), Nuclear Institute for Agriculture and Biology (NIAB), Pakistan. Fifty-one upland cotton genotypes were obtained from various national research institutes (Table S1). A two-factor factorial arrangement was used under a Completely Randomized Design (CRD), with three biological replications per treatment (Fig. S1 and S2). Temperature in the glasshouse was regulated using a programmable ASCON inverter air conditioning unit (2 Ton, 24000-BTU) and Maintained at 28± 2 °C during the day and 20± 2 °C at night. Relative humidity was kept within the range of 60–65%, as monitored by a digital temperature and humidity meter (HTC-1). A 16-hour photoperiod was provided using supplemental LED lighting. Before sowing, seeds were disinfected with 10% sodium hypochlorite for five minutes, rinsed with distilled water, and sown 3 cm deep in moist sand-filled bags. After seven days of post-germination, uniformly grown seedlings were transferred to a hydroponic setup supported with cotton wool. Containers were filled with tap water (EC = 0.8 dS/m−¹), and modified Hoagland’s nutrient solution was applied as per the standard composition (Table S2). The nutrient solution was replaced every five days, and the pH (5.8 ± 0.5) was adjusted daily using a pH meter (Model ST300, OHAUS Corporation, USA).

Salinity stress application

Salt stress was applied after the emergence of the fourth true leaf. A 200 mM NaCl solution was added gradually over four days to minimize osmotic shock. Two treatment groups were maintained: a control group (no NaCl) and a stressed group receiving 200 mM NaCl. The final concentration of 200 mM NaCl in the hydroponic solution was verified by measuring electrical conductivity (EC) on a daily basis using a calibrated conductivity meter (Model ST300C, OHAUS Corporation, USA). Salt stress conditions were maintained for 28 days.

Growth parameter measurement

After 28 days, the length of shoots and roots was recorded from base to apex using a standard ruler. Fresh weight was recorded immediately after harvesting by weighing the separated plant parts with a digital scale. For dry weight, samples were dried in a n oven at 80 °C for 72 h and then reweighed individually.

Physiological traits

Photosynthetic parameters, including net photosynthesis (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), and stomatal conductance (gs), were assessed using a CI-340 portable infrared gas analyzer (Analytical Development Co., UK). Data was collected from fully expanded upper leaves between 9:00 AM and 1:00 PM under uniform light conditions. Water use efficiency (WUE) was calculated as the ratio of the photosynthetic rate to the transpiration rate.

Water relation

Leaf water potential (WP) was evaluated using a pressure chamber (Arimad 2, Germany), while osmotic potential (OP) was measured with a vapor pressure osmometer (Wescor 5500, USA) using leaf tissue previously stored at −30 °C prior to analysis. Both instruments were calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before each measurement session. For each genotype and treatment, measurements were taken from three fully expanded upper leaves per plant, with three biological replicates per group. Turgor pressure (TP) was estimated by subtracting water potential from osmotic potential.

ELWL was measured using a modified protocol based on McCaig and Romagosa (1989) and Clarke and Townley-Smith (1986) [26, 27]. Freshly excised leaves were immediately weighed to obtain fresh weight (FW). The samples were then placed in a drying oven at 28 °C for 6 h, and the partially dried weight (W₆ₕ) was recorded. Afterwards, leaves were dried at 70 °C for 24 h to determine the dry weight (DW).

ELWL was calculated as follows:

ELWL (%) = [(Fresh Weight - Weight after 6 h)/(Fresh Weight - Dry Weight)] × 100.

Relative water content (RWC) was determined following a modified version of Barrs and Weatherley (1962) [28]. Fresh leaves were weighed immediately after excision (FW), then immersed in distilled water at room temperature for 4 h to achieve full turgidity. After gently blotting excess water, the turgid weight (TW) was recorded. Leaves were then dried at 70 °C for 24 h to obtain the dry weight (DW). RWC was calculated as:

RWC (%) = [(Fresh Weight - Dry Weight)/(Turgor Weight - Dry Weight)] × 100.

Quantification of photosynthetic pigments

The contents of chlorophyll a (Chl a), chlorophyll b (Chl b), total chlorophyll (Total Chl), and carotenoids (Car) were estimated following the procedure described by Arnon, (1949) [27]. A 0.5 g portion of fresh leaf tissue was finely cut and soaked in 80% acetone. Samples were left overnight at room temperature in complete darkness to ensure full pigment extraction. Absorbance was measured at 663 nm, 645 nm, and 470 nm using a Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea) and pigment concentrations were calculated using established formulae.

Chl a = [12.7 (OD 663) −2.69 (OD 645)] x V/1000x W.

Chl b = [22.9 (OD 645) −4.68 (OD 663)] x V/1000x W.

Total Chl (Chla + b) = [20.2 (OD 645) + 8.02 (OD 663)] xV/100x W.

Where V represents the volume of the respective sample and W is denoting the weight of the sample.

Ionic content analysis

A small quantity (0.01 g) of dried, finely powdered plant material was subjected to wet digestion using a mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide. After complete digestion, the solution was diluted with distilled water, filtered to remove residues, and analyzed for sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺) content. Ion concentrations were determined using a calibrated flame emission spectrometer (Jenway PFP7, UK), in accordance with standard procedures [29].

Biochemical traits

Antioxidant enzymes quantification

Leaf extract was obtained by homogenizing the tissue in phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) containing 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) [30]. SOD activity was determined using the method described by Giannopolitis and Ries (1977), based on the enzyme’s ability to inhibit the photochemical reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) [31]. Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm at Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea) and one unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause 50% inhibition of NBT reduction.

CAT activity was quantified by measuring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide in a reaction mixture containing phosphate buffer and enzyme extract. Absorbance was recorded at 240 nm at 20-s intervals using a Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea). One unit of CAT activity was defined as a decrease in absorbance of 0.01 units per minute [32].

POD activity was quantified using a 3-mL reaction mixture containing 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 5.0), 20 mM guaiacol, 40 mM hydrogen peroxide, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm was recorded at 20-s intervals using a Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea). One unit of POD activity was defined asan increase in absorbance of 0.01 units per minute [32].

Oxidative markers

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content was estimated according to a reported method [33]. The reaction mixture was read at 390 nm using a Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea). Concentrations were determined from a standard curve prepared with serial dilutions of H₂O₂ in distilled water. Lipid peroxidation was evaluated using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method of Dhindsa et al. (1981) [34]. Absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 and 600 nm.

Proline, Glycine betaine and saponin

Proline content was estimated following the method of Bates et al. (1973) [35]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid and filtered through Whatman No. 2 paper. Two milliliters of the filtrate were mixed with 2 mL acid ninhydrin reagent (1.25 g ninhydrin in 20 mL 6 M orthophosphoric acid and 30 mL glacial acetic acid) and 2 mL glacial acetic acid. The mixture was incubated at 100 °C for 1 h, cooled, and extracted with 10 mL toluene. The toluene phase was collected, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm using a Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea), with toluene as the blank.

Glycine betaine (GB) levels were assessed using the procedure described by Akram and Ashraf, (2011) [36]. Briefly, 0.1 g of fresh tissue was homogenized in 5 mL distilled water and filtered. In brief, 0.1 g of fresh tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of distilled water and filtered from the filtrate, 1 mL was mixed with an equal volume of 2 N sulfuric acid. Then, 0.5 mL of this acidified extract was reacted with 0.2 mL of potassium tri-iodide solution and incubated on ice for 90 min. Afterwards, 2.8 mL of chilled distilled water was added, and 1.5 mL of this solution was extracted with 2 mL of cold 1,2-dichloroethane. Absorbance of the lower organic phase was recorded at 365 nm to quantify glycine betaine on Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea).

Saponin content was measured by extracting 0.1 g of finely ground plant tissue with a 1:1 ethanol and distilled water solution. A 0.5 mL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 5 mL of 72% sulfuric acid and 0.5 mL of 10% vanillin, kept on ice, and then incubated at 60 °C for 10 min [37]. Absorbance was recorded at 535 nm on Nano-drop Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN Nano Q Plus, Republic of Korea).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all measured traits. Two-way ANOVA was performed in Statistix 8.1 to assess the effects of salt stress, genotype, and their interaction. Statistical significance was tested at the 0.05 probability level. Violin plots with embedded box plots were generated in R using the ggplot2 package to visualize trait distributions and treatment effects. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships among morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits in R by using the package corrplot. PCA was adopted to identify the main contributors to phenotypic variation and to simplify trait dimensions by using R in factoextra. A heatmap with hierarchical clustering was generated using the pheatmap package in R. Genotypic performance across multiple traits was assessed using the MGIDI, implemented package in R via metan package, applying a 15% selection intensity to identify the most desirable genotypes. From the MGIDI output, a strengths and weaknesses view was constructed.

Results

Growth-related traits

There were significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences in fresh shoot length (FSL), fresh shoot weight (FSW), dry shoot weight (DSW), fresh root length (FRL), fresh root weight (FRW), and dry root weight (DRW) among genotypes, salt stress treatments, and their interaction (SS × G) (Table S3). Salt stress significantly inhibited seedling growth across all cotton genotypes. FSL declined by 39.8% under salt stress compared with the control. The maximum and minimum FSL values under salt stress were recorded in FH-416 (43 cm) and USG-2794 (16 cm), respectively (Fig. 1a and Table S4 and S5). FSW And DSW decreased by 47.3%and 46.6%, respectively (Fig. 1b and c, Table S4). The highest FSW was recorded in FH-546 (11.9 g), while CIM-610 showed the lowest (2.9 g) as compared to the control. DSW Ranged from 4.09 g in BRL-2 (Mac-7 × MLR) to 0.24 g in CIM-632 under salt stress conditions (Tables S4 and S5). Root growth (FRL, FRW, DRW) decreased by 40–45% under salt stress (Fig. 1d, e and f and Table S4). Under salt stress, FH-546 consistently showed the highest FRL (38 cm) and FRW (5.35 g), with minimum values recorded in SS-32 (FRL, 12 cm) and Malmal (FRW, 0.78 g). For DRW, MNH-886 recorded the highest value (1.03 g), while NIAB-1059 had the lowest (0.17 g) (Table S4 and S5).

Physiological traits

Significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences in RWC, ELWL, Pn, Tr, gs, and WUE were found among genotypes, salt stress treatments, or their interaction (SS × G) (Table S3). Salt stress caused marked reductions in RWC (43.3%), Pn (44.3%), gs (42.6%), Tr (38.6%), and WUE (10.5%), while ELWL increased by 54.9% relative to control conditions (Fig. 2 and Table S4). Among the genotypes, IR-NIBGE-11 consistently exhibited the highest RWC (75.02%) and Pn (20.25 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) under salt stress, whereas USG-2794 and CRIS-580 recorded the lowest values for RWC (32.38%) and Pn (3.25 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), respectively. ELWL increased in FH-911, which exhibited the highest value (1.94%), whereas IR-NIBGE-11 maintained the lowest ELWL (0.63%) under salt stress. The highest gs (0.84 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹) was observed in BRL-2 (Mac-7 × MLR), whereas USG-2794 exhibited a markedly reduced value (0.13 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹). FH-416 exhibited the highest Tr (5.99 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹), while NIAB-1987 showed a substantially lower value (3.33 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹) under salt stress. WUE was highest in SS-32 (3.38 mmol CO₂ mol⁻¹ H₂O) and lowest in NIAB-777 (0.98 mmol CO₂ mol⁻¹ H₂O) under salt stress (Table S4 and S5).

Water relation parameters

OP, WP, and TP showed significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences across genotypes, treatments, and their interaction (SS × G) (Table S3). The stress-induced decline in OP (42.9%), WP (41.9%), and TP (45.5%) further reflected impaired water relations and cellular dehydration (Fig. 2 and Table S4). Maximum OP under salt stress was observed in IR-NIBGE-11 (1.59 -MPa) and the minimum in USG-2794 (0.68 -MPa). WP reached its highest in IR-NIBGE-11 (1.18 -MPa) and lowest in USG-2794 (0.54 -MPa). The highest TP was found in FH-417 (0.41 -MPa) and the lowest in NIAB-777 (0.13 -MPa) under salt stress (Tables S4 and S5).

Photosynthetic pigments

There were significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences in Chl a, Chl b, Total Chl, and Car contents among genotypes, salt stress treatments, and their interaction (SS × G) (Table S3). Salinity caused a noticeable reduction in photosynthetic pigments. Specifically, Chl a, Chl b, Total Chl, and Car content declined by 42.7%, 44.9%, 43.4%, and 45.8%, respectively (Fig. 3 and Table S4). Among the tested genotypes, CIM-448 recorded the highest Chl a concentration (1.33 mg g⁻¹ FW), while AH-67 (M) showed the lowest (0.70 mg g⁻¹ FW). Chl b was also highest in CIM-448 (0.64 mg g⁻¹ FW) and lowest in AH-67 (M) (0.30 mg g⁻¹ FW). Total Chl content peaked in SS-32 (1.97 mg g⁻¹ FW), with NIAB-777 displaying the lowest level (1.00 mg g⁻¹ FW). Car levels followed a similar trend, Ranging from 0.38 mg g⁻¹ FW in CIM-448 to 0.17 mg g⁻¹ FW in FH-513 (Table S4 and S5).

Ionic parameters

Significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences in root and shoot sodium and potassium concentrations, as well as K⁺/Na⁺ ratios, were observed among genotypes, salt stress treatments, and their interaction (SS × G) (Table S3). Salt stress led to marked shifts in ion accumulation, notably increasing Na+ content while reducing potassium levels and K⁺/Na⁺ ratios in both root and shoots (Fig. 4). The root Na+ content was highest in CIM-482 (30.33 mg g⁻¹ DW) and lowest in IR-NIBGE-11 (9.32 mg g⁻¹ DW). Shoot Na+ contents reached a maximum in VH-351 (49.63 mg g⁻¹ DW) and a minimum in FH-622 (19.63 mg g⁻¹ DW). K+ concentration in roots was highest in FH-613 (20.15 mg g⁻¹ DW) and lowest in VH-300 (2.93 mg g⁻¹ DW). In shoots, the maximum K+ level was recorded in NIAB-135-BB-69 (32.61 mg g⁻¹ DW), while the lowest was observed in USG-2794 (8.02 mg g⁻¹ DW). These alterations culminated in marked reductions in the K⁺/Na⁺ ratios (Fig. 4c and f). The highest root K⁺/Na⁺ ratio under salt stress was found in SS-32 (0.66%), and the lowest in NIAB-777 (0.31%). For shoots, the maximum K⁺/Na⁺ ratio was also observed in SS-32 (0.66%), while NIAB-777 had the lowest (0.41%) (Table S4 and S5).

Antioxidant enzymes and lipid peroxidation levels

There were significant (p ≤ 0.05) effects of salt stress, genotype, and their interaction (SS × G) on SOD, POD, CAT, H2O2, and MDA levels (Table S3). To counteract oxidative stress, the activities of antioxidant enzymes were enhanced. Under salt stress, the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT, as well as the contents of H₂O₂ and MDA, increased by 41.3%, 42.7%, 40.9%, 45.99%, and 47.58%, respectively (Fig. 5a, b, c, d and e and Table S4). However, certain genotypes were able to maintain lower H₂O₂ and MDA levels under salt stress, likely due to enhanced activities of SOD, POD, and CAT (Table S5). For example, the highest SOD activity was found in NIA-Noori (159.34 U g⁻¹ FW), and the minimum in AH-67 (M) (115.45 U g⁻¹ FW). Likewise, maximum POD activity was found in NIA-Noori (86.81 U g⁻¹ FW), and minimum in NIAB-1059 (54.51 U g⁻¹ FW). For CAT activity, NIA-Noori exhibited the highest value (303.81 U g⁻¹ FW), whereas Malmal showed the lowest (224.56 U g⁻¹ FW) (Table S4 and S5). Oxidative damage was elevated under salt stress, as indicated by increased H₂O₂ and MDA levels (Fig. 5d and e). Among the genotypes, USG-2794 exhibited the highest H₂O₂ content (1.85 µmol g⁻¹ FW), while CIM-448 had the minimal value (1.19 µmol g⁻¹ FW). Similarly, MDA concentrations were greatest in NIAB-1987 (28.31 nmol g⁻¹ FW) and least in NIAB-878-B (18.27 nmol g⁻¹ FW) (Table S4 and S5).

Proline, Glycine betaine and saponin

A significant (p ≤ 0.05) effect of salinity was observed for proline, GB, and saponin contents (Table S3). All three osmoprotective compounds accumulated under salt stress, with proline, GB, and saponin (Fig. 5f and g, and 5h) increasing by approximately 42%, 64.1%, And 42.25%, respectively (Table S4). NIA-Noori consistently exhibited the highest concentrations of proline (56.76 µg g⁻¹ FW), GB (23.49 µg g⁻¹ FW), and saponin (4.59 µg g⁻¹ FW) under salt stress, while the lowest values were recorded in Malmal for proline (36.70 µg g⁻¹ FW), CRIS-580 for GB (13 µg g⁻¹ FW), and NIAB-1987 for saponin (2.82 µg g⁻¹ FW) (Table S4 and S5).

Correlation analysis

Under control conditions, cotton genotypes displayed mostly moderate and positive correlations between traits, reflecting a stable and coordinated physiological state (Fig. 6a). For example, DSW was strongly correlated with FRW (r = 0.723), suggesting balanced shoot and root development. Shoot K⁺ also had a strong positive correlation with Shoot K⁺/Na⁺ (r = 0.902), and WP was closely related with OP (r = 0.871), indicating efficient water regulation. Photosynthetic traits followed a similar pattern, with Chl a strongly correlated with Total Chl (r = 0.912). A moderate correlation between FRW and FRL (r = 0.533) was observed (Fig. 6a and Table S6).

Under salt stress, trait relationships became more polarized. While some positive correlations strengthened, others shifted sharply in the negative direction (Fig. 6b). Morphological traits like FRW maintained strong positive relationships with DSW (r = 0.704), FSW (r = 0.530), and FRL (r = 0.539), indicating continued coordination in structural development. Ion homeostasis emerged as a critical factor, with shoot K⁺/Na⁺ showing positive correlations with Chl a (r = 0.692), Chl b (r = 0.548), and Total Chl (r = 0.664). WP and OP remained closely aligned (r = 0.919), suggesting stable osmotic adjustment under stress. Antioxidant enzyme activity was also tightly coordinated, with CAT correlating with POD (r = 0.951) and SOD (r = 0.939), indicating a unified oxidative stress response (Table S7). At the same time, salt stress triggered several strong negative correlations. Shoot Na⁺ was negatively correlated with both Shoot K⁺ (r = −0.403) and Shoot K⁺/Na⁺ (r = −0.868), reflecting significant ion imbalance. Root Na⁺ showed similar negative relationships with Root K⁺/Na⁺ (r = −0.784) and Shoot K⁺/Na⁺ (r = −0.788). H₂O₂ negatively correlated with Chl a (r = −0.703), pointing to oxidative damage affecting photosynthetic pigments. In terms of osmolyte accumulation, proline showed strong positive correlations with both GB (r = 0.940) and saponin (r = 0.973), while GB and saponin were also closely linked (r = 0.948). These relationships suggest a coordinated role of osmoprotectants in mitigating salt-induced cellular stress (Fig. 6b and Table S7).

MGIDI index analysis

The MGIDI method was employed to evaluate and rank cotton genotypes under salt stress, integrating a set of morpho-physiological and biochemical traits (Fig. 10). This multi-trait index enabled the identification of genotypes exhibiting superior overall performance while accounting for both trait directionality and potential for genetic improvement. Genotypes such as G2 (NIAB-868), G22 (NIA-Noori), G32 (FH-530), G3 (NIAB-878-B), G49 (FH-911), G28 (FH-416), G33 (FH-534), and G39 (FH-546) were ranked among the top performers (Fig. 10). These genotypes displayed balanced profiles across key adaptive traits, including reduced Na+ accumulation, higher K+/Na+ ratios, improved shoot and root biomass, and elevated antioxidant enzyme activity such as SOD, POD, and CAT. These characteristics are critical for mitigating salt-induced oxidative stress. Notably, the evaluated traits exhibited high heritability values (h² approximately 0.92 to 1.00) (Table S8), suggesting a strong genetic contribution and limited environmental influence under the tested conditions. In contrast, several low-ranked genotypes showed poor performance across these same traits. These lines were often characterized by excessive sodium levels, poor biomass development, and diminished antioxidant responses. These patterns were clearly visualized in the MGIDI ranking curve, where such genotypes remained outside the selection threshold and displayed considerable distance from the ideal genotype.

Discussion

Salinity tolerance is a highly complex quantitative trait that exhibits substantial genotypic variation, even among closely related lines within a species [38]. At the seedling stage, the capacity to withstand salt stress often determines successful plant establishment and later performance [39, 40]. Hydroponic culture under controlled glasshouse conditions offers an effective and precise platform for evaluating salt tolerance, as this method accentuates intrinsic differences among genotypes while minimizing environmental confounders [41].

To assess cotton’s growth responses to salinity, we measured shoot and root biomass under hydroponic conditions. Salinity exposure caused marked reductions in both shoot and root growth across all genotypes (Fig. 1 and Table S4 and S5). These results are in agreement with previous studies showing that salinity lowers biomass accumulation in cotton [42] and that rising salt concentrations, particularly from NaCl treatments, impair shoot elongation and overall plant growth [43, 44]. Although all genotypes were affected, FH-546, BRL-2 (MAC-7×MLR) and MNH-886 maintained higher biomass under stress, whereas NIAB-1059, Malmal and CIM-610 showed more severe reductions (Fig. 1 and Table S5). This variation can be attributed to differences in osmotic adjustment, ion regulation, and antioxidant capacity, mechanisms associated with salt tolerance in cotton and other crops [45, 46]. The inhibition of photosynthetic activity under salt stress likely contributed to reduced dry matter accumulation [47, 48]. FSL was particularly affected, with genotypes like FH-416 showing less reduction, suggesting lower sensitivity to ionic toxicity and osmotic imbalance as compared to others. Similarly, FH-546 maintained higher FRL under salt stress compared to SS-32 (Fig. 1; Table S4 and S5), suggesting better tolerance to salinity-induced osmotic stress. Salinity initially limits water absorption by the roots, leading to cellular dehydration and ultimately hindering plant growth [49].

Salinity stress substantially impairs water relations in cotton, and this effect was most pronounced in Malmal and USG-2794, which showed larger declines in RWC, WP, OP, and TP compared to IR-NIBGE-11 and FH-417 (Fig. 2 and Table S4 and S5). These reductions indicate a disruption of plant-water balance driven by the accumulation of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in the root zone. This ionic buildup increases the osmotic potential of the soil solution, creating a steeper gradient that pulls water out of plant cells, resulting in cellular dehydration and loss of turgor. Such stress inhibits cell expansion and overall growth [45, 50]. Salinity also caused marked decreases in Tr and increased ELWL in NIAB-1987 and FH-911(Fig. 2 and Table S4 and S5). This reflects the limited ability of these genotypes to conserve water under saline conditions, likely due to both compromised osmotic adjustment and altered water transport within the leaf tissues [51]. As transpiration becomes restricted, tolerant genotypes rely more on cell-to-cell water movement facilitated by aquaporins. Upregulation of aquaporin gene families, especially plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs), has been associated with improved water retention under salt stress [52–54].

Additionally, WUE varied among genotypes; SS-32 maintained or even improved WUE under salinity (Fig. 2 and Table S4 and S5). This increased WUE reflects their ability to sustain photosynthetic CO₂ assimilation relative to transpirational water loss, which is made possible by more effective stomatal regulation and maintenance of leaf water status. Thus, higher WUE in tolerant genotypes is a direct outcome of coordinated physiological and molecular adjustments that optimize the balance between carbon gain and water conservation during salt stress [55, 56]. In this study, marked decreases in Chl a, Chl b, Total Chl, and Car (Fig. 3) were recorded in AH-67(M) and NIAB-777 (Table S4 and S5). In contrast, genotypes like CIM-448 and SS-32, maintained greater pigment levels under similar conditions (Table S5). This superior pigment retention may be attributed to greater chloroplast membrane stability, reduced activity of degradative enzymes, and improved protection of the protein-pigment-lipid complex in tolerant lines [57, 58]. Furthermore, more effective osmotic adjustment, improved ion homeostasis, and a strengthened antioxidant defense system likely contributed to the preservation of chloroplast ultrastructure and the maintenance of pigment biosynthesis under salt stress [59, 60]. These differences in pigment stability and chloroplast integrity were closely linked to physiological performance [58].

Gas exchange parameters, including gs and Pn (Fig. 2) decreased more sharply in NIAB-1987 and USG-2794, respectively (Table S5), likely due to partial stomatal closure to conserve water and structural damage to chloroplasts caused by excess sodium and chloride ions [10]. IR-NIBGE-11 and BRL-2 (MAC-7×MLR) maintained higher Pn and gs under salt stress, respectively (Table S5), reflecting their ability to regulate stomatal function and protect the photosynthetic apparatus [61]. In cotton, salt tolerance is strongly linked to the regulation of sodium, potassium, and calcium ions. A high K⁺/Na⁺ and Ca²⁺/Na⁺ ratio is commonly regarded as a reliable marker of salinity tolerance [62, 63]. Salt stress disrupts this balance by promoting the influx of Na⁺, which can displace K⁺ and Ca²⁺ from plant tissues, ultimately leading to growth inhibition [64, 65]. In our study, SS-32 maintained higher K⁺/Na⁺ ratios in shoots and roots as compared to NIAB-777 (Fig. 4 and Table S5), a pattern consistent with earlier findings [66, 67]. This enhanced ion homeostasis in tolerant genotypes may be attributed to more efficient Na⁺ efflux and K⁺ and Ca²⁺ retention, which help mitigate osmotic and ionic stress [68]. Molecular and transcriptomic studies further support these physiological observations. For instance, Peng et al. (2016) and Balasubramaniam et al. (2023) reported that under salt stress, tolerant cotton genotypes show increased expression of key genes in the salt overly sensitive (SOS) pathway, including GhSOS1, GhSOS2, and GhCBL10. These genes regulate Na⁺ export from the cytoplasm and contribute to K⁺ and Ca²⁺ homeostasis [69, 70]. Transcriptome analyses have also identified differential expression of vacuolar membrane transporters like GhNHX1 and GhCAX1, which are crucial for sequestering excess ions and protecting cells from toxicity [71].

Salinity commonly disturbs the balance between the generation and detoxification of ROS, resulting in oxidative stress [72]. In the present study, cotton seedlings exposed to saline conditions exhibited increased concentrations of H₂O₂ and MDA (Fig. 5), with the most pronounced elevations observed in USG-2794 and NIAB-1987. In contrast, CIM-448 and NIAB-878-B effectively maintained lower levels of H₂O₂ and MDA (Table S5). An increase in MDA content indicates elevated lipid peroxidation and loss of membrane stability, which are key indicators of oxidative stress under saline conditions [73]. This observation aligns with previous studies reporting that rising NaCl concentrations intensify ROS production and elevate MDA levels in various plant species [74, 75].

To mitigate oxidative stress, plants activate antioxidant defense systems. In the present study, NIA-Noori exhibited significantly higher enzymatic activities of SOD, POD, and CAT (Fig. 5) compared to other genotypes (Table S4 and S5). SOD plays a critical role by converting superoxide radicals into molecular oxygen and hydrogen peroxide, forming the initial barrier against oxidative damage [69, 76]. POD assists in neutralizing hydrogen peroxide by converting it to water, while catalase efficiently removes excess H₂O₂, thereby reducing cellular injury [77, 78]. Previous studies have demonstrated that salt-tolerant cultivars consistently exhibit greater induction of these antioxidant enzymes under stress, highlighting their pivotal role in protecting against oxidative damage [79–81]. Moreover, research indicates that enhanced antioxidant capacity is often associated with upregulation of genes encoding these enzymes, further strengthening cellular defence [77, 82].

Salinity stress often triggers significant changes in the pool of secondary metabolites such as polyphenolics, flavonoids, saponins, anthocyanins, and tannins. Literature reports an increase in these compounds under stress conditions [83]. For saponins specifically, enhanced accumulation has been observed in several species, such as Acalypha wilkesiana, under salt stress [84]. In this study, NIA-Noori exhibited greater saponin accumulation under salt stress relative to genotypes like NIAB-1987 (Table S5). These results support the idea that salt-induced changes in secondary metabolites, including saponins, are part of the adaptive response and may contribute to improved stress tolerance. The degree of these alterations can differ according to plant species, developmental stage, and the severity of stress exposure. Further investigation is essential to unravel the metabolomic mechanisms involved [85, 86].

The buildup of compatible solutes like proline and GB represents another key strategy that plants use to adapt to saline conditions. These osmoprotectants facilitate intracellular osmotic adjustment, support ROS detoxification, and help preserve membrane structure and cellular metabolism [87, 88]. In our study, NIA-Noori accumulated significantly higher levels of proline and GB under salt stress (Fig. 5) compared to Malmal and CRIS-580, respectively (Table S5). GB aids osmotic adjustment, particularly in the chloroplast thylakoid membrane, thereby helping to maintain photosynthetic efficiency [89]. Proline acts as both a compatible osmolyte and antioxidant, supporting turgor, stabilizing proteins and membranes, and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity [10, 90].

Comparative analyses, including PCA, hierarchical cluster analysis, and correlation analysis, were performed to dissect the complex interactions among morpho-physiological and biochemical traits under salt stress (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). PCA provided a holistic view of variation, with the biplot distinctly separating trait combinations associated with growth maintenance from those linked with stress-induced damage [91]. In this study, traits such as shoot length, dry weight, K⁺ content, and antioxidant enzyme activities clustered together (Fig. 8b), highlighting their central role in mitigating salinity-induced oxidative damage and supporting plant growth [9, 92]. In contrast, parameters such as root Na⁺, shoot Na⁺, and the Na⁺/K⁺ ratio grouped in the opposite direction (Fig. 8b), consistent with previous reports that excessive Na⁺ and Cl⁻ disrupt ion homeostasis and promote oxidative stress [93].

In addition to PCA, hierarchical cluster heatmap analysis was performed to investigate the grouping patterns of cotton genotypes and their associated traits under salt stress (Fig. 9b). Under salt stress, genotypes such as IR-NIBGE-11, FH-416, NIA-Noori, and FH-622 grouped with higher biomass, chlorophyll, antioxidants, and potassium, while CRIS-580, MALMAL, USG-2794, and FH-911 clustered with higher Na⁺, MDA, H₂O₂, and Na⁺/K⁺ ratios (Fig. 9b). Similar clustering patterns have been reported in previous studies; for example, Zhang et al. (2014) demonstrated that salt-tolerant cotton genotypes clustered with high K⁺ and antioxidant traits, whereas susceptible lines were associated with elevated Na⁺ and Na⁺/K⁺ ratios [94]. Likewise, An et al. (2025) found that clustering analysis effectively distinguished salt-tolerant and susceptible cotton varieties based on physiological and ionic characteristics, confirming that beneficial ion balance and stress mitigation traits are consistently associated with salt tolerance across diverse germplasm [71].

Correlation analysis further clarified these relationships: strong positive correlations were observed among K⁺, chlorophyll content, and antioxidant enzyme activity, indicating that efficient potassium retention is closely linked to improved photosynthetic performance and enhanced oxidative stress defense under saline conditions (Fig. 6b and Table S7). Previous research has demonstrated that elevated K⁺ levels help stabilize the photosynthetic apparatus and upregulate antioxidant systems, both of which are essential for plant survival in saline soils [60, 79, 95]. In our study, the shoot K⁺/Na⁺ ratio also showed a positive association with net photosynthetic rate (Fig. 6b and Table S7), supporting the idea that potassium preserves the structural and functional integrity of PSII and facilitates efficient electron transport during salt stress [96]. Significant positive associations among proline, GB, and saponin were also identified (Fig. 6b and Table S7), reflecting their coordinated role in osmotic adjustment by maintaining cell turgor and minimizing dehydration during salt exposure [97]. Conversely, strong negative correlations between Na⁺ and both K⁺ and K⁺/Na⁺ ratios (Fig. 6b) highlight the disruptive effect of sodium on ionic balance; excessive Na⁺ influx leads to potassium displacement from intracellular sites, impairs enzyme activity, interferes with nutrient transport, and ultimately reduces growth and photosynthetic efficiency [45]. Elevated Na⁺ accumulation is further known to induce ionic toxicity and disrupt metabolic processes, thereby exacerbating physiological stress in susceptible genotypes [68].

To integrate these complex interactions for practical breeding, the MGIDI index (Fig. 10) was employed as a multi-trait selection tool. By synthesizing the information from all morpho-physiological and biochemical traits, MGIDI enabled the ranking of cotton genotypes for salt tolerance based on their proximity to an ideal genotype profile [24, 98]. This approach identified G2 (NIAB-868), G22 (NIA-Noori), G32 (FH-530), G3 (NIAB-878-B), G28 (FH-416), and G39 (FH-546) as superior performers, reflecting their lower Na⁺ accumulation, higher K⁺/Na⁺ ratios, greater biomass, and stronger antioxidant capacity.

It can thus be concluded that the balance between beneficial factors (such as K⁺ and antioxidants) and detrimental factors (such as Na⁺ and MDA) is critical for salt tolerance in cotton. Genotypes with the greatest resilience are those that effectively restrict sodium accumulation and strengthen antioxidant defenses, resulting in improved physiological performance under saline conditions.

Conclusion

The current study investigated the impact of salinity stress on morphological, physiological, and biochemical responses in upland cotton genotypes. PCA revealed clear separation of growth- and stress-related traits under salinity, while heatmap analysis supported these patterns and grouped genotypes into three clusters based on ion homeostasis and oxidative stress traits. The MGIDI index further integrated all traits into a single score and identified superior genotypes, including G2 (NIAB-868), G22 (NIA-Noori), G32 (FH-530), G3 (NIAB-878-B), G49 (FH-911), G28 (FH-416), G33 (FH-534), and G39 (FH-546). These genotypes maintained higher biomass, better water relations, and stronger antioxidant defenses under stress, while susceptible lines exhibited greater growth inhibition and oxidative damage. The use of multivariate and multi-trait indices enabled the identification of genotypes with broader adaptive profiles, providing practical targets for cotton improvement in salt-affected environments. However, field validation remains essential, as controlled hydroponic conditions may not fully capture the complexity of natural environments. Future research should test these genotypes across diverse field conditions and unravel the genetic and molecular mechanisms driving observed tolerance.
