Comparison of femoral triangle plus iPACK blocks with femoral triangle block alone for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction: a randomized controlled clinical trial on postoperative pain and knee function
Authors: Fabrice Ferré, Lise Boussaguet, Nicolas Vari, Fabien Pillard, Laetitia Bosch, Anne Ferrier, Cyndie Ba, Bernard Tissot, Rémi Menut, Matt Kurrek, François Labaste, Etienne Cavaignac, Vincent Minville
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) can cause severe postoperative pain. However, consensus regarding the most effective regional analgesia is lacking. We hypothesized that, compared with femoral triangle block (FTB) and local infiltration analgesia, adding an iPACK block would decrease postoperative morphine consumption.MethodsPatients scheduled for ACLR under general anesthesia were randomly allocated to the FTB (n=45) or the FTB+iPACK group (n=45). The primary outcome was the cumulative oral morphine equivalent (OME) consumption during the first two postoperative days. Secondary outcomes were maximum pain scores, opioid adverse effects, and knee functional scores (Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome (KOOS), International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) and Lysholm) 3, 6, and 9 months after surgery.ResultsCompared with FTB, FTB+iPACK resulted in similar OME consumption (median (IQR)=50 (14–103) vs 60 (32–89) mg, respectively; median of the difference (95% CI): 5 (−14, 28) mg, p=0.49). No significant intergroup differences were found in terms of pain scores, opioid-related side effects, or functional knee recovery. Pain and symptoms subscales of KOOS and IKDC at 9 months were higher for patients with an OME consumption <50 mg within the first two postoperative days, but these statistical differences did not reach the minimal clinically important difference.ConclusionsiPACK block has no additional analgesic benefits for primary ACLR in the setting of a multimodal analgesia regimen including FTB and local infiltration analgesia.Trial registration number NCT05136352 Keywords: Pain, Postoperative; Nerve Block; Analgesics, Opioid

Introduction

Surgical anterior cruciate ligament reconstructions (ACLRs) are currently performed under minimally invasive arthroscopic technique, enabling the ambulatory management of patients.1 However, moderate to severe acute pain is commonly observed during the first postoperative days2 3 which can compromise early rehabilitation and patient satisfaction4 and consequently delay functional recovery of the knee.

Regional analgesic techniques, including peripheral nerve blocks and local anesthesia infiltration, may have a role in the postoperative pain management as part of a multimodal analgesic regimen.5–8 Balance between analgesic efficacy and early ambulation could guide the choice of the regional analgesia technique best suited to the patient and his environment.9 In this context, evidence-based recommendations advocate for the use of proximal adductor canal block (also referred to as femoral triangle block, FTB) in ACLR, as this regional analgesia technique provides postoperative pain relief equivalent to that of femoral nerve block while preserving quadriceps motor strength.10–13 Ultrasound-guided infiltration of local anesthetic within the interspace between the popliteal artery and the capsule of the knee, also known as iPACK block, has been proposed to enhance rehabilitation after total knee arthroplasty surgeries by providing postoperative analgesia while preserving sciatic nerve motor function.14 Indeed, by involving the articular branches of the tibial, common peroneal and obturator nerves, this regional analgesia of the popliteal plexus contributes to anesthetizing the posterior capsule of the knee joint.15 Despite advances in our understanding of knee innervation, consensus regarding the most effective regional analgesia techniques for ACLR is still lacking.16 17 The main objective of our study was to evaluate the postoperative analgesic benefits of an added iPACK block when compared with FTB alone for patients scheduled for primary ACLR under general anesthesia. The secondary objectives were to compare the opioid adverse effects and the quality of functional knee recovery between FTB plus iPACK and FTB groups. We made the assumption that the regional analgesia strategy combining FTB and iPACK block provided better postoperative analgesia than FTB alone in the setting of a standardized multimodal postoperative analgesic regimen. We also hypothesized that late functional scores of postoperative knee recovery could be higher for patients with lower acute postoperative pain.

Methods

Design of the study

This interventional, single-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted at the University Teaching Hospital of Purpan in Toulouse, France. The first patient was enrolled on December 2, 2021. The last patient was enrolled on June 30, 2022. This trial was registered on November 29, 2021 on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05136352).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients ≥18 years old scheduled for elective primary ACLR under general anesthesia were eligible. The exclusion criteria were as follows: preoperative use of opioids; contraindication to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (allergy, gastroduodenal ulcer, or known renal insufficiency with an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min); patient refusal; major spontaneous or acquired hemostasis disorders; infection at the puncture site; allergy to local anesthetics; and pregnancy.

Randomization

Using computer-generated randomization (Stata Statistical Software, Release V.14, StataCorp), patients were successively randomized and assigned to either the FTB or the FTB+iPACK group. Allocation numbers were sealed in envelopes and opened at the time of inclusion on the day of surgery by the anesthetist who performed the regional anesthesia. Patients were not blinded to their group assignment (regional anesthesia with one or two punctures). However, they were instructed not to disclose their allocation to the observer physician, who evaluated the outcome criteria, was not involved in the anesthetic management, and was therefore blinded to the randomization results. Furthermore, the surgeon and nurses in charge of the patient were blinded to the group assignment. In all cases, skin disinfection with povidone-iodine was carried out in the popliteal region to make it less obvious that a patient had not received an iPACK block.

Ultrasound-guided blocks

Blocks were performed prior to general anesthesia and surgery.

Femoral triangle block

Patient were positioned supine. A high-frequency linear ultrasound probe (15–6 MHz, HFL50x, SonoSite SII, Fujifilm, Bothell, Washington, USA) was applied at the anteromedial part of the mid-thigh to visualize the superficial femoral artery. Sartorius and vastus medialis muscles delimiting the femoral triangle were visualized. A 22 G, 80 mm needle (Pajunk SonoPlex, Geisingen, Germany) was directed in plane toward the saphenous nerve located under the vasto-adductor membrane, laterally and close to the artery.18 A perineural injection of 15 mL ropivacaine 0.2% was realized.

iPACK

Patient were positioned in the lateral decubitus position with their dedicated lower limb downwards. A microconvex ultrasound probe (8–3 MHz, C35x, SonoSite SII, Fujifilm) was used to scan the popliteal fossa. The lateral and medial femoral condyles were identified, as well as the popliteal artery and the sciatic nerve. The probe was moved cephalad to the upper edge of the femoral condyles until the condyles disappeared and the femoral shaft became visible. A 22 G, 80 mm needle (Pajunk SonoPlex, Geisingen, Germany) was medially inserted and directed in plane targeting the area between the popliteal artery and the lateral femoral condyle.19 A fractionated injection of 15 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine was performed. After retracting the needle, an additional 10 mL was fractionally injected into the posterior part of the medial femoral condyle, for a total dose of 25 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine.

General anesthesia

For all patients, intravenous access was established. Standard vital sign monitors were placed and oxygen was delivered through a facemask. Surgery was performed under general anesthesia induced with propofol (2–3 mg/kg) and sufentanil (0.2 µg/kg). Each patient received a bolus of intravenous ketamine (0.15 mg/kg). For prophylaxis of postoperative nausea and vomiting, 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone was administered to each patient at the time of induction of anesthesia, following loss of consciousness. After placement of a supraglottic airway device (Ambu Aura-iTM, Ambu A/S, Ballerup, Denmark), anesthesia was maintained with 4–8 mg/kg/hour intravenous propofol. Patients’ lungs were mechanically ventilated with a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg ideal bodyweight.

Surgery

The surgical procedure of ACLR was standardized and carried out by a single surgeon (EC). Briefly, the first step was to harvest the tendons of the semitendinosus and gracilis muscles for graft preparation (suture and calibration). The second step consisted of arthroscopically introducing and fixing the graft into the femur (tunnel). At the end of the procedure, all patients were given periarticular infiltration of local anesthetic with 20 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine as previously described.20 21 Briefly, a first injection (8 mL) was performed in the empty space left by the harvest of the gracilis and semitendinosus tendons, a second injection (5 mL) in the iliotibial band through the surgical exposure used for femoral tunnel drilling, and finally, subcutaneous tissue was infiltrated (7 mL). The local infiltration was then followed by a plan-by-plan closure.

Postoperative analgesia

At the end of surgery, all patients received 1 g intravenous paracetamol and 100 mg intravenous ketoprofen. In the post anesthesia care unit (PACU), pain was evaluated by an 11-point Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain imaginable. Intravenous oxycodone was administered when the NRS was >3,22 with an initial bolus dose of 0.1 mg/kg. On hospital discharge, all patients received (oral route) 1 g paracetamol every 6 hours for 7 days; 100 mg sustained-release ketoprofen every 12 hours for 5 days; and 10 mg immediate-release oxycodone as required, maximum every 4 hours in case of pain, for 3 days.23

Sample size prediction

Personal data from a pilot study suggest that patients consume a mean (±SD) of 100±40 mg of OME in the first 48 postoperative hours after surgery of ACLR realized under general anesthesia and FTB. Our working hypothesis was that the addition of an iPACK block should reduce morphine consumption by 25% compared with FTB alone (analgesic benefit deemed clinically relevant). To demonstrate a significant difference in analgesic consumption between the FTB+iPACK and FTB groups with a two-sided alpha risk set at 5% and a power set at 80%, 41 patients per group had to be analyzed. In order to compensate for possible inclusion errors and lost to follow-up (generally estimated at 10%), 45 patients were to be included in each group.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Quantitative variables were expressed as median (IQR) or mean (±SD) as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (%). The comparison of continuous variables between the FTB and FTB+iPACK groups was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test and included the Hodges-Lehmann median of the difference (the Hodges-Lehmann estimate of location shift) and its 95% CI (Conover, 1999). Categorical variables were compared using the χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. Statistical analysis was performed using MedCalc software (V.12.6.1, MedCalc Software bvba, Ostend, Belgium; 2013). A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Determining the ideal peripheral nerve block for ACLR has proved challenging based on the complex innervation of the knee. Through this randomized controlled trial carried out in primary ACLR under general anesthesia, we demonstrated that an added iPACK block to FTB did not reduce cumulative morphine consumption during the first two postoperative days. In the setting of an adequate postoperative multimodal analgesia regimen including local infiltration by the surgeon, longitudinal evaluation of morphine consumption and pain scores did not show statistically significant differences when comparing FTB and FTB+iPACK groups. Moreover, knee functional-related outcomes were not different between groups.

Postoperative morphine consumptions and pain scores

Our results demonstrated that, in the context of a standardized postoperative analgesic regimen (including acetaminophen, NSAIDs, and sustained-release opioids on demand), an iPACK block combined with FTB is not superior to FTB alone in the management of postoperative pain after primary ACLR under general anesthesia.

Our results need to be compared with those recently published by Martin et al.27 Indeed, the authors identified that the combination of femoral nerve and iPACK blocks reduces morphine consumption during the first 24 hours after ACLR, when compared with local infiltration analgesia (mean OME difference 21 mg, p=0.03), without effect on other pain-related, early, or late functional-related outcomes. The discrepancy observed with our results may be explained by the absence of regional anesthesia in their control group, whereas all our patients benefited from an FTB to cover anterior knee pain.

A first element to explain our main result is that postoperative morphine consumption was low in both groups. In the overall population, the median OME consumption was 58 mg at day 2, corresponding to 2–3 tablets of oral oxycodone 10 mg. Therefore, postoperative pain appears to be well controlled by a multimodal analgesia regimen including at least a regional anesthesia of the anterior compartment of the knee. In this setting, a significant analgesic benefit from an added iPACK would be difficult to demonstrate.

Another explanation lies in the fact that a surgical infiltration of ropivacaine was performed in both groups. This infiltration is notably realized at the harvest site. It may, therefore, have minimized the analgesic benefit of an added iPACK by anesthetizing the tendon harvesting assuming that a significant proportion of postoperative pain is due to this procedure for which iPACK is not effective when added to local infiltration analgesia. Despite this possible bias, surgical infiltration has been shown to be effective in relieving pain and is part of the recommendations for pain management of ACLR.17

Amer also evaluated the benefit of an iPACK block for ACLR.28 Patients undergoing ACLR under spinal anesthesia and ACB ‘at mid-thigh’ were randomly allocated to receive either an iPACK block (20 mL of ropivacaine) or a periarticular injection (20 mL of ropivacaine during surgery, followed by two bolus injections administered postoperatively via an intra-articular catheter). The primary endpoint (time to first request for analgesia rescue with pethidine) was significantly longer in the iPACK group (mean±SD (hours): 32.3±3.2 vs 21.4±2.8, p<0.001). Additionally, total pethidine consumption during the first 48 postoperative hours was lower in the iPACK group (mean±SD (mg): 28.1±33.2 vs 61.3±73.6, p<0.001). Based on these results and those obtained in our study where all patients received surgical local infiltration analgesia, we believe that local infiltration analgesia (including harvest site!) may be redundant when combined with the iPACK block, effectively masking any potential benefit the iPACK block might have provided.

Concerning morphine side effects, insofar as morphine consumption was similar between the groups, so was the incidence of side effects. Additionally, no more falls were observed in the FTB+iPACK group when compared with FTB alone. This result was expected as iPACK block is known to spare motor function by exclusively targeting the articular nerves branches of the posterior capsule of the knee.14 15 27

Knee functional outcomes

As both FTB and iPACK are believed to have a reduced impact on muscle strength,11 14 15 27 we felt it was of paramount importance to properly compare functional outcomes after ACLR alongside analgesic impact. In our study, knee functional scores after surgery were comparable between groups. In the end, this result is not surprising. Indeed, how can we expect to have an impact on the functional recovery of the knee when an added iPACK block to FTB does not reduce morphine consumption and pain scores?

Multiple PROMs have been used to assess the efficacy of treatment after meniscal procedures or ACLR.21 29 In this setting, KOOS, IKDC and Lysholm are probably the most responsive PROMs as they can be used to assess knee functional outcome with reliability.29 One of our findings is the association that may exist between a high OME intake within the first 2 postoperative days and an impairment of the knee function. To the best of our knowledge, high pain scores and morphine consumption in the early postoperative period have never been identified as predictors of lower knee function improvement 9 months after ACLR.30 However, we cannot rely solely on a statistical association to draw definitive conclusions. Indeed, we might argue that our results did not reach the minimal clinically important difference reported in the literature for patients undergoing ACLR. In this setting, increases of at least 12 and 19 points in the IKDC and KOOS subscales, respectively, are expected to determine clinically relevant improvement 6 months after ACLR.31 Studies specifically designed with a functional recovery criterion would be necessary before confirming or refuting the existence of an association between acute postoperative pain or morphine consumption and impaired knee functional recovery.

Limits

There are notable limitations to our study. First, it was not possible in our study to demonstrate the success rate of our iPACK blocks. Since the iPACK block results in the diffusion of local anesthetic toward the articular nerve branches of the knee (ie, the inferior branches of the tibial nerve and the genicular branch of the obturator nerve),15 we believe that reliable skin sensory measurement is not feasible. However, our experience has shown that this block is relatively quick to learn, with an easy visualization of the anatomical landmarks on ultrasound. Second, the threshold to categorize patients into subgroups of low and high postoperative OME consumption was arbitrarily defined. However, we decided to use the median consumption of the FTB group because we are convinced that this threshold is the most clinically relevant. Finally, this interventional randomized controlled trial was single-blinded. Patients knew their group allocation, which may affect the interpretation of the results, particularly those relating to a subjective criterion such as pain. However, our hypothesis of the superiority of a combined approach (FTB plus iPACK) was stipulated in the patient information note. Consequently, the risk of this single-blind study was rather to overestimate the analgesic effect of the combined approach (through a placebo effect), which could have made a statistically significant result in favor of iPACK difficult to interpret. However, no statistical difference was found between groups.

Conclusions

In conclusion, FTB and FTB plus iPACK provide similar postoperative analgesia after ACLR, with an equivalent impact on late functional outcomes. Therefore, iPACK should not be considered for ACLR in the setting of a multimodal analgesia regimen including FTB and local infiltration analgesia. Further studies are needed to identify new personalized strategies for reducing early postoperative pain and morphine consumption.
