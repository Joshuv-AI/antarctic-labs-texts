A multi-phase framework for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and transparency in renal cell carcinoma grading using YOLOv8 and GradCAM
Authors: Amna Bamaqa, Wedad O. Alahamade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Kidney cancer, specifically renal cell carcinoma (RCC), represents a major global health issue, with increasing incidence rates, highlighting the necessity for effective and accurate diagnostic systems. Traditional methods for RCC grading, such as manual histopathological analysis, are labor intensive, susceptible to human error, and lack the scalability required for modern clinical environments. To address these issues, we propose a novel multiphase classification framework that combines YOLOv8 for high-accuracy RCC grading and GradCAM for enhanced model interpretability. The framework progressively refines WHO/ISUP grades through a cascading approach: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_1$$\end{document} distinguishes between Grades 0/1 and 2/3/4, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_3$$\end{document} differentiates between Grades 0 and 1, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_3$$\end{document} classifies Grades 2, 3, and 4. A unique “Avoid Error Propagation Layer” ensures that only high-confidence predictions are passed to subsequent phases, reducing cumulative errors. Our approach achieves remarkable accuracy (97.51%), precision (93.72%), recall (93.28%), specificity (98.32%), and F1 score (93.35%) on a publicly available RCC dataset. By integrating cutting-edge object detection with visual explanations, our model not only provides superior diagnostic performance but also offers transparent decision-making, which is crucial for clinical adoption. This approach addresses a major shortcoming of existing RCC grading systems by simultaneously prioritizing accuracy and interpretability, promising for revolutionizing kidney cancer diagnosis by enhancing both diagnostic precision and clinician trust.

Introduction

Kidney cancer has emerged as a significant global health concern, with rising incidence rates highlighting the urgent need for effective and accurate diagnostic systems1. Current statistics reveal an increasing number of new kidney cancer cases globally, underscoring the necessity for swift and precise detection methods. Kidney cancer ranks among the top 15 most prevalent malignancies in humans, accounting for approximately 2.4% of all cancer cases. According to projections by the American Cancer Society, there will be 80,980 new diagnoses of kidney cancer in the United States by 2025, with an estimated 14,390 deaths2.

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC), the most common form of renal cancer, accounts for more than 85% of cases and represents a diverse group of malignancies characterized by distinct molecular features, histological patterns, treatment responses, and clinical outcomes3. RCC subtypes are primarily categorized on the basis of morphological characteristics observed in histological samples stained with hematoxylin and eosin4. Accurate assessment of the stage and grade of renal tumors is critical for diagnosis and treatment planning. The stage reflects the tumor’s size, location, and spread to adjacent lymph nodes, whereas the grade indicates the degree of cellular abnormality compared with healthy cells under microscopic examination5,6. This information enables pathologists to evaluate cancer progression and metastatic potential, allowing clinicians to tailor treatment strategies effectively7–9.

Traditional methods for grading RCC, such as manual histopathological analysis, are labor intensive, prone to human error, and lack scalability for modern clinical environments10. Consequently, there is a growing demand for automated and accurate approaches to grade kidney cancer via histopathological images for the precise detection of malignant tumors11. The Fuhrman four-tier grading system, as described by Hong et al.12, categorizes RCC grades on the basis of nuclear morphology: Grade-1 nuclei exhibit tubular shapes and uniformity, Grade-2 nuclei show slight irregularities, Grade-3 nuclei display more pronounced abnormalities, and Grade-4 nuclei are marked by pleomorphic cells, mitotic activity, and multilobate structures13.

A more recent grading system developed by the WHO/International Society of Urologic Pathology (ISUP) classifies tumors into grades 1–3 on the basis of nuclear prominence, with Grade 4 identified by severe nuclear pleomorphism14. Research by Delahunt et al.15 demonstrated that the ISUP nucleolar grading system outperforms the traditional Fuhrman system and is now recommended as the primary grading method by pathologists.

The advent of deep learning (DL) algorithms has revolutionized histopathological analysis, demonstrating exceptional performance in tasks such as tumor segmentation, grading, and classification16,17. DL, a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), employs sophisticated computer algorithms to autonomously detect complex patterns in data, enabling automated and precise identification of intricate features within medical images18–21. These advancements have paved the way for innovative solutions to longstanding challenges in RCC grading.

Building on these developments, this study addresses the critical challenge of accurately classifying and grading RCC from histopathological images via YOLOv8 for multiphase classification and GradCAM for interpretability. The proposed framework uses the strengths of YOLOv8 to address multiphase classification problems, enhancing the granularity of subtype differentiation, which is a key factor in clinical decision-making. The GradCAM improves model transparency by generating visual explanations that highlight the regions influencing predictions, thereby addressing a major limitation of existing AI models in medical diagnostics. By integrating advanced techniques to minimize overfitting and employing rigorous evaluation criteria, this framework not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also fosters a deeper understanding of RCC pathophysiology.

Related studies

Recent research has indicated that developments in DL have profoundly altered the domain of medical image analysis, especially concerning the categorization and prognosis of RCC22. For example, Chanchal et al.23 in 2024 used a DL framework to forecast and categorize the grades of RCC on the basis of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) images. The developed system prioritized the extraction of features from H&E images across 5 grades and classified unseen images accordingly. The evaluation criteria, such as precision, recall, and accuracy, showed enhanced performance relative to seven innovative DL architectures across two distinct datasets of H&E-stained histopathological images. The reported metrics for precision, recall, F1 score, and accuracy were 0.9152, 0.9112, 0.9131, and 0.9123, respectively.

Additionally, Lal et al.24 emphasized the importance of automating cancer grade classification for kidney cancer prognosis via histopathological images. Their study focused on the design and implementation of an automated system utilizing histopathological images of noncancerous tissues and various grades of RCC sourced from Kasturba Medical College, Mangalore, Karnataka, India. The experimental results indicated that the Inception-V3 network demonstrated higher accuracy in kidney cancer identification than the other models did. The DenseNet-169 network exhibited enhanced accuracy in kidney cancer grading on an FPGA board, highlighting its effectiveness in this context.

Moreover, Mahmood et al.25 in 2024 suggested a hybrid model integrating vision transformers (ViT) and convolutional neural networks (CNN) for the assessment of RCC; the proposed model aimed to address challenges in neural network selection and configuration for histopathology image analysis. The renal cancer grading network incorporates an adaptive convolution (AC) block for effective feature extraction and a dynamic attention (DA) block utilizing a transformer encoder for enhanced representation learning. Experimental evaluations on four histopathology datasets, including RCC from KMC, demonstrated significant performance improvements. For the KMC dataset, the method achieved an accuracy of 90.62%, precision of 91.23%, recall of 90.63%, and an F1 score of 90.92. Similarly, it showed robust performance on other datasets, surpassing other methods with high accuracy and recognition rates. These findings highlight the model’s efficiency and adaptability across various histopathological image categorization tasks.

Furthermore, Kadirappa et al.26 emphasized the increasing importance of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), particularly with the increasing incidence of nearly 800,000 new cancer cases annually, as estimated by cancer population registries. Motivated to advance NCD diagnosis, they developed a DL-based cancer identification methodology. Their approach, DeepHistoNet, integrates attention-guided, densely connected residual, and dilated convolution neural networks to extract precise features crucial for classification. Experimental validation conducted on the KMC, TCGA-LIHC, and LC25000 datasets demonstrated superior performance metrics, including F1-scores, sensitivity, specificity, recall, and accuracy. DeepHistoNet achieved exceptional results, with 97.1% accuracy and an AUC-ROC value of 0.9867 on the KMC liver dataset, surpassing existing methods. Moreover, it attained 99.8% accuracy on the LC25000 dataset, highlighting its efficacy in multiple histopathological image classifications.

Research gaps

However, despite the high performance demonstrated by DL models such as DeepHistoNet in medical image classification tasks such as RCC grading, a critical research gap concerning AI explainability remains. While these approaches achieve impressive accuracy and robust metrics, the interpretability of their decisions is often limited. Understanding how and why a model arrives at a specific classification is crucial for gaining the trust of medical professionals and ensuring transparency in clinical decision-making. Current studies have focused primarily on optimizing performance metrics without adequately addressing the need for explainable AI (XAI) in medical image analysis. Therefore, there is a pressing need for future research to prioritize the development of AI models that not only excel in accuracy but also provide clear explanations of their decisions, particularly in complex and critical domains such as kidney cancer diagnosis.

Materials

The current study utilizes a publicly available dataset named the “KMC Kidney Histopathology Dataset”27. This dataset was introduced by Chanchal et al. in 2023 and consists of histopathological images of noncancerous (grade 0) and cancerous (grades 1 to 4) renal clear cell carcinoma (RCC) tissues stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The images were collected between October 2020 and December 2022 at Kasturba Medical College (KMC), Mangalore, India. Ethical approval for the study was granted, and it adheres to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and personal details were safeguarded in compliance with ethical guidelines.

The dataset was created via surgical biopsy samples that were processed via standard paraffin embedding techniques. The tissues were sectioned and stained with H&E to highlight the cellular and tissue structures. The images were captured via an Olympus BX-50 microscope equipped with a high-resolution camera system. The dataset includes images captured at multiple magnifications, including 40x, 100x, 200x, and 400x, enabling the analysis of both the macroscopic tissue architecture and microscopic cellular details. For the purposes of this study, we primarily utilized images captured at 400x magnification, as this resolution provides the most detailed view of cellular and nuclear features, which are critical for accurate RCC grading.

Grades description

In Grade 0, the kidney tissue appeared normal without any cancerous changes. The cells of the proximal tubules exhibit central nuclei and highly acidophilic cytoplasm, indicating healthy and well-functioning cells. The arrangement of cells is orderly, and their number is consistent with what is expected in noncancerous tissue. The structure of the glomerulus is normal, suggesting that kidney function is unimpaired.

Grade 1 cancer cells display early signs of malignancy. The nucleoli in these cells are basophilic, meaning that they stain blue with basic dyes but are not visible even under high magnification (400x). The overall morphology of these cells remains quite similar to that of normal cells, indicating minimal deviation from healthy tissue.

In Grade 2, the nucleoli become eosinophilic, stain pink with eosin dye, and can be seen at 400x magnification, although they are not very prominent at 100x magnification. Compared with those of normal cells, the nuclei of these cells are slightly irregular in contour, indicating the progression of malignancy. These cells begin to deviate more noticeably from their normal counterparts.

The number of Grade 3 cancerous cells increased with progression. Nucleoli are conspicuous and eosinophilic at 100x magnification, indicating a greater degree of abnormality. Tumors are clearly visible at this stage, and the cells often contain giant tumor cells, which are large cells with multiple nuclei. Significant irregularities in cell structure and organization mark this grade.

Grade-4 cancer cells exhibit pronounced nuclear pleomorphism, indicating considerable variation in the size and shape of the nuclei. This stage often includes rhabdoid or sarcomatoid differentiation, where the cells take on more aggressive and abnormal characteristics. The presence of these highly atypical cells indicates a very advanced and aggressive form of cancer.

Classification phases using YOLOv8

The classification approach employs the YOLOv8 model, an advanced architecture in object identification and image classification recognized for its exceptional equilibrium between speed and precision28. As the latest iteration in the YOLO (you only look once) series, YOLOv8 utilizes the strengths of earlier versions. It incorporates notable improvements that make it particularly suitable for processing high-resolution histopathology images29. YOLOv8’s architecture is a refinement over previous versions, incorporating several cutting-edge features that enhance both its performance and flexibility30.

YOLOv8, which was traditionally designed as an object detection model, was innovatively adapted in this study to address the multiphase classification task of grading RCC. While YOLOv8 is conventionally used to predict bounding boxes and classify objects within those boxes, our framework treats the entire histopathology image patch as a single “object” for classification purposes. This adaptation uses YOLOv8’s robust feature extraction capabilities while aligning its output with the grading task.

At its core, YOLOv8 employs a CNN backbone that is specifically tailored for speed and accuracy. In our approach, the input to YOLOv8 consists of whole-slide image patches cropped or tiled from the original histopathology slides. These patches are preprocessed to ensure uniformity in size and resolution, which is critical for maintaining consistency during training and inference. Rather than detecting multiple objects within an image, YOLOv8’s output layer was modified to interpret the entire image patch as belonging to a specific RCC grade. This simplification effectively transforms YOLOv8 into a classification model, where the predicted class corresponds directly to the RCC grade (e.g., Grade 0, Grade 1, etc.).

To clarify further, bounding boxes were not explicitly used in this framework. Instead, the entire image patch was treated as a single entity, and the classification task was performed without generating or utilizing bounding box predictions. This design simplifies the model’s architecture and focuses its computational resources on extracting hierarchical features relevant to RCC grading. The absence of bounding box predictions also eliminates the need for synthetic annotations, ensuring that the framework remains purely classification oriented.

Avoid error propagation layer

In a cascading classification system, one of the significant challenges is the potential for errors in early stages to propagate through subsequent stages, ultimately compromising the accuracy of the final predictions. To address this issue, an “Avoid Error Propagation Layer” is integrated into the suggested approach. This layer acts as a critical checkpoint that identifies and filters out potential misclassifications before they are passed on to the next stage, thereby enhancing the overall robustness and reliability of the model.

This layer functions as an intermediary validation step between different phases of the cascading classification approach. By filtering out misclassified or uncertain predictions at each stage, this layer minimizes the risk of errors affecting the final outcomes. The rigorous validation processes embedded within this layer ensure that only high-confidence, low-uncertainty predictions advance through the cascade, leading to more precise differentiation between noncancer and cancer grades in RCC.

Moreover, the Avoid Error Propagation Layer improves the interpretability and transparency of the model. By explicitly handling uncertainty and potential misclassifications, the approach becomes more trustworthy, especially in critical medical applications where the cost of errors can be high. Clinicians and researchers can have greater confidence in the model’s predictions, knowing that the system is designed to catch and correct possible errors before they influence the final diagnosis.

Performance evaluation

The effectiveness of the proposed method is thoroughly examined through an extensive array of evaluation metrics, each offering distinct insights into its effectiveness in distinguishing between different cancer subtypes from histopathology images33. Accuracy quantifies the ratio of correctly classified instances to total instances and serves as a fundamental parameter for overall model efficiency34,35.

Precision evaluates the model’s capacity to identify positive predictions within the total accurately predicted positives, which is crucial for minimizing false positives in clinical applications36. Recall measures the model’s ability to identify all actual positive instances, which is essential for identifying all potential cases of RCC19. Specificity complements recall by measuring the model’s accuracy in predicting negative instances, thereby assessing its ability to avoid false alarms37.

Moreover, the F1 score integrates precision and recall into a unified metric, offering a balanced assessment of the model’s predictive power. Intersection over union (IoU) evaluates the spatial overlap between the predicted and ground truth regions and is particularly useful in tasks involving object detection or segmentation in medical imaging38.

Balanced accuracy (BAC) provides an average measure of sensitivity and specificity, which is particularly valuable in scenarios where class distributions are uneven. The Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) considers true and false positives and negatives, offering a robust measure of classification performance across varying class distributions39,40.

Additionally, Youden’s index combines sensitivity and specificity into a single metric, emphasizing the model’s ability to accurately detect both positive and negative instances simultaneously. Yule’s Q measures the degree of association between the predicted and actual classifications, providing insights into the overall predictive reliability of the model. Each metric is computed via its specific formula, which is augmented with a small constant to ensure numerical stability during computation41,42.

Together, these metrics collectively assess and validate the model’s accuracy, reliability, and robustness in the critical task of diagnosing RCC from histopathology images, offering clinicians and researchers valuable tools for improved diagnostic decision-making.

Explainability of models with GradCAM

Explainability is a critical component of deploying AI models in medical diagnostics, where trust and transparency are paramount. Among various interpretability techniques, GradCAM (gradient-weighted class activation mapping) stands out because of its ability to provide spatially localized visual explanations directly on medical images. The GradCAM generates heatmaps that highlight regions of the input image most influential for the model’s predictions, offering clinicians intuitive insights into the decision-making process43.

While other interpretability tools, such as LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), are widely used, they are less suited for high-dimensional image data such as histopathology slides. Unlike GradCAM, which uses the inherent structure of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to produce class-discriminative localization maps, LIME and SHAP rely on perturbation-based methods that may not preserve the spatial coherence of features in images. Additionally, GradCAM is computationally efficient, requiring only a single backward pass through the network to compute gradients, whereas LIME and SHAP involve multiple evaluations of the model, making them less practical for large-scale datasets.

In the context of RCC classification, GradCAM’s ability to localize critical features (such as cellular arrangements, nuclear shapes, and tissue structures) is particularly valuable. By overlaying heatmaps on original histopathology images, clinicians can validate whether the model focuses on medically relevant areas, reinforcing confidence in its predictions. Furthermore, GradCAM enables iterative refinement of the model by identifying cases where irrelevant regions or artifacts influence predictions, guiding improvements in training data or architecture.

The GradCAM operates by utilizing the gradients of a specified target class, which flows into the final convolutional layer of the neural network, to generate a coarse localization map that emphasizes the significant areas within the image44. This localization map, typically represented as a heatmap, highlights the regions of the image that exert the most significant influence on the model’s prediction.

The process of generating GradCAM visualizations begins by identifying a specific layer in the neural network, typically the last convolutional layer, where feature maps are still spatially organized in a way that corresponds to the input image. This layer is chosen because convolutional layers retain spatial information, unlike fully connected layers, where spatial relationships are lost.

Experiments and discussion

The experiments were conducted with a Windows 11 operating system with 256 GB of RAM and a 16 GB NVIDIA GPU. The execution was performed via the Python programming language along with the PyTorch and Ultralytics DL libraries. The dataset was split into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) sets to ensure patient-independent division, thereby preventing data leakage and maintaining unbiased evaluation. This partitioning strategy is critical for assessing the model’s generalizability across unseen patient data. Data augmentation strategies, including random rotations, horizontal and vertical flips, and color jittering, were employed during training to increase the diversity of the dataset and improve model robustness.

The hyperparameters were carefully tuned to optimize the performance. The models were trained via the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, which was adjusted dynamically on the basis of validation performance. A batch size of 16 was selected to balance memory constraints and efficient gradient estimation. Training was conducted for a maximum of 100 epochs, with early stopping implemented to prevent overfitting. Specifically, training was halted if the validation loss did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs, ensuring efficient resource utilization while maintaining optimal performance.

The evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1 score, IoU, BAC, MCC, Youden’s index, and Yule’s Q, were computed via their respective formulas and adjusted for numerical stability with a small constant. These metrics collectively provide a comprehensive assessment of the model’s diagnostic accuracy, reliability, and robustness in classifying RCC grades from histopathological images.

The issue of class imbalance in this study was addressed through a combination of data augmentation and careful preprocessing to ensure that the model could effectively learn from all classes; particularly those with fewer samples. The utilzied dataset used in this study contains histopathological images labeled according to WHO/ISUP grades; which inherently exhibit varying distributions across grades. To mitigate potential biases arising from this imbalance, we employed extensive data augmentation techniques, such as random rotations, scaling, flipping, and color jittering, to artificially increase the diversity and size of underrepresented classes. Additionally, during model training, class weights were dynamically adjusted to emphasize the importance of minority classes, ensuring that the model did not disproportionately prioritize majority classes. These strategies collectively help improve the robustness and generalizability of the framework, enabling it to achieve high-performance metrics even for classes with fewer samples.

Quantitative assessment of GradCAM visualizations

While GradCAM provides valuable qualitative insights into the regions of an image that influence the model’s predictions, it is equally important to assess these visualizations quantitatively to ensure their clinical relevance. To achieve this goal, we compared the GradCAM-generated heatmaps with pathologist-annotated regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to key histopathological features indicative of RCC grade. The annotations were provided by experienced pathologists who identified critical areas such as abnormal nuclei, cellular density, and tissue architecture.

Two primary metrics were used to quantify the alignment between the GradCAM heatmaps and the annotated ROIs: localization error and intersection over union (IoU). The localization error measures the spatial discrepancy between the model’s focus areas and the annotated ROIs, whereas the IoU reflects the overlap between two regions. These metrics provide a robust framework for assessing the accuracy of the model’s attention to clinically significant features.

The localization error was computed as the average Euclidean distance between the centroids of the GradCAM-highlighted regions and the centroids of the annotated ROIs. Lower localization errors indicate better alignment between the model’s focus and the pathologist-identified regions. For example, in cases where the model correctly highlighted areas with abnormal nuclei or high cellular density, the localization error was significantly lower than that in cases where irrelevant regions were emphasized, where the area of overlap represents the intersection between the GradCAM heatmap and the annotated ROI, and the area of union includes all regions covered by either the heatmap or the annotation. Higher IoU values indicate a stronger correspondence between the model’s focus and the pathologist-annotated features.

Our analysis revealed that the GradCAM heatmaps achieved an average IoU of 0.78 across the dataset, demonstrating strong alignment with the annotated ROIs. The localization error averaged 12.3 pixels, which is within an acceptable range given the resolution of the images. These results underscore the effectiveness of GradCAM in guiding the model’s attention toward clinically relevant features, thereby enhancing its diagnostic utility.

Ablation study of the avoid error propagation layer

To better understand the contributions of each component within the “Avoid Error Propagation Layer”, we conducted an ablation study. This study quantifies the impact of confidence thresholding, uncertainty estimation, and ensemble voting on cascaded classification system performance. The results are presented in terms of key evaluation metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1 score) both before and after the integration of these components.

Initially, we evaluated the cascaded classification system without any error propagation mitigation mechanisms. In this baseline scenario, predictions from one phase were directly passed to subsequent phases without additional validation. The aggregate performance metrics for the cascaded approach were as follows: accuracy = 96.82%, precision = 92.14%, recall = 91.76%, specificity = 97.85%, and F1 score = 91.87%. While these results were promising, they revealed potential vulnerabilities due to the propagation of low-confidence or uncertain predictions across phases.

Overall discussion

Kidney cancer represents a significant global health challenge, with its incidence and mortality rates rising steadily, underscoring the urgent need for advanced diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Among the various types of kidney cancer, RCC is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 85% of all cases. RCC is characterized by a highly heterogeneous nature, with multiple subtypes that differ not only in their morphological and histopathological features but also in their molecular profiles and clinical behavior. This heterogeneity poses substantial challenges in diagnosis, prognostication, and treatment, making accurate classification and subtype differentiation crucial for patient management.

Morphological analysis of histological preparations has long been the cornerstone of RCC diagnosis. However, the intricate and overlapping features of different RCC subtypes often complicate the diagnostic process, potentially leading to misclassification and suboptimal treatment choices. Accurate subtype identification is critical, as it directly influences the therapeutic approach, including decisions regarding surgery, systemic therapy, and immunotherapy. Given the complexity of RCC, there is a growing need for more sophisticated diagnostic tools that can increase the precision of histopathological assessments.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) and AI offer transformative potential in the field of kidney cancer diagnostics. By utilizing large datasets of histopathological images, AI-driven systems can be trained to recognize subtle morphological patterns that may elude human observers, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and consistency. The approach proposed in this study, which integrates advanced DL models such as YOLOv8 with traditional histological analysis, represents a significant step forward in the automated classification of RCC subtypes. The use of CNNs enables the extraction of hierarchical features from histopathological images, facilitating the differentiation of RCC subtypes on the basis of nuanced cellular and tissue characteristics.

Moreover, the incorporation of explainability techniques such as GradCAM enhances the transparency of AI-driven diagnostics, addressing a critical barrier to their adoption in clinical practice. The GradCAM provides visual insights into the decision-making process of AI models, allowing clinicians to understand and validate the regions of the image that contribute most to the model’s predictions. This interpretability is particularly important in medical applications, where trust and clinical validation are paramount.

In addition to GradCAM, the proposed approach includes an “Avoid Error Propagation Layer”, which acts as a safeguard against the propagation of misclassifications through the cascading classification system. This layer ensures that only high-confidence predictions are passed to subsequent stages, thereby reducing the risk of error accumulation and improving the overall robustness of the diagnostic system. By filtering out low-confidence or uncertain predictions, this approach minimizes the likelihood of incorrect final diagnoses, which is especially crucial in the management of RCC, where treatment decisions are heavily influenced by the accurate grading and staging of the tumor.

The implications of this study extend beyond RCC to other forms of cancer where histopathological analysis plays a pivotal role in diagnosis and treatment planning. The hybrid approach combining traditional pathology with cutting-edge AI not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also facilitates personalized medicine by tailoring treatments to the specific characteristics of each tumor. This is particularly relevant in the era of precision oncology, where the goal is to optimize therapeutic outcomes on the basis of the molecular and morphological profiles of individual tumors.

Furthermore, the application of AI in kidney cancer diagnostics holds promise for improving early detection rates, which is critical for reducing mortality. Early-stage RCC is often asymptomatic and may only be detected incidentally during imaging studies for unrelated conditions. However, when RCC is diagnosed at an advanced stage, it has a poor prognosis because of its resistance to conventional therapies and propensity for metastasis. By integrating AI tools into routine diagnostic workflows, it may be possible to identify RCC at an earlier stage when curative treatment is more likely to be successful.

Potential limitations and challenges

While YOLOv8 demonstrates exceptional performance in terms of accuracy and speed, its computational demands can pose challenges, especially when the model is deployed in resource-constrained environments such as remote clinics or low-memory devices. The XLarge variant of YOLOv8, which achieved the highest accuracy in our experiments, requires significant computational resources, including high GPU memory and processing power. This could limit its scalability in settings where access to advanced hardware is limited. To address this, lightweight versions of the model could be developed via techniques such as knowledge distillation, pruning, or quantization, but these approaches may slightly compromise accuracy.

Although the proposed framework achieves remarkable results on the “KMC Kidney Histopathology Dataset,” its generalizability to other datasets remains untested. Variations in staining techniques, imaging equipment, and patient demographics across institutions could affect the model’s performance. External validation via multicenter datasets, such as TCGA-KIRC, is essential to ensure consistent performance in real-world clinical scenarios. Without such validation, there is a risk that the model may not perform as effectively when applied to data from different sources.

While GradCAM enhances model interpretability by highlighting regions of interest, it is not without limitations. The heatmaps generated by GradCAM provide coarse approximations of feature importance and may sometimes fail to capture fine-grained details, especially in complex histopathological images with overlapping features. Additionally, GradCAM relies heavily on the final convolutional layers of the network, which may overlook earlier-stage features that contribute to the classification decision. Future work could explore alternative explainability techniques, such as SHAP or LIME, to complement GradCAM and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model’s decision-making process.

The “Avoid Error Propagation Layer” significantly improves the robustness of the cascaded classification system by filtering out low-confidence predictions. However, this layer introduces additional complexity to the framework and may increase the inference time, particularly when uncertainty estimation and ensemble voting are employed. Moreover, setting appropriate thresholds for confidence and uncertainty requires careful calibration, as overly stringent thresholds may lead to excessive rejection of valid predictions, whereas lenient thresholds may fail to adequately mitigate error propagation.

The reliance on a single dataset for training and evaluation may introduce biases that limit the model’s ability to generalize. For example, the “KMC Kidney Histopathology Dataset” predominantly includes samples from a specific geographic region and population, which may not fully represent the global diversity of RCC cases. Expanding the dataset to include more diverse populations and incorporating domain adaptation techniques could help mitigate this limitation.

While the proposed framework offers significant improvements in diagnostic accuracy and interpretability, integrating it into existing clinical workflows presents practical challenges. Pathologists and clinicians may require extensive training to effectively use and trust AI-driven tools. Furthermore, regulatory approval processes for AI-based diagnostic systems can be lengthy and complex, potentially delaying their adoption in clinical practice. Collaborating with healthcare providers and regulatory bodies to streamline integration and ensure compliance with ethical and legal standards is essential.

The cascading nature of the classification system inherently involves multiple decision points, each of which introduces some degree of uncertainty. Even with the “Avoid Error Propagation Layer,” there is a risk that errors from earlier phases may subtly influence downstream decisions, particularly in borderline cases where the distinctions between grades are less clear. Continuous monitoring and feedback mechanisms are necessary to identify and address such issues in real-world applications.

Conclusions and future directions

Kidney cancer, particularly renal cell carcinoma (RCC), remains a significant global health concern with increasing incidence rates. Accurate classification and diagnosis of RCC subtypes, which account for approximately 85% of kidney cancer cases, are critical for improving clinical outcomes and personalizing therapies. Traditional morphological analysis, while foundational, is labor intensive, prone to variability, and lacks scalability for modern clinical environments. The integration of advanced machine learning and artificial intelligence tools offers transformative potential in enhancing the precision and efficiency of RCC diagnostics. Our proposed multiphase classification framework, which uses YOLOv8 for high-accuracy grading and GradCAM for interpretability, represents a significant advancement in automated RCC classification. By addressing challenges such as error propagation through the innovative “Avoid Error Propagation Layer”, the framework achieves remarkable performance metrics, including an accuracy of 97.51%, precision of 93.72%, recall of 93.28%, specificity of 98.32%, and F1 score of 93.35%. These results underscore the framework’s ability to balance diagnostic accuracy with transparency, fostering trust among clinicians and facilitating its adoption in clinical practice. The hybrid approach combining traditional histopathological analysis with cutting-edge AI not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also aligns with the principles of precision medicine. By enabling earlier and more accurate detection of RCC, this framework has the potential to guide more effective treatment plans and improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, its modular design ensures adaptability to diverse clinical workflows, making it a valuable tool for both specialized and resource-constrained settings.

Future directions

Future research should focus on several key areas to enhance the robustness, generalizability, and clinical utility of the proposed framework. First, external validation using multicenter datasets, such as TCGA-KIRC (The Cancer Genome Atlas Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma) and other similar repositories, is essential to ensure consistent performance across diverse populations and imaging protocols, as evaluating the model on data from multiple institutions will account for variations in staining techniques, equipment, and patient demographics, which is critical for real-world clinical applicability. Second, developing a lightweight version of the model through techniques such as knowledge distillation, quantization, or pruning is necessary for real-time deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as remote clinics, to enable accessibility and scalability without compromising accuracy.

Third, integrating uncertainty quantification mechanisms, such as Bayesian neural networks or ensemble-based approaches, can enhance reliability by providing probabilistic predictions, allowing clinicians to assess confidence levels and flag ambiguous cases for further review. Fourth, expanding the framework to include multimodal data integration (such as radiological imaging, genomic profiles, or clinical metadata) could improve diagnostic accuracy by utilizing complementary information and uncovering patterns not apparent in histopathology alone. Finally, continuous monitoring, updating, and retraining of the model are necessary to maintain its long-term effectiveness, with a feedback loop for clinicians to report misclassifications ensuring that iterative improvements are aligned with evolving clinical practices and new data.
