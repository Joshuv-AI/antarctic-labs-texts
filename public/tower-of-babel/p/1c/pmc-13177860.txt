Outcomes in Traumatic Brain Injury: Can Electroencephalography Predict the Unpredictable?
Authors: Atul Phillips
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to cite this article: Phillips A. Outcomes in Traumatic Brain Injury: Can Electroencephalography Predict the Unpredictable? Indian J Crit Care Med 2026;30(4):265–267.

The incidence of traumatic brain injury (TBI) has seen a disproportionate increase across India in contrast to global trends, signaling a public health emergency. Data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 showed an unprecedented 11.1% rise in the age-adjusted incidence rate of TBI (from 499 per 1,00,000 in 1990 to 554 per 1,00,000 in 2019), and a 22.4% increase in age-adjusted prevalence (from 818 per 1,00,000 in 1990 to 1,002 per 1,00,000 in 2019) over the same period in India.1 Severe TBI [defined as Glasgow coma scale (GCS) <8] accounts for approximately one-third of all head trauma cases in India, unevenly affecting males in younger age-groups, particularly between 10 and 24 years, which has serious socioeconomic consequences.2

The ancient Hippocratic aphorism, “No head injury is too severe to despair of, nor too trivial to ignore,” aptly captures the uncertainty and complexity faced by critical care physicians in prognosticating patients with severe TBI. Studies from high-income countries show that in severe TBI, nearly 86% of deaths in the intensive care unit (ICU) occur following the withdrawal of life-supporting therapies (WLST). In half of these patients, withdrawal occurred within 72 hours of injury.3 Conversely, in lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) like India, treatment is frequently discontinued and patients are discharged against medical advice due to perceived medical futility, poor prognosis, and lack of financial resources, resulting in postdischarge deaths.4

Yet, the natural history of severe TBI defies simplistic outcome prediction. Long-term follow-up studies suggest that with access to effective rehabilitative services and continued care, 74% of severe TBI patients are able to regain the ability to follow commands, and approximately 20% are able to live at home with supervision 5 years post-injury.5 This makes the stakes for the intensivist very high who are entrusted by surrogate decision-makers – usually family members – with prognostication to help them make informed decisions on the continuation of treatment or WLST.

Thus, as much as possible providing accurate neuroprognostication is critical for both intensivists and families as a means of potentially avoiding the self-fulfilling prophecy of mortality after TBI.6

In this context, the prospective, observational study by Sakrikar and colleagues is well-timed, addressing an important question in neuroprognostication in severe TBI.7 The authors systematically evaluated electroencephalographic reactivity (EEG-R) and bispectral index reactivity (BIS-R) in 40 patients admitted with severe TBI during the post-injury window of days 3–5. Electroencephalographic reactivity is defined as a transient change in baseline EEG activity (amplitude or frequency) following standardized auditory and painful stimuli, suggesting an intact neuronal network of thalamocortical loops and ascending arousal systems.8

In the published study, EEG-R was found to be absent in 22 (55%) and present in 18 (45%) patients. The absence of EEG-R was significantly associated with poorer neurological outcomes [Glasgow Outcome Scale - Extended (GOSE) below 3] at both 3 months (p = 0.001) and 6 months (p = 0.016). Six months mortality was significantly higher in the EEG nonreactive group as compared to the EEG reactive group (95 vs 62%, p = 0.04). Most strikingly, absent EEG-R predicted the non-recovery of consciousness at 3 months with 100% specificity and 81% overall accuracy.

Analysis of the quantitative EEG findings showed that absolute delta power was significantly elevated in the EEG-R absent group, while alpha, beta, and theta activity distributions remained similar between both groups. This neurophysiological pattern is suggestive of severe disruption of thalamocortical loops and cortical deafferentation—consistent with poor recovery potential.9 Electroencephalographic is a noninvasive, objective, real-time bedside tool that is widely accessible and relatively cost-effective, enhancing the intensivist's prognostic armamentarium by extending neuroprognostication beyond clinical examination and neuroimaging.

The study's negative findings are equally instructive. Bispectral index values are derived from processed EEG signals and provide a numerical representation of the depth of consciousness, which is widely used in operative settings. Bispectral index reactivity – defined as a ≥10-point increase from baseline within 20 seconds of stimulation was observed in 21 patients (52.5%) following painful stimulation. However, BIS-R showed no significant association with functional outcomes at 3 or 6 months (p = 0.06 and 0.27, respectively). Concordance between BIS-R and EEG-R was poor (kappa value was −0.24).

The discrepancy between EEG-R and BIS-R in predicting outcome reflects fundamental differences between multichannel EEG and processed frontal BIS signals. Bispectral index values are susceptible to movement-related EMG artifacts. Additionally, multichannel EEG is able to detect subtle changes in stimulus-induced responses from temporal and associative cortical networks that a single-channel frontal BIS signal may miss. Bispectral index values are averaged over 20–30 seconds, potentially diluting brief but clinically meaningful cortical responses that are detectable within the 10 seconds EEG-R window.

The Corticosteroid Randomization After Significant Head Injury (CRASH) trial and International Mission on Prognosis and Analysis of Clinical Trials (IMPACT) models are few of the prognostic tools commonly used in the prediction of TBI outcomes, derived from large datasets from high-income countries.10 Their performance in Indian settings highlights important limitations – while reasonably accurate for inpatient mortality prediction, they were found to be less reliable for prediction of for postdischarge mortality and long-term neurological outcome.11 EEG reactivity bridges the gap in prognostication by providing a functional assessment of neural networks when standard clinical exams are confounded by sedation or metabolic factors. It offers a practical, bedside tool for evaluating brain integrity in patients where neuroimaging is not feasible.

Neuroprognostication becomes particularly challenging in a subset of clinically unresponsive patients who demonstrate brain activation in response to motor command on EEG – a phenomenon termed cognitive motor dissociation (CMD).12 Estimates suggest that up to 14% of patients with acute brain injuries can have CMD, with profound implications for prognosis and clinical management. Patients with CMD showed distinctly better recovery trajectories and improved functional outcomes (GOS-E > 4) at 12 months (41%) when compared to those without CMD (10%).13 Additionally, shorter recovery times have been reported in patients with CMD with severe TBI (or subdural hematoma), which was an independent predictor of earlier time to recovery [HR 4.4 (95% CI: 1.4–14.0)].13 Severe TBI patients have shown delayed recovery of consciousness, with 59% of unconscious patients having regained consciousness by 1 year and 74% by 5 years.14 These figures underscore the danger of premature prognostication based solely on a clinical exam. Therefore, EEG-based assessment for CMD offers a suitable alternative in the ICU setting when there is non-availability and transportation for neuroimaging (functional magnetic resonance imaging) is logistically not feasible.

The authors demonstrate that EEG-R present in the acute post-injury period shows high specificity for the recovery of consciousness at 3 months. The study's strengths include systematic assessment in the acute-phase, a standardized stimulation protocol, blinded independent rating by experienced clinicians, and 6 months follow-up.

However, limitations in the study warrant careful consideration. Moderate inter-rater agreement (Fleiss kappa 0.38) highlights persistent reliability challenges, potentially stemming from the subjective interpretation of EEG reactivity patterns. The single-center design and modest sample size (n = 40) limit generalizability to other neurocritical care patients. Single time-point assessment cannot capture dynamic recovery trajectories—patients who are non-reactive at days 3–5 might develop reactivity later, and early reactivity may be lost with secondary clinical insults. The four patients (10%) lost to 6 months follow-up may introduce attrition bias, the treating physicians were not blinded to treatment so the potential for self-fulfilling prophecy is difficult to rule out despite blinded assessment of outcomes.

Future studies should validate these findings in larger cohort of patients, with serial EEG assessments being performed over extended durations at different time points, accounting for fluctuations in behavioral states. Integrating quantitative spectral matrix analyses with machine-learning models could boost the sensitivity for the detection of CMD. Moreover, blinded-clinical protocols in large randomized trials are essential to enhance reliability and ensure reproducibility before implementation in clinical practice.

Ultimately, the findings of Sakrikar and colleagues provide compelling evidence that EEG reactivity, assessable at the bedside with basic equipment, provides valuable prognostic information in severe TBI. Absent EEG reactivity predicting non-recovery at 3 months with 100% specificity is a tool complementing the clinical neurological examination. The way forward lies in multimodal neuroprognostication – integrating clinical neurological examination, EEG reactivity with neuroimaging, and serum biomarkers to enhance predictive accuracy. “Medicine is a discipline of informed probability.” When combined with serial evaluations in comatose patients, this approach will empower clinicians and families to navigate prognostic uncertainty while avoiding the premature withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy.
