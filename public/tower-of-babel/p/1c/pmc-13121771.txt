APR-246 drives ROS-dependent ferroptosis and apoptosis and enhances anti–PD-1 efficacy in bladder cancer
Authors: Chi Zhang, Senming Cao, Guineng Zeng, Yuhao Dong, Hongzhao Li, Xin Ma, Xu Zhang, Zhi Li, Yan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
TP53 is frequently mutated in bladder cancer and is associated with aggressive disease, yet actionable strategies that also improve responses to immune checkpoint blockade remain limited. Here we show that APR-246 (eprenetapopt), a mutant p53–reactivating agent, preferentially reduces viability in TP53-mutant bladder cancer cell lines and that its activity appears to be partially dependent on mutant p53. Mechanistically, APR-246 induces pronounced reactive oxygen species accumulation and engages ferroptosis and apoptosis in parallel, accompanied by reactivation of p53-associated transcriptional programmes. RNA sequencing and orthogonal validation further reveal increased expression and secretion of the T cell–recruiting chemokines CCL5 and CXCL10. In syngeneic subcutaneous and orthotopic bladder tumour models, APR-246 suppresses tumour growth and is associated with increased infiltration of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells and natural killer cells, though limited by sample size in animal models; This antitumour effect is attenuated in immunodeficient hosts. Notably, APR-246 augments the efficacy of anti–PD-1 therapy and further enriches effector immune infiltration. Together, these findings link mutant p53 targeting and redox-driven cell death to immune remodelling, providing a mechanistic rationale for combining APR-246 with PD-1 blockade in bladder cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44653-8.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is among the most common malignancies of the urinary tract, with initiation and progression driven by dysregulated molecular signalling networks1. Molecular epidemiological studies identify TP53 as one of the most frequently mutated somatic genes in bladder cancer; approximately 50% of patients harbour TP53 alterations, predominantly missense mutations2–4. TP53 orchestrates key programmes governing DNA damage responses, cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis and senescence5. TP53 mutation disables tumour-suppressive functions and may confer gain-of-function activities that promote proliferation, invasion and therapeutic resistance6. Clinicopathological evidence further indicates a higher prevalence of mutant p53 in muscle-invasive bladder cancer than in non–muscle-invasive disease, supporting an association between p53 aberrations, tumour progression and aggressive phenotypes7. Restoring mutant p53 activity has therefore emerged as a promising avenue for precision therapy in bladder cancer.

Bladder cancer has long been regarded as a partially immunogenic tumour8. Immunomodulatory approaches constitute standard care in non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer, and immune checkpoint blockade can induce durable responses in a subset of patients with advanced disease9,10. Yet the overall benefit remains limited at the population level. Primary refractoriness and acquired resistance are common, shaped by an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment and tumour-intrinsic variation in stress responses and cell-death programmes11. Combination strategies that couple direct tumour killing with immune microenvironment remodelling therefore represent a clear unmet clinical need.

APR-246 (eprenetapopt) is among the most extensively studied small molecules for reactivating mutant p5312–14. Its active metabolite forms reversible adducts with key cysteine residues in mutant p53, stabilising its conformation and partially restoring wild-type–like transcriptional activity, thereby re-engaging canonical tumour-suppressive pathways including p21, BAX and PUMA15,16. Beyond conformational rescue, APR-246 perturbs cellular redox homeostasis, interferes with glutathione metabolism, and increases intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)17,18. Under conditions of iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, ROS amplify lipid damage and can trigger or potentiate ferroptosis, a non-apoptotic form of cell death that may complement apoptosis-based strategies, particularly in tumours that evade canonical apoptotic control.

Ferroptosis is increasingly linked to anti-tumour immunity. In contrast to apoptosis, oxidative damage during ferroptosis may reshape antigen exposure and danger-signal release, influence dendritic-cell uptake and presentation, and ultimately tune T-cell responses19–22; ROS and lipid peroxidation within tumour cells can also remodel cytokine programmes and immunosuppressive circuits in the tumour microenvironment23–26. A phase II clinical study reported in 2022 described a complete response in a patient with bladder cancer treated with APR-246 plus pembrolizumab27, supporting the promise of combining APR-246 with immune checkpoint inhibition. However, mechanistic studies elucidating how APR-246 modulates tumour immunity in bladder cancer remain lacking.

Taken together, TP53 mutations are central to bladder cancer initiation, progression and prognosis. By reactivating mutant p53, inducing oxidative stress and potentially engaging immune-relevant cell-death pathways, APR-246 may offer a tractable therapeutic entry point for precision treatment. Elucidating its mechanism of action in bladder cancer cells should provide a clearer rationale and translatable biological cues for immunotherapy-based combinations.

Results

The cytotoxic activity of APR-246 in bladder cancer cells tracks with TP53 status

To define the genetic context of TP53 alterations in bladder cancer, we first examined TP53 mutation profiles in a public cohort. TP53 mutations were present in ~ 47% of cases (Fig. 1A) and were predominantly missense variants (69.1%; Fig. 1B). We then assessed APR-246 sensitivity in four human bladder cancer cell lines (UMUC3, EJ, T24, and SW780) and two mouse bladder cancer cell lines (MBT2 and MB49). UMUC3, EJ, T24, and MBT2 harbour TP53/Trp53 missense mutations, whereas SW780 and MB49 are wild-type.

APR-246 reduced cell viability across all lines tested, but sensitivity showed a genotype-associated pattern. Compared with TP53 wild-type SW780 cells, TP53-mutant EJ, T24, and UMUC3 cells displayed lower IC50 values (Fig. 1C). A similar trend was observed in murine cells, where Trp53-mutant MBT2 cells were more sensitive than Trp53 wild-type MB49 cells (Fig. 1D). These data suggest that APR-246 cytotoxicity is, at least in part, linked to TP53 status.

APR-246 responsiveness is partially dependent on mutant p53

To determine the contribution of mutant p53 to APR-246 efficacy, we generated TP53 knockdown models in UMUC3, EJ, and T24 cells and confirmed knockdown by immunoblotting (Fig. 2A). TP53 depletion reduced APR-246 sensitivity in all three cell lines, reflected by increased IC50 values (P = 0.0179; Fig. 2B).

We next performed a gain-of-function validation by ectopically expressing the common mutant p53R248S in UMUC3, EJ, and T24 cells, again confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 2C). Compared with controls, mutant p53 overexpression enhanced APR-246 responsiveness and decreased IC50 values (P = 0.0215; Fig. 2D). Together, these loss- and gain-of-function experiments indicate that APR-246 effects are partially dependent on mutant p53, while also implying an additional p53-independent component.

APR-246 induces ROS-dependent ferroptosis and apoptosis in parallel

We next dissected the mode of APR-246–induced cell death using pharmacological rescue with pathway-specific inhibitors, including ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1; ferroptosis inhibitor), N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC; ROS scavenger), Z-VAD (pan-caspase inhibitor), Nec-1 (necroptosis inhibitor), and 3-MA (autophagy inhibitor). In UMUC3 and EJ cells, APR-246 (20 µM, 24 h) markedly reduced viability. NAC provided the strongest protection (P < 0.05), whereas Fer-1 and Z-VAD partially rescued viability; Nec-1 and 3-MA had little effect (Fig. 3A). The partial rescue observed with single inhibitors suggests that APR-246 engages a multimodal cell death program. Given that ROS accumulation serves as an upstream driver for both ferroptosis and apoptosis, we propose that APR-246 concurrently induces these modalities via ROS upregulation. Consistently, combined treatment with Fer-1 and Z-VAD afforded significantly greater protection against APR-246-induced cell death than either agent alone in UMUC3 and EJ cells(Fig. 3B). Additionally, pretreatment with the iron chelator DFO significantly rescued cell viability, further confirming the iron-dependent component of cell death (Fig. 3B).

Consistently, ROS staining showed a robust increase in intracellular ROS after APR-246 treatment, which was effectively suppressed by NAC pre-treatment (Fig. 3C). Given the established links between ROS accumulation and both ferroptosis and apoptosis28–30, we examined marker proteins in each pathway. APR-246 reduced the expression of the ferroptosis-associated proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4 (Fig. 3D), consistent with ferroptotic engagement, and increased cleaved PARP (Fig. 3E), consistent with concomitant activation of apoptosis. Both signatures were partially attenuated by NAC. Lipid peroxidation analysis provided additional support for ferroptosis: the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe showed increased lipid peroxidation in UMUC3 cells following APR-246 treatment (Fig. 3F). Collectively, APR-246-induced cell death is driven by ROS-dependent oxidative stress that engages ferroptosis and apoptosis in parallel.

Transcriptomic and functional validation indicate reactivation of p53 downstream programs and induction of chemokines

To obtain a global view of APR-246–driven molecular responses, we performed RNA-seq on UMUC3 cells treated with APR-246 or vehicle. Differential expression and clustering analyses showed robust upregulation of canonical p53 target genes, including PUMA, P21, and IGFBP7, alongside marked induction of the chemokines CCL5 and CXCL10 (Fig. 4A). KEGG enrichment highlighted p53 signaling pathway, Ferroptosis, Apoptosis - multiple species, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 4B), consistent with restoration of p53-associated transcriptional output coupled to activation of immune-related programmes31.

We validated these findings in UMUC3 and EJ cells. RT–qPCR confirmed upregulation of P21, PUMA, IGFBP7, CCL5, and CXCL10 transcripts (Fig. 4C). Immunoblotting showed increased protein levels of P21, PUMA, and IGFBP7 (Fig. 4D). ELISA further demonstrated elevated secretion of CCL5 and CXCL10 into the culture supernatant (Fig. 4E). Notably, CCL5 and CXCL10 are well-characterized mediators of T cell and NK cell recruitment through CCR5 and CXCR3 signaling axes, respectively32–34. Together, these data indicate that APR-246 not only re-engages p53 downstream programmes but also increases chemokine output associated with immune cell recruitment.

APR-246 suppresses tumour growth in vivo and is accompanied by increased effector immune infiltration

Given the induction of CCL5/CXCL10 in vitro, we next evaluated whether these signals were associated with in vivo responses. In MBT2 cells, APR-246 similarly increased Ccl5 and Cxcl10 secretion (Fig. 5A). We then established MBT2 subcutaneous tumours in syngeneic C3H mice and treated animals with APR-246(Fig. 5B). Treatment efficacy was evaluated in 5 mice per group, with a balanced sex distribution. Body weight monitoring indicated that the regimen was generally well tolerated (Fig. S1B). APR-246 significantly restrained tumour growth and reduced endpoint tumour weight compared with controls (Fig. 5C).

Immune profiling revealed increased effector infiltration in treated tumours. Flow cytometry showed higher numbers of intratumoural CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and NK cells (Fig. 5D). Multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed enrichment of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells at the tissue level (Fig. 5E). To evaluate responses in a more physiologically relevant setting, we established a luciferase-labelled orthotopic MBT2 bladder tumour model and monitored tumour burden by bioluminescence. APR-246 reduced tumour signal in this model as well (Fig. 5F). These data demonstrate a clear in vivo antitumour effect of APR-246, accompanied by increased infiltration of effector immune cells.

APR-246 potentiates immune checkpoint blockade

To dissect the contribution of the host immune system to APR-246-mediated tumor suppression, we compared therapeutic efficacy in immunocompetent syngeneic mice versus immunodeficient nude mice. While APR-246 exhibited direct cytotoxicity in vitro, its in vivo efficacy was significantly attenuated in immunodeficient hosts (Fig. 6A). Tumor growth inhibition in nude mice was markedly reduced compared to syngeneic controls, despite identical dosing schedules. This discrepancy suggests that while direct cell killing occurs, the adaptive immune system is associated with the maximal antitumor response observed with APR-246 treatment.

Because APR-246 increased intratumoural T-cell infiltration, we next evaluated its potential to enhance anti–PD-1 therapy. In the C3H/MBT2 model, mice were assigned to vehicle, APR-246 monotherapy, anti–PD-1 monotherapy, or combination therapy (Fig. 6B). Body weight remained stable across groups, suggesting acceptable tolerability (Fig. 6C). The combination achieved superior tumour control compared with anti–PD-1 alone (Fig. 6D). Flow cytometry further showed that CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and NK cells were more abundant in tumours from the combination group than from either monotherapy group (Fig. 6E). Consistent effects were observed in the orthotopic model by bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 6F). Together, these findings indicate that APR-246 can augment immune checkpoint blockade, supporting its potential as an immunotherapy-sensitising partner.

Discussion

This study centres on TP53 mutations, a frequent molecular event in bladder cancer, and delineates both the anti-tumour activity of APR-246 and its immunological consequences. Analyses of public cohorts confirmed that TP53 alterations are common in bladder cancer, and experiments across multiple human and murine bladder cancer cell lines showed that APR-246 cytotoxicity stratifies with TP53 status, with TP53-mutant cells displaying greater sensitivity. Mechanistically, APR-246 induced a pronounced accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) accompanied by re-engagement of p53 downstream transcriptional programmes (including P21, PUMA and IGFBP7), followed by concurrent activation of ferroptosis and apoptosis and induction of the chemokines CCL5 and CXCL10. In vivo, APR-246 suppressed tumour growth and increased the abundance of tumour-infiltrating CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells and NK cells. Notably, combining APR-246 with anti-PD-1 further strengthened tumour control and increased effector immune infiltration, supporting its translational potential as an immunotherapy-sensitising agent.

A key question is whether tumor regression is driven primarily by direct cytotoxicity or immune-mediated clearance. Our data suggests a dual mechanism: APR-246 directly induces tumor cell death via ROS-dependent pathways, yet the significantly reduced efficacy in immunodeficient mice indicates that immune-mediated mechanisms are indispensable for full therapeutic potency. This aligns with the observed increase in CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and chemokine secretion. The comparative model data provide supportive evidence consistent with an immune-dependent component of APR-246’s in vivo activity. Future studies employing specific cell subset depletion will further delineate the relative contributions of direct versus immune-mediated effects.

These findings support a model in which tumour-intrinsic stress is coupled to immune microenvironmental remodelling. In a TP53-mutant context, APR-246 is likely to promote a wild-type-like p53 functional output while simultaneously perturbing redox homeostasis, thereby initiating ROS-driven cell death programmes that engage ferroptosis and apoptosis in parallel. This dual action would be expected to compromise tumour cell survival while also potentially influencing immune signalling outputs that may contribute to immune-cell trafficking. The stronger anti-tumour effects observed in immunocompetent hosts, together with synergy with PD-1 blockade, suggest that APR-246 does not act solely through tumour-intrinsic targeting; instead, it may be integrated into the anti-tumour immune response—an interaction that may contribute to improving checkpoint blockade efficacy.

Our interpretation aligns with prevailing views of the mechanism of action of APR-246 (eprenetapopt): its activity extends beyond restoring wild-type p53 function and includes induction of oxidative stress through disruption of thiol metabolism and redox balance, thereby engaging programmed cell death18,35–37. An expanding body of work indicates that ferroptosis and other ROS-associated death pathways can exert immunomodulatory effects, with downstream consequences for chemokine production, antigen presentation and immune-cell recruitment24,38. The observed induction of CCL5/CXCL10 together with increased T/NK infiltration is consistent with this concept in bladder cancer39. Reports that wild-type p53 can enhance innate immune pathways suggest that functional reprogramming of mutant p53 by APR-246 may contribute to immune activation40,41. Caution remains warranted, as the dominant mechanism of APR-246 may shift across tumour types, TP53 mutational spectra and therapeutic contexts; the magnitude and reproducibility of the immunotherapy-sensitising phenotype may depend on baseline immune infiltration, oxidative-stress thresholds and mutant p53 conformational heterogeneity.

The role of ROS in the tumor microenvironment is complex and context-dependent. While high levels of ROS can lead to T cell exhaustion and immune evasion42,43, moderate ROS accumulation has been shown to trigger immunogenic cell death and enhance chemokine production44. Our data suggests that APR-246 induces a level of ROS stress that is sufficient to trigger ferroptosis and release DAMPs/chemokines without completely abrogating the function of infiltrating lymphocytes20. This balance may be key to the observed synergy with anti-PD-1 therapy, although future studies should carefully monitor ROS levels to avoid potential immunosuppressive effects.

These results suggest a tractable strategy for improving response rates and durability of immunotherapy in bladder cancer. Although PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have become a mainstay for advanced disease, primary and acquired resistance remain common45–47. APR-246 could address this unmet need through two complementary mechanisms. First, in TP53-mutant tumours it may enable a more precise tumour-intrinsic attack, increasing the depth of tumour cell killing. Second, p53 functional restoration together with ROS-driven ferroptosis may be associated with amplified chemokine output and increased effector immune-cell infiltration, which could potentially contribute to enhanced PD-1 blockade responses through immune microenvironmental changes. In this view, APR-246 is not only a candidate targeted agent but also a mechanistic bridge between tumour-intrinsic stress responses and immunotherapeutic efficacy. Given the high prevalence of TP53 mutations in bladder cancer, a translational path based on TP53 stratification and combining APR-246 with PD-1 blockade appears both population-relevant and clinically feasible.

While our study focuses on immune checkpoint blockade, it is worth noting that the APR-246-induced ROS accumulation and ferroptosis described here may also contribute to radiosensitization. Previous studies have shown that mutant p53 reactivation combined with ROS elevation can impair DNA damage repair pathways, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to radiation48. The lipid peroxidation associated with ferroptosis further amplifies radiation-induced oxidative stress17,49. Thus, the mechanism identified in our work may have broader implications for combination strategies involving radiotherapy.

Our data reveal that APR-246 transcriptionally upregulates CCL5 and CXCL10 in tumor cells, which correlates with enhanced effector immune cell infiltration in vivo. CCL5 and CXCL10 are well-established drivers of CD8⁺ T cell and NK cell trafficking into the tumor microenvironment via CCR5 and CXCR3, respectively32–34,50, and their upregulation has been associated with favorable immunological responses in multiple cancer types. While our current study does not include chemokine neutralization or receptor blockade experiments to directly establish causality, the concordance between in vitro chemokine induction and in vivo immune remodeling is consistent with this mechanistic framework. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the present work; future studies employing antibody-mediated neutralization of CCL5/CXCL10 or pharmacological blockade of CCR5/CXCR3 in relevant in vivo models will be important to formally validate this proposed axis and delineate its contribution to the antitumor immune response elicited by APR-246.

Beyond cell death-mediated release, APR-246-induced ROS accumulation may also contribute to chemokine production through direct inflammatory signaling. Elevated ROS levels are known to activate redox-sensitive transcription factors, such as NF-κB, which may potentially contribute to CCL5 and CXCL10 upregulation independent of cell lysis44,51. This ROS-mediated inflammatory pathway may act in parallel with immunogenic cell death to amplify immune cell recruitment. While our data highlights the correlation between ferroptosis markers and chemokine induction, this alternative mechanism underscores the complexity of ROS-driven immune remodeling and warrants further investigation to delineate the relative contributions of cell death-dependent and independent pathways.

Several limitations warrant consideration. TP53 mutations are heterogeneous; although we validated dependence using multiple cell lines and p53R248S as a representative mutant, systematic comparisons across hotspot classes (for example, conformational versus DNA-binding domain mutants) will be required to refine patient stratification. ROS was positioned as a key upstream node, yet its dominant sources, the temporal ordering between ferroptosis and apoptosis, and the relative contribution of each death programme across dose and time windows remain to be resolved. The immune microenvironmental evidence currently rests on chemokine induction and increased immune infiltration; functional tests—such as chemokine-axis blockade or immune-cell depletion—will be necessary to establish whether the CCL5/CXCL10 axis and specific effector subsets are required for the combination benefit.

While our experimental design incorporated equal numbers of male and female mice with randomized allocation to minimize bias, the sample size within each sex subgroup restricts the statistical power to definitively resolve sex-specific treatment effects. Host sex represents a critical biological variable in tumor immunity; Besançon et al. (2022) recently highlighted significant sex-associated disparities in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and checkpoint marker expression in the MBT-2 model, consistent with broader observations of sex-dependent tumor growth dynamics in the MB49 system52. Although our aggregated data demonstrate robust therapeutic efficacy across a mixed-sex cohort, these precedents indicate that sex-specific immune mechanisms may modulate response. Consequently, future validation in larger, sex-stratified cohorts will be essential to fully elucidate these interactions and optimize translational potential. While our study utilized orthotopic models to assess therapeutic efficacy, detailed immune profiling was performed using the subcutaneous model. We acknowledge that the orthotopic microenvironment may exhibit distinct immune characteristics compared to subcutaneous tumors. Therefore, the immune mechanisms described here, while robustly supported by the syngeneic subcutaneous data, warrant further validation through immune phenotyping within orthotopic tumors in future studies to ensure full translational relevance. Finally, although the animal models provide proof of concept(n = 5 mice per group in the therapeutic studies), the treatment history, baseline immunity and ecological complexity of human bladder cancer are more variable, underscoring the need for patient-derived material and more clinically proximate models. Even with these caveats, the convergence of multi-layered evidence supports the central conclusion that APR-246 has immunotherapy-sensitising potential.

In summary, APR-246 shows enhanced anti-tumour activity in TP53-mutant bladder cancer, acting in part through mutant p53 and engaging ROS-driven ferroptosis and apoptosis. These tumour-intrinsic stress programmes are accompanied by induction of CCL5/CXCL10 and an increase in tumour-infiltrating CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells and NK cells. In syngeneic models, these microenvironmental changes coincide with improved therapeutic activity when APR-246 is combined with PD-1 checkpoint blockade. Collectively, our findings provide bladder cancer–specific support for APR-246 as an immunotherapy-sensitising partner and motivate further studies to causally define the chemokine axis and effector subsets that underpin combination benefit, and to optimise patient selection in TP53-altered disease.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Human bladder cancer cell lines SW780 (TP53 wild type), EJ (TP53 missense mutant) and UMUC3 (TP53 missense mutant), as well as HEK293T cells, were obtained from ATCC. The human bladder cancer cell line T24 (TP53 missense mutant) was purchased from Saibai Kang Bio (Shanghai, China). Mouse bladder cancer cell lines MB49 (Trp53 wild type) and MBT2 (Trp53 missense mutant) were obtained from CTCC (Zhejiang, China). All cell lines have been authenticated by STR profiling. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be mycoplasma negative. UMUC3, SW780, HEK293T and MB49 cells were cultured in DMEM. EJ and MBT2 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640. T24 cells were cultured in MEM. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cat. 164210, Wuhan Procell), 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Cat. PB180120, Wuhan Procell). In cell-based experiments, APR-246 was dissolved in DMSO.

Mouse strains

C3H/HeJ mice (5–6 weeks old) and BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Beiyou Biotechnology (Beijing, China). Sex was not considered as an experimental variable. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. In animal experiments, APR-246 was dissolved in PBS, Anti-PD-1 was dissolved in ddH₂O. Ethical approval. All animal experiments were conducted under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (AP: GPTBJ-102). This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition).

Sex as a biological variable

Both male and female mice were included in all animal experiments, but sex was not considered as a biological variable in downstream statistical analyses. In the animal experiments of this study, an equal number of male and female mice (10 or 20 males) were used for each experiment. After mixing all the mice, they were randomly assigned to each group (n = 5 per group).

Plasmid construction and lentiviral production

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting human TP53 were synthesised by Beijing BoMaide and cloned into the pLKO vector. Lentivirus was packaged in HEK293T cells by co-transfection of the lentiviral vector with the packaging plasmids PAX2 and VSVG. Supernatants were collected 48 h after transfection and concentrated overnight at 4 °C using a lentivirus concentration reagent (GenStar, China). To generate stable cell lines, cells were infected with lentivirus for 48 h and then selected with puromycin or the corresponding selection antibiotic for 1 week. shRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Generation of MBT-2-Luc Cells

To generate stably bioluminescent MBT-2 cells, the lentiviral transfer vector encoding firefly luciferase (pLenti-CMV-Luc-Neo) was purchased from Beijing Biomade Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Lentiviral packaging and concentration were performed as described above. MBT-2 cells were seeded at 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates 24 h before transduction. Concentrated lentiviral particles were added to the culture medium supplemented with polybrene (8 µg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) to enhance transduction efficiency. After 8 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were returned to culture for an additional 48 h. Stable integrants were subsequently selected by culturing in medium containing G418 (Geneticin; 500 µg/ml; Gibco) for 14 days, with medium refreshed every 3 days. Surviving colonies were expanded and pooled to generate a polyclonal stable cell line designated MBT-2-Luc.

Luciferase expression was validated by bioluminescence imaging. Cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in white 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. D-luciferin potassium salt (10 mg/ml; Lablead, China) was added directly to the culture medium at a final concentration of 150 µg/ml, and bioluminescence was measured immediately using an IVIS Lumina imaging system (PerkinElmer) with an exposure time of 45s. MBT-2-Luc cells exhibited robust and uniform bioluminescence signals, confirming stable luciferase integration and sustained transgene expression. Parental MBT-2 cells treated identically served as a negative control and produced no detectable signal above background.

Bioinformatics analysis of TP53 mutations

TP53 mutation data were obtained from the MSK and TCGA public databases via cBioPortal, including 4543 bladder cancer patients. The inclusion criteria were primary bladder cancer patients with available tumor tissue gene mutation testing results. TP53 mutations were classified as missense, truncating, inframe, splice, and fusion. The TP53 mutation type was determined based on somatic mutation results, and the number of mutations in each category was counted to calculate the mutation frequency.

IC50 assay

Bladder cancer cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2,000 cells per well). Cells were treated with serial dilutions of APR-246 for 24 h, followed by addition of 10 µl CCK-8 reagent. Plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Each condition was tested in triplicate wells and experiments were repeated three times.

Cell-death rescue assay

Bladder cancer cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2,000 cells per well). Cells were treated for 24 h with APR-246 (20 µM) or vehicle (DMSO), in the absence or presence of the indicated cell-death inhibitors. CCK-8 reagent (10 µl) was then added and plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark before measuring absorbance at 450 nm. Each condition was tested in triplicate wells and experiments were repeated three times. Each independent experiment was performed with four technical replicates.

ROS staining

Bladder cancer cells were seeded into 24-well plates (10,000 cells per well). After treatment with APR-246 (20 µM) for 24 h, cells were incubated with diluted DCFH-DA ROS probe at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark, followed by replacement with fresh medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. Each condition was tested in triplicate wells and experiments were repeated three times. For each biological experiment, five fields were selected to quantify ROS fluorescence intensity.

RT–qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using a commercial total RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesised from 1 µg RNA using a qPCR reverse-transcription premix. Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR chemistry on a Bio-Rad CFX 9600 system to determine relative transcript abundance. Each condition was analysed in triplicate and experiments were repeated three times. Primer sequences for target genes and the internal reference gene are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

Cells subjected to the indicated treatments were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF on ice for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30–50 µg) were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C with gentle agitation. Primary antibodies included anti-SLC7A11 (Cat. 98051 S), anti-GPX4 (Cat. 52455 S) and anti-PARP (Cat. 9532 S) (all from Cell Signaling Technology, USA); anti-TP53 (Cat. 14681-1-AP), anti-P21 (Cat. 10355-1-AP) and anti-IGFBP7 (Cat. 19961-1-AP) (all from Proteintech, Wuhan, China); and anti-PUMA (Cat. 4976, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Membranes were washed three times with TBST (5 min each), incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and developed using ECL chemiluminescence reagents. Signals were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. Experiments were repeated three times.

Lipid peroxidation assay

Lipid peroxidation was assessed using a BODIPY 581/591 C11 kit (Beyotime, China). A staining working solution (2 µM) was prepared by diluting 1 µl BODIPY 581/591 C11 (2 mM) into 1 ml PBS under light-protected conditions. For each well of a 6-well plate, 1 ml working solution was used. Culture medium was removed and cells were washed twice with PBS, followed by incubation with 1 ml staining solution for 20 min at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator. Cells were washed twice with PBS and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Reduced BODIPY 581/591 C11 exhibits red fluorescence (excitation 581 nm, emission 591 nm); upon lipid peroxidation, red fluorescence decreases and green fluorescence increases with a shift to ~ 488 nm excitation and ~ 510 nm emission. Lipid peroxidation was evaluated by comparing red and green fluorescence intensities.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected from 6-well plates. Cytokine/chemokine levels were quantified using human and mouse CCL5 ELISA kits (Abcam, USA) and human and mouse CXCL10 ELISA kits (Elabscience, USA), following the manufacturers’ protocols. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm and concentrations were calculated from optical density values. Samples were analyzed in technical duplicates. Data represent n = 3 biological replicates.

RNA-seq and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

RNA-seq was performed on UMUC3 cells due to its representative TP53 mutation status and response to APR-246. Total RNA was extracted using the MJZol Total RNA Extraction Kit (Majorbio, China). Sequencing libraries were constructed from 1 µg of total RNA per sample using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). High-throughput sequencing was performed by Majorbio Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw data were processed and analyzed via the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with thresholds of |log₂ fold change| > 1.5 and adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) to evaluate biological functions associated with interferon-I signaling.

Subcutaneous tumour model and treatment

Subcutaneous tumour experiments were performed in 6–8-week-old female C3H mice housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), and ad libitum access to food and water. MBT2 cells were harvested during logarithmic growth phase, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium at a final concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. A total of 1 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of each mouse using a 26-gauge needle on day 0. Cell viability was confirmed to exceed 95% by trypan blue exclusion prior to inoculation. No postoperative analgesia was required as assessed by the institutional veterinary guidelines for procedures scoring low on the pain severity scale. Tumour-bearing mice were randomized into treatment groups (n = 5 per group) using a random number table when tumour volumes reached 50–100 mm³ (approximately day 5–6 post-inoculation). Investigators responsible for tumour measurement and endpoint assessment were blinded to treatment allocation. Once tumours became palpable, tumour length (L) and width (W) were measured every 2 days using digital calipers, and tumour volume was calculated as V = L × W² × 0.5. Starting on day 4 post-inoculation, mice received daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of either vehicle control (5% DMSO in PBS) or APR-246 at a dose of 50 mg/kg body weight. For combination immunotherapy, anti–PD-1 antibody (Bio X Cell, clone RMP1-14, BE0241, USA) was administered intraperitoneally starting on day 5 post-inoculation at 10 mg/kg body weight every 3 days. Animals were monitored every 2 days for body weight, general health status, and tumour progression throughout the study. Humane endpoints were predefined as tumour volume exceeding 2000 mm³, tumour ulceration, or body weight loss > 20% from baseline. The study was terminated on day 28 post-inoculation, or earlier if humane endpoints were reached. No animals were excluded from the analysis.

Orthotopic bladder tumour model and bioluminescence imaging

Mice (C3H/HeJ) were anaesthetised by isoflurane and placed supine. A 1-cm midline incision was made above the pelvis, and the skin, fat and rectus abdominis muscle were dissected to expose the bladder. Luciferase-labelled MBT2 cells were resuspended in PBS (5 × 105 cells per mouse) and mixed with 50% Matrigel (Lablead, China). Cells were injected into the bladder wall using an insulin syringe. The bladder was returned to the abdominal cavity, the incision was sutured, and mice recovered on a warming pad. Perioperative analgesia was administered (buprenorphine 0.05 mg/kg, s.c. every 12 h for 48 h). Mice were monitored daily for signs of haematuria, distress, locomotor activity and body weight.Fourteen days later, bioluminescence imaging was performed after intraperitoneal injection of 200 µl D-luciferin potassium salt (15 mg/ml; Lablead, China) using a real-time imaging system (Berthold LB983, Germany). No animals were excluded. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition). Euthanasia: Mice were euthanized following the AVMA 2020 Guidelines. When tumor volumes reached predefined humane endpoints, animals were placed in an induction chamber and anesthetized with 5% isoflurane until loss of consciousness. Anesthesia was then maintained at a high concentration until respiration ceased. Cervical dislocation was subsequently performed to confirm death. All procedures were approvedby the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (AP: GPTBJ-102).

Flow cytometry of tumour-infiltrating immune cells

Tumors were harvested from mice at the study endpoint. For each treatment group, five mice (n = 5) were included for flow cytometric analysis. Tumours were minced and digested in DNase I and collagenase IV (Sigma, USA) at 37 °C for 60 min. Digestion was quenched with complete medium, and samples were filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (BIOFIL, China) to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis solution (Beyotime, China). Cells were stained for 30 min at room temperature in the dark with BV510 anti-mouse CD45 (BioLegend, 103137, USA), BV605 anti-mouse CD3 (BioLegend, 100237, USA), Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse CD4 (BioLegend, 100423, USA), APC/Fire 750 anti-mouse CD8a (BioLegend, 100766, USA) and APC anti-mouse NK1.1 (BioLegend, 108710, USA). Data were acquired by flow cytometry and analysed using FlowJo. Each data point in the figures represents an individual biological replicate (one mouse).

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

Tumours were fixed overnight in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin. Section (4 μm) were deparaffinised and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed in 10 mmol l⁻¹ sodium citrate buffer using microwave heating for 20 min, followed by gradual cooling to room temperature. Sections were permeabilised with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min and blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-mouse CD8a (Cell Signaling Technology, 98941, USA) and anti-mouse CD4 (Cell Signaling Technology, 25229, USA). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, counterstained with DAPI, and mounted. Imaging was performed using a CLSM 600 confocal laser scanning microscope (Sunnystoptop, China). Five fields per tissue were analysed, and T-cell numbers were quantified for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± s.d. Two-group comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for comparisons between each treatment group and the control group. Statistical significance was assessed using GraphPad Prism. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).
