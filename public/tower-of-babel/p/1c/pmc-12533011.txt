Therapeutic potential of Shigella phage SSG23 against Shigella sonnei biofilms and in BALB/c mice
Authors: Payel Mondal, Sanjib Das, Anaswara Ramesh, Arpita Sarbajna, Soumalya Banerjee, Subhadip Bhaumik, Hemanta Koley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Multidrug-resistant (MDR) Shigella spp. threaten global health, renewing interest in phage therapy. Here, we report the isolation and characterization of a novel lytic phage, SSG23, effective against four major Shigella serovars. Phage SSG23 possesses a prolate head and contractile tail, consistent with the Straboviridae family, and remains stable across a broad pH (4–11) and temperature range (−20 °C to 50 °C). It exhibits a latent period of 37 minutes, a burst size of 195 plaque-forming units per cell, and a 168,820 bp dsDNA genome with 40.8% GC content lacking resistance or toxin genes. SSG23 efficiently degrades biofilms, alone or with antibiotics, and is non-toxic to macrophages in vitro. In S. sonnei-infected BALB/c mice, oral administration reduced bacterial colonization and shedding while improving health without evidence of resistance emergence. Neutralizing antibodies developed in mice, yet the phage maintained its efficacy. These findings support SSG23 as a promising candidate for phage therapy against Shigella infection.

Introduction

Shigella species are the most prevalent cause of shigellosis, an acute, bloody diarrhea (dysentery), and contribute a significant proportion of the burden of mortality and morbidity related to diarrheal diseases worldwide1. The Shigella genus includes four species: Shigella flexneri, S. dysenteriae, S. boydii, and S. sonnei2. Shigella spp. are the most widespread and dangerous enteric infections globally, accounting for around 165 million cases yearly, of which approximately 163 million occur in underdeveloped nations and cause over one million deaths3. Shigellosis still has a high rate of morbidity and mortality, especially in children and young people, even if fewer fatality rates have been reported4. The primary transmission source is the consumption of contaminated food and drink by the fecal-oral route5,6.

Over the last few decades, significant effort has been invested in developing vaccines, and some effective treatments have been discovered. Nonetheless, the absence of shared immune protective epitopes among Shigella serogroups and serotypes presents a significant obstacle to vaccine development, restricting the widespread effectiveness of novel vaccinations7,8. Shigellosis can be treated with antibiotics; however, in many Asian nations, particularly India, resistance has developed to the commonly prescribed medications, such as azithromycin, fluoroquinolones, and Ciprofloxacin6,7,9. According to a recent WHO report, the prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria is rising in both industrialized and developing nations, endangering access to affordable and effective treatment and raising the risk of morbidity and mortality from various bacterial infections, including shigellosis10. Consequently, it is critically necessary to adopt alternative strategies for reducing the prevalence and severity of shigellosis. A potential method is to use bacteriophages that can eradicate Shigella within the gastrointestinal tract during the early phases of shigellosis11.

Bacteriophages are viruses that are perhaps the oldest and most prevalent organisms on Earth and that can infect and kill their host bacterial cells12. Unlike antibiotics, bacteriophages are a possible agent for therapeutic applications due to their ubiquitous nature, host specificity, safety, antibacterial activity, and surface disinfection capacity13. Phage therapy has gained attention in treating antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections with positive clinical outcomes. In a short duration, lytic bacteriophages may infect and destroy microorganisms. Thus, lytic phages are effective biocontrol agents providing a possible substitute for traditional antibiotic therapy in the treatment of bacterial illnesses14.

The first therapeutic application of bacteriophages in humans was for the treatment of shigellosis. In the 1930s and 1940s, Shigella phages were first used for treating shigellosis in the former Soviet Union and were often utilized by both military and civilian healthcare professionals15. A trial showed that phage administration reduced dysentery incidence, particularly in children under nine11. However, recent reviews suggest these studies were not rigorous and lacked biochemical and physiological analysis. Thus, the present study reports the isolation and detailed characterization of novel lytic bacteriophage SSG23. We also demonstrate the possibility of using phage as an alternative against Shigella infection by establishing its ability to eliminate S. sonnei bacteria successfully and establishing the safety of short and long-term oral administration of Shigella phage into infected BALB/c mice.

Results

Antibiotic resistance profile and biofilm production of S. sonnei

The antimicrobial resistance patterns of S. sonnei showed a higher degree of resistance to ampicillin (Amp), followed by cotrimoxazole (Co), furoxone (Fx), erythromycin (E 15), nalidixic acid (NA 30), sparfloxacin (Sc), and ciprofloxacin (CIP). However, it was intermediate to azithromycin (AZM 15) and gentamicin (GEN 10), and it was sensitive to chloramphenicol (C 30), ceftriaxone (CTR 30), doxycycline (DO), ofloxacin (OF2), and tetracycline (TE 30) (Table S1).

Assessment of phage stability

The pH stability of phage SSG23 was investigated across various Shigella spp. Phage SSG23 exhibited the greatest activity at pH 7.0 after an hour of incubation, along with great stability throughout a wide pH range (4 to 11) in both acidic and alkaline environments (Fig. 1d). The phage displayed minimal reduction in titer within the near-neutral range (pH 6.0 and 8.0), indicating optimal stability under physiological conditions. At mildly acidic (pH 5.0) and mildly alkaline (pH 9.0–10.0) conditions, infectivity remained high, whereas a moderate decline was observed at pH 4.0 and pH 11.0. SSG23 was completely inactivated in extreme acidic (pH ≤3.0) or alkaline (pH ≥12.0) conditions.

Furthermore, phage SSG23 exhibited outstanding stability across a broad temperature range. The phage maintained high infectivity at −20, 4, 25, and 37 °C, with no significant reduction in titer after 1 h of incubation (Fig. 1e). At 40 °C, infectivity remained relatively stable, whereas a moderate decline in phage titer was observed at 50 °C. However, exposure to 60 °C and above resulted in a marked loss of infectivity, indicating thermal sensitivity at elevated temperatures. These findings demonstrate that SSG23 is substantially stable at physiologically relevant and commonly used storage temperatures, but prolonged exposure to high heat leads to rapid inactivation.

MOI, one-step growth curve, and adsorption assay of phage SSG23

The bacteriolytic effect of phage SSG23 was examined using an early-phase culture of S. sonnei. The OD600 values in the uninfected control culture increased during the incubation phase. Conversely, at MOIs of 0.1, 1, and 10, the growth of bacteria infected with SSG23 was inhibited, with an MOI of 10 showing an optimal level of inhibition (Fig. 1f).

A one-step growth test was employed to examine the lytic development within cells and the growth kinetics of the phage SSG23 on different Shigella spp. including host bacteria (Fig. 1g). The latent period/ burst size for S. sonnei, S. boydii, S. flexneri 2a, and S. dysenteriae1 was found to be 30 min/195, 20 min/224, 35 min/121, 35 min/105 pfu, respectively (Table S2). Furthermore, Shigella spp. exhibited fast adsorption within the first 10 min, followed by a slower adsorption phase (Fig. 1h).

Phage SSG23 (PQ351811) exhibited strong homology with four T4-like Krischvirus phages: Ec_Makalu_001 (MN894885), Sf20 (MF327006), Jk32 (MK962753), and Brandy (MZ504876)18–21. The four main modules that comprised the anticipated functions of 111 ORFs were the DNA metabolism and replication module (22.61%), host cell lysis modules (1.41%), genome packaging and assembly module (1.06%), and structural proteins (14.13%) (Fig. 3b). The ORFs of SSG23 were widely distributed throughout the genome, as shown in Fig. 3c and Table S3 lists the gene annotation of the shown ORFs. A total of 283 ORFs were identified, of which 172 (60.78%) were designated hypothetical proteins, and only 111 (39.22%) were expected to encode functional proteins. Furthermore, the genome of phage SSG23 did not include any homologous sequences that were remarkably similar to existing phage virulence factors or antimicrobial-resistant genes (ARGs).

A typical eubacterial phage lysis cassette comprises two or more proteins, primarily holin, endolysin, and Rz-like spanin proteins, which are crucial for efficient phage progeny release22,23. A membrane-disrupting protein called holin (ORF 227) forms pores in the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, allowing endolysin (ORF 101) to degrade the peptidoglycan layer and cause cell wall rupture. Furthermore, the Rz-like spanin proteins (ORF 261 and ORF 262) are essential for rupturing the outer membrane of bacteria and ensuring complete cell lysis. These proteins enhance phage dispersion and infectivity through a precisely timed lytic process.

A set of essential proteins, including the portal protein, short terminase subunit (TerS), and large terminase subunit (TerL), aids in genome packaging in the Straboviridae phage SSG23. These proteins are essential for DNA cleavage, translocation, and encapsidation into the viral capsid24,25. The TerL (ORF 49) functions as the primary motor that promotes the encapsulation of the viral genome by providing the energy required to move the DNA through the portal by ATP-dependent DNA cleavage and translocation. In contrast, TerS (ORF 50) enhances the capabilities of TerL by facilitating the recognition of the viral genome and maintaining the connection between the genome and TerL, thereby regulating the packaging mechanism. Additionally, the portal protein (ORF 46) acts as a route for DNA entrance and exit during packaging and infection by forming a dodecameric ring at the capsid vertex, which is the essential entry point inside the phage capsid. These proteins work together to successfully coordinate the complex genome packaging processes in SSG23, ensuring proper genome encapsulation and infectivity. Furthermore, previous investigations demonstrated that the terminase large component is highly conserved among related phages26.

In phage SSG23, genome morphogenesis is regulated by 40 essential structural proteins, ensuring appropriate virion assembly and host infection. The major head protein (ORF 35) provides viral genome stability and protection by forming its primary capsid structure, and the head scaffolding protein (ORF 42) is essential for capsid assembly. Furthermore, the baseplate wedge proteins (ORF 56, 57, 58, 63, 64, 65, 68) act as a structural anchor for tail fiber attachment, facilitating effective bacterial adherence, while the tail proteins aid in host recognition and genome transport27,28. Moreover, the tail fiber protein (ORF 125) mediates early receptor binding and infection initiation, making it crucial for host specificity. These morphogenesis proteins function together to successfully propagate phage SSG23 by coordinating host interaction, phage assembly, and stability.

Phylogenetic analysis

Two distinct phylogenetic tree types were produced based on the terminase large subunit (TerL) and the tail fiber protein, respectively, to better comprehend the evolutionary relationships between SSG23 and other Shigella phages. These trees are displayed in Fig. 3d. The phage SSG23 was found to cluster with enteric phages that resembled T4, including Brandy, Sf20, JK32, FL20, E26, RB49, and Ec_Makalu_002, based on the tree. Consequently, this studied phage was regarded as a novel species within the T4 genus.

Quantification of Shigella biofilm in a microtiter plate

In a quantitative evaluation of biofilm degradation activity, phage SSG23, antibiotic Ceftriaxone, and their combination dramatically decreased the mature biofilm of the S. sonnei strain. In comparison to the control biofilm, phage SSG23 (1010 pfu/ml) and antibiotic Ceftriaxone (0.064 µg/mL) alone reduced biofilm by ~34% (p < 0.001) and ~49%, respectively, after 6 h treatment. However, the combination of both phage-antibiotics decreased biofilm at a higher rate than SSG23 and Ceftriaxone alone (~56.6%, p < 0.01). Additionally, every combination pair showed a statistically significant decrease in biofilm (Fig. 4). Cytotoxicity assay

The potential cytotoxicity of phage SSG23 was assessed on RAW 264.7 macrophage cells using the MTT assay. MTT reagent was added after cells were exposed to varying doses of SSG23 (102–109 PFU/mL) for 24 h. The findings showed that at all examined phage doses, cell viability remained above 90% and did not decline significantly compared to the untreated control (Fig. S1). This demonstrates that SSG23 is safe and biocompatible for use in therapeutic applications because it does not have cytotoxic effects on mammalian cells.

Detection of serum neutralizing antibodies against SSG23 phage

Serum-neutralizing antibodies were measured in treated mice to assess the host immunological response to phage SSG23 (Fig. 6a). A phage neutralization assay was used to measure the anti-phage activity of serum samples that were obtained at various times after phage administration (Fig. 6b). The findings indicate that mice exposed to SSG23 developed observable amounts of neutralizing antibodies over time, and sera taken on Day 7 after treatment showed a significant decrease in phage viability. Repeated doses of SSG23 caused a more noticeable neutralizing effect in mice, indicating an adaptive immune response to the phage. Comparing preventative and therapeutic phage therapy with light microscopy and transmission electron microscopy

The efficacy of treating phage SSG23 both therapeutically and prophylactically was assessed through a comparative study. Histopathological sections of the colon tissue of mice are shown in light microscopy images in Fig. 7e. The colon section of normal mice has a well-organized structure with low inflammatory cell infiltrates, proper crypt architecture, and intact epithelial layers. In contrast, there are notable histological changes in the colon of mice infected with S. sonnei bacteria. There was a significant inflammatory infiltration into the mucosal layer of the infected model colon due to an inflow of inflammatory cells, including neutrophils and lymphocytes. The presence of Shigella bacteria resulted in the proliferation of crypt cells, resulting in crypt hyperplasia. However, the preventive and therapeutic groups demonstrated that phage administration may significantly decrease inflammatory responses in Shigella-infected colonic tissues. Comparing phage-treated mice to infected controls, histological evaluations show reduced leukocyte infiltration, cryptitis resolution, and general colonic architecture preservation. Remarkably, the preventative group received less damage than the treatment group.

LM and TEM images of the small intestine from uninfected control mice revealed a homogeneous mucus layer, tight junctions, and undamaged microvilli, indicating a well-preserved epithelial lining (Fig. 8a). In the therapeutic group, the intestinal epithelium showed partial restoration, with reduced inflammation, improved microvillus structure, and decreased immune cell infiltration compared to untreated infected mice (Fig. 8d). Remarkably, the preventative group developed less damage than the treatment group. The intestinal epithelial structure in mice treated with prophylactic phage therapy remained mostly intact, with tight junctions and well-preserved microvilli (Fig. 8c). Discussion

S. sonnei, which was previously often isolated in developed nations, has become widespread throughout the industrializing regions of Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East9. The global burden of diarrheal diseases has increased due to a rapid increase in multidrug-resistant Shigella strains. Unlike antibiotics, virulent lytic phages have a mechanism that allows them to specifically infect and kill the bacterial population, even drug-resistant strains6,7,9. Consequently, there is a growing interest in employing phages to combat multidrug-resistant Shigella strains as a substitute for antibiotic therapy. In this study, the Straboviridae bacteriophage SSG23 was employed as a potential biocontrol candidate against shigellosis.

Shigella phage SSG23 was found to be sensitive to all four serogroups, including S. sonnei, S. boydii, S. dysenteriae, and S. flexneri, based on host range analysis and high EOP values. S. flexneri serotypes 1b, 2a, 3a, 4a, and 6 are more common in underdeveloped nations, although serotype 2a is more common in industrialized countries like India. Even though phages usually only infect one type of bacterium, certain polyvalent phages have also been reported previously29–33. Polyvalent phages can infect multiple species or genera due to their versatile mechanism of action, allowing them to recognize and attach to various bacterial receptors, circumventing the limitations of monovalent phages in dealing with heterogeneous infections. The capacity of this phage to lyse various strains of Shigella indicated that it might be investigated for phage treatments against shigellosis. Additionally, to the best of our understanding, this is the first reported Shigella phage effective against four Shigella serogroups.

Phage stability under various physicochemical environments is also crucial for storage and application32. The phage SSG23 employed in this investigation was stable between pH 5 and 9 and between −20 and 37 °C without observing a remarkable decrease in phage titers. These findings support the results of most of the prior research18,31,34. Due to their robustness and ability to endure harsh environments for years, tailed phages (T4, T5, and T7) are favored in therapeutic applications34. Previous studies have demonstrated that the tailed Myophage was inactivated at 50 °C for 60 minand remained stable at 4 to 37 °C35,36. Additionally, T4 phage reduced its titer at pH 9.2 and below 4.0 and showed maximum stability at pH 6.0–7.437. These findings are corroborated by our results, which suggest that phage SSG23 could be maintained at optimal pH and temperature ranges without undergoing a significant decrease in viability.

Moreover, another crucial factor in selecting phages for successful phage therapy is burst size, or the average number of phage particles released per infected bacterium. According to our findings, the phage SSG23 had latent periods of 20 to 35 min and burst sizes of up to 224 phages/infected cells for various Shigella serogroups. Several variables, including host physiology and phage lytic protein complexes like a holin, endolysin, and spanin, affect the latent period, or the duration of time it takes for the phage to cause the host cell to lyse38,39. Phages with a large burst size and a short latent period (~20 min) are better able to eradicate infecting bacteria and reduce the possibility of phage-resistant variants developing17. In the present study, phage SSG23 demonstrated encouraging lytic characteristics with large burst sizes and short latent periods, suggesting that it is promising for medicinal applications.

Phage SSG23 showed a high nucleotide sequence similarity with the T4-like phage (members of the genus Krischvirus and RB49 group) Ec_Makalu_001, JK32, Brandy, and Sf2018–21. Phage SSG23 shared a modular structure with these phages, considering the specific positions of the predicted genes, their associated functions, and the general arrangement of the genomes, which typically includes the GC content, DNA replication-transcription, DNA packaging mechanism, and structural genes. Moreover, the phage SSG23 and other phages from the RB49 group shared a tree of evolutionary relationships; it is interesting to note that they were isolated from distinct hosts and continents. According to Hendrix et al. (1999), homology might be the consequence of horizontal gene transfer from a shared pool among the ancestors of the contemporary phages during co-evolution40.

Notably, phage SSG23 does not have any tRNA genes, a trait of members of the genus Krischvirus, in contrast to other members of the family Straboviridae such as Ec_Makalu_001, Sf20, Jk32, etc.18–20. The absence is crucial because it indicates that Krischvirus depends entirely on the translational machinery of the host bacterium to synthesize proteins. Importantly, genome analysis of the phage SSG23 revealed the absence of a lysogenic cycle, such as integrase, recombinase, repressors, or excisionase, indicating that it would be a safe option for phage therapy with no adverse consequences. Moreover, the phage could form clear small plaques, and the presence of host lysis proteins (holin and endolysin) in its genome confirms that phage SSG23 is a virulent lytic phage and could be useful for therapeutic purposes.

The evaluation of phage cytotoxicity on macrophages using the MTT assay is crucial in determining the safety and efficacy of phage treatment, especially for applications aimed at intracellular bacterial infections. In this study, the cytotoxicity of phage SSG23 was assessed using an MTT assay on macrophage cells, revealing a high level of cell survival, indicating nontoxicity. This result is consistent with previous studies indicating that lytic phages often are not very harmful to human cell lines, and many of them do not have significant side effects on mammalian cells even at large concentrations41.

In this study, S. sonnei infection was established via intraperitoneal (i.p.) challenge to ensure reproducible systemic infection and consistent bacterial burden across experimental animals. While this route does not replicate the natural oral–fecal transmission of shigellosis, oral challenge models in mice are constrained by the inherent resistance of murine intestinal epithelium to Shigella colonization, often necessitating pretreatments such as streptomycin administration or colonic abrasion, which can introduce variability and confounding factors42,43. The i.p. route enabled the bypassing of these barriers, allowing for reliable infection and controlled evaluation of the prophylactic and therapeutic potential of SSG23. Moreover, one of the most significant findings of our investigation is that preventive therapy works better than the treatment group. This suggests that pre-exposure to phages may improve protection against Shigella colonization and infection, particularly in high-risk populations. Similar results have been observed for various types of bacterial illnesses when preventative phage therapy performed better than post-infection treatment44. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate phage therapy against a clinically isolated S. sonnei strain in a mouse model, incorporating direct visualization through histopathological analysis and ultrathin section transmission electron microscopy of intestinal tissues.

Despite these promising findings, several challenges still exist. One of the few hypothesized explanations for the failure of bacteriophage therapy is the development of neutralizing anti-phage antibodies15.In our study, such antibodies against phage SSG23 became detectable within 7 days after administration; however, bacterial clearance occurred predominantly within the first few days post-treatment, before significant antibody titers developed. This suggests that therapeutic efficacy was largely due to early, direct bacteriolytic activity rather than prolonged phage persistence. However, we did not directly assess post-treatment Shigella isolates for continued susceptibility to SSG23. Instead, we performed serum neutralization assays, in which sera from phage-treated mice were incubated with SSG23 and plated against the original S. sonnei strain. A reduction in phage infectivity was observed in sera from treated mice compared with untreated controls, indicating the development of neutralizing antibodies. Our findings, therefore, suggest that short-term preventive therapy is more successful than long-term preventive therapy. This implies that phages can quickly eliminate bacteria when used early, but longer exposure times may decrease effectiveness, possibly because of developing a memory immune response. Strategies including encapsulation, cocktail formulations, dose optimization, or phage engineering can be required to improve phage stability and avoid immune detection45.

The present study demonstrates that the newly isolated Shigella-specific phage SSG23 could be used effectively to treat infections caused by multidrug-resistant Shigella. Phage SSG23 significantly degraded biofilms and displayed potent lytic activity against many Shigella spp, both on its own and in conjunction with antibiotics. It is a strong candidate for treatment because of its stability across a wide pH and temperature range and its large burst size. The genomic study supported its safety for clinical use by confirming the lack of genes associated with lysogeny and toxicity. Moreover, oral treatment of SSG23 dramatically decreased S. sonnei colonization and shedding in in vivo experiments utilizing a BALB/c mouse model. These findings strongly suggest that phage treatment could be an appropriate alternative approach to treating Shigella infections, suggesting the need for more investigation and clinical trials.

Methods

Bacterial strains

The S. sonnei strain was employed as the host strain for phage isolation. All the Shigella bacteria used in the present study have been previously isolated from the diarrheal patient’s fecal sample and were obtained from the ICMR-National Repository of Antimicrobial Resistant Microorganisms (ICMR-NRAMRB), Kolkata, India. They were all stored in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Merck, Germany) containing 30% glycerol and kept at −70 °C at the Division of Bacteriology, ICMR-NIRBI, India. The bacterial strains were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) at 37 °C under aerobic conditions, adhering to institutional standard biosafety protocols.

Isolation and purification of bacteriophage

For phage isolation, a water sample was collected from the River Ganges near Kolkata, West Bengal, India. In brief, a 25 ml filtrate water sample, 25 ml 10× phage broth media, and 5 ml of the log-phase culture of the host bacteria S. sonnei were added together and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with constant shaking at 150 rpm. The residual bacterial cells were removed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was filtered with a 0.22-µm membrane filter. To confirm the presence of the phage in the filtrate, a spot assay was performed on the surface of lawn cultures of the host bacteria and incubated at 37 °C using the double-layer agar method45–47. Then, the single plaque was picked up three times by plaque assay to obtain the purified phage plaque. The phage was concentrated and purified by ultracentrifugation (25,000 rpm, 1.5 h, 4 °C) and sucrose step-gradient ultracentrifugation (30,000 rpm, 2 h, 4 °C) after proliferation.

Antibiotic sensitivity for host bacterial strains using the Kirby–Bauer Method

The host S. sonnei strain was tested for antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion technique on Muller–Hinton agar using 13 antibiotics from various classes. It includes aminoglycosides (gentamicin-10 µg/disc), second-generation cephalosporins (Cefoxitin-30 µg/disc), third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone-30 µg/disc), Beta-lactams (Ampicillin-10 µg/disc), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin-5 µg/disc, ofloxacin-2 µg/disc, sparfloxacin-5 µg/disc), sulfonamides (cotrimoxazole-25 µg/disc), tetracyclines (tetracyclin-30 µg/disc, doxycycline hydrochloride-30 µg/disc), and macrolides (azithromycin-15 µg/disc, erythromycin-15 µg/disc). The antibiotic disks were properly positioned and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Following CLSI 2018 guidelines, the zone of inhibition was measured and interpreted48.

Host range and efficiency of plating (EOP)

A host range assay was performed to assess the lytic spectrum of the isolated bacteriophages against the susceptible bacterial strains. It was determined by spot assay using 10 µl of phage lysate (1012 PFU/ml) on each agar plate containing different bacterial strains. After an overnight incubation at 37 °C, the plates were examined for clear zones that could indicate the susceptibility of the bacteria. Subsequently, the obtained phage was subjected to an EOP assay using the double-layer agar method to evaluate the lytic activity of the phage.

Transmission electron microscopy

Purified 5 µl phage lysate (1012 pfu/ml) was applied to a carbon-coated copper grid and negatively stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate for 30 s, and excess liquid was blotted off49. After air-drying, the grids were visualized with a JEOL JEM-1400Pl transmission electron microscope operating at 100 kV.

Stability

A thermal stability test was performed at pH 7.0 to evaluate the heat tolerance of the phage. At a range of temperatures (−20, 4, 25, 37, 50, and 70 °C, respectively), the phage was incubated for 1 h. Using the soft agar overlay technique, the phage titer was measured following the incubation50. Additionally, the pH stability of the phage was investigated by preincubating the phage suspensions at different pH ranges (pH 3 to 13) at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, the double-layer agar method was used to count the remaining phages. Each assay was performed in triplicate.

MOI determination of the phage

The multiplicity of infection is the ratio of bacteria to phages that infect or adsorb during an infection51. To determine the optimal MOI, the S. sonnei strain was first cultivated in LB at 37 °C until the early logarithmic growth phase. Different phage titers were prepared to reach MOIs of 10, 1, and 0.1, respectively. The experiment was carried out in triplicate, and the OD600 nm values were measured at 1-h intervals52.

One-step growth assay and adsorption assay

The growth kinetics of bacteriophage SSG23 were evaluated using a one-step growth curve. It was carried out using the previously mentioned methodology53. At an interval of 10 min, the exponentially growing bacterial culture (20 ml) was harvested, and the pellet was resuspended in around 1 ml of LB, followed by the addition of phage. The mixture was incubated for adsorption at 37 °C for 5 min. The double-layer agar method was used to plate the mixture as soon as it was collected. The ratio of the final number of released phage particles to the starting number of infected bacterial cells during the latent phase was used to determine the burst magnitude.

In addition, the adsorption assay was conducted as described previously with some modifications54. Briefly, the bacterial culture (1 ml) was infected with a phage suspension at an MOI of 0.1 and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. After infection, aliquots were taken at different time intervals (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 min) and immediately diluted in Tris-MgCl2 buffer, followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min. Then, the supernatant unadsorbed phage titers were estimated by the double-layer agar method.

Phage–host infection using ultrathin sectioning and transmission electron microscopy

The bacteriophage SSG23 was mixed with a bacterial culture of S. sonnei at an MOI of 10 and incubated at 37 °C at different time points (0, 20, 60, and 100 min, respectively). Following incubation, the samples were taken out and immediately centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, the cell pellets were resuspended in a 0.1 M sodium cacodylate solution containing 3% glutaraldehyde for fixation.

Ultrathin sectioning and electron microscopy methods were employed to investigate the bacteriophage-host interaction at the cellular level34. Briefly, after primary fixation, the sample was treated with secondary fixation in 1% OsO4, and ethanol concentrations were gradually increased to dehydrate the sample. After that, the samples were placed in Agar100 resin, and polymerization was carried out at 60 °C overnight. Using a Leica Ultracut UCT ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Germany), the ultrathin sections (40–50 nm) of the infected and control cells are cut. Next, the sections were collected into nickel or copper grids, which were then air-dried after being dual-stained with 0.2% lead citrate and 2% aqueous uranyl acetate. Then, the grids were examined under TEM at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

Microtiter plate-based assay of biofilm

Biofilm was developed in 96-well plates using a modified version of the previously published protocol to assess the anti-biofilm activity of phage SSG23 either alone or in combination with the antibiotic Ceftriaxone55. In brief, a fresh LB medium was utilized to cultivate the S. sonnei strain overnight at 37 °C. Next, 200 µl of the culture was added to 96-well plates, and the plates were left in an incubator for 24 h at 37 °C without agitation. After incubation, the supernatant was removed from the well plate. To examine the effect of phage SSG23 on biofilm, phage stock was added to the biofilm at a concentration of MOI 1 and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Thereafter, the wells were rinsed with 1X PBS thrice and allowed to air dry. The air-dried plate was then stained with crystal violet (0.1%, w/v) for 30 min. Using an iMark Microplate Reader S/N 21673, optical densities of the biofilm were determined at an absorbance of 595 nm.

Biofilm degradation assay using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The qualitative biofilm degradation activity of newly isolated phage SSG23 on the S. sonnei strain was assessed following a previously discussed method with some modifications20,55,56. A 20 µl of an overnight culture of S. sonnei was dispensed into a Petri dish containing glass coverslips (22 mm × 22 mm) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to form the biofilm. After that, phage SSG23 (1010 PFU/ml), Ceftriaxone (0.064 µg/ml), and a combination of both at the same individual concentration were applied to the biofilm. Further, every sample was incubated for 6 h at 37 °C. After that, planktonic cells were eliminated, and the biofilm samples of the control and treated groups were prepared for SEM analysis using the modified method described57. In brief, samples were fixed overnight at 4 °C using 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate solution. Following ethanol serial dehydration (30, 50, 70, and 90% for 10 min each, and 100% twice for 15 min each), the samples were subsequently treated consecutively with 100% hexamethyldisilazane (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for an hour. After dehydrating, the samples were coated with a gold sputter and examined using Zeiss evo 10 SEM (FEI, Netherlands).

Genomic DNA extraction

The concentrated and purified phage lysate (1010 PFU/ml) was required for effective DNA isolation. A phage DNA isolation kit (Norgen Biotek Corp., Canada) was used to extract DNA from the high-titer stocks of phage SSG23 as directed by the manufacturer. Using a spectrophotometer, the concentration and purity of DNA were determined.

Genomic characterization of phage SSG23

Purified genomic DNA of phage SSG23 was then sequenced by using an Illumina at the Neuberg Center for Genomic Medicine. Reads were assembled with SPAdes Metaviral v. 3.15.2 with the reads map back option. The putative ORFs were predicted and annotated by Pharokka. The phage sequences were submitted to NCBI BLAST and compared to the nearest viral genome using a cumulative assessment of the highest percentage of query coverage, the lowest reported E-value (0), and the highest percent identity match to determine taxonomy. Visualization of genome alignments of phage SSG23 with closely related Straboviridae phages was performed using the Easyfig application (http://mjsull.github.io/Easyfig/files.html). Additionally, the lifestyle, order, and family of SSG23 were predicted in silico using the PhageAI platform (https://phage.ai/), a machine-learning-based tool validated for high-accuracy classification of phage life cycles57.

The amino acid sequences of the tail fiber protein (ORF125) and terminase large subunits (ORF49) were chosen and obtained from the NCBI Genbank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to construct the phylogenetic tree. Additionally, the protein known as the tail fiber is responsible for the host specificity, and the terminase large subunits are typically regarded as genetic markers for the order Caudovirales58. The “ONE CLICK” default workflow at Phylogeny.fr was utilized to generate the phylogenetic trees59.

Cell lines and culture conditions

The macrophage cells were cultivated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS, pH 7.4), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (Sigma-Aldrich, Wicklow, Ireland). The cells were seeded (20,000 cells/ml) in 96-well tissue culture plates and incubated overnight.

Cytotoxicity assay

An MTT experiment was conducted using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay to evaluate the cytotoxic effects of bacteriophage SSG23 on Macrophage cells (RAW 264.7) as previously described60. The cells were incubated for 24 h with various concentrations of SSG23 (10⁶–10⁹ PFU/mL), and then MTT reagent (0.5 mg/mL) was added. Then, the formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 492 nm.

Selection of animals

To examine the effect of phage SSG23 on S. sonnei, BALB/c mice were collected from the NIRBI animal house facility. For the experiment, 6-week-old BALB/c male mice were selected; their weight ranged from 22 ± 2 g. The mice were disease-free and active. The mice were given drinking water containing broad-spectrum antibiotics for 5 days before being infected with S. sonnei. Before experimentation, animals were kept for 10 days to ensure that they could get used to the laboratory environment. The entire experiment, including the housing of the animals, took place in the ICMR-NIRBI animal house.

All procedures followed the applicable rules and regulations. As directed by the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA), Ministry of Environment and Forest, Government of India, the protocol for the animal experiment was examined and carried out following the standard operating procedure. The animal experiment protocol was authorized by the Institutional Animal Ethical Committee (IAEC) of ICMR-NIRBI and registered under registration number 68/GO/Rebi/S/1999/CPCSEA, valid 17/7/2024, Approval No. PRO/186/- Nov2022-25.

Simulating the immune responses of mice to phage administration

Mice were given the phage intraperitoneally once daily for 7 days at a dose of 1010 PFU/ml water to measure their innate immunity against phage. On the 7th day following the final dosage, the serum of the mice was collected. As previously mentioned, mice were given two 7-day therapies of phage SSG23 4 weeks apart to evaluate their adaptive immune response61. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation and terminally bled for serum collection on day 35, the day following the final dosage.

Serum collection

The posterior vena cava was used to obtain mouse serum for the final experiments. Blood was collected into Sarstedt Microvette serum collection tubes and treated following the manufacturer’s instructions. After being aliquoted, the serum was kept at 4 °C for instant use and −80 °C for long-term storage without frequent freeze-thaw cycles.

Phage neutralization assay

Neutralization assays were carried out using an established procedure with a few minor modifications62,63. Briefly, 5 μL of phage SSG23 (10¹⁰ PFU/mL), 4.5 μL of SM buffer, and 0.5 μL of mouse sera (collected at different time points) were mixed and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Following serial dilution in SM buffer, phage-serum combinations were mixed with the S. sonnei bacterial culture and were plated using the double-layer agar method. The results were presented as the efficiency of plating (EOP).

ELISA for the detection of phage-specific antibodies

Phage SSG23 was coated on high-binding assay 96-well Nunc-Immuno plates (Thermo Fisher) with a 1010 pfu/mL concentration at 4 °C overnight. After that, washing with PBST (0.1% Tween-20 in PBS) three times, the plate was blocked with PBSM (5% skimmed milk in PBS) and followed by applying tenfold serially diluted humanized mouse sera and incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature, then washed with PBST five times. About 100 mL of HRP-labeled secondary antibodies was applied to the plates and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The secondary antibodies included: (1) mice anti-human IgG Fc (HRP) pre-adsorbed (catalog no ab98624; Abcam); and (2) mice anti-human IgM mu chain (HRP) pre-adsorbed (catalog no ab98549; Abcam). After washing away the unbound secondary antibodies, TMB substrate was added, and the OD values at 450 nm were recorded using a BioTek Synergy 2 plate reader.

Efficacy of bacteriophage SSG23 in challenged BALB/c mice

The BALB/c mice were infected with a virulent strain of S. sonnei to compare the efficiency of prophylactic versus therapeutic application. BALB/c mice were divided into four groups, each consisting of six animals. The doses were fixed, prepared in PBS, and administered. Mice in Group I were given PBS as a control, while animals in Group II received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of S. sonnei (107 CFU/ml) diluted in PBS. Mice in groups III and IV represent the preventive and treatment, respectively. All four groups were kept in hygienic conditions with a continuous food and water supply for 7 days. To count the number of bacteria, fecal samples were collected on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 after infection. The samples were then plated in serial dilutions on HEA agar. A significant observation was made, and results were recorded.

Enumerations of Shigella and phages from mouse tissues

The colon and small intestine tissues of BALB/c mice were collected on day seven of the post-infection period, when the most prominent gut inflammation occurred. Tissues were immediately weighed and homogenized using a bead-beating machine with 1.0 mm diameter silica beads (Biospec Products, Bartlesville, United States), following a previously described method34. S. sonnei colony-forming unit (CFU)/g of tissue was determined by a serial dilution technique on the HEA agar.

Tissue section preparation and electron microscope imaging

Intestinal tissues were sliced into 1 mm × 1 mm sections for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) investigation, and they were then treated using the previously described method42. The tissues were preserved overnight at 4 °C in a cacodylate-buffered glutaraldehyde. Tissues were subsequently post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide and dehydrated using a series of ethanol grades (25, 50, 75, and 100%). After that, the samples were placed in Agar100 resin, and polymerization was carried out at 60 °C overnight. Using a Leica Ultracut UCT ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Germany) equipped with a Diatome diamond knife (Diatome, Hatfield, PA), gold sections of 60–90 nm were cut. The sections were transferred onto copper or nickel grids (EMS) with a mesh size of 200. After that, the sections were dual-stained using 0.2% lead citrate and 2% aqueous uranyl acetate, and the grids were allowed to air dry. Using an accelerating voltage of 120 kV, grids were imaged using a JEOL 1400 electron microscope.

H&E staining

At the end of day 7, the mice were euthanized, and intestinal organs were carefully removed and fixed with 10% buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Sections measuring six micrometers were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. A JENOPTIK GRYPHAX digital microscope was used to view the stained sections.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analysis was performed by t-test using GraphPad Prism (version 5, GraphPad Software, USA) software, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using two-way ANOVA (*P < 0.05; ***P < 0.001).
