Avapritinib for targeting KIT D816V-mutated acute myeloid leukemia relapsed after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant with extramedullary and central nervous system involvement
Authors: Marco Frigeni, Alessandra Algarotti, Silvia Salmoiraghi, Clara Belotti, Martina Milani, Chiara Pavoni, Orietta Spinelli, Irene Gotuzzo, Alessandro Rambaldi, Federico Lussana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Relapse of acute myeloid leukemia with CBFB::MYH11 fusion and KIT D816V mutation after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is uncommon and often associated with aggressive extramedullary spread and poor outcomes, particularly when the central nervous system is involved. We report a case of a 54‑year‑old man who developed extensive paravertebral, central nervous system, and multiorgan extramedullary relapse despite donor lymphocyte infusions and high‑dose chemotherapy. Avapritinib, a selective inhibitor of mutated KIT, was initiated as single‑agent therapy and resulted in rapid neurological improvement, complete metabolic response on positron emission tomography–computed tomography, and full molecular remission in peripheral blood.Due to temporary loss of off‑label access, avapritinib was discontinued, leading to a new extramedullary and central nervous system relapse. Remarkably, rechallenge with single‑agent avapritinib again induced molecular remission in both peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid, along with near‑complete metabolic response across all previously involved sites. The reproducibility of the response strongly supports a direct, target‑driven antileukemic effect.This case highlights the potential of avapritinib as an effective salvage therapy for KIT‑mutated acute myeloid leukemia with extramedullary and central nervous system involvement, including relapse after transplantation. The robust and repeated responses observed in this patient underscore the rationale the potential role of avapritinib as salvage therapy and support its investigation as a component of measurable residual disease-guided or post-transplant maintenance strategies.

Case report

A 54-year-old male was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) characterized by CBFB::MYH11 fusion transcript and a KIT D816V mutation. Conventional cytogenetic analysis at diagnosis showed 46,XY, inv(16)(p13.1q22)[20]. The patient achieved complete molecular remission (CRmol) after induction chemotherapy with standard 3 + 7 (daunorubicin 60mg/m2 days 1 to 3, cytarabine 200mg/m2 days 1 to 7), and gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO) and two consolidation cycles (cytarabine 1 g/m2 days 2 to 5, daunorubicin 60mg/m2 day 1, and GO). Six months after completing the first-line treatment, a molecular relapse was documented. The patient received reinduction therapy with fludarabine, high-dose cytarabine, and idarubicin (FLAI regimen), achieving a second CRmol. He then proceeded to consolidation with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT) from an HLA-matched unrelated donor. The conditioning regimen was fludarabine 40 mg/m2 for 3 days and busulfan 3.2 mg/Kg for 3 days; graft versus host disease (GvHD) prophylaxis was post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) 50 mg/Kg/day on post-transplant days + 3 and + 4, tacrolimus at a targeted blood concentration of 10 to 15 ng/mL from post-transplant day + 5 and mycophenolate 15 mg/Kg BID from post-transplant day + 5 to day + 35.

After 279 days from alloHSCT, the patient experienced a molecular relapse, with low levels of CBFB::MYH11 fusion transcript detectable in both bone marrow (BM) and peripheral blood (PB). Three escalating doses of donor lymphocyte infusions (DLI) were administered, but they failed to control the molecular relapse. The situation escalated when a 1-log10 increase in the transcript level was noted, coinciding with the rapid onset of neurological symptoms, such as severe right shoulder pain, sensory deficits, and paralysis of the lower limbs. Imaging studies revealed aggressive extramedullary disease, with paravertebral masses causing spinal cord compression. A central nervous system (CNS) involvement was documented (white blood cell count in cerebrospinal fluid [CSF] < 5/µL with 7.3% of AML blasts detected by multiparameter flow cytometry [MFC]). The presence of KIT D816V mutation was confirmed in both PB and BM samples using digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 0.1% and 0.04%, respectively, both above the assay detection limit.

Two cycles of intravenous high-dose methotrexate and cytarabine (methotrexate 3500 mg/m2 day 1 and cytarabine 2000 mg/m2 BID days 2 and 3), accompanied by intrathecal chemotherapy, were administered as an urgent debulking strategy. Lumbar punctures were halted after two administrations due to post-dural puncture headache with subdural hygromas. After this treatment, the patient’s neurological symptoms showed only partial improvement, and the spine MRI revealed a persistent disease at multiple paravertebral levels. In particular, the paravertebral lesion extending from D1 to D4, which had previously caused marked spinal cord compression, showed no notable changes in size or radiological features.

Due to the limited radiological and clinical response to conventional chemotherapy, along with the need for a more effective long-term treatment capable of achieving sustained remission – an outcome rarely achieved with conventional chemotherapy in this setting – the decision was made to explore an alternative therapeutic strategy.

Avapritinib is a potent, selective inhibitor of mutated KIT, with emerging evidence supporting its use in relapsed AML with KIT mutations [1–4]. Following hematologic recovery after chemotherapy (platelets > 50 × 10⁹/L), avapritinib was initiated at a reduced dose of 100 mg/day in 28-day treatment cycles.

Due to the patient’s favorable tolerance, the dose was increased to 200 mg/day starting from the second cycle. Only grade 2 periorbital and facial edema and grade 1 thrombocytopenia were observed as adverse events. These toxicities were consistent with the known safety profile of avapritinib as reported in clinical trials for advanced systemic mastocytosis [5]. Starting from the second cycle the patient was discharged and continued treatment as an outpatient.

The response to avapritinib was documented across multiple perspectives. Clinically, there was a gradual improvement in neurological symptoms, culminating in the recovery of autonomous ambulation with only minimal need for support. Radiologically, MRI scans after the third cycle showed a slight reduction in the size of the pathological paravertebral tissues, as well as a decrease in contrast enhancement, suggesting decreased tumor activity. This radiological response was further corroborated by a PET-CT scan performed after the fourth cycle, which documented a complete metabolic response. Additionally, the patient achieved a consistent decline in the CBFB::MYH11 fusion transcript levels in PB, ultimately reaching a state of CRmol after the fourth cycle of avapritinib (Fig. 1). Nine months after the extramedullary post-alloHSCT AML relapse, the patient was in persistent molecular and metabolic response and had completed the 7th cycle of avapritinib. However, following this response, avapritinib was discontinued due to difficulties in obtaining off-label drug access. Approximately three months after treatment interruption, a molecular relapse was first detected in PB, indicated by a progressive increase in the CBFB::MYH11 fusion transcript (Fig. 1). Almost simultaneously, the patient developed severe left-sided thoracolumbar pain. Given these clinical symptoms, an integrated diagnostic re-evaluation was undertaken. Spinal MRI revealed multiple findings highly suggestive of extensive disease relapse: a 30 × 17 mm heteroformative lesion in the right anterior paravertebral region at the C7-D1 level, a focus of extramedullary hematopoiesis at D2-D3, and a posterior epidural thickening with intense contrast enhancement at D10-D11. Additionally, a spiculated mass in the right upper hilar region of the lung was noted. A diagnostic lumbar puncture confirmed CNS involvement by AML, with blasts detectable by flow cytometry. To complete the re-staging, a PET-CT scan confirmed intense metabolic activity in all MRI-identified lesions, and showed additional FDG-avid foci in multiple bilateral lymph nodes, the pleura bilaterally, and retro- and parasternal regions at the level of D10-D11. Further pathological FDG uptake was documented at multiple skeletal and soft-tissue sites, including the left psoas muscle. A biopsy of the hilar pulmonary lesion confirmed myeloid sarcoma with CBFB::MYH11 expression and KIT D816V mutation (VAF 32.1%). Bone marrow evaluation was negative for morphological relapse. No significant mast cell infiltrates were observed in the pulmonary lesion or in bone marrow specimens. Following the renewed authorization for off-label use, avapritinib was resumed at a dose of 200 mg/day. Shortly thereafter, the patient received palliative radiotherapy (20 Gy in 4 fractions) targeting the epidural lesion at D10-D11 level. No additional therapy, including intrathecal chemoteherapy, was provided for the remaining extramedullary disease sites.

After three months, the patient achieved a CRmol in both PB (Fig. 1) and CSF. A PET-CT showed near-complete metabolic response in all previously involved sites: SUVmax of the right hilar mass decreased from 9.15 to 4.26 (a 53% reduction), only minimal uptake persisted in the left psoas muscle (SUVmax 2.47 vs. 5.74, a 57% reduction), while previously hypermetabolic foci were no longer metabolically active (Fig. 2). At six months, a complete metabolic remission was documented, with persistent CRmol in PB. No clinically significant exacerbation or new onset of GvHD was observed during either course of avapritinib.

To the best of our knowledge, we report for the first time a patient with relapsed KIT D816V-mutated AML involving both CNS and extensive extramedullary disease, who achieved molecular remission with avapritinib alone, even in the context of post-transplant relapse. Notably, the effective management of extramedullary disease involving the CNS, which is typically associated with dismal prognosis [6–8], highlights the potential of this agent in settings where conventional treatments often fail. CNS relapse in AML remains a major clinical challenge due to the limited ability of most systemic therapies to penetrate the blood–brain barrier. In this context, the observed CNS response is particularly significant. Although data on CNS penetration of avapritinib in AML are limited, and CSF levels of avapritinib were not available in this case, preclinical studies and early clinical data in PDGFRA-mutated gliomas suggest that the drug can effectively cross the blood–brain barrier and exert antitumor activity within the CNS [9]. Second, the patient experienced a second remission following rechallenge with avapritinib after drug discontinuation – supporting a direct, target-dependent therapeutic effect. Finally, the sustained duration of response – over one year from initial avapritinib exposure – represents the longest follow-up reported to date in this setting.

Our findings are in keeping with previous case series that support the efficacy of avapritinib in treating relapsed core-binding factor (CBF) AML with KIT mutations in both adult and pediatric patients [1, 3, 10]. Kong and colleagues further confirmed that avapritinib effectively eliminated measurable residual disease (MRD) in AML patients who relapsed post-alloHSCT, thereby preventing progression to hematological relapse [2]. Additionally, Wang and colleagues reported a pediatric series of four patients with t(8;21) AML and KIT exon 17 mutations who received avapritinib post-alloHSCT for MRD positivity after failing demethylating agents and DLI. All patients achieved molecular negativity within 1–9 months further supporting the feasibility of avapritinib as preemptive treatment in this setting [10]. More recently, Getz and colleagues described a case of relapsed RUNX1::RUNX1T1 AML with KIT D816H mutation following alloHSCT, in which avapritinib monotherapy induced a rapid and deep molecular remission, enabling a second alloHSCT, highlighting its potential as a salvage strategy in this setting [4]. Taken together, these findings support the potential role of avapritinib not only as salvage therapy, but also as a component of MRD-guided strategies or post-remission maintenance approaches in KIT-mutated AML. However, due to the risk of intracranial bleeding, particularly in patients with platelet counts < 50 × 10⁹/L, careful patient selection and timing of administration are important [4, 5]. Thus, using avapritinib in molecular relapse, or as maintenance treatment in hematologic remission may offer a favorable balance between efficacy and safety. In this regard, a parallel can be drawn between the use of FLT3 inhibitors (FLT3i) in FLT3-mutated AML and the potential use of avapritinib in KIT-mutated diseases. Similar to midostaurin or gilteritinib, which target FLT3 mutations and are used in both induction and post-transplant maintenance [11, 12], avapritinib could play a similar role in treating KIT-mutated diseases.

In conclusion, this case supports that avapritinib can induce meaningful remissions in high-risk KIT-mutated AML with extramedullary and CNS relapses following alloHSCT. The reproducible response to avapritinib monotherapy upon relapse rechallenge is consistent with a target-driven mechanism of action. Overall, these findings highlight avapritinib’s potential as a key component in MRD-guided, maintenance, or salvage strategies in KIT-mutated AML, warranting prospective clinical trials to optimize its use.
