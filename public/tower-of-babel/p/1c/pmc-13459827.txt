Whole-genome sequence of Rahnella aceris HPDA25 isolated from mycorrhizosphere of Gastrodia elata
Authors: Wenshu Zou, Kangchen Liu, Xiaoai Li, Shuyuan Cheng, Pengjie Han, Honghong Jiao, Zhongyi Hua, Tianrui Liu, Yuan Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe growth, development, and accumulation of active compounds in Gastrodia elata are influenced by its mycorrhizosphere bacteria, which indirectly enhances host nutrition by stimulating the growth of the orchid mycorrhizal fungus Armillaria gallica. However, data on the mycorrhizosphere bacterial community of G. elata, especially on key functional strains, remain limited, restricting their application in medicinal plant cultivation. Here, we report the complete genome sequence of the plant growth-promoting bacterium Rahnella aceris HPDA25, which was isolated from the mycorrhizosphere of G. elata and promotes the growth of A. gallica.Data descriptionThe genome sequence of Rahnella aceris HPDA25, isolated from the mycorrhizosphere of Gastrodia elata, is presented in this paper. The genome of R. aceris HPDA25 was sequenced using the PacBio Sequel platform and assembled into a 5.63 Mb genome comprising one circular chromosome and two circular plasmids, with a contig N50 of 4.98 Mb. Genome completeness and contamination were estimated with CheckM v1.2.3 to be 100.00% and 5.46%, respectively. Gene prediction identified multiple genomic features, including tRNAs, rRNAs, CRISPR loci, genomic islands and prophages. Functional annotation against public databases revealed extensive representation of Gene Ontology (GO) terms, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories, as well as genes related to antibiotic resistance, virulence and pathogen–host interactions. This dataset refines our understanding of bacterial assemblages in the G. elata mycorrhizosphere and provides a genomic resource for dissecting the mechanisms by which HPDA25 promotes fungal and plant growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12863-026-01444-4.Keywords: Mycorrhizosphere bacteria, Rahnella aceris HPDA25, Whole-genome sequencing, Gastrodia elata

Objective

Plant symbiotic microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, archaea, oomycetes, and viruses, colonize specific ecological niches within plants, such as inter-root zones and leaf rings, and establish various interactions, including symbiosis, mutualism, or pathogenicity with their hosts [1–3]. Among these, plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) are a group of microorganisms that form beneficial symbiotic relationships with plants and have been extensively studied in sustainable agricultural systems. These bacteria promote plant growth through both direct and indirect mechanisms, including the synthesis of phytohormones, enhancement of nutrient uptake (notably nitrogen and phosphorus), induction of systemic resistance in plants, and secretion of iron carriers to facilitate the acquisition of iron ions by host plants [4]. Additionally, research indicates that symbiotic microorganisms associated with medicinal plants play a crucial role in their growth and development, as well as in the accumulation of bioactive compounds [5]. Furthermore, these microorganisms are closely linked to the formation of quality characteristics and authenticity of medicinal herbs. Consequently, the strategic use of phytoproducing microorganisms to enhance the biomass of medicinal plants and increase the concentration of medicinal components has emerged as a vital approach to ensure the quality of medicinal herbs and promote sustainable agricultural development.

Gastrodia elata is a valuable Chinese medicinal resource with a history of use spanning over two thousand years in China. Contemporary research has validated its diverse pharmacological properties, including enhancements in cognitive function, neuroprotection, sedation, anti-epileptic effects, and immune regulation. This species belongs to the Orchidaceae family and is characterized as a perennial obligate symbiotic plant. It lacks a root system and exhibits underdeveloped leaves, rendering it incapable of photosynthesis. Consequently, its entire growth and developmental process relies entirely on Armillaria gallica for nutritional support. Therefore, in the context of artificial cultivation, effectively promoting the growth of Armillaria gallica and the formation of mycorrhizal branches is essential for ensuring the yield of G. elata and enhancing the quality of the medicinal material.

Previous work demonstrated that Rahnella aceris HPDA25 is a mycorrhizosphere bacterium isolated from G. elata and functions as a PGPB, with its detailed isolation, culture conditions and growth-promoting functional characteristics fully described in our previous studies [6, 7]. When co - cultured with A. gallica, HPDA25 increased the fungal fresh weight, growth rate, rhizomorph branching, and extracellular laccase activity. In the tripartite symbiotic system of G. elata - A. gallica - R. aceris HPDA25, we investigated that HPDA25 led to an increase in the fresh weight of G. elata tubers and the biomass of A. gallica rhizomorphs. In view of its significant growth - promoting activity and central role in the tripartite symbiosis, HPDA25 was selected for whole - genome sequencing and annotation. The complete genome sequence of R. aceris HPDA25 reported here provides a genomic framework for dissecting genes and pathways involved in plant growth promotion and mycorrhizosphere adaptation.

Materials and methods

Rahnella aceris HPDA25 was isolated from the mycorrhizosphere of Gastrodia elata, and the detailed isolation process, optimal culture conditions and strain preservation methods have been fully described in our previous study [6]. In brief, the strain was first activated on Luria–Bertani (LB) solid medium, then a single colony was picked and inoculated into LB liquid medium, cultured at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 12 h to the logarithmic growth phase. Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and high-quality total genomic DNA was extracted using the SDS method. The purity and integrity of the extracted DNA were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, the concentration was accurately quantified using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and qualified DNA samples were used for subsequent whole-genome sequencing.

The whole genome of R. aceris HPDA25 was sequenced using the PacBio Sequel single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing platform, and the Illumina NovaSeq platform was used for paired-end short-read sequencing to polish the assembly results. Raw sequencing reads were filtered to remove low-quality sequences, adapter contamination and redundant sequences to obtain high-quality clean data.

De novo assembly of the filtered PacBio long reads was performed using HGAP4 software, and the initial assembly results were corrected with Pilon software using Illumina clean short reads to obtain the final high-accuracy complete genome sequence. The completeness and contamination of the assembled genome were evaluated using CheckM v1.2.3 software.

Gene prediction of the assembled genome was performed using GeneMarkS v4.17 software. tRNAscan-SE v1.3.1 and rRNAmmer v1.2 were used to identify transfer RNAs (tRNAs) and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), respectively. CRISPRdigger v1.0 was used to detect clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) loci, IslandPath-DIMOB v0.2 was used for genomic island prediction, and phiSpy v2.3 was used to identify prophage sequences in the genome.

For functional annotation, the predicted coding genes were aligned against the Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) public databases to obtain corresponding functional annotation information. Meanwhile, the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD), Virulence Factors of Pathogenic Bacteria database (VFDB), and Pathogen–Host Interactions database (PHI) were used for the annotation of antibiotic resistance, virulence factor and pathogen-host interaction related genes.

The average nucleotide identity (ANI) values between strain HPDA25 and the type strains of related Rahnella species were calculated using JSpeciesWS online service. The digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were estimated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC 2.1) online platform. The taxonomic status of the strain was determined according to the widely accepted species demarcation criteria of ANI ≥ 95–96% and dDDH ≥ 70%.

Data description

The complete genome of HPDA25 has a total length of 5.63 Mb, comprising one circular chromosome of 4.96 Mb with a GC content of 52.25% and two circular plasmids measuring 0.14 Mb and 0.53 Mb, with GC contents of 51.89% and 52.16%, respectively (Table 1, Data file 1). CheckM v1.2.3 estimated the genome completeness and contamination of HPDA25 to be 100.00% and 5.46%, respectively.

Gene prediction identified 5,246 genes in HPDA25 using GeneMarkS v4.17 [8]. Genomic component analysis of strain HPDA25 revealed 76 transport RNAs (tRNAs), detected with tRNAscan-SE v1.3.1 [9], and 22 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) (5 S, 16 S, 23 S), identified using rRNAmmer v1.2 [10]. Additionally, eight clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat sequences (CRISPRs) were detected using CRISPRdigger v1.0 [11]. Seventeen genomic islands were predicted by IslandPath-DIMOB v0.2 [12], and 27 prephages were identified using phiSpy v2.3 [13]. The general characteristics of the HPDA25 genome are provided in Data File 1 (Table 1, Data file 1).

Functional annotations showed that 3,570 genes were assigned to 44 gene ontology (GO) categories [14]. In addition, 5,004 genes were mapped to 220 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways [15], and 4,204 genes were classified into 24 Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories [16] (Table 1, Data File 2). Screening against specialized databases identified 118 antibiotic resistance genes in the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD), 467 virulence factor–associated genes in the Virulence Factors of Pathogenic Bacteria database (VFDB) [17], and 735 pathogen–host interaction genes in the Pathogen–Host Interactions database (PHI) [18] (Table 1, Data File 3).

Average nucleotide identity (ANI) values between strain HPDA25 and related Rahnella species were calculated using JSpeciesWS (https://jspecies.ribohost.com/jspeciesws/#home). The ANI value between HPDA25 and the type of strain Rahnella aceris Y9602ᵀ was 98.55%, whereas the ANI values between HPDA25 and Rahnella aquatilis CIP 78.65ᵀ, Rahnella bruchi DSM 27398ᵀ, Rahnella inusitata DSM 30078ᵀ and Rahnella variigena CIP 105588ᵀ were 92.62%, 88.28%, 86.06% and 88.99%, respectively (Table 1, Data File 4). As ANI values of ≥ 95–96% are generally considered to indicate strains belonging to the same species, these results support the assignment of strain HPDA25 to R. aceris.

To further assess genomic relatedness, digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were estimated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC 2.1; https://ggdc.dsmz.de/ggdc.php). The dDDH value between HPDA25 and the type strain of R. aceris, SAP-19ᵀ, was 87.5%, whereas the corresponding values between HPDA25 and the type strains of R. aquatilis CIP 78.65ᵀ, R. victoriana DSM 27397ᵀ, R. variigena CIP 105588ᵀ and R. bruchi DSM 27398ᵀ were 48.2%, 36.3%, 34.6% and 32.9%, respectively (Table 1, Data File 5). Since dDDH values ≥ 70% are widely accepted as the species boundary, these data further confirm that strain HPDA25 belongs to Rahnella aceris.

Limitations

The dataset supports the generation of a complete genome assembly for strain HPDA25 using the PacBio Sequel platform, followed by polishing with Illumina paired-end reads. The final assembly comprises a circular chromosome and two circular plasmids. Genome completeness and contamination were assessed with CheckM v1.2.3, indicating 100.00% completeness and 5.46% contamination. As this report focuses on dataset generation and basic genome statistics, functional and comparative analyses of gene clusters potentially involved in plant growth–promoting activity, and experimental validation of their roles in enhancing the growth of Armillaria gallica, remain to be carried out.
