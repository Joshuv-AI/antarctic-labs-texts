Recent developments in analytical techniques for monitoring anabolic–androgenic steroids in biological and environmental matrices: challenges and future opportunities
Authors: Nouf M. Alourfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnabolic–androgenic steroids (AAS) are widely used in clinical practice but are also frequently misused, necessitating reliable analytical methods for their detection in biological and environmental matrices. This review summarizes recent developments in analytical techniques for AAS determination between 2011 and 2024, with emphasis on chromatographic and mass spectrometric approaches. Relevant literature was identified through searches of major scientific databases, including Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed, using keywords related to anabolic–androgenic steroids and analytical detection methods; studies were selected based on relevance to methodological advances. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) remains a robust platform, with high-resolution GC–HRMS enhancing selectivity and retrospective analysis capabilities. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) provides high sensitivity and throughput for targeted quantification, while LC–HRMS enables broader screening through suspect and non-targeted approaches. Advances in sample preparation, including microextraction and automated workflows, have improved analytical efficiency and reduced matrix effects. Emerging platforms such as ion mobility spectrometry and ambient ionization methods offer rapid screening but remain complementary to established techniques. Overall, modern AAS analysis reflects a shift toward integrated analytical strategies combining targeted and high-resolution approaches to address increasing analytical complexity and evolving regulatory demands.

Background and analytical relevance of anabolic-androgenic steroids

Anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS) are a class of synthetic derivatives of testosterone developed to enhance anabolic effects (e.g., protein synthesis and muscle growth) while modulating androgenic activity [1]. Structural modification of the testosterone backbone, such as 17α-alkylation or esterification at the 17β-hydroxyl group, has yielded a diverse range of compounds with varied physicochemical properties, metabolic behavior, and biological activity [1]. Historically, AAS were introduced for legitimate therapeutic purposes, including treatment of hypogonadism, muscle-wasting disorders, aplastic anemia, osteoporosis, and chronic catabolic conditions [2]. However, their non-medical use has expanded substantially over the past several decades, particularly in competitive sports, recreational bodybuilding, and veterinary growth promotion [3,4]. This widespread use and misuse have led to increased regulatory scrutiny and the establishment of stringent monitoring programs, most notably within anti-doping frameworks, food safety surveillance, and environmental protection initiatives [5].

From an analytical perspective, AAS are characterized by several features that complicate their detection and quantification. Many compounds are highly lipophilic, occur at trace or ultra-trace concentrations, and undergo extensive phase I and phase II metabolism, resulting in complex mixtures of parent compounds and conjugated metabolites [6,7]. In biological matrices such as urine, serum, blood, and hair, endogenous steroid backgrounds further obscure exogenous AAS signals, necessitating highly selective and sensitive analytical strategies [8]. In environmental [9] and food matrices [10], including wastewater, surface water, livestock tissues, and dietary supplements, matrix complexity, co-extractives, and low contamination levels present additional analytical hurdles [11].

The adverse health effects associated with AAS misuse, including hepatotoxicity [12], cardiovascular dysfunction [13], endocrine disruption [14], and neuropsychiatric disturbances [15], have reinforced the need for reliable analytical monitoring. Beyond human health concerns, environmental exposure to steroid residues has raised growing alarm due to their endocrine-disrupting potential in aquatic organisms and their persistence in environmental systems [16]. Consequently, AAS analysis is no longer confined to clinical or sports-related contexts but has become an important component of environmental monitoring, food safety control, and industrial regulation.

Accordingly, the following sections examine analytical techniques for AAS detection, with primary emphasis on biological matrices, while selected environmental applications (e.g., wastewater and surface waters) are considered where relevant to exposure pathways and analytical challenges.

These expanding application domains have driven continuous innovation in analytical chemistry. Traditional gas chromatography-based methods, long regarded as the gold standard for steroid analysis, have progressively been complemented or replaced by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), and emerging non-chromatographic and microsampling approaches [17,18]. Advances in sample preparation, particularly microextraction and solvent-minimized techniques, have further improved sensitivity, selectivity, and sustainability [19]. As a result, AAS serve as a representative model class for evaluating how modern analytical methodologies respond to evolving regulatory demands, complex matrices, and the need for high-throughput, reliable detection.

Analysis of AAS

Anabolic–androgenic steroids are associated with well-documented adverse health and environmental effects, prompting sustained analytical interest in their reliable detection and quantification in complex matrices such as urine, blood, and environmental water samples [17,18]. Consequently, the development and refinement of analytical methodologies capable of achieving high sensitivity, selectivity, and robustness have become a central focus of steroid analysis over the past decade. This section presents a narrative overview of analytical approaches for AAS detection based on representative literature, rather than a systematic or quantitative analysis. Relevant literature was identified through searches of major scientific databases (e.g., Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed) using keywords related to anabolic–androgenic steroids and analytical detection techniques. The selection was based on relevance and representativeness rather than formal systematic criteria.

A wide range of analytical methods has been reported for the determination of steroid compounds. Among these, chromatographic techniques remain the most widely applied and analytically reliable tools for the screening and confirmation of doping substances [20]. Gas chromatography (GC) has been extensively employed for the qualitative and quantitative determination of anabolic steroids in biological and environmental matrices [21]. To achieve enhanced analytical sensitivity and lower detection limits, GC and liquid chromatography (LC) are frequently coupled with mass spectrometric detection, including tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) [20]. Gas chromatography– mass spectrometry (GC–MS) has demonstrated excellent robustness and sensitivity for trace-level AAS analysis; however, it typically requires derivatization and is constrained by relatively long analytical run times [22].

In contrast, liquid chromatography and its hyphenated variants, particularly LC–MS/MS, have gained increasing prominence due to reduced sample preparation requirements, improved compatibility with polar and thermally labile analytes, and faster analytical turnaround for many applications. Despite these advantages, LC–MS/MS platforms are associated with higher operational costs, technical complexity, and practical challenges related to throughput and routine implementation [20,23]. In addition, LC–MS performance may be limited for certain steroid classes with poor ionization efficiency, particularly some compounds with fully saturated A-rings, which remain more amenable to GC-based detection [24]. These considerations have driven continued methodological diversification and innovation, encompassing high-resolution mass spectrometry, advanced sample preparation strategies, and alternative analytical platforms.

Gas chromatography–based techniques for AAS detection

Gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometric detection has historically constituted the cornerstone of anabolic–androgenic steroid (AAS) analysis and continues to play a central role in both regulatory and research laboratories. Between 2011 and 2024, GC-based methodologies have undergone substantial refinement, evolving from classical single-quadrupole GC–MS toward more selective and information-rich platforms, including GC–MS/MS and GC coupled to high-resolution mass spectrometry (GC–HRMS). Despite the emergence of alternative liquid-phase and non-chromatographic approaches, GC-based techniques remain analytically relevant due to their robustness, reproducibility, and well-established performance characteristics [21]. Early GC–MS and GC–MS/MS methods developed during the early part of the review period were primarily designed for targeted confirmation of known AAS and their metabolites in biological matrices, particularly urine. These methods typically relied on derivatization strategies, most commonly silylation using reagents such as N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyltrifluoroacetamide, to enhance analyte volatility and thermal stability [25]. Under optimized conditions, limits of detection (LOD) for many AAS were reported in the low ng mL−1 range, with corresponding limits of quantification (LOQ) generally between 1 and 10 ng mL−1, sufficient to meet regulatory requirements in anti-doping and forensic contexts. Triple-quadrupole GC–MS/MS systems operating in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode further improved selectivity by minimizing matrix interferences and reducing false positives, particularly in complex biological samples [25]. In addition, triple-quadrupole GC–MS/MS offered improved scan speed, enabling faster targeted methods and the monitoring of a larger number of analytes within a single run [26].

However, the inherent reliance on derivatization and relatively long chromatographic run times imposed practical constraints on throughput and method simplicity. These limitations became increasingly apparent as analytical demands expanded beyond routine doping control toward broader surveillance applications, including the detection of AAS in dietary supplements, veterinary products, and food matrices. In response, methodological developments between 2014 and 2018 emphasized streamlined extraction protocols, reduced derivatization complexity, and improved chromatographic efficiency [27–29]. Nevertheless, the omission of derivatization has generally been demonstrated only under specific analytical conditions or for limited groups of analytes, and derivatization remains broadly necessary for comprehensive GC-based detection of AAS at low concentrations across diverse matrices [27,30]. In seized pharmaceutical products and supplements, GC–MS workflows achieved reliable detection of multiple AAS with LODs typically on the order of 10 ng g 1, demonstrating suitability for forensic screening in selected solid materials [28].

A major analytical strength of conventional full-scan GC–MS lies in the use of 70 eV electron ionization (EI), which generates rich and reproducible fragmentation spectra that can be compared across instruments and laboratories using extensive spectral libraries [31,32]. This long-established library-based capability has historically facilitated the identification of unknown steroids in untargeted workflows. By contrast, GC–MS/MS, while highly effective for targeted confirmation, provides less information-rich spectra and therefore does not offer the same breadth of library-based unknown identification.

A significant analytical advance during the later review period was the integration of high-resolution mass spectrometry into GC-based workflows. GC–Orbitrap and GC–TOF platforms enabled accurate-mass full-scan acquisition with resolving power exceeding 60,000–200,000 (FWHM), providing enhanced confidence in analyte identification through exact mass measurement and isotopic pattern recognition [33]. Importantly, GC–HRMS did not uniformly outperform triple-quadrupole GC–MS/MS in terms of absolute sensitivity; reported LODs for GC–HRMS methods were often comparable to, rather than substantially lower than, those achieved by optimized MRM-based approaches, typically falling within the sub-ng mL−1 to low ng mL−1 range depending on the matrix and analyte [8,21]. The principal analytical advantage of GC–HRMS lay instead in its selectivity, retrospective data analysis capability, and expanded screening scope, particularly for long-term metabolites and structurally modified or previously uncharacterized steroids [8,21,34].

Importantly, GC–HRMS also retains a major advantage of GC-based analysis more broadly: the use of EI-derived, information-rich spectra that are amenable to spectral comparison and, in some cases, library-supported identification. In this respect, GC–HRMS combines the strengths of full-scan GC spectral richness with the added benefit of accurate-mass information, thereby enhancing confidence in unknown identification and retrospective analysis [35].

The application of GC–HRMS proved especially valuable in metabolism studies and non-targeted screening, where full-scan accurate-mass data facilitated post-acquisition interrogation of archived datasets [36]. This capability enabled the identification of novel or long-term metabolites weeks after administration, substantially extending detection windows relative to conventional targeted methods. Such features are of particular importance in regulatory and environmental monitoring scenarios, where emerging AAS and low-abundance residues may not be included in predefined target lists [37].

Recent studies have also shown that, although uncommon, GC-based methods can under specific conditions be applied to the analysis of intact conjugated steroids, indicating that such capability is not exclusively limited to LC-based platforms [38]. However, these applications remain less broadly established than conventional hydrolysis-based GC workflows and should be regarded as specialized rather than routine approaches.

Despite these advantages, GC-based techniques continue to face intrinsic analytical limitations. The necessity for derivatization introduces additional sources of variability and prolongs sample preparation, while thermally labile or highly polar conjugated metabolites remain less amenable to routine GC analysis without prior hydrolysis [30,39]. Moreover, although GC–HRMS enhances selectivity and expands screening capability, its higher acquisition cost, increased data complexity, and requirement for advanced data-processing expertise may limit routine implementation in industrial or environmental laboratories with constrained resources [40].

Overall, GC-based analytical techniques remain a highly reliable and analytically mature platform for AAS detection, particularly where robust confirmation, regulatory compliance, broad steroid coverage, and long-term metabolite detection are required. Their continued evolution, culminating in high-resolution GC–HRMS, illustrates how classical separation techniques can be successfully adapted to meet contemporary analytical challenges, even as complementary liquid-phase and non-chromatographic approaches gain prominence in parallel.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry techniques (LC –MS/MS and LC–HRMS)

Liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometric detection has become a widely applied analytical platform for the determination of anabolic–androgenic steroids (AAS), particularly in applications requiring high sensitivity, reduced sample preparation, and compatibility with polar or thermally labile analytes [41]. Between 2011 and 2024, LC-based methods evolved from predominantly targeted LC–MS/MS assays toward increasingly sophisticated high-resolution and full-scan strategies, reflecting expanding analytical demands across biological, environmental, and industrial matrices. Targeted LC–MS/MS methods, most commonly employing triple-quadrupole instruments operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, became widely adopted due to their excellent sensitivity, selectivity, and robustness [42]. Unlike GC-based techniques, LC–MS/MS does not require derivatization, enabling direct analysis of underivatized steroids and facilitating the inclusion of conjugated metabolites such as glucuronides and sulfates [43]. Across studies reported during the review period, LC–MS/MS methods routinely achieved limits of detection in the sub-ng mL−1 range for urine [44], serum, saliva [45], and blood matrices [46], with limits of quantification frequently below 1 ng mL−1 and, in optimized workflows, extending into the low pg mL−1 range. Such performance readily satisfies regulatory thresholds for anti-doping, clinical monitoring, and forensic investigations.

However, despite these advantages, LC–MS/MS performance is not uniform across all steroid classes. In particular, certain compounds exhibit limited ionization efficiency, especially under electrospray ionization (ESI) conditions, which may reduce sensitivity for specific structural classes of steroids [47]. As a result, GC-based techniques remain essential in applications such as doping control, where a broad range of structurally diverse steroids must be reliably detected.

Methodological refinement during this period focused heavily on improving throughput and minimizing matrix effects. Strategies such as dilute-and-shoot injection, online or automated solid-phase extraction, and selective immunoaffinity cleanup enabled high sample throughput while maintaining analytical performance [48,49]. In biological matrices characterized by high endogenous steroid backgrounds, these approaches reduced ion suppression and improved quantitative reliability without substantially increasing analytical complexity [50,51]. As a result, LC– MS/MS became a key tool for routine targeted monitoring of known AAS and metabolites across diverse biological matrices.

Despite these strengths, targeted LC–MS/MS approaches are inherently constrained by predefined analyte lists and optimized transitions. As regulatory and monitoring needs expanded to include emerging designer steroids, long-term metabolites, and unanticipated contaminants, these limitations became increasingly apparent [52]. This analytical gap drove the integration of high-resolution mass spectrometry into LC-based workflows. LC–HRMS platforms, including quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) and Orbitrap systems, enabled full-scan acquisition with high mass accuracy, providing enhanced analytical selectivity and enabling broader screening strategies beyond predefined target lists [53,54].

Importantly, LC–HRMS does not universally surpass LC–MS/MS in terms of sensitivity. Reported limits of detection for LC–HRMS methods are often comparable to those obtained with optimized triple-quadrupole assays, typically ranging from low pg mL−1 to low ng mL−1 depending on analyte, matrix, and acquisition mode [55]. Furthermore, LC–HRMS is frequently operated in full-scan mode for screening applications, where detection may rely on a limited number of ions. While accurate mass measurement enhances selectivity, this approach may provide less confirmatory power compared to LC–MS/MS methods that utilize multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions for multi-ion confirmation [56].

The principal advantage of LC–HRMS therefore lies in its ability to support suspect and non-target screening, retrospective data analysis, and the detection of structurally modified or previously uncharacterized AAS [54]. These capabilities are particularly valuable in research and surveillance contexts, where analytical targets may not be fully defined in advance.

LC–HRMS has also demonstrated strong applicability in the analysis of conjugated steroid species. In contrast to conventional GC-based workflows that typically require hydrolysis, LC–MS approaches enable the direct analysis of intact phase II metabolites. However, it should be noted that recent studies have demonstrated that, under specific analytical conditions, intact conjugated steroids may also be analyzed using GC-based techniques, although such approaches remain less commonly applied and are typically limited to specific compounds and experimental setups [38].

Despite these advantages, LC–HRMS faces several practical challenges. Data processing remains complex, requiring advanced computational tools and expertise for feature extraction, annotation, and validation [57]. In addition, the absence of universally applicable spectral libraries remains a limitation for LC–HRMS workflows, particularly in full-scan acquisition, where soft ionization often produces limited fragmentation patterns [58].

Overall, LC–MS/MS and LC–HRMS should be considered complementary analytical tools, rather than competing alternatives. Triple-quadrupole LC–MS/MS remains the most effective platform for high-throughput, quantitative determination of known AAS, while LC–HRMS provides broader screening capability and enhanced flexibility for detecting emerging or unknown compounds. Importantly, this complementarity extends beyond LC-based techniques and similarly applies to GC-based platforms, where targeted and high-resolution approaches provide distinct but complementary analytical advantages.

High-resolution and non-targeted mass spectrometric strategies

The increasing structural diversity of anabolic–androgenic steroids (AAS), coupled with the emergence of novel designer compounds and long-term metabolites, has progressively challenged conventional targeted analytical approaches [7]. In response, high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) has evolved from a confirmatory detection tool into a broader analytical strategy capable of supporting suspect and non-targeted screening across biological, environmental, and industrial matrices. Between 2011 and 2024, HRMS-based workflows have played a pivotal role in extending analytical coverage beyond predefined target lists, addressing limitations inherent to unit-resolution mass spectrometry [59].

A defining feature of HRMS strategies is full-scan accurate-mass acquisition, which enables simultaneous monitoring of known analytes and unknown features within a single analytical run [60]. Sub-ppm mass accuracy, high resolving power, and isotopic pattern recognition substantially enhance confidence in elemental composition assignment and reduce the likelihood of false-positive identifications [60,61]. These capabilities are particularly advantageous in AAS analysis, where structural similarities among steroid analogues and endogenous interferences often complicate identification using nominal-mass transitions alone.

Importantly, HRMS-based strategies do not universally deliver lower limits of detection compared with optimized triple-quadrupole MS/MS methods [61]. Across reported studies, limits of detection for HRMS workflows generally fall within the same order of magnitude as targeted LC–MS/MS or GC–MS/MS assays, ranging from low pg mL−1 to low ng mL−1 in biological matrices and ng g−1 levels in food and environmental samples [8]. The primary analytical advantage of HRMS therefore lies not in enhanced sensitivity, but in selectivity, qualitative confidence, and post-acquisition flexibility [62]. Full-scan datasets allow retrospective interrogation for newly identified AAS, metabolites, or transformation products without the need for reanalysis, a feature of particular relevance in regulatory and forensic contexts [8,63].

However, the performance and capabilities of HRMS are strongly dependent on the analytical platform and ionization technique employed. In LC–HRMS, soft ionization sources such as electrospray ionization (ESI) often generate limited fragmentation in full-scan mode, frequently dominated by (pseudo) molecular ions, which can restrict the applicability of spectrum-based library matching [64,65]. In contrast, GC–HRMS typically employs electron ionization (70 eV), producing rich fragmentation spectra with multiple diagnostic ions that are more amenable to spectral comparison and, in some cases, library-supported identification, analogous to conventional GC–MS workflows [66,67].

Suspect screening represents an intermediate strategy between fully targeted and fully non-targeted analysis and has become a prominent application of HRMS in AAS detection [68]. In this approach, compounds are screened based on predefined candidate lists derived from prior knowledge, using accurate-mass information, predicted molecular formulas, and expected fragmentation patterns to identify chemically plausible structures not included in validated target lists [68]. In contrast, non-targeted analysis does not rely on predefined candidate lists and instead aims to detect all measurable features within a sample. This strategy has proven especially effective for identifying designer steroids and minor metabolites that evade routine screening methods. When combined with reference standards or complementary orthogonal data, suspect screening significantly expands analytical scope while maintaining manageable false-positive rates [69]. Non-targeted HRMS analysis further extends this capability by enabling the detection of previously unknown features without prior assumptions regarding chemical identity [70]. In the context of AAS analysis, non-targeted workflows have been applied to metabolism studies, long-term excretion profiling, and environmental surveillance, where transformation products or low-abundance residues may not be anticipated [71]. However, non-targeted strategies also introduce substantial analytical challenges, including complex data processing, feature prioritization, and limited availability of reference spectra. As a result, confident identification often requires additional confirmatory experiments or complementary analytical techniques [72].

HRMS-based strategies have also demonstrated particular value in addressing complex metabolic behavior of AAS [73]. The identification of long-term metabolites and conjugated species has significantly extended detection windows in biological monitoring, with some metabolites detectable weeks after administration [73]. In environmental and food matrices, HRMS facilitates broader residue screening and supports investigation of contamination pathways, even when regulatory thresholds are low or analytical targets are ill-defined [74].

Despite these advantages, several limitations constrain the routine application of HRMS-based non-targeted strategies. High instrument acquisition and maintenance costs, substantial computational demands, and the need for specialized expertise in data interpretation may limit implementation in routine industrial or regulatory laboratories [58,75]. Furthermore, the availability and applicability of spectral libraries remain platform-dependent. While GC-based HRMS approaches can benefit from EI-derived fragmentation patterns suitable for spectral comparison, comprehensive and universally applicable spectral libraries remain limited for LC–HRMS workflows, particularly in full-scan acquisition [31,76].

In summary, high-resolution and non-targeted mass spectrometric strategies represent a powerful extension of conventional AAS analytical workflows. Rather than replacing targeted methods, HRMS complements established LC–MS/MS and GC–MS/MS platforms by expanding analytical coverage, improving identification confidence in appropriate contexts, and enabling retrospective and exploratory analysis. As analytical requirements continue to evolve, HRMS-based strategies are expected to play an increasingly important role in addressing emerging challenges associated with anabolic–androgenic steroids. Sample preparation and microextraction techniques

Sample preparation remains a critical determinant of analytical performance in the detection of anabolic–androgenic steroids (AAS), exerting a direct influence on sensitivity, selectivity, matrix effects, and overall method robustness [74]. Given the trace-level occurrence of AAS, their structural diversity, and the complexity of biological and environmental matrices, considerable methodological effort between 2011 and 2024 has focused on optimizing extraction and cleanup strategies to enhance analytical reliability while reducing solvent consumption, processing time, and environmental impact.

Conventional solid-phase extraction (SPE) has long served as a reference approach for AAS isolation from biological matrices, particularly urine, serum, and plasma [77]. SPE provides effective matrix cleanup and preconcentration, supporting low limits of detection typically in the sub-ng mL−1 range when coupled with GC–MS/MS or LC–MS/MS detection [49]. However, traditional cartridge-based SPE protocols can be labor-intensive and time-consuming in their classical formats. Importantly, recent developments in miniaturized and high-throughput SPE formats, including 48- and 96-well plate configurations, have significantly improved throughput by enabling parallel processing and automation [78]. These advances have enhanced the applicability of SPE in large-scale analytical workflows.

Microextraction techniques have emerged as prominent alternatives, offering reduced solvent usage, simplified workflows, and improved sustainability in line with green analytical chemistry principles [79]. Solid-phase microextraction (SPME), in particular, has been widely applied to AAS analysis across biological and food matrices [79]. Both fiber-based and in-tube SPME configurations have demonstrated effective extraction of a broad range of steroidal compounds, often achieving limits of detection comparable to SPE-based methods, typically in the low ng mL−1 or ng g−1 range depending on the matrix [80]. While SPME offers clear advantages in terms of solvent minimization and automation potential, extraction efficiency can be strongly influenced by coating chemistry, analyte polarity, and matrix composition, necessitating careful method optimization [81].

Dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (DLLME) and its modified variants represent another important class of solvent-minimized extraction techniques [82]. DLLME has been successfully applied to AAS determination in water [82], urine [83], and food matrices [84], providing high enrichment factors and low detection limits, often approaching sub-ng mL−1 levels when coupled with sensitive mass spectrometric detection [82,84]. Despite these advantages, DLLME workflows may suffer from limited selectivity in highly complex matrices and can exhibit reduced reproducibility if extraction conditions are not tightly controlled [85].

Recent methodological advances have emphasized hybrid and automated extraction strategies designed to improve reproducibility and throughput [86]. Techniques such as in-tube fiber-enhanced extraction solid-phase microextraction (IT-FEX-SPME) and related approaches integrate selective sorbent materials within automated chromatographic systems, enabling online extraction, improved reproducibility, and enhanced throughput. In addition, online solid-phase extraction (online-SPE) and turbulent flow SPE have emerged as important developments, allowing direct coupling of extraction with chromatographic analysis, minimizing manual handling, and significantly increasing analytical throughput while reducing solvent consumption [87,88]. These approaches are particularly valuable in high-throughput clinical, anti-doping, and environmental monitoring applications.

It is important to distinguish sample preparation from sample collection strategies. Techniques such as volumetric absorptive microsampling (VAMS) represent minimally invasive sample collection approaches that enable low-volume sampling and improved sample handling logistics [89]. While VAMS itself does not constitute a sample preparation method, it can be effectively integrated with downstream extraction and analytical workflows, particularly in decentralized or field-based sampling scenarios.

From an analytical performance perspective, it is important to emphasize that improvements in extraction efficiency do not automatically translate into lower instrumental detection limits [90]. Rather, optimized sample preparation primarily contributes to reduced matrix effects, improved signal reproducibility, and enhanced method robustness, thereby enabling reliable quantification at low concentration levels already attainable by modern mass spectrometric detectors [91–93]. In this context, matrix effects such as ion suppression or enhancement are particularly relevant in LC–MS workflows, especially those employing electrospray ionization, whereas GC-based techniques are generally less susceptible to such effects.

In summary, advances in sample preparation and microextraction techniques have played a pivotal role in shaping contemporary AAS analytical workflows. The progressive shift from classical SPE toward miniaturized, automated, and solvent-reduced approaches reflects broader trends in analytical chemistry toward sustainability, high-throughput capability, and operational efficiency. When appropriately matched with chromatographic and mass spectrometric detection platforms, these strategies provide robust and versatile solutions for the analysis of anabolic–androgenic steroids across biological, environmental, and industrial matrices.

Emerging and alternative analytical platforms

In parallel with advances in chromatographic and high-resolution mass spectrometric techniques, a range of emerging and alternative analytical platforms has been explored for the detection of anabolic–androgenic steroids (AAS), particularly in contexts where rapid screening, on-site analysis, or simplified workflows are required. These approaches are not intended to replace established GC –MS or LC–MS methodologies, but rather to complement them by addressing specific analytical needs related to speed, portability, or preliminary assessment.

Sensor-based analytical methods, including electrochemical and biosensor platforms, have received increasing attention for AAS detection [94]. These systems typically exploit specific interactions between steroid molecules and recognition elements such as molecularly imprinted polymers, antibodies, or nanostructured materials [95]. Reported limits of detection for sensor-based assays generally fall within the ng mL−1 range, which is sufficient for certain screening applications but remains inferior to mass spectrometry-based methods in terms of selectivity and multi-analyte capability [95,96]. However, in complex biological matrices, the presence of endogenous compounds may lead to nonspecific interactions and signal interference, potentially affecting analytical selectivity and quantitative reliability [97]. As a result, biosensor-based approaches are generally more suitable for preliminary screening or simplified matrices, rather than definitive analysis in complex biological samples.

Ion mobility spectrometry (IMS), when coupled to mass spectrometry, represents another emerging analytical approach with potential relevance to AAS analysis [98,99]. IMS provides an additional gas-phase separation dimension based on molecular size, shape, and charge, enabling improved discrimination of isomeric and isobaric steroid species [100,101]. This capability is particularly valuable given the structural similarity among many AAS and their metabolites [101]. While IMS– MS does not inherently improve instrumental sensitivity, it enhances analytical selectivity and confidence in compound identification, especially when integrated with high-resolution mass spectrometric detection [98,102].

Ambient ionization mass spectrometry techniques, such as paper spray, direct analysis in real time, and related approaches, have also been explored for rapid AAS detection [103]. These methods enable minimal or no chromatographic separation and often require limited sample preparation, allowing analysis times on the order of seconds to minutes [103,104]. Reported detection limits for ambient ionization techniques typically range from low ng mL−1 to ng g−1, depending on the matrix and ionization conditions [104,105]. While such performance may be adequate for qualitative screening of supplements, seized materials, or surface residues, the lack of chromatographic separation can result in significant matrix effects and reduced quantitative reliability in complex biological or environmental samples [106,107].

Lab-on-a-chip and microfluidic platforms represent a further area of methodological innovation, integrating sample handling, separation, and detection within miniaturized analytical systems [108,109]. Although still largely confined to proof-of-concept studies, these approaches offer potential advantages in terms of reduced reagent consumption, rapid analysis, and portability [108,110,111]. However, their application to AAS detection remains limited by challenges related to sensitivity, robustness, and integration with highly selective detection systems capable of handling complex matrices [112,113].

From an analytical performance standpoint, it is important to recognize that emerging and alternative platforms generally exhibit higher limits of detection and lower selectivity than chromatographic mass spectrometric methods [114,115]. Their primary value lies in rapid screening, qualitative assessment, and preliminary decision-making, rather than definitive confirmation or regulatory compliance [114,115]. Consequently, these techniques are most effective when deployed as part of a tiered analytical strategy, in which initial screening is followed by confirmatory analysis using validated GC–MS/MS or LC–MS/MS workflows [116,117].

In summary, emerging and alternative analytical platforms contribute valuable capabilities to the broader AAS analytical landscape, particularly in applications requiring speed, portability, or simplified operation. While their current limitations preclude routine use as standalone confirmatory methods, continued technological refinement may further enhance their applicability in industrial, environmental, and regulatory monitoring frameworks. When integrated with established analytical techniques, these platforms expand the versatility and responsiveness of contemporary AAS detection strategies.

Conclusions, challenges, and future perspectives

Between 2011 and 2024, analytical strategies for the detection of anabolic–androgenic steroids (AAS) have evolved in parallel with expanding analytical demands across doping control, clinical monitoring, environmental surveillance, and food safety applications. Early in the review period, targeted GC–MS/MS and LC–MS/MS approaches formed the foundation of analytical practice, providing robust and regulatorily accepted workflows for confirmatory quantification. Subsequent developments in high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) have further expanded analytical capabilities, particularly in the context of suspect and non-target screening.

Despite substantial methodological refinement, several analytical challenges persist. The structural diversity and metabolic complexity of AAS, including extensive phase I and phase II biotransformation, long-term metabolite formation, and the continual emergence of designer analogues, complicate purely target-based analytical strategies. Although HRMS has expanded analytical coverage and enabled retrospective data interrogation, confident identification of unknown or emerging compounds remains constrained by the availability and applicability of spectral libraries, which differ significantly between analytical platforms. GC-based techniques benefit from well-established EI-based spectral libraries, whereas LC–HRMS workflows are more limited in this regard, particularly under full-scan conditions where fragmentation information may be restricted.

Matrix effects continue to represent an important analytical consideration, particularly in complex biological and environmental samples. These effects are especially relevant in LC–MS workflows employing electrospray ionization, where ion suppression or enhancement can affect quantitative accuracy. In contrast, GC-based techniques are generally less susceptible to such matrix-induced ionization effects, although other sources of variability may still arise.

From a quantitative perspective, instrumental sensitivity is no longer the primary limitation in AAS analysis. Modern GC–MS/MS and LC–MS/MS systems routinely achieve detection limits well below regulatory thresholds. Instead, method robustness, reproducibility, and inter-laboratory harmonization remain key challenges. Variability in extraction protocols, calibration strategies, and data-processing workflows continues to limit comparability across studies and applications. The development of standardized validation protocols and improved access to certified reference materials will therefore remain essential priorities.

The increasing use of HRMS and non-targeted workflows introduces additional challenges related to data processing, feature prioritization, and result interpretation. While HRMS enables broader analytical coverage, its performance is highly dependent on acquisition strategy and data-processing workflows, and careful method design is required to ensure reliable identification and minimize false positives.

Advances in sample preparation, including microextraction techniques, automated workflows, and high-throughput SPE formats, have contributed significantly to improving analytical efficiency and sustainability. These developments align with broader trends in green analytical chemistry but must be balanced with method robustness and validation requirements to ensure reliable routine application.

Emerging analytical platforms, including ion mobility spectrometry, ambient ionization techniques, and sensor-based approaches, offer valuable opportunities for rapid screening and decentralized analysis. However, their current limitations in selectivity and quantitative reliability, particularly in complex biological matrices, restrict their role to complementary or tiered analytical strategies rather than standalone confirmatory methods.

Overall, advances in AAS analysis over the past decade reflect a trend toward methodological integration rather than technological replacement. GC–MS and LC–MS/MS remain essential for targeted, quantitative determination, while HRMS-based approaches expand analytical scope through suspect and non-target screening. Importantly, these complementary relationships apply across both GC- and LC-based platforms, with each offering distinct advantages depending on analytical objectives.

Future perspectives

Future progress in AAS analysis will likely focus on improved harmonization of analytical protocols, expansion of spectral libraries, development of more robust data-processing frameworks, and continued integration of sustainable and high-throughput methodologies. Over the next 5–10 years, HRMS-based workflows are expected to become increasingly integrated into routine analytical practice, particularly for retrospective analysis and the detection of emerging or previously unrecognized steroid analogues. Advances in bioinformatics, machine learning-assisted data interpretation, and automated feature annotation may further improve the reliability and efficiency of non-target screening approaches.

At the same time, continued developments in sample preparation, miniaturized analytical systems, and portable screening technologies may support faster and more decentralized testing strategies. Nevertheless, targeted GC–MS/MS and LC–MS/MS methods are expected to remain central confirmatory tools because of their robustness, sensitivity, and regulatory acceptance. Future analytical progress will therefore depend not on replacement of established techniques, but on the continued integration of complementary analytical platforms capable of addressing the growing complexity of AAS detection across clinical, environmental, forensic, and anti-doping applications.
