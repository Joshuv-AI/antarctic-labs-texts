Application and prospects of brain-computer interface technology for motor function reconstruction after brachial plexus injury
Authors: Su Song, Xia Li, Pinglei Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrachial plexus injury (BPI) is a severe peripheral nerve disorder leading to significant upper limb motor dysfunction. While traditional surgeries like nerve grafting and tendon transfer exist, functional outcomes are often suboptimal due to biomechanical limitations and slow neural recovery. Brain-computer interface (BCI) technology has emerged as a promising innovative pathway for motor function reconstruction.ObjectiveThis review systematically evaluates the current applications, physiological mechanisms, and technical challenges of BCI technology specifically within the clinical framework of BPI rehabilitation.MethodsWe analysed recent research breakthroughs focusing on neural repair mechanisms, clinical translational applications of BCI-controlled neuroprosthetics, and the integration of novel biomaterials.ResultsBCI technology facilitates cortical remapping after standard BPI procedures like nerve transfers by providing synchronised closed-loop feedback. Unlike applications for amputees that drive external prosthetics, BCI in BPI focuses on in-situ muscle activation via a “neural bypass” to prevent disuse atrophy and restore a sense of agency. Furthermore, BCI-mediated neuromodulation shows unique potential in alleviating chronic deafferentation pain by down-regulating pathological cortical hyperexcitability. Emerging technologies like conductive hydrogels and hybrid BCI systems are addressing current bottlenecks in signal stability and control accuracy.ConclusionBCI technology represents a transformative approach for BPI rehabilitation, moving from mechanical substitution to biological reactivation. Overcoming technical barriers in signal reliability and establishing personalised rehabilitation systems are essential for their broad clinical translation.

Introduction

In neurorehabilitation medicine, brain-computer interface (BCI) systems have brought innovative solutions for the restoration of motor dysfunction. Recent studies have confirmed that with the continuous improvement in the accuracy of neural electrical signal decoding, this technology demonstrates outstanding application value in the field of motor function rehabilitation. Particularly for cases of brachial plexus injury (BPI), BCI research has overcome the inherent limitations of conventional therapies. Traditional interventions such as nerve grafting and tendon transfer often yield suboptimal results, whereas BCI systems control assistive devices by directly decoding cortical activity signals, thereby establishing a new model for functional compensation. Clinical trial data indicates that this technology can significantly enhance upper limb motor function in patients with paralysis. After implementing a BCI-assisted intensive training program, subjects exhibited notable improvements in motor control capabilities. The technical principle of BCI allows for real-time processing of cortical potential signals, enabling subjects to manipulate exoskeletons or smart prosthetics through conscious activity, thereby completing self-controlled movements [1]. This breakthrough not only improves patients’ daily living abilities but also expands the research directions for the development of rehabilitation medicine.

Currently, the application of Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) technology in the field of brachial plexus nerve injury is still in the preliminary research stage, with several urgent issues yet to be resolved. The primary concern is to enhance the accuracy and reliability of neural signal interpretation, which directly impacts the practical implementation of this technology. Existing literature indicates that the operational efficiency of BCI devices is significantly constrained due to the time-varying characteristics of brain electrical activity and the interference of environmental noise [2]. In light of this, developing novel signal analysis methods and optimising hardware configurations have become essential pathways to facilitate the medical translation of this technology. Furthermore, tailoring the system to the specific needs of patients is also an important direction for development. Given the significant heterogeneity of electroencephalographic (EEG) characteristics among individuals, researchers need to focus on how to optimise system parameters based on the physiological characteristics of different cases, thereby maximising the effectiveness of motor function rehabilitation [3]. Implementing customised assessment and rehabilitation plans will effectively enhance the clinical benefits of BCI interventions. In terms of compensating for motor function in brachial plexus nerve injuries, this technology has already demonstrated outstanding application value. With the continuous advancement of technological iteration and clinical validation, BCI may become a key component of the neurological rehabilitation system. The related achievements not only open new avenues for improving the quality of life for patients but also construct a theoretical framework for expanding the medical application scenarios of brain-computer interaction technology.

Pathophysiological basis and clinical challenges of brachial plexus injury

Classification and pathological mechanisms of brachial plexus injury

Brachial plexus nerve injuries can be classified into three main types based on anatomical features: upper trunk type, lower trunk type, and total brachial plexus injury. Upper trunk injuries primarily affect the C5-C6 nerve roots, with clinical manifestations including reduced abduction and external rotation of the shoulder joint. Lower trunk injuries typically occur in the C8-T1 nerve root region, characterized by difficulties in fine motor skills of the hand. Total brachial plexus injuries involve the entire nerve plexus structure, with typical symptoms including complete loss of upper limb motor function and sensory loss; the clinical recovery probability for such injuries is relatively low [4,5]. From a pathological perspective, the core mechanisms of these injuries include disruption of axonal continuity and secondary Wallerian degeneration. Axonal structural rupture caused by external forces disrupts the conduction of nerve impulses, while Wallerian degeneration is characterized by progressive degeneration of the distal nerve fibers beyond the site of rupture, accompanied by pathological changes in the neuronal cell bodies leading to cell death. This process involves complex molecular biological events, such as programmed cell death, localized inflammatory cascades, and the activation of regeneration-related signaling pathways. Existing studies have confirmed that biochemical changes in the microenvironment during the early stages of injury have a decisive impact on the regenerative potential of nerve fibers, directly regulating the spatiotemporal characteristics of the regeneration process [6,7].

The regeneration process of brachial plexus nerve injury presents diverse challenges depending on the type of injury. Patients with upper trunk injuries may achieve better recovery of shoulder function, while those with lower trunk and total brachial plexus injuries face greater difficulties in functional reconstruction. Clinical studies indicate that early surgical intervention can optimize prognostic outcomes for specific types of injuries, particularly after Wallerian degeneration. Timely neuromuscular repair and regeneration measures can significantly enhance the probability of functional rehabilitation [8,9]. Accurately distinguishing the clinical types of brachial plexus nerve injuries and elucidating their pathological basis are crucial for establishing therapeutic strategies. A comprehensive understanding of the anatomical features and pathological evolution of various injuries aids in the development of individualised treatment systems, thereby effectively improving patients’ rehabilitation outcomes and quality of life [10,11].

Limitations of conventional treatment approaches

In the clinical intervention strategies for brachial plexus nerve injury, conventional treatment methods, such as nerve grafting and tendon transfer, can partially improve patient functionality; however, their applicability and efficacy stability are significantly constrained. Nerve grafting is primarily suitable for cases of complete nerve transection or severe nerve injury, particularly those involving severe damage to the C5-C7 cervical nerve roots. Although this surgical procedure can achieve functional reconstruction, there are significant individual differences in clinical outcomes. Multiple studies have confirmed that the surgical results are closely related to variables such as patient age, duration of injury, and the nature of nerve damage. Data indicate that younger patients generally exhibit a faster and greater degree of functional recovery post-surgery compared to older populations, who often require a longer rehabilitation period to achieve the expected therapeutic effect [12,13].

Tendon transfer surgery has inherent shortcomings in functional compensation. Although this procedure can partially restore limb motor capabilities, it cannot fully replicate the physiological functions of native muscles. Postoperatively, patients often need to rely on auxiliary compensatory methods to maintain daily activities. For instance, in elbow joint movement, patients frequently recruit non-target muscle groups to achieve flexion and extension actions, which not only reduces movement coordination but also affects exercise efficiency [14,15]. Beyond the universal challenge of postoperative pain, the clinical efficacy of tendon transfer is primarily constrained by its inability to fully replicate native muscle synergy. Patients often exhibit suboptimal motor coordination due to the inherent loss of donor muscle function at its original site [14,15]. Similar to the technical bottlenecks in BCI-mediated functional reconstruction, these biomechanical limitations underscore the need for more integrated rehabilitation strategies [16]. Existing studies indicate that conventional therapies have notable deficiencies in pain control and functional reconstruction, particularly concerning neurogenic chronic pain, where mere pharmacological intervention often fails to achieve ideal analgesic effects. The latest clinical evidence suggests that incorporating physical therapy alongside complementary medical approaches (such as acupuncture) into treatment plans can significantly enhance the clinical benefits of traditional therapies [13,17]. Although classical treatment models still hold a foundational position in managing brachial plexus nerve injuries, their clinical shortcomings have gradually become apparent. Research focus should shift towards developing innovative treatment systems, particularly integrative intervention plans that combine cutting-edge technologies with traditional therapies, thereby optimizing patient prognosis and quality of life.

Fundamental principles and classification of brain-computer interface technology

Technical characteristics of invasive and non-invasive BCI

The rapid advancement of Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) technology has made it an important means for establishing direct interaction between the human brain and external devices, enabling precise control without relying on the peripheral nervous system or muscular tissue. Based on the method of implementation, BCI systems can be mainly categorised into invasive and non-invasive types, each possessing distinct advantages in terms of technical characteristics and application domains. In terms of neural signal acquisition, Electrocorticography (ECoG) and Electroencephalography (EEG) are currently the two mainstream monitoring methods. ECoG technology utilises electrode arrays implanted in the cerebral cortex to obtain neural electrical activity with excellent temporal and spatial characteristics, which enables outstanding performance in motion command interpretation and real-time feedback systems. However, the implementation of ECoG requires craniotomy, which can lead to complications such as infections, and the clinical compliance of patients is relatively limited. In contrast, EEG adopts a non-invasive detection mode, recording brain electrophysiological activities through scalp electrodes, characterised by ease of operation, high cost-effectiveness, and good safety. Nevertheless, EEG signals are susceptible to environmental interference, and their spatial resolution is relatively limited, resulting in significantly insufficient decoding accuracy when processing complex commands. This limitation leads to an application bottleneck for EEG in the medical rehabilitation field, where precise control is required [18,19].

As a core component of invasive brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), microelectrode systems represented by the Utah array exhibit cutting-edge capabilities in this field. These devices, surgically implanted into brain tissue, can accurately capture the electrophysiological signals of neurons, with their excellent signal-to-noise ratio and resolution providing reliable assurance for neural decoding. However, maintaining long-term performance after implantation remains a critical issue that requires resolution. Experimental data indicate that the implantation of microelectrodes activates the immune system, leading to local inflammation and subsequently resulting in complications such as signal attenuation and electrode-tissue interface failure [20,21]. Notably, breakthroughs in novel biomaterials and interface engineering are gradually enhancing the durability of implanted devices, creating favourable conditions for clinical translation. From a technological perspective, invasive and non-invasive BCI systems exhibit significant differences in performance metrics, and the actual selection must weigh multiple factors such as application scenarios, patient tolerance, and potential risks. Current research directions in academia present a dual pathway: on one hand, optimising signal acquisition and processing algorithms for non-invasive systems; on the other hand, enhancing the biological stability of implanted devices through material modification and structural design, thereby promoting the synergistic development of both types of technologies in the medical field [22,23].

Neural decoding algorithms for motor imagery and motor execution

Motor Imagery (MI) and the neural signal analysis methods for actual motor behavior are core research topics in the field of Brain-Machine Interaction (BMI). The primary objective is to analyze the neural electrical activity recorded by scalp electroencephalography (EEG) to identify the characteristic brain functional patterns of subjects during motor imagery or actual limb movement. This technical process mainly encompasses two key stages: the selection of signal features in specific frequency bands and the construction of intelligent classification models. In studies related to motor imagery, the μ rhythm (8–12 Hz) and β rhythm (13–30 Hz) are regarded as important analysis targets, as these rhythms exhibit unique neurophysiological changes during the state of motor imagery. Specifically, the energy decrease of the μ rhythm (i.e. event-related desynchronization, ERD) often occurs alongside the motor imagery process, while the fluctuation characteristics of the β rhythm show differential changes depending on the type of motor task [24]. In the feature extraction phase, various computational strategies have been developed in academia. Among them, Common Spatial Pattern (CSP) is a classic EEG signal classification technique that enhances the distinguishability of different task states by optimizing spatial filters. However, this method’s classification performance is easily affected by inter-subject variability and faces challenges such as complex parameter optimization and low computational efficiency [25]. To overcome these limitations, an improved narrow-band adaptive CSP (Narrow-band Adaptive CSP, NBACSP) algorithm has been proposed. Its advantage lies in the ability to adaptively select the optimal frequency band, significantly enhancing computational efficiency while ensuring classification accuracy [25].

In the field of motor imagery decoding, deep learning methods have shown significant application prospects. Currently, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have become a common tool for processing Electroencephalography (EEG) signals, as their automatic feature extraction mechanism effectively addresses the technical challenges of traditional manual feature selection. Recent research findings indicate that the hybrid architecture of 3D Convolutional Neural Networks and Generative Adversarial Networks (3D-CNN-GAN) not only optimises the classification performance of motor imagery but also significantly enhances the model’s adaptability across sessions and subjects [26]. This approach expands the training samples through data augmentation strategies, effectively improving the algorithm’s generalisation performance. In addition to deep learning, traditional machine learning methods still hold significant value in motor imagery decoding. As a representative classifier, Support Vector Machines (SVM) have been well-established in the field of EEG signal processing. Recent innovative work has combined deep learning with SVM to develop a Multi-Feature Fusion Convolutional Neural Network (MF-CNN), which can process both raw EEG data and its time-frequency transformation features in parallel, resulting in a significant improvement in the recognition rate of unilateral limb motor imagery [27]. In the field of temporal modelling, researchers have utilised Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to analyse multi-channel EEG signals, achieving breakthrough classification accuracy due to their excellent modelling capabilities for temporal dependencies [28]. The decoding technology of movement-related neural signals is showing a trend towards increased intelligence. By integrating various technological approaches such as frequency domain feature analysis, machine learning, and deep learning, significant advancements in the practical application of brain-computer interfaces in motor function rehabilitation are anticipated. These technological breakthroughs not only expand the research dimensions of movement imagination decoding but also pave innovative pathways for clinical rehabilitation treatments.

Current applications of BCI in rehabilitation of brachial plexus injuries

Clinical studies on neural prosthesis control

The synergistic application of targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) and brain-computer interface (BCI) in the field of neuroprosthetic control has demonstrated significant therapeutic value. TMR has demonstrated efficacy in reducing neuroma-related pain in amputee populations. However, its application as a primary analgesic tool for BPI-specific deafferentation pain is not yet supported by standard clinical evidence. Instead, BCI technology addresses the unique pain profile of BPI—such as tearing sensations from nerve root avulsion—by modulating pathological cortical hyperexcitability through goal-directed motor imagery, rather than through peripheral rerouting like TMR. Experimental data indicate that subjects who underwent TMR intervention can achieve a high operational accuracy of 95%-99% when controlling prosthetics via BCI, confirming the reliability and efficacy of this technological framework under diverse experimental conditions [29]. Furthermore, the application of TMR technology enhances patients’ psychological acceptance of prosthetics, facilitating their adaptation to and use of assistive devices, ultimately improving their quality of life. In the domain of neuroprosthetic regulation, closed-loop functional electrical stimulation (FES) mechanisms also play a critical role. This technology utilises electrical currents to stimulate and activate muscle tissues, achieving functional compensation. A newly developed closed-loop FES control system possesses the ability to dynamically adjust stimulation parameters, adapting in real-time to the user’s activity state. This system continuously monitors muscle activity characteristics through a biofeedback mechanism, optimising the electrical stimulation scheme to achieve precise muscle control effects. Clinical validation data indicate that this closed-loop system is superior to conventional methods in reconstructing motor functions, significantly enhancing patients’ autonomy in movement [30]. These research findings not only expand the technological pathways for neuroprosthetic regulation but also provide theoretical support for clinical translational applications.

The primary clinical challenge in BPI remains the functional integration of transposed nerves, such as the phrenic or intercostal nerves. BCI technology facilitates the mandatory cortical remapping required for patients to repurpose these. To address this issue, novel neural interface technologies such as Regenerative Peripheral Nerve Interfaces (RPNI) have emerged, aimed at optimizing control signals for prosthetics. This technology involves transplanting severed peripheral nerves into muscle tissue, which can effectively enhance the stability of nerve signals and the signal-to-noise ratio, thereby significantly improving the reliability of real-time prosthetic control [31]. The successful implementation of RPNI has opened new avenues for long-term stable control of prosthetics in clinical settings. Research indicates that Targeted Muscle Reinnervation (TMR) combined with Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI), supplemented by a closed-loop control mechanism using Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES), fully demonstrates the application prospects of neuroprosthetic technology in the field of motor function reconstruction. The related outcomes not only enhance clinical efficacy but also lay a theoretical foundation for the development of devices and personalized treatment plans. With the deepening of clinical research, this technology is expected to achieve simultaneous improvements in precision and adaptability across diverse application scenarios, ultimately significantly enhancing the quality of life for patients. While targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) and regenerative peripheral nerve interfaces (RPNI) are established strategies for amputees, they are not currently standard reconstructive options for BPI. In this context, these techniques are envisioned as experimental biological interfaces to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio for BCI signal acquisition. Rather than a primary surgical treatment, they may serve as potential signal amplifiers to improve decoding accuracy in cases where traditional nerve transfers provide limited neural output.

A unique clinical hallmark of BPI management is the reliance on nerve transfer (neurotization), such as using the phrenic nerve or intercostal nerves to restore elbow flexion. However, this procedure introduces a significant challenge: the brain must learn to interpret ‘respiratory signals’ as ‘arm movement commands,’ a process known as cortical remapping. Traditional rehabilitation often struggles with the slow and stochastic nature of this plastic change. BCI technology offers a transformative solution by establishing a synchronised closed-loop system. By decoding the patient’s motor imagery of the target function (e.g. grasping) and pairing it with simultaneous functional electrical stimulation (FES) or haptic feedback, BCI can effectively ‘guide’ the motor cortex to reorganise. This spatiotemporal precision in neural feedback accelerates the functional integration of transposed nerves, potentially shortening the lengthy recovery window typical of plexus-level injuries and distinguishing BPI rehabilitation from the external prosthetic control seen in amputee populations [14,32,33].

A fundamental distinction in BCI applications lies between amputee populations and BPI patients. While amputees utilise BCI primarily to drive external robotic prosthetics, BPI patients retain their physical limbs, which suffer from total or partial denervation. In this context, BCI-controlled Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES) serves a dual purpose. First, it acts as a ‘neural bypass’ that circumvents the injured plexus structure by translating cortical motor intentions directly into muscle-stimulating currents. Second, this in-situ muscle activation is critical for preventing disuse atrophy of the native musculature and maintaining the integrity of the musculoskeletal system. By delivering synchronised electrical cues to the denervated muscles during motor imagery, BCI-FES systems can re-establish the proprioceptive feedback loop, restoring the patient’s sense of agency and control over their own limbs. This shift from mechanical substitution to biological reactivation addresses the unique clinical needs of plexus-level injuries [16,34–37].

Beyond motor reconstruction, BCI technology offers a unique therapeutic avenue for managing the chronic neurogenic pain specifically associated with brachial plexus avulsion. Unlike the phantom limb pain typically observed in amputees, BPI patients frequently suffer from severe deafferentation pain, characterised by burning or tearing sensations due to the maladaptive plasticity and abnormal hyperexcitability of the primary motor cortex (M1). BCI systems can be designed to suppress this pathological cortical activity by engaging the patient in goal-directed motor imagery tasks. By reinforcing functional neural pathways and restoring a more physiological state of cortical excitation, BCI-mediated training can effectively ‘down-regulate’ the pain-related circuits. This dual-function approach—simultaneously promoting motor recovery and alleviating chronic pain—addresses a critical clinical bottleneck in BPI rehabilitation that traditional pharmacological or surgical interventions often fail to resolve [35–37].

Rehabilitation mechanisms induced by neural plasticity

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) evidence of motor cortex reorganisation

Neuroplasticity refers to the intrinsic ability of the central nervous system to adapt to new environments by remodeling neural connections and functionally compensating in response to trauma or pathological changes. In the rehabilitation process for brachial plexus nerve injury, the functional remodeling of the motor cortex plays a critical role. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), as a non-invasive detection method, can dynamically observe the activation patterns of the brain while executing motor commands, providing objective evidence for the phenomenon of motor cortex functional remodeling. Experimental data confirm that in cases of stroke or brachial plexus nerve injury, the neural circuits of the motor cortex undergo significant reorganization. fMRI detection has shown that the affected motor cortex may exhibit a gradual reconstruction of function during the rehabilitation phase, and this functional remodeling is significantly correlated with the improvement of the patient’s motor abilities. With the continued implementation of rehabilitation training, the neural activity of the unaffected motor cortex shows an increasing trend, while the affected cortex also demonstrates heightened activation characteristics, suggesting that the central nervous system is compensating for functional deficits by strengthening neural circuits [38]. Further research indicates that the design parameters of rehabilitation training programs (including training intensity and frequency) can significantly affect the remodeling process of the motor cortex. High-intensity exercise interventions can stimulate the secretion of neurotrophic factors, thereby accelerating the functional reorganization and recovery of the motor cortex [39]. With the aid of fMRI technology, researchers can accurately locate cortical areas associated with specific motor functions, and the activation characteristics of these areas are clearly linked to the clinical rehabilitation outcomes of patients. As an important tool for studying the remodeling of the motor cortex, fMRI not only provides crucial neurobiological markers but also deepens our understanding of the mechanisms of neural plasticity following brachial plexus injuries. These research findings lay a theoretical foundation for optimizing clinical rehabilitation programs and open new avenues for subsequent neurological rehabilitation studies.

Molecular regulatory pathways of synaptic long-term potentiation (LTP)

Long-term potentiation (LTP), as a key manifestation of neural plasticity, is characterized by a persistent enhancement in synaptic transmission efficacy induced by high-frequency electrical stimulation. This biological phenomenon has been widely recognized as the physiological basis for the formation and maintenance of cognitive functions. Clinical observations have revealed a significant correlation between functional reconstruction following neural trauma and the induction process of LTP. Research on brachial plexus nerve injury has confirmed that this phenomenon involves the dynamic secretion of neurotransmitters, the functional state of receptors, and the synergistic action of intracellular signaling networks. Experimental data indicate that brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) plays a decisive role in the establishment and persistence of LTP. This factor triggers multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK, by specifically binding to the TrkB receptor, ultimately leading to the dynamic restructuring of synaptic architecture. Notably, the mechanisms underlying LTP are also closely related to fluctuations in intracellular calcium ion levels—calcium influx can activate calcium-dependent protein kinases such as calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), thereby strengthening synaptic connection strength. Rehabilitation medicine practice indicates that a well-designed exercise intervention program can effectively stimulate long-term potentiation (LTP) effects, thereby optimizing the functional connectivity of neural circuits. Specifically, exercise stimuli can upregulate the biosynthesis of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), creating favorable conditions for the generation of LTP, ultimately promoting compensatory neural functions. Recent clinical evidence confirms that the combination of physical therapy and neuromodulation techniques significantly enhances the success rate of LTP induction, which has positive implications for improving motor function disorders [39]. A thorough analysis of the molecular regulatory network of LTP not only helps to elucidate the biological basis of neural plasticity in functional rehabilitation but also provides a theoretical foundation for the selection of innovative rehabilitation treatment targets. Future research should focus on exploring how to enhance the efficiency of functional recovery after neural injury by intervening in LTP-related signaling pathways.

Current technical challenges and solutions

Signal attenuation and drift issues

The performance stability of brain-machine interface (BMI) systems is often constrained by signal attenuation and drift phenomena. The formation of glial scarring around chronically implanted electrodes is a primary factor contributing to signal degradation, with its formation process involving multiple mechanisms such as chronic inflammatory responses, extracellular matrix remodelling, and neuronal regenerative functions. The foreign body reaction triggered by electrode implantation stimulates the proliferation of glial cells, which subsequently form an isolating barrier. This structure not only impairs the conductive properties of the electrode but also leads to distortion and baseline shifts in neural electrical signals [36]. Experimental data indicate that the dynamic interaction at the electrode-brain tissue interface alters the characteristics of the local microenvironment, thereby inducing nonlinear distortions in the signals. To address this technical challenge, researchers are working on developing intelligent filtering technologies with self-calibrating parameters. These algorithms significantly improve the stability of signal transmission by dynamically optimising filtering parameters to match real-time signal characteristics. Specifically, a multi-channel neural signal compensation system constructed based on the recursive least squares principle has been shown to effectively suppress signal disturbances caused by variations in electrode-tissue contact impedance [40]. More advanced algorithms achieve drift correction by establishing a time-varying model of signal features, thereby comprehensively enhancing the system performance of brain-computer interfaces while maintaining the integrity of the original information. Currently, research on signal stability issues is showing a multidimensional development trend: in the pathological domain, there is an in-depth analysis of the mechanisms of glial scar formation, and in the technical domain, innovative adaptive signal processing solutions are being developed. Future research will focus on the optimisation of electrode interface materials and the collaborative improvement of algorithm architectures, aiming for a breakthrough in the reliability of brain-computer interface systems [41,42].

Accuracy limitations in multi-degree-of-freedom control

In the field of brain-computer interface (BCI) technology, the implementation of multi-degree-of-freedom control faces several technical challenges during the recovery of motor function after brachial plexus nerve injury. Among these challenges, the personalized characteristics of the motor cortex topology and the efficiency enhancement scheme of hybrid brain-computer interfaces (hBCI) constitute key limiting factors. The heterogeneity of motor cortex representation among individuals directly restricts the accuracy of multidimensional motor control. Due to biological variations in the neural structure of the human brain and the spatial distribution of the motor cortex, this specificity poses significant challenges to the neural decoding of motor intentions. Neuroscience research has confirmed that different subjects may exhibit pronounced heterogeneity in cortical neural electrical activity when performing the same action, a phenomenon that reduces the signal parsing efficiency of BCI systems [43]. During the execution of hand movements, the distribution of excitation areas in the motor cortex of specific subjects may display unique characteristics, and this variation can affect the consistency and reproducibility of electroencephalogram (EEG) signals. Therefore, the development of BCI systems must focus on addressing individual adaptation issues to enhance control accuracy. The collaborative optimization scheme of hybrid brain-computer interfaces (hBCI) can effectively improve the precision constraints in multidimensional control. This system integrates various bioelectrical signals (including EEG and surface electromyography, sEMG) and enhances the decoding efficiency and response timeliness through the collaborative analysis of multimodal physiological signals. Experimental data indicate that the use of multi-source signal fusion technology can significantly improve the accuracy of intention recognition in complex motor paradigms [44]. The optimization of signal processing workflows and feature selection algorithms using artificial intelligence technologies, such as machine learning and deep neural networks, can significantly enhance the overall performance of brain-computer interface (BCI) systems, achieving precise motor function control objectives [45]. This optimization method not only improves control accuracy but also significantly enhances the system’s adaptability to individual differences. The factors that restrict the accuracy of multi-degree-of-freedom control in BCI technology are complex, primarily stemming from the variability of users’ physiological characteristics and their synergistic effects with the system optimization schemes. Future research can be developed in two main directions: on one hand, deepening the analysis of the individual-specific mechanisms of the motor cortex; on the other hand, improving the architectural design of hybrid BCIs to optimize the clinical outcomes of motor function reconstruction and enhance the user experience (Figure 1).

Future directions and interdisciplinary integration

Breakthrough applications of novel biomaterials

The field of neural engineering is witnessing rapid advancements in biomaterial innovation, particularly in the restoration of motor function following brachial plexus nerve injuries. Conductive hydrogel electrodes, as a class of emerging functional materials, have garnered significant attention from the academic community due to their outstanding electromechanical coupling properties. These electrode materials exhibit excellent tissue compatibility and efficient electrical signal transmission capabilities, creating an ideal technological platform for neuroelectrophysiological monitoring and electrical stimulation interventions. Experimental data confirm that structurally optimized conductive hydrogel electrodes demonstrate exceptional electrical conductivity and mechanical stability at the biological tissue interface, laying a solid foundation for their potential applications in brain-machine interaction systems [46]. Optogenetic modulation strategies have opened innovative research directions for nerve injury repair. This technology precisely manipulates neuronal excitability through optical means, playing a crucial role in the reconstruction of motor function. Researchers have found that integrating optogenetic modulation modules with conductive hydrogel electrode systems enables spatially specific regulation of neural network activity, effectively promoting axonal regeneration and functional remodeling. Recent studies have confirmed that this synergistic strategy not only enhances nerve repair efficiency but also significantly reduces the reliance on conventional pharmacological therapies, thereby expanding the diversity of treatment options [47,48]. The synergistic application of conductive hydrogel electrodes and optogenetics marks a breakthrough in the research of neural engineering materials. With the aid of these advanced functional materials, enhanced functional regeneration of neural injuries may become achievable through these advanced interfaces. While surgical alignment of nerve stumps has reached high anatomical precision, the challenge remains in promoting effective axonal elongation and target reinnervation. The synergy of conductive hydrogel electrodes and optogenetics offers a platform for precise neuromodulation, which can provide highly localized electrical and optical cues to guide axonal growth and accelerate the restoration of neural circuit connectivity.

Construction of personalized rehabilitation system

The field of rehabilitation medicine is increasingly emphasizing the establishment of customized treatment plans, particularly in the context of using brain-computer interface (BCI) technology for functional reconstruction following brachial plexus nerve injuries. This targeted intervention model not only optimises clinical efficacy but also significantly enhances patient treatment compliance and rehabilitation beliefs. This study elucidates the architecture of a mobile home rehabilitation platform integrated with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), as well as how digital twin technology can achieve intelligent adaptation of training parameters. Wearable rehabilitation devices utilising fNIRS technology create a dynamic treatment environment for patients. This non-invasive neuroimaging method possesses excellent temporal and spatial resolution capabilities, allowing for continuous capture of brain functional states, making it an ideal monitoring tool for BCI systems. Research has shown that BCI tasks based on fNIRS signal decoding can accurately identify the neural activity characteristics of patients, thereby providing an objective basis for the formulation of precise rehabilitation plans [49]. The lightweight nature of this device enables patients to complete rehabilitation training in home settings, thereby improving their psychological comfort and treatment adherence. The intelligent home rehabilitation device employs an adaptive feedback system to dynamically adjust training parameters and modes, aligning with the varying recovery needs of patients at different stages, thus achieving individualised dynamic intervention. The application of digital twin technology in the design of rehabilitation programs highlights its advantages in the field of customised treatment. By constructing digital twins of patients, healthcare professionals can predict and evaluate rehabilitation progress in a simulated environment. This technology not only enhances the precision of treatment strategies but also aids clinical decision-making through multi-scenario simulations, optimising training plans. Continuous tracking of patients’ physiological indicators and neural signals allows rehabilitation programs to be dynamically adjusted based on real-time feedback, accurately responding to individualised needs. This data-driven, customised intervention model not only improves rehabilitation outcomes but also strengthens patients’ awareness of active participation [50]. The integration of cutting-edge technology into personalised rehabilitation frameworks provides more precise technical support for the clinical application of brain-computer interface technology following brachial plexus injuries. The collaborative application of portable home rehabilitation devices and digital twin technology meets individual difference requirements while enhancing patients’ self-management capabilities and rehabilitation participation, thereby optimising overall treatment ­outcomes. As technology evolves and clinical experience accumulates, the importance of customised rehabilitation systems in the field of neural function reconstruction will become increasingly prominent.

Conclusion

In the clinical intervention of patients with brachial plexus nerve injury, brain-computer interaction systems (BCI) demonstrate groundbreaking application value by achieving highly customized functional reconstruction strategies through technological advantages that surpass conventional therapies. The synergistic development of neuroprosthetic control technology and neural remodeling mechanisms not only expands the avenues for motor function rehabilitation but also promotes a deep integration of neural engineering and basic medical research. However, significant technical barriers still exist in the clinical translation of this technology, with the reliability of signal acquisition being a major limiting factor. Environmental noise interference and insufficient decoding algorithm efficacy can significantly reduce the system’s response speed and recognition accuracy. Furthermore, the synchronous integration of multi-source bioelectrical signals and the real-time feedback mechanism require improvement, as they are core elements for enhancing the system’s practicality. Future research and development should focus on breakthroughs in two aspects: developing implant materials with excellent tissue compatibility to extend device lifespan, and improving multi-source information fusion models to enhance the system’s ability to analyze complex physiological signals. System optimization tailored to patient specificity is another critical dimension for the clinical application of BCI. Considering the heterogeneity of injury severity, rehabilitation goals, and individual physiological parameters, implementing BCI configuration schemes under a precision medicine model will significantly enhance intervention outcomes. This necessitates the establishment of an interdisciplinary collaboration network that integrates the expertise of neuro-medicine specialists, bioengineers, and rehabilitation physicians, achieving seamless integration of technological innovation and clinical practice. In the context of the interdisciplinary integration of neuroscience, materials science, and artificial intelligence, brain-computer interface (BCI) technology may become a significant option for the rehabilitation treatment of brachial plexus nerve injuries. The academic community is focusing on optimizing technological performance and enhancing patient experience to facilitate the clinical application of this technology. This collaborative research paradigm across multiple disciplines can accelerate the advancement of BCI technology and provide a reference model for innovations in other medical technologies. Although BCI technology has significant advantages in the functional reconstruction of the brachial plexus nerves, the full release of its potential still requires breaking down disciplinary barriers, continuously addressing key technological challenges, improving system architecture, and establishing customized treatment systems. Only by achieving substantial progress in critical areas such as technological reliability, system compatibility, and individualized rehabilitation strategies can this technology bring revolutionary changes to effectively restore motor function and enhance social participation confidence for patients with brachial plexus nerve injuries.
