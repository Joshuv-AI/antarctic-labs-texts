Sex differences in lipid profile and response to statin treatment in pediatric patients affected by familial hypercholesterolemia
Authors: Isabella Tarissi De Jacobis, Elisabetta Straface, Elena Inzaghi, Camilla Cittadini, Maria Bartuli, Maria Teresa Pagano, Annalisa Grandin, Paola Sabrina Buonuomo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Differences between males and females in lipid profile are observed in distribution and trajectory during pediatric age and are more pronounced in hereditary lipid disorders such as familial hypercholesterolemia when absolute cholesterol levels are higher from birth onwards. A retrospective observational study was undertaken to explore the sex differences in lipid profiles of pediatric patients affected by heterozygous form of familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) and treated with statins. A cohort of 322 pediatric patients, aged 2-17 years, diagnosed with heterozygous FH and regularly followed in Rare Disease and Medical Genetics Unit of the Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital of Rome (Italy) from 2015-2024, was enrolled in this study. A subgroup of 112 patients, aged 8–17 years, was treated with statins. For all patients, the lipid profile was assessed at diagnosis. For patients treated with statins, the lipid profile was assessed at diagnosis and after 1 year of statin therapy. Differences between males and females in lipid profile were observed in the subgroup of patients aged 8-17 years. Females had significantly (p < 0.05) higher TC, LDL-C and TG plasma values than males, and clinically they respond better to statin treatment.Conclusion: Personalized therapy, accounting for these differences, could enhance treatment outcomes and optimize long-term cardiovascular health in affected children. What is Known:• Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is characterized by elevated plasma level of total cholesterol from the birth.• If left untreated, FH can lead to premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and young female with FH are at high risk of developing it.• FH girls have higher levels of TC, LDL-C, and TG plasma values levels than boys in a previous study and this may contribute significantly to the total lifelong cholesterol burden in FH women.What is New:• Significant sex differences in lipid profile were confirmed in heterozygous FH (HeFH) patients aged 8-17 years: females had significantly higher TC, LDL-C and TG plasma values than males.• Early statin treatment in children with FH, and girls in particular, may impact cholesterol burden in FH patients.

Introduction

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is a codominant monogenic disorder characterized by elevated plasma level of total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) from the birth that, if untreated, can lead to premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [1].

FH is caused by mutations in the genes encoding LDL- receptor (LDLR), apolipoprotein B (APOB) and pro-protein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), that lead to reduced clearance of LDL-C from the bloodstream [2, 3]. While homozygous form (HoFH) is rare, with a prevalence of about 1 in 250.000 - 360.000 and plasma values of TC > 400 mg/dl; the heterozygous form (HeFH) is much more common, has a prevalence of about 1 in 250 - 300 and plasma values of TC > 220 mg/dl [4]. Being an autosomal semi-dominant disease, the prevalence of FH is similar in both males and females [5]. They have a similar age at diagnosis and similar total plasma cholesterol levels. However, it has been reported that, compared to males with FH, young females with FH have a higher risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [5] and an increased risk of acute myocardial infarction (MI). Typically, females with FH are diagnosed approximately 3-7 years later than males. This results in increased cholesterol burden in women compared to men already at a younger age.

In the general population sex-based disparities in lipid metabolism and response to statin therapy have been well-documented [6]. In brief, females are diagnosed later, and they have higher cholesterol levels compared to males.

These differences might be further amplified in individuals with genetic disorders like HeFH, where the underlying genetic defect could interact with sex hormones and other biological factors to modulate lipid profiles [7].

Emerging evidence suggests that there may be sex differences in the lipid profiles of pediatric patients with HeFH, which could potentially influence both the clinical presentation of the disease and its response to treatment [5].

Holven et al. found that FH girls have higher levels of TC, LDL-C, and non–HDL-C levels than boys from birth up to 19 years of age [8].

The main therapeutic approaches for FH are lifestyle advice and lipid-lowering drug therapy. Lifestyles include a healthy diet and physical activity, while lipid-lowering drug therapy consist of the use of statins [7]. Statins are the first-line therapy used to reduce both TC and LDL-C plasma levels and lower atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk, especially in HeFH. They selectively inhibit hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) reductase, a key enzyme in the synthesis of cholesterol in the liver [9]. Statin treatment is generally well tolerated in children, and lead to clinically significant reductions in LDL-C and TC [10]. Longer-term observational evidence indicates that statins are both safe and effective at preventing cardiovascular events over the longer-term, and that greater reductions in LDL-C are associated with a significantly reduced risk of coronary heart disease [11].

Although statins are commonly prescribed to children and adolescents for HeFH, most pediatric research focuses on the overall safety and efficacy of statins in children, without deeply analyzing potential sex-based variations in response to the drug. Genetic factors may play a more significant role in cholesterol metabolism in children, which may overshadow sex-based differences in how statins work.

This study is aimed to explore the sex differences in lipid profiles of pediatric patients affected by HeFH. Awareness of sex differences in FH allows earlier initiation of treatments, reducing cardiovascular disease risks, and leading to better patient care.

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective observational study of a cohort of pediatric patients diagnosed with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH) regularly followed in Rare Disease and Medical Genetics Unit of the Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital of Rome (Italy) from 2015-2024.

A total of 322 (160 males and 162 females) patients genetically diagnosed with HeFH, aged between 2 and 17 years, were enrolled. A total of 298 patients came from Italy, while 14/322 (4%) came from the EU and 10/322 (3%) from the rest of the world. Italian patients were from different regions, especially from the South of Italy: 10 from Abruzzo, 3 from Basilicata, 11 from Calabria, 24 from Campania, 5 from Emilia Romagna, 204 from Lazio, 1 from Lombardia, 14 from Marche, 2 from Molise, 11 from Puglia, 4 from Sicilia, 2 from Toscana, 5 from Umbria and 1 from Veneto.

A subgroup of 112 patients, aged 8 to 17 years, was treated with statins. Patients aged 8 years were treated with pravastatin 20 mg daily, while those aged 10 years and older were treated with atorvastatin 10 mg daily. The presence of one pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the LDLR, APOB, or PCSK9 genes through genetic testing was mandatory for enrollment. Clinical data, including age, sex, height, weight, and laboratory data were obtained from patients’electronic medical records at the time of diagnosis for all patients included in the study.

Lipid profile was evaluated in blood samples collected in the morning after an overnight fast, by Enzymatic Assay Kit (ADVIA Chemistry, Bayer). For all patients, the lipid profile was assessed at diagnosis. If age and lipid levels required start of statin therapy, clinical and laboratory data were collected at diagnosis and after one year of treatment.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A written informed consent was obtained from parents of patients before genetic test was performed.

Ethical review and approval have been waived. This is an observational study that did not involve any new procedures or tests for the patient, no personal data were involved, but only approaches to patient management procedures in clinical practice were studied.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with descriptive and analytical methods using the Prism 6 statistical package (GRAPHPAD Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk was employed to examine the normal distribution of data, depending on their appropriateness. Data were parametric and were analyzed using the two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni test. To evaluate the correlation between age (continuous variable) to quantitative variables were used Pearson’s rank-order correlation coefficient. The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all tests.

Results

At diagnosis, all enrolled HeFH patients (322 patients) had TC and LDL-C values higher than age- and sex-matched reference values. In particular, we observed a mean TC value of 275 mg/dl in males and 273 mg/dl in females and a mean LDL-C value of 215 mg/dl in males and 212 mg/dl in females. For both sexes HDL-C and TG values were in the physiological range. In fact, males had a mean value of 51.9 mg/dl for HDL-C and a mean value of 72 mg/dl for TG, while females had a mean value of 52 mg/dl for HDL-C and a mean value of 73 mg/dl for TG.

It is interesting to note that in all patients analyzed (322 patients aged 2 to 17 years) no gender differences were observed for all parameters analyzed (data not shown).

Conversely, we found that in the subgroup of patients (112 patients) aged 8 to 17 years treated with statin, females had significantly (p < 0.05) higher plasma levels of TC, LDL-C and TG at diagnosis than males. As shown in Fig. 1 treatment with statins: 1) significantly lowered plasma TC (Fig. 1a) and LDL-C (Fig. 1b) values in both males and females (TC males: p = 0,0001; TC females: p = 0,001; LDL-C males: p=0,0001; LDL-C females: p=0,0001); 2) modestly increased HDL-C levels in both sexes (Fig. 1c) and 3) did not affect TG levels in either sex (Fig. 1d).

Our data indicate that there are no significant differences in the effects of statins between the two sexes, although we observed that, after statins treatment, TC levels decreased by 27.8% in males and 29% in females, while LDL-C levels decreased by 34.4% in males and 37.2% in females.

Discussion

In the present study, we analyzed a homogenous cohort of pediatric patients affected by genetically confirmed HeFH and with values of TC and LDL-C higher than reference levels. A subgroup of these patients aged 8–17 years was treated for 1 year with statins. As already observed in previous studies and consistent with findings from van Oortmerssen et al. (2025), Holven et al. (2018) and Klevmoen et al. (2023), sex-based differences in lipid profiles were evident at diagnosis [6, 8, 12]. Specifically, we found that females had significantly higher plasma levels of TC, LDL-C, and TG than males (p < 0.05). These differences align with prior literature showing that girls with FH tend to accumulate a higher LDL-C burden from early life onward [6].

Interestingly, statin treatment significantly reduced TC and LDL-C plasma levels in both sexes (TC males: p < 0.0001; TC females: p < 0.001; LDL-C males: p < 0.0001; LDL-C females: p < 0.0001). No significant sex differences were observed post-treatment.

Clinically, this is highly relevant, suggesting that statin therapy mitigates early sex-based differences in atherogenic lipid levels. Moreover, females showed a slightly greater LDL-C reduction (37.2% vs. 34.4%), although not statistically significant. This trend is consistent with emerging findings that, despite delayed diagnosis and less intensive treatment, women may respond comparably or even more favorably to statins [6].

Only in male HeFH patients, by Pearson’s correlation analysis, a significant inverse correlation between age and LDL-C (r = - 0.32; p = 0.0217) was found. This may reflect increased cholesterol utilization during puberty for steroid hormone biosynthesis, as suggested by Holven et al. (2018) [8] and elaborated in the review by van Oortmerssen et al. (2025) [6], who explain that cholesterol is the precursor for estrogen and testosterone synthesis and is actively used in puberty, particularly in males. Its use for hormone production could explain the decrease in plasma cholesterol levels during puberty.

Gender differences in atherosclerotic disease risk and lipid metabolism evolve throughout life. According to van Oortmerssen et al. (2025) [6], lipid profiles in females undergo dynamic changes during key hormonal transitions such as puberty, pregnancy, and menopause. During pregnancy, for example, LDL-C levels increase by 30–50%, and women with FH experience even more pronounced absolute increases, heightening their long-term cardiovascular risk. These transitions underscore the importance of initiating lipid-lowering therapy early, particularly in females with FH.

As mentioned above, FH often leads to the development of early ASCVD. Therefore, if diagnosed and treated early in childhood, individuals with FH can have normal life expectancy [13]. Thus, the main goal of FH treatment is to reduce plasma cholesterol levels [4]. Statins are the first-line therapy used to reduce both TC and LDL-C plasma levels and lower ASCVD risk [14]. They act by inhibiting endogenous cholesterol synthesis and increasing hepatic uptake of LDL-C. In the present study we found that one year of statin treatment significantly reduces TC and LDL-C plasma values in both sexes (Fig. 1). Patients aged 8 years were treated with pravastatin 20 mg daily, while those aged 10 years and older were treated with atorvastatin 10 mg daily. These treatments were well tolerated by patients and in both sexes no changes in transaminase values were detected (data not shown).

Gender differences in the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk are well known. In the atherosclerotic process, plaque composition varies by gender. In ischemic heart disease, female gender is associated with worse outcomes than male gender [15].

In a study Mulder et al., highlighted that, compared to the general population, young people with FH have a higher risk of developing coronary heart disease, and that this incidence is higher in females than in males. He also suggests treating patients at an early age and paying more attention to young women with FH [5, 16].

Understanding gender differences could not only improve the clinical management of HeFH, but also guide gender-specific therapeutic strategies, optimizing outcomes for both male and female pediatric patients.

The implication is clear: biological sex is not a minor variable but a fundamental factor influencing lipid trajectory, treatment response, and cardiovascular outcomes across the lifespan. Our findings reinforce the need for early and aggressive intervention in girls with FH, as well as continuous support to maintain therapy during vulnerable transitions such as adolescence and reproductive years. Nevertheless, these preliminary data are clinically informative and emphasize the urgent need for sex- and age-sensitive therapeutic strategies.

Ultimately, recognizing sex differences in lipid metabolism is a critical step toward truly personalized medicine in the management of familial hypercholesterolemia. Females exhibit more atherogenic lipid profiles than males from early childhood, leading to a greater cumulative cholesterol burden over time.

In conclusion, the results of our study confirm the importance of a personalized approach to the treatment of heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH) in pediatric patients, with particular attention to sex differences.

A limitation of our study is the single-center cohort, which may not fully capture the ethnic and regional diversity of the broader Italian population.

Further research with larger sample sizes and longitudinal follow-up is necessary to better understand the mechanisms behind these sex-based disparities and refine therapeutic approaches for this genetically predisposed group.
