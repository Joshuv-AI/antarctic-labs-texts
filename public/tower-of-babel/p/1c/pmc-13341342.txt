Outcome and cost‐effectiveness of transdiagnostic cognitive behavioral therapy compared with management as usual for youth with common mental health problems: Long‐term results from the Mind‐My‐Mind randomized trial
Authors: Ditte Vassard, Martin Køster Rimvall, Rasmus Trap Wolf, Robin Christensen, Sabrina M Nielsen, Kerstin Jessica Plessen, Frank Verhulst, Niels Bilenberg, Per Hove Thomsen, Mikael Thastum, Simon‐Peter Neumer, Anne Katrine Pagsberg, Wendy K Silverman, Christoph U Correll, Pia Jeppesen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCredible long‐term outcomes from randomized trials evaluating the effectiveness and cost‐effectiveness of preventive programs for mental health problems are needed. We compared long‐term effects of the Mind My Mind (MMM) transdiagnostic cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) program to management as usual (MAU).MethodsThe study was a pragmatic, multisite, randomized superiority trial (2017–2019) involving youths aged 6–16 years with anxiety, depressive symptoms, and/or behavioral disturbances, recruited through family self‐referral. The MMM intervention included 9–13 weekly CBT sessions. The primary outcome was change from baseline in parent‐reported impact of mental health problems at 3‐year follow‐up post‐randomization using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) impact scale. Register‐based outcomes tracked youths' psychiatric diagnoses in mental health services. To assess cost‐effectiveness, we calculated the incremental costs and incremental quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs). All primary analyses followed the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) approach. Clinical trials registration: ID NCT04804917.ResultsAmong 396 youths randomized (baseline mean [SD] age, 10.3 [2.4] years; 52.0% boys; MMM n = 197, MAU n = 199), the 3‐year follow‐up (median 167 weeks; range 124–203 weeks) primary outcome data were available in 69.0% and 59.3%, respectively. The decrease in SDQ‐impact‐score from baseline to 3‐year follow‐up (4.12→1.79 points [MMM] and 4.21→1.85 [MAU]) was similar (between‐group difference, 0.06 [95% CI −0.41 to 0.52]; p = .81). An equal proportion (25%) of youths in MMM and MAU were diagnosed with any mental disorder during follow‐up (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.68–1.50). Total costs over the intervention period were higher in the MMM group (incremental costs 3,014 Euros [95% CI: 2.174–3.855]). Cost‐effectiveness analyses favored MMM: QALY net gain 0.121 (95% CI 0.045–0.196); the cost‐effectiveness ratio was 24,789 Euro/QALY.ConclusionsAlthough MMM was potentially cost‐effective, the beneficial effects diminished over 3 years post‐treatment. The findings highlight the need for strategies to sustain long‐term effects.Keywords: Cognitive behavioral therapy, mental health problems, school‐based, children and adolescents

Introduction

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is widely accepted as first‐line treatment for children and adolescents with common mental health problems, including anxiety (Walter et al., 2020), depression (National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence, 2019), and conduct problems (National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence, 2013), and generally yields moderate effect sizes in youths (Weisz et al., 2017). Long‐term follow‐up of randomized clinical trials beyond 12 months is scarce, often limited by naturalistic and uncontrolled designs (Gibby, Casline, & Ginsburg, 2017), and the existing evidence suggests that beneficial effects of CBT regarding anxiety and depression are most often not sustained beyond 1 year follow‐up (Furlong et al., 2013; Hetrick, Cox, Wiit, Bir, & Merry, 2016; James, Reardon, Soler, James, & Creswell, 2020).

Over the past decade, a transdiagnostic approach to delivering CBT for indicated prevention has shown similar effects as diagnosis‐specific interventions (Barlow et al., 2017; Fusar‐Poli et al., 2019; Rapee et al., 2023), potentially increasing scalability and swift delivery of evidence‐based psychological treatments (Schaeuffele, Schulz, Knaevelsrud, Renneberg, & Boettcher, 2021). Effects of transdiagnostic CBT on emotional problems and disorders have been shown to be sustained until up to 1‐year follow‐up (Alaee et al., 2022; Fernández‐Martínez, Orgilés, Morales, Espada, & Essau, 2020; Schwartz et al., 2023), and one study indicated some persistence of effects after 2 years (Chorpita et al., 2013).

To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies examining whether treatment benefits of transdiagnostic approaches are durable beyond 1 and 2 years. The absence of such data is a huge gap in knowledge empirically and clinically. Empirically, it is a gap because intervention developers/evaluators need to know whether efforts to improve those interventions are required given that the interventions, while beneficial in the short term, may not be in the long term. Clinically, it is a gap because clinicians and families need to know the current limits of the interventions being delivered and received, respectively, so that reasonable expectations are instilled, with an eye towards ascertaining how durability might be improved for a given case.

Guided by these empirical and clinical gaps, we conducted a 3‐year follow‐up study of the Mind My Mind (MMM) transdiagnostic manualized CBT program. In addition to parent‐reported outcomes, we examined register‐based administrative outcomes to ensure follow‐up data on adverse outcomes for all youth participants enrolled in the initial RCT, along with cost‐effectiveness analyses. Since MMM was designed for indicated prevention targeting youths with mental health problems below the threshold for psychiatric treatment, the adverse outcomes include the broad spectrum of clinically significant psychiatric illnesses and treatments. Results of the RCT involving 396 youths showed that MMM was effective in reducing parent‐reported impact of mental health problems compared with community management as usual (MAU) in a clinically meaningful manner, effect size 0.6, measured 18 weeks after allocation to treatment (Jeppesen et al., 2021). MMM also improved secondary outcomes, including symptoms of anxiety, depression, behavioral problems, school attendance, and everyday functioning with maintained effects after 26 weeks (Jeppesen et al., 2021). Cost‐utility analyses suggested that the intervention was likely cost‐effective (Wolf et al., 2022). Finally, the study of effect modification showed superior treatment benefits of MMM compared to MAU for the majority of youths with mental health problems that fulfilled DSM‐IV/−5 criteria for at least one mental disorder (Rimvall et al., 2023). To note, three‐year follow‐up refers to the approximate follow‐up for the set of analyses included in this study, although every separate analysis has specific adaptations, which will be described in detail.

We hypothesized that the beneficial effects of MMM, relative to MAU, at the end of the 17‐week treatment period and sustained at 26 weeks could be maintained at the 3‐year follow‐up regarding the primary and key secondary outcomes, as well as a lower risk of psychiatric diagnosis in hospital‐based mental health services, plus better health‐related quality of life for the MMM group when compared to the MAU group in the intention‐to‐treat population.

Methods

Study design for the main Mind‐My‐Mind trial

The MMM RCT was designed as a pragmatic, open‐label, evaluator‐ and analyst‐masked, parallel, 2‐arm, superiority trial comparing MMM to MAU, with enrollment in the RCT from September 7, 2017, to December 18, 2018 (Jeppesen et al., 2021). A total of 396 youths were recruited in four Danish municipalities (Holstebro, Helsingør, Vordingborg, Næstved) in a school‐based primary care setting, and randomized n = 197 to MMM or n = 199 to MAU (Jeppesen et al., 2021). There were minor amendments to trial sampling methods after the trial commenced (see Jeppesen et al., 2021). We followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline (Moher et al., 2010). The statistical methods and prespecified reporting of the current 3‐year follow‐up were pre‐defined in two statistical analysis plans (Appendices S1 and S2) for the analyses of questionnaire‐based and register‐based outcomes, respectively. Clinical trials registration: 3‐year Follow‐up of the Mind My Mind RCT (MindMyMindFU), https://classic.clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04804917; ID NCT04804917. Three sets of analyses are reported: the questionnaire‐based follow‐up, the register‐based follow‐up, and cost‐effectiveness analyses.

Ethical considerations

The scientific ethics committee approved the trial (Journal‐nr.: 17011408, Date of approval: June 20th, 2017) and legal guardians provided written informed consent. Data management and protection complied with the European Union General Data Protection Regulation.

Participants

Recruitment for the RCT was performed via family self‐referral (formal referral not required) (Jeppesen et al., 2021). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age 6–16 years and in 0–9th grade (excluding the second semester of the 9th grade); (2) Scores reported by the parent above the lower cutoff on the SDQ: total difficulties score of ≥14 and/or emotional problems ≥5, and/or conduct score ≥3; combined with a functional impairment score of ≥1, see ‘measures and outcomes’ section for details on the SDQ; (3) A top psychological problem of anxiety, depressive symptoms, or behavioral problems formulated by the psychologist's and the family's collaborative assessment at the screening interview; (4) The child and ≥1 parent with sufficient Danish language; (5) Informed consent. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Overt neurodevelopmental or severe mental disorder, substance misuse, and repeated self‐harm; (2) Indications of intellectual functional impairment, severe learning difficulties, or other special needs; (3) Prior diagnosis made in specialized mental healthcare services; (4) Prior participation in the MMM pilot or current study; and (5) Inability to participate in weekly sessions. See Appendix S1, pp. 13–14, for further details on inclusion/exclusion criteria.

Out of 573 youths screened for eligibility, 75 (13%) had mental health problems below the SDQ threshold, 52 (9%) were excluded due to severe mental health problems, and 50 (9%) were excluded for other reasons (e.g., no consent or practical barriers from participating in weekly sessions). See recruitment‐procedure details elsewhere (Jeppesen et al., 2021). Thus, 396 (69%) were included in the RCT. The Development and Well‐Being Assessment (DAWBA) (Goodman, Ford, Richards, Gatward, & Meltzer, 2000) was used for diagnostic assessment before randomization. Considerations regarding sample‐size calculation were presented with the original RCT (Jeppesen et al., 2021).

Randomization and blinding

Allocation concealment 1:1 to MMM or MAU was performed through centralized randomization through the Data & Documentation department in the Central Denmark Region who had no role in the analyses. Participants were stratified based on region (two strata), child age (6–10 vs. 11–16 years), and top problem of the child (anxiety, depression, or behavioral problems). Evaluators and analysts were masked to group allocation (see Appendix S1, p. 9 for details).

Interventions

MMM is a transdiagnostic and modular CBT program for children and adolescents with anxiety, depressive symptoms and/or behavioral problems. The program focuses on identifying and changing maladaptive patterns of cognition and behavior through methods such as psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring, problem‐solving, and exposure techniques, that is, the common elements of disorder‐specific CBTs for childhood emotional and behavioral disorders.

We developed a transdiagnostic and modular CBT manual for emotional‐behavioral problems thereby facilitating the implementation of evidence‐based CBT for a large and heterogeneous group of youths in a flexible manner. The MMM manual was recently translated into English (Appendix S3). The therapy was individually tailored by dosing and sequencing of the modules to match the problems at hand and delivered over 9–13 sessions during a 17‐weeks period, followed by one booster session. Parents were engaged in their child's therapy. Parent management training was offered for youth behavioral problems. Educational psychologists delivered the therapy after 1 week of training, followed by weekly supervision. The youths were the primary recipients of CBT for anxiety and depressive symptoms and for behavioral problems in adolescence, with parents present either throughout their sessions or at the end of sessions depending on the age and developmental needs of the young person. For younger children with behavioral problems, the parents were the main recipients of the intervention and agents of change of their child's behavior.

The MAU group was offered two additional coordinating care visits provided by psychologists in the municipality (at week 2 and week 17) to support them in seeking help for the child. MAU varied considerably from no intervention to counseling, talk therapy, manualized group therapy, pedagogical advice, network meetings, and/or individual support in the school setting. Youths received on average 11 (SD = 2.6) sessions in MMM and 1.6 (SD = 0.6) coordinating care visits in MAU (Jeppesen et al., 2021). See the original MMM paper for intervention details (Jeppesen et al., 2021).

Three‐year follow‐up

Outcomes for the questionnaire‐based follow‐up and the register‐based follow‐up were on average collected 3 years after randomization, but with specific variations in follow‐up time as specified in this section. The questionnaire‐based data collection was conducted between March 22, 2021, and August 31, 2021. We invited all parents of participants who had not withdrawn their consent to participate through a mandatory electronic mailbox for citizens in Denmark to the questionnaire‐based follow‐up (reply time 20 min). Upon non‐response, we sent two reminders and parent(s) were contacted by telephone. The median follow‐up time was 167 weeks, ranging from 124 to 203 weeks due to the discrepancy between the 1½‐year enrollment period and the brief ½‐year period with administration of questionnaires for the 3‐year follow‐up. For the register‐based follow‐up, we retrieved follow‐up data using the Danish population registers for a duration of 2.0–4.1 years (until September 2021) after the random allocation to MMM or MAU (Appendix S2). Using the personal ID number assigned to all Danish residents, we could link individual‐level data to psychiatric diagnoses from the Danish National Patient Register, redeemed prescriptions for psychotropic medication, and administrative records of notifications of concern to the municipality and school absence during follow‐up.

The cost‐effectiveness‐analyses covered the longest possible follow‐up period for which all 396 children had the same follow‐up time in the registers from their baseline assessment at randomization, ensuring that quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) were estimated consistently across all children. The register‐based records included data until 30th June 2021, while the limiting factor was the latest parent‐reported baseline CHU9D (8th Jan 2019). This corresponds to a follow‐up time of 899 days for all children (128 weeks). The cost‐effectiveness analysis followed the same method as the base case in an earlier cost‐effectiveness analysis of MMM (Wolf et al., 2022) including adopting a health care sector perspective. The current analyses of the 899 days since randomization included the latest CHU9D reporting and the additional cost based on data from administrative registries covering visits to general practitioners, pediatricians, publicly funded psychologists and psychiatrists in non‐hospital settings, prescription medications, and both inpatient and outpatient services at hospital‐based mental healthcare facilities. The consolidated health economic evaluation reporting standards 2022 (CHEERS, 2022) checklist (Husereau et al., 2022) was applied in the original analyses (Wolf et al., 2022) (Appendix S4), to which the current analyses serve as a long‐term follow‐up.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the same as in the original RCT; parent‐reported impact of mental health problems measured with the impact scale of the SDQ (Goodman, 1999; Stringaris & Goodman, 2013). Following 25 questions regarding strengths and difficulties related to the mental health of the child, the impact scale evaluates everyday distress and impairment of mental health problems in four domains: family life, friendships, classroom learning, and leisure activities, scored 0–10.

The key secondary parent‐reported outcomes were (1) emotional and behavioral problems based on the SDQ Total Difficulties score (range 0–40) including hyperactivity/inattention, emotional problems, conduct problems, and peer problems (Goodman, 1997), (2) the Parental Stress Scale (PSS, score range 0–72) (Louie, Cromer, & Berry, 2017), (3) preference‐based Health Related Quality of Life (HRQOL) using the Child Health Utility instrument (CHU9D) (Furber & Segal, 2015; Wolf, Ratcliffe, Chen, & Jeppesen, 2021), and (4) school attendance (percentage of school days with absence over the last 4 weeks). Parent‐reported exploratory outcomes included (1) proportion of children with a reduction in the SDQ‐impact‐score ≥1 point from baseline (‘response’), (2) proportion of children with SDQ‐scores below the cutoff for inclusion (‘remission’), (3) proportions of youths who received psychosocial treatments within the last 3 months, (4) report of parental reduced work hours due to caring for their child, and (5) parent‐reported, current unmet needs of their child relating to emotional and behavioral problems, eating/weight problems, self‐harm, and substance misuse that they were not currently being treated for.

Register‐based secondary outcomes

Utilizing data from Danish population‐based registers, we were able to ensure 100% follow‐up of all participating youths (except for data on school absenteeism). All Danish residents have personal identification numbers that can be linked to a range of administrative registers through Statistics Denmark (Statistics Denmark, n.d.). The two main secondary register‐based outcomes include (1) any psychiatric diagnosis made in outpatient or inpatient specialized mental healthcare services (ICD‐10 codes F00‐F99) from the Danish National Patient Register (Lynge, Sandegaard, & Rebolj, 2011), and (2) any redeemed prescriptions of psychotropic medications, including antipsychotics, ADHD‐medications, antidepressants, anxiolytics, hypnotics, or sedatives (Appendix S2) from the Danish National Prescription Registry (Pottegård et al., 2017). Further register‐based exploratory outcomes included: (1) subcategories of mental disorders and types of psychotropic medications, (2) parental psychiatric diagnosis or use of psychotropic medications, (3) notifications of concern, that is, most often written concerns to the municipalities about a youth <18 years, issued by either professionals (e.g., teachers, doctors) or other citizens/parents, (4) school absenteeism, accessed from the Ministry of Children and Education, and (5) parental labor market attachment from Statistics Denmark.

Costs directly related to MMM and MAU in the trial period, as well as costs for private sector psychologists based on parent‐reporting, were used from the original cost‐effectiveness analysis. These costs are outlined in detail in the original analyses (Wolf et al., 2022). The updated costs on prescription medicine, and specialized mental healthcare services were retrieved from complete national registers on an individual level for the full follow‐up period. We retrieved the costs of health care in the primary health care sector including general practitioners, pediatricians, publicly funded psychologists and psychiatrists from the Danish National Health Service Register (Sahl Andersen, De Fine Olivarius, & Krasnik, 2011). The costs are based on providers' activity‐based remuneration. We retrieved costs of prescription medication from the Danish National Prescription Registry (Wallach Kildemoes, Toft Sørensen, & Hallas, 2011) that includes all prescription drugs. Costs of inpatient and outpatient services at hospital‐based mental healthcare facilities were retrieved from the National Patient Register (Lynge et al., 2011) and valued using the National Board of Health's per diem charge and ambulatory charge for mental health care. The additional cost data introduced in this study are derived from national registers and reflect actual costs at the individual patient level. For instance, GP visits are not costed per visit but based on the specific activities conducted during each encounter. As the analysis follows a single trial cohort, costs have not been adjusted for calendar year.

QALYs were estimated using parents' answers to CHU9D, and the utilities associated with each health state were derived from tariffs based on preferences of youth aged 11–17 years (Ratcliffe et al., 2016) and with adjustment for baseline utility similar to the original analysis. The difference from the original analysis is the addition of the latest CHU9D reporting. In the estimation of QALYs, it is assumed that there is a linear development from week 26 to week 128 and that the latest reporting of CHU9D is reflective of week 128, although there were differences in the exact timing of the reporting.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed between March 18th, 2022, and October 1st, 2023. Our primary analyses were based on the intention to treat (ITT) population (Detry & Lewis, 2014): for continuous outcomes, individual‐level observations from 11 time points for the primary outcome measure (baseline and 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 18, 26, and 156 weeks from baseline) were analyzed using repeated measures mixed linear models. Missing data in these models were handled indirectly by the mixed model which is valid assuming that the data were missing at random (Detry & Ma, 2016). In the analysis of RCTs, evaluating outcomes as either ‘change from baseline’ or ‘post‐intervention’ scores typically produce the same inference when the baseline value is included as a covariate; thus, the choice is often guided by content experts (Vickers & Altman, 2001). In the primary analyses, change from baseline to year 3 constituted the response (dependent) variables, and the treatment group (two levels), time (11 levels) and the three stratification variables were included as factors, as well as the interaction between treatment group and time; baseline outcome value as a covariate, and participant ID was handled as a random effects factor.

Categorical outcomes for dichotomous endpoints were analyzed at the 3‐year follow‐up with logistic regression models including a fixed factor for treatment group and the two stratification variables as above. For missing data on the dichotomous outcomes, we applied simple non‐responder imputation. To ease interpretation, odds ratios were converted to risk ratios for interpretability as well as a subsequent conversion into the number‐needed‐to‐treat (NNT). To assess the robustness of the primary findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed, repeating the primary analyses with missing data handled by multiple imputation techniques (multiple imputation, as well as extreme scenarios with best–best, best–worst, worst–best, and worst–worst imputations) (Christensen, Ranstam, Overgaard, & Wagner, 2023).

For the register‐based outcomes, time‐to‐event analyses were performed for all outcomes. Statistical comparisons were made using Cox proportional‐hazards regression analysis, presenting hazard ratios (HR) illustrating the relative risk of adverse outcomes between MMM and MAU over the 3‐year period. Adjustment for randomization stratification factors age, region, and top problem were conducted as sensitivity analyses.

To assess cost‐effectiveness, we calculated the incremental costs (in Euros) and incremental quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) between MMM and MAU, reporting incremental QALYs and cost as well as the Incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER). Missing data were handled with multiple imputation using chained equations performed on utility score level and cost‐component level. Due to the nature of utility scores and costs, predictive mean matching was used in the imputation process. No discounting was applied for costs or QALYs. For further details on the methodology, see the original analysis (Wolf et al., 2022).

The statistical analyses were performed in statistical packages R, SAS 9.4, and STATA. All 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and p values are two‐sided. We did not apply explicit adjustments for multiplicity; rather, we analyzed the key secondary outcomes in a prioritized order. The analyses and interpretation of the key secondary outcomes were performed in sequence until one of the analyses failed to show a statistically significant difference, or until all analyses had been completed at a statistical significance level of 0.05. See Appendix S1 (questionnaire outcomes and cost‐utility) and Appendix S2 (register‐based outcomes) for details on the statistical procedures, including sensitivity analyses.

Results

Trial population

Based on the records of withdrawals, a total of 333 out of the 396 youths were invited for the questionnaire‐based follow‐up, of which n = 243 (73%) participated. As illustrated in Figure 1, primary outcome data were available for 64.1% of the ITT population (136 [69.0%] in MMM, 118 in MAU [59.3%]). Register‐based follow‐up data were available for all 396 participating youths, except for school absenteeism (available for 164 [83.2%] in MMM, 174 [87.4%] in MAU). As presented in Table 1, baseline characteristics were similar across MMM and MAU as anticipated (mean age [SD] 10.3 years [2.37], 190 [48%] girls). Length of follow‐up time showed no significant differences between MMM and MAU for any outcomes.

Outcomes

Figure 2 presents that trajectory of least squares mean scores over time for the impact of the child's mental health problems reported by the parent (Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire [SDQ] Impact scale score) from baseline up till 3 years. Parent‐reported impact of problems improved equally from baseline to 3‐year follow‐up for MMM (−1.79 points) and MAU (−1.85), corresponding to a difference between groups of 0.06 (95% CI −0.41 to 0.52; p = .81) SDQ‐impact‐scores (Table 2). Hence, the primary benefit observed (superiority) from the MMM intervention compared with MAU at week 18 (−1.10 [95% CI −1.45 to −0.75]) was not maintained. The primary analysis results were robust since the sensitivity analyses did not change the interpretation of the results (handling missing data with multiple imputations: Table S1 in Appendix S5).

Questionnaire‐based secondary and exploratory outcomes

Response and remission rates at 3‐years follow‐up appeared slightly higher in MMM compared with MAU: response in 88/197 (45%) in MMM versus 80/199 (40%) in MAU, RR 1.21 (95% CI 0.81 to 1.81), NNT = 21; remission in MMM 77/197 (39%) versus 60/199 (30%) in MAU, RR 1.51 (95% CI 1.00 to 2.31), NNT = 11 (Table 2). MMM and MAU were similar regarding the secondary outcomes with effect sizes <0.1, and the remaining exploratory measures did not substantially differ between MMM and MAU (Table 2). For SDQ response, the sensitivity analysis handling missing data with multiple imputation did not lead to different results (Table S1 in Appendix S5). Yet when imputing extreme scenarios of missing data regarding SDQ response, results were not robust, resulting in scenarios where both MMM and MAU were superior (Table S1 in Appendix S5).

Register‐based secondary and exploratory outcomes

A similar proportion of youths were diagnosed with mental disorders in both MMM and MAU (equally 49 [25%] in both), HR 1.01 (95% CI 0.68 to 1.50), and further, no substantial differences were seen for specific diagnostic groups (Table 3). Second, prescriptions of psychotropic medication were redeemed by 31 (16%) in MMM and 36 (18%) in MAU, HR 0.86 (0.53 to 1.38), also with no substantial differences within different classes of psychopharmaceutic agents. See Figures S1, S2 in Appendix S5 for graphical representations of the time‐to‐event analyses. The municipalities received fewer notifications of concern about youths in MMM compared to MAU (68 [35%] versus 97 [49%], HR 0.59 [85% CI 0.43 to 0.79]). Around one in three school‐aged youths in both MMM and MAU had more than 25% school absence for a total of ≥3 months (49 of 164 [30%] in MMM and 62 of 174 [36%] in MAU). Sensitivity analyses with adjustment for randomization stratification factors age, region and top problem did not change any of the results.

Cost‐effectiveness

A total incremental cost of €3,014 (95% CI: 2,174 to 3,855) was estimated for MMM relative to MAU (See Appendix S6: Table S2 for details). Assuming linearity in change of HRQOL between follow‐ups and based on the difference in HRQOL between MMM and MAU at baseline, week 18 + 26, and 3‐year follow‐up, resulted in an incremental QALY of 0.121 (95% CI: 0.045 to 0.196) in favor of MMM, corresponding to a net benefit of MMM over the MAU of ICER of 24,789 euros/QALY (Appendix S6: Table S3). The ICER is sensitive to the assumption of linear development in HRQOL from week 26 to week 128. Given the lack of data during this period, it is not possible to validate this assumption. In Denmark, there is no official cost‐effectiveness threshold. However, using common cost‐effectiveness thresholds, like the 20,000–30,000 UK pound used by the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE), MMM would be assessed as cost‐effective. See Appendix S6 for further details and Figure S3 showing the cost‐effectiveness acceptability curve. The results show that there was no difference in health care sector utilization between the groups during the full follow‐up period of 899 days after random allocation to intervention in the trial. Hence, the incremental costs are still driven by the difference in MMM and MAU costs during the trial period.

Discussion

Primary findings

The current study shows that although the MMM transdiagnostic, modular CBT intervention was effective at week 18 (end‐of‐treatment) and week 26, the superior effect of MMM compared to MAU was not sustained at the 3‐year follow‐up regarding the primary outcome of parent‐reported impact of problems and all other questionnaire‐based outcomes. Additionally, one in four youths was diagnosed with an incident mental illness in psychiatric services over the following 3 years in both MMM and MAU. Despite the lack of long‐term benefits of the MMM intervention, the intervention was effective in the short term with the potential of being cost‐effective.

Implications

It is well established that most mental disorders originate in childhood (Solmi et al., 2022), underlining the importance of identifying children and youths early in a potential trajectory towards increased impairment and persisting developmental and mental health challenges (Arango et al., 2018). Early identification in school‐based settings and easily accessible psychological treatment seems feasible, given the level of mental health problems and the prediction of mental disorders found in this study. However, despite the short‐term benefits of the transdiagnostic approach to treat a broad group of school‐aged children with anxiety, depression and/or behavioral disturbances, there was no long‐term effect of the MMM program compared to MAU. The lack of maintenance of treatment effects after 3 years does raise the question of how interventions can be improved to achieve longer‐lasting effects to prevent the persistence and deterioration of the mental health problems and illnesses. Shorter‐term follow‐up studies indicate some extended effects of CBT interventions until around 1 year after the end of treatment, yet results from long‐term studies are less encouraging (Furlong et al., 2013; Hetrick et al., 2016; James et al., 2020).

The current study is unique, given the long follow‐up period of a large sample, adhering to the strict RCT design, plus availability of register data in 100% of the sample, preventing imprecision or bias due to dropout. Taken together, the findings suggest a need to carefully follow youths after completion of the early interventions and have readily available support at hand in a need‐based stepped‐care approach to harness long‐term benefits from programs such as MMM. Our findings generalize to CBT programs targeting youths with impacting emotional and behavioral problems which tend to exhibit heterogeneous courses regarding persistence, recurrence, and remission throughout childhood into young adulthood (Copeland, Angold, Shanahan, & Costello, 2014; Costello, Copeland, & Angold, 2011). We propose that future programs like MMM should introduce a systematic evaluation of needs after the initial treatment course to revise the case formulation and either extend the CBT, provide educational and social support in the municipality and/or make a referral to CAMHS, based on the measurements of unmet needs. The transdiagnostic modular approach was originally designed to permit the flexible application of common treatment elements for multiple disorders in one treatment course of variable lengths (Weisz et al., 2012), and our findings do support the need for individually adapted continuity of care for some youths.

The cost‐effectiveness analyses suggested that the MMM has the potential of being within the scope for a generally acceptable price per QALY, underscoring that effective treatments aimed at youths impaired by mental health problems and who actively seek help are indeed warranted. The positive results from the original MMM study on short‐term outcomes have contributed to a strong focus from policymakers in Denmark to ensure nationwide provision of evidence‐based individual psychotherapeutic treatments to treat common mental health problems and prevent deterioration (Sundhedsministeriet, 2022). Nonetheless, our results from this 3‐year follow‐up must bring into question whether preventive psychotherapy aimed at individual youths is indeed an optimal strategy for preventing the need for psychiatric treatment in youths. From a related field of clinical high‐risk research in early psychosis development, an individual‐focused ‘clinical high‐risk’ approach has largely been unsuccessful in preventing later psychotic disorders on a population level (Catalan et al., 2021; Lång et al., 2022). We believe it is crucial to consider the lessons learned from other high‐risk approaches across medical specialties (Fusar‐Poli et al., 2021), and the current study clearly shows that we cannot solely rely on individually oriented psychotherapy to prevent severe mental disorders. We must consider a wide range of coherent preventive strategies, ranging from universal strategies for health promotion to selective and indicated, pedagogical and psychological interventions with a focus to connect vulnerable young people with peers and supportive adults and integrating them in society (Arango et al., 2018; van Os et al., 2023; van Os, Guloksuz, Vijn, Hafkenscheid, & Delespaul, 2019); all without forgetting the needs of young people with severe mental illness that is not readily preventable.

While the study was conducted in a school‐based primary care setting, one in four included youths were diagnosed with a mental disorder in a specialized setting within 3 years. This finding is perhaps not surprising, given that four in five youths fulfilled criteria for one or several diagnoses of mental disorders at baseline according to DAWBA‐based diagnostic procedures, even though these youths benefited most from the MMM treatment at 18 weeks (Rimvall et al., 2023). The youths recruited for MMM constituted a ‘high‐risk’ sample at a group level, with most being non‐remitters according to a‐priori defined threshold for impairing mental health problems (61% in MMM and 70% in MAU) after 3 years. Hence, the MMM as a ‘preventive’ intervention was likely not an accurate definition for many participants, for whom MMM was rather ‘early intervention’ while on a trajectory towards a clinically significant mental disorder treated in the mental health services. Hence, in the long term, 9–13 sessions of MMM as a ‘stand‐alone’ early intervention was insufficient. Easily accessible follow‐up, stepped‐care, or booster session approaches may be mandatory for years beyond the intervention period for this vulnerable group of youths.

Methodological considerations

Strengths include a unique, long‐term follow‐up of a large sample of youths, reinforced with register‐based data without attrition, supporting the findings from the questionnaire‐based measures. Limitations include lack of blinding of participants and therapists, lack of youth self‐reports, and varying 3‐year follow‐up time from 124 to 203 weeks (median 167 weeks), although follow‐up time did not differ between MMM and MAU. It was not possible to examine in nuance how continuous strained life circumstances, and the course of problems can have influenced participation as well as non‐participation in follow‐up assessments. The follow‐up was conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic, possibly affecting participation, although general referrals to mental health services did not show significant changes during this time (Rømer et al., 2021). Similar outcome patterns among MMM and MAU, however, do not suggest differential distribution of causes for non‐participation to have influenced the results. A limiting factor in the cost‐effectiveness analyses was the lack of questionnaire‐based outcome data from week 26 to week 128. The ICER is sensitive to the assumption of linear development in HRQOL, and assumptions about this development could not be validated. A limitation is the lack of discounting in the analyses. Further, we applied a health care sector perspective, although a previous review has shown that most of the costs of mental disorders among youth are in the education and social sectors (Snell et al., 2013). Furthermore, possible spillover effects of the parents' costs and benefits are not included, as we lack data on these broader costs and benefits.

Conclusions

The research and clinical implications of short‐term effectiveness without longer‐lasting preventive effects is a call for action to improve the coherence and continuity of prevention and early intervention for youth mental health problems. The 9–13 sessions of individual transdiagnostic CBT cannot stand alone as a brief preventive intervention for youths with common mental health problems. There is a need to further integrate and adapt interventions into a need‐based stepped‐care model with easily accessible treatments for a substantial number of youths with common emotional and behavioral problems. Furthermore, the longer‐term preventive effects of a need‐based stepped‐care model will probably also depend on continuous universal strategies for promotion of mental health in youths.

Key points

What's known

Transdiagnostic cognitive behavioral psychotherapy programs for treating emotional and behavioral problems in youths, like Mind My Mind (MMM), have proved scalability and short‐term benefits in randomized trials, yet less is known about the long‐term preventive effects.

What's new

The short‐term beneficial effects of MMM compared to management as usual were not maintained after 3 years regarding both the primary parent‐reported impact of problems and secondary parent‐reported outcomes. Additionally, over 3 years, 25 per cent of youths were diagnosed with psychiatric illness in child and adolescent mental healthcare settings in both groups (100% register‐based follow‐up), yet the MMM intervention has the potential to be cost‐effective.

What's relevant

The high incidence of psychiatric illness following the indicated prevention and early intervention for emotional and behavioral problems clearly indicates a need for continued care for non‐remitters following the time‐limited psychotherapy intervention.
