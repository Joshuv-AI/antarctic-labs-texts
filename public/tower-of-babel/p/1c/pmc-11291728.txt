Association between erectile dysfunction and the prevalence and prognosis of hyperglycemia in adults in the USA based on NHANES 2001–2004
Authors: Guangshu Chen, Lehua Huang, Meizheng Lai, Jianmin Ran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Prior research has demonstrated that erectile dysfunction (ED) is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and premature mortality. Few studies have examined the link between ED and hyperglycemia, and the predictive power of ED for mortality in individuals with hyperglycemia. A cohort of 1584 adults diagnosed with hyperglycemia, consisting of 583 individuals with diabetes and 1001 individuals with prediabetes, was selected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted between 2001 and 2004. The study found a positive correlation between severe ED and hyperglycemia (OR, 2.03; 95% CI 1.53–2.68), while no significant relationship was observed between severe ED and CVD events (OR, 1.60; 95% CI 0.91–2.80). Additionally, no statistical association was found between diabetes or prediabetes status and ED. After multivariable adjustments, severe ED was found to be significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 1.67; 95% CI 1.16–2.39), while no significant association was observed between severe ED and CVD mortality (HR, 1.92; 95% CI 0.92–3.98). Our study indicates a significant correlation between ED and hyperglycemia status. Hyperglycemia Individuals with ED generally exhibited an unfavorable prognosis for mortality due to all causes and CVD, particularly among those with low levels of physical activity.

Introduction

Erectile dysfunction, defined as the incapacity to attain and sustain an erection suitable for satisfactory sexual intercourse1, can manifest as a complication of diabetes2, affecting approximately 75% of men with diabetes3. Additionally, it has been identified as a risk factor for the development of diabetes and prediabetes4. The precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the association between hyperglycemia and ED remain uncertain. However, it has been observed that individuals with ED are more prone to experiencing hyperglycemia due to factors such as low testosterone levels, central obesity, depressive symptoms, and unhealthy lifestyle choices3–5. In the population of individuals with diabetes, the primary mechanism for ED is endothelial dysfunction in the corpus cavernosum6,7. Elevated levels of glucose lead to the accumulation of advanced glycosylation end-products8,9, which can contribute to the development of vascular disease and neuropathy, thereby accelerating the occurrence and progression of ED. A meta-analysis has demonstrated that individuals with ED not only experience a diminished quality of life but also face an increased risk of mortality10. This suggests that the additional risk associated with ED may not be solely dependent on traditional cardiovascular risk factors. However, the severity of ED is often overlooked, especially in patients with diabetes, with most patients responding that their doctors have never investigated their sexual problems11. Current research focuses on complete ED among individuals without diabetes12–14, and few studies are exploring ED status and survival prognosis in hyperglycemic populations. Our study aims to explore whether there is a correlation between different degrees of ED status and hyperglycemic status and CVD event, as well as to further explore the association between ED and adverse mortality.

Methods

Study design and target population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database is publicly accessible and systematically gathers data on the health and nutritional status of individuals in the United States, encompassing both adults and children. This comprehensive information is obtained through interviews and physical examinations, enabling the identification of disease prevalence and associated risk factors. For a thorough understanding of the NHANES study methodology, a comprehensive account can be found at the United States (US) Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. NHANES was granted ethical approval by the National Center for Health Statistics, ensuring that written consent was obtained from each participant. Over the course of two NHANES cycles spanning from 2001 to 2004, data pertaining to the survey of ED was gathered. Out of the total population of 20,120 individuals registered during this 4-year timeframe, a subset of 9769 subjects aged 20 and above were initially included in the study. Following the exclusion of 5653 cases due to incomplete interview data about ED, a total of 4116 cases with comprehensive data met the inclusion criteria. Additionally, 155 cases lacking results for glycosylated hemoglobin and blood glucose were also excluded. Consequently, the final sample consisted of 3961 men, comprising 2377 individuals with normal glucose levels, 583 with diabetes, and 1001 with prediabetes. Furthermore, the sample included 764 subjects with mild ED, 684 with moderate ED, 482 with severe ED, and 2031 without ED, as illustrated in Fig. 1.Figure 1Flowchart of eligible participants.

Flowchart of eligible participants.

The general characteristics of the hyperglycemia subjects were extracted based on the severity of ED. Initially, the association between ED status and hyperglycemia status was analyzed, followed by an examination of the relationship between ED status and CVD in hyperglycemia subjects. Our study aimed to investigate the relationship between different ED statuses and the occurrence of all-cause and CVD-related mortality in individuals with hyperglycemia. To account for any confounding factors that may influence overall and CVD prognosis, our models incorporated relevant variations. The models were adjusted for covariates including age, race, marital status, education level, family income-to-poverty (FIP) ratio, BMI, healthy eating index (HEI) score, metabolic equivalent (MET) score, drinking and smoking habits, cancer, C-reactive protein (CRP), eGFR, HbA1c, anemia, CVD, history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia, use of hypotensive drug, and use of lipid-lowering drug. Additionally, the relationship between ED status and both all-cause and CVD mortality was further explored by stratifying various potential confounding factors.

Assessment of hyperglycemia and ED status

Diabetes is characterized by fasting plasma glucose levels exceeding 7.0 mmol/L, random plasma glucose levels or 2 h-glucose levels after 75-g oral glucose tolerance exceeding 11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c levels exceeding 6.5% (with serum hemoglobin greater than 100 g/dL), and the presence of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medications or self-reported diagnosis of diabetes. The diagnosis of prediabetes is based on fasting plasma glucose levels ranging from 5.6 to 7.0 mmol/L, random plasma glucose levels or 2 h-glucose levels after 75-g oral glucose tolerance ranging from 7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L, and HbA1c levels ranging from 5.7 to 6.4% (with serum hemoglobin greater than 100 g/dL).

The majority of contemporary research employs the International Erectile Function Questionnaire-5 (IEF-5) to diagnose ED. Conversely, the present study opted for a condensed approach by utilizing a subset of questions from the IEF-5, specifically questions 2, 3, and 5. Participants were specifically prompted to assess their capability to achieve and sustain an erection deemed satisfactory for engaging in sexual intercourse. The responses encompassed the categories of "always or almost always able; usually able; sometimes able; or never able", while erectile dysfunction was classified as none, mild, moderate, or severe, correspondingly. Due to the commendable diagnostic specificity exhibited by this single-question self-report, it is widely believed to effectively ascertain the presence of erectile dysfunction in men15.

All-cause and cardiovascular mortality

The National Death Index, which was linked to the NHANES database until December 31, 2019, furnished comprehensive data on all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality rates. The duration of the follow-up extended until the event of death or the conclusion of the study period.

Covariates assessment

The NHANES protocol was utilized to assign weights to all baseline variants. Demographic information such as age, race, marital status, education level, FIP ratio, drinking and smoking habits, past disease history, and medicine usage were obtained through the administration of the Family and Sample Person Demographics questionnaires and the Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing system. Cardiovascular disease encompassed heart attack, coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, angina, or stroke. The Laboratory Procedure Manual for NHANES was strictly adhered to throughout the meticulous execution of the blood test procedure. Blood lipid measurements including total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting glucose, and CRP were obtained through laboratory tests. Hyperlipidemia was classified when a positive result met any of the following criteria: TC > = 200 mg/dL, TG > = 150 mg/dL, LDL-C > = 130 mg/dL, HDL-C < = 40 mg/dL in males or < = 50 mg/dL in females, or if the individual self-reported the use of antihyperlipidemic medication. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was determined using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (CKD-EPI). Anemia was defined based on serum hemoglobin levels, with a threshold of less than 120 g/dL for non-pregnant women over 15 years old and less than 130 g/dL for men over 15 years old. The Healthy Eating Index-2015 scores may serve as an indicator of diet quality, with a higher score suggesting a healthier diet16. Physical activity was assessed following the national physical activity guidelines, which classify low physical activity as less than 500 MET/wk and high physical activity as equal to or greater than 500 MET/wk. The measurement of physical activity was conducted using metabolic equivalent scores17.

Statistical analysis

Data in our study were analyzed after adjustments for survey weights under NHANES analysis guidelines due to the intricate design. For categorical variables, the data were shown as numbers (percentages), and for continuous variables, as the mean (standard error).

Weighted Chi-Square and Kruskal–Wallis tests were utilized to compare the disparities among four groups of baseline characteristics. To examine the correlation between different ED statuses and hyperglycemia status, as well as mortality in individuals with hyperglycemia, three clinical models were constructed. Model 1 was adjusted for age (< 45 year, 45–65 year or ≥ 65 year), race (Hispanic Mexican, black, white, or others), marital status (married, not married, or cohabitating), BMI (< 30 or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2), FIP ratio (< 1.0, 1.0–3.0, or ≥ 3.0), and education level (less than high school, high school or equivalent, or college or above). Based on model 1, model 2 was further corrected by MET (low physical activity, high physical activity), HEI scores, drinking status (non-drinker or once-drinker), smoking status (never, ever or current), and cancer (no or yes). And model 3 was fully adjusted for HbA1c level, CRP level, eGFR (< 30, 30–60, ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73 m2), CVD (no or yes), hypertension (no or yes), hyperlipidemia (no or yes), anemia (no or yes), hypotensive drug usage (no or yes), and lipid-lowering drug usage (no or yes) except the variants in model 2. The statistical analysis in this study utilized a reference group consisting of individuals with normal ED status. This approach facilitated the examination of the association between ED status and the occurrence and prognosis of adult hyperglycemia using carefully constructed models. To address missing values in the covariates, the multiple imputation method was employed in this investigation.

Weighted Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were utilized to depict cumulative overall and CVD survival probability, stratified by ED status. A risk table was employed to present the precise number and probability of survivors at various follow-up intervals. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the association between ED status and both CVD and all-cause mortality in individuals with hyperglycemia. The subgroups were differentiated based on several factors, including age, race, marital status, BMI, drinking and smoking habits, presence of cancer, CVD, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and MET scores. The data analysis was conducted using R software version 4.2. Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided P value of less than 0.05, as defined by the R Foundation for Statistical Computing in Vienna, Austria.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Approval for NHANES was obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics, and written consent was provided by all participants.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The present study included a total of 1584 adult participants aged 20 years or older who exhibited hyperglycemia, with 583 individuals diagnosed with diabetes and 1001 individuals diagnosed with prediabetes. The average age, as determined by weighted means, was 52.6 years, while the weighted mean HbA1c level was 6.13%. Among the participants, 35.59% identified as non-Hispanic white, 63.38% had a BMI below 30 kg/m2, 54.04% presented with hypertension, and 82.13% had hyperlipidemia. Additionally, 19.7% of the participants had a history of cardiovascular disease, and 12.75% had a history of cancer.

The subjects were categorized into four groups based on their ED status, as determined by a meticulously constructed questionnaire. Out of the total number of subjects, approximately one-fifth (306 individuals) experienced severe ED. In comparison to those with mild-moderate ED, participants in the severe ED group exhibited characteristics such as older age, non-Hispanic white ethnicity, previous smoking habits, lower educational attainment, moderate family income, higher HEI scores, a higher probability of having a medical history encompassing CVD, hypertension and cancer, and a greater frequency of medication intake for chronic ailments. However, no significant disparities were observed in terms of marital status, drinking habits, BMI, CRP levels, and physical activity across the four groups (as indicated in Table 1). Furthermore, among the final cohort of eligible participants, none reported the use of medications such as PDE-5 inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil) or testosterone replacement therapy to ameliorate erectile dysfunction. Discussion

The majority of existing perspectives consistently indicate a correlation between ED and glycemic control18,19. People with diabetes exhibit a prevalence of ED that is approximately 3.5 times greater than that of men without diabetes20. Consequently, the status of ED may serve as a valuable screening tool for identifying individuals at high risk for diabetes. While scholars generally concur on the close association between ED and diabetes21,22, the precise relationship with prediabetes remains uncertain. The occurrence of ED in individuals with prediabetes is more prevalent among younger males23. A comprehensive analysis of prospective cohort studies24 indicates a significant correlation between ED and mortality from all causes, as well as CVD mortality when compared to control groups. Moreover, this heightened risk may be unrelated to conventional cardiovascular risk factors.

To delve deeper into the association between the severity of ED and hyperglycemic status, the present study was conducted. While most contemporary studies employ the IIEF-5 questionnaire, our study employed a self-reported erectile dysfunction question, which aligns closely with the gold standard of clinical urologic examination. By accounting for the influence of other clinical variables, a significant positive correlation was observed between severe ED status and hyperglycemic status, as compared to a hyperglycemic population without ED (OR, 2.03; 95% CI 1.53–2.68). This correlation remained significant even in patients with moderate ED (OR, 1.75; 95% CI 1.29–2.36). However, when our study focused specifically on the diabetes or prediabetes group, no statistical relationship was found between ED status and hyperglycemia. This lack of association may be attributed to the limited sample size in this study and the presence of confounding factors. It is unclear when hyperglycemia and ED first manifest, but there is little doubt that the two conditions are strongly related. The uncertain temporal relationship between ED and hyperglycemia implies the importance of screening for potential glycemic abnormalities in patients with ED. Additionally, it is crucial to evaluate erectile function in patients with glycemic abnormalities.

Prior research has posited that individuals with moderate to severe ED exhibit a notably heightened 10-year susceptibility to coronary heart disease or stroke25,26. Furthermore, in comparison to patients with type 2 diabetes devoid of erectile complications, those with ED face a 34% to 53% escalated likelihood of developing cardiovascular disease27. These discoveries robustly reinforce the concept that ED could serve as a surrogate indicator for forthcoming coronary heart disease. In our study, a positive correlation between ED and CVD was observed in the hyperglycemic population after appropriate adjustments. However, statistical significance was not achieved. This lack of significance could be attributed to the fact that cardiovascular events typically manifest 2–5 years after the onset of ED28–30. Alternatively, it is possible that the patients included in the study were in an asymptomatic stage of CVD and had not yet exhibited symptoms that could have been timely diagnosed.

Araujo et al.13 conducted a study examining the prognostic implications of ED in a cohort of 1,709 men aged 40 to 70 years. The findings revealed that, within the general population, ED was linked to a 26% higher risk of all-cause mortality and a 43% higher risk of CVD mortality when compared to individuals without ED. Conversely, among individuals with diabetes, the presence of ED was associated with a significantly greater risk of all-cause mortality (95% higher) and CVD mortality (62% higher) compared to those without ED. A comprehensive analysis of 1,790 patients diagnosed with complete ED in the NHANES database31, employing a similar methodology to ours for assessing the severity of ED, revealed a 70% elevated susceptibility to premature all-cause mortality among individuals with ED, even after accounting for various confounding factors. The unfavorable prognosis observed in the ED cohort has been extensively corroborated; nevertheless, no investigations have been conducted to examine the adverse outcomes of ED in patients with hyperglycemia. A 67% increased risk of all-cause mortality was found in this study, which focused on a hyperglycemic population and included more prognostic factors, including marital status, BMI, education, physical activity, drinking status, cancer, HbA1c, eGFR, and anemia. Consistent with the correlation analysis, the risk of death from both ED and CVD did not reach statistical significance, but still indicated a positive trend. Before adjusting for potential confounding variables, models 1 and 2 identified a significant association between severe ED and an increased risk of CVD mortality. However, after comprehensive adjustments, no statistically significant relationship was observed between severe ED and CVD mortality in individuals with hyperglycemia. Despite the lack of statistical significance, the observed trend suggests a potential temporal sequence between ED and CVD, with ED potentially preceding the development of CVD and subsequent mortality. This discovery aligns with the results of two prior meta-analyses10,32, indicating that ED heightens the likelihood of mortality from any cause, while its correlation with cardiovascular mortality remains inconclusive. This ambiguity may stem from the fact that the risk of cardiovascular death linked to ED is akin to certain established CVD risk factors, and that ED ceases to be a predictor of cardiovascular mortality following comprehensive adjustment for recognized CVD risk factors, implying that ED could be a manifestation of other established risk factors for cardiovascular disease33. Endothelial dysfunction, marked by diminished nitric oxide bioavailability34, frequently precedes the emergence of atherosclerotic lesions in individuals with ED. This dysfunction is believed to serve as a crucial connection between ED and CVD, as the formation of atherosclerotic plaques of comparable dimensions in smaller penile arteries may lead to earlier symptom manifestation compared to larger coronary arteries35. Consequently, it is plausible that by the end of the follow-up period, CVD has not yet advanced to a fatal outcome.

Relevant studies are pointing out the correlation between physical activity and erectile dysfunction36. It has been proposed that exercise is associated with better erectile function in men under 40 years of age37, and that physical activity and lifestyle interventions can improve erectile function38, with effect sizes even comparable to those of therapeutic regimens such as testosterone therapy and phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors39. Our study in a subgroup analysis found substantial associations between ED status and level of physical activity with all-cause as well as CVD mortality, with the low physical activity subgroup having higher all-cause mortality, corrected risk ratio (95% CI) 1.58 (1.30, 1.91), and risk of CVD mortality, corrected risk ratio (95% CI) 1.73 (1.12, 2.68). According to Klotz et al40, the cost of sildenafil does not appear to be the primary factor influencing the discontinuation of treatment. Rather, patients commonly cited inadequate drug efficacy as the determining factor. This finding aligns with previously published data22. The aforementioned evidence serves as a reminder to prioritize physical activity levels in hyperglycemia individuals with ED, as exercise and lifestyle modifications not only enhance ED but also offer potential benefits for hyperglycemia and CVD, while also yielding significant economic advantages. Hence, our hypothesis posits that timely detection of ED among individuals with hyperglycemia, coupled with comprehensive intervention, is imperative not only for mitigating complications and enhancing quality of life but also for potentially diminishing the likelihood of premature mortality.

This study employs a straightforward and dependable questionnaire format to establish the definition of ED. It is the inaugural investigation to examine the prevalence and prognosis of varying degrees of ED in people who have high blood sugar. The findings of this study indicate the necessity of acknowledging the potential hazards associated with hyperglycemia, including the unfavorable prognosis of even mild forms of ED. Moreover, the study suggests that an early evaluation of ED status and a moderate escalation in physical activity among subjects with hyperglycemia could potentially enhance their prognosis. People with ED may withhold information during interviews on sexual performance. However, this study employed a self-reported questionnaire that addressed a single straightforward question, aligning closely with the findings of the urinary tract examination. Consequently, it is imperative to replicate this study in a clinical setting to promptly detect potential cases of ED. It is worth noting that this study's limitations lie in the high prevalence of low testosterone among individuals with ED20, as testosterone levels were not assessed as an adjusting variable during the two study cycles. Furthermore, there exists a strong association between ED and depression41, particularly among young males42. Regrettably, the absence of psychological disorder questionnaires in the two survey cycles encompassed within our study hindered the evaluation of depressive conditions, consequently impeding the analysis of psychogenic erectile dysfunction. Nevertheless, it is acknowledged that organic ailments predominantly contribute to erectile dysfunction in hyperglycemic populations3, thereby partially compensating for the lack of depression as a variable. Lastly, the duration of diabetes is important for the development of diabetic complications especially diabetic neuropathy which has a role in the pathogenesis of ED. And there is no such “duration” for prediabetes, to balance the covariates of subjects with hyperglycemia, the duration of diabetes is not mentioned in the study.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates a positive correlation between ED and hyperglycemia. Individuals with ED generally exhibit a poor prognosis for all-cause and CVD mortality, particularly among those with low levels of physical activity. Early evaluation of ED status and intervention in people with hyperglycemia may yield a beneficial impact on reducing the risk of mortality and enhancing quality of life.
