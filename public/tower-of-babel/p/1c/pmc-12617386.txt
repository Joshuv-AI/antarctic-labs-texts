Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia in the young (aged 50 years and younger): Divergent clinical and molecular characteristics from the elderly
Authors: Chi Young Ok, Anuya Natu, Parnaz Daneshpajouhnejad, Lamiaa A Fathalla, Emily Symes, Paul Barone, Anna Shestakova, Kathryn K Foucar, Carlos Bueso‐Ramos, Guillermo Montalban‐Bravo, Attilio Orazi, Mrinal Patnaik, Robert Hasserjian, Daniel Arber, Julia T Geyer, Adam Bagg, Kaaren K Reichard, Sa A Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is largely a disease of individuals older than 50 years; its occurrence in patients aged 50 years and younger is rare. The authors sought to characterize the clinicopathologic features and genetic profile of young‐onset (age 50 years and younger) CMML (y‐CMML) compared with older onset (older than 50 years) CMML (o‐CMML).MethodsThe authors conducted a multi‐institutional study of 32 patients with y‐CMML and 119 patients with o‐CMML, analyzing clinical, morphologic, cytogenetic, and molecular data.ResultsPatients who had y‐CMML frequently presented with splenomegaly (59% vs. 11%; p < .001) and hepatomegaly (25% vs. 2%; p < .001) compared with those who had o‐CMML. Although the overall incidence of cytogenetic abnormalities was similar, patients who had y‐CMML exhibited distinct mutational patterns. PTPN11 mutations were significantly more common in y‐CMML (13% vs. 3%; p = .034) than in o‐CMML, whereas TET2 (6% vs. 57%; p < .001) and SRSF2 (16% vs. 46%; p = .002) mutations were significantly less frequent. In addition, multi‐hit TET2 (6% vs. 34%; p = .002) and TET2‐SRSF2 (3% vs. 29%; p = .003) co‐mutations were rare in y‐CMML. However, the molecular differences did not affect the CMML‐specific risk‐stratification categories because the clinical outcomes, including overall survival and leukemia‐free survival, did not differ between the two groups.ConclusionsCMML in patients aged 50 years and younger is rare and presents with unique clinical and mutational features, suggesting a distinct pathogenesis.Keywords: chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), multi‐hit TET2, SRSF2‐TET2 co‐mutation, young

INTRODUCTION

Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a myeloid neoplasm with overlapping features of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN). 1 , 2 Cytogenetic abnormalities are present in 20%–30% of patients, of which trisomy 8 (+8), chromosome 7 alterations (−7 or del[7q]), loss of the Y chromosome (−Y), and +21 are the most common. 3 Somatic mutations occur in approximately 90% of patients, frequently affecting genes involved in epigenetic regulation (most commonly TET2 or ASXL1), spliceosome complex (most commonly SRSF2), and signal transduction pathways (NRAS, KRAS, or CBL). 4 , 5 Based on a white blood cell (WBC) count (threshold, 13 × 109/L), CMML is classified into myelodysplastic CMML (MD‐CMML) and myeloproliferative CMML (MP‐CMML) subtypes. 6 Patients who have MD‐CMML typically present with symptoms related to cytopenias, such as fatigue, recurrent infections, or bruising. In contrast, patients who have MP‐CMML commonly exhibit splenomegaly and constitutional symptoms, including fever, weight loss, and night sweats. 7 Several prognostic models have been developed for CMML‐specific risk stratification, with more recent models incorporating cytogenetic and molecular data. 5 , 8 Leukemic transformation occurs in approximately 15%–30% of patients, and the median overall survival (OS) ranges from 48 to 51 months. 5 , 8 , 9

CMML is a disease of older age, with the median age at diagnosis between 70 and 75 years. This age‐related predilection is further highlighted by an analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database from 1999 to 2020, which estimated that the prevalence of CMML is 4.3, 8.9, and 23.5 per 100,000 in the cohorts aged 18–30, 30–40, and 40–50 years, respectively. 10 The prevalence rises sharply in older populations, reaching 28.9, 40.8, and 885.3 per 100,000 in individuals aged 60–70 years, 70–80 years, and older than 80 years, respectively. Another study corroborated these data, demonstrating an age‐standardized incidence rate of 0.01 in the cohort aged 18–49 years. The age‐standardized incidence rate sharply increased to 0.25 in the cohort aged 50–59 years. 11 Overall, only 10%–20% of patients with CMML, including therapy‐related cases, are aged 60 years and younger. 12 , 13

One investigations 10 identified molecular (JAK2, ASXL1, SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, and SETBP1) and cytogenetic predictors of outcome in a cohort of younger patients with CMML (aged 65 years and younger), but it did not specifically characterize the disease features in this younger end of the CMML age spectrum.

In our clinical practice, we have observed that CMML cases occurring in patients younger than 50 years, outside of a therapy‐related context, often present with atypical clinical features and unusual genetic profiles, suggesting that CMML in this age group may represent a biologically distinct subgroup. To better understand the unique clinical, pathologic, and genetic characteristics of CMML in patients aged 50 years and younger, we initiated a multi‐institutional clinicopathologic Bone Marrow Pathology Group study specifically focused on this younger subset of patients. This effort aimed to address the existing knowledge gap and provide insight into the heterogeneity of CMML across different age groups.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

We collected cases from the pathology archives of nine medical centers: The University of MD Anderson Cancer Center, the University of Pennsylvania, the University of Chicago, the Mayo Clinic, the University of Utah, the University of New Mexico, Texas Tech Health Sciences Center, Weill‐Cornell Medical College, and Massachusetts General Hospital. Inclusion criteria were patients who were diagnosed with de novo CMML at between ages 20 and 50 years, based on World Health Organization (WHO) classification (fifth edition) or International Consensus Classification diagnostic criteria. 1 , 14 The age cutoff (≤50 years) was determined based on previous data and our clinical experience indicating that the prevalence of CMML is very low in patients younger than 50 years and that the incidence sharply increases after age 50 years. Patients with oligomonocytic CMML (monocytes from 0.5 to <1.0 × 109/L and 10% WBCs) and therapy‐related CMML were not included. For comparison, 119 consecutive patients with de novo CMML who were older than 50 years were collected from The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. Clinical information was collected from the electronic medical records. Splenomegaly and hepatomegaly were defined by increased size of the organs measured by computed tomography scan (>14 cm and >15.5 cm, respectively). This study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating institutions.

Bone marrow morphology

Routine hematoxylin and eosin–stained histologic sections of bone marrow (BM) trephine biopsy specimens and Wright‐Giemsa–stained BM aspirate smears were reviewed at participating institutions. A 500‐nucleated cell BM differential cell count was performed in patients who had adequate aspirate smears or touch preparations. Dysplasia was determined using the International Working Group on Morphology of MDS guidelines. 15 Megakaryocytic dysplasia was assessed as either MDS‐like, MPN‐like, or MDS/MPN–like dysplasia as previously described. 16 BM fibrosis was assessed according to the WHO grading system for myelofibrosis. 14

Cytogenetics

Conventional cytogenetic analysis was performed on G‐banded metaphase cells prepared from unstimulated BM aspirate cultures using standard techniques. Twenty metaphases were analyzed, and the results were reported using the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature. A complex karyotype is defined as the presence of three or more independent chromosomal abnormalities. Cytogenetic risk stratification was conducted following the Spanish CMML‐specific cytogenetic risk‐stratification system. 17

Targeted next‐generation sequencing

Targeted next‐generation sequencing was performed to detect gene mutations commonly found in hematologic malignancies in each participating institution, as described previously. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 The next‐generation sequencing panels varied at each institution, but all panels interrogated commonly mutated 20 genes in CMML (ASXL1, CBL, DNMT3A, EZH2, FLT3, IDH1, IDH2, JAK2, KRAS, NF1, NPM1, NRAS, RUNX1, SETBP1, SF3B1, SRSF2, TET2, U2AF1, and ZRSR2). 4 The CMML genetic risk score was assessed in each patient and was adopted following the CMML‐specific prognostic scoring system combined with a molecular data model. 5

Statistical analyses

Comparisons among categorical variables and numerical variables were performed using the Fisher exact test and the Mann–Whitney test, respectively. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or the last date of follow‐up, whichever occurred earlier. Patients who underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) were censored at the time of the procedure. Leukemia‐free survival (LFS) was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of progression to acute myeloid leukemia or the date of death from any cause. Survival probability was determined using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences compared using the log‐rank test. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software), with significance set at a p value < .05 (two‐sided). Artificial intelligence‐generated content tools, such as Chat GPT and others based on large language models, were not used in developing any portion of this article.

RESULTS

Patient cohort

We identified 32 young patients (aged 50 years or younger) with de novo (primary) CMML (y‐CMML) from participating institutions (Table 1). Their median age at diagnosis was 43 years (range, 23–50 years). The cohort included 24 men (75%) and eight women (25%), with a male‐to‐female ratio of 3:1. Splenomegaly was observed in 17 patients (59%), and hepatomegaly was observed in eight (25%). Seventeen cases (52%) were classified as MD‐CMML, and 15 (48%) were classified as MP‐CMML. By blast count, 24 patients (76%) had CMML‐1, and eight (24%) had CMML‐2. Notably, six patients (19%) had a documented history of autoimmune disease or systemic inflammatory syndromes.

Peripheral blood and bone marrow findings

Anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia were observed in 27 (84%), six (19%), and 22 (69%) patients, respectively. One patient had thrombocytosis (>450 × 109/L). Eleven (35%), 14 (45%), and five (16%) patients had cytopenia in a single, two, and three lineages, respectively. The median hemoglobin level was 104 g/L (range, 69–158 g/L), the median WBC count was 11.5 × 109/L (range, 3.5–214.5 × 109/L), and the median platelet count was 109 × 109/L (range, 9–654 × 109/L). The median blood monocyte percentage was 22% (range, 10%–55%), and the median absolute monocyte count was 2.1 × 109/L (range, 1.0–30.0 × 109/L). Peripheral blood blasts and promonocytes were generally absent, with median values of 0% (range, 0%–14%).

BM examination revealed a median cellularity of 90% (range, 50%–100%). Morphologic dysplasia was most frequently observed in the megakaryocytic lineage, present in 29 patients (91%), followed by granulocytic dysplasia in 18 patients (56%) and erythroid dysplasia in nine patients (28%). The number of megakaryocytes increased in 18 patients (56%) and decreased in five patients (16%). Advanced BM fibrosis (WHO myelofibrosis categorized as MF‐2 or MF‐3) was identified in two patients (13%).

Cytogenetic data

Cytogenetic abnormalities were identified in nine patients (28%), and the remaining patients had a normal karyotype. The most common abnormality was loss of Y (−Y), observed in four patients. Other abnormalities included monosomy 7 (−7), isochromosome 17q (i[17][q10]), and isochromosome Xq (i[X][q10]), each identified in one patient. Two patients exhibited complex karyotypes. According to the Spanish CMML‐specific cytogenetic stratification system, 17 27 patients (84%) were classified with low‐risk disease, two (6%) were classified with intermediate‐risk disease, and three (9%) were classified with high‐risk disease (Table 2). Of the five patients classified with an intermediate or high cytogenetic risk, four had MP‐CMML.

Mutation data

Somatic mutations were identified in 28 of 31 (90%) tested patients. The median number of somatic mutations was three (range, zero to eight) per patient. ASXL1 (n = 16; 52%) was the most commonly mutated gene followed by DNMT3A and NRAS (n = 6; 19%); RUNX1 and SRSF2 (n = 5; 16%); and CBL, KRAS, and PTPN11 (n = 4; 13%). Only two patients (6%) had TET2 mutations, both of which were biallelic TET2 mutations. TET2‐SRSF2 co‐mutations were identified in a single patient (3%). To further explore age‐associated trends, we analyzed the frequencies of these features across three age groups (50 years and younger, 51–65 years, and older than 65 years). Biallelic TET2 mutations were identified in two (6%), 11 (31%), and 30 (35.7%) patients in these age groups, respectively. Similarly, TET2‐SRSF2 co‐mutations were detected in one (3%), seven (20%), and 27 (32%) patients, respectively. Other mutations are illustrated in Figure 1A. The median variant allelic frequency of the mutation was 32.2% (range, 1%–97.9%). Of note, apparent subclonal mutations (variant allelic frequency, <10%) accompanied the major clones (variant allelic frequency, >25%) in all patients. Genetic risk groups based on the clinical/molecular chronic myelomonocytic‐specific prognostic model by Elena and colleagues were low, intermediate‐1, intermediate‐2, and high in 11 (32%), 11 (35%), four (13%), and six (19%) patients, respectively (Table 2). 5

Clinicopathologic comparisons with older patients (>50 years) with CMML

In total, 119 older patients (older than 50 years at initial diagnosis) with CMML (o‐CMML) were collected for comparison. Their median age at diagnosis was 71 years (range, 53–90 years). There were 82 men and 37 women (ratio, 2.2: 1). The proportions of MD‐CMML versus MP‐CMML and CMML‐1 versus CMML‐2 in the o‐CMML cohort were similar to those in the y‐CMML cohort (Table 1). Compared with the o‐CMML cohort, patients in the y‐CMML cohort more commonly had splenomegaly (59% vs. 11%; p < .001) and hepatomegaly (25% vs. 2%; p < .001). There was no significant difference in the median size of enlarged spleen between the y‐CMML and o‐CMML cohorts (median, 23.8 vs. 18 cm, respectively; p = .215). Patients in the y‐CMML cohort had significantly lower hemoglobin (median, 104 vs. 115 g/L; p = .028). There were no significant differences in the WBC count, platelets, monocyte percentage or absolute monocyte level, or circulating blast equivalents in peripheral blood between the two groups (p > .05). Patients in the y‐CMML cohort had a significantly higher median BM cellularity (90% vs. 80%; p < .001). No significant differences were observed in other BM findings (Table 1).

Cytogenetic abnormalities were present at similar frequencies (28% vs. 25% for young and older patients, respectively; p = .821). Among the common cytogenetic abnormalities observed in CMML (trisomy 8 [+8], −Y, chromosome 7 abnormalities, and complex karyotype), −Y trended toward being more common (12.5% vs. 4.2%; p = .096), and +8 was less common in patients who had y‐CMML (0% vs. 8.4%; p = .089). There were no significant differences in the incidence of chromosome 7 abnormalities or complex karyotypes between the two groups (p > .05).

Mutation analysis demonstrated that patients with y‐CMML had fewer somatic mutations (median number, three vs. four; p < .001). When each mutation was compared, patients with y‐CMML had more frequent mutations in PTPN11 (13% vs. 3%; p = .034) and less frequent mutations in SRSF2 (16% vs. 46%; p = .002) and TET2 (6% vs. 57%; p < .001). Furthermore, combined TET2 and SRSF2 mutations (3% vs. 29%; p = .003) and multi‐hit TET2 mutations (6% vs. 34%; p = .002) were less common patients with y‐CMML (Figure 1B,C). The remaining mutations in both the y‐CMML and o‐CMML cohorts are illustrated as an Oncoplot in Figure 2. The distribution according to CMML‐specific cytogenetic risk group was similar between the younger and older cohorts (low risk, 84% vs. 79%, respectively; intermediate risk, 6% vs. 8%, respectively, high risk, 9% vs. 13%, respectively; p = .786; Table 2); and the CMML‐specific genetic risk group distribution was similar (low risk, 32% vs. 27%, respectively; intermediate‐1 risk, 35% vs. 33%, respectively; intermediate‐2 risk, 13% vs. 17%, respectively; high risk, 19% vs. 24%, respectively; p = .866; Table 2).

Treatment and follow‐up

Among patients in the y‐CMML cohort, 18 (60%) underwent HSCT with or without preceding treatments (hypomethylating agents, low‐dose cytarabine, or induction chemotherapy). Of the patients who did not undergo HSCT, seven (23%) received low‐intensity therapies (all hypomethylating agents), and five (17%) received only supportive care. With a median follow‐up duration of 2.6 years (range, 0–12.2 years), six patients (19%) progressed to acute myeloid leukemia.

In the o‐CMML cohort, 37 (31%), 54 (46%), and 25 (21%) patients underwent treated with HSCT, low‐intensity therapies (hypomethylating agents or cladribine, low‐dose cytarabine, and venetoclax) or supportive care, respectively, which differed significantly from the y‐CMML cohort (p = .017). The median follow‐up duration was 2.4 years (range, 0–7.8 years), and 20 patients (17%) progressed to acute myeloid leukemia.

After censoring the patients who underwent HSCT, the median OS was 3.7 years in both the y‐CMML and o‐CMML cohorts (p = .263; Figure 3A). Similarly, LFS did not differ significantly between the two cohorts (median LFS, undefined for both groups; p = .465; Figure 3B).

DISCUSSION

CMML is rare in patients younger than 50 years old, accounting for approximately 4% of all CMML cases. 11 Our multi‐institutional cohort provides the first comprehensive clinicopathologic and genetic characterization of CMML in younger patients outside of a therapy‐related setting.

Clinically, patients in the y‐CMML cohort exhibited statistically significant splenomegaly (59%) and hepatomegaly (25%) compared with their older counterparts. These prominent clinical features bear a resemblance to juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML), which is characterized by splenomegaly in virtually all patients and frequent hepatomegaly. 25 Morphologically, the median BM cellularity was slightly higher (90%) in the y‐CMML group versus the o‐CMML group; however, it is uncertain whether this reflects the higher intrinsic BM cellularity in younger individuals or represents a biologically meaningful difference. 26

Cytogenetic abnormalities and risk distribution were similar between the y‐CMML and o‐CMML cohorts (28% and 25%, respectively), although we observed a trend toward an increased incidence of −Y and a decreased incidence of +8 in the patients with y‐CMML. Because −Y is usually associated with older age, its higher frequency in younger patients is seemingly paradoxical and may have biologic relevance. 27 Of note, −Y is associated with a more favorable prognosis in MDS 28 ; however, its prognostic affect in CMML is unknown. The age of the four patients who had −Y were 27, 29, 44, and 45 years, respectively. Loss of Y was observed in all assessed metaphases in three patients. Partial loss of Y was present in a single patient (13 of 20 metaphases). Larger studies are needed to clarify its potential clinical relevance in this setting.

The most notable difference between the y‐CMML and o‐CMML cohorts emerged at the molecular level. The mutational profile of o‐CMML was consistent with previously reported CMML cohorts, which are associated with frequent mutations in TET2 (60%), SRSF2 (50%), ASXL1 (40%), NRAS (15%), and KRAS (10%). 9 In contrast, the mutational landscape appears distinctively different in y‐CMML, with significantly less frequent mutations in TET2 (6% vs. 57%) and SRSF2 (16% vs. 46%). Moreover, the characteristic co‐mutations of SRSF2 and TET2 and multi‐hit TET2 mutations (defined as two or more TET2 mutations and/or a TET2 variant allelic frequency ≥55%) were remarkably infrequent in the patients with y‐CMML; in our cohort, only 3% of TET2‐SRSF2 co‐mutations and 6% of multi‐hit TET2 mutations occurred in the y‐CMML cohort compared with 29% and 34%, respectively, in the o‐CMML cohort. 29 , 30 , 31 Interestingly, although TET2‐SRSF2 co‐mutations and multi‐hit TET2 mutations were rare in patients with CMML younger than 50 years, their incidence was similar in CMML among those aged 51–65 years versus those older than 65 years, suggesting a significant shift in the CMML genetic profile after age 50 years. Another notable finding was the significantly higher frequency of PTPN11 mutations in y‐CMML compared with o‐CMML (13% vs. 3%). PTPN11 encodes SHP‐2, which is a critical mediator of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway, and it is one of the hallmark mutations in JMML, occurring in approximately 35%–40% of patients. 32 In our cohort, the increased frequency of PTPN11 mutations in y‐CMML, along with the higher prevalence of hepatosplenomegaly, which is a highly prevalent feature in JMML, suggests a possible shared pathogenesis of a PTPN11‐driven myeloproliferation between y‐CMML and JMML. Overall, the enrichment of PTPN11 mutations and the strikingly lower frequency of the typical TET2 and SRSF2 mutations in y‐CMML suggest a distinct molecular pathogenesis in this younger subgroup.

Despite the differences in molecular genetic features, the clinical outcomes, including the rate of leukemic transformation, OS, and LFS, were also not significantly different between the y‐CMML and o‐CMML cohorts. Notably, a substantially higher proportion of young patients underwent HSCT (60% vs. 31%), a potentially curative therapy that can significantly alter disease course. In addition, whereas the specific mutational frequencies varied, the overall prognostic profile, as captured by established CMML‐specific genetic risk models, appeared to be similar between y‐CMML and o‐CMML. This suggests that, although the types of driver mutations may vary with age in CMML, the similar incidence of high‐risk mutations (ASXL1, NRAS, RUNX1, and SETBP1) and complex karyotype across the age spectrum leads to comparable risk stratification and outcomes.

Although the retrospective nature of the current study introduced some inherent limitations, efforts were made to ensure consistency across institutions. All centers used targeted next‐generation sequencing platforms covering key CMML‐associated genes, with only minor variations in panel composition; morphologic assessment and diagnoses were reviewed by a panel of hematopathology experts to confirm the diagnoses and morphologic features. This y‐CMML cohort represents the largest reported to date of CMML in this age group, offering a valuable foundation for further investigation. Finally, although this study is primarily descriptive, it reveals distinct molecular features that warrant future functional studies to elucidate the role of PTPN11 mutations and the low frequency of TET2‐SRSF2 co‐mutations in the pathogenesis of CMML in younger individuals, which may inform age‐specific therapeutic strategies. 33 , 34

In summary, to our knowledge, our current study provides the first comprehensive clinicopathologic and genetic characterization of younger patients with CMML (aged 50 years and younger). We demonstrate that patients with y‐CMML commonly present with splenomegaly and hepatomegaly and harbor a distinct mutational landscape characterized by more frequent PTPN11 mutations and significantly less frequent TET2‐SRSF2 co‐mutations and multi‐hit TET2 mutations. These molecular differences suggest a distinct pathogenesis of CMML in the younger age group.
