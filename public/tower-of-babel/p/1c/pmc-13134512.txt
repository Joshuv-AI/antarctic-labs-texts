FOLFIRINOX vs. Gemcitabine/Nab‐Paclitaxel for Pancreatic Cancer by BRCA2 and TMB Status: A Japanese C‐CAT Database Study
Authors: Kazuyuki Mizuno, Takuya Ishikawa, Takanori Ito, Yuko Takano, Osamu Maeda, Hiroki Kawashima, Yuichi Ando
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe clinical impact of BRCA2 variants and tumor mutational burden (TMB) on first‐line regimen selection—FOLFIRINOX (FFX) versus gemcitabine plus nab‐paclitaxel (GnP)—for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains unclear. Using the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C‐CAT) database, we analyzed patients with unresectable or recurrent PDAC treated with first‐line FFX or GnP. Overall survival (OS) and time to treatment failure (TTF) were compared, stratified by BRCA2 pathogenic variants (PVs) and TMB status. A total of 4356 patients were included. In the overall population, GnP was associated with superior adjusted OS compared with FFX (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 0.90, p = 0.015), while TTF was comparable. In patients with BRCA2 PVs (3.3%), FFX demonstrated a significantly higher objective response rate (66.7% vs. 33.3%) and longer TTF compared with GnP. However, OS in patients with BRCA2 PVs was comparable between the two regimens (HR 1.11, p = 0.665). Among patients with BRCA2 PVs treated with GnP, OS was numerically longer with second‐line FFX than with nal‐IRI/5‐FU/leucovorin (HR 0.51; p = 0.119). In patients without BRCA2 PVs, OS was longer with GnP. TMB‐high status (2.9%) predicted significantly longer OS regardless of the first‐line regimen (HR 0.64, p < 0.001). In conclusion, GnP was associated with superior adjusted OS in the overall population. In BRCA2 PV tumors, greater tumor shrinkage with first‐line FFX did not translate into longer OS, underscoring the importance of sequencing strategies that ensure timely exposure to platinum‐based therapy. Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a 5‐year relative survival rate of approximately 13% [1]. For patients with unresectable or recurrent disease, systemic chemotherapy is the mainstay of treatment. Two multidrug regimens, FOLFIRINOX (FFX) and gemcitabine plus nab‐paclitaxel (GnP), have demonstrated significant survival benefits over gemcitabine monotherapy [2, 3] and are recommended as standard first‐line options in international guidelines [4, 5]. In Japan, however, due to concerns regarding the toxicity of the original FFX regimen, a modified FFX regimen—with reduced doses of the anticancer agents—was developed [6, 7, 8]. Although the GENERATE trial directly compared these regimens [8], a consensus on optimal regimen selection remains unestablished. In clinical practice, treatment decisions currently rely on performance status (PS), comorbidities, and physician preference rather than on robust predictive biomarkers.

Advances in comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) have highlighted the therapeutic relevance of homologous recombination repair (HRR) gene alterations. Germline pathogenic variants (PVs) in BRCA1, BRCA2, and other HRR genes, such as PALB2, occur in approximately 5%–10% of patients with PDAC [9, 10]. These alterations cause homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), conferring sensitivity to DNA‐damaging agents such as platinum agents and PARP inhibitors [11, 12]. While the efficacy of platinum‐based regimens (e.g., FFX) and PARP inhibitor strategies in patients harboring germline BRCA1/2 or PALB2 PVs is increasingly recognized [13, 14], gaps remain regarding the optimal first‐line chemotherapy strategy. Clinical evidence is most established for germline BRCA1/2 variants (supported by prospective trials), whereas evidence for somatic BRCA/HRR alterations in PDAC remains limited [15]. Furthermore, CGP often detects BRCA2 and PALB2 variants without distinguishing between germline and somatic origin. Accordingly, it remains unclear whether BRCA2 variants specifically predict a superior benefit from FFX compared with non‐platinum regimens such as GnP in a real‐world setting.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) has emerged as a clinically relevant biomarker in immune‐oncology [16]. Although PDAC is typically characterized by low TMB, a small subset exhibits TMB‐high status. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have limited activity in unselected PDAC, pembrolizumab has shown clinically meaningful activity in TMB‐high advanced solid tumors [16]. The implications of TMB for first‐line cytotoxic chemotherapy selection and subsequent treatment sequencing in PDAC are poorly defined. To address these issues, we used the nationwide Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C‐CAT) database, which aggregates genomic and clinical data from patients across Japan [17]. In this retrospective cohort study, we investigated the impact of BRCA2 variants, representing the majority of HRR alterations in PDAC, and TMB on treatment outcomes in patients with unresectable or recurrent PDAC treated with first‐line FFX or GnP.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the C‐CAT database. In Japan, CGP is publicly reimbursed for patients with advanced solid tumors after completion of standard therapies or when no standard treatment options are available. We identified patients with PDAC registered in the database between July 2019 and July 2025 who received first‐line chemotherapy with either FFX or GnP. Patients were included if their tumors were profiled using either of the two approved panels: the FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profile (Foundation Medicine, Cambridge, MA, USA) or the OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). Data extraction was performed on October 14, 2025. This study was approved by the Nagoya University Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 2022‐0025) and the C‐CAT Review Board (No. CDU2022‐030N).

Patient Selection

The patient selection process is summarized in Figure 1. Among 16,971 patients with pancreatic cancer registered in the C‐CAT database, we initially identified those profiled using either the FoundationOne CDx or the OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel systems. We excluded patients with non‐PDAC histology, those who had received prior neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy, and those with missing data regarding the initiation or follow‐up of first‐line systemic therapy. The final analysis included 4356 patients with unresectable or recurrent PDAC: 1276 treated with FFX and 3080 with GnP. FFX was defined as a regimen comprising 5‐fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan, including the modified FFX regimen. GnP was defined as a regimen comprising gemcitabine and nab‐paclitaxel. Second‐line regimens were defined as those administering agents other than the components of the first‐line regimen.

Genomic Analysis

Tumor samples (either previously collected surgical or biopsy specimens, or newly obtained biopsies) were formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded before DNA extraction. Genomic profiling was performed using the next‐generation sequencing–based assays described above. While FoundationOne CDx reports variants without distinguishing between somatic and germline origin, the NCC Oncopanel distinguishes them using paired normal blood samples. We extracted variant data from the C‐CAT database regardless of allele frequency, but restricted the analysis to those classified as “cancer‐related” (C‐CAT evidence level F or higher [17] according to the C‐CAT clinical interpretation). For BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2, variants meeting these criteria were defined as pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (PVs).

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the initiation of first‐line chemotherapy to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the last follow‐up were censored on that date. Secondary outcomes included time to treatment failure (TTF), objective response rate (ORR), and disease control rate (DCR). TTF was defined as the time from the initiation of first‐line chemotherapy to permanent discontinuation of the regimen for any reason, including disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or death. Patients who continued treatment at the last follow‐up were censored on that date. TTF was used as a real‐world measure of treatment durability because reasons for discontinuation were not uniformly recorded in the C‐CAT database [18]. ORR was defined as the proportion of patients who achieved a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), and DCR as the proportion who achieved CR, PR, or stable disease, according to the response categories recorded in the dataset. Patients without evaluable response data were excluded from the ORR and DCR analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Student's t‐test or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. OS and TTF were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log‐rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. To mitigate calendar‐time–related confounding due to the introduction of nal‐IRI in Japan, analyses of second‐line treatment (FFX vs. nal‐IRI/5‐FU/LV) and treatment sequencing were restricted to patients who initiated second‐line therapy on or after June 1, 2020.

Multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between the first‐line regimen and outcomes, adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, and PS recorded at the time of genomic profiling, BRCA1/2 and PALB2 PV status, and TMB status. Subgroup analyses stratified by BRCA2 PV status and TMB status were conducted using Kaplan–Meier analyses and Cox models. ORR and DCR were compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test. In exploratory analyses, post‐treatment failure survival was evaluated as the time from discontinuation of first‐line GnP to death. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR [19] (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 4356 eligible patients were included in the analysis: 1276 (29.3%) received FFX, and 3080 (70.7%) received GnP as first‐line therapy (Table 1). Patients treated with FFX were significantly younger (median 60.0 vs. 67.0 years; p < 0.001), more likely to be male (58.5% vs. 53.5%; p = 0.003), and had a higher prevalence of smoking history (53.6% vs. 48.0%; p = 0.001) compared with those in the GnP cohort. Although the prevalence of BRCA1 and PALB2 PVs and TMB‐high status was comparable between groups, BRCA2 PVs were significantly more frequent in the FFX cohort than in the GnP cohort (4.2% vs. 2.9%; p = 0.032).

Treatment Outcomes in the Overall Population and by Treatment Sequence

In the overall population, GnP showed a trend toward longer OS compared with FFX in the unadjusted analysis (median OS: 19.4 months vs. 19.1 months; HR, 0.93 [95% CI: 0.85–1.01]; p = 0.077; Figure 2A). In a multivariable Cox model adjusted for age, sex, PS recorded at the time of genomic profiling, and BRCA1/2 and PALB2 PV status and TMB status, GnP was significantly associated with superior OS compared with FFX (adjusted HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.98; p = 0.015; Table 2). TTF was similar between the two regimens (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.86–1.01; p = 0.070; Figure 2B).

In the subset of patients who received both regimens sequentially, the GnP–FFX sequence was associated with significantly longer OS than the FFX–GnP sequence (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.69–0.94; p = 0.006; Figure 2C). Among patients who initiated second‐line therapy after GnP, OS was similar between second‐line FFX and nal‐IRI/5‐FU/LV (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.81–1.09; p = 0.429; Figure 2D).

Influence of BRCA2 Variants on First‐Line Treatment Outcomes

Among patients treated with FFX, those with BRCA2 PVs had significantly better OS than those without BRCA2 PVs (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.43–0.86; p = 0.005; Figure 3A). Regarding TTF, patients with BRCA2 PVs showed a trend toward longer treatment duration than those without BRCA2 PVs (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.54–1.04; p = 0.085; Figure 4A). Patients with BRCA2 PVs also had a significantly higher ORR than those without BRCA2 PVs (66.7% vs. 31.5%; p < 0.001), and the DCR was also significantly higher (88.9% vs. 74.0%; p = 0.023; Table 3).

In the GnP‐treated cohort, patients with BRCA2 PVs likewise had prolonged OS compared with those without BRCA2 PVs (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.95; p = 0.023; Figure 3B). Conversely, patients with BRCA2 PVs had significantly shorter TTF than those without BRCA2 PVs (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.14–1.90; p = 0.003; Figure 4B). This association persisted in analyses restricted to patients who transitioned to second‐line therapy before CGP results became available, minimizing the impact of CGP result–driven early switching (Figure S1). Tumor response did not differ significantly by BRCA2 PV status: ORR was 33.3% vs. 31.8% (p = 0.785), and DCR was 68.3% vs. 78.4% (p = 0.064; Table S1).

Treatment Outcomes and Sequencing by BRCA2 Status

In subgroup analyses stratified by BRCA2 status, patients with BRCA2 PVs showed no significant difference in OS between the FFX and GnP groups (HR 1.11, 95% CI 0.70–1.75; p = 0.665; Figure 3C). However, GnP was associated with significantly shorter TTF than FFX (HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.28–2.97; p = 0.002; Figure 4C).

In contrast, patients without BRCA2 PVs treated with GnP had superior OS compared with those treated with FFX (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.84–1.00; p = 0.040; Figure 3D). Consistent with the OS results, GnP was also associated with significantly longer TTF than FFX in patients without BRCA2 PVs (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.84–0.98; p = 0.018; Figure 4D).

When focusing on treatment sequencing in patients with BRCA2 PVs who were exposed to both regimens sequentially, OS was comparable between the GnP–FFX and FFX–GnP sequences (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.42–1.89; p = 0.756; Figure 3E). Among patients with BRCA2 PVs who received second‐line therapy after GnP, OS was numerically longer with FFX than nal‐IRI/5‐FU/LV (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.22–1.19; p = 0.119; Figure 3F). As descriptive data, in the overall cohort of patients with BRCA2 PVs after first‐line GnP, second‐line regimens included FFX in 34.4% (31/90) and nal‐IRI/5‐FU/LV in 21.1% (19/90) (Table S2).

To further characterize outcomes after first‐line discontinuation, we evaluated post–treatment failure survival (defined as the time from discontinuation of first‐line GnP to death). In patients who received GnP followed by second‐line FFX, those with BRCA2 PVs had longer post–treatment failure survival than those without BRCA2 PVs (HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.28–0.77; p = 0.003; Figure 4E). In contrast, in patients who received GnP followed by second‐line nal‐IRI/5‐FU/LV, post–treatment failure survival was similar by BRCA2 PV status (Figure 4F).

Olaparib was administered at some point during the treatment course in 24 of 54 patients (44.4%) who received first‐line FFX and in 26 of 90 patients (28.9%) who received first‐line GnP. In the FFX group, olaparib was initiated as the next line of therapy after discontinuation of first‐line FFX in 18 of 54 patients (33.3%), whereas the remaining 6 of 54 patients (11.1%) received olaparib in later lines. In the GnP group, olaparib was initiated as the next line therapy after discontinuation of first‐line GnP in 4 of 90 patients (4.4%), while the remaining 22 of 90 patients (24.4%) received olaparib in later lines (Table S2).

Impact of Other DNA Repair Variants ( BRCA1 , PALB2 )

Among patients who received first‐line FFX, the presence of BRCA1 PVs was not associated with OS compared with patients without BRCA1 PVs (HR 1.43, 95% CI 0.60–3.46; p = 0.421; Figure S2A), and OS was also similar according to BRCA1 PV status in the GnP‐treated cohort (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.54–1.38; p = 0.539; Figure S2B).

PALB2 PVs likewise showed no significant impact on OS in patients treated with FFX (HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.67–2.01; p = 0.568; Figure S2C). By contrast, among patients who received first‐line GnP, PALB2 PVs were associated with superior OS compared with patients without PALB2 PVs (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.30–0.95; p = 0.032; Figure S2D). ORR and DCR were broadly similar irrespective of BRCA1 or PALB2 PV status in both the FFX and GnP cohorts, and no clear association between these genes and tumor response was identified (Table 3 and Table S1).

Outcomes According to Germline BRCA1/2 Variant Status

We evaluated treatment outcomes according to germline BRCA1/2 status in the subset of patients who underwent the OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel systems. Given the small number of patients with germline BRCA1/2 PVs, these results should be interpreted with caution. Among patients treated with first‐line FFX, OS did not differ between those with germline BRCA1/2 PVs and those without (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.37–1.90; p = 0.675; Figure S3A). Similar results were observed in the GnP cohort (HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.60–2.11; p = 0.715; Figure S3B).

With first‐line FFX and GnP, ORR and DCR were numerically higher in patients with germline BRCA1/2 PVs than in those without, although these differences were not statistically significant (Table S3).

Among patients with germline BRCA1/2 PVs, OS did not differ between first‐line GnP and FFX (HR for GnP vs. FFX: 1.07, 95% CI 0.38–3.02; p = 0.900; Figure S3C).

Impact of TMB on Outcomes

Among the 4014 patients with available TMB data, 115 (2.9%) had TMB‐high tumors and 3899 (97.1%) had TMB‐low tumors (Table 4). In the TMB‐high group, BRCA2 PVs were more frequent than in the TMB‐low group (11.3% vs. 3.0%; p < 0.001), whereas the prevalence of BRCA1 PVs was similar between groups, and PALB2 PVs showed a trend toward enrichment (2.6% vs. 0.9%; p = 0.088). Patients with TMB‐high tumors had longer OS than those with TMB‐low tumors (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.50–0.83; p < 0.001; Figure 2E). Within the TMB‐high subgroup, OS did not differ between first‐line GnP and FFX (HR for GnP vs. FFX: 1.58, 95% CI 0.93–2.69; p = 0.091; Figure 2F).

Discussion

In this nationwide clinico‐genomic cohort, GnP was associated with superior adjusted OS compared with FFX in the overall population, while TTF was largely comparable between the two regimens. These findings are consistent with recent randomized trials showing no clear overall survival advantage of modified FFX over GnP in advanced pancreatic cancer [20] and support the use of GnP as a standard first‐line option in real‐world practice.

Regarding specific molecular subgroups, the association between BRCA1/2 variants and platinum sensitivity is well‐established across ovarian, breast, and prostate cancers, providing the biological rationale for platinum‐based chemotherapy and PARP inhibition in these settings [21, 22, 23]. In pancreatic cancer, molecularly informed cohorts suggest benefit of platinum‐based therapy in tumors with germline BRCA1/2 or PALB2 variants and other HRD‐related alterations [13, 24, 25]. In addition, using the same C‐CAT database, Ishigaki et al. [18] reported an association between homologous recombination deficiency and longer time to treatment failure with platinum‐based chemotherapy. In our pancreatic cancer cohort, patients with BRCA2 PVs exhibited a higher ORR to platinum‐based therapy. Although overall survival did not differ significantly between those starting with FFX versus GnP, the superior tumor shrinkage achieved with FFX highlights the potential clinical relevance of platinum‐based regimens, particularly when considering strategies such as conversion surgery for locally advanced or borderline resectable disease.

Notably, patients with BRCA2 PVs treated with first‐line GnP experienced significantly shorter TTF compared to those without BRCA2 PVs. We further restricted the analysis to patients who transitioned to second‐line therapy before CGP results became available (Figure S1), and the shorter TTF in patients with BRCA2 PVs persisted, suggesting that this finding is unlikely to be explained solely by CGP‐driven switching and that additional factors may also contribute. Moreover, although not statistically significant, disease control with GnP tended to be lower in patients with BRCA2 PVs (Table S1), raising the possibility of biological heterogeneity and/or relatively reduced benefit from a non‐platinum regimen such as GnP. Despite the shorter TTF on first‐line GnP, overall survival in patients with BRCA2 PVs remained favorable. This observation suggests that long‐term survival may be driven by the efficacy of subsequent platinum‐containing therapy (FFX) or PARP inhibitors rather than the first‐line regimen alone. In other words, while the initial choice (FFX vs. GnP) may be flexible, the incorporation of platinum agents at some point during the treatment course appears important to maximize survival benefit.

For PALB2 PVs, an association with improved OS was observed in patients who started with GnP. However, considering that tumor response did not differ significantly according to PALB2 status, this survival advantage should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. Detailed inspection revealed that long‐term survivors in this group often received oxaliplatin‐based therapy in later lines. Since starting with FFX did not inherently confer a better prognosis in our data, the apparent advantage of the GnP‐start group may be attributable to imbalances in patient characteristics or the successful sequencing of therapies rather than the superiority of GnP itself.

TMB‐high tumors, observed in 2.9% of evaluable patients, appeared to be associated with markedly favorable OS. This advantage is likely attributable to the robust efficacy of subsequent ICI therapy. Notably, 48.7% of patients with TMB‐high tumors received ICIs compared to only 0.2% of those with TMB‐low tumors. This underscores the importance of CGP to identify candidates for immunotherapy.

This study has limitations inherent to retrospective real‐world analyses. Treatment selection bias, incomplete capture of dose modifications and subsequent therapies, and heterogeneity in response assessment may have influenced the observed associations. Notably, PS was recorded at the time of genomic profiling, which may not necessarily reflect the PS at the initiation of first‐line chemotherapy. Since detailed information on baseline PS at treatment initiation was not available in the C‐CAT dataset, unmeasured confounding by patient fitness could not be fully excluded. Comparisons of second‐line regimens are particularly susceptible to confounding by indication. In addition, biomarker‐defined subgroups were relatively small, limiting power for interaction testing. Finally, the analyses restricted to germline variants and the impact of PARP inhibitors (e.g., olaparib) could not be robustly evaluated due to the limited number of patients.

In conclusion, this nationwide study supports GnP as a standard first‐line regimen for advanced PDAC in real‐world practice. While BRCA2 PVs confer sensitivity to platinum agents, survival benefits are likely driven by the sequence of therapies received rather than the first‐line regimen alone. Thus, CGP may help optimize timely access to platinum‐based chemotherapy for patients with BRCA2 PVs and immunotherapy for those with TMB‐high tumors.
