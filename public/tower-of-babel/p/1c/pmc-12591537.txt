Marking a milestone as we forge on
Authors: Kee Chong Ng, Troy Hai Kiat Puar, Tiing Leong Ang
Published: 2025

The year 2025 marks the 90th anniversary of Changi General Hospital (CGH). Since its humble beginnings as a 150-bed Royal Air Force (RAF) Hospital set up by the British in 1935, it has transformed into a modern, integrated care hospital.[1] While CGH continues to fulfil its mission of providing comprehensive acute medical care, it is also actively engaging in medical education, research and innovation as part of SingHealth’s Academic Medical Centre. Thus, it is fitting to commemorate this occasion with a supplementary issue in Singapore Medical Journal that showcases the academic achievements and healthcare insights of the CGH team.

The first paper traces the origins of CGH from its beginnings as the British RAF Hospital in 1935 to the merger of Changi Hospital and Toa Payoh Hospital in 1997. History serves as both a mirror of the past and a lesson for the present and the future. It is the study of change and serves as important guideposts for the future so that CGH continues to forge ahead to meet the needs of the 21st century and beyond.[1]

The four commentaries in this issue address topical issues that are important to the medical community. Tan highlights the importance of exercise in the prevention and management of chronic diseases and advocates the inclusion of physical activity as a standard component of medical treatment. Barriers to implementation are discussed and strategies to translate clinical evidence into actual practice are suggested.[2]

Although our current healthcare model is based on Western medicine, complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) remains an integral part of our Asian heritage. In this light, Siau and Tan provide a concise review of the integrative care medicine model. This approach merges conventional Western medicine with evidence-based CAM. The commentary offers insights into a structured integration programme that can provide clarity and clinical data to guide the development of integrative care medicine in Singapore.[3] There is now increasing emphasis on the concept of value-based medicine to ensure the sustainability and effectiveness of Singapore’s healthcare system in the era of ever-rising healthcare costs. Koh et al. discuss the eight pillars of value for adoption: (1) clinical excellence: mortality and morbidity outcome measures; (2) operational excellence: length of stay, wait times and other operational metrics; (3) care transition excellence: process measures to determine care transition quality; (4) sustainability excellence: carbon footprint, waste reduction and other sustainability metrics; (5) service excellence: patient-reportable outcome and experience measures; (6) patient empowerment excellence: behavioural and observational measures; (7) staff wellbeing excellence: ‘joy at work’ measures; and (8) cost excellence: cost-effectiveness and cost avoidance measures.[4]

By 2026, Singapore will become a superaged society, where one in five persons will be aged 65 years or above. In this context, Chen et al. discuss frailty and sarcopenia, conditions that result in greater healthcare costs, increased hospitalisation rates, increased caregiver burden and diminished quality of life for older adults. To effectively address frailty and sarcopenia, a multifaceted approach that incorporates physical activity, nutritional support and social interventions is needed.[5]

The issue also includes seven review articles that provide in-depth discussions on approaches to disease management and the adoption of healthcare processes. The increasing prevalence of cardiometabolic disorders, attributed to an ageing population, lifestyle changes and increased prevalence of overweight and obesity, poses a major healthcare burden to society. Khoo et al. examine strategies for the prevention and management of such disorders as key components of individualised health plans under Healthier SG.[6] In Singapore, colorectal cancer is the second most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Teo et al. discuss strategies to improve clinical outcomes and patient quality of life. These include the use of image-enhanced endoscopy and artificial intelligence to improve the detection and diagnosis of colonic adenomas and early cancer, endoscopic resection as the preferred curative treatment option for colonic adenoma and T0 and T1 colorectal cancer limited to the superficial submucosa, emergency colonic stenting as a bridge to interval curative surgery and the adoption of minimally invasive surgery for curative resection.[7]

Correctional facilities are a major hub for hepatitis C virus (HCV) transmission. Kumar et al. summarise the scientific evidence and progress towards HCV elimination in correctional health systems. Their review consolidates the strong evidence that prison-based HCV treatment is cost-effective, promotes health equity, supports the World Health Organization’s goal of eliminating HCV as a public health threat by 2030 and reduces the societal burden of the disease.[8] There is increasing interest in the use of nature-based therapy to complement traditional medical treatment, although the best approach remains uncertain. Kwok et al. discuss the potential of nature-based therapy to holistically address the physical and mental facets of health and wellbeing, and discuss how it can be implemented to complement current healthcare strategies.[9]

The intertwined relationship between healthcare and environmental sustainability is now well recognised. With the world’s second highest healthcare emissions per capita, Singapore’s healthcare sector poses a challenge to the nation’s pledge to reduce emissions by 2030 and achieve net zero emission by 2050. Koh et al. examine measures to mitigate the impact of healthcare on the environment. This review highlights both opportunities and challenges across medical disciplines, using the ‘R’s of sustainability — rethinking, reducing/refusing, reusing/repurposing/reprocessing, repairing, recycling and research.[10]

Aviation Medicine and Correctional Health are niche areas of clinical service delivery at CGH. Aviation Medicine ensures flight safety by addressing three critical areas: managing the physiological challenges of the aviation environment, preventing in-flight medical incapacitation and ensuring psychological fitness for flight. Soh et al. discuss the ongoing challenge of balancing rigorous medical standards with the need to maintain an adequate pilot recruitment pool, highlighting the need for evidence-based approaches to aeromedical assessment and certification.[11] The final review article focuses on building an artificial intelligence and digital ecosystem as an enabler for healthcare excellence.[12]

As we reflect on the past and contemplate the present, we also dream of the future and plan for the next phase. We hope this supplementary issue provides valuable insights into current healthcare challenges and serves as a platform for inspiration as we continue our healthcare journey in this little red dot we call Singapore.
