Learning Curve for CO2 Laser Stapedotomy: A Single‐Surgeon Experience in South Korea
Authors: Dong Woo Nam, Hyoungwon Jeon, Ja‐Won Koo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveMastering otosclerosis surgery is challenging, especially in low‐prevalence Asian populations. This study defines the learning curve for a single surgeon's first 100 consecutive CO2 laser‐assisted stapedotomies by analyzing audiometric and complication outcomes. Despite widespread descriptions of laser‐assisted stapes surgery, there is a paucity of data examining the learning process in low‐incidence, low‐volume regions like East Asia. We aim to characterize the unique challenges and surgical adaptations required in such an environment.MethodsA retrospective review of 100 primary stapedotomy cases was performed. The proficiency changepoint was identified using Bayesian and cumulative sum (CUSUM) analyses based on success (postoperative air‐bone gap [ABG] ≤ 10 dB and no significant bone‐conduction deterioration). Audiometric outcomes—including postoperative ABG, achievement of “Negative ABGe” (postoperative air conduction superior to preoperative bone conduction), and change in high‐tone bone conduction—were compared between the pre‐proficiency (cases 1–30) and post‐proficiency (cases 31–100) phases.ResultsA proficiency changepoint was robustly identified at 30 cases. Subsequently, outcomes improved significantly: mean postoperative ABG decreased from 12.1 dB to 5.2 dB (p < 0.001), and the success rate (ABG ≤ 10 dB) increased from 53.3% to 91.4%. The rate of achieving a Negative ABGe surged from 3.3% to 50.0% (p < 0.001). The mean change in high‐tone bone conduction also shifted from −0.3 to +6.1 dB of overclosure (p < 0.001). All major complications occurred within the initial 30 cases.ConclusionFor CO2 laser‐assisted stapedotomy, proficiency significantly improves after approximately 30 cases. This study uniquely highlights how surgical mastery develops under conditions of limited disease prevalence and restricted training exposure. The findings suggest that even in low‐volume environments, structured experience enables not only consistent ABG closure but also meaningful gains in patient‐centered outcomes such as Negative ABGe and cochlear preservation. Our results advocate for simulation‐enhanced education strategies tailored to resource‐constrained surgical contexts.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Stapes surgery is performed to improve the conductive hearing loss caused by stapes fixation such as otosclerosis or congenital stapes fixation. Expert surgeons have reported excellent hearing outcomes in previous studies [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, inappropriate management can lead to unexpected complications, such as sensorineural hearing loss or vertigo. Complications such as footplate subluxation, a floating footplate, or bleeding after footplate fenestration can result in permanent morbidity [5].

The surgical procedure requires exceptional attention and delicate technique due to the intricate fenestration of the inner ear. Consequently, multiple studies have investigated the learning curve surgeons must navigate to achieve optimal results [6, 7, 8].

Otosclerosis is considerably less prevalent in the Asian population compared to Caucasian populations, which have a prevalence of 0.3%–0.4% [9]. Despite an increasing number of fellowship‐trained otologists in Korea, the number of surgical corrections for stapes fixation remains limited. Most trainees have minimal or no opportunities to perform stapes surgery during their training period, a situation compounded by medico‐legal concerns and the scarcity of surgical cases.

This lack of surgical opportunities can significantly prolong surgeons' learning curves, and trainees often struggle to achieve procedural proficiency within the standard training period [10]. The senior author of this manuscript started stapes surgery in 2003 using CO2 laser and performed 123 stapes surgeries for various etiologies of stapes fixation over 16 years. An annual surgical volume of approximately 10 cases is typically considered low, suggesting that the learning curve for this procedure would likely be longer than initially expected.

While CO2 laser‐assisted stapedotomy has been extensively described in Western populations, the surgical learning process in Asian otologic practice remains underexplored. In countries like South Korea, the dual challenge of disease rarity and low case volume imposes a fundamentally different learning trajectory compared to high‐volume settings. Thus, understanding how surgical proficiency evolves in such an environment is both clinically and educationally valuable.

Recent literature demonstrates considerable variation in the number of surgeries required to reach proficiency in stapes surgery. Some studies report proficiency can be achieved with as few as 15 cases, while others suggest 70–80 cases are necessary, depending on surgical technique, success criteria, and patient complexity [11]. Moreover, the rarity of otosclerosis in Korea may stem from true ethnic differences, underdiagnosis, or potentially reduced disease severity compared to Caucasian populations.

While successful closure of the postoperative air‐bone gap (ABG) is the conventional measure of success, a more ambitious, patient‐centered goal is to restore hearing to a level that meets or even exceeds the patient's preoperative cochlear potential. Evaluating the final hearing outcome against the preoperative bone conduction provides a more stringent benchmark for what might be termed “superior functional restoration.” This approach moves beyond merely closing a gap to assessing whether the patient's hearing has been restored to its best possible level.

Therefore, in this study, the authors aim to determine the learning curve of primary incus stapedotomy, analyzing the path to proficiency using both conventional and more stringent success criteria.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

A total of 123 stapes surgeries performed by a single surgeon between November 2003 and April 2019 were retrospectively reviewed for eligibility. As detailed in the patient flow diagram (Figure 1), cases were excluded if they involved malleostapedotomy, revision surgery, or a congenital ossicular anomaly. After applying these criteria, the results of 100 consecutive cases undergoing primary CO2 laser stapedotomy for otosclerosis were included in the final analysis. The study was conducted at the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, and the patient ages ranged from 13 to 77 years. During residency and fellowship, the surgeon gained exposure to stapes surgery through assisting opportunities. These 100 cases represent his first independent stapedotomies as an attending faculty member, and a senior faculty surgeon supervised the entire procedure during his first independent operation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B‐2103‐672‐102). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical guidelines. The requirement for patient informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of fully de‐identified data.

Flow diagram of the study population. The diagram illustrates the process of including and excluding patient cases for the final analysis.

Surgical Procedure for Incus‐Stapedotomy

The operation was performed via an endaural approach. The middle ear cavity and ossicles were exposed following tympanomeatal flap elevation. After confirming stapedial fixation by gentle palpation, the incudostapedial joint was separated. The stapes superstructure was then divided using a CO2 laser (Lumenis, Tel Aviv, Israel) and removed. After measuring the distance from the medial side of the incus to the stapes footplate, a Teflon platinum piston wire prosthesis (PWP) was tailored. After laser footplate fenestration, the PWP was inserted into the fenestration and the wire was anchored over the incus long process using a crimper. The fenestration site was sealed with soft tissue and fibrin glue, the tympanomeatal flap was repositioned, and the ear canal was packed.

Audiometric Evaluations

All audiometric analyses were based on the 1995 guidelines from the American Academy of Otolaryngology—Head and Neck Surgery's Committee on Hearing and Equilibrium guidelines [12]. The four‐tone pure‐tone average was calculated from thresholds at 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 kHz [13]. Postoperative audiograms obtained 6 months or longer after the operation were used for evaluating hearing outcomes.

For the primary analysis, surgical success was defined as a postoperative air‐bone gap (ABG) ≤ 10 dB with no significant deterioration in bone conduction (< 10 dB). The standard postoperative ABG was calculated as the four‐tone PTA for air conduction (AC) minus the four‐tone PTA for bone conduction (BC) from the same postoperative audiogram.

Furthermore, to assess a more patient‐centered and stringent measure of success—which we term “Superior Hearing Restoration”—the “eventual ABG” (ABGe) was calculated. The ABGe is defined as the postoperative AC PTA minus the preoperative BC PTA. An ABGe of less than 0 dB, referred to as a “Negative ABGe,” was used as a key indicator of achieving this superior outcome, signifying that the patient's final hearing surpassed their preoperative cochlear potential.

Other audiometric changes were also assessed to provide a comprehensive view of the outcomes. The change in ABG (ABG closure) was determined by subtracting the postoperative ABG from the preoperative ABG. To monitor for potential cochlear enhancement (“overclosure”) or iatrogenic damage, the change in the high‐tone BC average (1, 2, and 4 kHz) was calculated as the preoperative BC average minus the postoperative BC average. Additionally, changes in AC thresholds at high frequencies (4 and 8 kHz) were analyzed separately. For reporting purposes, postoperative ABG results were also classified into standard bins (“Best,” “Good,” etc.) as recommended by the committee.

Type of Otosclerosis

Otosclerosis was classified as either fenestral or cochlear type based on temporal bone CT findings. In addition to the overall analysis, surgical success rates were compared between these two subtypes.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics; continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

The primary analysis focused on identifying the surgical learning curve's proficiency changepoint using two independent methods. First, a Bayesian changepoint model was implemented on binary surgical success outcomes (postoperative ABG ≤ 10 dB and no significant BC deterioration). Second, a cumulative sum (CUSUM) chart of the same outcomes was analyzed using a piecewise linear regression model to find the point that minimized the residual sum of squares. Concordance between these two methods was considered evidence of a robust transition point.

In addition, a Bayesian changepoint model was applied to the time intervals between consecutive surgeries to evaluate whether operative frequency influenced the learning process. This analysis provided both the most probable changepoint and posterior estimates of mean inter‐surgery intervals before and after the transition. Inter‐surgery intervals showed a gradual decline during the early phase, reaching a stable plateau around case ~20. For parsimony, we summarized this transition using a Bayesian changepoint model with different pre‐ and post‐transition means (μ1, μ2), which captures the convergence to a new steady state without assuming an abrupt jump. For clarity, we briefly summarize the Bayesian approach: the Bayesian changepoint model estimates the most likely case number at which the distribution of outcomes (or intervals) shifts, while providing a credible interval that quantifies the uncertainty of this estimate. Unlike traditional hypothesis testing, this Bayesian framework allows direct probabilistic statements about when proficiency was most likely achieved.

Following the identification of the changepoint, a secondary analysis was performed to compare surgical outcomes between the “pre‐proficiency” phase (cases 1–30) and the “post‐proficiency” phase (cases 31–100). Independent‐samples t‐tests were used to compare the means of continuous audiometric variables (e.g., preoperative and postoperative ABG, mean ABGe, change in high‐tone BC). Fisher's exact test was used to compare the proportions of categorical outcomes, such as the rate of achieving a postoperative ABG ≤ 10 dB and the rate of achieving a Negative ABGe.

Finally, a separate subgroup analysis using Fisher's exact test was conducted to compare surgical success rates between patients with fenestral‐type and cochlear‐type otosclerosis. For all statistical tests, a two‐tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics

A total of 100 stapedotomy procedures were performed in 77 patients over a 16‐year period. The study population consisted of 50 women and 27 men, with a mean ± SD age of 44.8 ± 12.3 years (range, 13 to 77 years). The right ear was operated on in 52 cases and the left ear in 48.

Learning Curve Analysis and Changepoint Identification

To objectively estimate the point of transition to surgical proficiency, two independent changepoint analyses were conducted on binary surgical success outcomes (postoperative ABG ≤ 10 dB and no significant BC deterioration). A Bayesian changepoint model revealed a posterior peak centered at the 30th case (95% credible interval: 28–33), and a piecewise linear regression applied to the CUSUM chart identified the same changepoint at the 30th case (Figure 2). This strong concordance provided robust evidence for dividing the cohort into a “pre‐proficiency” phase (cases 1–30) and a “post‐proficiency” phase (cases 31–100) for subsequent analyses. Notably, the first 30 cases spanned a period of approximately 9 years, underscoring the limited surgical exposure in this low‐prevalence setting.

A complementary Bayesian changepoint analysis of inter‐surgery intervals demonstrated an earlier transition, with the most probable changepoint at the 20th case (95% credible interval: 12–34). The estimated mean interval between surgeries decreased from approximately 84 days before the changepoint (μ1, 95% credible interval: 66.6–103.0) to approximately 42 days thereafter (μ2, 95% credible interval: 32.1–54.3). This indicates that the surgeon's operating frequency nearly doubled after approximately 20 cases, suggesting that more condensed and repetitive practice opportunities became available at that stage (see Figure S1).

Notably, the preoperative ABG was significantly larger in the pre‐proficiency group compared to the post‐proficiency group (29.2 ± 7.8 dB vs. 23.5 ± 8.7 dB, p = 0.003), indicating that the initial surgical cases involved patients with poorer baseline hearing.

Despite this, postoperative outcomes improved dramatically with experience. The mean postoperative ABG decreased significantly from 12.1 ± 8.5 dB in the pre‐proficiency phase to 5.2 ± 3.9 dB in the post‐proficiency phase (p < 0.001). Consequently, the proportion of successful outcomes increased markedly: cases with a postoperative ABG ≤ 10 dB rose from 53.3% to 91.4% (p < 0.001), and cases with an ABG ≤ 5 dB rose from 10.0% to 61.4% (p < 0.001). Interestingly, the mean amount of ABG closure did not differ significantly between the groups (17.0 ± 11.3 dB vs. 18.3 ± 8.9 dB, p = 0.563).

Patient‐centered outcomes, as measured by ABGe, showed a similar dramatic improvement. The mean ABGe improved from +11.1 ± 10.2 dB to −1.3 ± 7.5 dB (p < 0.001), signifying that the average outcome in the proficiency phase surpassed the preoperative cochlear reserve. The rate of achieving a Negative ABGe (ABGe < 0 dB) correspondingly increased from 3.3% (1 of 30 cases) to 50.0% (35 of 70 cases) (p < 0.001).

Cochlear health outcomes also improved significantly. The change in high‐tone bone conduction shifted from a slight mean worsening in the pre‐proficiency phase (−0.3 ± 5.0 dB) to a significant mean improvement (overclosure) in the post‐proficiency phase (+6.1 ± 5.7 dB, p < 0.001).

To further investigate the evolution of surgical performance, the distribution of outcomes was analyzed in consecutive 10‐case blocks (Figure 3). The outcomes were categorized based on postoperative ABG as Best (≤ 10 dB), Good (> 10 to ≤ 20 dB), Fair (> 20 to ≤ 30 dB), and Poor (> 30 dB). During the initial 30 cases, the results were inconsistent; while a majority of outcomes were “Best” or “Good,” “Poor” outcomes (ABG > 30 dB) were exclusively observed within this early pre‐proficiency phase. After the 30‐case changepoint, a dramatic and sustained improvement is evident. The proportion of “Best” outcomes increased substantially, and importantly, no “Poor” outcomes were recorded in the subsequent 70 cases. This visualization strongly corroborates the learning curve analysis, clearly illustrating the transition from a period of variable performance to one of consistent, high‐quality surgical results.

Subgroup Analysis: Otosclerosis Type

Of the 100 cases, 82 were classified as fenestral‐type and 18 as cochlear‐type. Surgical success (postoperative ABG ≤ 10 dB and no BC deterioration) was achieved in 68 out of 82 cases (82.9%) with fenestral‐type otosclerosis and 12 out of 18 cases (66.7%) with cochlear‐type. This difference was not statistically significant (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.189).

High‐Frequency Air‐Conduction Threshold Changes

The changes in AC hearing thresholds between pre‐ and post‐operation were analyzed at high frequencies of 4 and 8 kHz (Figure 4). The analysis revealed a mean improvement of 15.5 ± 14.6 dB at 4 kHz and 4.3 ± 14.3 dB at 8 kHz. As shown in the scatter plot, a majority of cases demonstrated improved thresholds (points appearing below the “No Change” line), particularly at 4 kHz.

Complications

No intraoperative complications were recorded. Postoperative complications included three cases of BPPV (patient 3, 56, and 82), one case of sensorineural hearing loss, one postoperative infection, and one case of prosthesis subluxation. Notably, all three major complications (sensorineural hearing loss in patient 3, prosthesis subluxation in patient 17, and infection in patient 21) occurred within the initial 30‐case pre‐proficiency phase (Figure 2A).

Discussion

Our results demonstrated that surgical proficiency was most likely achieved after approximately 30 cases, as independently estimated by two complementary statistical methods. A Bayesian changepoint model using binary success outcomes (postoperative ABG ≤ 10 dB and no BC deterioration) identified the 30th case as the most probable transition point, with a 95% credible interval of 28–33. This was supported by a RSS‐minimizing piecewise linear regression applied to the CUSUM curve of surgical outcomes, which yielded the same changepoint at case 30 (Figure 2).

In addition to outcome‐based changepoint detection, we performed a separate Bayesian analysis of the time interval between surgeries. This model identified a significant reduction in inter‐surgery intervals around the 20th case (95% credible interval: 12–34), with the estimated mean interval nearly halved (from ~84 to ~42 days). Notably, surgical mastery emerged after only 10 further procedures, at approximately the 30th case. In absolute terms, it took about 9 years for the surgeon to accumulate the first 30 cases, underscoring the limited surgical exposure in this low‐prevalence setting. Importantly, this learning curve should also be interpreted in the context of the surgeon's background: since he did not have the opportunity to operate independently during residency and fellowship, these cases represented his first autonomous stapedotomies as an attending faculty member, initially performed under senior supervision. Thus, the observed learning curve primarily reflects the acquisition of new operative proficiency in independent practice, and the experience accumulated from other middle ear procedures—including manipulation of the stapes and other ossicles—also influenced its development. This temporal pattern highlights that proficiency was facilitated not only by the cumulative case number but also by the clustering of operative experiences within a shorter timeframe, reinforcing the notion that both the volume and frequency of practice are critical drivers of surgical learning.

After this point, surgical outcomes improved markedly, with the rate of achieving a postoperative ABG ≤ 10 dB increasing from 53.3% to 91.4%. Interestingly, while the mean amount of ABG closure was not significantly different between the two phases, the final postoperative ABG was significantly superior in the proficiency phase (5.2 dB vs. 12.1 dB). This suggests that the surgeon's proficiency evolved from merely reducing a large initial gap to more effectively and completely eliminating a smaller one. This is particularly noteworthy given that the initial cohort presented with significantly larger preoperative ABGs, potentially representing more challenging cases.

The analysis of “Superior Hearing Restoration,” as measured by the rate of Negative ABGe, provides even more compelling evidence for this 30‐case transition point. The rate of achieving this exceptional outcome increased dramatically and significantly from a single case (3.3%) in the pre‐proficiency phase to 50.0% in the post‐proficiency phase. This was accompanied by a marked shift in the mean ABGe from a positive value (+11.1 dB) to a negative value (−1.3 dB) after proficiency was reached. This finding provides powerful evidence that the surgeon's proficiency evolved beyond simply performing safe and effective surgery to consistently delivering a final hearing level superior to the patient's preoperative cochlear baseline in half of the cases. It solidifies the 30‐case mark not just as a point of competence, but as a critical transition to a higher level of surgical mastery.

The procedure's safety profile was favorable and aligned with the learning curve. All three major complications (sensorineural hearing loss, infection, and prosthesis subluxation) occurred within the initial 30 cases, a pattern supported by studies indicating that such risks are highest for novice surgeons [14]. This clear temporal distribution strongly suggests that surgical experience is critical for minimizing serious complications. This interpretation is further strengthened by the significant improvement in high‐tone bone conduction in the post‐proficiency phase (+6.1 dB). This shift from a slight cochlear function decline (−0.3 dB) early on to a consistent and strong overclosure later indicates that the surgeon's technical delicacy evolved to a point of not only avoiding inner ear trauma but also maximizing the mechanical advantages of the surgery.

In our cohort, surgical outcomes did not differ significantly between fenestral‐type and cochlear‐type otosclerosis. While anatomical differences between these subtypes have been suggested to influence postoperative results in prior studies [15, 16], our findings did not demonstrate a measurable impact. Further studies with larger sample sizes may help clarify whether otosclerosis subtype meaningfully affects surgical outcomes.

Postoperative hearing changes were noted at low/mid frequencies (0.5, 1, 2, 3 kHz) and at high frequencies (4, 8 kHz). The consistent improvements observed suggest that correction of stapes fixation can enhance high‐frequency hearing in many cases, and these thresholds should therefore be closely monitored when evaluating postoperative success.

In South Korea, the prevalence of otosclerosis remains unknown due to its rarity, and many hospitals have limited experience with diagnosis and management. The operator in this study works at a tertiary referral hospital that receives referrals from other institutions. This context may explain not only the steady case volume that enabled the learning curve to be overcome but also our finding that the initial patient cohort presented with a significantly larger preoperative air‐bone gap, as these could have been more complex or long‐standing cases.

Despite these promising results, our study has several important limitations. First, it is a single‐center, retrospective investigation at a tertiary referral hospital, raising questions about potential selection bias and limited generalizability. This possibility is supported by our data showing that patients in the initial phase had significantly worse preoperative hearing. Such case‐mix differences may either inflate procedural difficulty or, conversely, provide more opportunities to gain proficiency. To address these limitations, future research should prioritize multicenter, prospective studies. Additionally, this study utilized postoperative audiograms from 6 months onward, whereas the 1995 AAO‐HNS guidelines recommend a 1‐year interval for reporting long‐term ABG outcomes. While our results provide a robust view of the learning curve, future studies with longer‐term follow‐up would be beneficial to confirm these findings. Furthermore, given the low surgical volume in many environments, implementing structured simulation programs is critical. A hybrid curriculum that combines direct surgical observation with simulator‐based practice may offer the most effective strategy for producing competent stapes surgeons in the current training landscape [17, 18, 19].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that approximately 30 cases are required to reach a performance plateau in CO2 laser‐assisted incus‐stapedotomy under current surgical training conditions. This turning point was consistently identified using both Bayesian changepoint analysis and residual sum of squares–based piecewise regression, supporting the robustness of the observed learning curve. Once proficiency was achieved, surgical outcomes—particularly postoperative air‐bone gap closure and preservation of bone conduction—remained stable and favorable. While no statistically significant differences in outcomes were observed between fenestral‐type and cochlear‐type otosclerosis in this cohort, future studies with larger sample sizes may help clarify any subtype‐related trends. Given the rarity of otosclerosis and limited operative exposure during training in many institutions, simulation‐based educational tools may play a critical role in enabling young otologists to acquire surgical skills efficiently and safely.

Although laser stapes surgery has been widely studied, this study uniquely characterizes how surgical mastery emerges in low‐incidence, resource‐limited environments such as South Korea. Our findings reinforce that despite low surgical volumes, competence and even advanced performance can be achieved through consistent exposure and longitudinal case accumulation. Importantly, this research also introduces patient‐centered success metrics such as Negative ABGe, providing a more nuanced framework for evaluating proficiency beyond ABG closure. These insights support the development of tailored training programs—potentially including simulation and targeted mentorship—to bridge the learning gap in rare disease surgical scenarios.
