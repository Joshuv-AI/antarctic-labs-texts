Hepatoprotective Potential of Bacteroides eggerthii on Fatty Liver Disease: A Mouse Model Study
Authors: LI-WEI CHEN, I-WEN WU, TZU-CHIEN CHENG, LUN-CHING CHANG, CHENG-KAI HSU, LING YEUNG, LIANG-CHE CHANG, SHIH-CHI SU
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is a significant global health burden, with increasing evidence pointing to gut microbiota as a modifiable contributor to disease pathogenesis. Yet, the causality of gut microbiota in liver health is not fully established. This study aimed to clarify a causal relationship of specific gut microbes with MASLD.Materials and MethodsIn this pre-clinical study, we assessed the hepatoprotective potential of Bacteroides eggerthii (B. eggerthii), a gut commensal bacterium, in a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced mouse model of MASLD.ResultsOur in vivo experiments showed that mice given with HFD exhibited prominent metabolic abnormalities, intestinal dysbiosis, liver steatosis, and drivers of pathological changes in the livers (autophagic dysregulation and lipid peroxidation). Oral supplementation with a strain of B. eggerthii that was isolated from the fecal specimen of a healthy subject improved the perturbation of fecal microbiota and mitigated lipid peroxidation and lobular inflammation in the liver of MASLD mice, without ameliorating hyperlipidemic conditions and hepatic steatosis.ConclusionB. eggerthii may potentially protect against diet-induced fatty liver diseases and further extend our understanding on the gut-liver axis in MASLD.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common liver disease worldwide and presents a significant global health burden (1). Due to its strong association with metabolic syndromes, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes, NAFLD is recently redefined as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) (2). It is currently known that many factors related to a disrupted gut microbiome, such as increased intestinal permeability, altered bile acid metabolism, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated low-grade inflammation, are involved in the pathogenesis of MASLD (3-5). Accordingly, gut microbiota has been proposed as a target for drug design against MASLD (6). These findings support the emerging concept of a “gut-liver axis”, suggesting that shifts in gut microbiota may affect liver health by modulating systemic inflammation and metabolic pathways.

Bacteroides eggerthii (B. eggerthii), originally isolated from the normal flora of the human gastrointestinal tract, is a Gram-negative, anaerobic, non-spore-forming, commensal bacterium (7). Numerous lines of evidence from analyses of different B. eggerthii strains have implicate this gut commensal bacterium as a potential next-generation probiotics. Firstly, B. eggerthii was found to possess unique catalytic enzymes for converting complex carbohydrates into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) to maintain gut homeostasis and counteract inflammation (8-11), as depletion of SCFAs has been linked to MASLD pathogenesis (12). Moreover, B. eggerthii was found to contribute to secondary bile acid biosynthesis (13) and to produce a less immunostimulatory form of LPS (14), indicating a role in regulating steatosis formation and inflammatory reactions in MASLD. In addition, a decrease in the levels of B. eggerthii was detected in the gut of patients with diverse conditions related to metabolic dysfunction, including primary biliary cirrhosis (15), obesity (16), chronic kidney disease (17), and age-related macular degeneration (18). As manipulation of the gut microflora by oral administration with specific gut bacterium attuned the pathophysiology of the intestine and other distant organs in murine models of different human disorders (19-22), a cause-and-effect relationship of specific gut microbes with liver health remains unknown.

To determine the causal role of specific gut commensals in MASLD, we investigated the hepatoprotective effects of B. eggerthii in a mouse model of diet-induced fatty liver disease.

Materials and Methods

MASLD mouse model. Four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (National Laboratory Animal Center, Taiwan) were housed with free access to food and sterile drinking water under a 12 hours dark-light cycle. Following a one-week acclimation period, mice were fed for 16 weeks with standard chow diet (chow, 13.5% of energy from fat; LabDiet 5001; LabDiet, St. Louis, MO, USA) or high-fat diet (HFD, 60% of energy from fat; TestDiet 58Y1; TestDiet, St. Louis, MO, USA). Mean energy intake was evaluated by converting the amount of food taken into calories using the information provided by the manufacturer. A list of blood tests (glucose, triglycerides, albumin, total cholesterol, and liver conditions) was conducted to randomize the mice into individual experimental groups. This animal experiment was approved by the ethics committee at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Keelung, Taiwan (IACUC NO2022110201).

Bacterial isolation and treatment. Strains of B. eggerthii were isolated from the fecal samples of healthy donors who had not used antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics for three months prior to the study or family history of common disorders as previously described with some modifications (7). In brief, fresh feces were initially suspended in sterile PBS, further diluted in Bacteroides minimal medium, and then plated anaerobically on Bacteroides Bile Esculin (BBE) Agar (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37˚C until colonies are visualized as previously prescribed (23). Single colonies were collected, and their identity was evaluated firstly by matrix-assisted laser-desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) with a log (score) of ≥2.0 and subsequently verified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing with a homology level >98.65%. For cultivation of clinically-isolated bacterial strains, B. eggerthii S001 was grown in brain heart infusion (BHI) agar supplemented with 10% horse blood and tryptone-yeast extract-glucose (TYG) medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under anaerobic conditions at 37˚C, using a Whitley DG250 anaerobic chamber (Don Whitley, Bingley, UK) with mixed anaerobic gas (5% carbon dioxide, 5% hydrogen, 90% nitrogen). Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in sterile saline. Mice fed with/without HFD for 16 weeks were treated daily with sterile saline or 4×109 colony-forming units of B. eggerthii S001 for 8 weeks by oral gavage (6 animals/group). At week 25, fecal, blood, and liver samples were collected from each group for subsequent analyses.

Fecal microbiota analysis. Bacterial DNA from mouse feces was extracted by using FastDNA SPIN Kit for Feces (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA), and profiling of stool microbiota was performed as described previously (24). In briefly, the variable region 3 and 4 (V3-4) of 16S rRNA gene were PCR-amplified, purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit on a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Qubit, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries of sequence reads were generated using NEB Next® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). Purified libraries were quantified, normalized, pooled, and applied for cluster generation and sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Paired-end reads were processed by DADA2 to remove reads with one-time occurrence (singletons) or detected in only one sample. Bacterial taxonomy was assigned by QIIME2 according to the information retrieved from the SILVA database.

Measurement of circulating cytokines/chemokines/growth factors. Plasma levels of a panel of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors were measured by using the ProcartaPlex Multiplex Immunoassay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Each sample was tested in duplicate. Plates were read with the Luminex 200 system (Luminex, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed by using ProcartaPlex software (eBioscience, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Evaluation of hepatic lobular inflammation. NAFLD activity score (NAS) was applied for evaluating the status of hepatic lobular inflammation (25). Survey was performed by a pathologist (Dr. Cheng TC) and under traditional microscopy. Slides are scanned at 40× magnification using a Hamamatsu Nanozoomer S360 scanner and NDP image (Hamamatsu, Japan). Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually selected, and the scores were measured on at least 5 fields: Score 0 (none), 1(<2 foci), 2 (2-4 foci) and 3 (>4 foci).

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis of mouse livers and image quantification. Hepatic tissue sections were cut from the liver specimen at 4 μm and stained with H&E and trichome. For immunohistochemical staining, paraffin sections underwent antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0) at 95-100˚C for 20 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), followed by incubation with affinity-purified F(ab) fragment anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (ab6668, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:1,000 dilution). Sections were then incubated overnight at 4˚C with antibodies against the following proteins for detection: anti-Ki67 antigen (clone MIB-1, Dako, Carpinteria, CA, USA; 1:200 dilution), anti-cleaved cytokeratin 18 (M30 CytoDEATH antibody, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany; 1:100 dilution), anti-LC3B (NB100-2220, Novus Biologicals¸ LLC, Centennial, CO, USA; 1:200 dilution), and anti-4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4 HNE, ab48506, Abcam; 1:200 dilution). After washing, sections were then incubated with a peroxidase-conjugated Polymer and visualized using DAB+Chromogen provided by the REAL EnVision Detection System, Peroxidase/DAB (K5007, Dako). The positivity of Ki-67 staining was in nucleus, and the positive signals for CK18, LC3B and 4 NHE were localized in cytoplasm. For Ki-67, positivity was measured by calculating the percentage of positively stained nuclear area (labeling index) by using a color deconvolution algorithm for separating the staining components and using adaptive thresholding for nuclear area segmentation through QuPath platform as previously described (26). For cytoplasmic staining, the percentage of positively stained area in each ROI was calculated. Measurement was run based on pixel size, adjusted for thresholding, and recorded.

Statistical analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 18.0) for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Blood test and histological data between groups were compared by Student’s t test and Wilcoxon rank sum test, respectively. Bacterial α diversity (within-sample) estimated by the Chao1 index based on the taxonomical profile of each sample and β diversity (between-sample) evaluated by the UniFrac distance were compared by using Wilcoxon rank sum test. Discrimination in community composition among groups was analyzed with analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) test. Differentially abundant taxa were detected by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) of effect size (LEfSe) analysis. Results are expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges, unless otherwise indicated. All statistical tests are two-tailed. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Oral administration of B. eggerthii alleviated intestinal dysbiosis but not hyperlipidemic conditions in a mouse model of MASLD. Intestinal dysbiosis has been associated with the development and progression of MASLD (4). To explore a causal relationship of specific gut microbes with liver health, hepatoprotective potentials of B. eggerthii were examined in a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced mouse model of MASLD (Figure 1A). A strain of B. eggerthii (B. eggerthii S001), with its identity being verified by sequencing of full-length 16S rRNA gene (99.30% sequence similarity with its type strain, DSM 20697), was isolated from the stool of a healthy subject. We observed that oral delivery of B. eggerthii S001 did not influence body weight gain (Figure 1B) in HFD-fed mice. Further blood tests demonstrated that supplementation with B. eggerthii S001 failed to improve hyperlipidemic conditions, liver damages, and other systematic metabolic abnormalities induced by prolonged overnutrition (Figure 1C and Figure S1).

Besides host metabolism, we also explored the composition of fecal microbiota in HFD-fed mice after oral administration with B. eggerthii. Our profiling of intestinal microflora showed that HFD treatment caused an obvious shift in the composition of fecal microbiota as compared with that of chow-fed controls (Figure 2). Strikingly, supplementation with B. eggerthii S001 led to a significant change in alpha diversity (within samples) (Figure 2A), beta diversity (between samples) (Figure 2B-C), and taxonomical profiles of gut microbiota in HFD-fed mice (Figure S2), indicating an improvement of HFD-induced gut dysbiosis by B. eggerthii supplementation.

Oral gavage of B. eggerthii attenuated lipid peroxidation in the liver of MASLD mice. Although no significantly beneficial impact of our biotic intervention on mitigating the induction of circulating markers for liver injuries (GOT and GPT) in MASLD mice was observed, we next evaluated whether supplementation with B. eggerthii exhibits local effects through performing histological and immunohistochemical analyses of mouse livers. These assays capture many aspects of MASLD pathology, including the levels of steatosis, liver cell regeneration/proliferation (staining of Ki67), autophagy (staining of LC3B, microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3β), lipid peroxidation (staining of 4-HNE, 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal), and apoptosis (staining of CK18, an epitope of cytokeratin 18 that is presented after cleavage by caspases). We found that HFD treatment significantly increased levels of steatosis and lipid peroxidation whereas decreased levels of autophagy (Table I), as neither alteration in levels of liver cell proliferation and apoptosis (Table I) nor fibrosis (Figure S3) was detected in our animal experiments. Of note, oral administration of B. eggerthii S001 dampened HFD-induced lipid peroxidation in the liver of MASLD mice (Table I), as evidenced by a significant decrease in the percentage of positively stained area for 4-HNE. Our data unveil an effect of B. eggerthii supplement on ameliorating oxidative stress of hepatocytes induced in obesity-related MASLD, protecting these cells from HFD-induced lipid peroxidation.

Replenishment of B. eggerthii mitigated systematic and hepatic lobular inflammation in MASLD mice. Chronic inflammation serves as a bridge connecting metabolic abnormalities to MASLD (27). Through surveying a panel of cytokines, chemokine, and growth factor in the plasma, a suppressive effect of B. eggerthii supplement on chronic systematic inflammation was observed, highlighted by attenuated plasma levels of several pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-18, and IL-1β) in MASLD mice (Figure 3). Levels of other chemokines and growth factors, such as monocyte chemotactic protein-3 (MCP-3), interferon gamma-induced protein 10 (IP-10), and leptin, were also elevated in the HFD-fed group, but their induction was not reduced by our biotic intervention (Figure S4). Furthermore, in addition to systematic inflammation, evaluation of the hepatic lobular inflammation, a component of NAFLD activity score (NAS) (25), revealed an elevation of lobular inflammation by HFD (Table II). Such elevation in hepatic lobular inflammation was ameliorated after supplementation with B. eggerthii S001. These results suggest that B. eggerthii treatment improved systematic and hepatic inflammation in MASLD mice, further extending our understanding on the gut-liver axis in MASLD.

Discussion

Recent metagenomic surveys have unveiled a potential association of intestinal dysbiosis with the development and progression of MASLD (4,6). Clarification of the preventive or therapeutic potential of MASLD-associated next-generation microorganisms could be a vital improvement of our knowledge, facilitating the implementation of these discoveries into clinical practice. In this pre-clinical study, oral supplementation with a strain of Bacteroides eggerthii alleviated hepatic oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in a diet-induced mouse model of MASLD. As a proof of concept, our findings highlight a functional role of the gut-liver axis in fatty liver diseases, raising the possibility of targeting individual species of the gut microbiota for the management of MASLD.

Dysregulation of hepatic lipid metabolism is a key contributor to MASLD development, as manifested by excessive accumulation of lipid droplets in hepatocytes (28). Long-term HFD consumption disrupts systemic lipid homeostasis and promotes intracellular vacuolization within the liver tissue, which is tightly associated with autophagy-dependent granule degradation (29). In MASLD, hepatic uptake and de novo lipogenesis are augmented, while a compensatory enhancement of fatty acid oxidation is insufficient in normalizing lipid levels and may even elicit cellular damage and disease progression by inducing oxidative stress (30). Intestinal microflora is known to serve as a vital modulator of host lipid metabolism via microbial metabolites and bile acid signaling pathways (31,32). By partially restoring microbial symbiosis, B. eggerthii, as a gut commensal involved in secondary bile acid synthesis (13), may attenuate systemic lipotoxicity and improve lipid handling, indirectly protecting the liver from lipid-derived oxidative stress. In addition to bile acids, B. eggerthii expresses unique catalytic enzymes for converting complex carbohydrates into SCFAs to maintain gut homeostasis (8-11). SCFAs derived from B. eggerthii are demonstrated to engage G-protein coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43 and activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which can enhance lipid oxidation and suppress lipogenesis (33,34). These pathways may in some degree account for the hepatoprotective effects observed in our study.

LPS-activated low-grade inflammation is a major pathogenic driver of MASLD and a conceivable connection between intestinal dysbiosis and fatty liver diseases (4,5), largely through the formation of TLR4-MD-2-LPS complex. Rather than a highly inflammatory form of LPS that is hexa-acylated (6 acyl) and commonly found in the outer membrane of pathogenic bacteria, B. eggerthii produces a hypo-acylated LPS (4 acyl and 5 acyl) (14) known to be much less immunostimulatory and able to antagonize immune activation by hexa-acylated LPS (35,36). In addition, generation of SCFAs by B. eggerthii exhibited a variety of anti-inflammatory properties (37). Notably, our study detected a suppressive effect of B. eggerthii supplement on the induction of circulating inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-18, and IL-1β) and hepatic lobular inflammation in MASLD mice, implicating an immunomodulatory role for this potentially hepatoprotective bacterium in orchestrating the gut-liver axis.

There were several limitations in this study, despite the identification of some hepatoprotective effects. One caveat is that although steatosis and inflammation were consistently developed in our experiments, the duration of our HFD treatment was too short to cause hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even tumorigenesis (38-40). Exploration of beneficial effects on these advanced conditions will require a much longer period of overnutrition or a co-treatment with HFD and additional agents. Another issue is that administration of B. eggerthii S001 improved HFD-mediated intestinal dysbiosis. Nevertheless, survey on the detailed alterations in taxonomic compositions of gut microflora in B. eggerthii-treated MASLD mice is beyond the scope of the present study, as one recent investigation of next-generation probiotics has demonstrated that supplementation with a B. eggerthii strain modulated gut microbiota composition in 5-fluorouracil-treated mice by suppressing pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella, γ-Proteobacteria, and Shigella) and replenishing beneficial taxa (e.g. Lactobacillus and Akkermansia muciniphila) (41). Moreover, measurement for the percentage of abnormal or positively stained area in histological and IHC experiments could be interfered by fat droplets of variable sizes within hepatocytes (macrovesicles or microvesicles) produced by HFD treatment, in spite of the use of computation software that avoided potential intro-observers bios. Lastly, specific host-microbe co-metabolites and connections that contribute to the beneficial effects of B. eggerthii remain to be scrutinized, necessitating integration of metabolomic, transcriptomic, and immunological analyses.

Conclusion

Taken together, our study showed that potential next-generation probiotics, exemplified by B. eggerthii, can mitigate major drivers of HFD-induced pathological changes seen in MASLD, including lipid peroxidation and inflammatory responses. Our data support the gut-liver axis as a therapeutic target and indicate manipulation of gut microbiome as a tentative strategy to protect hepatocytes from diet-induced oxidative stress and inflammation.
