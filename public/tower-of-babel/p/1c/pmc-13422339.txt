Impact of exercise on myopia in children and adolescents and its modifying factors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jiacheng Ren, Xuan Liu, Lu Peng, Wei Sun, Aona Chen, Miaomiao Zhou, Meizhu Chen, Zixuan Peng, Chenggen Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study systematically evaluated the effects of exercise interventions on functional visual outcomes (dynamic and static visual acuity) and structural myopia indicators (refractive error and axial length) in children and adolescents, and compared the effectiveness of different intervention strategies to provide evidence for myopia prevention and control.MethodsWe systematically searched CNKI, Wanfang, PubMed, and Web of Science for randomized controlled trials investigating the effects of exercise on myopia in children and adolescents. Meta-analyses, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessments were conducted using Stata 18.0. Multivariate meta-regression analyses were performed in R (version 4.6.0) to identify potential sources of between-study heterogeneity.ResultsA total of 31 studies involving 9,892 children and adolescents were included. Meta-analysis demonstrated that exercise significantly improved both dynamic visual acuity (SMD = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.21–0.96, P < 0.001) and static visual acuity (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.46–0.92, P < 0.001), with both outcomes showing moderate effect sizes. Exercise also produced a small but significant improvement in refractive error (SMD = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12–0.45, P < 0.001) and significantly inhibited axial length growth (SMD = − 0.13, 95% CI: − 0.19 to − 0.07, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses indicated that students in grades 1–3, vision training interventions, programs conducted 4–6 times per week, and intervention durations of 120–300 min per week were associated with greater improvements in dynamic visual acuity, while interventions implemented in combined indoor–outdoor settings yielded the largest effect sizes. For static visual acuity, physical and mental health exercises, ball sports, intervention frequencies of 1–3 times per week, and durations of 120–300 min per week were associated with more favorable outcomes. Multivariate meta-regression identified intervention setting as a potential independent moderator of improvements in dynamic visual acuity, whereas differences among certain exercise types were attenuated after multivariable adjustment. These findings remained robust following CRVE correction. No stable or significant independent moderators were identified for static visual acuity. Axial length outcomes exhibited low heterogeneity, indicating a high degree of consistency across studies regarding the inhibitory effect of exercise on axial elongation.ConclusionExercise interventions exert a consistently positive effect on the visual function of children and adolescents, significantly improving both dynamic and static visual acuity. They may also help slow the structural progression of myopia, with particularly consistent effects on inhibiting axial elongation. Exercise modality and intervention characteristics may act as moderating factors influencing intervention efficacy. Specifically, vision training exercises conducted in combined indoor–outdoor settings and delivered at moderate intervention doses appear to be more effective for enhancing dynamic visual, whereas mind–body exercises and ball sports may provide greater benefits for static visual acuity. Overall, exercise interventions represent a promising adjunctive strategy for the comprehensive prevention and management of myopia in children and adolescents.

Introduction

Myopia has emerged as a major global public health concern [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the prevalence of myopia continues to increase worldwide and is affecting progressively younger populations [2]. The prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents increased from 24.32% in 1990 to 35.81% in 2023 and is projected to reach 39.80% by 2050, with the number of affected individuals expected to exceed 740 million [3]. In Europe, myopia prevalence among White populations remains relatively low, affecting approximately 3–5% of 10-year-olds and 20% of those aged 12–13 years. The lowest prevalence has been reported among Black African populations, with only 5.5% of 15-year-olds affected [4]. In contrast, the prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents in East Asia is substantially higher than the global average, reaching up to 69% among 15-year-olds [5]. Compared with Western populations, Asian populations exhibit a greater susceptibility to myopia [6]. Clinically, myopia in children and adolescents is primarily characterized by excessive ocular growth and axial elongation, causing incident light to focus anterior to the retina and resulting in visual impairment. Furthermore, each 1-diopter increase in myopia is associated with a significantly elevated risk of ocular complications, including retinal detachment, myopic macular schisis, and myopic choroidal neovascularization [7]. Beyond its clinical consequences, myopia adversely affects quality of life by restricting daily activities, impairing attention, and negatively influencing academic performance. In addition, many medical interventions remain costly and difficult to implement in low-resource settings [8], contributing to psychosocial stress and a sustained economic burden [2].

Exercise is widely recognized as a key non-pharmacological strategy for preventing the onset of myopia and slowing its progression [9]. Previous studies have shown that regular participation in sports not only helps preserve visual acuity in children and adolescents but may also enhance visual function in those who are already myopic [10]. However, “improved vision” is a multifaceted concept, and different visual outcome measures reflect distinct physiological dimensions. Dynamic and static visual acuity primarily assess functional visual performance, whereas refractive error and axial length are more indicative of the structural progression of myopia. Despite growing interest in this field, evidence regarding the effects of exercise on myopia in children and adolescents remains inconsistent. For instance, Zhu et al. reported that outdoor activities significantly reduce the incidence of myopia among children and adolescents [11], whereas Karthikeyan et al. suggested that myopia prevention may be primarily attributable to retinal dopamine secretion induced by natural light exposure rather than exercise itself [12]. Similarly, Cheng et al. identified an optimal exercise frequency of 6–7 sessions per week [13], while Luo et al. reported the greatest benefits with three sessions per week [14]. Existing meta-analyses have several limitations. Although their findings generally support a protective effect of exercise, most included cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, or non-randomized controlled trials [9, 15, 16], which are more susceptible to confounding bias and may reduce the overall quality of evidence. In addition, many studies have relied on the average refractive error of both eyes or the visual acuity of the dominant eye as outcome measures, potentially obscuring interocular differences in visual function [17, 18]. Furthermore, previous research has rarely distinguished between functional visual performance and structural myopia progression [19]. Compared with refractive error, axial length is considered a more reliable indicator of structural myopia progression, underscoring the importance of differentiating visual function improvement from myopia control. Another important gap is the lack of clear evidence regarding intervention effectiveness across educational stages. Most studies have analyzed elementary school students aged 6–12 years as a single population, with few rigorously evaluating stage-specific sensitivity to intervention timing [20–22]. Given the distinct developmental characteristics of the eye across childhood and adolescence, the timing of intervention may substantially influence the effectiveness of myopia prevention and control strategies [20].

Based on this rationale, the present study systematically synthesizes existing evidence through a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantitatively evaluate the effects of exercise interventions on myopia prevention and progression control in children and adolescents. Particular emphasis is placed on comparing the effects of exercise on functional visual outcomes and structural myopia-related indicators. In addition, the study examines potential heterogeneity across educational stages and other moderating factors, providing evidence-based insights and practical implications for promoting visual health in children and adolescents.

Methods

This meta-analysis adheres to the PRISMA statement (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) and is registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration ID: CRD420261307272).

Literature search strategy

Two researchers independently conducted the literature search. Any discrepancies in the search results were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when consensus could not be reached. Both Chinese and international databases were searched. For the Chinese-language search, a cross-referencing strategy was used, with keywords including “children,” “adolescents,” “exercise,” “sports,” “myopia,” “uncorrected visual acuity,” “static visual acuity,” “dynamic visual acuity,” “refractive error,” and “axial length.” The search covered studies published between 2010 and 2026. The English search strategy was as follows: (“myopia” OR “nearsightedness”) AND (“exercise” OR “sports”) AND (“adolescents” OR “teens” OR “youth” OR “pediatric” OR “children”) AND (“dynamic visual acuity” OR “DVA” OR “moving target” OR “motion perception” OR “visual tracking” OR “static visual acuity” OR “SVA” OR “stationary target” OR “visual acuity”) AND (“diopter” OR “refractive error” OR “refraction”) AND (“axial length” OR “ocular axial length” OR “eye axial length” OR “AL”).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) The study participants were physically and mentally healthy children and adolescents; (2) The study design was a randomized controlled trial (RCTs); (3) Outcome measures included dynamic visual acuity (DVA), static visual acuity (SVA), refractive error (RE), and axial length (AL), with complete reporting of mean values and standard deviations for each outcome measure; (4) The intervention details included the duration, frequency, and method of the intervention.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Non-randomized controlled trials; (2) studies for which the full text was unavailable; (3) Studies involving adults, special populations, or athletes; (4) studies with incomplete experimental data that prevented the extraction of valid outcome measures.

Literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment

Two researchers independently screened the literature and extracted the relevant data. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with consultation from a third reviewer when necessary to reach consensus. Duplicate publications were excluded from the initial list of eligible studies. Data extraction included the following variables: first author, publication year, study population, and study characteristics (sample size, gender, grade or age). Intervention-related variables included the intervention method, intervention frequency, intervention cycle, and total intervention duration. The outcome measures included DVA, SVA, RE and AL. Quality assessment was conducted using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool, which reported the percentage risk of bias across studies and provided detailed evaluations for each bias category. The Risk-of-Bias2 (ROB2) assessed study quality across five domains: (1) risk of bias in randomization; (2) risk of bias in detection; (3) bias of missing outcome data; (4) measurement bias; (5) selective reporting bias. Each study was rated as “low risk of bias,” “some concern,” or “high risk of bias,” and an overall assessment of bias was made.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis of the extracted results was performed using Stata 18.0 and heterogeneity tests were conducted to evaluate the variability of effect sizes across the included studies. Due to differences in the units used to measure visual acuity across trials, the pooled effect size for continuous outcomes was expressed as the standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Effect sizes are categorized according to Cohen’s criteria: 0.2 indicates a small effect size, 0.5 indicates a moderate effect, and 0.8 or above indicates a large effect size [23]. Heterogeneity was quantitatively assessed using the I2 statistic. I2 values of 0%, 25%, 50% and 75% are conventionally interpreted as indicating no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [24]. An I2value greater than 50% was considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity, warranting the use of a random-effects model. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Subgroup analyses were performed for the following variables: (1) educational stage: Grades 1–3, Grades 4–6, and Grades 7–9; (2) Implementation Environment: indoor, outdoor, or combined indoor-outdoor; (3) Intervention Method: ball sports, mind–body health exercises, visual training, or outdoor daily activities; (4) intervention duration: less than 120 min per week, 120–300 min per week, or more than 300 min per week; (5) intervention frequency: 1–3 times per week, 4–6 times per week, or 7 or more times per week. Due to the negligible heterogeneity observed among studies assessing axial length (I2 = 0.8%), indicating high consistency across findings, no further subgroup analyses were conducted for this outcome. Additionally, subgroup analyses for refractive error were not performed because of the limited number of eligible studies. For outcomes with a small number of studies, publication bias was assessed primarily through visual inspection of funnel plots. When at least 10 studies were available, Egger’s linear regression test and the trim-and-fill method were additionally applied, together with funnel plot symmetry, to evaluate potential publication bias [25, 26]. A P value > 0.05 was considered indicative of no significant publication bias.

To further investigate potential sources of heterogeneity and account for confounding among moderator variables, multivariable meta-regression analyses were performed using R software (version 4.6.0) to estimate the independent effects of each moderator on effect size. Model parameters were estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, and standard errors were adjusted using cluster-robust variance estimation (CRVE) to improve the robustness of parameter estimates. Multivariable meta-regression was conducted primarily for functional visual outcomes with substantial heterogeneity and a sufficient number of studies. Owing to the limited number of studies on refractive error and the negligible heterogeneity in axial length, no multivariable meta-regression was performed for structural myopia outcomes.

Literature search results

A database search identified 272 relevant publications. After removing 103 duplicates, 169 publications remained. Following a screening process based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 31 publications were ultimately included [27–57]. Basic characteristics of included studies

This study reviewed 31 publications, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 30 to 3,051 participants. In total, 9,892 mentally and physically healthy children and adolescents were included, comprising 5,166 in the experimental group and 4,726 in the control group. Sample sources included preschoolers [40, 56], elementary school students [27, 31, 33–38, 41–55, 57], junior high school students [28–30, 32, 39]. All 31 studies included in this analysis were randomized controlled trials. Intervention durations ranged from 6 [37] to 117 weeks [57] with exercise frequencies varying from twice [29, 47] to ten times per week [44], The total intervention duration ranged from 576 min [27] to 87,360 min [55]. The intervention for the experimental group included multiple types of exercise, which were classified into four categories: Ball Sport [35–41, 43, 46, 47, 50] and Mind–Body Health Exercises [27, 34, 42, 44, 45], Visual Training Category [28, 29, 31–33, 46, 48, 51] and Outdoor Daily Activities Category [30, 49, 52–57]. Meta-analysis results

Functional visuals

This study classified DVA and SVA as functional visual outcomes, which are commonly used to evaluate visual system performance and accommodative function. The meta-analysis demonstrated that exercise interventions significantly improve functional visual performance in children and adolescents, although the magnitude of improvement varied across visual outcomes.

Dynamic visual acuity

Twelve studies were included [27–33, 40, 41, 44, 47, 50], encompassing a total of 1,566 participants. The heterogeneity among these studies was high (I2 = 91.1%), and a random-effects model was applied for analysis. Educational stage

Regarding educational stages, DVA [SMD = 0.86, P = 0.001] and binocular SVA [SMD = 0.90, P < 0.05] exhibited significant improvements in students from grades 1–3. Binocular SVA also showed improvement in students from grades 4–6 [SMD = 0.49, P < 0.05]. Implementation environment

Regarding the implementation environment, DVA demonstrated significant improvement in both combined indoor-outdoor settings [SMD = 1.13, P = 0.011] and outdoor settings [SMD = 0.63, P = 0.011]. SVA in both eyes [SMD = 0.88, P < 0.05] improved significantly in indoor environments, whereas DVA showed no statistically significant effect (P > 0.05).

Intervention method

Regarding intervention methods, SVA significantly improved following mind–body health exercises [SMD = 0.52, P < 0.001] and ball sports [SMD = 1.23, P < 0.001], whereas no significant effects were observed for DVA. Visual training, however, produced a significant enhancement in DVA [SMD = 0.64, P = 0.005]. Outdoor daily activities yielded a statistically significant improvement in SVA [SMD = 0.22, P = 0.022], but did not result in a significant change in DVA (P > 0.05).

Intervention duration

Regarding the duration of intervention, moderate-dose interventions produced significant improvements in DVA [SMD = 1.06, P = 0.01], SVA in the left eye [SMD = 1.13, P = 0.001] and SVA in the right eye [SMD = 1.27, P = 0.002].

Intervention frequency

Regarding intervention frequency, performing 4–6 sessions per week significantly improved DVA (SMD = 0.87, P = 0.005). In contrast, SVA in both the left (SMD = 0.72, P = 0.001) and right eyes (SMD = 0.77, P = 0.002) showed significant improvement with only 1–3 sessions per week.

Discussion

The effects of exercise on functional improvement and associated modifying factors in children and adolescents

DVA

Research suggests that exercise significantly enhances dynamic visual performance in children and adolescents. Previous studies have demonstrated that goal-directed perceptual–motor training can improve visuomotor integration, dynamic visual processing, and hand–eye coordination in children [58], indicating a beneficial effect of exercise on dynamic visual performance and visual function. Biologically, dynamic visual performance relies on the brain’s capacity to rapidly process continuously changing visual information. Exercise may enhance neural transmission efficiency within visual pathways and improve cortical encoding of motion direction and speed. During exercise, vestibular and proprioceptive inputs are closely integrated with visual signals, enabling the brain to compensate for head movements through information derived from body posture and muscle activity [59]. The benefits of visual training interventions may likewise be mediated by enhanced neurovisual plasticity. Exposure to dynamic visual stimuli can improve the visual cortex’s ability to detect and track moving targets, enhance eye-movement coordination, and increase visual information-processing efficiency, thereby indirectly supporting accommodative function and refractive status. In addition, DVA appears particularly sensitive to visual training, accommodative function, and visual fatigue. Visual tracking tasks performed at varying distances during exercise may relax and strengthen the ciliary muscles responsible for lens focusing, reducing accommodative spasm associated with prolonged near work and improving accommodative sensitivity and accuracy [60].

However, the pooled effect size for DVA demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 91.1%). Based on the subgroup analyses and multivariable meta-regression, heterogeneity in DVA outcomes may primarily arise from three factors. First, differences in the intervention environment appear to play an important role. Subgroup analysis showed that interventions conducted in combined indoor–outdoor settings produced the greatest improvements in DVA, while multivariable meta-regression confirmed that the intervention environment remained a significant predictor after adjustment for CRVE. These findings suggest that DVA is influenced not only by exercise itself but also by environmental characteristics, including the complexity of visual stimuli, target-tracking demands, and lighting conditions. Hybrid indoor–outdoor environments provide dynamic target tracking, depth-of-field variation, and natural light exposure, which may facilitate activation of the visual accommodation system and enhance dynamic visual function [12, 61]. Second, exercise modalities may differ in their training-specific effects. Visual training–based exercises showed larger effect sizes in the subgroup analysis, possibly because they place greater emphasis on target tracking, rapid recognition, and eye-hand coordination, thereby directly improving dynamic visual processing. However, after further adjustment for the training environment, the independent effects of different exercise modalities were attenuated, suggesting that observed differences may be partly attributable to environmental characteristics and training dose. Third, methodological variations among studies may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Differences in testing equipment, assessment criteria, and study design, as well as inconsistencies in target speed, testing distance, and visual stimulation protocols, may have increased variability in effect estimates across studies.

The multivariate regression analysis indicated that the intervention environment may be a significant independent factor associated with improvements in DVA, whereas differences among exercise types were attenuated after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that improvements in DVA are unlikely to be attributable to a single exercise modality alone and are more likely driven by the combined effects of multiple factors, including the intervention environment, visual stimulus characteristics, target-tracking demands, and accommodative load. Furthermore, this study employed the correlated and hierarchical effects with robust variance estimation (CRVE) approach to account for within-study dependence and potential unit-of-analysis bias, thereby enhancing the robustness of effect size estimates and strengthening the reliability and interpretation of the findings.

SVA

The results indicate that exercise significantly improves SVA in children and adolescents. This finding is consistent with numerous previous studies [11, 62], suggesting that exercise exerts a positive protective effect on visual function. From a biological perspective, regular exercise enhances systemic blood circulation, increases the diameter and blood flow velocity of the central retinal artery, and facilitates the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to retinal neurons, thereby improving the efficiency of visual signal acquisition and processing. In addition, exercise places the eyes in a state opposite to prolonged near work, promoting alternating contraction and relaxation of the ciliary muscles. This process helps relieve accommodative spasm, restore accommodative responsiveness, flatten the lens, and improve the ability to focus distant objects clearly on the retina [63].

In this study, static visual acuity outcomes showed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 91.6%). Subgroup analyses suggested that exercise type, intervention frequency, and intervention setting may influence improvements in SVA. However, subsequent multivariable meta-regression with CRVE robust variance correction identified no moderator with a stable independent effect. These findings indicate that improvements in SVA are unlikely to be attributable to a single exercise modality and may instead reflect the combined effects of exercise interventions on visual function. Ball sports and mind–body exercises may enhance static visual performance through multiple mechanisms, including reducing visual fatigue, improving ciliary muscle accommodation, increasing exposure to distant visual stimuli, and decreasing prolonged near-work demands. Moreover, SVA, as a functional visual outcome, is influenced by several factors, such as testing conditions, assessment timing, fatigue level, participant compliance, and short-term visual adaptation. Variations in visual acuity assessment methods, intervention protocols, and follow-up durations across studies may have further contributed to the observed heterogeneity.

The effects of exercise on structural myopia control and associated modifying factors in children and adolescents

RE

Research findings indicate that exercise can significantly improve refractive status in children and adolescents. This finding is consistent with previous studies [64, 65], suggesting that exercise plays an important role in mitigating RE in this population. Biologically, these benefits may be primarily attributed to improved ciliary muscle accommodation. Prolonged near work can cause sustained ciliary muscle tension, leading to accommodative spasm and pseudomyopia, which maintains the lens in a highly refractive state. Regular exercise may alleviate visual fatigue, reduce the burden of prolonged near work, and promote periodic relaxation of the ciliary muscles, thereby enhancing accommodative function and potentially reducing the severity of RE [66]. In addition, exercise may influence refractive status by improving ocular blood circulation and choroidal perfusion. Previous studies have shown that regular exercise enhances systemic circulation, increases oxygen delivery and metabolic activity in ocular tissues, and promotes nutrient supply to the retina and choroid [67]. Notably, substantial heterogeneity was observed in the present analysis, indicating considerable variation in the effects of exercise on refractive status across studies. Refractive status is influenced not only by AL but also by the accommodative state of the ciliary muscles. Consequently, changes in refractive status reflect both structural and functional components, making this outcome less stable than AL measurements. Furthermore, differences in measurement timing, visual fatigue, and near-work activities among studies may have contributed to the observed variability. These factors may partly explain why heterogeneity for refractive status was substantially greater than that for AL.

AL

Exercise was associated with a significant reduction in AL growth among children and adolescents. This finding is consistent with previous studies [68, 69], suggesting that exercise may help mitigate myopia progression by inhibiting axial elongation, a key indicator of the structural development of myopia. Biologically, increased outdoor activity may stimulate retinal dopamine release through greater exposure to natural light, thereby suppressing excessive axial elongation [70]. In addition, regular exercise may support healthy ocular development by improving choroidal blood flow, reducing prolonged near-work exposure, and alleviating accommodative stress. AL is widely regarded as the most important biological marker of myopia progression [71, 72]. Compared with functional visual outcomes, axial length is less susceptible to short-term visual fluctuations and subjective measurement bias, resulting in greater consistency across studies. These findings suggest that the protective effect of exercise on the structural progression of myopia may be relatively robust. Therefore, the present results have important clinical implications for myopia prevention and control.

Limitations

(1) Meta-analyses are limited by the quality of the original studies. Furthermore, the instruments and methods used to assess visual acuity were not fully standardized and lacked internationally recognized benchmarks. Future research should integrate a broader range of literature and standardize both participant populations and visual acuity assessment criteria; (2) Despite comprehensive searches, an overrepresentation of positive studies was observed. Potential publication bias is suggested by inconsistent results from funnel plots and Egger’s test, as well as the risk of overestimating true effects due to the omission of negative or ineffective studies. Although the effect remained statistically significant after correction, its magnitude may be smaller than initially estimated, highlighting the need for further evaluation through high-quality studies; (3) Given the limited number of studies reporting RE and AL outcomes, multilevel subgroup analyses and advanced adjustment models could not be performed. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future high-quality, large-scale studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to further validate the effects of exercise on the progression of structural myopia; (4) All included studies were conducted among children and adolescents in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should include participants from diverse geographic and ethnic backgrounds to enhance external validity; (5) The RoB2 tool was employed to assess the quality of all included studies; however, its reliance on subjective judgment may introduce bias into the evaluation results.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis evaluated the effects of exercise interventions on myopia-related visual outcomes in children and adolescents and explored potential moderating factors. The findings indicate that exercise interventions exert a generally consistent positive effect on functional visual performance, with moderate effect sizes observed, particularly for improvements in DVA and SVA. In addition, exercise may help slow the structural progression of myopia, as evidenced by the high consistency of outcomes related to AL. This study further found that improvements in DVA are more sensitive to factors such as the intervention setting, characteristics of dynamic visual stimuli, and accommodative demands, whereas improvements in SVA demonstrate a more stable overall beneficial effect. Exercise interventions may facilitate visual function development in children and adolescents through mechanisms including multidimensional visual stimulation, enhanced dynamic visual tracking, and improved accommodative function. However, further high-quality studies with long-term follow-up are required to determine the sustained effects of exercise on structural myopia progression and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
