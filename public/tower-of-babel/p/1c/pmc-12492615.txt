Serum metabolic disparity between patients with lymph node tuberculosis and patients with sarcoidosis: towards differential diagnosis
Authors: Alok Nath, Sachin Yadav, Kritika Singh, Vikas Agarwal, Ajmal Khan, Zia Hashim, Mansi Gupta, Dinesh Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and hypothesisSarcoidosis (SAR) and lymph-node tuberculosis (LNTB) are granulomatous diseases that present diagnostic challenges, especially in TB-endemic regions. We hypothesized that serum-metabolic profiles would help in differentiating SARs from LNTBs.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify serum metabolic biomarkers to distinguish SAR from LNTB using NMR-based metabolomics analysis.MethodsSerum samples were collected from 26 SAR and 22 LNTB patients. The serum metabolic profiles were measured using 800 MHz NMR spectroscopy and quantified using the commercial software CHENOMX. The serum metabolic profiles were compared using multivariate partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and potential discriminatory metabolites were identified using variable importance in projection (VIP) scores and subsequently evaluated for statistical significance using a volcano plot. The diagnostic potential of the discriminatory metabolites was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsPLS-DA demonstrated significant metabolic disparity between the SAR and LNTB groups. The key metabolic features identified included elevated levels of glutamate, pyroglutamate, acetate, and leucine and a decreased glutamate-to-glutamine ratio (EQR) and decreased levels of glutamine, pyruvate, and myo-inositol in TB patients. These metabolic changes suggest that TB-infection involves activated glutaminolysis and elevated host lipid metabolism. ROC curve analysis revealed several metabolites with high diagnostic potential (AUC > 0.8), including glutamate, pyroglutamate, and glutamine (AUC > 0.98).ConclusionIn conclusion, this study underscores the potential of serum metabolic profiling as a noninvasive tool for distinguishing SARs from LNTBs. However, further studies are imperative to validate these findings on independent patient cohorts and to facilitate their integration into routine clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03756-0.

Introduction

Sarcoidosis (SAR) is an autoimmune inflammatory disease characterized by multisystem involvement; however, its etiology is still elusive. It can involve almost any organ system of the human body, but lung, skin and eye involvement are relatively common [1]. A combination of compatible clinical presentation, demonstration of noncaseating granulomas in tissue or body fluids and exclusion of known etiologies of granulomatous inflammation is needed for establishing a diagnosis of sarcoidosis [2, 3]. The clinical presentation of the disease ranges from no symptoms to severe respiratory insufficiency, cardiac death, neurological disease, and blindness [4–6]. A challenging aspect in the management of sarcoidosis is to differentiate it from tuberculosis (TB), especially in countries with a high prevalence of tuberculosis infection [7, 8]. Like SAR, TB is also a granulomatous disease, and the most common sites of granulomatous inflammation in both SAR and TB are the lymph nodes [9]. Therefore, these diseases not only share overlapping clinical, radiological, and histopathological features [4] but can also present with nonspecific symptoms such as fever, weight loss, fatigue, and night sweats, making clinical differentiation difficult. Lymphadenopathy with or without lung infiltrates is also common in both SARs and TBs, and furthermore, there are no definitive radiological imaging features that can conclusively differentiate SARs from LNTBs [10, 11].

TB is a common infectious disease worldwide caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, but the cause of sarcoidosis is unknown; however, TB is classified as an autoimmune disorder owing to the abnormal accumulation of inflammatory cells resulting in the formation of granulomas in the affected organ system [12]. Often, tuberculosis granulomas exhibit a foamy phenotype owing to lipid-laden foamy macrophages and subsequently form caseum (a cheese-like structure) due to macrophage necrosis [13]. On the other hand, sarcoidal granulomas are sterile without caseum and form the basis for the suggestive diagnosis of SAR in tertiary-care settings [14]. These distinctive features of sarcoidal granulomas (i.e., noncaseous granulomas and negative pathogenic demonstrations) are confirmed through histopathological and microbial analysis of biopsy samples obtained from granulomatous tissue [15, 16], which is an invasive and very painful procedure that is difficult to perform in general primary hospitals and secondary healthcare centers. Therefore, there is immense clinical interest in investigating noninvasive biomarkers for differentiating SAR from lymph node TB (LNTB). The identification of individuals with active TB infection (ATBI) is also a priority set under the National TB Elimination Program (NTEP) both (a) to control TB or its progression through starting appropriate clinical/public health interventions and (b) to reduce further spread in the community [17–19].

Metabolomics is an emerging exploratory tool in rheumatic and infectious diseases [20, 21]. Studies have identified differing metabolic profiles in the serum of patients, differentiating latent TB from active TB [22] and differentiating active TB from malignancy and community-acquired pneumonia [23]. Metabolomics is an analytical approach to metabolism and aims to identify disease-specific metabolic patterns through quantitative and comparative analysis of quantitative profiles of metabolites in biofluids [24]. NMR spectroscopy is a one of the two powerful analytical platforms for the quantitative profiling of metabolites in biological and clinical samples such as blood plasma/serum or urine [25, 26]. NMR offers several advantages, especially, high reproducibility and quantitative accuracy without the need for extensive sample preparation, which is critical for analyzing complex biofluids like serum. Alternative powerful technique with high sensitivity for detecting specific metabolites at low concentrations is liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS). As LC–MS typically requires predefined metabolite panels and it is less suited for untargeted metabolomics; thus preferred for targeted metabolomics analysis. NMR is deemed more appropriate for untargeted metabolomics as explored in this study. However, we acknowledge the complementary potential of LC–MS for targeted validation studies in future. A recent study used a metabolomic approach to compare patients with sarcoidosis with healthy controls [27], and there are only few studies available to distinguish patients with TB from SAR patients. Another study by our group compared the serum metabolic profiles of SAR patients and treatment-naïve patients with active TB and revealed metabolic disturbances that align closely with various previous reports [28]. Moreover, several metabolic features demonstrated very high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing between SAR and TB patients, suggesting that serum metabolomics may serve as a promising and noninvasive tool for improving differential diagnosis and clinical disease management in SAR/TB patients [28].

Methods

Ethical considerations

This was a prospective experimental study consisting of two groups, namely, patients with lymph node tuberculosis (LNTB) and patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis (SAR). The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration after obtaining approval from the Institutional human Ethics Committee (IHEC), SGPGIMS, Lucknow (IEC Code: 2018–145-IMP-106; File Number: PGI/BE/549/2018; Dated: November 28, 2018). Informed consent was obtained from all the enrolled subjects/patients before obtaining the blood samples.

Sample collection for NMR analysis

Serum samples were obtained from 48 patients, including 26 SAR patients (diagnosed on the basis of ATS criteria) and 22 patients with microbiologically confirmed LNTB. Briefly, patients with clinical features suggestive of active tuberculosis (TB) were clinically evaluated and subjected to microbiological confirmation (Sputum AFB, GeneXpert). All patients with microbiologically confirmed active TB meeting the inclusion criteria (i.e. treatment-naive patients with microbiologically confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis aged between 20 and 60 years) were recruited for this study, and baseline clinical, radiological and laboratory features were recorded. Patients aged less than 20 years, patients with comorbid conditions such as diabetes mellitus, renal failure, chronic liver disease or any other chronic disease and patients who were already receiving treatment with antitubercular drugs, steroids, or h/o steroid intake in the last three months or who were taking disease-modifying drugs or immunosuppressants were excluded. Simultaneously, all other patients with a diagnosis of pulmonary granulomatous inflammation were screened for sarcoidosis (SAR) following standard clinical immunological investigations. Clinically, the patients who exhibit significant improvement following anti-tuberculosis treatment (ATT) are considered to have TB. Conversely, those who show marked improvement after steroid therapy are considered to have SAR. Accordingly, fasting venous (3 h) blood samples were obtained from the patients and the clinical, demographic and investigational profiles were recorded in the predefined study proforma. The serum samples were extracted from the blood samples as described previously [29] and immediately stored at −80 °C in a deep freezer for further NMR investigations.

NMR-based serum metabolomics analysis

The stored serum samples of SAR (N = 26) and TB (N = 22) patients were thawed and serum metabolic profiles were measured using 800 MHz NMR spectroscopy as described previously [28, 30–32]. The recorded 1D 1H CPMG (Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill) NMR spectra were analyzed for metabolite specific concentration profiling using the profiler module of the NMR suite of commercial software CHENOMX (Chenomx Inc., Edmonton, Canada) as per the technical details provided in these studies [33–36]). The 1D 1H CPMG NMR spectra of serum samples were assigned based on previously reported serum metabolite assignments from our group [28, 31, 35] and further validated by matching chemical shifts and coupling patterns using the inbuilt 800 MHz database library of CHENOMX NMR suite. The stacked 1D 1H CPMG NMR spectra for SAR and LNTB samples with their peaks annotated for serum metabolite assignments presented in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information. The concentrations of the following twenty-five metabolites were measured: glucose, creatine, creatinine, betaine, glutamate, glutamine, glycine, histidine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, lactate, acetate, citrate, 3-hydroxybutyrate (3HB), myo-inositol, phenylalanine (Phe), proline, pyroglutamate (PyGlu), pyruvate, succinate, tyrosine, trimethylamine n-oxide (TMAO), and threonine. Additionally, we included two ratiometric parameters involving biosynthetically linked metabolites: the glutamate-to-glutamine ratio (EQR; elevated levels in the serum indicate activated glutaminolysis [36]) and the phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio (PTR; elevated serum levels are because of oxidative stress [37]). These serum metabolic profiles were saved in the CSV file format and added with the class information. The metabolic variability in study cohort and metabolic disparity between the study groups was elucidated employing unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) and supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), respectively [26, 38] carried out using MetaboAnalyst (v5.0, a web-based free metabolomics data analysis tool [39, 40]. Two- and three-dimensional (2D and 3D) score plots were used to demonstrate the metabolic disparity between the different study groups; whereas the metabolic features significantly contributing to group differentiation were identified using variable importance in projection (VIP) scores [26, 41]. The performance of the PLS-DA model was evaluated using accuracy (how well the model classifies the samples), R2 (a measure of goodness-of-fit i.e. validity of models against overfitting), and Q2 (a measure of predictive ability) values [26, 38].

Statistical analysis

The key discriminatory features were evaluated for statistical significance using volcano plot which shows the relationship between fold change (FC) and significance based on student t-tests (statistical significance is considered when the p-value is less than 0.05). Univariate statistics helps to confirm whether the serum metabolic changes observed between the study groups are genuine or are merely random fluctuations [26, 38]. Univariate statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v7.0, www.graphpad.com) and SPSS software (version 20.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). The Mann‒Whitney U test was used to compare clinical parameters between the study groups. The comparative results are represented as mean ± SD (standard deviation), unless otherwise specified. For all comparisons, a p value < 0.05 was considered to mark statistical significance. The concentrations of circulatory metabolites are reported in micromolar (µM) units, except for glucose and lactate, for which the values are reported in millimolar (mM) units.

Results

Clinical and demographic features of patients

Twenty-six patients with sarcoidosis (diagnosed on the basis of the ATS criteria) and twenty-two patients with microbiologically confirmed lymph node tuberculosis were recruited for the study. Sarcoidosis patients were significantly older, with a median age of 47.50 years. (IQR: 2.00, 56.00), compared to TB patients (Table 1). The median duration of illness was lower among patients with TB, i.e., 4.50 months (IQR: 3.00, 14.00) vs. 12.00 months (IQR: 3.00, 18.00); however, the difference was not statistically significant (Table 1). A total of 54% and 57% of the subjects in the TB and sarcoidosis groups, respectively, were females. The majority of patients were from urban backgrounds, constituting 95% of patients in the sarcoidosis group and approximately 85% in the TB group. Fever (61.5% vs. A total of 59.1% and cough (65.4% vs. 54.5%) were the most common presenting symptoms in TB and sarcoidosis patients, respectively, and there was no significant difference between the two groups. As expected, appetite loss and weight loss were significantly greater in the TB group (57.7% vs. 27.3%; 57.7% vs. 22.7%, respectively) than in the SAR group (p = 0.025; 0.020). Articular involvement was observed in 27.3% of patients with sarcoidosis and 15.4% of patients with TB. Uveitis was detected in 9.1% of patients with sarcoidosis; however, none of the patients with TB had ocular involvement. Only one patient in our cohort among patients with sarcoidosis had a history of intake of anti-tubercular treatment before the diagnosis of sarcoidosis. There was no significant difference in laboratory parameters between the two groups (Table 1). Serum metabolic disparity between SAR and LNTB

The serum metabolic disparity between patients with Sarcoidosis (SAR) and Lymph Node Tuberculosis (LNTB) was assessed using multivariate analysis. The variability of serum metabolic profiles between SAR and LNTB patients was first assessed using unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and the results are summarized in Figure S2 of the supporting information (SI). The 2D score plot from PCA is shown in panel A of Figure S2 and demonstrates distinct clustering of the samples of two study groups along principal components 1 (PC1) and 2 (PC2). Further, the two clusters of samples demonstrate a clear separation between SAR (blue squares) and LNTB (red circles) samples suggesting that the serum metabolic profiles of SAR and LNTB patients are significantly different. The panel B of Figure S2 shows the scree plot and represents variance explained by each principal component in the PCA model. The first principal component (PC1) accounts for 53.8% of the variance, followed by PC2 at 23.7%, with subsequent components (PC3 to PC5) contributing smaller amounts (5.9%, 4.8%, and 3.9%, respectively). The cumulative variance (green line) reaches 92.1% by PC5, indicating that the first five components capture most of the metabolic variability in the dataset. This high cumulative variance suggests that the PCA model effectively summarizes the metabolic differences between SAR and LNTB, supporting the robustness of the observed separation in Panel A. The robustness of these metabolic differences was further confirmed employing PLS-DA which is a supervised method that specifically optimizes for group separation. The results derived from supervised PLS-DA modeling are shown in Fig. 1. The score plots clearly revealed a statistically significant separation between the SAR and LNTB groups (Fig. 1A and B), with R2 and Q2 values close to 0.8 (Fig. 1C), suggesting that the discriminatory model is adequately valid and can make better predictions than chance. Although the separation between the SAR and TB groups is primarily observed along Component 1 in the PLS-DA model, Component 2 explains a larger portion of the total variance (50.7%) compared to Component 1 (25.9%). This indicates that Component 2 may capture variance arising from metabolic features unrelated to the disease classification such as alanine and glycine as evident from the comparative evaluation of PLS-DA loading and biplot results shown in Figure S3 of SI. The loading plot identifies metabolites contributing most to class separation and accordingly, glycine and alanine seems to be contributing most to class separation as evident from the PLS-DA loading plot shown in Figure S3A. On the other hand, the biplot identifies key metabolites driving the separation between study groups. Clear separation between SAR and LNTB groups is observed along the principal components (Figure S3A). TB samples cluster on the right, associated with higher levels of glutamate, acetate, and PyGlu. SAR samples cluster on the left, linked to metabolites like glutamine, citrate, myo-inositol, and pyruvate. Whereas, PLS-DA biplot shows that both alanine and glycine are oriented away from the study groups reinforcing their poor discriminative relevance for differentiation between SAR and LNTB. PCA biplot shown in FigureS3C also suggests that specific serum metabolites contribute significantly to differentiating between SAR and LNTB, with glutamate, acetate, and PyGlu being key discriminators favoring TB, and glutamine and citrate indicative of SAR. The discriminatory performance of the PLS-DA model was further validated in terms of the three performance measures i.e. prediction accuracy, multiple correlation coefficient R2, and explained variance in prediction Q2, obtained after the 10-fold cross-validation analysis of multivariate data. The PLS-DA model performance data is shown in Fig. 1C and clearly evident that, the PLS-DA model performs well with two components and adding more components does not improve predictive performance significantly (Fig. 1C). The red asterisk indicates a slightly improved Q2 value when the third component is added suggesting three component PLS-DA model may generalize well as evident from 3D score plot as well (Fig. 1A). Serum metabolic features of discriminatory potential

The serum metabolic profiles of discriminatory potential in separating the study groups were identified using VIP score plot shown in Fig. 1D. Five serum metabolic features (i.e. glutamine, glutamate, acetate, leucine, and pyroglutamate) were considered here as most discriminatory features based on the VIP score values and were further evaluated for statistically significant differences with the help of a volcano plot, which allows rapid visualization and identification of the most significant metabolic changes (Fig. 2A). All five discriminatory metabolic features (glutamine, glutamate, acetate, leucine and pyroglutamate) and three other metabolic features (i.e. EQR, myo-inositol and pyruvate) exhibited pronounced and statistically significant changes, as also evident from the violin plots (showing the data distribution as density curves, Fig. 2B). Serum metabolic features of diagnostic potential

The serum metabolic features capable of discriminating between the SAR and TB groups were further subjected to ROC curve analysis to evaluate their diagnostic potential. Considering an AUC > 0.8 and a p value < 0.001 as the criteria for diagnostic potential, all eight key metabolite entities demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for discriminating SARs from TBs (Fig. 3). Three metabolite entities (glutamate-to-glutamine ratio, pyroglutamate, and glutamine) had AUROCs greater than 0.98, suggesting their very high diagnostic potential in differentiating SAR from TB; therefore, these metabolites can be considered candidate biomarkers. Though, these serum metabolic features demonstrated high discriminatory potential in our cohort (AUROC > 0.9, Fig. 3), the study’s relatively small sample size (n = 26 for SAR, n = 22 for LNTB) and lack of an independent validation cohort limit the ability to assess the robustness and disease specificity of these markers. Further, without external validation, it remains uncertain whether these metabolites are uniquely specific to the SAR vs. LNTB comparison or reflect broader inflammatory or metabolic changes associated with granulomatous diseases. Discussion

Both the TB and SAR lymph nodes involve granulomatous inflammation, mostly in the lungs and intrathoracic lymph nodes [44, 45]. Furthermore, both have common clinical presentations, including fever, cough, dyspnea, and fatigue, and constitutional symptoms, such as loss of appetite, fatigue and weight loss, are the most common presenting symptoms [46]. There are no specific serologic tests available to diagnose and differentiate SAR from TB; therefore, the suggestive diagnosis of pulmonary SAR always remains a challenge because of its clinical, radiological and pathological similarities with pulmonary TB [8]. Often, significant weight loss and prominent constitutional symptoms favor the diagnosis of TB, which was also observed in our cohort; however, these features have very poor diagnostic accuracy [47, 48], and imaging and histological findings often fail to make a confident diagnosis of sarcoidosis because patients are incorrectly classified as having TB or sarcoidosis based on these tests [49]. Traditionally, a compatible clinico-radiological presentation, demonstration of caseating granulomas and necrosis on histological examination and a positive tuberculin test have been used to diagnose tuberculosis with reasonable accuracy, but a confident diagnosis of sarcoidosis vs. tuberculosis is still formidable in a TB endemic country where the predominant clinical presentation is extrapulmonary and the microbiological yield is poor [7, 50]. In this subgroup, caseous necrosis is often not observed, acid-fast staining and nucleic acid amplification tests (Gene Xpert) are negative for biopsy/cytological specimens, and culture can take a long time; therefore, a confirmed diagnosis of tuberculosis is often difficult to ascertain [51, 52].

The search for an ideal biomarker to distinguish TB from sarcoidosis is ongoing, and few biomarkers, such as soluble interleukin-2 receptor (IL-2R), ACE, and Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6), have been evaluated; however, unfortunately, these biomarkers are not sensitive or specific enough to separate these diagnoses [53–55]. Recently, a combination of leptin and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 has been reported to have a sensitivity of 86.5% and a specificity of 73.1% as a marker of sarcoidosis [56]. Furthermore, molecular techniques such as multitargeted polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for MTB, simultaneous amplification and testing methods for Mycobacterium tuberculosis rRNA (SAT-TB), CBNAAT and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) have been demonstrated to have a modest diagnostic performance in distinguishing TB and sarcoidosis even in TB endemic areas [57].

Therefore, there is immense clinical interest in investigating noninvasive and objective biomarkers to distinguish TB from sarcoidosis. Several studies involving clinical metabolomics of saliva and blood plasma/serum samples have shown that NMR-based metabolomics analysis can distinguish sarcoidosis patients from normal control subjects [58–60]. On the other hand, a plethora of metabolomics studies have been carried out in the past and established that host–pathogen interactions during active tuberculosis (TB) infection have a significant impact on metabolic alterations (see reference [28] and references within). Therefore, it is legitimate to explore the metabolomics approach using NMR spectroscopy and perform metabolomics analysis of blood plasma or serum samples to investigate disease-specific patterns of metabolic variations and their diagnostic potential to differentiate sarcoidosis and LNTB. In our study, the sera of TB patients were characterized by (a) elevated circulatory levels of glutamate, pyroglutamate, acetate, leucine and EQR and (b) decreased levels of glutamine, pyruvate, and myo-inositol compared to those of TB patients. The decreased glutamine and myo-inositol levels and concomitant increase in glutamate, pyroglutamate, and acetate levels in TB patients suggested that TB infection involves activated glutaminolysis and elevated host lipid metabolism [28]. The metabolic shifts in LNTB patients were also found to be consistent with the metabolic changes reported recently in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from TB meningitis (TBM) patients [61]. An NMR-based CSF metabolomics study [61] compared the CSF metabolic profiles of 36 TBM patients and 18 healthy control (HC) subjects and revealed 11 discriminatory metabolites that could distinguish TBM patients from HCs, among which 7 (alanine, arginine, formate, lactate, glutamate, 2-hydroxyisobutyrate, and cis-aconitate) were upregulated and 4 (glutamine, myo-inositol, glucose, and fructose) were downregulated in TBM patients compared to HCs. The elevated levels of glutamate and decreased levels of glutamine and myo-inositol are consistent with the findings of this study [61].

Consistent with previous reports [43, 62], the glutamate-to-glutamine ratio (EQR) demonstrated the highest diagnostic potential, with an AUROC equal to one. Like EQR, the other significant predictor identified in our study was pyroglutamate, which also had an AUROC equal to one. Due to activated glutaminolysis, the circulatory abundance of glutamate in LNTB patients might increase, and this elevated level of glutamate is maintained through its conversion to pyroglutamate (also known as 5-oxoproline). As such, pyroglutamate serves as an intermediate in glutathione metabolism (Fig. 4), may function in glutamate storage, and opposes the action of glutamate, including in the brain, as glutamate functions as an excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, and its excess is considered to be harmful. Although the previous CSF based metabolomics study also reported the NMR signals of pyroglutamate in the CSF samples of definite TBM patients at a chemical shift near 4.2 ppm (see Fig. 1 of this reference [61]), this metabolite failed to demonstrate discriminatory potential because of its normalization with respect to the total spectral intensity.

The elevated serum levels of leucine and decreased serum levels of glutamine were also found consistent with a previous targeted LC–MS based serum metabolomics study on pediatric patients with active and latent tuberculosis [63]. Particularly, the elevated serum levels of leucine are in line with previous reports identifying elevated serum levels of leucine-rich alpha-2-glycoprotein (LRG) as a new marker of TB [64].

Nevertheless, the decreased circulatory levels of myo-inositol were found to be pathophysiologically very relevant and may be associated with their augmented utilization in the cell membrane metabolism of MTB (Fig. 4). The MTB pathogen possesses a unique cell envelope composed of distinctive lipids and glycans. Among these, phosphatidyl-myo-inositol mannosides (PIMs) play a crucial role. These glycolipids are not only structural components of the mycobacterial cell envelope but also precursors for other lipoglycans, such as lipomannan (LM) and lipoarabinomannan (LAM).

Another recent study demonstrated the significant potential of metabolomic analysis of biological fluid samples in differentiating active TB infection from latent TB infection (LTBI). It reported higher serum levels of glutamate, aspartate and sulfoxy-methionine, alongside lower levels of glutamine, asparagine and methionine in active TB patients compared to LTBI subjects [43].

While the identified serum metabolites (e.g., EQR, pyroglutamate, glutamine, acetate, glutamate, leucine) demonstrated high discriminatory potential in our cohort (AUROC > 0.9, Fig. 3), the study’s relatively small sample size (n = 26 for SAR, n = 22 for LNTB) and the absence of an independent validation cohort limit the ability to fully assess the robustness and disease specificity of these markers. Demographic differences, such as the older median age in the SAR group (47.50 years vs. 36.50 years for LNTB, Table 1), and potential confounding factors, such as comorbidities or disease stage, may introduce variability that affects the generalizability of these findings. Without external validation, it remains uncertain whether these metabolites are specific to the SAR vs. LNTB comparison or reflect broader metabolic changes associated with granulomatous inflammation. Further, significant differences in clinical variables such as loss of weight (LOW: 22.7% in SAR vs. 57.7% in LNTB, p = 0.020) and loss of appetite (LOA: 27.3% in SAR vs. 57.7% in LNTB, p = 0.045) were observed between SAR and LNTB groups (Table 1). These factors are known to contribute to systemic metabolic changes, such as altered energy metabolism and amino acid catabolism, which could confound the observed metabolic profiles (e.g., elevated glutamate, pyroglutamate, and acetate in LNTB; higher glutamine and myo-inositol in SAR, Table 2). Due to the binary nature of LOW and LOA and the small sample size (n = 26 for SAR, n = 22 for LNTB), statistical adjustment using ANCOVA was not feasible. Consequently, these variables represent potential confounders that may impact the interpretation of the metabolic differences, particularly in LNTB patients where LOW and LOA are more prevalent and likely exacerbate catabolic processes like glutaminolysis (Fig. 4). To address these limitations, future studies should validate these findings in larger, independent cohorts, incorporating healthy controls and other granulomatous diseases, and employ advanced statistical approaches, such as (a) ANCOVA or propensity score matching, to adjust for these confounders and confirm the specificity of the identified metabolic markers (e.g., EQR, glutamate, glutamine) for differentiating SAR from LNTB and (b) machine learning or pathway enrichment analysis, to confirm the diagnostic potential and specificity of these metabolic markers. Conclusion

The present clinical metabolomics study by NMR highlights the utility of serum metabolic profiling in distinguishing SARs from LNTBs. Five serum features (EQR, pyroglutamate, glutamine, acetate and glutamate) demonstrated very high sensitivity and specificity for differentiating sarcoidosis from LNTB, with AUROC values greater than 0.9. After validation in independent patient cohorts, these findings have important clinical implications in areas with a high prevalence of tuberculosis, where a decision to start anti-tuberculosis therapy could potentially be made on the basis of serum metabolic features and a positive TST. Based on the consistency of the metabolomics results of this study with those from numerous previous metabolomics studies (as discussed above), it is now essential to advance the clinical translation of these serum metabolic biomarkers. In the future, the diagnostic panel of metabolic biomarkers may be translated into affordable (cost-effective) and portable (laboratory-free) point-of-care diagnostics for differentiating SAR from active TB to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes and streamline the management of these complex conditions, even in remote areas and resource-restricted developing countries such as India, where there is a very high prevalence of TB.
