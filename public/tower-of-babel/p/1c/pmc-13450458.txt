Transglutaminase-mediated glycosylation of whey protein isolate with glucosamine: effects on structure, functional properties, and intestinal barrier function
Authors: Yu Wang, Sharuna De, Hui Chu, Yujun Jiang, Jia Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effects of transglutaminase (TGase)-mediated glycosylation on the structure, functional properties, and intestinal barrier function of whey protein isolate (WPI). Structural analysis revealed that the covalent interaction between glucosamine and WPI led to a more stable and ordered protein structure. Compared with WPI, the glycosylated WPI exhibited improved foaming, emulsifying, and antioxidant properties. TGase-mediated glycosylation also reduced the degree of hydrolysis of WPI under in vitro digestion conditions. Furthermore, a cellular intestinal barrier model was established using Caco-2 cells, and treatment with digested glycosylated WPI significantly increased transepithelial electrical resistance while decreasing paracellular permeability. RT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that the digest of glycosylated WPI up-regulated the expression of tight junction proteins (Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1), thereby enhancing intestinal barrier function. This study provides new insights into the development of food proteins that combine desirable functional properties with biological activities.

Introduction

Whey protein isolate (WPI) is a natural ingredient in milk, typically recovered from waste streams in the dairy industry. WPI contains many active ingredients, is easy to digest, and has unique functional advantages (Li et al., 2024a). It has been extensively utilized in the food industry as a protein supplement to improve human health. However, native WPI has certain functional limitations, including poor heat stability, low emulsifying capacity under acidic or high-salt conditions, and inadequate foaming properties, which restrict its broader application in food processing (Li et al., 2024b). To satisfy food processing requirements, some methods such as physical, chemical, and enzymatic modifications were used to enhance the functional properties of WPI (Zhu, 2024). Glycosylation has been proven to be an effective alternative with unparalleled advantages in specific modification of protein active groups and improvement of protein functional properties, particularly foaming, emulsifying, and antioxidant activities (Hu, et al., 2023; Ke & Li, 2023; Li et al., 2024b). The Maillard reaction, as the most extensively studied glycosylation reaction, is a classic nonenzymatic browning reaction that induces ideal functional changes in proteins (Ke & Li, 2023). However, the Maillard reaction can produce carcinogenic byproducts and cause nutritional loss, while its strict requirement for high temperature conditions may pose certain challenges (Li et al., 2022). Therefore, alternative methods for preparing glycosylated proteins should be explored.

Transglutaminase (TGase; EC 2.3.2.13) is a natural enzyme that catalyzes protein glycosylation reactions involving the γ-amide group of glutamine residues in proteins and primary amine compounds (acyl acceptors) in amino-containing polysaccharides (Shi et al., 2021). Compared with the Maillard reaction under high-temperature conditions, TGase-mediated glycosylation offers a milder and safer alternative, effectively improving protein functional properties such as solubility, emulsifying stability, water-holding capacity, and thermal stability (Wang, Jiang, & Shi, 2025). Consequently, TGase-mediated glycosylation has gained considerable attention for the modification of various protein substrates. The mild reaction conditions and the safe nature of the glycosylation products make this approach an attractive strategy for protein modification. However, whether TGase-mediated glycosylation can simultaneously enhance the functional properties and biological activity of WPI remains unclear, particularly regarding its potential impact on intestinal health.

Maintaining intestinal barrier integrity is crucial for preventing pathogen invasion and maintaining systemic homeostasis (Barbara et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2024). This barrier function is primarily governed by epithelial cells, the overlying mucus layer, and intercellular tight junction (TJ) complexes (Chopyk & Grakoui, 2020). TJ proteins, including Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1, play critical roles in regulating paracellular permeability, and their expression levels directly influence barrier competence (Horowitz et al., 2023). Disruption of TJ integrity is associated with various intestinal disorders, highlighting the need to evaluate dietary proteins for their potential to modulate barrier function and promote gut health. Indeed, recent studies have demonstrated that dietary protein hydrolysates can positively modulate intestinal barrier function; for instance, whey protein hydrolysates enhance TJ integrity (Li et al., 2023), and glycosylated or cross-linked proteins, such as caseinate oligochitosan glycation products, exhibit improved barrier-protective effects in intestinal epithelial cells (Shi et al., 2021). However, the specific impact of TGase-mediated glycosylation of WPI with glucosamine on intestinal barrier function remains unexplored.

This study aimed to explore the mechanism of TGase-mediated glycosylation modification of WPI and its effects on both functional properties and biological activities. Glycosylated WPI was prepared by reacting WPI with glucosamine under TGase catalysis. The structure, morphology, and hydrophobicity of the glycosylated WPI were characterized, and the effects of glycosylation on the foaming, emulsifying, and antioxidant activities of WPI were evaluated. In addition, the in vitro degree of hydrolysis (DH) and intestinal barrier function of glycosylated WPI were investigated. Overall, this study provides a promising novel approach to enhancing the effective utilization of animal proteins in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

WPI was bought from Hilmar Co., Ltd. (CA, USA). Glucosamine was purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). TGase was provided by Jiangsu Yiming Fine Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Taizhou, China). Trypsin and porcine bile salt were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Pepsin was purchased from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China).

Preparation of glycosylation modification of WPI

The glycosylated WPI was prepared referring to a method of Shi et al. (2021). A WPI solution (80 g/L) was combined with a glucosamine solution (28.6 g/L) such that the molar ratio of glutamine residues in WPI to primary amino groups of glucosamine was 1:3. TGase (10 U/g protein) was added to the mixed solution containing whey protein and glucosamine at final concentrations of 50 g/L and 8.58 g/L, respectively, and the mixture was placed in a shaking water bath at 37 °C for 4 h to perform glycosylation. The enzyme was inactivated for 5 min at 85 °C and immediately cooled to room temperature. The mixture was performed in two isoelectric washings. The glycosylated WPI was obtained after adjusting the pH to 7.0. Cross-linked WPI was prepared as glycosylated WPI without the addition of glucosamine. WPI and cross-linked WPI were used as a control for subsequent experiments.

Determination of free primary amino group content

3 mL of OPA reagent was added to 200 μL of diluted sample. Immediately after 5 min of reaction, the absorbance for the sample was determined at 340 nm. The amount of free amino groups from the sample was calculated (Costa-Santos et al., 2024).

Structural characterization

SDS-PAGE

The experiment was performed using a 12% separating gel and a 5% stacking gel to verify protein glycosylation and analyze the molecular weight changes of WPI. Protein samples (2 mg/mL) were mixed with SDS loading buffer, boiled for 5 min, and then loaded for electrophoresis. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained for 30 min, destained, and imaged using a gel imaging system (Gel Doc EZ, Bio-Rad, Shanghai, China).

Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy

The diluted samples (0.6 mg/mL) were assessed for absorbance values using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadsu, Japan) at 190–400 nm.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The samples (0.1 mg/mL) were excited with deionized water at 280 nm using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7100, Hitachi, Kyoto, Japan). The emission fluorescence range was 300–500 nm and the excitation and emission slits were 5 nm.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The samples were mixed with KBr, ground to a fine powder and pressed into thin slices. An infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Nicolet, USA) was scanned and recorded 32 times in the spectral ranges of 4000–400 cm−1.

Circular dichroism (CD)

The secondary structure variations of samples were measured by CD spectra. Protein samples (0.15 g/L) were tested using a circular dichroism chromatograph (Chirascan, Applied Photophysics, UK). CD spectra were recorded with a speed of 100 nm/min.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The samples were viewed by SEM (S-3400 N, Hitachi, Japan) at different magnifications (500×, 1000× and 2000×) under the accelerating voltage of 20 kV.

Functional properties

Surface hydrophobicity analysis

4 mL of diluted sample was added to 20 μL of ANS solution (8 mmol/L), and the reaction was carried out for 15 min. The relative fluorescence intensity of protein samples was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7100, Hitachi, Japan) with an excitation wavelength of 390 nm, and an emission wavelength of 470 nm. The initial slope between the relative fluorescence intensity and the sample concentration was taken as the surface hydrophobicity index (H0).

Emulsifying properties

The emulsifying properties including emulsification activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI) of protein samples were assessed referring to the previous method (Yang et al., 2022). The protein samples (1 g/L) were mixed with soybean oil and then homogenized at 12,000 r/min for 1 min.

The emulsion following homogenization and the emulsion after standing for 10 min were each added to 5 mL of SDS solution, and the absorbance were measured at 500 nm. The emulsifying properties were calculated by the equations:(1)EAIm2/g=2×2.303×A0×Dilution factorC×1−Φ×104(2)ESI%=A10A0×100where, A0 and A10 represent the absorbance value measured at 0 min and 10 min. C represents the concentration of the sample before emulsification (g/mL). Φ represents the volume fraction of the oil phase 0.25.

Foaming capacity (FC) and foaming stability (FS)

The 1 g/L sample was stirred continuously at 10,000 r/min for 2 min. The volume of solution before stirring was recorded as VL, the volume of foam when stirring just stopped was recorded as V0, and the volume of foam after stirring stopped and standing for 30 min was recorded as V30. The FC and FS were calculated using the formula:(3)Fc%=V0VL×100(4)Fs%=V30V0×100

Antioxidation properties

ABTS free radical scavenging rate was determined using the previous method with slight modifications (Wang et al., 2024). 7 mmol/L ABTS solution was mixed with 2.45 mmol/L K2S2O8 solution in a 2:1 volume ratio. The mixed solution was stored in the dark for 12–16 h to obtain a stable ABTS+• solution, and diluted with anhydrous ethanol until the absorbance value stabilized at 0.7 ± 0.02 before the measurement. The sample was mixed with ABTS+• for 10 min, and the absorbance value was measured at 734 nm. According to the following formula:(5)ABTS⁺•free radical scavenging rate%=A0−ASA0×100where, As is the absorbance value measured when the sample reacted with ABTS+•, and A0 is the absorbance value measured when anhydrous ethanol reacts with ABTS+•.

6 mmol/L FeSO4 solution and 6 mmol/L H2O2 were mixed and reacted for 10 min. 1 mL of 6 mmol/L salicylic acid ethanol solution was added to the mixture and reacted at 37 °C for 30 min. The absorbance value of the total mixture was measured at 510 nm. The hydroxyl radical scavenging rate was calculated using the following formula:(6)Hydroxyl radical scavenging rate%=1−Ai−AjA0×100where, Ai, Aj, and A0 are the absorbance values of the samples, the control group, and the blank group, respectively.

In vitro digestive assay

An equal volume of simulated gastric fluid (SGF) containing 2000 U/mL of pepsin was added to samples (pH 3.0), and then the mixture was heated at 37 °C for 2 h. Afterward, the pH was adjusted to 7.0 to stop the gastric digestion, and an equal volume of simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) containing 100 U/mL of trypsin and 10 mmol of bile salt was added to the solution, the digestive solution was shaken at pH 7.0 for 2 h at 37 °C. After all the digestive reactions were completed, the enzymes were inactivated at 90 °C for 10 min, and the digest was collected to adjust the pH to 7.0 and lyophilized for spare use. The free primary amino group content of the digested proteins was measured by the OPA assay and the DH of all samples was calculated based on the free amino groups content.

Effect of glycosylated whey protein digest on the barrier function of Caco-2 cells

Cell culture

Caco-2 cells were obtained from the Typical Culture Preservation Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The medium was changed every 2 days. The cells reached 80–90% confluence.

Assays of cell viability

Caco-2 cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at a cell density of 2 × 105/mL, and after 24 h of cell attachment, the cells were treated with the addition of protein digest dilutions (12.5–200 μg/mL) and incubated for 24–48 h. MTT solution was added and incubated in an incubator for 4 h, and 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added. The absorbance value was measured at 490 nm using an enzyme meter (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The cell viability of each group was determined based on the untreated cells.

Trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) assay

Caco-2 cells (1 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded onto 12-well Transwell inserts. TEER was monitored using an ohmmeter (Millipore, Bedford, MA), and when resistance reached 500 Ω·cm2, protein digests were added to the apical chamber for 24–48 h. Cells cultured in medium served as the control (TEER set as 100%).

Assay of paracellular permeability

Caco-2 cells were seeded onto 0.4 μm Transwell inserts and cultured to confluence. TEER was monitored until 500 Ω·cm2 was reached, the cells were incubated with protein digests in the apical chamber for 24–48 h. Subsequently, FITC-dextran (0.5 mg/mL) was added for 6 h. Then, 100 μL of medium was collected from the basolateral chamber, and fluorescence was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 490/520 nm using a microplate reader. Results were expressed as a percentage of the control values.

RT-PCR assay

Total RNA from cells cultured in digest samples for 24 h was extracted using the TRIzol method. The RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary genes, which were amplified using specific primers using amplified DNA as a template:

Occludin:5′-ATTAACTTCGCCTGTGGATGACTT-3′(For).

5′-ATCTTGCTCTGTTCTCTTTGACCTTC-3′(Rev).

Claudin-1:5′-ATGGTGGTTGGCATCCTCCTG-3′(For).

5′-TCATCGTCTTCCAAGCACTTCATAC-3′(Rev).

ZO-1:5′-GCGGATGGTGCTACAAGTGATG-3′(For).

5′-GCCTTCTGTGTCTGTGTCTTCATAG-3′(Rev).

The cDNA was made into a reaction mixture using a reverse transcription kit and run on the PCR system. Changes in relative expression of TJ protein genes were analyzed by the 2-∆∆Ct assay.

Western-blot assay

Caco-2 cells were cultured in protein digest for 24 h and then lysed with RIPA buffer containing PMSF for 25 min to extract total protein. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE (5% stacking gel/12% separating gel), transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ultrasensitive chemiluminescence solution and imaged with the Amersham Imager 600 (General Electric Company, Boston, MA, USA). Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for gray value analysis. The expression levels of target proteins (Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1) were normalized to GAPDH as an internal reference.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were the mean of three independent repeated tests, and the experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS software at p < 0.05. Adobe Photoshop CS5, origin and Image J were used to process the experimental data and images.

Results and discussion

Glycoconjugate and free primary amino group content

The glucosamine content of glycosylated WPI was 7.67 ± 0.13 g/kg protein, indicating that glucosamine underwent glycosylation with WPI. We determined the changes in free primary amino group content of modified WPI using the OPA method (Fig. 1A). The free primary amino group content of WPI changed significantly after modification (p < 0.05). Briefly, compared with WPI, the free primary amino group content of cross-linked WPI decreased, reflecting that the ε-amino group of lysine in WPI was involved in inter- and intramolecular cross-linking of protein molecules under the catalytic action of TGase (Chen et al., 2025). The reduction in free primary amino group content of glycosylated WPI indicated that the competition between the primary amine group of glucosamine and the ε-amino group of lysine in glycosylated WPI reduced the self-crosslinking reaction in the protein system. Therefore, the decrease in free primary amino group content in glycosylated WPI was less than that in cross-linked WPI, which also suggested that a portion of the free amino groups in the protein was directly linked to the primary amine group of glucosamine to undergo glycosylation under TGase catalysis (Qi et al., 2024). This finding was consistent with the reports by Hu et al. (2023) regarding the decrease in free primary amino group groups in protein molecules after glycosylation of ovalbumin. Structural characterization of glycosylated WPI.

SDS-PAGE

The SDS-PAGE profiles of modified WPI are shown in Fig. 1B. WPI, cross-linked WPI, and glycosylated WPI exhibited the major characteristic bands of WPI (approximately 17 kDa, β-lactoglobulin) (Zhang et al., 2023). Compared with unmodified WPI (Lane 1), cross-linked WPI (Lane 2) and glycosylated WPI (Lane 3) showed large protein copolymers at the top of the gel (above 200 kDa), with reduced band intensity at the original protein positions, confirming TGase-catalyzed cross-linking. In contrast to WPI, glycosylated WPI formed continuous trailing macromolecular bands in the range of 70–200 kDa, and the band intensity at approximately 40 kDa increased (Gantumur et al., 2023). This band was enhanced only in glycosylated WPI and may be attributed to a glucosamine-bridged β-lactoglobulin dimer (protein–glucosamine–protein covalent conjugate), formed via TGase-catalyzed isopeptide bonds between glucosamine and glutamine residues of WPI. The observed molecular weight increase further confirms that TGase successfully catalyzed the conjugation of glucosamine to WPI, forming protein–glycoconjugates.

UV absorption analysis

As shown in Fig. 1C, all samples exhibited absorption peaks in the range of 260–300 nm and shared a similar spectral trend. The UV absorption of WPI around 280 nm is primarily attributed to the aromatic rings of tyrosine (Tyr) and tryptophan (Trp) residues (Hu et al., 2021). After modification, the UV absorption characteristics of WPI changed. The absorption peak of WPI was slightly shifted upon TGase-induced cross-linking, and a similar shift was observed after the introduction of glucosamine. Notably, the UV absorption intensity of glycosylated WPI increased significantly. These results suggest that the glycosylation reaction promoted unfolding of the protein structure, thereby facilitating the exposure of Trp and Tyr residues and enhancing the UV absorption intensity of glycosylated WPI (Dai et al., 2025).

Fluorescence spectral analysis

Hydrophobic amino acids such as Trp in proteins have a maximum excitation wavelength of 290 nm and are sensitive to changes in the local microenvironment, making them useful as endogenous fluorescent probes to study protein conformational changes. As shown in Fig. 1D, the maximum emission wavelengths of WPI, cross-linked WPI, and glycosylated WPI were observed at 338 nm and 342 nm, respectively. Glycosylated WPI exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity accompanied by a slight emission shift, indicating that the tryptophan residues became more sensitive to the microenvironment due to protein unfolding and interactions with glucosamine (Yu et al., 2021). At an excitation wavelength of 290 nm, untreated WPI showed the lowest fluorescence intensity compared with cross-linked and glycosylated WPI. This result suggests that tryptophan residues originally buried within the whey protein molecule were exposed and surrounded by a more hydrophilic microenvironment (Gantumur et al., 2023), further confirming that TGase successfully induced the glycosylation of glucosamine to WPI and caused a conformational change in the protein microenvironment.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy offers high sensitivity and resolution, making it suitable for protein structure analysis. The conjugation of glucosamine catalyzed by TGase affects the chemical bonds and functional groups of the protein; therefore, conformational changes were investigated by FTIR spectroscopy in the range of 400–4000 cm−1. The results are shown in Fig. 1E. Several characteristic regions were identified in the secondary structure of WPI, namely the amide I band at 1647 cm−1, the amide II band at 1535 cm−1, the amide III band at 1078 cm−1, and the amide A band at 3296 cm−1. Specifically, the absorption bands at 1647, 1535, 1078, and 3296 cm−1 are attributable to the C=O stretching vibration (amide I), the N-H bending and C-N stretching vibrations (amide II), the combined N-H bending and C-N stretching (amide III), and the O-H and N-H stretching vibrations (amide A), respectively. (Zhang et al., 2020a). The spectra of modified and unmodified WPI were similar in shape, but the intensities of the absorption peaks varied. For glycosylated WPI, the amide III peak shifted from 1078 cm−1 to 1076 cm−1, and its intensity slightly decreased compared with unmodified WPI. This shift can be attributed to the overlap of the amide III band with the newly formed C–O–C glycosidic bonds from the covalent conjugation of glucosamine to WPI, providing direct evidence for successful glycosylation. The slight decrease in intensity also reflects the depletion of amino groups, which is consistent with the results of free primary amino group content. (Yu et al., 2024). The absorption peak at 1647 cm−1 is associated with the α-helix structure, and it appeared in both unmodified and modified WPI. Notably, the intensity of this peak was significantly weaker for glycosylated WPI than for cross-linked WPI, suggesting that the introduction of glucosamine under TGase catalysis increased the rigidity of the WPI molecule, reduced its flexibility, and led to a decrease in α-helix content (Yang & Zhao, 2021). These results indicate that TGase-catalyzed modification affects the transformation and alteration of the secondary structure of whey proteins.

CD analysis

The effect of TGase modification on the secondary structure of WPI was analyzed by far-ultraviolet CD spectroscopy in the range of 190–260 nm. As shown in Fig. 1F, unmodified WPI exhibited a distinct positive band near 190 nm and a slightly weaker negative band at 220 nm, which are characteristic of the α-helical structure in proteins. Compared with unmodified WPI, both cross-linked WPI and glycosylated WPI showed a slight shift in their negative absorption bands after TGase treatment, accompanied by a reduction in α-helix content. This observation may be attributed to the transformation and partial loss of certain structural elements of WPI during the reaction. Hou et al. (2024) also reported that the conjugation of whey protein with pectin led to a reduction in α-helix content. However, the α-helix content of glycosylated WPI was higher than that of cross-linked WPI. This increase could be due to the interaction between the carbonyl group of glucosamine and the amino groups within the α-helix of WPI, which induces a change in the secondary structure and alters the rigidity of the protein (Meng et al., 2023). Therefore, TGase-mediated glycosylation modulates the protein structure of WPI, inducing a more ordered secondary structure, which provides a structural basis for the improved emulsifying and foaming properties, thereby enhancing the overall functional properties of WPI.

SEM analysis

The structural changes of WPI, cross-linked WPI, and glycosylated WPI were observed by SEM at different magnifications (500×, 1000×, and 2000×), revealing the morphology of protein molecules. As shown in Fig. 1G, all three protein samples exhibited different particle sizes and aggregate states. Untreated WPI showed a more heterogeneous size distribution, whereas after TGase treatment, cross-linked WPI became more irregular in shape and non-uniform in particle size, suggesting that TGase-catalyzed covalent cross-linking altered the intra- and inter-molecular structure of the protein (Chang et al., 2023). Compared with WPI, after the introduction of glucosamine, the protein molecules were transformed from disordered aggregates into a relatively ordered structure, and the incorporation of glucosamine resulted in a more uniform distribution of the glycosylated WPI structure. TGase catalyzed the formation of covalent cross-links and other bonds within the protein, leading to structural changes in WPI and inducing the formation of dense, irregular internal protein aggregates, which contributed to a more stable protein structure (Geng et al., 2024). In summary, SEM analysis confirms that glucosamine incorporation promotes a more uniform and ordered protein aggregation state.

Functional characterization of glycosylated WPI

Surface hydrophobicity

H0 is an important indicator of the number of hydrophobic groups on the protein surface and is widely used to assess conformational changes and functional properties of proteins. As shown in Fig. 2A, the H0 value of untreated WPI was 168, whereas that of cross-linked WPI after TGase modification significantly increased to 266 (p < 0.05). The increase in surface hydrophobicity following cross-linking indicates that this modification facilitates the exposure of hydrophobic amino acid residues, allowing the hydrophobic regions to gradually become exposed from the interior of the protein molecule (Li, Jiang, & Shi, 2024). In contrast, the H0 value of glycosylated WPI after glucosamine incorporation was 250, showing a decrease in surface hydrophobicity compared with cross-linked WPI. This reduction may be attributed to the introduction of hydrophilic monosaccharide molecules, which bind to hydrophobic groups on the protein side chains (Tian et al., 2023). ABTS+• radical scavenging and hydroxyl radical scavenging analysis

The effect of enzymatic glycosylation on the antioxidant activity of WPI was investigated by measuring the ABTS+• radical scavenging rate and hydroxyl radical scavenging rate. The results are shown in Fig. 2B. The ABTS+• and hydroxyl radical scavenging rates of untreated WPI were 38.06% and 21.22%, respectively. After TGase modification, the scavenging rates of cross-linked WPI increased to 40.90% and 36.24%, respectively, while those of glycosylated WPI (via TGase-mediated glycosylation) further increased to 44.27% and 39.68%, respectively. These results indicate that glycosylated WPI exhibited the strongest radical scavenging activity. This finding is consistent with the report by Chang et al. (2022), who found that the antioxidant activity of glycosylated zein was significantly higher than that of unmodified zein (p < 0.05). The enhanced antioxidant activity can be ascribed to the incorporation of glucosamine, which contributes additional hydroxyl moieties, as well as to the increased exposure of reactive side chains of amino acids in WPI, especially the phenolic hydroxyl groups of tyrosine residues and the indole rings of tryptophan residues. These moieties are capable of scavenging free radicals by donating hydrogen atoms, thus acting as effective radical scavengers (Hu, Du, et al., 2023). It may also be related to the increased content of hydrophobic amino acids in glycosylated WPI, which can react synergistically with free radicals, thus enhancing the radical scavenging capacity and improving the functional properties of WPI.

EAI and ESI analysis

In this study, the emulsifying properties of glycosylated WPI modified by TGase were investigated. As shown in Fig. 2C, the EAI and ESI of unmodified WPI were 16.5 m2/g and 78.97%, respectively. After TGase and glucosamine treatment, the EAI and ESI of glycosylated WPI significantly increased to 26.97 m2/g and 82.69%, respectively (p < 0.05). TGase facilitated the cross-linking reaction between protein molecules as well as between protein molecules and amino monosaccharides. The incorporation of amino monosaccharide molecules enables covalent binding to protein molecules, rendering them amphiphilic and more readily adsorbed onto the droplet surface, thereby effectively improving the EAI and ESI of WPI. Glycosylation modification of proteins can result in a more stable spatial structure, and the hydrophilic monosaccharide groups can further inhibit the aggregation of oil droplets in the emulsion, thus enhancing emulsion stability (Shi et al., 2024). Notably, glycosylated WPI exhibited slightly lower EAI and ESI values than cross-linked WPI, likely because its increased hydrophilicity compromised interfacial amphiphilic balance, while cross-linked WPI's stronger hydrophobicity facilitated droplet adsorption. However, glycosylated WPI remained significantly superior to unmodified WPI, as the incorporated monosaccharide groups effectively increased interphasic amphiphilicity, thus enhancing emulsifying performance (Hu, Du, et al., 2023).

FC and FS analysis

The foaming properties of protein samples were determined using a high-speed stirring method to investigate the effect of glycosylation on the FC and FS of WPI. As shown in Fig. 2D, glycosylated WPI exhibited better FC and FS than unmodified WPI and cross-linked WPI. Under the catalytic action of TGase, the secondary structure of the protein unfolded, exposing some hydrophobic groups. The addition of glucosamine further exposed more hydrophobic groups, which enhanced protein adsorption at the air-water interface. The exposure of hydrophobic groups increased the affinity and reduced the adsorption barrier, consistent with our measured surface hydrophobicity results (Liu et al., 2021). In addition, when protein accumulation occurs at the air-water interface, the reduction in adsorption barrier decreases the surface tension, thereby increasing the foaming properties of the protein. The covalent attachment of monosaccharide groups to proteins facilitates the formation of thicker and more viscoelastic interfacial layers, which promotes interfacial stability and improves foam stability (Yi et al., 2023). Although cross-linked WPI showed high surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 2A), its Fc remained low, owing to the large aggregates generated by TGase-mediated cross-linking, which decreased conformational flexibility and diffusivity, thus impairing rapid interfacial rearrangement. Glycosylated WPI, however, maintained a flexible architecture (Fig. 1G), enabling better interfacial adsorption and consequently leading to enhanced foaming properties.

The free primary amino group content and DH analysis of glycosylated WPI in vitro digestion

The changes in free primary amino group content of WPI, cross-linked WPI, and glycosylated WPI during simulated gastrointestinal digestion were determined using the OPA method. The results are shown in Fig. 3. During the gastric digestion phase, the free primary amino group content of all three protein samples showed no significant change, which may be mainly attributed to the moderate resistance of WPI to pepsin. Among them, the free primary amino group content of unmodified WPI was markedly higher than that of cross-linked WPI and glycosylated WPI. This difference can be explained by the formation of inter- and intramolecular isopeptide bonds during TGase catalysis, which rendered the protein structure more compact. In the intestinal digestion stage, as shown in Fig. 3A, the free primary amino group content of the three proteins was significantly higher, indicating that trypsin digests proteins more thoroughly than pepsin and exerts a stronger disruptive effect on peptide bonds (Leng et al., 2025). After digestion, proteins are broken down into smaller peptides or amino acids, which are more easily digested and absorbed, thereby providing rapid nutrient supplementation and improving protein utilization. Bioactive peptides can be produced during protein hydrolysis, which is beneficial to human health. DH is an important indicator of the stability of protein digestion. As shown in Fig. 3B, the DH of unmodified WPI was 11.79%, while that of cross-linked WPI was 10.58%. The main reason for the reduced DH is the cross-linking within protein molecules induced by TGase, which forms new bonds that are not recognized by trypsin, thereby reducing the effectiveness of digestion. In addition, the cross-linking reaction between protein molecules makes the intermolecular connections tighter, which increases the difficulty for enzymes to digest them (Gantumur et al., 2023). The DH of glycosylated WPI was 11.14%, which was higher than that of cross-linked WPI due to the loosening of the protein structure upon the introduction of monosaccharide molecules. The decrease in DH compared with unmodified WPI indicates that the amino monosaccharides form covalent bonds with WPI during the glycosylation reaction, making the glycosylated WPI more structurally stable and less susceptible to digestion (Ma et al., 2022).

Effect of glycosylated WPI digest on intestinal barrier function

Effect of glycosylated WPI digest on cell viability

Different doses (12.5–200 μg/mL) of protein digests were applied to cells for 24–48 h. As shown in Fig. 4A, all modified proteolytic digests promoted cell proliferation. The cell viability after 24 h of treatment with WPI digest ranged from 102.9% to 109.7%, and no cytotoxic effects were observed at any concentration tested. The cell viability after 24 h of treatment with cross-linked WPI digest and glycosylated WPI digest ranged from 102.8% to 110.1% and from 103.6% to 110.2%, respectively, and similarly, no toxic effects were detected. In general, milk proteins and their hydrolysates modulate various biological activities, including cell proliferative activity and the promotion of cytokine and antibody secretion (Chen et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 4B, after 48 h of treatment with WPI digest, cell viability ranged from 97.02% to 114.8%. For cross-linked WPI digest and glycosylated WPI digest, cell viability after 48 h ranged from 98.01% to 113.6% and from 98.1% to 115.1%, respectively. At concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 100 μg/mL, all three digests promoted cell viability in a time-dependent manner. However, at 200 μg/mL, a significant decrease in viability was observed, revealing a dose-dependent effect. Accordingly, the concentration range of 25–100 μg/mL was chosen for subsequent functional evaluations, since 12.5 μg/mL produced only marginal effects (at 24 h), whereas 200 μg/mL slightly suppressed viability (at 48 h). Effect of glycosylated WPI digest on TEER

As shown in Fig. 4C, all tested doses of the modified protein digests promoted the integrity of the cellular barrier. When WPI digest was applied to cells at 25 μg/mL for 24 h, the TEER value reached a maximum of 114.5%. Under the same conditions (25 μg/mL, 24 h), cross-linked WPI digest and glycosylated WPI digest achieved maximum TEER values of 115.6% and 116.2%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4D, after 48 h of treatment at 25 μg/mL, the maximum TEER values for WPI digest, cross-linked WPI digest, and glycosylated WPI digest were 117.8%, 118.6%, and 119.2%, respectively. All three digests increased TEER values in a time-dependent manner. An increase in TEER is indicative of improved barrier function (Zhang et al., 2020b). Among the three digests, glycosylated WPI digest was the most effective in enhancing intestinal barrier integrity. Consistent with these findings, our previous study demonstrated that glycosylated casein hydrolysate provided better barrier protection for IEC-6 cells than casein hydrolysate (Shi et al., 2021).

Effect of glycosylated WPI digest on paracellular permeability

The FD-4 transport rate of cells in the blank control group was set as 100%. The results are shown in Fig. 4E. All protein digests decreased cell membrane permeability. After 24 h of treatment with different concentrations of WPI digest, the FD-4 transport rate decreased to 85.9–94.9%. For modified cross-linked WPI digest and glycosylated WPI digest, the FD-4 transport rates decreased to 83.3–93.6% and 82.1–92.3%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4F, cell membrane permeability further decreased with prolonged treatment time. After 48 h of exposure to WPI digest, the FD-4 transport rate decreased to 83.8–93.8%; for cross-linked and glycosylated WPI digests, the rates decreased to 81.9–90.6% and 80.0–90.0%, respectively. Increased intestinal barrier permeability may increase the risk of obesity and other related conditions (Maqoud et al., 2024). In contrast, all three digests reduced cell permeability, and this effect became more pronounced over time. Collagen and its hydrolysates can protect and improve the intestinal barrier by reducing paracellular permeability (Wang et al., 2025). Therefore, glycosylated WPI digest exhibited a strengthened physical barrier function.

Effect of glycosylated WPI digest on cellular TJ gene expression

The expression levels of three key TJ genes (Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1) in the treated cells were analyzed by RT-PCR. Relative gene expression changes in cells treated with WPI digest, cross-linked WPI digest, and glycosylated WPI digest were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with the blank control group as the reference. As shown in Fig. 5A, compared with the control group, treatment with WPI digest for 24 h up-regulated the expression of all three TJ genes. The up-regulation folds for Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1 were 1.34, 1.81, and 1.61, respectively. Lactoferrin and osteopontin are known to improve the structure and expression of TJ proteins, thereby contributing to intestinal barrier stability (Li et al., 2023). Treatment with cross-linked WPI digest for 24 h resulted in 1.62-, 1.92-, and 1.73-fold up-regulation of Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1, respectively. Glycosylated WPI digest further increased the expression of these three TJ genes by 1.85-, 2.12-, and 1.83-fold, respectively. These results indicate that glycosylated WPI is more effective than unmodified WPI digest in enhancing TJ gene expression. TJ proteins are essential for maintaining intestinal integrity and play a critical role in stabilizing intestinal homeostasis and barrier function (Feng et al., 2020). Effect of glycosylated WPI digest on TJ proteins expression

Fig. 5B illustrates the normal Caco-2 intestinal barrier model. The effect of modified WPI digests on the expression of intestinal TJ proteins (Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1) was evaluated by Western blotting, and the results are shown in Fig. 5C–D. Compared with the control group, treatment with WPI-based digests for 24 h up-regulated the expression of TJ proteins. Specifically, cross-linked WPI digest increased the expression of Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1 by 1.16-, 1.17-, and 1.17-fold, respectively. Zhang et al. (2020a) demonstrated that protein hydrolysates from fermented soybean meal can promote the up-regulation of TJ protein expression, thereby effectively modulating intestinal barrier function. After 24 h of treatment with glycosylated WPI digest, the expression levels of the three TJ proteins were up-regulated by 1.34-, 1.26-, and 1.41-fold, respectively. These results are consistent with the RT-PCR findings described above, indicating that glycosylated WPI is more effective than unmodified WPI in promoting TJ protein expression. WPI digests exert their intestinal barrier-enhancing effects via the release of bioactive peptides that regulate TJ proteins and AMPK/PKC signaling pathways. Glycosylation further augments their bioactivity by conferring resistance to proteolytic degradation and promoting peptide–receptor interactions at the intestinal epithelium. The enhanced barrier function of glycosylated WPI arises from the combined synergy of glucosamine (modulating TJs through O-GlcNAc modification) and whey-derived peptides (supplying amino acid precursors for TJ protein synthesis) (Mao et al., 2024). Overall, glycosylated WPI can more effectively improve the permeability and integrity of the intestinal epithelium and prevent the invasion of harmful substances (Zeng et al., 2020).

Conclusions

In this study, glycosylated WPI was prepared by combining glucosamine with WPI via TGase catalysis, and its structural and functional properties were investigated. The molecular structure of the glycosylated protein became more stable, and the incorporation of glucosamine led to a more ordered secondary structure of WPI. In addition, glycosylation with glucosamine enhanced the antioxidant activity, emulsifying activity, and foaming performance of WPI. In vitro digestion experiments showed that TGase-mediated glycosylation reduced the DH of WPI. Furthermore, intestinal barrier function assays revealed that the glycosylated WPI digest was more effective in increasing TEER, reducing cell membrane permeability, and up-regulating the expression of TJ proteins, thereby enhancing the integrity and functional characteristics of the intestinal barrier. Overall, TGase-mediated glycosylation represents a novel protein modification approach that effectively improves the functional properties and enhances the biological activities of proteins. This strategy holds promising application prospects in the development of whey protein-based functional food ingredients that combine excellent processing properties with intestinal health-modulating functions.
