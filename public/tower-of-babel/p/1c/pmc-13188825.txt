PLIN3 overexpression in lung adenocarcinoma promotes M2 macrophage–myofibroblast transition in the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Kang Tian, Guoyuan Ma, Deyu Guo, Hui Jiang, Xinjie Zhao, Guanghui Wang, Jiajun Du, Zhaofei Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMacrophage–myofibroblast transition (MMT) promotes tumor progression. Studies have primarily emphasized macrophage-intrinsic regulation; however, whether lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cells actively drive MMT in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) remains unclear.MethodsWe integrated LUAD single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bulk transcriptomic datasets, applying MMT-infiltration-stratified differential analysis to identify tumor cell-derived upstream regulators of MMT. Multiple machine learning algorithms were used to construct an MMT-related risk score. SHAP analysis prioritized key genes, and NicheNet inferred tumor–macrophage ligand–receptor–target axes. Tumor cell–conditioned medium (CM)–driven MMT induction in M2 macrophages was examined by dual immunofluorescence and in vitro experiments.ResultsSingle-cell analysis showed that M2 macrophages in LUAD exhibit variable activation of an MMT-related transcriptional program. Tumor-level differential analysis across MMT infiltration strata identified tumor cell-derived candidate regulators of MMT. A 9-gene MMT prognostic risk model was established using univariate Cox regression and machine learning analyses. This model achieved a higher C-index than previously reported models, and high-risk tumors displayed an immunosuppressive infiltration pattern. SHAP analysis identified PLIN3 as a key driver. NicheNet analysis suggested that PLIN3-high tumor cells may induce MMT through TGF-β1-related signaling. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated PLIN3 upregulation in LUAD tissues, while immunofluorescence further revealed enrichment of CD68⁺α-SMA⁺ macrophages around tumor cells in PLIN3-high samples. LUAD cell-derived CM induced MMT in M2 macrophages. PLIN3 knockdown in LUAD cells attenuated CM-induced MMT, whereas PLIN3 overexpression enhanced it. PLIN3 knockdown also suppressed LUAD cell migration, invasion, and colony formation.ConclusionHigh PLIN3 expression in LUAD promotes MMT through tumor–macrophage crosstalk in the tumor microenvironment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00770-y.

Introduction

Recent global cancer statistics indicate that lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, underscoring the urgent need for improved prevention, early detection, and more effective therapies [1]. LUAD is the most common histological subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounting for approximately 40% of lung cancer cases [2]. Although screening strategies for high-risk populations and advances in molecularly targeted therapies and immunotherapy have improved survival in subsets of patients, overall prognosis remains poor due to tumor heterogeneity and the complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [3–5], especially in advanced-stage LUAD. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic targets and developing mechanism-guided intervention strategies for LUAD remain pressing needs.

With growing interest in the TME, TAMs have emerged as a major focus because of their remarkable plasticity and functional diversity [6]. TAMs are commonly classified into M1-like (anti-tumor) and M2-like (pro-tumor) phenotypes, although their states exist along a continuum. Numerous studies have shown that increased infiltration of M2-like macrophages is associated with poorer prognosis [7]. M2-like macrophages promote tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., IL-10) and pro-angiogenic mediators (e.g., VEGF), thereby fostering an immunosuppressive TME [8]. Given their central role in tumor progression, a deeper understanding of TAM polarization and tumor–TAM interactions may facilitate the development of new therapeutic strategies for lung cancer.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute another critical cellular component of the TME. CAFs promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis by remodeling the extracellular matrix and suppressing anti-tumor immune responses [9, 10]. Notably, recent studies have suggested that a subset of CAFs in NSCLC originates from the transdifferentiation of TAMs through MMT [11]. Upon activation of TGF-β1/SMAD3 signaling, TAMs upregulate CAF markers such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; ACTA2) and fibroblast activation protein (FAP), thereby acquiring fibroblast-like phenotypes and functions [11]. Further studies have identified RUNX1 as a key regulator of MMT, and inhibition of the TGF-β1/SMAD3/RUNX1 axis has been shown to reduce MMT-derived CAFs and suppress tumor progression [12]. However, most existing studies have focused on defining MMT in NSCLC and delineating macrophage-intrinsic regulatory mechanisms. The upstream cues that initiate TAM reprogramming toward MMT remain poorly defined, especially with respect to direct evidence that tumor cells actively initiate or sustain this process. This gap limits mechanistic understanding of a potential tumor cell–macrophage–tumor cell positive-feedback loop. From a TME communication perspective, these observations suggest that MMT initiation and maintenance may depend on tumor cell-derived extrinsic cues. Tumor cells are known to reshape macrophage phenotypes and functional states through secreted cytokines [13] and extracellular vesicles, including exosomes [14]. It is therefore plausible that tumor cells may also drive deeper fate transitions in TAMs, including activation of the MMT program, via paracrine and vesicle-mediated signaling. Consequently, a critical next step is to shift focus from macrophage-intrinsic regulation toward tumor–immune cell communication and to determine whether tumor cells provide upstream signals and key molecular axes that induce MMT.

Here, we hypothesize that in LUAD, tumor-derived secreted cues drive M2-like TAMs toward MMT, thereby linking matrix remodeling with immunosuppression and tumor progression. To test this hypothesis, we integrated single-cell and multi-cohort bulk transcriptomic data to characterize MMT-associated TME programs and systematically identify tumor-derived candidate upstream regulators. We then developed an MMT-related risk score to quantify MMT–TME coupling and evaluate its prognostic value and immunologic associations. SHAP-based interpretable analysis further prioritized PLIN3 as a key tumor-intrinsic driver of MMT-promoting tumor–macrophage crosstalk. PLIN3, originally identified as TIP47 and now recognized as a lipid droplet-associated perilipin family protein, functions in early lipid droplet biogenesis and links neutral-lipid storage to membrane dynamics [15–17]. PLIN3 has been reported to be frequently overexpressed from early stages of lung cancer tissues, supporting the idea that lipid-droplet-based storage programs contribute to tumor growth and starvation survival [18]. More broadly, lipid droplets are now understood as dynamic metabolic and signaling organelles that shape stress responses and intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment [19–21]. These features position PLIN3 as a plausible node coupling tumor lipid metabolism to the paracrine and secretory outputs that shape neighboring immune and stromal cell states, and potentially to MMT induction in TAMs. Finally, we combined NicheNet-based cell–cell communication inference with validation in clinical specimens and in vitro assays to interrogate the underlying mechanisms. Collectively, our findings shift the focus from macrophage-intrinsic regulation to tumor-initiated intercellular crosstalk, providing mechanistic insights and highlighting the tumor cell–TAM–MMT axis as a potential therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Data sources and preprocessing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data

Two publicly available LUAD scRNA-seq datasets (GSE117570 and GSE203360) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. After samples containing fewer than 1,000 cells were excluded, 2 normal lung and 7 LUAD samples were retained for downstream analyses. Genes detected in ≥ 3 cells were retained. Cells with < 200 or > 8,000 detected genes were excluded. Doublets were identified using DoubletFinder (pN = 0.25; pK optimized by parameter sweep), with the expected doublet rate set to 0.8% per 1,000 recovered cells. Cells with > 10% mitochondrial reads, or > 5% hemoglobin gene expression were excluded. During normalization and scaling, mitochondrial/hemoglobin gene proportions as well as cell-cycle scores were regressed out. Cell-cycle scores (S and G2/M) were calculated using Seurat’s CellCycleScoring and included as covariates in ScaleData. Batch effects were corrected using Seurat integration with canonical correlation analysis (CCA; FindIntegrationAnchors, reduction = “cca”; IntegrateData). The integrated dataset was scaled and subjected to principal component analysis (PCA). Based on the elbow plot and biological interpretability, the top 30 principal components PCs were selected. The cells were clustered at a resolution of 0.4 and visualized using UMAP.

Bulk transcriptome and clinical data

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD gene expression profiles and corresponding clinical annotations were obtained from the TCGA database and used as the training cohort. Five Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) LUAD cohorts (GSE7670, GSE31210, GSE3141, GSE30219, and GSE50081) were downloaded from GEO. Among these, GSE7670 served as an independent tumor–normal reference dataset for candidate-gene filtering, whereas GSE31210, GSE3141, GSE30219, and GSE50081 were used exclusively for external validation. All raw expression data were subjected to background correction and normalization before analysis to ensure comparability across datasets.

Tissue sample information

Tumor tissues and paired adjacent normal tissues were collected from 20 patients with LUAD who underwent thoracic surgery at Shandong Provincial Hospital from March to May 2025. Ten patients had pathologically confirmed lymph node involvement (N+), and ten were node-negative (N0). All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital (Approval No. SWYX2025-842) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines.

Single-cell cell type identification and macrophage subset classification

After clustering, cell types were annotated based on established marker genes. Cluster-specific markers were identified using Seurat’s FindMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; default parameters). Cell types were annotated using previously reported canonical markers, including macrophages (C1QC, C1QA), epithelial cells (PIGR, CXCL17), NK cells (NKG7, GNLY), dendritic cells (GPR183, PKIB, CD1C), proliferating cells (TOP2A, MKI67), plasma cells (IGKC, IGLC2, IGHA1, JCHAIN), endothelial/smooth muscle cells (SPARCL1, IGFBP7, MGP), and T cells (LTB, CCR7). Clustering results were visualized using UMAP plots, and marker gene expression across clusters was displayed using DotPlot to facilitate cell type annotation. Given that MMT predominantly occurs in M2-like TAMs, macrophage clusters were extracted for further subclustering. Macrophages were reprocessed using the same Seurat workflow (normalization, highly variable gene selection, PCA, and UMAP-based clustering) to obtain refined macrophage subpopulations. Macrophage polarization states were assigned based on established markers, with M1-like macrophages expressing CD86 and M2-like macrophages expressing CD163, MRC1 (CD206), and COLEC12.

MMT scoring and candidate gene screening

To quantify MMT programs in M2-like TAMs, a literature-derived MMT/CAF marker gene set was curated (ACTA2, FN1, FAP, MFAP5, TNC, COL11A1, PDPN, and CSPG4 [NG2]) [22]. MMT scores were calculated for each M2 macrophage using Seurat’s AddModuleScore, which computes a gene set expression score relative to matched control genes [23, 24]. M2 macrophages were dichotomized into MMT⁺ and MMT⁻ cells based on the median MMT score. For each sample, the fraction of MMT⁺ cells among M2 macrophages was calculated; samples with fewer than 20 M2 macrophages were excluded to ensure stable estimates. Samples were then classified as MMT_high or MMT_low using the median MMT⁺ fraction, and this classification was added to the Seurat metadata.

To characterize sample-level transcriptomic differences associated with MMT infiltration, a whole-sample pseudobulk count matrix was generated by summing raw UMI counts across all cells within each sample. Differential expression analysis between MMT_high and MMT_low samples was performed using edgeR (quasi-likelihood negative binomial generalized linear modeling (GLM)), with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Genes with FDR < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 were considered differentially expressed and used for downstream pathway enrichment analyses. Because counts were aggregated across all cells, this analysis captures both cell-compositional and cell-state differences.

Integration of differentially expressed genes and candidate gene selection

To prioritize MMT-associated candidates that are also robustly upregulated at the bulk-tumor level, scRNA-seq–derived differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were integrated with bulk tumor–normal differential-expression analyses. Bulk differential expression (tumor vs. normal) was performed in TCGA-LUAD and an independent GEO dataset (GSE7670) using limma. Genes significantly upregulated in tumors were defined by FDR < 0.05 and log2FC > 2. These genes were intersected with those upregulated in the MMT_high versus MMT_low whole-sample pseudobulk comparison, yielding 97 overlapping candidate genes associated with both MMT infiltration and tumor overexpression. In the TCGA-LUAD training cohort, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to assess associations between candidate gene expression and overall survival (OS), and genes with P < 0.005 were retained for subsequent prognostic modeling.

Development and evaluation of the prognostic risk model

Based on the prognostic candidates identified above, prognostic risk models were constructed using machine-learning–based survival modeling. Twelve algorithms were evaluated, including XGBoost, LASSO, random forest, glmBoost, stepwise GLM, plsRglm, linear discriminant analysis, elastic net, ridge regression, Naive Bayes, support vector machine, and gradient boosting machine [25]. By combining different algorithms in a unified modeling framework, 113 candidate models were generated. All models were trained in the TCGA-LUAD cohort, and internal performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation. The final model was selected based on cross-validated Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and overall robustness. External validation was performed in four independent GEO cohorts (GSE31210, GSE3141, GSE30219, and GSE50081). For each cohort, Harrell’s C-index (with 95% confidence interval) was calculated to quantify discrimination. The risk score for the final model was computed as follows:

Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Survival differences were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test (“survival” and “survminer” packages). Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves at 1, 3, and 5 years were generated using the timeROC package, and the area under the curve (AUC) was used to evaluate predictive accuracy.

Somatic mutation profiling and TMB analysis

Somatic mutation data (MAF format) for TCGA-LUAD were obtained from TCGA and analyzed using the maftools R package. Mutation landscapes for the high- and low-risk groups were visualized using maftools::oncoplot. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated from the MAF as the number of somatic mutations per megabase. Differences in TMB levels between the two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

TME analysis

To characterize the tumor microenvironment (TME) across risk groups, ESTIMATE was applied to calculate ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore. To obtain a robust global assessment of TME composition, immune and stromal infiltration was further inferred using multiple deconvolution algorithms, including xCell, TIMER, quanTIseq, MCPcounter, EPIC, and CIBERSORT-ABS. These methods were used to compare immune and stromal components between the two risk groups.

Drug sensitivity and prediction of immunotherapy response

Drug sensitivity was predicted using pRRophetic and oncoPredict to estimate IC50 values for 198 compounds in TCGA-LUAD samples, followed by between-group comparisons. Predicted responses to immunotherapy were assessed using the Immunophenoscore (IPS) from The Cancer Immunome Atlas and the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) framework.

Model interpretability using SHAP

To interpret gene contributions to the model-predicted risk score, we performed Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis [26]. SHAP values were computed using the kernelshap package in R to quantify each gene’s marginal contribution to the predicted risk score. Global feature importance and overall effect direction were summarized using SHAP bar and beeswarm plots. Cohort-level feature effects and potential interactions were examined using SHAP dependence plots, and patient-level explanations were illustrated using waterfall plots for representative cases.

Gene-associated immune infiltration and CAF-related stromal analyses

To investigate gene-associated immune infiltration, immune-cell fractions were estimated from TCGA-LUAD bulk transcriptomic profiles using CIBERSORT with the LM22 signature matrix [27]. Relative proportions of 22 immune-cell subsets were obtained, with particular attention to macrophage populations relevant to this study. To assess CAF-related stromal features, candidate gene was correlated with canonical CAF markers, including ACTA2, FAP, and FN1, and TIDE-derived CAF score was compared between the corresponding groups.

CopyKAT-based tumor/normal epithelial assignment and marker validation

CopyKAT was applied to epithelial cells to infer large-scale copy-number alterations [28]. The cells classified as aneuploid by CopyKAT were defined as malignant epithelial cells, whereas diploid cells were considered as non-malignant epithelium. This classification assignment was validated using canonical markers, with normal epithelial cells expressing PDPN and SCGB1A1 and malignant LUAD epithelial cells expressing EPCAM, MDK, SOX4, MMP7, and KRT8. These gene expression differences between malignant and non-malignant epithelial compartments were visualized using faceted box plots.

NicheNet cell–cell communication analysis

Tumor epithelial cells were stratified into PLIN3-high and PLIN3-low groups based on median PLIN3 expression. NicheNet was applied to infer ligand–receptor–target relationships between PLIN3-stratified tumor epithelial cells (sender populations) and MMT⁺ macrophages (receiver population). Candidate ligands were required to be expressed in ≥ 5% of cells in the sender population, and candidate receptors in ≥ 5% of cells in the receiver population, according to the NicheNet human prior knowledge network. To define receiver target genes, differential expression analysis was performed between MMT⁺ and MMT⁻ macrophages using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (log₂FC ≥ 0.25; Benjamini–Hochberg FDR ≤ 0.05). Genes significantly upregulated in MMT⁺ macrophages were used as the target gene set. Ligand activities were then quantified using the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPR) and ranked accordingly, and the top 30 ligands were retained for downstream analyses. Finally, weighted ligand–receptor links, ligand–transcription factor connections, and transcription factor–target relationships were integrated to construct a putative hierarchical regulatory network linking prioritized ligands (e.g., CSF1 and TGFB1) to myofibroblast-associated targets (e.g., ACTA2, FN1, FAP, and COL1A1), thereby generating a mechanistic hypothesis for PLIN3-associated tumor–macrophage communication.

In vitro cell experiments

Cell lines and culture conditions

LUAD cell lines (A549 and PC9), normal human bronchial epithelial (NHBE) cells, and the human monocytic cell line THP-1 were purchased from Procell Life Science (China). A549, PC9, and NHBE cells were cultured in F12K, RPMI-1640, and DMEM, respectively, each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. THP-1 cells were maintained in THP-1 complete medium supplemented with 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and were routinely tested to confirm mycoplasma-negative status.

PLIN3 knockdown and overexpression

PLIN3-specific siRNAs and a negative control siRNA were purchased from Keyybio (Shandong, China). The PLIN3 overexpression plasmid was synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transfections were performed using jetPRIME® (PolyPlus Transfection, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA sequences and plasmid information are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Transwell migration assay

Cells were harvested, resuspended in serum-free medium, and seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (Corning; 24-well format) at 4 × 10⁴ cells/well in 200 µL. Complete medium containing 10% FBS (600 µL) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed using a cotton swab. The cells on the lower surface were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Images were acquired from three randomly selected non-overlapping fields per insert, and migrated cells were quantified using ImageJ.

Wound-healing assay

Cells were seeded in 12-well plates and grown to 90%–95% confluence. A uniform scratch was created using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip. After the plates were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove detached cells, fresh medium was added. Wound closure was imaged in the same fields at 0 and 24 h. The wound area was quantified using ImageJ, and the relative closure rate was calculated.

Colony formation assayColony formation assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at 1,000 cells/well and cultured for 14 days. The medium was refreshed every 3 days. For PLIN3 knockdown, transfection was repeated every 3 days to maintain gene modulation throughout the assay. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with crystal violet for 20 min, washed with PBS, imaged, and counted.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the AG RNAex Pro reagent (Accurate Biotechnology, China) and used to synthesize cDNA with a commercial reverse transcription kit (Accurate Biotechnology, China). qRT–PCR was performed using a SYBR Green master mix on a real-time PCR system. The relative expression of the target gene was determined using GAPDH as an internal control. All primer sequences used for PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, followed by sonication and centrifugation to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai). Equal amounts of protein were separated through sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PLIN3 (1:1000, ProteinTech, 66523-1-Ig), α-SMA (1:1000, ProteinTech, 14395-1-AP), CD68 (1:1000, Santa Cruz, sc-20060), and GAPDH (1:1000, Santa Cruz, sc-47724), followed by treatment with horse radish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using chemiluminescent substrate (Advansta, USA) and imaged.

THP-1 differentiation, M2 polarization, and conditioned-medium stimulation

THP-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 24 h to induce adherence and differentiation into M0 macrophages. The cells were washed three times with PBS to remove PMA and rested in PMA-free medium for an additional 24 h. The M0 macrophages were polarized toward an M2-like phenotype using IL-4 (20 ng/mL) and IL-13 (20 ng/mL) for 48 h. Tumor cell-conditioned medium (CM) was collected after 48 h, clarified through centrifugation at 1,000 rpm to remove insoluble material, mixed 1:1 with fresh macrophage medium, and applied to M2 macrophages. The cells were harvested after 24 h for RNA extraction and after 48 h for protein extraction.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature.

For PLIN3 immunohistochemistry (IHC), the sections were incubated with an anti-PLIN3 primary antibody (1:100, ProteinTech, 66523-1-Ig) for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB development. IHC was used to evaluate differential PLIN3 expression between the tumor and adjacent normal tissues.

To determine immunofluorescence (IF), PLIN3 single staining was performed to stratify an independent clinical cohort into PLIN3-high and PLIN3-low groups based on tumor-cell PLIN3 signal intensity. To assess MMT-like macrophages, matched serial (adjacent) sections from the same cases were subjected to double IF staining for CD68 and α-SMA. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD68 (1:100, Santa Cruz) and anti-α-SMA (1:1000, ProteinTech), followed by species-matched Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and DAPI counterstaining. Images were acquired through fluorescence or confocal microscopy, and CD68⁺α-SMA⁺ double-positive cells in the peritumoral region were quantified as indicated.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3). Survival differences were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test, and Cox proportional hazards regression was applied for univariate and multivariate analyses. Differential expression analyses were conducted using methods appropriate to each data type (e.g., Wilcoxon rank-sum test for single-cell marker identification, edgeR for scRNA-seq pseudobulk comparisons, and limma for bulk tumor–normal comparisons), with Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied where appropriate. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome analysis identifies major cell populations in the LUAD microenvironment and distinguishes M1/M2 TAMs

An overview of the study design is shown in Fig. 1A. To construct a cellular atlas of the LUAD TME for subsequent MMT-focused analyses, we performed unsupervised clustering of integrated single-cell transcriptomes from two LUAD scRNA-seq datasets (GSE117570 and GSE203360). After excluding samples with fewer than 1,000 cells, 2 normal lung samples and 7 LUAD samples were retained (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Following quality control, doublet removal, and dataset integration, dimensionality reduction identified 16 clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Based on canonical marker genes, clusters were annotated as major TME cell populations, including macrophages, epithelial cells, and T cells (Fig. 1B). Epithelial clusters were enriched for EPCAM, WFDC2, and SFTPB, whereas macrophage clusters showed high C1QA and C1QC expression (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. 1C–D), consistent with the assigned identities. Macrophages represented the largest cellular fraction (Fig. 1D), and sample-wise cell-type distributions are summarized in Fig. 1E.

Given previous reports that MMT predominantly occurs in M2-like TAMs, we further analyzed the macrophage compartment at higher resolution by subclustering macrophages. UMAP visualization identified two major macrophage states (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Fig. 1E): a CD86-high population consistent with an M1-like program and a second population enriched for M2-like markers, including CD163, MRC1 (CD206), and COLEC12 (Supplementary Fig. 1F). Accordingly, these populations were designated as M1-like and M2-like macrophages (Fig. 1G). The proportions and distributions of M1-like and M2-like macrophages across the nine samples are shown in Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. 1G. This classification provides the foundation for downstream analyses of macrophage functional states and MMT-related programs.

A CAF-marker–based MMT score identifies MMT-like states within M2-like macrophages

MMT is a continuous, dynamic transdifferentiation process, and discrete classification based on a limited set of markers fails to capture intermediate and transitional states. To avoid overlooking M2-like macrophages undergoing or primed for MMT, we calculated a per-cell MMT score using Seurat’s AddModuleScore based on a predefined CAF-associated gene set. This score reflects activation of myofibroblast-like transcriptional programs and enables systematic identification of MMT-high macrophages.

MMT scores were significantly higher in M2-like macrophages than in M1-like macrophages (Fig. 2A), consistent with previous reports that MMT is enriched in M2-like TAM states. We next stratified M2-like macrophages into MMT-high (MMT⁺) and MMT-low (MMT⁻) groups using the median MMT score as the cutoff. As expected, the MMT⁺ group exhibited markedly higher scores than the MMT⁻ group (p < 0.0001; Fig. 2B), indicating that this stratification captures distinct degrees of MMT-like activation within the M2-like compartment. Across samples, the fraction of MMT⁺ macrophages varied substantially (Fig. 2C). Notably, MMT⁺ macrophages were detected in all LUAD samples but were nearly absent in normal lung, suggesting that MMT is characteristic of the LUAD TME.

To characterize transcriptomic differences associated with MMT infiltration and to identify candidate regulators of tumor–macrophage crosstalk driving MMT, we compared gene expression between the MMT-high and MMT-low groups. Compared with the MMT-low group, the MMT-high group upregulated 966 genes (FDR < 0.05, log2FC > 1), including the CAF-associated gene FN1 (Fig. 2D). GO analysis indicated that genes upregulated in the MMT-high group were enriched for cell-cycle–related terms (e.g., chromosome segregation and nuclear division) and regulation of immune signaling and the MAPK cascade (Fig. 2E), consistent with increased proliferative and immune-modulatory programs. By contrast, genes upregulated in the MMT-low group were enriched for cytoplasmic translation and ribosome biogenesis, along with adaptive immune-related terms such as T-cell activation (Fig. 2F). KEGG analysis showed that the MMT-high group was enriched in lysosome, cell cycle, and phagosome pathways. This pattern suggests that the MMT-high subgroup may exhibit more active phagosome maturation and lysosome-dependent degradative programs. By contrast, the MMT-low group was enriched in immune-related pathways, including infection- and immunodeficiency-associated signatures. Collectively, these findings indicate that MMT-high samples are characterized by CAF-associated remodeling programs, whereas MMT-low samples are characterized by adaptive immune signatures.

A nine-gene MMT–TME risk model predicts prognosis and immune exclusion in LUAD

To derive clinically relevant MMT-associated candidates at the bulk-tumor level, genes upregulated in the MMT-high group were intersected with those consistently upregulated in LUAD tumors versus normal lung in both TCGA-LUAD and the independent cohort GSE7670. This approach yielded 97 overlapping genes (Fig. 2G), representing candidate factors associated with high MMT infiltration, consistent tumor upregulation, and potential involvement in MMT-related microenvironment remodeling and tumor–macrophage interactions. Univariate Cox regression of these 97 candidates identified 26 prognostic genes significantly associated with OS (P < 0.005; Fig. 2H). All 26 genes exhibited hazard ratios of > 1, indicating that higher expression was associated with poorer survival (Fig. 2H). Differences in the expression of these 26 genes between LUAD and normal tissues are presented in Fig. 2I.

To optimize prognostic performance, we systematically evaluated 12 machine-learning algorithms (113 algorithm combinations) in the TCGA-LUAD training cohort and performed external validation in four independent GEO cohorts (Fig. 3A). As the glmBoost + GBM combination exhibited the highest and most consistent performance, it was selected as the final MMT–TME risk model. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups using the median risk score as the cutoff. Across the training cohort, the pooled validation set, and each external cohort, the high-risk group consistently exhibited poorer OS (Fig. 3B–G; all P < 0.05). Time-dependent ROC analyses demonstrated good discrimination for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival across cohorts (Fig. 3H). C-indices across six cohorts were 0.663, 0.836, 0.803, 0.744, 0.697, and 0.707 (Fig. 4A), indicating robust cross-cohort stratification.

To characterize the biological features captured by the risk score, we compared clinical, genomic, microenvironmental, and treatment-associated characteristics between risk groups. Risk scores were significantly associated with clinical variables, including sex, stage, and T, N, and M classifications (Supplementary Fig. 2A). The risk model remained an independent prognostic factor in univariate and multivariate Cox analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). Mutation profiling revealed broadly similar landscapes dominated by TP53, TTN, MUC16, and CSMD3 (Fig. 5A, B); however, the high-risk group exhibited significantly higher TMB (Fig. 5C), consistent with increased genomic instability.

Integrated deconvolution across seven methods revealed that risk scores were positively correlated with myeloid/stromal components (including TAMs and CAFs) and negatively correlated with antitumor effector populations, such as CD8⁺ T cells, effector and memory T cells, B cells, and NK cells (Fig. 5D). Consistently, the high-risk group showed a higher TIDE total score and T-cell exclusion score, whereas the T-cell dysfunction score was relatively lower (Fig. 5E). This pattern suggests that immune evasion in the high-risk group may be driven predominantly by exclusion-related mechanisms rather than by dysfunction of infiltrating T cells. Notably, despite elevated TMB, the high-risk group still exhibited reduced effector-cell infiltration and stronger exclusion-related features, indicating that increased TMB alone may be insufficient to translate into an effective antitumor immune response. IPS analyses further indicated higher predicted responsiveness in the low-risk group, with significantly higher IPS values in the CTLA4–/PD-1– and CTLA4+/PD-1– categories, whereas no significant differences were observed in the CTLA4–/PD-1+ or CTLA4+/PD-1+ categories (Fig. 5F). Drug response prediction identified 36 compounds with significant between-group differences, all showing higher predicted sensitivity in the low-risk group (Fig. 5G; Supplementary Fig. 3A), consistent with broader predicted therapeutic opportunities in low-risk tumors.

Finally, GSEA revealed that high-risk tumors were enriched for programs related to chromatin organization, DNA replication, and epithelial keratinization, whereas low-risk tumors were enriched for ciliary structure and motility (Supplementary Fig. 2D). KEGG analysis similarly highlighted enrichment of cell cycle, DNA replication, and p53 signaling pathways in high-risk tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2E), consistent with an increased proliferative phenotype. Correlation analyses further demonstrated that model genes and risk scores were positively associated with proliferation- and stroma/inflammation-related hallmarks (e.g., E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, MYC targets, and mTORC1 signaling) (Supplementary Fig. 2F, G). In addition, risk scores correlated with multiple KEGG pathways, reflecting oncogenic and microenvironmental regulation, including p53 and Wnt signaling. Collectively, this nine-gene MMT–TME risk signature robustly stratifies LUAD prognosis and captures a myeloid/stroma-enriched, immune-excluded TME.

PLIN3 promotes MMT programs in macrophages via tumor–macrophage communication

To investigate how tumor-cell PLIN3 may induce macrophage MMT through intercellular signaling, we examined tumor–macrophage communication using scRNA-seq data. Re-clustering of epithelial cells identified multiple epithelial subpopulations (Fig. 7A). CopyKAT analysis inferred diploid and aneuploid epithelial populations (Fig. 7B); aneuploid cells were annotated as tumor epithelium and diploid cells as normal-like epithelium, concordant with sample origin (Fig. 7C). This classification was further supported by marker expression, with normal-like epithelium expressing PDPN and SCGB1A1, and tumor epithelium enriched for malignant-associated epithelial markers, including MDK, SOX4, MMP7, EPCAM, and KRT8 (Fig. 7D).

Using NicheNet, PLIN3-high and PLIN3-low tumor epithelial cells were defined as sender populations and MMT⁺ macrophages as receivers to infer ligand–target regulatory axes. In the PLIN3-high tumor epithelium, NicheNet prioritized CSF1 and TGFB1 as top-ranked ligands (Fig. 7E), and both were expressed at higher levels in the PLIN3-high tumor epithelial cells. Ligand–target analysis indicated strong regulatory potential toward myofibroblast-associated targets, including ACTA2, FN1, FAP, and COL1A1 (Fig. 7E). A ligand–receptor heatmap revealed high CSF1 expression in the PLIN3-high tumor epithelium alongside CSF1R in MMT⁺ macrophages, as well as coordinated expression of TGFB1 and TGFBR1/2 (Fig. 7F), consistent with active CSF1–CSF1R and TGFB1–TGFBR signaling.

Within the inferred regulatory network, TGFB1 ranked among the top ligands and was linked to myofibroblast markers (ACTA2, FN1, FAP, and COL1A1) through transcription factors such as SMAD3 and JUN (Fig. 7G). This supports a TGFB1-centered route through which PLIN3-high tumor cells may bias macrophages toward an MMT-like state. CSF1 was also predicted to influence MMT-related targets via STAT-family transcription factors (Supplementary Fig. 4H). Collectively, these analyses suggest that PLIN3-high tumor cells may engage TGFB1- and CSF1-mediated signaling to activate MMT-like programs in macrophages, providing testable mechanistic axes for subsequent experimental validation.

PLIN3 is upregulated in LUAD and associated with poor prognosis

To assess PLIN3 expression and its clinical relevance in LUAD, we analyzed public datasets and validated the findings in an independent institutional cohort. In TCGA-LUAD, PLIN3 expression was significantly higher in tumors than in normal lung tissue (Fig. 8A). High PLIN3 expression was associated with significantly poorer OS (Fig. 8B), and ROC analysis demonstrated its discriminatory ability for survival prediction (Fig. 8C). Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses further identified PLIN3 as an independent adverse prognostic factor (HR > 1; Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Clinically, high PLIN3 expression was associated with more advanced AJCC stage and higher T and N classifications (Fig. 8D; Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). PLIN3 levels were also significantly higher in N⁺ than in N⁻ patients (Fig. 8E), supporting an association with nodal involvement.

To validate PLIN3 at the protein level, we performed IHC in an institutional cohort. Of the 20 cases, 4 were excluded because of inadequate slide quality, leaving 16 paired tumor and adjacent normal samples for scoring. PLIN3 exhibited diffuse and strong cytoplasmic staining in tumor cells, whereas staining was weak or absent in adjacent normal epithelium (Fig. 8F). Accordingly, IRS scores were significantly higher in tumor tissues than in paired adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 8G). In this cohort, PLIN3 expression did not differ significantly between N⁻ and N⁺ groups (Fig. 8J), suggesting that the analysis may be underpowered and warrants validation in larger cohorts. In parallel, qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed markedly higher PLIN3 expression in LUAD cell lines (A549 and PC9) than in NHBE cells (Fig. 8H, I), corroborating the tumor-associated upregulation observed in public datasets and IHC.

High PLIN3 expression is associated with a myeloid-dominant, immune-excluded TME and predicted ICB non-response

Given the association between PLIN3 and immune and stromal remodeling, we next assessed PLIN3-associated TME features. ESTIMATE analysis indicated significantly higher StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATE score in PLIN3-high tumors (Supplementary Fig. 4E), consistent with a stroma- and immune-infiltrated microenvironment. However, a higher ImmuneScore does not necessarily reflect increased antitumor immunity, prompting further resolution of immune composition. CIBERSORT deconvolution revealed increased proportions of M2 and M0 macrophages, activated mast cells, and neutrophils in PLIN3-high tumors, accompanied by reduced B cells and plasma cells (Supplementary Fig. 4F). This pattern is consistent with a myeloid-dominant, immunosuppressive microenvironment and enhanced stromal remodeling. TIDE analysis further revealed higher TIDE total, T-cell exclusion, and T-cell dysfunction scores in PLIN3-high tumors (Supplementary Fig. 4G), suggesting that elevated PLIN3 expression may be associated with a broader immune-evasion program involving both impaired T-cell infiltration and functional compromise of infiltrating T cells. Together, these analyses indicate that high PLIN3 expression is associated with a stroma-enriched, myeloid-dominant, immunosuppressive TME, consistent with an immune-excluded high-risk state. These observations provided the rationale for subsequent functional validation of the PLIN3–MMT axis.

To assess tumor-intrinsic functions of PLIN3, we knocked down PLIN3 in A549 and PC9 cells using siRNA. These results indicate that PLIN3 supports LUAD cell motility, invasion, and clonogenic growth, highlighting a tumor-intrinsic pro-malignant role of PLIN3 along with its microenvironmental associations.

Tumor conditioned media induces MMT in M2 macrophages

To confirm the presence of MMT in LUAD tissues, we performed dual IF staining for CD68 and α-SMA. D68⁺α-SMA⁺ double-positive cells were present in peri-epithelial and stromal regions (Fig. 10A), consistent with macrophages acquiring myofibroblast-like features in patient tumors. To test whether tumor-derived soluble factors can induce this transition in vitro, THP-1–derived M2 macrophages were treated with exogenous TGF-β1, A549-conditioned medium (A549-CM), PC9-conditioned medium (PC9-CM), or NHBE-conditioned medium (NHBE-CM). Both TGF-β1 and A549/PC9-CM significantly increased ACTA2 (α-SMA) mRNA and α-SMA protein levels, whereas NHBE-CM exerted no significant effect (Fig. 10B, C). Morphologically, TGF-β1 and tumor CM induced a shift in macrophage morphology from a round shape to an elongated, spindle-like fibroblast-like shape with prominent cytoplasmic α-SMA staining. Importantly, most α-SMA⁺ cells retained CD68 expression, and merged channels exhibited clear co-localization, supporting a macrophage-derived transition rather than a carryover of contaminating fibroblasts (Fig. 10D, E). Conversely, control conditions and NHBE-CM mostly preserved macrophage morphology with minimal α-SMA induction. Together, these findings indicate that LUAD cell-derived CM can induce MMT-like M2 macrophage transdifferentiation, phenocopying TGF-β1–induced changes and implicating soluble factors—potentially involving TGF-β signaling—in this process.

Tumor-cell PLIN3 bidirectionally regulates MMT induction via tumor–macrophage crosstalk

We next integrated clinical and experimental evidence to determine whether tumor-cell PLIN3 directly modulates MMT. In TCGA-LUAD, PLIN3 expression correlated positively with canonical MMT markers, including ACTA2, FAP, and FN1 (Fig. 11A). Consistently, CAF scores were significantly higher in the PLIN3-high group (Fig. 11B), indicating a more pronounced MMT-associated stromal program in PLIN3-high tumors. To extend these transcriptomic findings to tissue-level evidence, we stratified an independent clinical cohort into PLIN3-high and PLIN3-low groups based on tumor-cell PLIN3 staining intensity and performed dual IF staining for CD68 and α-SMA. Notably, PLIN3-high tumors harbored substantially more CD68⁺α-SMA⁺ double-positive macrophages in peritumoral regions, whereas such cells were rare in PLIN3-low tumors (Fig. 11C). Together, these findings suggest that PLIN3-high tumors are enriched in MMT-like macrophages, supporting tumor-cell PLIN3 as an upstream determinant of MMT induction.

To directly test this hypothesis, we established PLIN3 knockdown and overexpression models in A549 and PC9 cells, collected CM, and treated THP-1–derived M2 macrophages. Compared with control CM, CM from PLIN3-knockdown tumor cells remarkably reduced ACTA2 (α-SMA) mRNA and α-SMA protein levels, whereas CM from PLIN3-overexpressing tumor cells significantly increased both readouts (Fig. 11D, E). These bidirectional effects provide direct functional evidence that tumor-cell PLIN3 levels determine the capacity of tumor-derived CM to induce an MMT-like program in M2 macrophages.

Discussion

This study provides convergent evidence from bioinformatics analyses, clinical histology, and in vitro functional experiments to systematically investigate the role of LUAD cells in MMT. By shifting the focus from macrophage-intrinsic regulators to tumor-cell–initiated signals, we demonstrate that LUAD cells act as upstream drivers of MMT in TAMs. Through integrated single-cell transcriptomics, multi-cohort bulk transcriptomics, and interpretable machine-learning approaches, we identified the lipid droplet-associated protein PLIN3 as a key tumor-intrinsic factor associated with MMT and poor clinical outcomes. Tumor–immune cell communication analyses suggested that PLIN3-high tumor cells may deliver enhanced TGF-β1 and CSF1 signals to TAMs, thereby activating intrinsic MMT programs. More importantly, LUAD cell-derived CM increased the expression of MMT markers in M2 macrophages, whereas CM from normal epithelial cells or M2 macrophage monoculture did not. Manipulating PLIN3 levels in LUAD cells bidirectionally altered the magnitude of MMT induction in M2 macrophages. Given the established role of MMT in promoting tumor progression, these findings indicate that LUAD cells not only sustain growth through cell-intrinsic proliferation and metabolic adaptation but also actively drive TAMs to undergo MMT, thereby promoting tumor development and progression.

MMT was originally described in renal fibrosis rather than cancer [29]. Subsequent studies by Tang et al. demonstrated that MMT presents a novel mechanism underlying chronic renal interstitial fibrosis [30], highlighting the capacity of immune cells to transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts via MMT and drive fibrotic remodeling. Given the mechanistic parallels between tumor stromal remodeling and fibrosis, these findings provide crucial insights into the origin of CAFs in tumors and their pro-tumorigenic functions. More recently, Tang et al. showed that a CAF subtype in NSCLC is derived from M2 TAMs using single-cell sequencing and pseudotime analysis, and further demonstrated that MMT contributes to tumor progression [11]. However, existing studies largely focus on macrophage-intrinsic regulation, and upstream cell types and exogenous signals that trigger and sustain MMT in the occurrence and development of tumors remain poorly defined, particularly in LUAD. Moreover, evidence for specific tumor cell-derived mediators is particularly lacking in LUAD. Notably, tumor cell-derived signals may regulate MMT and reinforce a positive feedback loop that promotes tumorigenesis. This would enable multi-level intervention along the “tumor cell-MMT-tumor progression” axis. Targeting upstream tumor-derived signals could reduce MMT at its origin, thus restraining tumor progression. Therefore, deep exploration of the overall occurrence mechanism of MMT in the TME of LUAD is of great significance.

Multiple studies have identified TGF-β signaling as a core pathway driving MMT, with Smad3 and RUNX1 acting as key downstream effectors. In line with this mechanistic framework, our cell–cell communication analysis implicated TGF-β and CSF1 signaling pathways as candidate mediators of PLIN3-driven MMT. Using exogenous TGF-β1 as a positive control, we performed CM stimulation assays to verify whether tumor cell-derived CM can induce MMT in M2 macrophages. Exposure of M2 macrophages to LUAD cell-derived CM strongly induced MMT, and exogenous TGF-β1 elicited a comparable effect. These results suggest that tumor-derived soluble factors can drive MMT in macrophages and implicate TGF-β signaling as a candidate mediator. We then assessed whether the magnitude of MMT induction is modulated by PLIN3 levels in tumor cells. In LUAD–macrophage co-culture, PLIN3 overexpression in LUAD cells increased the proportion of M2 macrophages undergoing MMT, whereas PLIN3 knockdown attenuated this process. Moreover, IF analyses across independent cohorts exhibited a higher density of CD68⁺ α-SMA⁺ macrophages in PLIN3-high tumor regions. This spatial association offers histological evidence supporting that PLIN3 expression is significantly correlated with MMT-related features. Collectively, these results are consistent with those of our transcriptomic analyses and in vitro functional assays. Extensive evidence indicates that tumor cells serve as major signaling hubs within the TME. They continuously secrete cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors [31]. In addition, tumor-derived exosomes can deliver TGF-β and regulatory miRNAs/proteins to reprogram the microenvironment such that it supports tumor survival [31–33]. Considering the role of PLIN3 in lipid droplet biology and the metabolic state of tumor cells, PLIN3-associated metabolic rewiring may affect secretory pathways, including exosome biogenesis and release. Accordingly, we hypothesize that PLIN3 upregulation in tumor cells reshapes the quantity and composition of tumor-derived signals (including soluble factors and exosomal cargo), thereby regulating MMT induction in macrophages.

Moreover, our bioinformatics analyses suggested that high-risk tumors may actively remodel the TME, functioning not only as passive responders to microenvironmental cues but also as organizers of the immune–stromal ecosystem. Specifically, tumors with higher risk scores exhibited relative enrichment of myeloid and stromal compartments, including TAMs and CAFs, whereas effector lymphocytes, particularly CD8⁺ T cells, were comparatively depleted. These tumors also exhibited a higher overall TIDE score and stronger T-cell exclusion signals, whereas the T-cell dysfunction score was relatively lower. Together, these findings suggest a TME characterized more by stromal remodeling and immune exclusion than by predominant dysfunction of infiltrating T cells. This interpretation is consistent with the enrichment of TAMs and CAFs and the relative depletion of effector T-cell populations observed in the high-risk group. This view is also supported by previous evidence that TGF-β–driven extracellular matrix remodeling promotes T-cell exclusion and reduces the benefits of immunotherapy [34]. Mechanistically, studies have reported that macrophage-intrinsic TGF-β–SMAD3 signaling, along with its downstream effector RUNX1, is a key axis for MMT initiation. Accordingly, in a niche marked by upregulated TGF-β signaling, myeloid/stromal predominance, and immune exclusion, MMT is more likely to be facilitated in this context and to coexist with CAF-like matrix programming. In addition, KEGG analysis showed that the MMT-high group was significantly enriched in lysosome- and phagosome-related pathways. This pattern suggests that the MMT-high state may be accompanied by enhanced phagosome maturation and lysosome-dependent degradative activity, extending beyond transcriptional features alone. Previous studies have shown that, in addition to secreting proteases, macrophages can internalize collagen and other extracellular matrix components through receptor-mediated uptake and degrade them within lysosomes, thereby contributing to matrix turnover and fibrosis-like tissue remodeling [35]. These observations raise the possibility that the CAF-like remodeling program associated with MMT may be functionally coupled to autophagy-related lysosomal pathways. Prior studies have shown that TGF-β can activate autophagy-related programs and amplify profibrotic responses, providing additional mechanistic support for a potential link between enhanced lysosome/phagosome activity and fibrosis-like matrix remodeling [36]. In particular, LC3-associated phagocytosis (LAP), a non-canonical autophagy-related process, promotes LC3 recruitment to phagosomal membranes and facilitates phagosome–lysosome fusion, providing a potential mechanistic bridge between phagocytic processing and autophagy-related machinery [37]. However, it should be noted that pathway enrichment alone does not demonstrate increased autophagic flux, and this possibility will require direct functional validation in future studies.

Meanwhile, the myeloid enrichment reflected in the risk score is consistent with the established evidence that the CSF1–CSF1R axis promotes macrophage recruitment and aids in shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Moreover, inhibition of the CSF1–CSF1R axis has been reported to increase intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. This supports its potential role as an upstream link between myeloid-dominant immunosuppression and MMT-associated matrix remodeling [38, 39]. In summary, a myeloid-dominant immunosuppressive niche possibly promotes MMT, potentially via cues such as TGF-β and CSF1. MMT-driven CAF-like matrix remodeling may then aggravate immune exclusion and immunosuppression. Together, these processes may form a self-reinforcing loop sustaining a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment and further favor MMT.

Overall, our findings have implications for both mechanistic studies of the TME and translational development. First, the MMT–TME risk score offers a framework to include MMT-associated microenvironmental features for prognostic stratification. In our analyses, it showed a higher C-index than existing models, suggesting that MMT-related features capture clinically meaningful tumor microenvironmental states. In other words, the score reflects a composite state characterized by stromal remodeling and immunosuppression. Second, we identify PLIN3, a lipid droplet-associated protein, as a regulator of MMT. To our knowledge, this is the first study to link PLIN3 to MMT. Notably, unlike the risk-group comparison, the PLIN3-high subgroup exhibited increases in both T-cell exclusion and T-cell dysfunction scores. This pattern suggests that elevated PLIN3 expression may be associated not only with impaired T-cell infiltration but also with functional compromise of infiltrating T cells, reflecting a broader immunosuppressive state. This finding extends the regulatory landscape of MMT and highlights a metabolic axis in tumor–immune–stromal crosstalk. Importantly, our findings support a TME-guided therapeutic concept. Targeting tumor cell proliferation together with PLIN3 attenuates tumor invasiveness and dampens macrophage MMT, thereby limiting CAF-associated stromal remodeling. This dual impact could provide a rationale for combinatorial strategies to improve therapeutic responses.

Although this study improves our understanding of MMT and PLIN3, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our conclusions are mostly based on in vitro experiments, as direct in vivo validation was lacking. Future studies should validate these findings in vivo, for example, in subcutaneous xenograft models established using LUAD cells with PLIN3 knockdown or overexpression. Tumor growth, together with the frequency of CD68⁺α-SMA⁺ macrophages, can be quantified to assess PLIN3 functions. Furthermore, although LUAD cell-derived CM induced MMT in macrophages, the key mediators underlying this effect remain undefined. Proteomic or secretomic profiling of LUAD cell-derived CM, followed by functional validation of prioritized ligands and pathways, will help establish a causal mechanism. Although our scoring-based approach was designed to identify transitional transcriptional features of MMT, it cannot fully resolve spatial context or cellular state dynamics. Integrating spatial transcriptomics with multiplex imaging will help delineate the spatial organization of macrophages and their alignment with the MMT trajectory.

In summary, our study is the first to reveal that PLIN3 in LUAD promotes MMT through tumor–macrophage interactions. Conceptually, this work shifts the focus from macrophage-intrinsic effector pathways to tumor cell-initiated, communication-driven MMT regulation. These findings aid in filling a mechanistic gap in our understanding of MMT in LUAD and provide a rationale for developing microenvironment-oriented intervention strategies.
