Accelerating clinical development outside the usual regions for future pandemics: a comparative analysis of COVID-19 therapeutic development between the USA and Japan
Authors: Miyuki Katayama, Mayumi Shikano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, the clinical development of therapeutics progressed rapidly. However, regions outside the areas where most clinical development occurred experienced delayed access and had fewer options for new therapeutics. To adequately respond to future pandemics, these regions must be prepared to expedite the development of necessary therapeutics. In this study, we compared the clinical development of COVID-19 therapeutics between the USA and Japan and proposed strategies for enhancing global therapeutic deployment in future pandemics.DesignCross-sectional analysis.SettingThe regulatory documentation for COVID-19 therapeutics granted Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) in the USA or approved in Japan during the WHO-declared pandemic period (30 January 2020 to 5 May 2023) was analysed.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe development timelines and submitted data in both regions were analysed.Results14 therapeutics were authorised in the USA compared with 9 in Japan, of which 8 were authorised in both countries. For all eight therapeutics, authorisation was obtained earlier in the USA, with an average difference of 4 months. The number of clinical studies submitted for authorisation was 1.0 in Japan and 4.0 in the USA. The data packages submitted for approval in the USA generally followed the standard structure for a typical application, including phase 1, 2 and 3 studies, whereas in Japan, phase 3 study data were often the primary focus.ConclusionCompared with the USA, fewer therapeutics were approved in Japan, and the approvals occurred later. Most therapeutics approved in Japan had previously received EUA in the USA, with Japanese approvals largely dependent on participation in large-scale global studies and the US review schedule. Application data in Japan were primarily based on data from large-scale global studies that had been submitted for the US application; the observed delay in approval was considered attributable to the time required for application preparation. In preparation for future pandemics, it will be necessary to establish systems that take these characteristics into account.

Introduction

The clinical development of COVID-19 therapeutics was characterised by a high level of urgency. Three months after the WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC),1 the first therapeutic agents became available for use in Europe, the USA and Japan.2 One effective mechanism that facilitated the early availability of these therapeutics was the Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) system implemented in the USA. Under Section 564 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) can issue an EUA for unapproved drugs or expand the indications of approved drugs based on limited data, provided that the estimated benefits outweigh the risks in emergency situations, even if clinical trials are still ongoing.3 4 The European Union has a similar Compassionate Use Opinion and Conditional Marketing Authorization system, while Japan uses Special Approval and Emergency Approval mechanisms.2 The EU’s Compassionate Use Opinion allows for the use of investigational drugs that have shown preliminary efficacy in clinical trials when urgent treatment is required. Conditional Marketing Authorization is granted when formal data are not yet available, but the therapeutic benefits are clear, enabling early market entry to address urgent medical needs, contingent upon the subsequent submission of complete data. Japan’s Special Approval system allows the use of unapproved drugs in emergencies where public health is at significant risk, provided no alternative treatments are available and the drug has already been approved for use in countries with comparable regulatory standards. The Emergency Approval system eliminates the requirement for prior approval of a drug in other countries, allowing provisional acceptance of its efficacy based on estimations.5

By leveraging these systems, multiple therapeutics have been approved or authorised in each region during the 38-month PHEIC declaration period.6 Typically, the research and development process for new drugs, from clinical trials to approval, requires nearly 10 years to complete.7 Therefore, the rapid pace of clinical development during the COVID-19 pandemic is noteworthy. This suggests that, in response to one of the most serious recent public health crises, strategies for regulation and development involved complex risk-taking behaviours that would rarely occur in ordinary situations.6 However, a closer examination revealed both disparities in the availability of therapeutics across regions and differences in the timing of their approval, suggesting that these delays may have stemmed not from differences in therapeutic necessity but from challenges in clinical trial execution and regulatory submission processes.

The ‘100 Days Mission’, proposed during the G7 Summit in June 2021 in the UK, outlined an international goal to have rapid diagnostics, vaccines and therapeutics for future pandemics available within 100 days of a WHO PHEIC declaration.8 This initiative aimed to address the challenges exposed by the COVID-19 pandemic, proposing that the rapid availability of diagnostics, vaccines and therapeutics (medical countermeasures) would reduce global infections and deaths, allowing the earlier control of pandemics. As infectious diseases can spread globally, swift and widespread availability of therapeutic options is crucial. The disparity in access to therapeutics between major drug-developing regions and other areas is particularly significant in this context.

This study focused on the clinical development of COVID-19 therapeutics during the PHEIC declaration period, comparing the USA, which represents a major drug-developing region, with Japan, which represents a region outside the core drug development countries. This study aimed to examine the clinical development timelines, clarify the characteristics of submission data packages and explore the potential influence of regulatory elements and clinical study infrastructure including medical institutions’ clinical study systems and pharmaceutical companies’ capabilities to accommodate global studies.

The USA and Japan have established systems that enable clinical development in accordance with ICH GCP, with the USA being home to numerous megapharmaceutical companies with global drug development capabilities. The relatively early onset of the COVID-19 outbreak in the USA created an urgent need for therapeutic development; therefore, the USA emerged as a primary site for clinical trials for various therapeutics. Japan, on the other hand, has established drug development capabilities; however, a significant proportion of the pharmaceuticals available in its market are imported. Additionally, since the large-scale COVID-19 outbreak in Japan had a relatively late onset, its urgent need for therapeutic development was heightened by outbreaks in neighbouring countries.

Based on this comparison, we sought to propose strategies to ensure the efficient availability of therapeutics in regions beyond major clinical trial countries, in preparation for future pandemics.

Materials and methods

This was a cross-sectional study that employed a quantitative approach.

Identification of COVID-19 therapeutics available in the USA and Japan

We defined the state in which therapeutics became available for use during the pandemic as the issuance of EUA in the USA and as approvals including special approval and emergency approval in Japan.

We identified COVID-19 therapeutics for which EUAs were issued in the USA during the PHEIC declaration period (30 January 2020 to 5 May 2023)1 9 from the FDA website.10 We also identified the COVID-19 therapeutics approved in Japan during the same period using the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency website.11 We examined the category, route of administration, therapeutic target, therapeutic availability date and drug characteristics for each identified therapeutic.

Analysis of submitted studies and data

For therapeutics authorised in the USA and Japan, we reviewed the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) review reports1226 in the USA and the review reports in Japan2735 to identify the submitted clinical trials and clinical study registration numbers. We obtained information on study phases, designs, objectives, endpoints and participating countries from the clinical trial registration such as ClinicalTrials.gov.36 37 Data organisation and calculation of summary statistics, such as the average number of studies submitted by drug category, were performed using Excel.

Analysis of clinical development timelines

For therapeutics authorised in the USA and Japan, we analysed the clinical development timelines by reviewing the clinical trial registration,36 37 EUA submission, and EUA issuance dates for the USA, as well as the submission and approval dates for Japan. We examined the timeframe from the PHEIC declaration to the approval date for each product and further broke down the process into its constituent stages. The durations were summarised in Excel and illustrated by product for each country. For each stage, we also calculated the differences in days between the USA and Japan. Median, mean and SD were calculated using Excel.

Results

During the COVID-19 pandemic, 14 therapeutics were available in the USA: 5 anti-inflammatory agents, 3 antivirals and 6 neutralising antibodies. Conversely, in Japan, nine therapeutics were available: two anti-inflammatory agents, four antivirals and three neutralising antibodies, indicating that more therapeutics were available in the USA.

The median number of clinical studies included in the submission data was greater in the USA than in Japan, regardless of whether the products had been previously approved for other indications. Specifically, in the USA, the median was 1.5 for drugs approved for other indications, 5.0 for drugs not approved but under clinical studies for other indications and 6.0 for new molecular entities. Meanwhile, in Japan, the corresponding medians were 1.0, 3.0 and 1.5, respectively.

The median duration of each process in the clinical development period also differed between the two countries. In the USA, the median number of days was 113.0 for the time from the PHEIC declaration to the first clinical study registration (study start-up period), 238.5 for the clinical study period, and 62.5 for the review period, totalling 496.5 days. In contrast, the corresponding durations were 249.0, 213.0 and 23.0 days, respectively, totalling 650.0 days, indicating a longer time to availability in Japan.

Among anti-inflammatory agents, the following five products were identified: baricitinib, Propofol-Lipuro 1%, tocilizumab, anakinra and vilobelimab. All five products were authorised for use in the USA, whereas only baricitinib and tocilizumab were approved in Japan. All of these products were either previously approved or were evaluated in ongoing clinical trials for other indications.

Antiviral agents included remdesivir, molnupiravir, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir and ensitrelvir. In the USA, three of these products were authorised for use, with the exception of ensitrelvir, whereas all four were available for use in Japan. Remdesivir did not have approval but was undergoing evaluation in clinical trials for other indications at the onset of the pandemic. The remaining drugs, molnupiravir, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir and ensitrelvir, were identified as new active therapeutics.

Six neutralising antibodies were identified: bamlanivimab, casirivimab/imdevimab, bamlanivimab/etesevimab, sotrovimab, tixagevimab/cilgavimab and bebtelovimab, all of which were new active entities. All six agents received authorisation in the USA, whereas three agents—casirivimab/imdevimab, sotrovimab and tixagevimab/cilgavimab—were available for use in Japan.

14 antiviral products received EUA in the USA, whereas 9 were approved for use in Japan, 8 of which were available in both countries. All products approved for use in Japan, except ensitrelvir, had previously obtained EUA in the USA. Furthermore, tixagevimab/cilgavimab was authorised only for prevention in the USA, whereas it was approved for both prevention and treatment in Japan. In Japan, baricitinib and tocilizumab, which were previously approved for other indications, received standard approval, whereas all other agents were submitted for approval of new applications. Remdesivir, casirivimab/imdevimab, sotrovimab, tixagevimab/cilgavimab, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir and molnupiravir, which had EUA in the USA, were granted special approval, whereas ensitrelvir received emergency approval.

Products that were already approved for other indications in the USA included baricitinib, Propofol-Lipuro 1%, tocilizumab and anakinra, with one to four submitted trials. In Japan, baricitinib and tocilizumab were the two products for which applications were submitted, with each having one large-scale global study and one local study conducted in Japan included in the submission. The two products that were not approved but were under clinical development for other indications were remdesivir and vilobelimab, the latter of which had not been submitted for approval in Japan. Nine studies were submitted for remdesivir, including those for other indications. Of these, only three trials were conducted specifically for COVID-19 after the PHEIC declaration. In Japan, two of the three studies conducted after the PHEIC declaration were submitted.

Regarding new active therapeutics, eight products were submitted in the USA (bamlanivimab, casirivimab/imdevimab, bamlanivimab/etesevimab, sotrovimab, tixagevimab/cilgavimab, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, molnupiravir and bebtelovimab), with 1–12 clinical studies submitted per product. In Japan, five of these products (excluding bamlanivimab, bamlanivimab/etesevimab and bebtelovimab) were submitted, accompanied by one to five clinical studies for each. In Japan, for many products, only a subset of the studies conducted in the USA was submitted, regardless of their approval or availability of clinical study data for other indications, resulting in a higher number of submitted trials in the USA. In the application data for Japan, global study data were used in the submission packages of all drugs except tocilizumab. Tocilizumab was not included in a global study; thus, only local studies were submitted as evaluation studies. However, a global study was submitted as reference data. For products for which global studies did not include Japanese data, a separate study targeting Japanese participants was conducted. Among the nine drugs, Japanese data were not included only in the submission of sotrovimab. Three products, tocilizumab, casirivimab/imdevimab and tixagevimab/cilgavimab, were used in a local study conducted solely in Japan.

Data submitted as part of the application packages for COVID-19 therapeutics

Tables6 7 present the data included in the application data packages for the eight therapeutics that became available in both the USA and Japan, categorised by prior approval or presence of clinical trials for other indications, with detailed information for each country. Baricitinib and tocilizumab, previously approved for other indications, were evaluated primarily based on clinical study data involving large patient populations in the USA and Japan. However, in Japan, tocilizumab was assessed in a single-arm phase 3 study conducted solely within the country. Pharmacokinetic parameters were examined in the study population and compared with global study data, which were submitted as supporting documentation. For remdesivir, which had not been approved but was in ongoing clinical development for other indications related to Ebola haemorrhagic fever, trial results were submitted in the USA from studies involving healthy adults, a small number of patients, and placebo-controlled comparative trials. In contrast, in Japan, only results from a placebo-controlled comparative trial were submitted.

Discussion

This study compared the clinical development of COVID-19 therapeutics in the USA and Japan, highlighting the differences between regions where clinical development was advanced and those where it was not. The accelerated development of COVID-19 therapeutics has provided valuable insights; however, no systematic evaluation of regional differences in therapeutic development timelines and access has been undertaken to date.

Drugs available for use

During the PHEIC declaration, 14 drugs became available for use in the USA, compared with only nine in Japan, highlighting the smaller number of approvals in Japan. All products that became available in the USA and Japan were recommended by the WHO therapeutic guidelines. However, some recommendations were later restricted or withdrawn due to the emergence of circulating variants.40 Except for ensitrelvir, all products approved in Japan had earlier undergone clinical development in the USA. As drugs are typically submitted for approval earlier in countries where clinical studies are conducted, establishing systems that facilitate the rapid and efficient acceptance of drugs developed and trialled in other regions is crucial for regions not involved in drug development. For products such as remdesivir, which underwent large-scale government-led clinical trials, reliable channels must be established that allow timely access to information. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the WHO facilitated the integration and sharing of such data; however, obtaining information from the sources and regulatory authorities at the appropriate times remains essential. Clarifying the role of specialised institutions in determining whether participation in clinical studies is warranted is also necessary. Additionally, establishing an infrastructure that allows the smooth participation of study sites in global studies during emergencies, creating a list of study-capable sites and defining the roles of related institutions in the procedures for clinical study participation would be beneficial.

Clinical development period for each product

The total duration (D) until a drug becomes available for use encompasses multiple stages. In this study, all these stages of the application procedure were compared between the USA and Japan.

Study start-up period (A)

The average time of study start-up period (A) in Japan was approximately 5 months longer than that in the USA, indicating that clinical development for many products was initiated earlier in the USA. Japan is believed to have had limited opportunities for participation in early-phase studies, making it difficult to definitively determine when pharmaceutical companies decided to start clinical development in Japan based solely on this result. As identical studies were submitted as pivotal studies for many products in both the USA and Japan, and the timing for obtaining the necessary data for submission was the same in both countries, it can be inferred that the study start-up period (A) was not directly related to the time until the products became available (clinical development period (D)). While submitting and receiving approval based solely on clinical study results conducted in other regions is possible, promptly participating in the pivotal clinical studies that serve as primary evaluation materials for submission is critical as they facilitate the submission and approval processes.

Most of the analysed studies were registered in clinical study registries near the time of initiation, indicating a certain degree of transparency. During public health emergencies, timely and accurate disclosure of clinical trial information is important.

Clinical study period (B)

Despite the variability across products, the overall time was longer on average in Japan. The duration of obtaining clinical study data (B) refers to the period from the initiation of the first clinical study to the submission of the application. This period includes the time required to acquire clinical study data for the application and that needed to prepare the application documents. For many products, the pivotal studies submitted as part of the application were identical in the USA and Japan; therefore, differences in this timeframe can be attributed to the time required for application preparation after obtaining clinical study data. Additionally, implementing local clinical studies in Japan has sometimes contributed to delays in approval timelines.

Review period (C)

In Japan, the timelines of review period (C) were shorter than those in the USA for a greater number of products. For all products approved in both the USA and Japan, the approval timelines were consistently longer in Japan, which allowed Japan to reference the review content from the USA, potentially contributing to the efficiency of the review process. Furthermore, the increased communication between the applicants and regulatory authorities prior to submission may have facilitated a smoother review process.

Clinical development period (D)

The average clinical development period (D) was over 4 months longer in Japan. The study start-up period (A) did not affect the approval timeline, with the review period (C) being shorter in Japan. This indicates that the delay was primarily attributed to later submission dates in Japan. The 4-month delay in Japanese approval can be attributed to the time required for application preparation after completing the clinical study period (B), or to the time required to collect data from Japanese sources.

In Japan, six products (remdesivir, casirivimab/imdevimab, sotrovimab, tixagevimab/cilgavimab, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir and molnupiravir) received special approval. One of the requirements for special approval included prior approval of the drug in other regions, which contributed to a delayed approval in Japan compared with that in the USA. However, with the implementation of the emergency approval system on 20 May 2022, the requirement for prior approval in other regions was eliminated, thereby addressing the issue. To further shorten the application preparation period, accepting application documents submitted to the regulatory authorities of other regions as part of the submission may also prove effective. However, to avoid delays in review and approval due to language or format differences in such documents, establishing a streamlined review system, including simplified review reports, is essential. Additionally, defining standard conditions for flexible review measures and facilitating information sharing among regulatory authorities from the application preparation stage onward is advisable to enhance review efficiency and shorten review timelines. For instance, in the case of remdesivir, for which simultaneous application and approval were achieved in the USA and Japan, benefited from collaborative efforts between regulatory authorities, allowing the product to be made available in multiple regions, including the EU, the USA and Japan, within 100 d of the PHEIC declaration.2

In Japan, clinical study applications generally require data on Japanese participants. Initially, we hypothesised that Japan would require additional data specific to its regulatory systems, which might explain delays in approval. While there are some therapeutics, such as casirivimab/imdevimab, where Japan did not have the opportunity to participate in pivotal global studies, which required a local Japanese study, contributing to delays in approval timelines, this example was not typical. Some products, such as propofol, anakinra and vilobelimab, underwent pivotal clinical studies conducted in only certain regions, resulting in authorisation for use in those specific regions. Several factors may explain why pivotal global studies are conducted in only limited regions. These include a preference for prioritising the speed of study initiation over expanding the participating regions, resource limitations faced by sponsors, and considerations that the prevalence of the disease was not sufficiently significant to warrant study participation. Additionally, variations in circulating strains across regions could complicate the interpretation of trial results. To address these issues, first, exploring the possibilities for participation in pivotal studies is essential. Developing an international framework that enables the rapid deployment of large-scale global studies, even for sponsors with limited capital or less experience, would be beneficial. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the WHO collected and disseminated information not only on candidates in the clinical development phase but also in the preclinical stage. Building a mechanism to concurrently transition this information into large-scale global studies would be advantageous. While implementing government-led large-scale global studies during the COVID-19 pandemic primarily involved agents with clinical study data for other indications, having a framework that allows for the important new active entities to transition into government-led clinical studies would be beneficial.

Second, regarding the variability in the timing of outbreaks across different regions, countries should establish regulations that allow for applications based solely on data from other regions when a significant concern exists about the influx of outbreaks or when heightened vigilance is warranted owing to the severity of the disease. For instance, in Japan, a general requirement for Japanese data in applications is imposed. Although a pathway for approval may exist based on limited clinical study data through a conditional early approval system, specifying that applications for drugs targeting infectious diseases prevalent in other regions could be submitted using data from other regions would be beneficial. Moreover, proactive measures taken before the introduction of infections into a region, such as accepting application documents from other regions and clarifying the conditions for providing incentives, could enhance preparedness. Disseminating information about these responses to regions beyond the local jurisdiction would also be advantageous.

Application data packages

In many cases, both countries submitted the same study data to their respective regulatory authorities, effectively sharing data to create common datasets for regulatory submissions. Data packages submitted for approval in the USA generally followed the standard structure for a typical application. In contrast, in Japan, phase 3 study data were often the primary focus, with fewer submissions including studies on healthy volunteers or dose-finding studies. This applied not only to products that had been previously approved for other indications but also to products that had not been approved, those under clinical studies for other indications and new molecular entities. The delayed approval in Japan compared with that in the USA allowed Japan to benefit from reviewing the regulatory outcomes of other regions, which likely contributed to this difference.

This approach likely stems from the judgement that adhering to the usage precautions established by countries that had already approved the product would ensure a minimum level of safety. Additionally, given the urgency, prioritising post-marketing safety monitoring over waiting for comprehensive safety data from clinical studies may have been considered more beneficial. Leveraging regulatory reviews from countries with earlier drug development can facilitate both clinical studies and regulatory approval processes. Depending on the extent of the infectious disease spread and the risk of its introduction, simultaneous submission and approval may not always be necessary; a more flexible approach may be preferable.

Variations were observed when the application data packages for each product in Japan were reviewed. Some products had certain data requirements waived or accepted foreign application documents at the time of initial submission, while others included data from Japanese participants, but not all. In preparation for future needs, refining the mechanisms for sharing information between regulatory authorities to ensure responsiveness during emergencies and establishing platforms that facilitate smooth communication between regulators and applicants would be beneficial. Additionally, clearly defining the conditions for applying flexible measures would help reduce the burden on regulatory consultations during emergencies. As any system is likely to face challenges in responding to emergencies, such a system should be established before the next pandemic.

Limitations

For a medication to be made available for use in each region, obtaining regulatory approval alone is insufficient; the challenges related to manufacturing and distribution must also be addressed. However, this study focused primarily on the clinical development of therapeutics. Furthermore, this study addressed the initial applications and approvals of each medication for COVID-19 that became available in the USA and Japan, without delving into subsequent reports or changes to approval conditions. The individual efficacy and safety of each medication were not considered in this context. Despite these limitations, the data sources used in this study—namely, government agencies and official clinical study registries—are generally reliable and accurate. Moreover, restricting the analysis to products that received regulatory approval enhances the credibility of the findings.

Conclusion

During the COVID-19 pandemic, an unprecedented acceleration in clinical development occurred. However, the types and timelines of approved products varied across regions. Outside major trial-performing countries, the range of available drugs was limited, and the timing of their approval was often delayed. To respond effectively to a pandemic, treatments must be disseminated quickly and efficiently across a wide range of global regions. Future preparations should include establishing mechanisms for the global expansion of public institution-led clinical trials and enhancing information sharing between regulatory authorities prior to the application process. Additionally, clarifying the conditions for accepting data from regions with earlier development, as well as the criteria for accepting application materials and exemptions for submitted data from leading regions, could be effective. Critically assessing the individual regulatory flexibilities applied during the COVID-19 pandemic and refining the conditions under which such flexibilities may be implemented would also be beneficial.
