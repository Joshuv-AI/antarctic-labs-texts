Clinical study of area-decreasing technique and conventional post-closure technique for V-A ECMO weaning application: study protocol of a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Junchu Wei, Jing Zhao, Chenliang Pan, Juan Ma, Nan Bai, Kang Dong, Andong Lu, Ming Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTwo techniques are commonly used for the femoral artery puncture closure during decannulation in venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO): the conventional post-closure technique and the small-sheath technique, also referred to as the area-decreasing technique. To date, no standardised protocol has been established for femoral artery closure following V-A ECMO decannulation. This study aimed to determine whether the area-decreasing technique results in reduced arterial blood loss compared with the conventional post-closure technique.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, single-centre, open-label, randomised controlled trial. Introduction

Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) is being increasingly used in clinical practice.1,3 Ensuring safe and effective closure of the femoral artery during decannulation remains a critical concern for intensivists.

In recent decades, achieving haemostasis in the femoral artery during V-A ECMO decannulation has typically involved surgical repair or manual compression, both of which are associated with significant risk and complications.4,6 To address the limitations of these methods, minimally invasive percutaneous techniques for femoral artery closure have been developed.7 A prospective randomised study by Torsello et al demonstrated that, compared with conventional surgical repair, percutaneous closure using endovascular suture devices offers several advantages, including reduced operating time and earlier ambulation.8 With the advent of the Perclose ProGlide device (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL, USA), its safety and efficacy have become widely recognised.9,11 One study reported that the use of endovascular suture devices significantly enhances patient comfort, underscoring the technique’s clinical potential.6

The ProGlide-based pre-closure technique is a safe and effective method for arterial closure in V-A ECMO, with reported success rates ranging from 90.2% to 95% and lower limb complication rates between 5.9% and 11.3%.12,14 While this technique can shorten the time required for V-A ECMO decannulation, it also extends the operative duration at the time of ECMO initiation, increases the postoperative care demands and elevates the risk of suture-related infections. Notably, although it expedites decannulation, it complicates ECMO cannulation logistics and may increase perioperative morbidity.

To overcome these drawbacks, the Perclose ProGlide-based post-closure technique—referred to as the conventional post-closure method—was introduced. Preliminary research from the ECMO Centre of Lanzhou University First Hospital, along with corroborating evidence from other institutions, has demonstrated that the conventional post-closure technique using two Perclose ProGlide is a feasible and safe strategy for femoral artery closure during V-A ECMO decannulation.49 11 15,17 Although this approach has achieved wide acceptance due to its high success and low complication rates, it is consistently associated with significant intraoperative blood loss.16

In clinical practice, we identified a novel post-closure technique known as the small-sized sheath technique.9 18 This approach involves progressively reducing the size of the arterial defect so that each suture device closes a smaller segment of the puncture site, ultimately achieving complete closure. Accordingly, it is referred to as an area-decreasing technique. The method allows a staged reduction of the bleeding orifice during the procedure; by the time the first closure device is fully deployed (including knot tying and suture cutting), the bleeding site is already diminished by approximately half. In contrast, with the conventional post-closure technique, the size of the arterial opening remains unchanged after the first device is deployed, as the sutures are not tied until both devices have been placed. However, whether this intraoperative reduction in orifice size translates into a meaningful reduction in arterial blood loss has not been definitively established, as robust clinical evidence is still lacking.

The aim of this trial is to compare two ProGlide-based post-closure techniques—the conventional post-closure technique and the area-decreasing technique—in terms of their safety and efficacy for femoral artery closure in patients undergoing V-A ECMO decannulation.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This was a single-centre, prospective, randomised controlled trial. The control group undergoes the conventional post-closure technique, while the intervention group receives the area-decreasing technique, with a particular focus on evaluating the outcomes of the latter. The first participant was randomly assigned to one of the two techniques on 1 April 2024, and the final follow-up for the last enrolled patient is expected to be completed by 31 December 2025 (figure 1).

Study population

All patients received adequate anticoagulation with bivalirudin during V-A ECMO support. Coagulation status during ECMO is primarily monitored using activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), along with activated clotting time (ACT), D-dimer levels and other coagulation indices to guide dose adjustment. Considering the relatively hypocoagulable profile of the Chinese population, the APTT is maintained within a target range of 40–60 s, with concurrent monitoring to ensure relatively stable D-dimer, fibrinogen and prothrombin levels.

Patients aged ≥18 years are eligible for inclusion if they require V-A ECMO support due to cardiogenic shock of any aetiology or as assistance for high-risk percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and if they meet the following criteria: (1) fulfil the clinical weaning criteria and (2) are successfully randomised to receive one of the closure techniques. Patients were excluded if they did not undergo closure device placement due to economic constraints, treatment discontinuation or death during ECMO, or if their data were incomplete.

All participants provided written informed consent (online supplemental material S1). If a patient’s condition initially precluded informed consent, retrospective consent was obtained once their clinical condition improved. In cases of death, coma or severe brain dysfunction, consent was obtained from the patient’s close relatives. If informed consent could not be obtained, the patient’s data were deleted from the study database and excluded from the final analysis.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint is intraoperative arterial blood loss. Blood loss is assessed using the gauze weight method, calculated as follows: intraoperative arterial blood loss=weight of blood-soaked gauze – weight of dry gauze. Weight is expressed in grams (g), with 1.0 g considered equivalent to 1.0 mL of blood.

Secondary endpoints include efficacy measures such as immediate technical success rate, arterial suture time, in-hospital mortality and in-hospital lower limb-related complications after decannulation, as well as 30-day mortality and 30-day limb-related complications. Safety endpoints included the incidence of complications requiring surgical or interventional management and the rate of limb amputation.

The immediate technical success rate is defined as successful femoral artery closure without the need for adjunctive surgical or endovascular interventions and without visible pulsatile bleeding. Arterial suture time is defined as the time elapsed from the moment the puncture needle pierced the arterial conduit until the completion of the suture cutting from the second device, measured in minutes. Lower limb-related complications are defined as adverse events at the femoral artery cannulation site and include arterial thrombosis, pseudoaneurysm, arterial dissection, lymphatic fistula, major bleeding, groin infection and compartment syndrome.

Arterial thrombosis, pseudoaneurysm, arterial dissection and lymphatic fistula are defined based on duplex ultrasound findings of the lower limbs obtained after decannulation. Major bleeding is defined as a decrease in haemoglobin concentration of ≥30.0 g/L and/or the need for transfusion of three or more units of packed red blood cells.19 A groin infection is defined as the presence of redness, swelling, warmth and pain at the cannulation site following decannulation. Compartment syndrome is defined as impaired lower limb mobility combined with a positive passive stretch test after decannulation.

Interventions

The procedural steps for both techniques have been previously described in the literature.18 20 All procedures were performed by a three-member physician team: one performed percutaneous suturing, one assisted with the suturing process, and one was responsible for haemorrhage control. In addition, a nurse was tasked with removing the V-A ECMO cannula and reinfusing blood. All team members received standardised training and had prior experience with approximately 100 ECMO decannulation procedures using suture-based closure devices. Both techniques followed similar initial steps: disinfection of the cannulation site and catheter, clamping of the arterial catheter, cessation of the V-A ECMO pump, and execution of the suturing procedure (detailed descriptions are provided in the post-closure and area-decreasing sections). After both knots are tightened and the sutures trimmed using the knotter, haemostasis is assessed. If adequate, the guidewire is removed, and local pressure is applied to manage residual oozing. In cases of inadequate haemostasis, the procedure was deemed unsuccessful, and supplementary measures—such as manual compression or a third closure device—were used.

Post-closure technique

The physician inserted a puncture needle to directly access the proximal end of the arterial cannula. A second physician advanced a 0.035-inch coated guidewire and, after confirming correct positioning, removed the arterial cannula with nursing assistance. A third physician applied compression to the proximal femoral artery puncture site to control bleeding. The first ProGlide device was then deployed over the guidewire and rotated approximately 45° towards the patient’s right side, with the knot left untightened. The guidewire was reinserted, and the second ProGlide device was introduced and rotated about 45° towards the patient’s left side, creating an approximately 90° angle between both devices. Once both devices were in place, the knots were sequentially tightened, and the threads were trimmed using a knotter.

Area-decreasing technique

Compared with the conventional post-closure technique, the area-decreasing technique involves the use of an additional guidewire and an 8F sheath. At our ECMO centre, we routinely use an 8F sheath for this procedure. A physician uses two puncture needles to directly access the proximal end of the arterial cannula. A second physician advances two 0.035-inch coated guidewires, and on confirmation of correct positioning, the arterial cannula is removed with nursing assistance. A third physician applies proximal compression to the femoral artery puncture site to control bleeding. An 8F sheath is then introduced to one guidewire. The first ProGlide device is deployed into the femoral artery via the second guidewire, and the knot is tightened and trimmed following standard procedures. Subsequently, the 8F sheath is removed while the guidewire remains in place, and a second ProGlide device is deployed along the same guidewire. After deployment, the knot is tightened and trimmed.

The differences between the conventional post-closure technique and the area-decreasing technique are illustrated in figure 2.

The key differences between the conventional post-closure technique and the area-decreasing technique are outlined as follows. In the conventional post-closure technique: (A) a single guidewire (black arrow) is inserted into the femoral artery (red arrow); (B) the first closure device (blue arrow) is deployed along the guidewire; (C) the second closure device is placed at a 90° angle to the first; and (D) there is minimal change in the size of the bleeding orifice. In contrast, the area-reduction technique involves: (A) the insertion of two guidewires into the femoral artery; (B) deployment of the first closure device along one guidewire and simultaneous placement of an 8F sheath (purple arrow) along the other; (C) completion of knot tying and suture cutting with the first device, followed by sheath removal and deployment of the second closure device; and (D) a noticeable reduction in the bleeding orifice area.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using SAS V.9.0 software (Statistical Analysis System, Cary, NC, USA) based on the primary endpoint of this study: the difference in arterial blood loss during suturing. The clinical trial aimed to determine whether arterial blood loss in the intervention group would be significantly lower than in the control group. We retrospectively collected data from 26 patients who had previously undergone the conventional post-closure technique at our centre. Analysis of these data indicated that the mean blood loss in the control group was 70 ± 15 mL. Based on published literature and expert opinion, the target blood loss for the intervention group was estimated at 60 mL, yielding a projected between-group difference of 10 mL. Assuming homogeneity of variance between groups and a SD of 15, the sample size was calculated using a two-sample independent t-test. The calculation incorporated the pooled variance method, a power of 90%, and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, resulting in a required sample size of 49 participants per group. After adjusting for a 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was set at 110 participants.

Randomisation

Block randomisation was used in this trial. An independent statistician, not involved in any other aspect of the study, generated the random allocation sequence using SAS V.9.4 (or later). Participants were assigned to one of two groups in a 1:1, with 55 participants per group. The randomisation data were reproducible, and parameters such as random seed and block size were securely recorded. Once a participant signed the informed consent form and was confirmed to meet eligibility criteria, their randomisation code was retained even if they withdrew or were removed from the trial, regardless of intervention status. Participants who withdrew were not allowed to re-enrol.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be conducted using SPSS software (V.25.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data following a normal or approximately normal distribution are expressed as mean±SD, while non-normally distributed continuous data are expressed as median and interquartile range (M (Q1–Q3)). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages (N (%)). Kaplan–Meier analysis will be used to generate survival curves for the time to major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs). All tests will be two-tailed, with a p value <0.05 considered statistically significant. The Cox proportional hazards model will be used to estimate HRs, and logistic regression models will be used to calculate the ORs.

If needed, subgroup analyses will be performed based on cannula size, duration of arterial cannulation and bleeding status.

Safety consideration

The safety evaluation in this study primarily focused on procedure-related complications. The decannulation procedure employed here is routinely performed in daily clinical practice. Therefore, the risks associated with participation in this trial did not exceed those of standard care that patients would ordinarily receive. Throughout the study period, all adverse events were documented and managed in accordance with established clinical protocols.

Trial status

Recruitment commenced in the second quarter of 2024, and 36% of the target sample size has been enrolled to date. The study is expected to reach its recruitment target of 110 participants by the fourth quarter of 2025. Currently, no interim or final analysis has been planned.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design or conduct of this study.

Discussion

This prospective randomised controlled interventional trial was designed to provide clinicians with robust evidence to guide the selection of suture techniques for femoral artery closure following V-A ECMO support. The findings are expected to clarify whether arterial blood loss differs significantly between the area-decreasing technique and the conventional post-closure technique.

Based on the demonstrated efficacy of the ProGlide device, it can be applied in multiple clinical settings. The pre-closure technique was the first to be proposed; this involves placing the suture before initiating V-A ECMO. However, the conventional post-closure technique is considered more appropriate for acutely ill patients requiring urgent ECMO initiation. The reported success rate of the conventional post-closure technique ranges from 84% to 100%, while the incidence of lower-limb complications ranges from 14.3% to 19.1%. This post-closure approach—where the suture is deployed at the time of V-A ECMO withdrawal—has been shown to be both safe and effective for arterial closure.49 11 15,17

Two key considerations should be noted regarding the ProGlide-based post-closure technique.

There is a risk of insufficient capture of the anterior arterial wall, which may hinder the successful closure of large-bore access sites during decannulation. The Perclose system indicates arterial defects ranging from 8F to 21F, while arterial cannulas used in V-A ECMO typically range from 15F to 19F, making closure feasible. In fact, one study has shown that dual ProGlide devices can close even larger defects ranging from 22F to 26F.21 Furthermore, due to the elastic recoil of the femoral artery on cannula removal, the arterial defect size naturally decreases. Based on these anatomic and procedural considerations, the use of two ProGlide devices is considered sufficient for closure in most V-A ECMO cases.

To address the challenge of closing large arterial defects, Maeno et al developed a small-sized sheath technique.18 This method utilises a dual-guidewire approach. One guidewire is placed inside a sheath, which is then used to laterally retract the perforated arterial wall, stretch the defect and approximate the vessel edges. The second guidewire is introduced through the suture device, enabling lock and trimming. Finally, the sheath is removed, and the second suture device is deployed. This technique has proven effective for closing large arterial defects.

The ProGlide procedure may result in greater intraoperative arterial bleeding. When a sheath is inserted during the operation, as in the small-sheath technique, it reduces the breach area, thereby limiting arterial blood loss.

In conclusion, this prospective clinical trial was designed to provide evidence supporting the selection of the optimal femoral artery closure technique for V-A ECMO decannulation. The area-decreasing technique demonstrates success rates and postoperative complication profiles comparable to those of the conventional post-closure technique but with reduced arterial blood loss. It should be recommended as the preferred method for V-A ECMO decannulation. The conventional post-closure technique may still be reserved for specific clinical scenarios or future interventions. Ultimately, our findings are intended to support clinicians in evaluating the relative benefits and risks of the area-decreasing vs conventional post-closure techniques.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University (LDYYLL2024-36) on 1 February 2024 and conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and disseminated to the media and the public.
