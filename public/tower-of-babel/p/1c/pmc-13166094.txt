Risk of skin and subcutaneous diseases in paediatric HIV-exposed uninfected individuals in the USA
Authors: Ghida EL Banna, Sydney Wishner, Natalie Baker, Bryan L Peacker, Natalie Braun, Kevin Sheng-Kai Ma, Steven T Chen, Lisa M Bebell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
HIV-exposed uninfected (HEU) children experience increased early-life morbidity, but dermatological outcomes have not been well studied. In this retrospective cohort study of 262 HEU individuals matched to 499 HIV-unexposed uninfected controls, HEU participants had significantly higher prevalence of candidiasis, eczema, seborrhoeic dermatitis and skin/soft tissue infections. These findings highlight dermatological disease as an under-recognized contributor to HEU morbidity and underscore the need to explore underlying mechanisms.

Dear Editor, HIV-exposed uninfected (HEU) individuals represent a growing population globally. Despite remaining HIV-uninfected, HEU children experience disproportionately poorer health outcomes in infancy and childhood, including increased morbidity and mortality from severe infections,1,2 although knowledge of the underlying mechanisms is limited. Little is also known about their dermatological health.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study within the Mass General Brigham healthcare system (Boston, MA, USA) from 1995 to 2024. Using the Research Patient Data Repository, we identified 262 HEU individuals, confirmed by maternal HIV diagnosis and manual chart review, and matched them 1 : 2 to 499 HIV-unexposed uninfected (HUU) controls by age, sex, race/ethnicity and zip code (a sociodemographic measure). Dermatological diagnoses were extracted using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9th and 10th edition codes and validated by manual chart review. We analysed skin conditions individually and calculated relative risks (RRs) comparing HEU with HUU individuals using χ2 and two-tailed independent sample t-tests. We controlled the false discovery rate using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. In exploratory logistic regression analyses, we examined relationships between maternal HIV parameters – including CD4+ T-cell count, viral load, infection duration and antiretroviral therapy (ART) – and skin disease, adjusting for maternal age at delivery, gestational age and delivery mode.

The median [interquartile range (IQR)] age was 9 (4–18) years for HEU and 10 (4.5–19) years for HUU individuals; sex and racial/ethnic distribution were similar. HEU children were more frequently delivered preterm (30.7% vs. 15.3%) and by Caesarean (51.5% vs. 21.4%). The median (IQR) follow-up time was comparable [2.15 (0.02–9.8) for HEU vs. 1.69 (0.01–8.6) years for HUU; P = 0.11].

At delivery, the mean (SD) HEU maternal age was 31.6 (6.1) years, with a median (IQR) HIV infection duration of 8.9 (2.2–8.7) years. In the first pregnancy trimester, 186 (71.0%) had HIV viral load ≤ 400 copies mL–1, and 19 (7.3%) had no viral load documented. Of 228 (87.0%) mothers with documented CD4+ T-cell counts, 19 (7.3%) had < 200, 42 (16.0%) had 200–349, 37 (14.1%) had 350–500 and 130 (49.6%) had > 500 cells mm–3.

HEU individuals had significantly higher risks of mucocutaneous candidiasis [RR 2.65; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.67–4.21], seborrhoeic dermatitis (RR 2.85; 95% CI 1.30–6.27), skin and soft tissue infection (RR 1.76; 95% CI 1.15–2.70), contact dermatitis and other eczema (RR 1.59; 95% CI 1.11–2.29) and atopic dermatitis (RR 1.57; 95% CI 1.09–2.24) than HUU individuals. There were no significant differences in risk of acne, urticaria, viral exanthems, molluscum contagiosum, herpes simplex virus, warts, scabies or nonspecific rashes between the groups (Table 1). In a logistic regression model restricted to HEU individuals, maternal viral suppression during pregnancy was associated with lower odds of any offspring skin disease (odds ratio 0.85; 95% CI 0.73–0.99). Maternal CD4+ T-cell count, ART regimen and HIV infection duration were not associated with dermatological outcomes.

Our findings extend the evidence of adverse health outcomes in HEU children to dermatological disease. The observed pattern of dermatological disease suggests phenotype-specific immune alterations. Our results align with a Zimbabwean HEU cohort that observed more skin infections and oral thrush,3 although another US cohort study found no increased risk of skin and soft tissue infections.1 Increased susceptibility to candidal and bacterial infections may reflect altered innate immunity and T-cell responses.4–6 The increased prevalence of eczematous conditions may reflect T-helper 2-skewed immunity, which has been reported in HEU individuals and linked to increased atopy. This shift could also explain the increased atopic dermatitis risk in our cohort.4 Increased risk of candidiasis and seborrhoeic dermatitis in HEU individuals suggest that early detection and treatment may be beneficial for candida and yeast-related skin conditions.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, follow-up time was relatively short, potentially underestimating the risk of conditions developing later in adolescence. Secondly, reliance on ICD coding may bias diagnosis ascertainment. Thirdly, as 97% of HEU individuals were exposed to both maternal HIV and ART peripartum, we cannot disentangle their independent effects. Fourthly, our study population was drawn from a US tertiary healthcare system, limiting generalizability. Fifthly, although we used zip code and self-reported race/ethnicity to adjust for socioeconomic factors, residual confounding could remain. Lastly, while our results are statistically significant, effect sizes were moderate. However, even modest RR increases for these common childhood conditions may translate into clinically meaningful absolute differences in this growing population.

Despite these limitations, we highlight dermatological manifestations as an under-recognized morbidity in HEU individuals. Dermatologists and paediatricians should be aware of this potential risk, particularly for HEU children with recurrent dermatitis or infections. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify persistence of these skin conditions, and mechanistic research is needed to define the immunological pathways by which perinatal HIV exposure shapes cutaneous health.
