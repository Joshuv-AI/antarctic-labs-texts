Effect of organic material addition combined with tillage systems on organic carbon components and microbial communities in corn-fields of North East China
Authors: Qiulai Song, Yu Gao, Yu Sun, Xiannan Zeng, Quanxi Liang, Manli Wang, Yanjiang Feng, Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic material return (OMAR) and tillage systems are crucial agricultural management practices that govern soil microbial properties and organic carbon (OC) content, thereby fundamentally determining soil fertility. However, the precise mechanisms by which these practices mediate microbial activity to enhance distinct OC fractions remain poorly understood. Through a field experiment, we systematically evaluated changes in OC components and soil microbial properties under four treatments (CT, CTS, NTS, and BC). Results indicated that, compared to the CT treatment, BC significantly increased the concentrations of total organic carbon (TOC), light fraction organic carbon (LFOC), and particulate organic carbon (POC) by 181.9%, 1649.4%, and 2778.6%, respectively. Conversely, NTS and CTS increased the content of labile organic carbon (LOC) by 225.6% and 206.6%, and dissolved organic carbon (DOC) by 9.7% and 21.9%. Furthermore, OMAR significantly altered microbial α-diversity and community structure, driving a proportional increase that favored copiotrophic bacteria, such as Bacteroidetes (26.0%-209.4%), Firmicutes (90.5%-268.4%), and Actinobacteria (2.2%-52.1%), while concomitantly depleting oligotrophic K-strategists, including Alphaproteobacteria (24.9%-39.4%) and Acidobacteria (2.9%-34.2%). Random forest modeling revealed that NTS drove LOC accumulation (explaining 32.95% of the variance) primarily by stimulating Actinobacteria, Ruminococcaceae_UCG-013, Romboutsia, Parasutterella, and Candidatus_Pelagibacter. In contrast, BC maximized TOC (73.51%), LFOC (77.77%), and POC (74.02%) accumulation by significantly boosting the abundances of Bacteroidetes, Leptospirillum, Filimonas, Dyadobacter, and Anaerolinea. This investigation elucidates the mechanistic linkages between microbial taxonomic shifts and specific OC fraction dynamics under varying OMAR and tillage regimes, providing critical insights for optimizing carbon sequestration and sustaining long-term soil fertility in agricultural ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56399-4.

Introduction

Enhancing soil fertility and optimizing carbon (C) sequestration are paramount to achieving global Sustainable Development Goals, particularly the mandate to secure soil health while providing plants with adequate, balanced nutrition1. Among the foremost contemporary challenges is satisfying the escalating dietary demands of a burgeoning global population amid increasingly erratic climatic extremes, all while minimizing reliance on external synthetic inputs and mitigating environmental degradation2.

Conventional tillage physically degrades soil structure via compaction3, and the exclusive application of inorganic fertilizers frequently fails to restore or sustain adequate soil organic matter (SOM) pools4. Consequently, contemporary soil health paradigms are pivoting from strict reliance on synthetic agrochemicals toward the integration of organic amendments (e.g., straw incorporation and biochar) and conservation tillage regimes, aiming to rehabilitate low-fertility and degraded agricultural soils5. The incorporation of exogenous organic materials (OMAR) fundamentally bolsters direct soil C inputs6, serving as a cornerstone for SOM stabilization. Extensive literature has documented that straw return enhances soil aggregation, augments topsoil C storage, and counteracts the deleterious effects of intensive chemical fertilization7,8. Concurrently, biochar amendments are universally recognized for their profound efficacy in agricultural soil C sequestration, greenhouse gas mitigation, and overall agronomic enhancement9–11. Furthermore, integrating no-till practices with straw mulching can match or exceed conventional tillage yields while generating superior ecosystem services2,12.

While previous studies have extensively cataloged the general effects of straw return and biochar on overall SOC fractions, as well as broad microbial community shifts under various tillage practices13, a critical knowledge gap remains. Most existing research relies heavily on site-specific phenomenological observations, providing limited mechanistic insight into how distinct tillage systems and OMAR synergistically dictate underground biological pathways. Specifically, the precise microbial mediation pathways—how distinct organic inputs selectively drive specific bacterial life-history strategies to regulate discrete active carbon pools (e.g., dissolved, labile, and particulate organic carbon)—remain poorly elucidated14.

Soil microbial communities typically participate in the decomposition of exogenous organic residues and serve as key mediators for OC sequestration through the mineralization and immobilization of SOM15. The decomposition of externally supplied OC releases substantial amounts of C that alter the activity of bacterial taxa16, potentially influencing microbial functions associated with carbon metabolism17. The addition of OMA such as straw supplements a rich C and nitrogen (N) matrix, potentially supporting the growth, activity, and succession of soil microbial communities18. The response of soil microbial communities to the quantity and quality of exogenous substrates determines the extent and direction of microbial succession—from opportunistic microorganisms that prioritize unstable resources to slow-growing microbes capable of consuming recalcitrant components19,20. However, the changes in microbial community structure and composition mediated by exogenous OM, along with their effects on OC components, have not been thoroughly investigated.

SOC may originate from certain low-molecular-weight organic compounds produced by microbial decomposition and synthesis21, The addition of various composite carbon sources may induce alterations in these organic compounds and influence SOC composition, incorporating both unstable and persistent OC22. High-quality SOC provides substrates that are readily available for microbial degradation23, thereby increasing its own relative importance24. The chemical changes in SOC induced by exogenous OM are associated with alterations in bacterial communities and soil biological properties, and are largely controlled by the quality and quantity of OC25. Dissolved organic carbon (DOC), labile organic carbon (LOC), particulate organic carbon (POC), light fraction organic carbon (LFOC), microbial biomass organic carbon (MBC), and a portion of particulate organic carbon (POC) constitute the primary components of active OC26,27. They are more sensitive to environmental changes than other types of OC28. However, different SOC components exhibit inconsistent responses to environmental changes29, which may be related to the incorporation of different carbon sources and the activity of specific microbial groups mediated by tillage practices.

To transcend site-specific observations and establish broader mechanistic insights, we deploy advanced predictive modeling to robustly quantify the direct contributions of specific microbial taxa to distinct SOC fractions. We hypothesize that: (i) different OMAR types (labile crop residues versus recalcitrant biochar), when combined with specific tillage regimes, exert distinct selective pressures that assemble soil microbiomes based on specific substrate utilization capabilities; and (ii) these specifically assembled microbial communities serve as the primary mechanistic drivers that differentially partition carbon into active SOC pools (e.g., DOC, LOC, POC, LFOC). To rigorously test this hypothesis, we conducted field experiments evaluating conventional plough tillage without straw return (CT), plough tillage with corn straw return (CTS), no-till with corn straw mulching (NTS), and plough tillage with biochar incorporation (BC). By mapping the intricate associations between microbial structural diversity and sensitive SOC fractions, this investigation elucidates the precise microbial pathways mediating C dynamics. Ultimately, this study provides a robust, predictive conceptual framework to optimize C storage strategies and sustain soil fertility across diverse agricultural ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The experiment was conducted at the Minzhu Demonstration Area of the Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Located in Harbin City, Heilongjiang Province, Northeast China (45°50′N, 126°50′E) (Fig. S1), the site features a cold-temperate continental climate with distinct seasonal variations. Spring is windy with little rainfall, summer is warm and rainy, autumn is short, and winter is cold and dry. The annual average temperature is 3.6 °C, the frost-free period is 135 days, the accumulated temperature ≥ 10 °C is 2780 °C, and the annual average precipitation is 500 mm. The soil is classified as Phaeozems30. The region follows a single-crop-per-year system, with dryland farming primarily characterized by continuous corn cropping. The soil type is black soil.

Experimental design

The experiment commenced in 2015, with all treatments conducted on concrete-paved plots measuring 1.0 m × 1.0 m. Each plot was filled with approximately 50 cm of topsoil from a field that had been continuously cultivated with corn. The soil is black earth with the following base fertility values: OM 18.26 g kg− 1, NH₄⁺-N 56.43 mg kg− 1, NO₃⁻-N 36.85 mg kg− 1, available phosphorus (P) 41.95 mg kg− 1, and available potassium (K) 132.00 mg kg− 1. For the NTS treatment, 1 kg of corn straw (10,000 kg ha− 1) was shredded and used to cover the soil surface for overwintering. Direct no-till seeding was subsequently performed on the reconstructed ridges retaining straw and stubble. For the CTS treatment, shredded straw (10,000 kg ha− 1) was incorporated into the soil at a depth of 20–25 cm. For the BC treatment, 0.35 kg of biochar (3,500 kg ha− 1) was thoroughly mixed with the top 20 cm of soil. For the CT treatment, the straw from the fields was removed after corn harvest and the land was plowed. At sowing (early May), chemical fertilizers were applied to all plots to simulate standard local agricultural practices, ensuring basal crop nutrient requirements were met. The plots were maintained under rainfed conditions without supplemental irrigation.

Nutrient content of corn stover used in the experiment: Total C 451.23 g kg⁻¹, total N 11.23 g kg⁻¹, total P 3.45 g kg⁻¹, total K 21.38 g kg⁻¹; Biochar particle size: 1.5–2.0 mm, pH 9.23, containing total N 15.3 g kg⁻¹, total P 7.8 g kg⁻¹, and total K 16.8 g kg⁻¹.

Plots were arranged in randomized blocks with three replications per treatment, planting the corn variety Xianyu 335. At sowing, each plot received 7.5 g (75 kg ha⁻¹) of urea (N: 46%), 15 g (150 kg ha⁻¹) of diammonium P (N: 18%, P₂O₅: 46%), and 7.5 g (75 kg ha⁻¹) of K sulfate (K₂O: 50%). During the seedling stage, an additional 22.5 g (225 kg ha⁻¹) of urea (N: 46%) was applied as top dressing. Other management practices followed standard local corn production practices.

Sample collection

Soil samples were collected after the corn harvest in early October, 2017. Five random points were selected in each plot, and samples were taken using a soil auger (0–15 cm deep, 3 cm diameter). Soil from each plot was thoroughly mixed, and visible plant and animal debris, as well as stones, were removed. The soil sample was then divided into three portions: one fresh soil sample was sieved through a 2 mm mesh and stored at 4℃ for analyzing soil MBC and DOC content. Another portion was air-dried for analysis of LFOC, POC, LOC, and total organic carbon (TOC). The third portion was wrapped in aluminum foil, frozen in liquid N, and stored at −80 °C for DNA extraction and analysis of soil microbial diversity.

Determination method

Determination of TOC by external heating method using K₂Cr₂O₇31. Soil was mixed with deionized water (1:5) to extract DOC, and C content was determined using a TOC-LCPH soil total C analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan)32. MBC was determined using the chloroform fumigation extraction method33. Determination of LOC by KMnO₄ digestion method34. Soil LFOC analysis employs density flotation35,36. POC was determined using the modified method of Cambardella and Elliott37. Methods for soil bacterial DNA extraction and Illumina MiSeq sequencing are detailed in Supplementary Material 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 and R 4.3.0, with data visualization conducted using the “ggplot2” package and Origin software. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to evaluate the effects of different OMAR treatments on OC components and alpha diversity indices. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Duncan’s multiple range test, with differences between treatments indicated by lettering. Prior to analysis, all variables underwent homogeneity of variance tests. Nonparametric tests were applied to variables failing homogeneity of variance.

LEfSe analysis was performed using the “microeco” package in RStudio, identifying microorganisms with p < 0.05 and LDA scores > 3 as differentially abundant. Random forest analysis and Spearman correlation analysis were conducted using the “hmisc”, “randomforest”, “reshape2”, “caret”, and “psych” packages to evaluate relationships between differentially abundant microorganisms and OC components content, with results visualized. We conducted 5-fold cross validation using the “caret” package.

Microbial communities in relation to soil organic carbon

Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed that the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes showed a significant positive correlation with LFOC (r = 0.839, p < 0.01) and POC content (r = 0.832, p < 0.01); the relative abundance of Planctomycetes exhibited a significant negative correlation with DOC (r=−0.580, p = 0.048) and LOC content (r=−0.622, p = 0.031); the relative abundance of Proteobacteria demonstrated a significant negative correlation with POC content (r=−0.657, p = 0.020); and the relative abundance of Actinobacteria showed a significant negative correlation with LOC content (r=−0.650, p = 0.022) (Fig. 7 and Table S1).

Discussion

Although the influences of tillage practices and OMAR on OC components have been frequently studied, existing conclusions have yet to reach a unified consensus22,38. Multiple studies across China indicate that returning crop residues to fields leads to an increase in OC content39–41. However, changes in SOC dynamics are intricately influenced by the type of organic material, the method of field return, and the intensity of soil tillage1,22,25. Our study hypothesized that different OMAR practices—specifically straw return, straw mulching, and biochar incorporation—combined with varying tillage systems would alter the dynamics of distinct OC components by selectively driving shifts in microbial communities. Our findings strongly support this hypothesis, revealing a clear divergence in how these treatments optimize specific C pools: BC is the most effective treatment for maximizing stable, long-term C fractions, whereas NTS is the optimal strategy for enhancing highly active, short-term labile C fractions.

To understand why different treatments favor specific C pools, it is crucial to examine the physical impacts of tillage versus no-tillage systems. In our study, straw incorporation exerted the most significant effect on LOC and DOC, with NTS showing a significantly greater capacity to retain LOC compared to CTS. Assessing OC pools through oxidation sensitivity demonstrates that NTS physically protects these highly active fractions42. Under CTS and CT treatments, mechanical plowing disrupts soil macroaggregates, exposing previously protected OM to rapid microbial mineralization and oxidative degradation, consistent with the trend observed in the study by Huang et al.43. Conversely, the absence of physical soil disturbance under the NTS treatment preserves the integrity of the soil aggregate structure44. This lack of disturbance, combined with surface straw mulching, creates a protective physical barrier that slows the rapid turnover of LOC-L (low-recalcitrance labile carbon), effectively trapping surface-derived soluble compounds—such as polysaccharides, peptides, and aliphatic compounds released during straw decomposition—within intact aggregates. These retained nutrients provide a continuous, slow-release substrate that stimulates targeted microbial growth, thereby promoting the sustained accumulation of DOC and LOC22,45–47.

While NTS excels at enhancing labile C, the BC treatment proved remarkably superior in accumulating TOC, LFOC, and POC, despite the absence of freshly added exogenous labile C like straw. The POC content in the BC treatment was a remarkable 28.7 times higher than that of the CT treatment (Table 1). This extraordinary accumulation can be explained by several interconnected physical and biological mechanisms. First, biochar itself is a highly recalcitrant, C-rich material that directly contributes to the mass fraction of POC and TOC upon incorporation. Second, the highly porous structure and immense surface area of biochar provide critical micro-habitats that physically shield both native SOM and colonizing microorganisms from environmental stressors and predation48. Furthermore, biochar possesses a high adsorption capacity, allowing it to sequester root exudates and native volatile organic compounds, bringing them into close proximity with microbial decomposers. This promotes co-metabolism and stimulates the degradation of complex native biopolymers, such as lignin49. Lignin degradation products, such as lignin phenols, possess complex structures and strong resistance to further degradation, leading to their selective retention and long-term stabilization within the POC pool50.

These distinct physical and chemical environments dictated by OMAR and tillage practices subsequently act as powerful selective filters for the soil microbiome, which drives the ultimate composition of the soil C pool. The decomposition of externally supplied OM induces biological changes primarily by altering C source availability, thereby modifying the dynamics, diversity, and life-history strategies of soil microbial communities51–53. Our diversity analysis revealed that the CTS treatment exhibited the highest alpha diversity (Simpson, Shannon, Chao1, and ACE indices). This aligns perfectly with its elevated DOC content, as DOC provides a carbohydrate-rich, nutrient-enriched environment that alleviates resource limitations, allowing a highly diverse, complex bacterial community to thrive54. In contrast, the BC and NTS treatments reduced alpha diversity, indicating a strong selective pressure that favors specific, highly adapted microbial taxa over generalists55. OMAR treatments fundamentally reshaped the microbial community by increasing the relative abundance of eutrophic bacteria (such as Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria) that quickly utilize unstable C substrates, while suppressing oligotrophic K-strategists (such as Alphaproteobacteria and Acidobacteria) that dominate in nutrient-poor environments56,57.

The most critical finding of this study is the direct, mechanistic linkage between these selectively enriched microbial taxa and the accumulation of specific C fractions. Our correlation and random forest analyses provide robust quantitative evidence that distinct treatments produce specific C profiles by enriching specialized microbial processors. In the NTS treatment, the highly active LOC and DOC fractions were primarily driven by the enrichment of Actinobacteria and Romboutsia (Firmicutes). Actinobacteria are highly efficient at utilizing the abundant unstable C substrates (like cellulose and chitin) provided by the undisturbed straw mulch. Romboutsia, sensitive to C/N ratios, is deeply involved in the rapid decomposition of this fresh OM58. Ruminococcaceae can convert lactic acid and acetic acid into medium-chain carboxylic acids, which aid in the formation of SOM and carbon sequestration59.Random forest analysis confirmed that these specific bacterial taxa explained 32.95% of the variance in LOC under NTS, identifying them as the primary biological drivers of short-term nutrient availability in no-till systems.

Conversely, the massive accumulation of stable POC and LFOC in the BC treatment was overwhelmingly driven by the proliferation of Bacteroidetes and Leptospirillum. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed that the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes exhibited a highly significant positive correlation with LFOC (r = 0.839, p < 0.01) and POC content (r = 0.832, p < 0.01). Random forest models further demonstrated that this specific microbial consortium explained an exceptional 73.51%, 77.77%, and 74.02% of the variance in TOC, LFOC, and POC, respectively. Bacteroidetes are renowned as highly efficient decomposers of complex, recalcitrant high-molecular-weight organic matter. Nestled within the protective pores of the biochar, these enriched Bacteroidetes and Anaerolinea are highly efficient decomposers of unstable SOC57,60. Leptospirillum and Dyadobacter play a significant role in C fixation61,62. They showed a positive correlation with C component content, indicating that they were specifically driven by BC treatment and favored increases in TOC, LFOC, and POC content (Fig. 8D).

In contrast, under treatments lacking significant exogenous organic amendments, such as CT, the relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Planctomycetes, Nitrospirae, and Gemmatimonadetes exhibited significant negative correlations with SOC fractions (Fig. 8B). Because CT systems rely exclusively on inorganic fertilizers without supplemental carbon or nitrogen inputs, they selectively enrich oligotrophic taxa, including Alphaproteobacteria and Nitrospirae57,63, which thrive in nutrient-limited environments compared to OMAR systems. Furthermore, the concurrent proliferation of Gemmatimonadetes is likely attributable to their distinct substrate specificity for N63. Notably, the response of Planctomycetes to organic amendments remains a subject of ongoing debate. While Zhao et al.64. reported that Planctomycetes dominate in soils following corn straw incorporation, our results are consistent with Lang et al.65, demonstrating an increased abundance primarily under non-residue-return conditions. These discrepancies highlight the complex ecological sensitivity of Planctomycetes, suggesting that their community dynamics are highly contingent upon specific cropping configurations and localized soil physicochemical conditions.

By identifying these direct relationships, our study provides compelling evidence that agricultural C sequestration can be precisely engineered: physical aggregate protection via NTS utilizes Actinobacteria to boost active C for immediate crop nutrition, whereas structural fortification via biochar utilizes Bacteroidetes to permanently lock carbon into stable particulate fractions.

The findings from this research hold significant practical value for sustainable agricultural development in the Northeast black soil region, particularly within dryland farming systems. By identifying that no-till with straw mulching efficiently boosts labile OC, whereas biochar incorporation maximizes stable particulate C, these practices provide precise data support for regional low-C agricultural extension programs. Ultimately, integrating these optimized soil health strategies can assist local agricultural enterprises in establishing sustainable value chains, promoting the cultivation of premium, eco-friendly agricultural products while simultaneously mitigating climate impacts through enhanced carbon sequestration.

Despite the clear mechanistic linkages identified in this study, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, the experiment utilized artificially filled soil (50 cm depth) within small concrete microplots (1 m × 1 m). While this rigorously controlled design is highly effective for preventing lateral nutrient leaching and standardizing baseline environmental variables, it inherently disrupts the natural, undisturbed soil profile and may introduce edge effects that constrain extensive root-soil-microbe interactions, thereby limiting the immediate representativeness of broad field conditions. Furthermore, while rigorous statistical frameworks were applied, the use of three biological replicates provides a relatively conservative statistical foundation for resolving complex ecological variance. Finally, the two-year sampling timeframe captures only the initial, transient stabilization phase of exogenous C incorporation, which is insufficient for determining the definitive long-term equilibrium dynamics of SOC. However, this early-stage assessment serves as the critical functional baseline for an ongoing, continuous six-year microplot experiment, which is specifically designed to systematically monitor the long-term stabilization and multi-year trajectory of these metabolic C pools and microbial successions under sustained agricultural management.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that the application of organic materials significantly overrides variations in tillage practices in shaping both soil organic carbon fractions and microbial community structure. Our study reveals that different treatments optimize entirely different carbon pools through distinct physical and microbial pathways. For short-term agricultural productivity and rapid nutrient cycling, NTS is the optimal treatment. NTS produces the highest levels of LOC and DOC. The lack of mechanical tillage preserves soil macroaggregates, physically protecting these active carbon fractions from rapid oxidation, while selectively enriching Actinobacteria and Romboutsia to efficiently process the surface straw into accessible plant nutrients. For long-term climate mitigation and sustained soil carbon sequestration, BC is the vastly superior treatment. BC produces the highest quality of stable organic carbon, maximizing TOC, LFOC, and POC. Although no fresh labile C is added, biochar provides a highly porous, recalcitrant structural refuge that heavily selects for Bacteroidetes. This specific microbial community efficiently co-metabolizes complex native organics and stabilizes them as particulate C. Therefore, regional soil management in Northeast China should be tailored to specific agronomic goals: utilizing NTS to rapidly remediate nutrient-depleted soils through active carbon enhancement, or deploying BC to permanently build structural carbon reserves in degraded black soils. Future research should transition from these controlled microplots to long-term, multi-year continuous monitoring to fully capture the multi-decadal equilibrium and stabilization dynamics of distinct soil organic C pools. Additionally, integrating advanced multi-omics approaches—such as metagenomic and metatranscriptomic sequencing—alongside high-resolution soil enzymatic profiles will be indispensable to conclusively validate the precise metabolic pathways and functional behaviors of the key driving taxa identified herein. Finally, scaling up these optimized management frameworks to broader regional field trials across diverse dryland farming systems in the Northeast black soil region will provide crucial empirical baselines to support large-scale low-C agricultural extension programs and promote sustainable ecological product value chains.
