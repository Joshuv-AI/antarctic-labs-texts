Glucosyl Hesperidin Benefits in Primary Biliary Cholangitis: A Multicenter, Open-Label, Randomized Controlled Study
Authors: Kei Moriya, Kiyoshi Asada, Naoki Ozu, Shota Suzuki, Yukihisa Fujinaga, Masahide Enomoto, Yuki Tsuji, Yusuke Komeda, Junichi Hanatani, Daisuke Kaya, Yuya Matsuo, Shinsaku Nagamatsu, Hideki Matsuo, Masakazu Uejima, Masaharu Yamazaki, Hitoshi Yoshiji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) has steadily increased in incidence, but its causes remain to be completely elucidated. Moreover, the potential for disease progression is not uncommon, especially when normalization of hepatobiliary enzyme levels is difficult, making the emergence of new treatments highly anticipated. We clarified the therapeutic effects of glucosyl hesperidin (G-Hes), which activates the antioxidant NRF2/KEAP1 pathway, on improving PBC pathophysiology.METHODS:This randomized controlled trial evaluated changes in serum levels of hepatobiliary enzymes, lipids, and heme oxygenase 1, which is an NRF2/KEAP1 pathway target gene protein, after G-Hes administration (500 or 1,000 mg/d) for 24 weeks in 71 patients on maintenance therapy for PBC.RESULTS:Gamma glutamyl transferase at week 24, the primary end point of this study, decreased significantly by 6.2% only in the high dose group. Among the secondary end points, alkaline phosphatase increased plausibly because of G-Hes-induced bone metabolism enhancement and bilirubin decreased, both reaching significance only in the standard dose group. There were no significant changes in transaminases in either group. On subgroup analyses of patients with abnormal baseline biochemical levels, significant decrease in gamma glutamyl transferase at week 24 (6.4%) still remained only in the high-dose group. On the other hand, there was no significant change in alkaline phosphatase at any time point in both groups. The blood protein concentration of heme oxygenase 1 increased by 4.8% at week 24, although not significant. There were no G-Hes-related adverse events.DISCUSSION:Inclusion of G-Hes to existing treatment safely provided additional therapeutic effects in patients with PBC.

INTRODUCTION

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic cholestatic liver disease caused by an immunological disorder of the bile ducts and leads to bile stasis. Over the long term, it can progress from liver fibrosis to cirrhosis (1). Although autoimmune mechanisms are central to its pathogenesis, cholestasis can induce toxic stress in hepatocytes and bile duct epithelium and potentially amplify secondary oxidative stress (2–4). Therefore, reducing oxidative stress is considered a theoretically plausible adjunctive strategy for disease modification. Furthermore, the antioxidant NRF2/KEAP1 pathway was found to be impaired in patients with PBC (5).

The first-line treatment of PBC is ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), which has been shown to reduce hepatobiliary enzymes and improve long-term prognosis. For patients with inadequate response, bezafibrate may demonstrate efficacy (1). Although these agents may indirectly alleviate oxidative stress by correcting bile acid homeostasis or reducing bile acid toxicity, they are not direct antioxidant agents. Therefore, antioxidants that are capable of suppressing reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, which are produced during the cascade of immune hyperactivity and bile acid toxicity, are considered to hold significant potential as adjunctive therapies. However, large-scale clinical evidence specific to PBC has been scarce, and the optimal dose and duration of administration for consistent effects on clinical outcomes have not been established (6,7). Considering these facts, we hypothesized that continuous administration of antioxidants could suppress cholestasis-associated lipid peroxidation and membrane damage in patients with PBC. This approach could be a novel and promising adjunctive therapeutic strategy for disease modification. However, prolonged administration of high doses of antioxidants requires careful consideration of associated risks, such as bleeding tendencies and effects on metabolism (8–10). Therefore, we designed a multicenter intervention study to sequentially evaluate the safety and efficacy of adding glucosyl hesperidin (G-Hes), which is a widely used antioxidant in the general society and considered to be highly safe, to the standard treatment regimen for PBC.

METHODS

Study design, eligibility criteria, and end points

This randomized, open-label, multicenter, parallel-group clinical trial was concurrently initiated with the publication of its protocol, which fully described all the details, including the inclusion and exclusion criteria (11). Briefly, we enrolled patients aged 20 years and older who had PBC and performance status of 0 or 1 from June 2022 to December 2022. The definitive diagnosis of PBC was made in accordance with the guidelines of the American Association for the Study of Liver Disease and the European Association for the Study of the Liver (12,13). Specifically, the diagnostic criteria required the presence of at least 2 of the following 3 conditions: (i) persistent elevation of cholestatic liver enzymes; (ii) positivity for antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA) or antimitochondrial M2 antibodies; and (iii) characteristic histological findings on liver biopsy, including destructive cholangitis of the interlobular bile ducts. Those who had G-Hes hypersensitivity, were routinely taking G-Hes, or were physically fragile were excluded. In addition to the ongoing treatment, G-Hes was administered for 24 weeks. Using envelop method, participants were randomly divided into 2 groups based on the daily G-Hes dose: standard dose (SD, 500 mg/d) and HD (1,000 mg/d). The random table was generated by a statistician using SAS (version 9.1.3; SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and participants were stratified according to age and sex.

The primary end point was the rate of change in serum gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) level from 0 to 24 weeks after G-Hes administration, and it was calculated as follows: serum GGT level at week 24—serum GGT level at week 0/serum GGT level at week 0. The secondary end points were rates of changes in serum GGT, aspartate transferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and total bilirubin (TBIL) levels; protein expression levels of NRF2 and its target gene heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) at each time point (week 8, 16, 24, and 36) after G-Hes administration. Details of these secondary end points have been fully described in our protocol (11). Rate of change was calculated as follows: serum X level (week Y)—serum X level (week 0)/serum X level (week 0). Safety was evaluated during the entire observation period and was included as a secondary end point. In this study, a checklist was prepared during each visit for comprehensive assessment of medication adherence. Exploratory end points were the rates of changes in total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglyceride (TG). Subgroup analyses of patients who had laboratory values exceeding the upper limit of normal were also performed.

Laboratory assessments

The upper limit of normal values for the various biochemical tests are as follows: GGT (male), 64 U/L; GGT (female), 32 U/L; ALP, 113 U/L; AST, 30 U/L; ALT (male) 42 U/L; ALT (female) 23 U/L; TBIL, 1.2 mg/dL; TG, 149 mg/dL; TC, 219 mg/dL, and LDL-C, 139 mg/dL. Serum heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) and NRF2 levels were measured in duplicate using the following commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, according to the manufacturer's instructions: human HO-1 ELISA kit #ab207621 (Abcom, Cambridge, UK) and human NRF2 ELISA kit #MBS744368 (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA), respectively.

Statistical analyses

Numerical variables were expressed as median with interquartile range or average with 95% confidence interval. Mann–Whitney U test was adopted to compare 2 groups with non-normal distribution. Unpaired categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ2 test. The correlation between each variable—HO-1 concentration, HO-1 change rate, body weight, and G-Hes dose per BW—and patient age was assessed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. Two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. For the outliers in each population, Smirnov–Grubbs test was used only once on the most suspicious values; if the statistical significance level of ɑ = 0.05 was exceeded, the value was removed. Because these analyses were exploratory in nature, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed, and all P values should be considered nominal. JMP version 14.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) software was used for statistical analyses. The study protocol was published in advance (11), and analyses strictly adhered to its content.

Ethical issues

This study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and the principles of the Japanese ethics guideline for life science and medical research involving human subjects (https://www.mext.go.jp/lifescience/bioethics/files/pdf/n2373_01.pdf, accessed on December 14, 2025). Furthermore, the ethics committees of Nara Prefecture General Medical Center and Nara Medical University approved this study (approval numbers 421 and 739, respectively). All participants provided written informed consent.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of the study population

A total of 75 individuals were enrolled during the study period. After excluding 2 participants from each group who withdrew consent because of personal reasons, the final number of participants included in the analysis was 36 in the SD group and 35 in the HD group (see Supplementary Figure 1, Supplementary Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/CTG/B573). Most participants in this study were continuously taking UDCA, and none of the patients showed a complete lack of improvement in hepatobiliary enzyme levels after initiating UDCA. Nevertheless, G-Hes was administered at the discretion of the attending physicians with the expectation of achieving further reductions in cholestatic enzymes. In addition, a small number of patients hesitated to continue UDCA after the diagnosis of PBC had been confirmed and were followed without medication. Furthermore, several patients declined continued UDCA therapy because of gastrointestinal symptoms—such as diarrhea, loose stools, or nausea—that had occurred during previous UDCA treatment. At baseline before G-Hes administration, no significant differences were observed between the 2 groups regarding age, sex, and BW; median daily UDCA dose (600 mg); clinical symptoms, such as general fatigue or pruritus, clinical disease classification, and rate of cirrhotic liver; and serum levels of hepatobiliary enzymes and lipids (Table 1). The UDCA dose per kilogram of BW was 11.3 mg/kg in the SD group and 11.8 mg/kg in the HD group, and the values were comparable between the 2 groups. During the 24-week G-Hes administration period and the subsequent 12-week follow-up period, exclusion of liver injury related to excessive alcohol consumption or drug intake was successfully assessed at each visit. In addition, it was confirmed that none of the participants had newly acquired HBV or HCV infection before or after the entire observation period.

Long-term G-Hes administration and changes in lipid metabolism

In all patients, TG levels significantly decreased by 8.9% at week 36 and TC and LDL-C significantly increased by 4.5% and 11.5%, respectively, at week 24 in the HD group (Table 2). On subgroup analysis of patients who had high baseline TG (n = 21), TC (n = 22), and LDL-C, (n = 5), no significant changes were observed in the SD group but TG levels significantly decreased by ≥ 20% at week 36 in the HD group (Table 3). There were no significant changes in TC and LDL-C at any time point in both groups.

Long-term G-Hes administration and changes in serum HO-1 level

Serum HO-1 concentrations did not significantly change overall but significantly increased by 14.2% at week 24 in the SD group and significantly decreased by 9.3% at week 16 in the HD group (Table 4). Furthermore, the serum HO-1 concentration was >20% higher in the HD group (2,535 pg/mL) than in the SD group (2,054 pg/mL) at week 0 before G-Hes administration and continued to show this trend at weeks 8, 16, and 24.

Safety of long-term G-Hes administration

All patients, except one in the HD group, achieved at least 70% medication adherence rate. Throughout the entire observation period of this study, 3 adverse events were reported in one patient each: fracture because of fall, ischemic colitis, and abnormal liver function. The respective treating physicians deemed that none of these events had a direct causal relationship with continued G-Hes administration.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this was the first clinical intervention study that evaluated the long-term effects of G-Hes administration for 24 weeks as add-on to maintenance therapy in patients with PBC. Analysis of the primary end point demonstrated novel scientific findings of significant reduction in GGT levels after 24 weeks of G-Hes administration at a dose of 1,000 mg/d. However, some of the results on the secondary end points were contrary to our hypothesis; this may be explained by the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study. Although patients with normal blood biochemistry before medical intervention with G-Hes were included in this study, physiological variations in the values assessed may have occurred throughout the observation period. Furthermore, considering that the end point was ratio rather than difference before and after intervention and assuming the same magnitude of changes, including physiological variations, patients with normal preintervention test values were more affected than those with abnormally high values. Our results on the changes in hepatobiliary function and lipid metabolism clearly showed that our failure to consider these facts during the research planning stage was the primary factor that led to some unexpected results. In actual clinical practice, proposing to intensify treatment when hepatobiliary enzyme levels are normal may not be easily understandable for some patients. Therefore, one of the major findings of this novel and pioneering study was the necessity to exclude patients with normal baseline blood test results in future research.

In addition, from a basic science perspective, the mechanism underlying the ALP elevation observed in this study may plausibly involve G-Hes-induced enhancement of bone metabolism. G-Hes is a derivative of hesperidin with improved water solubility and bioavailability, and it is metabolized to hesperetin in vivo. The effects of hesperidin and its metabolites on bone formation have already been demonstrated in several experimental studies. Li et al (14) reported that hesperidin and its zinc complex promote osteoblastic differentiation and significantly increase ALP activity and mineralization. Upregulation of osteogenic genes such as Runx2 and Col1a1 was also observed, suggesting that hesperidin enhances bone formation at the molecular level. Similarly, Hu et al (15) showed that hesperidin stimulates osteoblast proliferation and increases ALP activity through the estrogen signaling pathway, accompanied by reductions in oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines, thereby exerting a bone forming effect. On the other hand, hesperidin has also been shown to suppress RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis (16), indicating that it may contribute to overall bone homeostasis by attenuating bone resorption. Given that G-Hes exhibits higher bioavailability than native hesperidin, it is theoretically reasonable to assume that similar bone metabolic effects may occur more efficiently with G-Hes intake.

ALP is a representative bone formation marker expressed in osteoblasts, and its levels can increase when bone metabolism is activated. Therefore, if G-Hes intake enhances bone formation, the hypothesis that ALP—particularly bone-specific ALP—may rise is biochemically coherent. However, because total ALP reflects the combined contributions of multiple organs, including the hepatobiliary system, bone, and intestine, mild changes in bone metabolism may not always manifest as detectable increases in total ALP. Moreover, clinical studies specifically evaluating G-Hes alone in humans remain limited, and current evidence is insufficient to establish a direct causal relationship with ALP fluctuations. Taken together, the possibility that G-Hes intake contributed to ALP elevation through its effects on bone metabolism is consistent with existing experimental findings and is scientifically plausible. Future prospective studies incorporating bone metabolic markers will be necessary to further clarify this association, and it also seems reasonable from a retrospective standpoint that we selected GGT as the primary end point of the present study, given that this choice was based on our own previously published findings (17). Furthermore, among the potential confounding factors that Granito et al (18) have reported to influence UDCA treatment response in patients with PBC, no cases suggestive of newly developed overlapping liver diseases, such as MASH during the observation period, or cases of concomitant celiac disease—which is more common in Western populations—were identified in this study. However, some patients had a history of receiving bile acid sequestrants such as cholestyramine for pruritus management (2 patients, 2.7%), and 5 patients (6.7%) exhibited a reduction in prescribed UDCA days without medical justification, raising the possibility of decreased adherence to UDCA therapy. Therefore, the therapeutic effect of UDCA may have been attenuated in these patients and these facts may have accounted for the improvement in GGT observed, while ALP showed no corresponding improvement.

Several reports have indicated that oxidative stress is deeply involved in the pathogenesis of various liver diseases (19–23). Therefore, antioxidants are expected to be useful in controlling the pathophysiology of chronic inflammatory diseases by reducing oxidative stress and suppressing excessive production of inflammatory cytokines (24,25). Some essential antioxidants are produced within the body, whereas others can be obtained from food, such as fruits, vegetables, and grains. There is substantial evidence that individuals who consume large amounts of these food products that are rich in antioxidants have relatively low risk of chronic diseases (26,27). However, formulated antioxidant supplements may not prevent cardiovascular disease or cancer, because these purified chemicals may function differently from those consumed from food containing complex mixtures of substances. In any case, excessive intake of antioxidants carries the risk of inhibiting important cellular functions, such as cellular defense mechanisms and normal signaling pathways (24,25). Evidently, high intake is not necessarily beneficial, and maintaining an appropriate balance is crucial.

Long-term safety is critically important for patients with dysfunctional liver, which is a vital organ for numerous metabolic pathways. The G-Hes used in this study is a widely used antioxidant that has been classified as food and certified as safe for consumption by regulatory authorities in Europe, the United States, and Japan (28–31), and it is a low molecular weight compound with a mildly bitter taste but is processed using a Japanese bittersweet orange flavor for commercial consumption as a supplement, which has been demonstrated to be excellent for long-term use in the study. The dose-dependent antioxidant effects and safety of prolonged continuous G-Hes administration have already been demonstrated in animal models and humans (32–34). Furthermore, hesperidin is the main component of dried citrus peel (Chenpi), which has been traditionally valued in China as one of the qi-regulating agents, and G-Hes derived from natural sources holds great potential to play a significant role in treating a wide range of refractory diseases, such as PBC.

G-Hes is a low molecular weight compound that activates the NRF2/KEAP1 pathway. Therefore, we analyzed changes in the serum gene protein concentrations of HO-1, a target gene of NRF2. Changes in the serum HO-1 gene protein concentrations were different between the SD and HD groups and showed dose-dependent changes in the weeks that followed G-Hes administration (Table 4). In addition, HO-1 concentrations were comparable across age before G-Hes administration but became higher with age at weeks 8 and 16 (Figure 1). This finding suggested that G-Hes may strongly promote activation of antioxidant mechanisms in the elderly. Consequently, it is likely to yield favorable outcomes for patients with PBC, which predominantly affect middle-aged and elderly postmenopausal women, in whom oxidative stress is increased (35–37).

This study had several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The first limitation was the small sample size. Although the study protocol planned for 110 patients including 10 percent of dropout cases, the final number enrolled in this study was only 71 except 4 withdrawal cases. The initial sample size calculation was based on a high-power level of 90%. When recalculated using a standard power level of 80%, the required number of cases was 74. Given that PBC is a rare disease and extending the enrollment period at the 2 participating institutions was unlikely to increase the sample size, we prioritized completing the study within the planned timeframe to expedite disclosure of interesting findings. The second limitation was the definition of research outcomes. For long-term prognostication of patients with PBC, reliability was reported to be higher for the 2 quantitative prognostic scores, the Global Assessment of Liver Outcomes score for PBC (GLOBE) (38) and the United Kingdom Primary Biliary Cholangitis score (UK-PBC) (39) than for the rate of changes in GGT and ALP levels after therapeutic intervention. However, these scores require clinical laboratory values at 1 year after treatment initiation and could not be set as study outcomes, because G-Hes administration was only for 24 weeks in this study. Given the scarcity of previous research findings on the safe duration of G-Hes administration for patients with chronic liver disease, this decision can be considered reasonable. The third limitation was the lack of clarification of the mechanism by which G-Hes improves liver function in patients with PBC. Although Miwa et al have previously demonstrated that improved very low-density lipoprotein metabolism was involved in reducing TG levels (34), the mechanism of improved liver function remains unclear and highly intriguing. However, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of G-Hes in refractory liver disease, and its pathophysiological and biochemical mechanisms remain to be elucidated in future research. The fourth limitation was that given the multiple exploratory comparisons performed in this study, the possibility of inflated type 1 error should be acknowledged. Therefore, these findings in the current study should be interpreted cautiously and require external validation. As the fifth limitation, the open label design adopted in this study should also be acknowledged. Although exploratory studies inherently involve relatively large limitations, such limitations are an essential and highly valuable investment to reduce clinical and scientific uncertainty and to enhance the likelihood of success in subsequent confirmatory trials. With respect to whether the open-label nature of this study substantially influenced participants' liver function, such an effect is presumed to be highly limited, clearly differing from subjective symptoms such as pain or fatigue. Unlike placebo-controlled trials, all participants in this study received active treatment, a design that likely reduced psychological barriers to study participation. This was particularly advantageous for recruiting patients with a rare disease. Moreover, this design may have contributed to maintaining the dropout rate within an acceptable range throughout the study period, thereby making the comparison between the 2 different dose regimens realistically feasible. Moreover, racial differences and international research collaboration is desired in the future.

Despite these limitations, our study demonstrated that G-Hes administration provided additional therapeutic effect in patients with PBC who were on existing standard treatment. Fibrate agents, positioned as the second-line treatment of PBC after UDCA, are peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha agonists. In addition to lowering TG and very low-density lipoprotein through enhanced β-oxidation, they promote bile acid secretion and exhibit anti-inflammatory effects. However, side effects such as renal impairment, elevated liver enzymes, and myopathy have been reported (1). Therefore, the addition of G-Hes, which possesses an activating effect on the NRF2/KEAP1 pathway independent of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor pathway and whose TG-lowering effect was also demonstrated in this study, may contribute to suppressing the occurrence of these side effects by reducing the dosage of fibrate preparations. Furthermore, G-Hes taken into the body undergoes hydrolysis by intestinal bacteria and is then glucuronidated as the biologically active compound hesperetin, circulating in the bloodstream (40). Hesperetin is known to activate sirtuin1 (SIRT1), which plays a crucial role in anti-aging mechanisms, and to inhibit NF-κB signaling, which is broadly involved in various inflammatory processes. Consequently, its long-term consumption holds promise for additional benefits, such as suppressing cardiovascular events (41–46). Progression of tissue damage because of sustained inflammatory cytokine production is known to occur in chronic inflammatory diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (47). Therefore, future research to verify the medical efficacy of G-Hes in PBC and other chronic inflammatory diseases of unknown cause would be remarkable.
