Stigma and reintegration: does age influence the perceptions of older people on parole?
Authors: Angela Murolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a consequence of the “graying prison population,” the number of older people leaving prison and on parole has grown. Because older people on parole are a growing proportion of the paroled population, there is a greater need to understand their reentry experiences. Therefore, the aim of this study was to understand what role age played in perceptions of stigma and reintegration. Surveys were conducted with people (n = 74) who left prison within a year to understand their perceptions of stigma and reintegration. Fisher’s exact tests were conducted to determine the relationship between personal characteristics and perceptions of reintegration and stigma. Results indicated that age was only significant on one of several measures. Variables including sex, race, living situation and employment since release were consistently associated with perceptions of stigma or reintegration regardless of age. Implications for future research will be discussed.

Introduction

Due to increasingly harsh sentencing punishments, the phenomenon of mass incarceration has created an aging correctional population that will continue to grow over time, with projections indicating that 30% of incarcerated people will be over the age of 50 years old by 2030 (Gordon, 2025). Nearly 95% of those people will leave prison, with many returning to non-incarcerated communities under parole supervision leaving parole officers with an aging caseload (Hughes & ten Bensel, 2022; Murolo, 2026). Consequently, there is increased interest in understanding the needs of this population including their overall wellbeing and successful reintegration, which includes the impact of stigma on their reentry efforts. Stigmatization is a negative label cast upon by others and is a form of disintegrative shaming (Braithwaite, 1989a; LeBel, 2012). This label focuses on the person who has committed the crime, rather than focusing on the action or behavior itself (Braithwaite, 1989a).

Fifty years old is the lower-end point of departure for one who is considered “old” or “elderly” among the formerly incarcerated population and the most frequently cited in the relevant literature (Merkt et al., 2020). People who are 50 years old or more with a history of incarceration often suffer from premature aging due to poor health, lack of care, drug use, and incarceration itself (Bedard et al., 2022; Skarupski et al., 2018). Because of their advanced age, criminal history, and challenged family relationships, older people’s ability to reintegrate into society is a growing area of inquiry and concern.

Emerging research on older incarcerated and formerly incarcerated people has begun to focus on wellbeing as a measure of community based- success (National Academies, 2022). As part of this research, perceptions of successful reintegration and conversely, stigmatization are part and parcel of understanding how these factors impact older adults’ community adjustment including finding employment and housing, the use and building of social capital and repairing familial relationships. Perceptions of stigma and reintegration can differ from actual outcomes, however how people leaving prison view their ability to live in the community is one barometer of adjustment after incarceration. While prior research has focused on reoffending outcomes and obtaining employment or housing as a measure of community success, understanding whether older adults feel they are capable of positively reintegrating and avoiding the stigma that is associated with a criminal record is warranted. Perception and reality often differ. The study of stigma in the past has focused on younger people and the likelihood of reoffending (Benson et al., 2011) but among older people, stigma negatively impacts help seeking and support, access to healthcare as well as mental health and substance abuse treatment which adversely affects wellbeing as one advances in age (Clarke & Allen-McCombs, 2025; Tyler & Brockman, 2017). Because of the aforementioned factors, including advanced age, declining health, and uncertain familial relationships, understanding how perceptions of stigma impacts reintegration experiences among older adults are needed. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate what role age, traditional reentry concerns such as the ability to find housing, and employment, and more personal concerns like making and keeping friends and avoiding isolation were associated with stigma and reintegration among older adults who recently left prison.

Background information

The stigma of a criminal record does not end after incarceration and is stigmatizing across many social constructs regardless of age (Erikson, 1962; Maruna, 2001). When people are shamed, no efforts are made to reintegrate or accept them back into society. They are shunned, labeled, and marginalized (Braithwaite, 1989b; Goffman, 1963). Most noted is the impact of criminal history when in search of employment. Devah Pager’s (2002) well known study on employment after incarceration highlighted the difficulties of finding work with a criminal conviction, especially among Black people with a criminal history. More recent research by Visher et al. (2011) also found that searching for work after incarceration is a stigmatizing experience, wherein after eight months following release from prison 35% of respondents in this study could not find work and 55% were employed half time or less. Employment after prison also includes lower wages and low-level jobs for many, further magnifying the effects of stigmatization (Kling, 2002). The search for employment is more challenging for older people. Older people face hiring discrimination and ageism; further, it takes older formerly incarcerated people more time to find employment compared to their younger counterparts (Murolo, 2022).

Searching for housing is also a stigmatizing experience, especially for older formerly incarcerated adults who may not have family members to return to (Clarke, 2017). Securing both public or private housing requires a criminal history background check which can lead to the rejection of housing applications and limited options (Oyama, 2009; Pager & Shepard, 2008). Younger people often return to their families after prison, predominantly living with female family members until they can venture on their own (Stone et al., 2025; Western et al., 2015). But if their family members live in public housing, criminal history restrictions prohibit people with records from living there, which can jeopardize their relatives’ living situation (HUD Exchange, 2022; Travis, 2005). Among those who do not have the option of living with friends and family, halfway housing is a common first step after prison which is a stigmatizing experience in and of itself that undermines confidence and community success (Keene et al., 2018; Nahra et al., 2025; Stone et al., 2025). Housing instability and criminality are intrinsically related (Bryan, 2023) and result in double marginality, whereby being homeless and having a criminal history increases the likelihood of experiencing housing instability. Such a distinction decreases the likelihood of having a positive reentry experience or reducing the stigma associated with criminal history and homelessness creating an endless cycle of instability (Keene et al., 2018). This is especially true among older people (Garcia-Grossman et al., 2022).

While searching for housing and employment are stigmatizing experiences, research has shown that family members tend to hold fewer stigmatizing views compared to the general public, especially among younger justice–impacted people (Benson et al., 2011). Family is a key contributor to social and instrumental support which lessens the stigma of criminal history and increases social bonds, a sense of belonging and a successful reentry experience (Nahna et al., 2025; Mowne et al., 2019) but this is not always the case, especially among older adults leaving prison. Parent child relationships are strained by incarceration, creating stigmatizing environments for children and parents alike (Aiello & McQueeny, 2016). However, research on parental stigmatization by their children is sparse at best and is mentioned briefly in Murolo’s (2022) research on formerly incarcerated people, where several respondents who committed felony offenses were stigmatized by their children due to the seriousness of their offense in some cases, but not always. Siegel’s (2011) research on children of incarcerated mothers also indicates a mixed response by the children when considering their mother’s criminality, but little is explored here or elsewhere considering the parent’s reactions to their children’s condemnation of their behavior and warrants further research.

Education serves as a source of both stigmatization and redemption. Incarcerated people are less educated than the general public, but Black people are less likely to have their high school diplomas compared to White people further stigmatizing this population by reducing employment prospects and lifetime earnings (Tyler & Brackmann, 2017). Furthermore, this results in “self-stigmatization” which emerges in the form of “low self-esteem and personal barriers to reentry” (Evans et al., 2018, p. 255). Conversely, having an education or receiving one in prison is a source of pride and can counteract the effects of stigma (Evans et al., 2018). Furthermore, prison-based education creates an opportunity to create a prosocial identity and is a “hook” to encourage desistance and effective reintegration (Murolo & Campagna, 2025; Runell, 2017).

Health status also contributes to the stigmatization of formerly incarcerated people. People with a criminal history, especially Black, Hispanic, Indigenous people, and older adults are less healthy, underinsured, and low-income, which negatively contributes to stigmatization (Brown & Greco, 2024: Tyler & Brockmann, 2017). Formerly incarcerated people are more likely to suffer from mental and physical disorders and substance misuse and abuse (Howell et al., 2022). Stigma by health professionals as well as the quality of services one receives has been shown to impact the likelihood of seeking appropriate care after prison further contributing to negative health outcomes (Seaward et al., 2023; Tyler & Brockman, 2017).

Older formerly incarcerated people, stigma and reintegration

While older people leaving prison face many of the same challenges leaving prison as their younger counterparts, their age and health status negatively contribute to the stigma of incarceration. Older people have financial difficulties or face poverty after prison. Many older adults cannot work due to illness or disability regardless of sentence length (Brown & Greco, 2024; Western et al., 2015). Older people who are reentering society face a double marginalization when searching for employment. Having a criminal history compounded with advanced age, negatively contributes to both ageism and discrimination during the job search itself (Grisholz et al., 2024). This double marginalization is further compounded by poor health or disability. This is especially true among those with a history of substance abuse or mental illness (Seaward et al., 2023); however, older people who were employed before incarceration were more likely to find work after prison, decreasing the effects of stigma and increasing the likelihood of effective reintegration (Maschi et al., 2014). Lack of digital literacy also increases the likelihood of experiencing stigma and negatively contributes to reentry outcomes among this population (Grosholz et al., 2024). Those who have served long sentences are especially prone to hiring discrimination and stigmatization due to their lengthy prison stay as well as a lack of appropriate skills for the modern world (Grosholz et al., 2024; Murolo, 2022).

Obtaining housing is also stigmatizing; many older adults suffer from housing instability due to damaged or broken family bonds, lack of financial security not to mention the mark of a criminal record (Stone et al., 2025). Many older people leaving prison require supportive housing which is in short supply (Clarke, 2017). Without a significant stream of capital, housing leaves few older adults with enough money to pay for much else, leaving this community marginalized and heavily reliant on social services for support (Stone et al., 2025).

Unlike younger people with a criminal history, older adults have damaged and severed relationships which makes reentry a particularly stigmatizing experience. Due to advanced age and lengthy sentences older adults have few members to return to and in many cases waning support among family members over time (Stone et al., 2025; Western et al., 2015). In some cases, the severity of their crimes has left them isolated from their family members who are normally in the best position to provide housing support (Clarke, 2017; Stone et al., 2025). Often this leaves older adults more dependent on parole officers and social service providers to provide guidance in securing housing (Hughes & ten Bensel, 2022; Murolo, 2026; Stone et al., 2025).

Older adults are also stigmatized regardless of their educational background. Older adults with college degrees face ageism and discrimination in the workplace, regardless of having a college degree, unless they work with organizations that support the formerly incarcerated population (Cerda-Jara et al., 2024; Clarke & Allen-McCombs, 2025; Murolo, 2022). Older adults without advanced degrees encounter discrimination and stigmatization due to low educational levels in addition to advanced age and criminal history (Clarke & Allen-McCombs, 2025; Grosholz et al., 2024). In prison, educational programs are prioritized for younger people in their prime working years, and too often prisons do not offer educational programs at all, despite the benefits of an education for younger and older alike (Murolo & Campagna, 2025). However, reductions in recidivism and “post-release employment” have borne no fruit among those who have participated in such programs (Clarke & Allen-McCombs, 2025). However, for older adults, education aids in successful reintegration, slows cognitive decline and provides opportunities for socialization that might not be available otherwise for this population (Murolo & Campagna, 2025).

The relationship between age, health, criminal history, and stigma is interconnected. Older people leave prison with significantly more illnesses and are on more medications than their younger counterparts making them vulnerable to illness and premature death (Barry et al., 2018; Stone et al., 2025; Tyler & Brockman, 2017). Often, many older adults have unaddressed health issues while incarcerated that extends out into the community. Seeking out appropriate care is complicated for many reasons. Often finding appropriate care with Medicaid is complicated by co-pays that older adults leaving prison cannot afford (Murolo, 2022: Tyler & Brockman, 2017). Conversely, engaging with medical professionals includes measures of “stigma-management” to lessen negative perceptions of those with mental health issues (Clarke & Allen-McCombs, 2025; Seaward et al., 2023). Older adults with mental health and substance abuse issues are unlikely to seek-out care because of the stigma associated with their criminal label, further jeopardizing their health and well-being (Clarke & McCombs, 2025).

Theoretical framework

There is an abundance of research on perceptions of stigma (Benson et al., 2011; Evans et al., 2018; LeBel, 2012) but less is known about how older adults with a criminal history experience this phenomenon. Family is the primary means of social control (Braithwaite, 1989a). But for older people, family bonds may be broken or non-existent, putting greater onus on parole officers and social service providers to provide a positive model of social control (Murolo & Campagna, 2025; Stone et al., 2025). Conversely, those who are not bound by family ties, which may be the case of older adults, or returning, lack informal social controls, which may lead to increased criminality (Hirschi, 1998). Stigmatization, or disintegrative shaming, decreases social control effectiveness and increases deviance and criminality (Braithwaite, 1989a).

Braithwaite (1989a) posited that community members are the best arbiters of crime control; they have the greatest effect on shaming those close to them who have committed a crime. After this action they can then work towards reintegrating them back into the community of honorable citizens (Braithwaite, 1989a). According to Braithwaite’s (1989a) theory of reintegrative shaming, one can hypothesize that people on parole who encounter reintegrative shaming by community members and society more broadly will likely feel less stigmatized and will effectively reintegrate into society. According to this theory, reintegrative shaming includes:“Expressions of community disapproval, which range from mild rebuke to degradation ceremonies, are followed by gestures of reacceptance into the community of law-abiding citizens” (Braithwaite, 1989a, p.55)

“Expressions of community disapproval, which range from mild rebuke to degradation ceremonies, are followed by gestures of reacceptance into the community of law-abiding citizens” (Braithwaite, 1989a, p.55)

Based on this premise, the behavior is labeled, not the person; here the aim is to fully reintegrate formerly incarcerated people back into mainstream society. This is especially relevant for older adults on parole, because they do not fit the “traditional profile” of someone with a criminal history. Further, they are less likely to experience a hearty welcome home after being incarcerated (Western et al., 2015; Wyse, 2018), underscoring the increased likelihood of stigmatization and decreased likelihood of reintegration.

Methods

Research design

Survey data was collected as part of two mixed methods projects employed in three North Eastern States. The studies explored the needs and challenges of older people leaving prison and parole officer perceptions of this population using both identical surveys and semi-structured interviews to meet that end, essentially serving as a replication of the first study (see Murolo, 2022). IRB approval was reviewed and approved by the CUNY University Integrated Institutional Review Board, Office of Advancement in Research/ Human Research Protections Protocol (IRB File #2020–0676) for research in two northeastern states (2020–2021). St. Francis College Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the 2023–2024 study (IRB# 2022–0040).

Sampling strategy and participants

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to ensure people on parole who were released from prison within the past year were targeted. People over the age of 18 were surveyed, with a focus on those over 50 years old. People on parole were recruited from 2020–21 in two northeastern states, and 2023–2024 in one northeastern state. Parole officers shared recruitment flyers with people who were on parole and released from prison in the past year. Flyers were also hung in parole districts with the researcher’s contact information and a link to access the survey directly or to contact the researcher by phone for further details about the study. Incentives in the form of a $25 gift card were provided for participation. A total of N = 74 people participated in this study across three states. Participant ages ranged from 21–76 years old, with an average age of 47.7 years old (SD = 13.5). Forty-six percent of the sample was between the ages of 18–49, and 54% of the sample was 50 years old or more. The sample was 95% male, and 5% female. Instruments and plan of analysis

Survey questions were modeled from the research questions posed in this study, current literature on this topic, and Benson et al.'s (2011) study on stigmatization and reintegration. The survey instrument consisted of preliminary questions on the respondent’s age, a cognitive assessment of their understanding of the purpose of the survey, demographic questions and current housing and employment. A preliminary question was created and utilized to ensure the respondents understood the survey’s purpose. Questions on feelings of stigma and reintegration were derived from the Benson et al. (2011) questionnaire. Questions were reworded to reflect participants’ status as people on parole, rather than incarcerated which was the case in the original study. The current study included a five response Likert-scale to measure perceptions of stigma and reintegration. Questions were also reverse coded to ensure consistency and reduce bias. Responses ranged from Strongly agree to Strongly disagree. Closing questions inquired about having children and one’s level of education, both to understand the surveyed population but also to provide a measure of family bonds and potential embeddedness and commitment in the community (Hirschi, 1998).

Dependent variables

The dependent variables under study are perceptions of stigmatization and reintegration. Stigmatization was operationalized as the respondent’s perception of others’ views cast upon them; this includes feelings of marginalization or the perceptions of being discredited by others and is therefore disintegrative in nature (Braithwaite, 1989b; Goffman, 1963). To measure stigma, the researcher used five Likert-scale questions with responses ranging from Strongly disagree to Strongly agree. Some questions were reverse coded to ensure consistency across responses. Reintegration is the perception that one will be accepted and supported by others after committing a crime or being incarcerated (Benson et al., 2011). According to Braithwaite (1989a) this includes “gestures of reacceptance into the law-abiding community” (p.55). Therefore, reintegration was operationalized as the perception that the respondents have been accepted back into the community and their families based on the gestures of others. To measure reintegration, the researcher used five Likert-scale questions with responses ranging from Strongly disagree to Strongly agree. Unlike the Benson et al (2011) study, a question was added to measure family trust of the respondent by allowing them to stay in their home. Family support among older formerly incarcerated people wanes over time (Clarke, 2017; Western et al., 2015), therefore providing respondents the opportunity to gauge their perceptions of family support is warranted to further understand familial relationships among this population.

Independent variables

Demographic characteristics

Demographic variables were used to determine if they were associated with feelings of stigma and reintegration. Research on age and stigma shows that younger people are more likely to reconnect with family members and friends and therefore have greater feelings of redeemability (Benson et al., 2001). Research by Western et al (2015) and others (Clarke, 2017) has shown that family support after prison declines with age, therefore indicating the relationship between age and perceptions of stigma and reintegration may be predicated on age. Age was measured as a binary variable, with ages 20–49 comprising the “younger group” and the 50 and over category comprising the “older group.” Fifty years old was used as the cut off because most research uses 50 as the lower end cut off for “elderly” in prison (Skarupski et al., 2018).

Research on sex, race, stigma, and reintegration consistently shows that African American and Hispanic men are stigmatized and are overly represented in the criminal justice system (Tyler & Brockman, 2017). Therefore, understanding the relationship between race and perceptions of stigma and reintegration are relevant here. In this study, there were only three Hispanic male participants, so Black and Hispanic participant categories were collapsed into one category “non-White,” with “White” being the other racial/ethnic category. Education level was included in the analysis to understand the relationship between the dependent variables. Based on prior research one can hypothesize that those that have a higher level of education are no more likely to be accepted back into society than those without (Cerda-Jara, Elster, & Harding, 2020) and continue to experience stigma but may have greater feelings of reintegration (Evans et al., 2018).

Traditional reentry concerns

Current housing and employment since release were included in the analysis. The relationship between stable housing and criminality is well known in that the more likely one has stable housing, the less likely they are to engage in criminality, and therefore the more likely they feel reintegrated into society (Couloute, 2018; Keene et al., 2018). Further, the more likely someone is homeless, the more likely they experience stigmatization (Tyler & Brockmann, 2017). Here, the variable living situation consists of five attributes: living with family and friends, rent/own, shelter, halfway house, or other. Those that are living with family and friends have been accepted back into their families; this would be indicative of familial support and intact social bonds which are supportive of reintegration. However, those who are living in shelters or halfway houses may not have strong family or friendship bonds, which may indicate stigmatization (Clarke, 2017; Clarke & Allen-McCombs, 2025).

Employment was also measured as an indicator of both reintegration and stigma. Past research has indicated that the inability to gain employment after incarceration is a stigmatizing experience (Grosholz et al., 2024). Here, employment was measured as a binary variable, where respondents would share whether they have found a job since their release from prison. Obtaining employment within the period of the study, up to one year since release from prison, provides a window into how successful their efforts have been in finding employment and would indicate whether they perceived they were facing stigmatization during their search.

Personal concerns

Personal concerns such as whether someone has children or has a serious medical history were included in the analysis. The presence or attachment of children may be indicative of the social bonds needed to remain crime free. Having children does not determine whether someone feels stigmatized or reintegrated but may offer insight into family dynamics and relationships. A question about one’s medical history was included as a binary yes/no question. Prior research has shown a negative relationship with mental illness, substance abuse, cognitive decline, and stigma (Link et al., 2019; Seaward et al., 2023). Therefore, this variable was evaluated in this study.

Plan of analysis

Fisher’s Exact test was used as the primary method of analysis. All the quantitative data collected in this study was categorical in nature; therefore, contingency tables were used to analyze the association or relationship between the variables. Because the sample size is small, the Chi-square analysis is only an approximation and may produce inaccurate results, especially where the expected cell counts or values are less than five (Bower, 2003). Fisher’s exact tests are more precise because “it uses the exact hypergeometric distribution” compared to the chi-square approximation to calculate the p-value (Bower, 2003, p.3). Like the Chi-square analysis, when using Fisher's exact test, there is an assumption that the variables are independent (Freeman & Campbell, 2007). This means that “the outcome for one observation is not associated with the outcome of any other observation” (Nowacki, 2017, e22.) Chi-square significance tests were also executed and reported in the tables, but should be interpreted cautiously, possibly indicating potential relationships between variables. Data files from all three states and both study periods were merged for analysis.

Results

Despite prior research on the positive relationship between age, criminal history, and stigma (Benson et al., 2011; Maschi & Leibowitz, 2018), results indicated age was only associated with perceptions of reintegration on one measure addressing friendships after prison and no stigma related measures met statistical significance based on age. Several of the statistically significant findings are based on sex, and given the small female population, should be interpreted with caution. Stigma

I think I will be able to find a job (reverse coded)

Results on measures of stigma can be found in Table 2. When considering the relationship between employment and stigma, race was the only variable that was statistically significant. A 2 × 2 Fisher’s exact test (FET) indicated that the relationship between race and whether people felt they were able to find a job was marginally significant at the .10 level (p = .087). Descriptive statistics indicated that 57% (n = 17) of White and 89% (n = 17) of non-White respondents agreed and strongly agreed that they would be able to find a job (n = 34, 71%). This indicated that non-White respondents were less likely to report perceptions of stigma related to their job search than the White respondents.

Since my release, my family has supported me. (reverse coded)

There was also a statistically significant association between the variables sex, living situation, job since release, and perceptions of family support since release. Sex was statistically significant at the .05 level (p = .017), where 75% (n = 51) of the men agreed or strongly agreed that their family has supported them since they were released from prison. Among the female respondents, 75% (n = 3) strongly disagreed that their family has supported them since their release from prison. In this study, men were more likely to report believing their family would support them compared to women overall. While the female sample is considerably smaller than the male sample, they did not feel supported. One’s reported living situation and perceptions of family support were also statistically significant (p = .024). Over 80% (n = 17) of respondents who rent or own their own apartment, 77% (n = 11) of respondents that lived with their family or friends and 73% (n = 19) or respondents who reported living in a halfway house agreed or strongly agreed that their family has been supportive since their release. The relationship between job since release and family support is marginally statistically significant at the .10 level (p = .056). Seventy-two percent (n = 28) of those reporting having a job since release agreed or strongly agreed that their family has supported them. Conversely, approximately 74% (n = 26) of those who did not have a job also agreed or strongly agreed, indicating that employment since release was not a factor in family perceptions of support after prison in this study.

I think I can make friends with people who are not involved in criminal activity. (reverse coded)

The ability to build pro-social connections after prison is a barometer of social capital and community support which impacts perceptions of stigma or reintegration. Fisher’s exact tests revealed that there were marginally significant associations at the .10 level with sex (p = .098) and race (p = .058) and the perception that one can make friends with people who are not involved in criminal activity. Over 90% (n = 64) of the male respondents agreed or strongly agreed that they could make friends with people who are not involved in criminal activity. All of the non-White respondents (100%, n = 30) and 81% (n = 35) of White respondents agreed or strongly agreed that they could make friends with people who are not involved in criminal activity.

Reintegration

My family trusts me to stay in their home

When questioning whether their family trusts the respondents in their home, the FET indicated that race, sex, and living situation were statistically significant. Sex was statistically significant at the .01 level (p = .007), where 74% (n = 48) of men agreed or strongly agreed that their family trusted them to stay in their home. While only three women responded to this question, it was clear that they did not feel that their family trusted them in their home. Race also was statistically associated with family trust in staying their home at the .000 level (p = .000) where 54% (n = 22) of White respondents and 96% (n = 27) of non-White respondents agreed or strongly agreed that their family trusts the respondents to stay in the family home. In this case, all non-white respondents perceived that their family held fewer stigmatizing views of them compared to the White respondents. Living situation and trust staying in the family home was also statistically significant (p = .000), where those living in their own apartment (n = 16, 89%) and those living with family and friends (n = 11, 85%) were most likely to agree or strongly agree with this statement.

Even if my family is angry about my criminal past, they have been supportive since my release

Three variables were statistically significant when considering the above statement. FET revealed that the variables sex (p = .019), race (p = .025), and living situation (p = .019) were statistically significant at the .05 level. Over 90% (n = 62) of male respondents agreed or strongly agreed that their family has been supportive since their release, even if they were angry in the past. Considering race, 97% (n = 28) of non-White respondents and 83% (n = 35) of White respondents agreed or strongly agreed with the above statement. Those staying with family and friends (n = 12, 100%), or renting/owning their own apartment (n = 19, 90%) were most likely to agree that their family has been supportive since their release, and for at least twelve respondents, that included living with them after release from prison.

Despite my conviction, my friends will still like me now that I am out of prison

Age (p = .079) was marginally statistically significant at the .10 level when respondents were given the statement “despite my conviction, my friends will still like me now that I am out of prison,” with 85% (n = 29) respondents under the age of 50 years old agreeing and strongly agreeing with this statement compared to 70% (n = 28) of respondents over 50 years old. Race was also statistically significant (p. = 029); 83% (n = 25) of non-White respondents and 72% (n = 31) of White respondents agreed or strongly agreed that their friends would still like them now that they were out of prison. Having a job since release was also statistically significant (p = .019), with 85% (n = 33) of respondents who found employment agreeing or strongly agreeing with this statement, compared to 68% (n = 23) of respondents who were not employed. Living situation was marginally significant at the .10 level (p = .055), with 86% (n = 19) of those who rent or own and approximately 77% (n = 10) of people who were living with family and friends agreeing or strongly agreeing with this statement.

I believe I’ll be all alone in the world

The final question that measured stigma addressed feelings of isolation and stigmatization. The FET indicated that there was a significant association between sex and perceptions of being alone (p = .029), have children (p = .001) and job since release (p = .018). Fifty-three (75%) male respondents disagreed or strongly disagreed with this statement, rejecting the notion that they would be stigmatized and isolated from others. Eighty percent (n = 37) of respondents who had children disagreed or strongly disagreed with this statement compared to 64% (n = 18) who did not have children. There was also a significant relationship between having a job since being released from prison and perceptions of being alone (p = .018). Over 89% (n = 34) of respondents who were employed or had found a job since they were released from prison disagreed or strongly disagreed with this statement, compared to 56% (n = 19) of those who were not employed, showing that those who were already connected to others through employment were less likely to report stigma in this category.

Questions without statistical significance

Fisher’s exact tests revealed that there was no statistically significant relationship between the independent variables listed above and the statements:”now that I am out of prison my friends will help me find a job” (reintegration), “at this point, I feel as though the world is against me” (stigma), and “if I straighten up my life, I should not have a problem readjusting back into society” (reintegration).

Discussion

While the aim of this study was to understand the relationship between age and perceptions of stigma and reintegration, results indicated that age was not associated with the two outcome variables. Other variables like sex, race, living arrangements and employment were associated with perceptions of stigma and reintegration, supporting prior research on this topic especially as it relates to housing and employment (Keene et al., 2018; Leasure, 2019; Tyler & Brockmann, 2017). Unsurprisingly, one’s employment status after release from prison and their subsequent living arrangements were statistically significant and a well-known barometer of reintegration and stigma after prison. Having stable housing and secure employment meets Maslow’s (1943) primary physiological and safety needs, not to mention meeting parole requirements and reintegrating into society after prison.

The men in this study overwhelmingly did not report perceptions of stigmatization, rather it can be argued that they had a positive view of their ability to retain and build new friendships and received the help and support of their families. This highlights how important family support is to minimize feelings of stigma and improve the reentry experience. In this study, it was clear that the male respondents perceived their family members as supportive. Theoretically it can be argued that they felt reintegrative shaming was being practiced by their families given their perceptions of support (Braithwaite, 1989a). Non-White respondents (Black and Hispanic) disproportionately believed in their ability to find work, make friends, and be supported by family members compared to White respondents. This may be indicative of stronger familial support, effective reintegration, or a more positive outlook compared to White respondents. Further research should investigate the relationship between race and perceptions of stigma, reintegration and support among family and friends.

Age was not associated with perceptions of stigma in this study; this could be considered an encouraging finding. Although there are few prior studies that have explored the differences in stigma based on age (Western et al., 2015), results indicated that age was not a factor in perceptions of stigma. This indicates that future research on reintegration and stigma should be centered around other outcome measures such as age-appropriate housing or diverse types of employment, specific familial relationships, such as among children or grandchildren, and perceptions of acceptance or rejection.

Age was also not associated with perceptions of reintegration, except for one measure related to acceptance by friends after incarceration. Here, younger people were more likely to report that they agreed or strongly agreed with this statement, supporting prior research (Benson et al., 2011). Among older people, it is possible that they were incarcerated for longer or more serious offenses, therefore rejection by peers is more likely (Feng & Cornwell 2025). Furthermore, it is quite possible that acceptance and number of friends is less relevant as one ages, however social connections are important, so the fact that this measure was not significant can be cause for concern later in life.

Given the results here, one cannot understate the importance of familial support and access to housing and employment after incarceration. While causality could not be established, there appears to be a reciprocal relationship between family support, housing, and employment. Prison programs should address repairing family relationships so they can be “activated” upon release (Draine & Wolff, 2009). Familial visitation while incarcerated enables maintenance of familial relationships and provides stability during the reentry experience and reduces stigma upon release and should be encouraged by correctional institutions.

The benefits of secure housing and perceptions of reintegration, as opposed to stigma, cannot be understated. Given the challenges people face acquiring housing when leaving prison, greater housing options should be available. Understanding this, organizations like Osborne Association and Fortune Society have developed housing programs that provide social services for older and younger people alike to reduce the stigma of incarceration and increase the likelihood of effective reintegration. In New Jersey, a program titled “the village of hope” provides transitional housing and support services for people who are prone to homelessness (Pugliese & Mellow, 2025). Future research on such programs should consider their impact on perceptions of stigma, reintegration, and well-being.

Given the findings, Braithwaite’s (1989a) theoretical framework does not adequately account for older adult’s experiences with reintegrative and disintegrative shaming. This is especially true among those that do not have family members as a means for informal social control. Furthermore, granted that older people are less likely to commit crimes anyway and respondents in this study were less concerned about friendships, the need for disintegrative shaming among older adults is debatable at best. However, given that older people in general are less socially connected, reintegrative practices should be emphasized among this population.

Limitations

The aim of this study was to understand how reentry variables impact perceptions of stigma and reintegration among older adults. This study is not without limitations. First, the sample size is small, therefore one cannot generalize the findings based on the population under study. Second, the respondents in this study were predominantly male, therefore the findings are not likely generalizable to the female formerly incarcerated population on parole. Third, this survey was conducted in English, thereby excluding people who speak other languages. Fourth, this sample may be impacted by participation bias. Prior research has shown there are significant differences between people who choose to participate in research and those who do not, which would skew survey results (Elston, 2021). Lastly, further information about the participants, including criminal history, would have increased the understanding of the findings here and potentially determined if responses were based on sentence length.

Conclusion

The central aim of this was to determine if age was a significant factor in perceptions of stigma or reintegration, especially among formerly incarcerated older adults. While research indicated that age was not significant in this study, race, sex, housing arrangements, and employment since release were associated with measures of stigma and reintegration. Further research should investigate other variables that might provide further insight into what impacts perceptions of stigma or reintegration, particularly among the older returning population. Providing appropriate opportunities for family bonding while incarcerated will strengthen feelings of support and reduce perceptions of stigma. Having stable employment and housing will also reduce perceptions of stigma and increase overall wellbeing as one reenters society, providing a greater measure of public safety for us all.
