Seizures and Apneas as clinical manifestations of brain lesions in term infants: a single center experience
Authors: Aleksandra Skubisz, Andrea Calandrino, Francesco Vinci, Paolo Massirio, Chiara Andreato, Samuele Caruggi, Marcella Battaglini, Alessandro Parodi, Giorgia Brigati, Mariya Malova, Marta Bertamino, Domenico Tortora, Mariasavina Severino, Martina Resaz, Elisabetta Amadori, Sara Uccella, Lino Nobili, Andrea Rossi, Pasquale Striano, Luca Antonio Ramenghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeizures and apneas are always concerning symptoms in near-term and term newborns, but finding a clear explanation is not always easy. In addition, it is not always clear which investigations should be promptly conducted. We aimed to evaluate the relationship (or lack thereof) between apneas and seizures and the role of MRI and EEG in their investigation.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis including infants born at ≥ 36 + 0 weeks of gestational age who presented with seizures or apneas soon after birth and underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) during their stay at a tertiary-level academic Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) in Italy between January 2016 and July 2024. Brain MRI was systematically planned for to all term infants with clinical presentations of seizures, recurrent unexplained apneas, or abnormal aEEG/EEG.ResultsOur inclusion criteria were met by 58 (18%) patients; 31/58, (53%) presented with seizures and 27/58 (47%) with apneas without overt seizures manifestation. Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were similar across clinical subgroups. Neurophysiological monitoring (EEG/aEEG) was performed in 50/58 (86%) infants; all 31 infants with clinical motoric seizures had abnormality on the EEG, though in 20 (65%) apneas were still their primary manifestation. Of the 27 presenting with apneas without documented EEG seizures, 6 experienced apneas only shortly after birth, 6 only with feeding (EEGs not done consistently) and of the other 15 the EEG was normal in the 12 who were monitored. Abnormal MRI findings were present in 27/31 (87%) infants with seizures and in 10/27 (37%) in the apnea only group. Focal arterial territory ischemic stroke was the commonest single diagnosis. Seizure-related apneas were associated with punctate white matter injury, focal ischemic stroke and severe intraventricular hemorrhage.ConclusionsSeizures and apneas occur with similar frequency in newborns born near or at term investigated with cerebral MRI for seizures and apneeas-related encephalopathy. The majority of infants had abnormal brain imaging, almost all of an ischaemic or haemorrhage nature. We strongly recommend brain imaging to assess potential brain injury in (near)term neonates with either of these clinical signs.

Introduction

Seizures and apneas are common clinical manifestations in newborns and may indicate a variety of underlying pathologies. Preterm newborns, due to their developmental immaturity, frequently experience apneas, typically associated with prematurity-related conditions, while seizures often remain unrecognized in this population. However, in term newborns, these symptoms occur less frequently and often require further investigation to identify their etiology [1–3].

Compared to seizures occurring at later developmental stages, neonatal seizures differ in their clinical presentation, electroencephalographic (EEG) features, etiologies, management, and outcomes. The estimated rate of seizures in term newborns is approximately 1 to 3 per 1000 live births. Most neonatal seizures are acute symptomatic events resulting from recent brain injury or ongoing neurological dysfunction [4]. These seizures can arise from a wide range of causes, including hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, focal injurious brain lesions (such as intracerebral, subarachnoid, intraventricular hemorrhage, or ischemic stroke), transient metabolic disturbances (e.g., hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hypoglycemia), central nervous system or systemic infection, genetic disorders or brain malformations [4, 5]. Seizures not only signal underlying brain injury but can also exacerbate it, as the developing brain is particularly vulnerable to both the primary injury and secondary seizure-induced damage [6]. One of the clinical manifestations of subtle seizures is seizure-related apnea. This phenomenon has been well-documented in term newborns and is often associated with bradycardia, differing from non-ictal apneas by the presence of focal rhythmic epileptic activity, frequently (though not exclusively) localized to the temporal region [7–11]. In term neonates, apnea is observed in approximately 0.1–0.2% of all live births [12].

Apnea is a more common event in the neonatal period, particularly in preterm infants, and can be due multiple etiologies. Apnea in term newborns occurs at a rate of one per 1000 live births and is less well understood. Apneic episodes in term or near-term newborns are nearly always associated with serious identifiable causes, such as birth asphyxia, intracranial hemorrhage, seizures, or drug-induced respiratory depression. However, apnea has also been observed in conjunction with electrographic seizure activity, which is more commonly seen in term newborns then in preterm ones [7, 13, 14].

In this study, we aimed to describe the underlying causes of apneas and seizures in a cohort of late preterm and term newborns, but also to examine the relationship between clinical manifestations and MRI findings. In particular, we intended to determine whether patients presenting with apneic seizures show specific patterns of brain injuries.

Methods

Study settings

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on newborns admitted to the highest-level neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at the Instituto Giannina Gaslini in Genoa, Italy. The patients underwent brain MRI due to indications such as neonatal seizures, apnea or abnormal findings on amplitude-integrated electroencephalogram (aEEG) or conventional electroencephalogram (EEG) between January 2016 and July 2024. Conventional full‑array EEG was usually initiated within 2 h from the suspicious event and, when feasible, continued for at least 24 h. In infants whose first manifestation of seizures was apnea, EEG/aEEG monitoring was initiated after the apneic episodes had been recognized as potentially seizure‑related, whereas in infants presenting with other clinical seizure manifestations, neuromonitoring was usually started during the ongoing events or immediately thereafter. Cranial ultrasound (CUS) was routinely performed in term and near‑term infants with seizures or apneas as part of the standard neuroimaging work‑up. In our unit, CUS is usually carried out within the first 24–48 h after admission using high‑frequency transducers through the anterior and mastoid fontanelle, with additional acoustic windows used when clinically indicated. Brain MRI was systematically planned for all late preterm and term infants with clinical presentations of seizures, recurrent unexplained apneas, or abnormal aEEG/EEG, and was withheld only in cases of severe clinical instability, lack of parental consent, or anticipated transfer before MRI could be safely scheduled. The NICU admits both outborn patients referred for specialized care and inborn newborns with pregnancy-related complications. Referral was made for diagnostic evaluation, neuromonitoring and, when required, life‑supportive treatment, because referring centers were not equipped to provide advanced neurodiagnostic work‑up or specialized respiratory support for these patients. The study was conducted in compliance with the terms of the Helsinki Declaration, and written informed consent for enrollment and publication of individual clinical details was obtained from parents. In Italy, this type of clinical study does not require the approval of the Institutional Review Board/ Institutional Ethics Committee to publish the results.

Definitions

A position paper published by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) Task Force on Neonatal Seizures in 2021 established a diagnostic framework for neonatal seizures [15]. It recommended that neonates in critical condition or with suspected seizures undergo conventional full-array continuous EEG (EEG) – considered the gold standard – or if EEG is unavailable, a limited-channel amplitude-integrated EEG (aEEG). The paper defined a seizure as: “an electrographic event with a pattern characterized by sudden, repetitive, evolving stereotyped waveforms with a beginning and end” While no specific duration is mandated, the seizure must last long enough to exhibit changes in frequency and morphology, allowing for the recognition of onset, progression, and resolution of the abnormal discharge in the recordings.

Seizures identified via electrography are classified as either (1) electrographic-only seizures (without clinical manifestations) or (2) electroclinical seizures (with clinical signs). Electroclinical seizures are further subdivided into motor, non-motor, sequential, or unclassified categories. Motor seizures include clonic, tonic, generalized, and myoclonic seizures. Among other motor seizure types are automatisms or subtle seizures, which may present as apneas. The clinical manifestations of seizures can evolve over time, meaning the initial signs may differ from those that become predominant ones. In our study, we classified seizures based on their predominant clinical presentation, following the ILAE guidelines [15].

Apnea is considered pathologic (an apneic episode) when airflow absence persists for 20 s or more, or when it is accompanied by bradycardia (heart rate below 100 beats per minute) or hypoxemia, which may be clinically evident (cyanosis) or detected via oxygen saturation monitoring, defined in this study as peripheral oxygen saturation below 90%. Apnea is categorized based on whether the absence of airflow is accompanied by continued inspiratory efforts and upper airway obstruction, allowing for the differentiation of three types of apneas: (1) Central apnea, where inspiratory efforts are absent (2), Obstructive apnea, where inspiratory efforts persist in the presence of airway obstruction, and (3) Mixed apnea, where airway obstruction with inspiratory efforts either precedes or follows central apnea [16].

The primary outcome measure in this study was a result on the first MRI scan performed.

Data collection

Data were extracted from an institutional NICU registry. We retrospectively reviewed all the clinical records of included patients. Patient demographic characteristics included gestational age (GA) at birth, birth weight and sex. Perinatal history included place of delivery (Istituto Giannina Gaslini or another center of Liguria region), plurality of pregnancy, prenatal disorders (intrauterine growth restriction), mode of delivery, Apgar scores at first and fifth minute after delivery, need for mechanical ventilation, surfactant administration, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), necrotizing enterocolitis, patent ductus arteriosus, sepsis, need for transfusions: platelets, plasma and red blood cells. We collected the onset time and characteristics of apneas and seizures, the results of EEG monitoring, either using conventional full-array continuous EEG (EEG) or limited-channel amplitude-integrated EEG (aEEG) and the need of antiseizure medications (ASMs). aEEG recordings were obtained using the Olympic Brainz® Monitor, which provides two‑channel aEEG together with raw EEG from two electrodes over each hemisphere; recordings typically lasted 12–24 h. Continuous EEG monitoring was performed with a small‑footprint 32‑channel Micromed Brain Quick® system, with sampling rates of 128–256 samples/s/channel. We took data from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed on patients to diagnose clinical symptoms occurring during their hospitalizations. Brain MRI was performed using a 3T MRI scanner with Turbo Spin Echo (TSE), isotropic T1- weighted 3D (3DT1), Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery 3D (3DFLAIR), Susceptibility Weighted Imaging (SWI) sequences in the three planes of space. Examination was completed with diffusion study (Diffusion Weighted Imaging (DWI), Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC), Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI)), perfusion study (3D pseudo-Continuous Arterial Spin Labeling (pCASL)), and angioRM (arterial TOF3D and venous PC), and resting-state fMRI (BOLD) techniques.

We excluded infants with incomplete clinical or neuroimaging documentation (n = 2). Additionally, we did not include patients diagnosed with congenital cytomegalovirus (cCMV) infection or neonatal meningoencephalitides, and cases in which sepsis was clinically present at the time of neurological symptoms, as these etiologies could independently account for apneas or seizures.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies (percentages); while continuous variables are described using mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS for Windows, version 18 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, Illinois USA).

Results

Patients characteristics

Of the 320 patients (≥ 36 weeks GA) who underwent brain MRI during the study period, 58 (18%) met our inclusion criteria. (Fig. 2) The median gestational age of the study cohort was 39,1 weeks (IQR 2,4 weeks), and the mean birth weight was 3208 ± 548,7 g (mean ± SD). The study population included 32 males (55%) and 26 females (45%). As the region’s highest referral center, most patients (74%) were born at other facilities and transferred after delivery. Nearly all newborns were from singleton pregnancies, with the mode of delivery evenly distributed between cesarean section and vaginal birth. One patient was diagnosed with intrauterine growth restriction during pregnancy. Mechanical ventilation was required for 16 (28%) newborns, although only one needed surfactant administration. Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were similar across clinical subgroups and did not show relevant differences. (Table 1) Ten patients from the study group were diagnosed with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). None of the patients was diagnosed with necrotizing enterocolitis, but seven had a patent ductus arteriosus presented at birth. Sepsis was diagnosed in three patients, but this occurred significantly later and was unrelated to the neurological symptoms described in the study. No patients required a red blood cell transfusion, however, two received plasma transfusions, and another one received platelet transfusions.

Cranial ultrasound (CUS) was performed in most infants as part of routine NICU evaluation. CUS detected abnormalities in only three patients, all representing high-grade intraventricular hemorrhage. In the remaining newborns, CUS was normal despite the later identification of ischemic lesions, temporal-lobe hemorrhages, venous infarctions or white matter lesions on MRI.

Seizures and apneas

Electroencephalogram monitoring was performed in 50/58 infants: 48 underwent conventional EEG (7 also with aEEG) and 2 had aEEG alone. Among the 58 patients included in the study, 31 presented with clinical seizures, including seizure manifestations such as apnea or motor presentations. The median age of seizure onset was the second day after birth (IQR 1). Abnormal EEG or aEEG recordings were detected in 27 of these 31 newborns, and 24 received antiseizure medications.

Within this group of 31 patients with clinical seizures, 20 patients had seizures primarily manifesting as apneas. All 20 patients underwent EEG, which showed abnormal findings. Of these 20 patients, 19 received antiseizure medications. Five newborns later developed other seizure presentations, such as limb tremors or abnormal ocular movements (eye deviation) (Fig. 1).

The remaining group consisted of 27 patients who experienced apneas without associated seizure activity. Within this group, six patients had apneas around feeding times, and another six experienced apneas during the first minutes after birth, which did not recur later during hospitalization. The remaining 15 patients experienced apneas that did not correlate with the above scenarios. Of these, 12 underwent EEG monitoring, all of which showed normal results, suggesting an absence of detectable electrical abnormalities despite the occurrence of apneas. Among the remaining three cases, one patient was diagnosed with laryngomalacia, another with a PHOX2B mutation associated with congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, and the third had bronchiolitis. Notably, only three patients with isolated apneas exhibited symptoms on the first day after birth, with the rest showed a later onset (range 1 to 5 days after birth).

In our cohort, 10 patients were diagnosed with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). Among these, five had apnea-related seizures, and one presented with generalized motor seizures.

Seizure-related apneas

Among the 20 children classified as having seizure-related apneas, the sequence of symptom presentation varied significantly. (Fig. 2) Continuous EEG monitoring of at least 24 h was only initiated following specific clinical indications, meaning the earliest symptoms frequently occurred without corresponding EEG data. In many cases, these initial symptoms were observed several hours to 1–2 days before the next set of symptoms, which were subsequently captured by EEG recordings (up to a maximum of 5 days). Apnea was the sole clinical manifestation of seizures in 10 newborns. In the remaining cases, additional clinical signs, such as limb tremors and abnormal eye movements, were observed and EEG recordings showed concomitant abnormalities during the apneic episodes, supporting their interpretation as seizure‑related.

MRI Findings

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed the highest percentage of brain injuries in patients presenting with seizures (87%). (Fig. 2) The most common pathology identified was ischemic stroke, observed in apnea-related seizures or another motor seizures. The ischemic regions differed between these groups; in apnea-related seizures, the injuries frequently involved the temporal lobe or were severe enough to affect this region. Additionally, two patients had lobar hemorrhages in area of temporal lobes, one of which was bilateral and there also presented apnea and motor seizures. Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) was identified in ten patients. In five of these newborns, MRI showed white matter injuries and 1 patient had isolated mild hypoplasia of the pons (reduction of the anteroposterior diameter), which is an atypical finding, particularly in the context of additional HIE. The remaining 4 of the patients has no obvious changes in MRI imaging. Three newborns in the cohort developed severe intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) complicated by post-hemorrhagic ventricular dilation (PHVD). In two cases, IVH occurred without antecedent CSVT, while in one patient, CSVT was initially diagnosed and led secondarily to IVH. All three patients experienced both seizures and apneas and were treated with antiseizure medications. The last patient in the group patients with motor seizures are the one with hypoplasia of the pontine protrusion accompanied by macrocrania and a nonsignificant venous malformation. (Fig. 3)

The two patients with pons hypoplasia (one mentioned above) exhibited apneas without seizures, and their EEG results were normal. Genetic analysis revealed chromosomal abnormalities in these patients: one had trisomy 21 and the other a 7q22.3 chromosomal anomaly.

In the group of patients with apnea unrelated to seizures, nearly 40% had abnormal MRI results. The most common finding was punctate white matter lesions. Other intracranial abnormalities included a fourth-ventricle cyst and germinolytic cysts. One patient was diagnosed with arterial ischemic stroke affecting multiple vascular territories. The only hemorrhagic lesions in this group were a sub-galeal hematoma and a cephalohematoma, both without adjacent brain injury and therefore considered extracranial birth-related lesions. (Fig. 3) Brain injuries were more prevalent in newborns whose apneas occurred later during the hospital stay, rather than immediately after birth or in relation to feeding. The correlations between clinical presentation (seizures, apnea) and MRI findings are summarized in Fig. 2, highlighting the prevalence of specific brain injuries in each clinical subgroup.

Discussion

The distinction between apneic manifestation of seizures and non-epileptic apneas remains a major clinical challenge. Our study demonstrates that seizures and apneas in term and near-term neonates frequently reflect underlying brain injury, although the mechanisms and radiologic patterns differ substantially between electroclinical seizure-related apneas and isolated, non-epileptic apneas. The clinical presentation of most seizures in our cohort appeared to be apneas. Nearly all patients with seizures had abnormal MRI findings, though the characteristics of these injuries varied slightly between the group with seizure-related apneas and those with other seizure presentations. In patients with apneas without electroencephalographic activity, brain injuries were less common and predominantly consisted of punctate white matter lesions [17].

Identifying whether an apneic episode represents a seizure-related event remains clinically challenging. Apnea may be the initial or even the only manifestation of a seizure, and subtle focal electrographic abnormalities can easily be missed without prolonged or full-array EEG monitoring. In our cohort, all infants with seizure-related apneas exhibited clear EEG abnormalities, whereas those with isolated apneas had normal recordings, underscoring the need for early and comprehensive neurophysiological evaluation. These observations highlight that unexplained or recurrent apneic episodes in term neonates should prompt timely EEG assessment, especially when no respiratory, metabolic or infectious cause is identified.

Our findings are consistent with previous research identifying seizures as significant indicators of brain injury in newborns. Different etiologies may slightly modify the timing of seizure onset, but our findings remain consistent with reports indicating that the vast majority of seizures occur within 72 h after birth [18]. The unique characteristics of the developing brain along with the activation of common pathways by both the initial injury and secondary seizure-induced injury, complicate the differentiation of their specific contributions to the appearance and characteristics of brain injuries [6]. Previously studies have identified the most common etiologies of brain injuries in patients with seizures as: HIE, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, IVH or other types of hemorrhagic stroke, congenital brain abnormalities and chromosomal abnormalities [19].

Our study demonstrates that seizure-related apnea corresponds to specific injuries which differ slightly from those associated with other seizure presentations. (Fig. 3) These seizures are rarely isolated and are associated with intraventricular hemorrhage or other hemorrhagic lesions in the temporal and occipital lobes. Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the phenomenon of apneic seizures in newborns [10, 20–23]. The 2021 ILAE classification identifies autonomic seizures, which may involve respiratory disturbances such as apnea and recommends mandatory EEG or aEEG for diagnosis [15]. Recent evidence emphasizes the clinical significance of apneic seizures, supporting ILAE recommendations that apneic seizures should be confirmed by rapid EEG monitoring or, in cases with no clear etiology, by considering genetic testing [15, 21, 24].

In our cohort, amplitude-integrated EEG (aEEG) served primarily as a bedside screening tool, whereas conventional full-array EEG provided substantially higher diagnostic precision. All infants with apneic seizures showed electrographic abnormalities on conventional EEG, including subtle focal discharges not consistently visible on aEEG. This is consistent with ILAE recommendations, which emphasize the superior sensitivity of EEG for detecting brief or focal ictal events. aEEG remained useful for early monitoring, particularly in clinically unstable infants, but lacked the spatial and temporal resolution required to characterize seizures associated with apnea [15]. These findings highlight that, in term infants with unexplained apneas, conventional EEG should be performed whenever available.

Our findings showed that patients presenting with apneas without a seizure origin may also have abnormal MRI results, most commonly consisting of white matter injury and punctate white matter lesions (PWML), which have been described as characteristic white matter abnormalities of the developing brain in preterm infants and also in neonates with congenital heart disease or perinatal asphyxia [25–27]. In previous studies of preterm infants, PWML were frequently observed and apneas at birth were common among affected infants [28, 29]. The nature of these lesions is considered ischemic or inflammatory, probably reflecting the mild forms of periventricular leukomalacia originally described in post-mortem studies of premature infants, although more recent work has suggested that some lesions may be hemorrhagic in origin [1, 30]. (Fig. 2) Hayman et al. further reported that punctate white matter lesions in full‑term newborns can be associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcome, particularly in the presence of genetic disorders [26]; however, we did not have systematic long‑term follow‑up or detailed aetiological data, so we cannot directly assess similar motor outcomes in our cohort.

Non-ictal apneas, especially when prolonged and accompanied by bradycardia, can reduce systemic blood pressure and contribute to cerebral hypoperfusion, leading to further hypoxic-ischemic insults in the immature brain, potentially aggravating existing harm [31]. Additionally, various structural lesions and acute injuries affecting the brainstem and surrounding regions can severely impair respiratory functions in newborns and then trigger apnea [32]. The level or duration of bradycardia or desaturation that may increase the risk of neurodevelopmental impairment remains unclear [16]. Studies investigating the significance of apnea are complicated by monitoring challenges and inconsistent nursing reports, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions [33, 34]. Despite these limitations, evidence suggests that a greater number of apnea days, combined with prolonged assisted ventilation, is associated with worse neurodevelopmental outcomes [34].

The timing of apneas may be crucial for further understanding, although it can be interpreted in different ways. Apneic episodes typically begin at 1 or 2 days after birth; if they do not occur within the first 7 days, they are unlikely to appear later [16]. Failure to breathe at birth in the absence of drug-induced depression or asphyxia, is usually due to irreversible structural abnormalities of the central nervous system [16]. Conversely, a difficult delivery can lead to asphyxia and respiratory distress at birth, which, in 10 of our patients, resulted in hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). Six of them later had an abnormal MRI showing lesions consistent with hypoxic-ischaemic-encephalopathy [35]. However, in our cohort, apneas occurring only after birth were also associated with mild hypoplasia of the pons. (Fig. 2) The timing of apneas is also significant when considering their association with feeding. The relationship between apnea and feeding has been established, with irregular respiratory effort in these newborns linked to central nervous system impairment, as indicated by significant elevations in maximum end-tidal carbon dioxide pressure during sleep and abnormal tomography scans, well as abnormal cranial ultrasound imaging [36, 37]. In our study, feeding-related apneas were associated with MRI findings in only two cases, consisted of punctate white matter injury and hypoplasia of the pons (reduction in anteroposterior diameter). The remaining patients with apneas had a higher percentage of abnormal MRIs with varying characteristics. (Fig. 3)

The MRI strategy employed at the study’s center is consistent with the most recent recommendations [38]. However, timing does not result in being always so timely soon after the clinical onset. As shown in Fig. 2, most MRIs in the seizure-related apnea group were performed within a few days after first symptom onset. The median age at which MRI was performed in infants born at the center, without hypothermia for HIE, was 5.5 days after birth, while for those suffering asphyxia who underwent hypothermia, the median was 7 days. For patients transferred from other facilities due to concerning symptoms, the median age for MRI was 8 days. (Table 1) Given that the study population consisted of near-term and full-term infants, the first week after birth seems short enough to assume that the clinical symptoms observed were related to the brain abnormalities detected on MRI.

In our study, six patients presented with cerebral hemorrhagic insults on MRI image, five of them were delivered vaginally, all of whom clinically exhibited apnea. We believe that venous congestion around the time of labor and delivery may contribute to these lesions [39, 40]. Birth-related subdural hemorrhage has been reported as a relatively frequent finding in term infants, particularly after vaginal delivery, and is usually small and self‑limited [41, 42] but none of our infants had sub‑dural haemorrhage on MRI. However, in our cohort one infant had a sub‑galeal haematoma without adjacent brain injury, which we considered separately as an extracranial birth‑related lesion rather than a brain lesion.

Two of our patients were found to have pons hypoplasia on MRI imaging. Both exhibited apneas, but no seizures were observed, and their EEG results were normal. Cerebellar hypoplasia is a relatively common and nonspecific finding that occurs in a very heterogeneous group of disorders. While cerebellar hypoplasia has a broad differential diagnosis, pons hypoplasia significantly narrows the diagnostic options [43, 44]. Although genetic consultation confirmed chromosomal abnormalities in our patients (confirmed trisomy 21 and a 7q22.3 chromosome anomaly), such genetic findings are not typically associated with our MRI findings.

Although cranial ultrasound is a valuable first-line tool in neonatal care, its diagnostic contribution in our cohort of term infants with seizures or apneas was limited. In most infants, CUS was performed only in the early phase after admission, and the majority of MRI‑detected lesions were not visible on ultrasound. This is in line with previous studies in term neonates with arterial ischaemic stroke, where early CUS had limited sensitivity and normal scans did not exclude infarction, particularly when performed within the first days after birth [45, 46]. The higher sensitivities reported for expert‑performed and later CUS in term neonates with arterial stroke suggest that serial and targeted ultrasound might have increased our detection rate; however, even in such settings MRI remains necessary to fully characterize lesion type and extension, and to reliably assess brain injury in term infants with seizures or apneas.

While this study provides valuable insights, it is limited by its retrospective nature. Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up on neurodevelopmental outcomes limits our ability to definitively associate brain injuries detected on MRI with later developmental delays or impairments. Further studies with larger cohorts and prospective follow-ups are needed to assess the long-term neurodevelopmental impact of early brain injuries in neonates with seizures and apneas.

Interestingly, these results suggest that early and routine MRI imaging in neonates with apneas and seizures can be crucial for detecting brain injuries. Additionally, most patients with seizures present apnea as the first clinical manifestation. Therefore, we recommend comprehensive neurological evaluations, including EEG to differentiate between apneas and seizure-related events and MRI to assess potential brain injury in term neonates presenting with either of these clinical signs. This could prompt a shift in current clinical practices, encouraging earlier neuromonitoring and neuroimaging in newborns (at near term or term age) presenting with apneas.
