Lactobacillus johnsonii Alleviates Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor-Associated Colitis by Modulating Macrophage Polarization, Th1/Treg Cell Balance, and Intestinal Barrier Function via the Tryptophan Metabolism-AhR Pathway
Authors: Wei Liang, Shuying He, Heqing Tao, Yuerong Lv, Xianjie Chen, Liang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to clarify the effect of Lactobacillus johnsonii (L. johnsonii) on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)-associated colitis and the efficacy of ICIs. Administration of L. johnsonii mitigated the severity of colitis in the experimental ICI-associated colitis model, along with a decrease in the proportions of M1 macrophages and Th1 cells and an increase in those of M2 macrophages and Tregs in the colon. L. johnsonii intervention upregulated the expression of tight junction proteins. L. johnsonii promoted tryptophan metabolism and the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites. Correlation analysis identified indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) as a significant anti-inflammatory tryptophan metabolite, and gavage of IAA alleviated ICI-associated colitis. After blocking the AhR pathway, the protective effect of L. johnsonii against ICI-associated colitis was abrogated. Notably, in the tumor-bearing ICI-associated colitis model, L. johnsonii potentiated the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs. These findings support the potential clinical applications of L. johnsonii for ICI-associated colitis.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated promising efficacy across a range of tumors, driving their growing adoption in clinical practice. However, alongside their therapeutic benefits, ICIs can trigger immune-related adverse events (irAEs) as a result of excessive immune system activation. These adverse events may involve multiple organs, including the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, and lungs. Among these, ICI-associated colitis stands out as a common irAE, a form of autoimmune colitis caused by the exaggerated immune activation that occurs during treatment with ICIs. ICI therapies primarily encompass anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 treatments, both of which are capable of inducing ICI-associated colitis. Among these, anti-CTLA-4 therapy exhibits the highest incidence rate, at 9.1%. Given the risk of resistance developing with single-agent ICI therapy, combined anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 regimens have emerged in clinical practice. Although combination therapy demonstrates superior efficacy compared to single-agent ICI and its utilization is gradually increasing, the incidence of ICI-associated colitis with combination therapy, approximately 13.6%, is higher than that observed with either anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy.

ICI-associated colitis inflicts substantial harm on patients, however, existing therapeutic approaches remain suboptimal. Clinically, ICI-associated colitis is categorized into grades 1 to 5. Patients with grade 3 or higher ICI-associated colitis require discontinuation of ICIs, which has become a prevalent cause of immunotherapy failure. Furthermore, ICI-associated colitis of grade 4 or higher may even be life-threatening. Current pharmacological management of ICI-associated colitis primarily involves glucocorticoids and biologics such as anti-TNF-α and anti-α4β7-integrin agents. However, these treatments have notable limitations: 1) significant side effects, 2) suppression of antitumor immunity, which may lead to tumor progression, , and 3) nonresponses in some patients, a condition termed refractory ICI-associated colitis. There is an urgent clinical need to develop novel therapeutic approaches for ICI-associated colitis, especially those that do not compromise the efficacy of ICIs.

The gut microbiota is closely linked to human health and has been demonstrated to play a significant role in the pathogenesis of intestinal diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), colorectal cancer, and irritable bowel syndrome. Emerging evidence also indicates a strong association between gut microbiota and the development of ICI-associated colitis. Research has identified distinct compositional differences in the gut microbiome between ICI-treated patients who developed ICI-associated colitis and those who did not. The gut microbiota has also been recognized as a therapeutic target for ICI-associated colitis. A study documented clinical improvements in patients with refractory ICI-associated colitis, who were unresponsive to glucocorticoids, infliximab, and vedolizumab, after fecal microbiota transplantation. Furthermore, preclinical evidence suggested that specific probiotic strains from the genera Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium might promote the remission of ICI-associated colitis without compromising the efficacy of ICIs.

Therefore, probiotics exhibit promising potential for the treatment of ICI-associated colitis. Nonetheless, such probiotics must not only alleviate intestinal inflammation but also avoid compromising the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs. Lactobacillus johnsonii (L. johnsonii), a typical intestinal probiotic, meets these criteria as it promotes the remission of IBD while also being reported to enhance ICI efficacy, rendering it clinically promising for ICI-associated colitis treatment. L. johnsonii is a Gram-positive bacterium widely distributed in the gastrointestinal tracts of various hosts, including humans and mice. Currently, it has also gained recognition for its medical applications. This bacterium has shown therapeutic benefits in conditions such as IBD, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and diabetes. What’s more, L. johnsonii has been demonstrated to possess favorable gastric acid tolerance, intestinal colonization capacity, microbiota adaptability, and safety in humans, highlighting its notable clinical translational potential. −

Despite the demonstrated potential of Lactobacillus probiotics, no studies have yet reported the effects of L. johnsonii on ICI-associated colitis or whether it can preserve ICI efficacy. This study aimed to fill this gap. We investigated the influence of L. johnsonii on the development of ICI-associated colitis by elucidating its effects on intestinal immunity, intestinal barrier, microbiota composition, and metabolomic profiles. Additionally, we evaluated whether this probiotic modulated the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains

L. johnsonii (ATCC 33200) was cultured in de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) broth at 37 °C. When the bacterial cultures reached mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.8), cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/100 μL. Each mouse received 100 μL of this bacterial suspension via daily oral gavage.

Cell Culture and Treatment

The melanoma cell line B16–F10 and the macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 were maintained in DMEM medium (Gibco, C11965500BT, Waltham, America) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10270106, Waltham, America) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122, Waltham, America). The colorectal cancer cell line CT26 was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, C11875500BT, Waltham, America) containing the same supplements. All cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. For macrophage polarization assays, cells were treated with 1 μg/mL LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, L2880, St. Louis, America) for 24 h to induce the M1 phenotype, or with 20 ng/mL IL-4 (PeproTech, 214–14, New Jersey, America) and 20 ng/mL IL-13 (PeproTech, 210–13, New Jersey, America) for 24 h to induce the M2 phenotype. Cells were pretreated with 50 μM indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, MedChemExpress, HY-18569, New Jersey, America) for 12 h prior to costimulation with the polarization-inducing factors for 24 h. Polarization markers were subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry.

Animal Experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the Affiliated First Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Approval No. 20250179), and followed relevant regulations of the China Animal Protection Committee. Eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Mice were given 3% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS, MP Biomedicals, 0216011080, Santa Ana, America) in drinking water for 7 days. Gavage of L. johnsonii at 1 × 109 CFU/mouse/day was initiated 10 days prior to DSS provision in drinking water. Three days before DSS administration, mice received intraperitoneal injections of anti-CTLA-4 (BioXcell, BE0164, West Lebanon, America) 100 μg/mouse and anti-PD-1 (BioXcell, BE0146, West Lebanon, America) 100 μg/mouse once every 3 days. Body weight, stool consistency, and blood in stools were monitored daily. On day 7 after DSS administration, mice were euthanized, and colon lengths were measured. For indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) treatment, the mice were gavaged with 20 mg/kg indole-3-acetic acid daily, 10 days prior to DSS administration. The indole-3-acetic acid dose was set based on its effective range in colitis models reported in previous studies. − For the mouse aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) blocking experiment, the AhR antagonist CH-223191 (MedChemExpress, HY-12684, New Jersey, America) was administered to mice via intraperitoneal injection at 10 mg/kg every 2 days, beginning simultaneously with L. johnsonii gavage.

For the tumor-bearing ICI-associated colitis model, building upon the ICI-associated colitis model above, 2 × 105 B16–F10 or CT26 cells were inoculated into the armpit of mice. Gavage of L. johnsonii at 1 × 109 CFU/mouse/day was initiated after inoculation with B16–F10 or CT26 cells. Once tumor diameters reached approximately 3–5 mm, anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 treatments were initiated. Three days after ICI treatment, 3% DSS was administered in drinking water for 7 days. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula (L × W 2)/2. On day 7 after DSS administration, mice were euthanized, and tumor weights were measured.

The information for each animal experiment in this study has been summarized in Table S1.

Disease Activity Index (DAI) Scoring

Scores were assigned based on severity of weight loss (scale 0–4), stool consistency (scale 0–4), and blood in stools (scale 0–4). The DAI was calculated as the sum of these three subscores.

Histopathological Analysis

Distal colon tissue segments were resected and fixed in 4% formaldehyde. After dehydration, the tissues were embedded in paraffin. Sections were prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Alcian blue-periodic acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining. Histological damage was quantified by scoring three parameters on a 0–5 scale: epithelium damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and crypt destruction. The summed composite score was defined as the histological score.

Isolation of Total RNA and qPCR Analysis

50 mg of distal colon was excised. Total RNA was extracted using the Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, RC113, Nanjing, China) and quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. RNA was synthesized into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R323, Nanjing, China). Gene expression analysis was subsequently performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711, Nanjing, China). qPCR conditions were as follows: hold stage, 95 °C 30 s; PCR stage, 95 °C 10 s, 60 °C 30 s, 40 cycles; melt curve stage: 95 °C 5 s, 65 °C 5 s, 95 °C 5 s. The relative expression of target mRNAs was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method with β-actin as an internal control. Primers for qPCR were described in Table S2.

Quantification of L. johnsonii in Feces by qPCR

DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the fecal genomic DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, DP328, Beijing, China). The qPCR was performed following the steps outlined above. Almost all bacteria in the gut are Eubacteria, and the 16S rRNA gene is present in all of these Eubacteria. Universal primers designed against the conserved region of the Eubacterial 16S rRNA gene can amplify the 16S rRNA gene of most bacteria in feces, which represents the total bacterial load in feces. Therefore, universal Eubacterial 16S has been used as an internal reference in fecal qPCR in many studies. , Universal Eubacterial 16S was also used as an internal reference gene for stool samples in this study. Primers for qPCR were described in Table S3.

Western Blot

Protein extraction from mouse colon tissues was performed using RIPA lysis buffer. After separation by SDS-PAGE, proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked for 2 h, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following TBST washing, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an imaging system, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ. Antibodies used are listed in Table S4.

Isolation and Treatment of CD4+ T Cells

CD4+ T cells were isolated from mice spleens using a magnetic cell separation kit (Beaver, 70904, Suzhou, China). Isolated CD4+ T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Macklin, M917637, Shanghai, China), 10 ng/mL IL-2 (TargetMol, TMPY-02788, Boston, America), and activated with Dynabeads mouse anti-CD3/CD28 beads (Beaver, 70912, Suzhou, China) for 72 h. To induce Th1 differentiation, cells were treated with 10 ng/mL IL-12 (TargetMol, TMPY-02350, Boston, America) and 10 μg/mL anti-IL-4 antibody. For Treg differentiation, cells were stimulated with 2 ng/mL TGF-β1 (TargetMol, TMPY-00608, Boston, America). 50 μM indole-3-acetic acid was administered to the cells. Cell differentiation markers were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Isolation of Colon Lamina Propria Cells

Following dissection, murine colons were longitudinally incised and flushed with PBS to remove fecal contents. The tissues were rinsed in PBS and sectioned into 1–1.5 cm segments. These segments were incubated in calcium/magnesium-free HBSS (Servicebio, G4203, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 5% FBS, 10 mM HEPES (Gibco, 15630080, Waltham, America), 5 mM EDTA (Solarbio, E1170, Beijing, China), and 1 mM DTT (Biosharp, BL552A, Hefei, China). Samples were agitated on an orbital shaker at 200 rpm under 37 °C for 20 min, followed by vigorous vortexing for 30 s. This procedure was then repeated once. Following epithelium removal, intestinal segments were transferred to calcium/magnesium-supplemented HBSS (Servicebio, G4204, Wuhan, China) containing 5% FBS, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV (Sigma-Aldrich, C4–22, St. Louis, America), 0.2 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, 11284932001, Basel, Switzerland), and 1 mg/mL Dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich, D4693, St. Louis, America). Tissues were digested on an orbital shaker at 200 rpm under 37 °C for 40 min, followed by vigorous vortexing for 30 s. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min. After removing the supernatant, the pellet containing lamina propria mononuclear cells was collected.

Dissociation of the Subcutaneous Tumor into Single Cells

Minced tumor tissues were incubated in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, C11875500BT, Waltham, America) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV, 0.2 mg/mL DNase I, and 1 mg/mL Dispase II at 37 °C for 1 h. The tissue pieces were ground into a single-cell suspension, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting pellet was tumor single cells.

Flow Cytometry

Single cells were first stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Biosciences, 564406, New Jersey, America) to distinguish live/dead cells, followed by Fc receptor blocking. Subsequently, cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (specific antibodies listed in Table S4). For intracellular or nuclear factors, staining was performed using a fixation/permeabilization kit (Invitrogen, 00–5523–00, Carlsbad, California). To detect intracellular cytokines, single cells were stimulated with a Leukocyte Activation Cocktail (BD Biosciences, 550583, New Jersey, America) for 4 h prior to staining. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted on an LSRFortessa X-20 instrument, with data processed using FlowJo software version 10.0. The marker combinations used to identify each cell population were as follows. M1 macrophages: CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+ CD86+; M2 macrophages: CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+ CD206+; Dendritic cells: CD45+ MHCII+ CD11b+ CD11c+; Neutrophils: CD45+ CD11b+ Ly-6G+; Monocytes: CD45+ MHCII– CD11b+ Ly-6C+; Th1 cells: CD45+ CD3+ CD4+ IFN-γ+; Th2 cells: CD45+ CD3+ CD4+ IL-4+; Th17 cells: CD45+ CD3+ CD4+ IL-17A+; Tregs: CD45+ CD3+ CD4+ CD25+ Foxp3+; CD8+ T cells: CD45+ CD3+ CD8+. Gating strategy for flow cytometry analysis was shown in Figure S6.

Fecal 16S rRNA Gene Sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from mouse fecal samples using the TGuide S96 Magnetic Soil/Stool DNA Kit (TIANGEN, DP812, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were examined both by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. The hypervariable regions V3–V4 of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using universal primers. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. After quality control and filtering of low-quality sequences, denoising, sequence merging, and chimera removal were performed using the DADA2 plugin in the QIIME2 environment with default parameters. Representative sequences were annotated using the SILVA database (Release 138.1) via BLAST alignment.

Fecal Untargeted Metabolomics Profiling

50 mg of fecal sample was transferred to a centrifuge tube, and mixed with 1000 μL of extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 2:2:1, v/v/v) containing an internal standard, followed by vortexing, grinding, and sonication to ensure adequate mixing and dissolution. Metabolites in colonic content extracts were analyzed using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Analysis was performed on a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF high-resolution mass spectrometer operated in MSe mode under the control of MassLynx v4.2 software. Raw data acquired via MassLynx were processed using Progenesis QI software for peak picking, peak alignment, and other data processing operations. Metabolite identification was performed by referencing the Progenesis QI software online METLIN database and Biomark’s self-built library. After normalizing the original peak area data against the total peak area, subsequent analysis was conducted. Principal component analysis and Spearman correlation analysis were employed to evaluate the repeatability of the samples within the group and the quality control samples. The identified compounds were searched for classification and pathway information in KEGG, HMDB, and LipidMaps databases.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. For intergroup comparisons of continuous variables, unpaired Student’s t tests were used for two-group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons involving three or more groups. Longitudinal analysis of DAI score curves and tumor growth curves was conducted using two-way ANOVA. For multiple comparisons, Tukey’s test was employed for correction. In both 16S rRNA gene sequencing and untargeted metabolomics analyses, P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) approach. Correlation analysis presented via a heatmap was conducted using the Spearman method. For the gut microbiota co-occurrence network analysis, correlations were calculated using the Spearman method, and edges were retained at a threshold of |r| ≥ 0.5. In LEfSe analysis, discriminative taxa were identified with an LDA score threshold of ≥3. For untargeted metabolomics, differential metabolites were screened based on the criteria: fold change (FC) ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 0.833, FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.05, and variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1. Differential metabolites between two groups were further classified into upregulated and downregulated sets, and KEGG enrichment analysis was performed separately for each set. Pathways with an FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

L. johnsonii Alleviated ICI-Associated Colitis

Mice typically do not develop ICI-associated colitis when treated with ICIs alone. We adopted a commonly used ICI-associated colitis model established by means of DSS-containing water combined with anti-CTLA-4+anti-PD-1 treatment ,, (Figure A). Mice were administered L. johnsonii by gavage at a dose of 1 × 109 CFU/mouse/day, the optimal dose for alleviating ICI-associated colitis as confirmed by pre-experiment (Figure S1). The results showed that fecal L. johnsonii levels in both the DSS group and DSS+ICI group were lower than in the PBS group. However, after administration of L. johnsonii, its levels in feces increased significantly (Figure B). This confirmed that L. johnsonii was successfully delivered and its abundance in feces rose markedly after gavage. After drinking DSS-containing water, the mice showed manifestations of colitis. When anti-CTLA-4+anti-PD-1 treatment was combined, the severity of colitis further increased, as evidenced by greater weight loss, higher DAI score, and shorter colon (Figure C–F). This indicated that the ICI-associated colitis model was successfully established, and the exacerbation of intestinal inflammation caused by anti-CTLA-4+anti-PD-1 treatment was demonstrated. After gavage of L. johnsonii, ICI-associated colitis was alleviated: body weight increased by 7.7%, DAI score decreased by 52%, and colon length increased by 12.4% (Figure C–F). We further evaluated intestinal histological damage by examining epithelial damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and crypt destruction in intestinal tissues via H&E staining. The results showed that after drinking DSS-containing water, the mice exhibited histological damage, with the colon displaying intestinal epithelial injury, crypt damage, and inflammatory cell infiltration. When anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 antibodies were coadministered, these histological changes were further exacerbated, which again confirmed the successful establishment of the ICI-associated colitis model (Figure G–H). After administration of L. johnsonii, the histological damage was mitigated (Figure G–H). These findings suggested that L. johnsonii could alleviate colitis in the experimental ICI-associated colitis model.

L. johnsonii Modulated Macrophage Polarization and Th1/Treg Cell Balance

Disrupted immune homeostasis plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of colitis. We performed quantitative analysis of immune cell subsets in the lamina propria using flow cytometry. Macrophages are categorized into two main subsets: pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2. Our results demonstrated that the proportion of M1 macrophages was significantly higher in the DSS+ICI group compared to the PBS and DSS groups, but decreased markedly following gavage of L. johnsonii (Figure A). In contrast, the proportion of M2 macrophages in DSS+ICI group was reduced relative to the PBS and DSS groups, yet rebounded significantly after L. johnsonii gavage (Figure B). Th1 cells, as a major type of pro-inflammatory cells, showed a significantly higher proportion in the DSS+ICI group compared to the PBS group, but decreased markedly following administration of L. johnsonii (Figure C). Conversely, regulatory T cells (Tregs), key anti-inflammatory cells, exhibited a significantly lower proportion in the DSS+ICI group relative to both the PBS and DSS groups, yet rebounded substantially after L. johnsonii gavage (Figure D). In tumor therapy, ICIs exert their functions mainly by influencing CD8+ T cells, therefore, we also analyzed the proportion of CD8+ T cells. The results revealed that the proportion of CD8+ T cells in the DSS+ICI group was significantly higher than that in the PBS and DSS groups (Figure S2D). This is consistent with the immunopathological characteristics of the colon in patients with ICI-associated colitis, suggesting that this murine model can simulate the clinical condition with respect to increased CD8+ T cells in the colon. However, administration of L. johnsonii did not induce a significant change in the proportion of CD8+ T cells within the colonic lamina propria (Figure S2D). Additionally, we assessed the proportions of dendritic cells, neutrophils, monocytes, Th2 cells, and Th17 cells, yet L. johnsonii administration showed no significant effects on these cell populations (Figure S2).

We further investigated the expression of inflammatory cytokines in colonic tissues. IL-1β, IL-6, IL-23, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) are key pro-inflammatory mediators, with macrophages being their primary cellular source. Consistent with the changes observed in M1 macrophages, the levels of these inflammatory mediators were significantly elevated in the DSS+ICI group compared to the PBS group, but were reduced following L. johnsonii administration (Figure E–I). As a major pro-inflammatory cytokine secreted by Th1 cells, interferon-γ (IFN-γ) was significantly elevated in ICI-associated colitis but reduced following L. johnsonii administration, which was consistent with the alteration pattern of Th1 cells (Figure J). IL-10, a key anti-inflammatory cytokine secreted by Tregs and M2 macrophages, functions to inhibit the progression of colitis. Compared to the PBS group, IL-10 levels showed a numerical reduction in DSS+ICI group, but this decrease lacked statistical significance. However, IL-10 levels were restored after L. johnsonii administration (Figure K). Gavage of L. johnsonii exerted no influence on IL-17A expression (Figure L). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that L. johnsonii administration alleviated colonic inflammation by suppressing pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, Th1 cells, and their inflammatory mediators, while promoting anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, Tregs, and their associated cytokines.

L. johnsonii Restored Intestinal Mucosal Barrier Integrity

Colitis is characterized by impaired mucosal integrity, and reinforcing intestinal barrier function is important for disease amelioration. Goblet cell-secreted mucins, in conjunction with water, inorganic salts, antimicrobial peptides, and other components within the digestive tract, form a viscous gel-like mucus layer, an essential component of the intestinal mucosal barrier. We quantified colonic goblet cells using AB-PAS staining. The results revealed that DSS-treated mice displayed a significant reduction in goblet cell numbers, and this decrease was further aggravated by combined treatment with anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 antibodies. Notably, gavage of L. johnsonii reversed this reduction, restoring goblet cell counts (Figure A,B).

The mechanical barrier represents the most pivotal component of the intestinal barrier, with tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells forming its core structural basis. We quantified the expression levels of tight junction proteins, zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), occludin, claudin-1, and claudin-3, in colonic tissues via qRT-PCR. Results demonstrated that these proteins were significantly downregulated following the onset of ICI-associated colitis, whereas their expression was restored upon administration of L. johnsonii (Figure C–F). Western blot analysis of ZO-1 and Occludin demonstrated a similar trend of alterations (Figure G–I). These findings indicated that the intestinal mucosal barrier was severely impaired in the experimental ICI-associated colitis model, and L. johnsonii contributed to its repair.

L. johnsonii Modulated the Gut Microbiota Composition

The composition of gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in the progression of intestinal inflammation. We further investigated the impact of L. johnsonii on gut microbial communities. Both the Shannon and Simpson indices, metrics used to assess the α-diversity of microbial ecosystems, were significantly reduced in the context of ICI-associated colitis. Notably, administration of L. johnsonii restored these α-diversity indices, indicating its beneficial role in preserving microbial diversity (Figure A,B). We next evaluated β-diversity using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). Both methods revealed distinct microbial profiles between the DSS+ICI and PBS groups, whereas the DSS+ICI+L.j group displayed an intermediate composition (Figure C,D). Collectively, these findings indicated that L. johnsonii could regulate the composition of gut microbiota.

The Venn diagram showed the shared and unique amplicon sequence variants across the groups (Figure E). Figure F depicted microbial community composition at the phylum level. Compared with the PBS group, the DSS+ICI group showed an increase in Proteobacteria, accompanied by a decrease in Verrucomicrobiota and Actinobacteriota. In contrast, relative to the DSS+ICI group, the DSS+ICI+L.j group exhibited an increase in Actinobacteriota and a decrease in Proteobacteria. Figure G illustrated microbial community composition at the genus level. Compared with the PBS group, the DSS+ICI group showed an increase in the abundance of Bacteroides, Escherichia-Shigella, Dubosiella, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Parabacteroides, and Blautia, alongside a decrease in unclassified_Muribaculaceae, Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, Limosilactobacillus, Lactobacillus, Alistipes, and Muribaculum. In contrast, relative to the DSS+ICI group, the DSS+ICI+L.j group exhibited an increase in Bifidobacterium, Limosilactobacillus, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Lactobacillus, and Turicibacte r, as well as a decrease in Escherichia-Shigella and Blautia.

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis was subsequently conducted to identify taxa with differential abundance. During ICI-associated colitis, abundances of beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus, Roseburia, and Muribaculum were reduced, alongside increased abundances of potentially harmful genera like Escherichia-Shigella (Figure A). At the species level, probiotic species, L. johnsonii, Limosilactobacillus reuteri, and Roseburia faecis, were significantly depleted, whereas pathogenic species such as Escherichia coli were enriched (Figure A). Following administration of L. johnsonii, the abundances of beneficial genera, including Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, and Roseburia, were restored (Figure B). At the species level, probiotic species such as L. johnsonii and Limosilactobacillus reuteri exhibited significant recovery after gavage of L. johnsonii (Figure B).

However, the effects of most bacteria shown in the LEfSe analysis on colitis have not yet been clarified. To better characterize changes in beneficial and harmful gut bacteria, we conducted a separate analysis of some detected probiotic or pathogenic bacteria with well-characterized functions in colitis. In the context of ICI-associated colitis, marked reductions were observed in the abundances of Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Roseburia, Muribaculum, Akkermansia, and Limosilactobacillus, whereas Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides displayed increased abundances, with some of these changes reaching statistical significance. Following administration of L. johnsonii, we noted elevated levels of Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Roseburia, alongside reduced abundances of Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides, though not all alterations achieved statistical significance (Figure C–J). Collectively, the analyses of gut microbiota differences across groups suggest that L. johnsonii reversed the intestinal microbiota dysbiosis in the experimental ICI-associated colitis model, restoring it to a state comparable to that of the PBS group.

We conducted correlation analysis between the gut microbiota and a range of parameters (Figure H). Overall, Lactobacillus, Muribaculum, unclassified_Muribaculaceae, and Limosilactobacillus exhibited positive correlations with body weight, colon length, M2 macrophages, Tregs, and tight junction proteins. Conversely, they showed negative correlations with DAI score, histological score, M1 macrophages, and Th1 cells (Figure H). These findings indicated that these genera may exert protective effects in ICI-associated colitis. On the other hand, Bacteroides, Escherichia-Shigella, and Parabacteroides showed the opposite correlation (Figure H), indicating their potential detrimental roles in ICI-associated colitis. Finally, co-occurrence network analysis revealed positive correlations, either direct or indirect, among probiotics such as Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, Muribaculum, and Akkermansia, implying mutualistic interactions. Similarly, positive correlations were observed among pathogenic bacteria including Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides. Conversely, negative correlations existed between the aforementioned probiotic bacteria and pathogenic bacteria, indicating that probiotics may exert antagonistic effects against pathogens (Figure I).

AhR Was Involved in the Alleviative Effect of L. johnsonii on ICI-Associated Colitis

We further investigated the mechanism by which L. johnsonii alleviated ICI-associated colitis. The results above suggested that L. johnsonii alleviated ICI-associated colitis by promoting the production of tryptophan metabolites. Previous studies have found that tryptophan metabolites primarily exert their anticolitis effects through the AhR pathway. All five aforementioned metabolites in the tryptophan metabolism pathway have been reported to act on AhR. ,,, Therefore, we hypothesized that AhR was involved in the alleviative effect of L. johnsonii on ICI-associated colitis. We detected the expression of AhR and its downstream gene CYP1A1 in the mouse colon by qRT-PCR. After inducing colitis with DSS, AhR expression was decreased. Co-treatment with anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 antibodies exacerbated colitis and further reduced AhR expression. Administration of L. johnsonii restored AhR expression (Figure A). The expression of CYP1A1 showed a change trend consistent with that of AhR (Figure B). Western blot analysis of AhR and CYP1A1 demonstrated a similar trend of alterations (Figure C–E). These findings demonstrated that L. johnsonii could activate the AhR pathway.

To assess whether the AhR pathway is critical for L. johnsonii’s effect, we used the AhR antagonist CH-223191 to block this pathway in the murine ICI-associated colitis model, and observed changes in L. johnsonii’s effect (Figure F). As expected, compared with the DSS+ICI group, mice in the DSS+ICI+L.j group showed significant improvements in colitis indicators. However, these colitis indicators in the DSS+ICI+CH-223191+L.j group only showed slight changes compared with the DSS+ICI+CH-223191 group, with no statistical significance (Figure G–L). Consistently, the regulatory role of L. johnsonii in macrophages (Figure M,N), Th1 cells (Figure O), Tregs (Figure P), and the expression of tight junction proteins (Figure Q–T) was abrogated following AhR blockade. Thus, it was evident that AhR was involved in the alleviative effect of L. johnsonii on ICI-associated colitis.

L. johnsonii Enhanced the Therapeutic Efficacy of ICIs while Alleviating ICI-Associated Colitis

Conventional therapies for ICI-associated colitis, such as corticosteroids and biologics, may compromise the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs by suppressing immune system, thereby potentially promoting tumor progression. Whether L. johnsonii alleviates ICI-associated colitis without impairing the efficacy of ICIs remains unclear. To investigate this, we established a murine ICI-associated colitis model bearing subcutaneous B16–F10 melanoma tumors (Figure A). Mice in this model had both tumors and ICI-associated colitis, thereby simulating the clinical situation in which cancer patients developed ICI-associated colitis during treatment with ICIs. B16–F10 is a highly invasive melanoma cell line with poor response to ICIs. As indicated by both the tumor growth curves and the tumor weights, anti-CTLA-4+anti-PD-1 treatment led only to a slight reduction in tumor volume and weight, which was not statistically significant (Figure B–D). Surprisingly, gavage of L. johnsonii did not compromise the efficacy of ICIs, instead, it enhanced their efficacy. Tumor volume and weight in the DSS+ICI+L.j group were reduced compared with those in the DSS and DSS+ICI groups (Figure B–D).

ICIs primarily exert their antitumor effects by activating CD8+ T cells. Granzyme B (GZMB), IFN-γ, and TNF-α are key effector cytokines and activation markers of CD8+ T cells. Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells showed that anti-CTLA-4+anti-PD-1 treatment failed to enhance the expression of these cytokines. However, combining anti-CTLA-4+anti-PD-1 treatment with administration of L. johnsonii significantly upregulated the expression of GZMB, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in intratumoral CD8+ T cells (Figure E–G).

Furthermore, we evaluated the impact of L. johnsonii on ICI-associated colitis in this tumor-bearing ICI-associated colitis model. Compared with the DSS+ICI group, DSS+ICI+L.j group showed significantly improved colitis indicators (Figure H–M). We also evaluated the effect of L. johnsonii on ICI therapeutic efficacy in a murine model of ICI-associated colitis bearing subcutaneous CT26 colorectal tumors. Similar to B16–F10 melanoma tumors, CT26 colorectal tumors showed poor response to ICIs. However, upon coadministration of L. johnsonii, ICIs significantly reduced tumor volume and weight (Figure S5A–D). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that L. johnsonii alleviated ICI-associated colitis while simultaneously enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs.

Discussion

Over the past decade, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising anticancer strategy, with ICIs representing the most widely used modality. ICIs exhibit substantial clinical efficacy across diverse malignancies, fueling their rapidly expanding clinical application. However, while delivering therapeutic benefits, ICIs concurrently trigger irAEs due to systemic immune activation. Among these irAEs, ICI-associated colitis is frequently observed and constitutes a major cause of immunotherapy discontinuation. Current management of ICI-associated colitis primarily relies on immunosuppressive agents, such as corticosteroids and biologics, which inherently risk diminishing the efficacy of ICIs and may potentially promote tumor progression by impairing host immunity. The therapeutic role of gut microbiota modulation in IBD has been extensively investigated, however, research on its implications for ICI-associated colitis remains limited. A recent study has reported that fecal microbiota transplantation could treat refractory ICI-associated colitis unresponsive to corticosteroids or biologics. Furthermore, some probiotics can ameliorate ICI-associated colitis while enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs, , underscoring the growing recognition of probiotics’ therapeutic potential in ICI-associated colitis. L. johnsonii, a typical probiotic, exerts anti-inflammatory effects in IBD and enhances the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs in colorectal cancer. However, its role in ICI-associated colitis remains unreported. The present study was designed to clarify this issue. We employed a murine ICI-associated colitis model established via combined DSS and ICIs treatment. Due to DSS-mediated chemical injury, this model cannot fully recapitulate clinical conditions, representing a limitation of the model. Nevertheless, our results indicated that combined DSS and ICIs treatment exacerbated colitis compared with DSS alone, indicating that the effects of ICIs were well manifested in this model. Additionally, the abundance of colonic CD8+ T cells was markedly increased following combined DSS and ICIs treatment (Figure S2D), which was consistent with the immune features of clinical ICI-associated colitis. Therefore, this model possesses favorable reference value. Our study demonstrated that administration of L. johnsonii promoted colitis remission in the experimental ICI-associated colitis model (see Figure C–H for details). Based on these findings, we further explored the effects of L. johnsonii on intestinal immunity, intestinal barrier integrity, microbiota composition, and metabolic profiles.

Current understanding attributes ICI-associated colitis to autoimmune intestinal inflammation arising from excessive immune activation. ICIs exert antitumor effects chiefly via the activation of CD8+ T cells. In the present study, we observed increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells in colonic tissues during ICI-associated colitis. Macrophages are well-established contributors to the pathogenesis of IBD, but their role in ICI-associated colitis remains poorly characterized. Macrophages exist as pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 types, and their equilibrium maintains intestinal immune homeostasis. In the present study, we observed an increase in M1 macrophages and a decrease in M2 macrophages during ICI-associated colitis. Research on the mechanism by which ICIs exert effects on macrophages is limited. It is relatively certain that upon activating CD8+ T cells, ICIs promote their secretion of IFN-γ, which then acts on macrophages to induce their polarization toward the M1 type. Additionally, some studies have suggested that PD-1 is also expressed on macrophages and that blocking the PD-L1/PD-1 axis can induce M1 polarization of macrophages. Notably, administration of L. johnsonii reduced M1 macrophages and increased M2 macrophages. Th1 cells exert pro-inflammatory effects mainly through the secretion of IFN-γ, whereas Tregs act as critical immunosuppressive regulators. During the progression of ICI-associated colitis, we observed increased Th1 cell infiltration accompanied by decreased Treg frequencies. The effect of ICIs on Th1 cells is well-established. CD4+ T cells express PD-1, which exerts an inhibitory effect, while anti-PD-1 therapy promotes their differentiation into Th1 cells and the secretion of IFN-γ. , However, the mechanism underlying the inhibition of Tregs by ICIs remains unclear. PD-1 is also expressed on Tregs, but whether it suppresses or enhances the suppressive functionality of Tregs remains controversial. A recent study has yielded some intriguing findings that the inhibitory effect of anti-CTLA-4 therapy on Tregs was dependent on the Fc domain of the anti-CTLA-4 antibody and the Fcγ receptors expressed on Tregs. Administration of L. johnsonii reduced the populations of Th1 cells and expanded those of Tregs, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects. Collectively, ICI-associated colitis exhibited immunological alterations analogous to those observed in IBD. L. johnsonii mitigated immune-mediated tissue damage by rebalancing the M1/M2 macrophage polarization and restoring Th1/Treg homeostasis.

In ICI-associated colitis, goblet cell damage and reduced tight junction protein expression contribute to intestinal barrier dysfunction. Goblet cells are instrumental in forming the intestinal mucus layer through mucin secretion, a process that is critical to the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier. In the present study, goblet cells were damaged in ICI-associated colitis, whereas gavage of L. johnsonii resulted in the restoration of goblet cells. Tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1, and Claudin-3, are intimately involved in intercellular junction formation, where they sustain cellular barrier function. In this study, the expression of tight junction proteins in colonic tissues was found to be reduced in ICI-associated colitis, however, L. johnsonii significantly reversed this decline. These results suggest that restoration of the intestinal mucosal barrier was an important process by which L. johnsonii alleviated ICI-associated colitis.

Emerging evidence indicates that intestinal dysbiosis is linked to the pathogenesis of colitis and can be restored by probiotics. As is well-known, gut microbiota members interact reciprocally through cross-feeding, exerting mutual influences on one another. Probiotic supplementation not only increases the abundance of the administered beneficial strains but also promotes the growth of beneficial commensals and inhibits pathogenic colonization. In the present study, ICI-associated colitis was characterized by a decreased abundance of Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Roseburia, and Muribaculum, accompanied by an enrichment of Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides. Conversely, administration of L. johnsonii increased the relative abundance of Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, and Roseburia, suggesting a remodeling of the microbiota toward an anti-inflammatory profile. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are classic probiotic genera, with multiple species in these genera exhibiting anti-inflammatory efficacy in colitis of diverse etiologies. − Roseburia produces butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) that supports colonocyte energy metabolism to promote epithelial repair while suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exerting anticolitic effects. Limosilactobacillus is an important genus that has been taxonomically separated from the Lactobacillus genus. Its representative species, Limosilactobacillus reuteri and Limosilactobacillus fermentum, have been demonstrated to exert inhibitory effects on colitis. , Although research on Muribaculum remains limited, current evidence suggests it harbors anti-inflammatory potential through SCFA production, pathogen antagonism, and promotion of mucin secretion. Conversely, Escherichia-Shigella represents a prototypical pathogenic genus that exacerbates intestinal inflammation. While certain Bacteroides species exert protective effects in IBD, specific species such as Bacteroides vulgatus and Bacteroides dorei are reported to correlate with irAE. Thus, the development of ICI-associated colitis is linked to gut dysbiosis characterized by a reduction in beneficial bacteria and an enrichment of pathogenic microorganisms. L. johnsonii enhances the abundance of beneficial microbiota while suppressing pathogenic colonization. Previous studies have suggested that the potential mechanisms underlying gut microbiota modulation by L. johnsonii include cross-feeding, direct effects via metabolites, competitive colonization, and modification of the intestinal microenvironment (e.g., luminal pH, mucin production). However, several limitations should be noted when interpreting the 16S rRNA gene sequencing results of this study. First, the sample size (n = 6 per group) is limited for LEfSe, correlation analysis, and co-occurrence network analysis. Larger sample sizes are required in future studies to obtain more reliable results. Second, although the V3–V4 hypervariable region provides moderate species-level resolution for dominant intestinal bacteria and offers certain referential value to 16S rRNA annotations, reliable taxonomic identification with 16S rRNA is generally restricted to the genus level, and full accuracy at the species level cannot be guaranteed. Accordingly, all species-level findings herein remain preliminary and require further validation via metagenomic sequencing.

Gut microbiota exert their functions mainly through metabolites. Nontargeted metabolomics analysis of mouse feces indicated that administration of L. johnsonii altered intestinal metabolites in the context of ICI-associated colitis. KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites revealed that the tryptophan metabolism pathway was significantly upregulated after administration of L. johnsonii. Among the metabolites enriched in this pathway, indole-3-acetamide, indole-3-acetic acid, kynurenic acid, xanthurenic acid, and 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid have been reported to possess anti-inflammatory effects. Tryptophan metabolism involves the indole, kynurenine, and 5-HT pathways. The indole pathway exhibits close and direct association with the gut microbiota, which is capable of directly metabolizing tryptophan into a range of indole derivatives with anti-inflammatory properties. The kynurenine pathway is also influenced by the gut microbiota, though this effect may be indirect. For instance, the gut microbiota could exert effects on intestinal epithelial cells via their metabolites, regulating the expression of indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), a key enzyme in the kynurenine pathway, and thereby modulating this pathway. Indole-3-acetamide and indole-3-acetic acid are part of the indole pathway, while kynurenic acid, xanthurenic acid, and 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid belong to kynurenine pathway. Correlation analysis between these five tryptophan metabolites and colitis phenotypes, immune cells, and tight junction proteins indicated that indole-3-acetic acid was an important candidate mediator to alleviate colitis. Subsequent experiments confirmed that indole-3-acetic acid alleviated colitis in the experimental ICI-associated colitis model. Taken together, in the context of ICI-associated colitis, L. johnsonii promoted tryptophan metabolism to produce anti-inflammatory metabolites, with indole-3-acetic acid as an important candidate mediator, thus facilitating disease alleviation.

Tryptophan metabolites primarily exert their anti-inflammatory effects through the AhR pathway. All five metabolites enriched in the tryptophan metabolism pathway, indole-3-acetamide, indole-3-acetic acid, kynurenic acid, xanthurenic acid, and 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, have been reported to act on AhR. In the present study, we found L. johnsonii activated AhR pathway. After blocking the AhR with CH-223191, the protective effect of L. johnsonii against ICI-associated colitis was abrogated. Thus, AhR was involved in the alleviative effect of L. johnsonii on ICI-associated colitis. Indeed, AhR is expressed in intestinal epithelial cells as well as various immune cells, and mediates the anticolitis effect. Tryptophan metabolites are capable of activating AhR in intestinal epithelial cells, which in turn enhances epithelial cell proliferation and the expression of tight junction proteins. For macrophages, AhR signaling mediates their polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 type. Activation of the AhR pathway in CD4+ T cells decreases their differentiation into Th1 cells and increases that into Tregs. These reports are consistent with our findings in the murine model of ICI-associated colitis, where L. johnsonii exerted effects on macrophages, Th1 cells, Tregs, and tight junction proteins, and these effects were abolished when AhR was blocked with CH-223191. Thus, AhR was involved in the alleviative effect of L. johnsonii on ICI-associated colitis. However, it should be noted that pharmacologic inhibition using CH-223191 does not rule out off-target effects or parallel signaling pathways. Therefore, from a rigorous perspective, the AhR pathway may not be the sole mechanism. L. johnsonii may act through multiple microbiota- and metabolite-mediated mechanisms in addition to AhR pathway.

The primary treatments for ICI-associated colitis include corticosteroids and biologics such as anti-TNF-α and anti-α4β7-integrin agents. However, these immunosuppressive therapies may potentially compromise antitumor immunity and the efficacy of ICIs, thereby promoting tumor progression. Probiotics have been reported to alleviate ICI-associated colitis without impairing the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs. , In our tumor-bearing ICI-associated colitis model, administration of L. johnsonii did not impair the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs, instead, it augmented the efficacy by promoting the expression of effector cytokines including GZMB, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in CD8+ T cells. We integrated previously reported intestinal interventions and those employed in this study for ICI-associated colitis into Table . In this table, L. johnsonii is one of the few interventions that can simultaneously alleviate colitis and enhance ICI efficacy. These findings underscored the advantage of L. johnsonii in treating ICI-associated colitis, which lay in alleviating the disease while enhancing the efficacy of ICIs. Certainly, in our study, ICI efficacy enhancement was demonstrated in only two experimental tumor models. Further investigation is needed to validate this finding (e.g., test it in more tumor types, or conduct a broader characterization of the tumor immune microenvironment).

In summary, our findings revealed that L. johnsonii ameliorated ICI-associated colitis through modulation of M1/M2 macrophage polarization, regulation of Th1/Treg cell balance, and reinforcement of the intestinal mucosal barrier. One notable mechanism was that L. johnsonii promoted tryptophan metabolism and the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites, with indole-3-acetic acid as an important candidate mediator, thereby alleviating ICI-associated colitis via the AhR pathway. What’s more, L. johnsonii not only alleviated ICI-associated colitis but also enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs (Figure ). Probiotics have been demonstrated to mitigate colitis of various etiologies, such as IBD. However, studies on their role in ICI-associated colitis are limited. Our work addresses this research gap and provides a robust theoretical foundation for the potential clinical application of L. johnsonii in this disease. Our study has several limitations: 1) Small sample size in the animal experiment; 2) The absence of patient fecal samples for clinically validating the correlation between L. johnsonii abundance and disease severity; 3) Certain pathophysiological differences between the DSS+ICI mouse model and clinical ICI-associated colitis due to the involvement of DSS effects in the former; 4) Lack of germ-free mouse experiments to assess how much the effects of L. johnsonii depend on the gut microbiota. These limitations need to be addressed in our future studies.

Schematic diagram of the mechanism by which L. johnsonii alleviated ICI-associated colitis. L. johnsonii promoted tryptophan metabolism and the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites, with indole-3-acetic acid as an important candidate mediator, subsequently activating the AhR pathway. This led to the inhibition of M1 macrophages and Th1 cells, the promotion of M2 macrophages and Tregs, and reinforcement of the intestinal barrier, thereby alleviating colitis in the experimental ICI-associated colitis model. Meanwhile, L. johnsonii also enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs. ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor; IAA, indole-3-acetic acid; AhR, aryl hydrocarbon receptor; ZO-1, zonula occludens-1; TNF-α, tumor necrosis factor-α; IFN-γ, interferon-γ; iNOS, inducible nitric oxide synthase.
