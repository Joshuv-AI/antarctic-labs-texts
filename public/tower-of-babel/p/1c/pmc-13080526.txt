Bortezomib-Induced Myocarditis in a Patient With Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Adriana Domínguez-Linares, Ana Karen Nieto-Dolores, Mauricio Garcia-Cardenas, Alfonso Gonzalez-Trejo, Estela Ayala-Bustamante, Hugo Armando Valencia-Hernandez, Adriana Viñas-Mendieta, Enrique Ruiz-Mori, Nilda Espinola-Zavaleta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBortezomib is a reversible proteasome inhibitor used as first-line therapy for multiple myeloma and mantle cell lymphoma. Although effective, it can in rare cases cause cardiotoxicity, including myocarditis.Case SummaryA 58-year-old man with multiple myeloma (MC I, lambda light chain type) developed progressive dyspnea and edema after VTD (bortezomib, thalidomide, dexamethasone) therapy. Proposed mechanisms include proteasome inhibition–induced endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired NF-κB signaling.Take-Home MessageEarly recognition, prompt drug withdrawal, and timely heart failure therapy are essential to ensure full recovery.Key words: bortezomib, myocarditis, cardiotoxicity, multiple myeloma, proteasome inhibitor, case report

Bortezomib is widely used as first-line therapy in multiple myeloma, and it has a well-established efficacy profile, however rare but potentially severe cardiovascular toxicities have increasingly been recognized.

History of Presentation

We present the case of a 58-year-old male patient who initially sought evaluation for 2 months of asthenia and gait limitation due to pelvic limb pain. His baseline echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF]: 58%, global longitudinal strain [GLS]: –18%). Four months later, given progression of symptoms, he was referred to an oncology service and was diagnosed with multiple myeloma (MC I, lambda light chain type). He subsequently began VTD (bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone) induction therapy: bortezomib 1.3 mg/m2 on days 1, 8, 15, and 22 (cumulative dose: 5.2 mg/m2), thalidomide 100 mg/d, and dexamethasone 40 mg weekly. Notably, he reported progressive dyspnea and worsening lower extremity edema after each dose of bortezomib.

One week after the last administration, the patient presented to the emergency department with rapidly dyspnea, orthopnea, and edema. On arrival, he had a blood pressure of 92/64 mm Hg, heart rate of 124 beats/min, oxygen saturation 94% on room air, and respiratory rate of 24 beats/min. Pulmonary examination revealed diffuse crackles and diminished breath sounds at bases, while cardiac auscultation demonstrated a third heart sound and significant peripheral edema. He was transferred to a critical care unit for oxygen support and intravenous diuretics.

Past Medical History

The patient's only known cardiovascular history was hypercholesterolemia, which was treated with rosuvastatin.

Investigations

The initial electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with ST-segment depression in the precordial leads. Lung ultrasonography revealed diffuse B lines and moderate pleural effusion, for which thoracic drainage was performed. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated new severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF: 35%, GLS: –10%) (Figure 1A), global hypokinesia with apical predominance, grade III diastolic dysfunction, moderate mitral regurgitation, and elevated filling pressures (E/e′: 30), indicating pulmonary congestion (Video 1, Figure 2).

High-sensitivity troponin I was markedly elevated at 2,380 ng/L (reference: <34.2 ng/L), increasing to 3,176.7 ng/L the following day and then decreasing to 2,007 ng/L. Given the acute presentation with elevated biomarkers and severe left ventricular dysfunction, ischemic etiology was promptly evaluated. Coronary computed tomography angiography showed normal coronary arteries (CAD-RADS [Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System]: 0), a calcium score of 0, and incidentally identified a thrombus in the left atrial appendage. Full anticoagulation was initiated. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) subsequently revealed extensive myocardial edema and heterogeneous intramyocardial and subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement consistent with acute myocarditis (Figure 3, Video 2).

The close temporal association between bortezomib administration and symptom worsening, the exclusion of coronary disease, the MRI findings consistent with active myocardial inflammation, and the rapid improvement after discontinuation supported the diagnosis of bortezomib-induced myocarditis.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included coronary ischemia, cardiac amyloidosis, viral myocarditis, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, and drug-induced myocarditis. Amyloidosis was considered unlikely given the sudden onset and previously normal echocardiogram. The absence of a viral prodrome and the pattern of dysfunction made viral myocarditis less likely.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Bortezomib cardiotoxicity, although uncommon, has been described in multiple observational reports and mechanistic studies. The patient was started on guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, including dapagliflozin 10 mg/d, enalapril 5 mg/d, spironolactone 25 mg/d, and furosemide 40 mg/d. His clinical condition improved steadily, allowing discharge after 11 days of hospitalization.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At the 3-week follow-up visit, the patient remained asymptomatic. Electrocardiography normalized, and echocardiography showed substantial recovery of systolic function (LVEF: 52%, GLS: –17%) (Figure 1B), although the left atrial appendage thrombus persisted. Troponin level was 37 ng/L. Bortezomib was discontinued after the event, and the patient was scheduled for autologous stem cell transplantation.

Discussion

The VTD regimen remains a first-line induction therapy for multiple myeloma patients eligible for autologous stem cell transplantation and has demonstrated superior complete response and progression-free survival compared with other regimens.1 Our patient received VTD as standard of care.

While all 3 agents in this regimen have been associated with some degree of cardiovascular toxicity, their mechanism and clinical patterns differ. Thalidomide is mainly linked to sinus bradycardia and venous thromboembolism rather than direct myocardial injury.2 Dexamethasone can promote pathological remodeling through alterations in calcium handling and calcineurin activation, but these effects typically occur after prolonged exposure.3 Given that this patient received only short-term dexamethasone, and given the known cardiac profile of thalidomide, both were considered unlikely primary drivers of the acute dysfunction we observed.

In contrast, bortezomib has been consistently implicated in acute heart failure, conduction disorders, atrial fibrillation, ischemic heart disease, pericardial effusion, and orthostatic hypotension.4 Among proteasome inhibitors, it is one of the most frequently associated with cardiovascular adverse events, reported in 3.8%,5 although bortezomib-induced myocarditis remains extremely rare, with only isolated cases reported.

Bortezomib is a reversible inhibitor of the 26S proteasome responsible for degradation of ubiquitinated proteins. Its cytotoxic effect on malignant plasma cells involves accumulation of unfolded/misfolded proteins and activation of endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways.6 Because proteasome inhibition is not selective for tumor cells, disruption of proteostasis in cardiomyocytes can cause endoplasmic reticulum stress, proteotoxicity, and cellular injury. Experimental studies have demonstrated that bortezomib exposure results in cytoplasmic accumulation of polyubiquitinated proteins, widening of the endoplasmic reticulum lumen, and induction of autophagy markers, along with mitochondrial swelling and loss of cristae structure.6 These abnormalities impair ATP production and contractility and may contribute to an inflammatory myocardial process.

Another proposed mechanism involves impaired processing of p105 and p100 proteins, preventing activation of canonical and noncanonical NF-κB pathways.7 Because NF-κB regulates genes related to cell survival and angiogenesis, its inhibition may promote myocardial vulnerability. Additional contributors include mitochondrial dysfunction6 and altered vascular smooth muscle cell behavior, which may increase susceptibility to ischemia. Initially, NF-κB suppression was believed to be the principal mechanism underlying the antineoplastic action of bortezomib, but later work showed that upstream inhibition of NF-κB does not fully reproduce the drug's cytotoxic profile, suggesting multiple overlapping mechanisms.7

The clinical evolution of our patient aligns closely with the described pathophysiology: progressive dyspnea after each bortezomib dose, new severe systolic dysfunction, elevated troponin, and cardiac MRI confirming extensive acute myocarditis. The improvement in LVEF to 52% after discontinuation supports a reversible drug-induced injury rather than structural cardiomyopathy. The left atrial appendage thrombus was most likely secondary to low-flow states during acute systolic dysfunction.

Although viral infections remain the most common etiology, drug-induced myocarditis is increasingly recognized in oncology. For example, immune checkpoint inhibitors cause myocarditis in approximately 1% of treated patients and may present with arrhythmias or complete atrioventricular block even in asymptomatic individuals.8 Diagnostic evaluation relies on biomarkers, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, and endomyocardial biopsy when feasible.9 In immune checkpoint inhibitor–associated myocarditis, early discontinuation of therapy and administration of high-dose corticosteroids are essential.9 Additional immunomodulatory therapies may be required for refractory cases.9

Current myocarditis management guidelines emphasize early intervention and a stepwise treatment approach, including immunosuppressive therapy when indicated.10 In our case, early discontinuation of bortezomib and initiation of guideline-directed heart failure therapy resulted in complete recovery, reinforcing a causal association.

Conclusions

This case illustrates an uncommon but clinically significant manifestation of proteasome inhibitor cardiotoxicity. Clinicians should maintain high suspicion for myocarditis or acute heart failure symptoms during bortezomib therapy. Early recognition, prompt discontinuation of the offending agent, and initiation of heart failure therapy are critical to ensure full functional recovery and avoid life-threatening outcomes.

Take-Home Messages

•Bortezomib-associated myocarditis is a rare but serious cardiotoxicity that should be suspected in patients who develop acute heart failure symptoms or myocardial injury during therapy.
