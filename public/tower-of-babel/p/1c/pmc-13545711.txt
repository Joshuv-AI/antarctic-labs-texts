Corticosteroids in severe alcohol-related hepatitis: A multicenter randomized clinical trial
Authors: Christophe Moreno, Pierre Deltenre, Astrid Marot, Hassan Njimi, Luc Lasser, Delphine Degré, Axel Hittelet, Jean Delwaide, Anja Geerts, Silke François, Boris Bastens, Thierry Gustot, Eric Trépo, Christophe Moreno, Christophe Moreno, Pierre Deltenre, Delphine Degré, Thierry Gustot, Eric Trépo, Pierre Deltenre, Astrid Marot, Luc Lasser, Axel Hittelet, Jean Delwaide, Anja Geerts, Silke François, Boris Bastens, Njimi Hassan, Sergio Negrin Dastis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsSevere alcohol-related hepatitis (AH) has high short-term mortality, and corticosteroid therapy is recommended in the absence of contraindications. However, its benefit in patients with early spontaneous bilirubin improvement remains unknown. We aim at determining whether corticosteroid therapy improves survival compared with placebo in this specific population.MethodsIn this multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled trial conducted in 10 Belgian hospitals (from February 2018 to May 2024), patients aged ≥18 yr with biopsy-proven severe AH and spontaneous bilirubin decline >10% between admission and Day 5–10 were randomized 1:1 to methylprednisolone 32 mg daily or placebo for 28 days. The primary outcome was 90-day survival; other outcomes included 30-day survival and cumulative incidence of infection at 90 days.ResultsWe analyzed 69 (49%) of the 140 planned patients (38 corticosteroid, 31 placebo). Baseline characteristics were well balanced. At 90 days, survival was 79% (95% CI 67–93%) vs. 83% (95% CI 70–98%) (hazard ratio 1.19, 95% CI 0.39–3.63, p = 0.76). Eight deaths occurred in the corticosteroid arm vs. five in the placebo arm. At 30 days, survival was 95% vs. 94% (p = 0.83). Cumulative incidence of infection at 90 days was 47% vs. 34% (subdistribution hazard ratio 1.55, 95% CI 0.72–3.34, p = 0.26). Lille score was an independent predictor of 90-day mortality.ConclusionsIn patients with severe AH and early spontaneous bilirubin improvement, corticosteroid therapy was not associated with improved survival, but the underpowered trial cannot exclude a benefit. A short observation period after admission may help identify patients who can be managed without corticosteroids.Impact and implicationsCorticosteroid therapy is recommended for patients with severe alcohol-related hepatitis (AH), but whether this benefit extends to those who show early spontaneous improvement in bilirubin after admission was previously unknown. In this multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled trial, corticosteroid therapy was not associated with improved 90-day or 30-day survival in patients with severe, biopsy-proven AH and a spontaneous bilirubin decline of >10% within 5–10 days of admission. Corticosteroids were associated with a numerically higher, though not statistically significant, risk of infection. These findings are most relevant to hepatologists and other clinicians managing severe AH, and to researchers designing future AH trials, as they identify a subgroup in whom the benefit of corticosteroids is uncertain. They support a risk-stratified approach in which a brief observation period after admission—incorporating bilirubin trend, infection screening, and liver biopsy—may help identify patients with a relatively favorable prognosis who can be spared corticosteroid therapy and its associated risks. However, because the trial was stopped early after enrolling only 69 of the 140 planned patients, it was underpowered, and a modest survival benefit of corticosteroids cannot be excluded. Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as definitive evidence against corticosteroid use in this population. Larger, adequately powered studies are required to validate these findings before informing clinical guidelines or being generalized to broader patient populations.Clinical trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03160651), EudraCT number: 2016-005136-16.

Introduction

Alcohol-related hepatitis (AH) is an acute manifestation of alcohol-related liver disease characterized by recent-onset jaundice, chronic alcohol consumption, and typical laboratory findings (increased bilirubin level, aspartate aminotransferase [AST] >50 IU/L, alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and AST <400 IU/L, AST/ALT ratio >1.5).1,2 In its severe forms, defined by a Maddrey discriminant function (mDF) score ≥32 or a Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score >20, the short-term mortality is high and estimated at ∼30% at 90 days.3,4 Corticosteroid therapy is the only treatment demonstrating a survival benefit and is recommended by clinical guidelines in severe AH.5 The STOPAH trial, the largest randomized trial in the field, showed reduced 30-day mortality with corticosteroids.6 Furthermore, a meta-analysis of individual patient data involving 2,111 patients with severe AH reported a 1-month survival rate of 84% with corticosteroids vs. 76% without treatment.7 However, corticosteroids do not improve survival beyond 1 month and may increase infection risk.6,8

AH is heterogeneous in severity, and treatment benefit varies across the disease spectrum. At the most severe end of the disease spectrum, patients with multiple organ dysfunctions (acute-on-chronic liver failure [ACLF] grade 3) have a very low probability of responding to corticosteroids and a very high risk of short-term mortality.9 In a large retrospective, international multicenter cohort study including 3,380 patients with clinical and/or histological diagnosis of AH, corticosteroids improved survival in AH patients with MELD scores between 21 and 51, with the maximum effect observed with MELD scores between 25 and 39.10 Apart from their retrospective nature, these studies assessed disease severity at a single time point without considering early spontaneous evolution after admission.

Importantly, there is a clear lack of data and clinical guidance regarding corticosteroid use in the subset of patients with severe AH who demonstrate spontaneous improvement in total serum bilirubin level shortly after admission. Although previous cohort data suggest that patients with severe AH and spontaneous bilirubin improvement early after admission had better 3-month survival than those without improvement, there was particular uncertainty on whether corticosteroids provide any additional benefit in this subgroup. Focusing on this population allowed us to address this knowledge gap, while patients without spontaneous improvement of bilirubin already have more robust data regarding corticosteroid efficacy.

The CORTISAVE study aimed at determining whether corticosteroid therapy improves survival compared with placebo in patients with severe AH and spontaneous bilirubin improvement after admission.

Patients and methods

Study design

This multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial was conducted in 10 Belgian hospitals between February 2018 and February 2024. The study was approved by the ethics committee of CUB Hôpital Erasme (Brussels, Belgium) and by local institutional review boards at each participating center. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03160651) and was supported by the Belgian Association for the Study of the Liver.

Participants

Eligible patients were aged ≥18 yr, consumed >40 g of alcohol daily, had recent-onset jaundice within 3 months, had serum bilirubin level >5 mg/dl, had biopsy-proven steatohepatitis, and had mDF ≥32 at admission. Spontaneous improvement was required, defined as serum bilirubin decrease of >10% between admission and Day 5 to 10. Investigators had therefore a window of 5–10 days after admission to the hospitalization ward to assess eligibility for randomization, whereas the baseline bilirubin value was defined as the level measured at initial hospital admission. For some patients admitted via the emergency department, there could therefore be a delay between the initial bilirubin assessment and the admission to the hospitalization ward. Consequently, in certain cases, the time required to observe a ≥10% decrease in serum bilirubin could extend beyond 10 days after hospital admission, although it remained within the 5–10 days of window after admission to the ward. Maximum time from admission to randomization was 14 days. Histological confirmation of AH required steatosis, hepatocyte ballooning, and polymorphonuclear neutrophil infiltrate.1

Exclusion criteria included HBsAg positivity, HIV antibodies, chronic hepatitis C infection (positive HCV RNA), severe concurrent disease compromising 90-day survival, uncontrolled infection (required control for ≥2 days), uncontrolled upper gastrointestinal bleeding (required control for ≥5 days), serum creatinine >2.5 mg/dl, receipt of renal replacement therapy, terlipressin, or pentoxifylline.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; proxy consent from a relative was obtained for patients with marked encephalopathy.

Randomization

Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive methylprednisolone 32 mg once daily or matching placebo. Randomization sequences were generated centrally at CUB Hôpital Erasme (Brussels, Belgium) by an independent statistician using variable block sizes of 2, 4, or 6 without stratification, and provided to each site. At the time of patient inclusion, the investigator asked for a randomization number, which was then communicated to the site pharmacist. The pharmacist, who was the only unblinded member of the study team at each site, prepared the corresponding treatment according to the pre-established randomization list.

Randomization lists were accessible only to the pharmacists, ensuring that investigators and other study personnel remained blinded throughout the trial.

Procedures

After admission, patients with suspected severe AH (mDF >32) underwent infection screening and liver biopsy according to local practices. Liver function was reassessed 5–10 days later. Patients with serum bilirubin decrease of >10% who met all eligibility criteria were randomized. Patients could be randomized before biopsy results were available, but they were excluded if steatohepatitis was not confirmed histologically.

At admission, serum bilirubin and mDF were measured. At randomization, BMI, encephalopathy and ascites grade, international normalized ratio, bilirubin, albumin, serum sodium, creatinine, AST, ALT, and MELD score were evaluated. Follow-up visits at Day 7, Month 1, and Month 3 included clinical examination and laboratory analyses. Treatment response was assessed using the Lille score at Day 7.11 Early corticosteroid discontinuation based on Lille score was recommended but left to investigator discretion. Alcohol consumption was assessed by patient self-report at each visit.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was 90-day survival after treatment initiation. Other exploratory endpoints included 30-day survival and the 90-day cumulative incidence of infection, with death considered as a competing risk.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was based on an anticipated 90-day mortality of 35% in the placebo group and 15% in the methylprednisolone group, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 20%. Assuming a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, 80% power, and a 1:1 allocation ratio, and applying a continuity correction, in total at least 140 patients (70 per group) were required to detect this difference. The population analyzed was a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, in which patients who were randomized before the biopsy result was available and those without steatohepatitis lesions were excluded. We chose to perform mITT to improve the homogeneity of the study population by restricting the analysis to patients with histologically confirmed AH.

Variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± SD, and skewed variables are presented as median with IQR. Categorical variables are expressed as count and percentage. Baseline characteristics were compared between treatment groups using Student's t test for normally distributed continuous variables, Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Chi-square test for categorical variables.

Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were compared between treatment groups using Cox proportional hazards regression with Wald test. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CIs were calculated. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with 90-day mortality. In addition to treatment arm, covariates with a p value <0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. Composite prognostic scores (Lille, mDF, MELD) and their individual components were not included together in regression models to avoid collinearity.12

The cumulative incidence of infection was estimated, treating death without prior infection as a competing event. Differences between treatment groups were assessed using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model, and subdistribution HRs with 95% CIs were reported. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Between February 2018 and February 2024, 348 patients were assessed for suspected AH across 10 Belgian centers, with 276 patients ineligible primarily owing to absence of spontaneous bilirubin improvement (196 patients, 71%). Other reasons were absence of biopsy-proven AH (44 patients), mDF <32 at admission (eight patients), refusal to participate in a clinical trial (three patients), or others (25 patients) (Fig. 1). Seventy-two patients were randomized (40 in the corticosteroid arm, 32 in the placebo arm). Three patients were excluded after randomization owing to absence of steatohepatitis on liver biopsy, leaving 69 patients in the mITT analysis (38 in corticosteroid arm, 31 in the placebo arm). Primary endpoint

At 90 days, the cumulative probability of survival was 79% (95% CI 67–93%) and 83% (95% CI 70–98%) in the corticosteroid arm and the placebo arm, respectively (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.39–3.63, p = 0.76; Fig. 2). Deaths occurred in eight of 38 patients (21%) in the corticosteroid arm and five of 31 patients (16%) in the placebo arm. Of the 56 patients who survived beyond Day 90, 41 completed the full 90-day follow-up; 15 were censored before Day 90 (corticosteroid arm: six patients, median 88 [IQR 86–89] days; placebo arm: nine patients, median 77 [IQR 28–87] days). All censoring was owing to study termination; no patient was lost to follow-up. In univariate Cox regression analysis, age (HR per year 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.15, p = 0.027) and Lille score (HR per 0.1-unit increase 1.33, 95% CI 1.06–1.67, p = 0.015) were significantly associated with 90-day mortality, whereas treatment assignment and other baseline variables were not (Table 2). Other exploratory endpoints

At 30 days, the cumulative probability of survival was 95% (95% CI 88–100%) in the corticosteroid arm and 94% (95% CI 85–100%) in the placebo arm (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.11–5.76, p = 0.83; Fig. 2). Two deaths occurred in each treatment group.

The cumulative incidence of infection at 90 days, with death as a competing risk, was 47% (95% CI 30–63%) in the corticosteroid arm (17 patients with infection) and 34% (95% CI 17–51%) in the placebo arm (10 patients with infection) (subdistribution HR 1.55, 95% CI 0.72–3.34, p = 0.26; Fig. 3). Discussion

In this randomized, placebo-controlled trial of patients with biopsy-proven severe AH and early spontaneous bilirubin improvement, corticosteroid therapy did not improve survival at 90 days compared with placebo. These findings suggest that patients with severe AH demonstrating spontaneous bilirubin decline of >10% within 5–10 days of admission do not benefit from corticosteroid therapy and may be exposed to unnecessary infection risk.

The heterogeneity of AH has important implications for treatment selection. Although corticosteroids remain the standard of care for severe AH, their benefit is not uniform across the disease spectrum. Patients with mild disease (mDF <32) or multiorgan failure derive minimal or no benefit,10,13 and a large cohort study found maximal benefit in patients with MELD scores between 25 and 39.10 In the present study, we identified another subgroup unlikely to benefit—patients with severe disease at admission and early spontaneous improvement. Previous cohort data suggested better outcomes in such patients, but because all received corticosteroids, the specific contribution of therapy remained uncertain.14 Our randomized trial demonstrates that spontaneous improvement identifies favorable prognosis independent of corticosteroid treatment.

These findings align with observational data showing better prognosis in patients with rapid bilirubin decline (“fast-fallers”) without clear corticosteroid benefit;3 however, that study was limited by selection bias. Importantly, treatment delay was not associated with worse outcomes in observational studies,3,15 supporting feasibility of a 5- to 10-day observation period.

The mechanism likely reflects disease biology.13 Bilirubin decline within the first week suggests resolution of acute inflammation, the pathophysiologic target of corticosteroid therapy. Patients without spontaneous improvement have ongoing hepatocellular injury that may be corticosteroid-responsive. This interpretation is supported by the fact that Lille score was the only independent predictor of 90-day mortality in our analysis, reinforcing that early treatment response, whether spontaneous or treatment-induced, is the critical outcome determinant.11,16

A key limitation of our study was the smaller-than-planned sample size, with only 72 of the 140 required patients enrolled. Recruitment was particularly challenging because of the COVID-19 pandemic, decreased indication for liver biopsies in some centers, and strict entry criteria requiring histologically confirmed steatohepatitis and evidence of spontaneous bilirubin decline. Of 348 patients with suspected AH, 276 (79%) were not recruited. This highlights the difficulty of conducting randomized trials in AH and underscores the need for consideration of broader or more pragmatic entry criteria in future studies (e.g. potentially relaxing biopsy requirements or incorporating broader inclusion criteria, such as the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism criteria,2 and considering pragmatic, community-based approaches to enhance recruitment).

We performed the mITT analysis, excluding three patients without histological evidence of steatohepatitis. We implemented this approach to ensure a homogeneous study population. Given that only three patients were excluded owing to absence of confirmed histological steatohepatitis, significant selection bias is unlikely.

Mortality was numerically lower in the placebo arm, but this difference was not statistically significant, and the trial was underpowered to detect a modest effect. The initial assumption of a 20% absolute reduction in mortality (anticipating 35% mortality in the placebo arm vs. 15% in the corticosteroid arm) appears to have overestimated both the expected event rate and the plausible benefit of corticosteroids in this population. Patients with AH who exhibit spontaneous improvement in bilirubin levels have a more favorable prognosis, which contributed to the lower-than-anticipated mortality and further limited the power to detect any treatment effect.

The clinical implications are significant. We propose a risk-stratified approach: upon admission with suspected severe AH, observe bilirubin trajectory for 5–10 days while completing infection screening and liver biopsy. Patients with bilirubin decline >10% can be managed without corticosteroids, achieving survival rates comparable with non-severe disease.17 Patients without spontaneous improvement should be considered for corticosteroid therapy. This approach optimizes patient selection while ensuring rigorous infection screening before treatment initiation—critical given that infection is common before corticosteroids and associated with worse outcomes.18

The cumulative incidence of infection at 90 days was numerically higher in the corticosteroid arm, with a more frequent pulmonary location. This finding was consistent with prior studies,6,8 underscoring the importance of avoiding unnecessary corticosteroid exposure in populations without survival benefit.

Our study has important strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluating corticosteroids specifically in patients with severe AH with early spontaneous improvement. All participants had biopsy-proven AH, eliminating diagnostic uncertainty. Although the study was terminated early because of recruitment challenges, the findings provide meaningful insights for clinical practice.

In conclusion, corticosteroid therapy was not associated with improved survival in patients with severe AH who demonstrate spontaneous bilirubin decline of >10% within 5–10 days of admission; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution given that the trial was underpowered owing to incomplete recruitment and lower-than-anticipated 90-day mortality in the placebo arm, precluding a definitive conclusion on the absence of corticosteroid benefit. Observation for 5–10 days after admission may help identify patients with favorable prognosis who could be spared corticosteroid therapy and its associated infection risk; however, this requires confirmation in adequately powered studies.
