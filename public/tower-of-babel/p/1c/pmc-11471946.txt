Treatment of Antineutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibody–Associated Vasculitis With Diffuse Alveolar Hemorrhage With Avacopan
Authors: Sam D. Falde, Amos Lal, Rodrigo Cartin‐Ceba, Lester E. Mertz, Fernando C. Fervenza, Ladan Zand, Matthew J. Koster, Kenneth J. Warrington, Augustine S. Lee, Nabeel Aslam, Andy Abril, Ulrich Specks
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAvacopan, an activated complement factor 5 receptor antagonist, has been approved as adjunct therapy for severe active antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis (AAV). Current evidence supports the management of AAV presenting with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) by administering glucocorticoids combined with either rituximab or cyclophosphamide in addition to supportive care. The role of avacopan in patients with DAH as a primary severe disease manifestation of AAV has not been well established. Furthermore, concerns remain regarding timely access to avacopan, the best glucocorticoid tapering regimen, and long‐term efficacy and safety of the drug. We sought to identify clinical features and outcomes of patients presenting with DAH secondary to AAV who received avacopan in addition to glucocorticoids and rituximab or cyclophosphamide.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of all consecutive patients presenting with DAH as part of active severe granulomatosis with polyangiitis or microscopic polyangiitis. Demographic and clinical characteristics were collected at presentation and follow‐up.ResultsFifteen patients met inclusion criteria and were observed for a median time of 17 weeks (interquartile range [IQR] 6–37 weeks) after initiation of avacopan. Patients were predominantly female and White, had never smoked, and were a median age of 66 years (IQR 52–72 years) at diagnosis. The majority had newly diagnosed severe AAV with renal involvement. Three patients progressed to respiratory failure. The timing of avacopan introduction and patterns of glucocorticoid tapers varied widely in this cohort. Two serious adverse events related to infection were observed, including one opportunistic infection leading to the patient's death, although neither was directly attributed to avacopan administration.ConclusionWe describe the clinical course of patients who presented with the severe AAV disease manifestation of DAH and received avacopan as adjunct therapy. Most patients achieved remission during follow‐up, and adverse events, including infection, were observed.

INTRODUCTION

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)–associated vasculitis (AAV) has been referred to as “pauci‐immune” because the histopathologic lesions contain little to no detectable Ig or complement deposits. 1 , 2 However, the alternative complement pathway plays a fundamental role in various mechanisms invoked in the pathogenesis of AAV, including neutrophil recruitment and activation, perpetuation of the inflammatory cascade, and expression of ANCA target antigens on neutrophil membranes. 1 , 2 , 3 Avacopan, a small molecule that blocks the interaction between the anaphylatoxin, activated complement factor 5 (C5a), and its receptor has been shown to interfere with these mechanisms in a murine model of myeloperoxidase (MPO)–AAV. 4 Subsequent clinical trials have shown efficacy of avacopan in AAV and led to its recent approval as an adjunct to standard therapy for active severe AAV by regulatory agencies. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Questions and challenges remain regarding the administration of avacopan in patients with acute diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH). These include the immediate effect on the progression of DAH, the glucocorticoid‐sparing effect in the early phase of the disease, and the administration of avacopan to intubated patients. We conducted this analysis of a real‐world practice cohort to obtain data that can inform the design of future studies addressing these unresolved issues and contribute to a standardized administration of avacopan in patients with DAH caused by AAV.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

After obtaining approval from the institutional review board, we conducted a retrospective analysis of all consecutive patients with DAH caused by AAV treated with avacopan during induction of remission at Mayo Clinic sites in Rochester, Minnesota; Jacksonville, Florida; and Scottsdale, Arizona, between January 1, 2021, and August 18, 2023. A proprietary medical record search algorithm was used to identify a list of patients who received avacopan prescriptions linked to a diagnosis of AAV. Currently, there is no protocol for the initiation of avacopan in addition to standard remission induction regimens; therefore, these patients received avacopan at the discretion of their treating physicians. Patients were included if they were 18 years of age or older at the time of presentation, had provided research authorization, and were diagnosed with AAV, including granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), proteinase 3 (PR3)–AAV, MPO‐AAV, or ANCA‐negative AAV. Patients with eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis were excluded.

This patient list was then analyzed using a proprietary advanced text explorer with natural language processing to search the electronic medical record for all clinical notes with query terms including “diffuse alveolar hemorrhage,” “alveolar hemorrhage,” or “DAH” (Figure 1). On completing this query, the medical records underwent direct thorough review to ensure the identified patients met Chapel Hill consensus definitions and American College of Rheumatology and EULAR classification criteria for GPA or MPA. 10 , 11 , 12 We also ensured that patients met the diagnostic criteria for DAH obtained by bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and received avacopan as part of remission induction therapy. 13 Individuals who had not undergone a diagnostic bronchoscopy were considered for inclusion if they presented with radiographic evidence of bilateral alveolar infiltrates without a more likely alternative diagnosis in addition to one or more of the following: hemoptysis, unexplained drop in hemoglobin level (>2 g/dL), or anemia (hemoglobin level <10 g/dL), as used in the study by Quartuccio et al. 14 Finally, to be included without diagnostic bronchoscopy, we also required evidence of extrapulmonary life‐ or organ‐threatening involvement of AAV (including glomerulonephritis, central nervous system involvement, etc). 9 Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted by Mayo Clinic. 15 Abstraction from the medical record included demographic data, comorbidities, clinical symptoms, concomitant medications, laboratory test results, bronchoscopy and imaging findings, pathology reports, and patient outcomes, including adverse events.

The Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score for Wegener's Granulomatosis (BVAS/WG), a validated instrument to score disease activity in GPA and MPA, was used to measure disease activity and organ involvement. 16 Remission was defined as a BVAS/WG of 0, independent of the dose of prednisone. Complete remission was defined as a BVAS/WG of 0 with complete discontinuation of prednisone. Relapse was defined as an increase in the BVAS/WG of ≥1, resulting in therapy changes after remission had been achieved. Disease flare was defined as an increase in the BVAS/WG of ≥1 before achievement of remission, resulting in a therapy change. The degree of hypoxemia on presentation was quantified as the ratio of oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry to the fraction of inspired oxygen as documented during the first outpatient, emergency department, or hospital admission encounter. 13 We defined respiratory failure as requiring additional respiratory support, including oxygen supplementation by high‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC), noninvasive ventilation (NIV), and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV). 13 Serious adverse event definitions were used according to National Institute on Aging Adverse Event and Serious Adverse Event Guidelines. 17 Continuous variables were summarized as means ± SDs, with a normal distribution as determined by the Shapiro‐Wilk test, or as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) for skewed distributions.

RESULTS

Fifteen patients met the inclusion criteria among all Mayo Clinic sites (Figure 1). Patients were predominantly White (n = 14) and female (n = 9), had never smoked (n = 11), and were a median age of 66 years (IQR 52–72 years) at presentation with DAH. Of the patients presenting with DAH and AAV, nine were newly diagnosed, and six presented with relapsing disease. The diagnosis of vasculitis was confirmed by biopsy in nine patients (kidney, n = 5; skin, n = 2, lung, n = 1; and temporal artery, n = 1). Eight patients tested positive for PR3‐ANCA, and seven tested positive for MPO‐ANCA. No patient was positive for both PR3‐ANCA and MPO‐ANCA or for any ANCA type in combination with anti–glomerular basement membrane antibody. DAH was confirmed by BAL in 14 patients. The single individual without a diagnostic BAL presented with hemoptysis, pulmonary infiltrates, and new anemia (with a >3‐g/dL drop in hemoglobin level), and active pauci‐immune glomerulonephritis was confirmed by kidney biopsy. Given the presentation with DAH, all patients had severe disease activity. Their median BVAS/WG was 8 (IQR 5–10), and more than half (n = 9) of the patients also had active renal involvement, but only two required renal replacement therapy. Of those who required renal replacement therapy, both occurred before administration of avacopan. Neither patient had renal recovery; one of these patients died during follow‐up and is described later in this discussion.

All patients received intravenous methylprednisolone therapy and avacopan given orally at a dosage of 30 mg twice a day. Methylprednisolone at 500 to 1,000 mg was given for three days in 12 of 15 patients, with lower doses given in three patients for patient‐specific factors. Rituximab was given to 14 of 15 patients, dosed at standard remission induction dosing (375 mg/m2 once a week for four weeks or 1 g, twice, two weeks apart). Cyclophosphamide was given to 3 of 15 patients, administered intravenously at weight‐based dosing (ranging from 12 to 19 mg/kg). In these three patients receiving cyclophosphamide, a total of one to seven cumulative doses were given, with intervals ranging from weekly to monthly between doses and a total cumulative dose of 1,000 mg to 8,400 mg. Two patients received both intravenous cyclophosphamide and rituximab for remission induction, although in both patients, this was not administered concurrently. One patient received intravenous bolus cyclophosphamide after having received remission induction doses of rituximab because of progressive respiratory failure, described in more detail in the next paragraph. Another patient received a single dose of intravenous cyclophosphamide before receiving plasma exchange (PLEX) and was subsequently transitioned to rituximab. PLEX was used in addition to standard remission induction therapy (glucocorticoids with rituximab or cyclophosphamide) in five patients, consisting of two (n = 1), four (n = 3), and five (n = 1) PLEX sessions. Two of these patients had received PLEX at other facilities before their care at Mayo Clinic. In three patients, PLEX was initiated at Mayo Clinic sites by the intensive care unit management team, with indications cited as renal failure, recurrent alveolar hemorrhage, and progressive respiratory failure. Median follow‐up time at our institutions was 17 weeks (IQR 6–37 weeks) after initiation of avacopan therapy. Three patients developed respiratory failure requiring respiratory support ranging from HFNC to IMV. In two of these, the respiratory failure developed after initiation of avacopan.

The first individual (patient 6 in Figures 2 and 3) had persistent supplemental oxygen needs during the first nine days of his hospitalization (although not meeting criteria for respiratory failure). Seven days after initiation of remission induction therapy, which included intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone at 60 mg daily, rituximab, and avacopan, the patient developed respiratory failure requiring oxygen supplementation by HFNC. Evaluation at that time revealed no evidence of progression of DAH, but an acute pulmonary embolism prompting initiation of anticoagulation. At that time, the patient's renal function worsened. Given concern for refractory disease activity, methylprednisolone pulses, cyclophosphamide, and PLEX were given. After these interventions, the patient's respiratory status and renal function gradually improved, and he subsequently achieved complete remission.

The second patient (patient 13 in Figures 2 and 3) presented with mild hypoxemia requiring supplemental oxygen during the initial six days of his hospitalization (not meeting criteria for respiratory failure), which slowly improved after remission induction therapy with methylprednisolone, rituximab, and avacopan. He was weaned off supplemental oxygen completely for three days while on avacopan and prednisone at 60 mg daily. Given clinical evidence of resolution of DAH and a history of cerebrovascular disease, therapeutic antiplatelet therapy was resumed. Within 24 hours of starting antiplatelet therapy, he was noted to have increased bruising, cutaneous purpura, and hemoptysis. In the subsequent 24 hours, he developed hypoxemic respiratory failure and worsening hemoptysis requiring intubation and IMV (12 days after receiving remission induction therapy including avacopan). At that time, repeat bronchoscopy revealed progressively bloody return consistent with DAH. PLEX was performed for presumed refractory disease activity despite normalized markers of inflammation and without other evidence for refractory disease activity outside the lung. Hematology was consulted given cutaneous findings suggestive of coagulopathy and the evidence of recurrent alveolar hemorrhage. Although fibrinogen levels were normal, D‐dimer and soluble fibrin monomer levels were elevated, and the nadir platelet count was 57 × 109/L. These findings were felt to be compatible with chronic disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In the context of this coagulopathy without other evidence of refractory vasculitis, this DAH recurrence was attributed to DIC rather than a disease flare. No disease flares or relapses occurred in any of the other 13 patients after starting avacopan.

The median overall time to initiation of avacopan was 18 days (IQR 2–24 days) after starting standard remission induction therapy with the first pulse of intravenous methylprednisolone. Avacopan was started within one week of diagnosis of DAH in the hospital in seven patients. In the other eight patients, it was initiated after dismissal from the hospital and more than 14 days after the beginning of standard remission induction therapy. For this reason, we stratified outcomes by those patients who received outcomes early (seven days or less) and those who received outcomes late (more than seven days) in Figure 3. Notably, there were no significant differences in disease activity by BVAS/WG or degree of hypoxemia on presentation comparing both groups (Table 2). Three patients had respiratory failure, all of whom received avacopan early. Two of these patients progressing to respiratory failure after avacopan administration have been described in detail previously. Progression to renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy, overall rates of remission, and death were similar in these subgroups (Table 2). Common reasons for delayed avacopan dosing included unavailability of the drug in the hospital setting and lack of familiarity with the prescription process on the part of providers.

Tapering regimens of oral glucocorticoids administered after initiation of avacopan were variable among the patients (Figure 2). Two patients received no oral glucocorticoids after completion of three intravenous methylprednisolone pulses, and in four patients, oral prednisone had been discontinued by week 4. In the other patients, tapering of oral glucocorticoids was much more protracted (Figure 2). The details of both remission induction and maintenance regimens, including the timing of avacopan administration during the disease course, are illustrated for each patient in Figure 3. Of the 14 patients alive at the conclusion of the follow‐up period, 10 patients had achieved complete remission, with a median time to complete remission of 52 days (IQR 26–114 days) after initiation of avacopan therapy. The remaining four patients achieved remission but had not completed their prednisone taper.

Three patients developed serious adverse events during the follow‐up period. Two patients had serious infections. The first of these developed a severe opportunistic meningoencephalitis caused by varicella‐zoster virus 30 days after starting avacopan. This was diagnosed during a prolonged hospitalization after having received four sessions of PLEX and protracted high‐dose glucocorticoid therapy (6 days of intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy, and a total of 40 days on prednisone equivalent at >20 mg daily) and rituximab. Meningoencephalitis was complicated by intraparenchymal hemorrhage, obstructive hydrocephalus, midline shift, and progression to brain death. The second patient developed gangrene with osteomyelitis not attributed to disease activity but rather chronic damage from vasculitis. After operative debridement and antibiotic therapy, the patient improved clinically and is in remission. The third serious adverse event consisted of progression to renal failure requiring intermittent hemodialysis occurring four months after original disease onset with DAH and active renal disease and initiation of remission induction therapy. The patient showed no evidence of active glomerulonephritis by urinalysis and microscopy or any other sign of vasculitis activity; consequently, the progression to renal failure was attributed to damage from vasculitis.

Avacopan was discontinued in three patients before completion of 12 months of therapy. The out‐of‐pocket co‐payment for avacopan was cost‐prohibitive for two patients. In another patient, the treating physician felt that continuation of avacopan beyond 180 days of therapy was not warranted. No adverse events attributed to avacopan were cited as the primary reason for discontinuation of the drug. Table 3 provides additional patient outcome data.

DISCUSSION

DAH represents a severe vasculitis manifestation of AAV causing substantial morbidity and mortality. 13 , 14 , 18 , 19 , 20 This disease manifestation remains associated with an early mortality rate of about 10% and progression to respiratory failure in 10% to 60% of patients. 18 , 21 , 22 , 23 Rapid diagnosis and implementation of effective treatment are crucial to mitigate these outcomes. 9 , 24 , 25 High‐dose glucocorticoid therapy has been the only fast‐acting remission induction treatment. Both cyclophosphamide and rituximab take weeks to exert their full effect on the adaptive immune system, allowing the tapering of glucocorticoids. 26 , 27 The only other potentially fast‐acting intervention, PLEX, has not been shown to be beneficial in DAH caused by AAV and is therefore no longer recommended by the most recent treatment guidelines for AAV. 9 , 13 , 23 , 24 , 28

This sole reliance on high‐dose glucocorticoid therapy in the acute phase of the disease including DAH may have changed with the recent approval of avacopan as an additional treatment option for patients with AAV. This agent selectively interferes with the binding of C5a to its receptor on neutrophils and myeloid cells. 5 This pathway plays a fundamental role in neutrophil activation and chemotaxis and is thought to be the cause of endothelial damage, necrosis, and end‐organ dysfunction in AAV. 1 , 29 Furthermore, C5a is expressed in a variety of effector cells, including alveolar macrophages, and has been implicated in various pathways of acute and chronic alveolitis. 30 , 31 , 32

Avacopan was introduced into the management of AAV primarily because it was effective in a mouse model of MPO‐AAV, and two subsequent phase 2 trials suggested benefit in mitigating early renal damage in patients with AAV and active renal disease. 4 , 6 , 7 , 29 The subsequent label‐enabling phase 3 trial showed noninferiority of avacopan compared to a standard glucocorticoid dosing regimen at 26 weeks when added to either rituximab or cyclophosphamide for remission induction. 8 For sustained remission at 52 weeks, avacopan was found superior to standard glucocorticoid dosing, and there was a higher improvement in renal function at week 52 in patients treated with avacopan. 8 Avacopan was also better tolerated compared to glucocorticoids; the glucocorticoid toxicity index and patient‐reported outcome measures were significantly better in the avacopan group compared to the glucocorticoid group. 8 The report of the phase 3 study has not provided much information on patients with DAH, possibly because only 12 participants in the trial were reported to have DAH and because patients with respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation were excluded from the trial. 8

Because the histopathologic appearance of pulmonary capillaritis is most consistent with the lessons learned from the MPO‐ANCA mouse model about the pathogenesis of AAV, one can hypothesize that avacopan is at least as effective for DAH in AAV as it is for glomerulonephritis in AAV. Avacopan is approved as an adjunct for treatment of active severe AAV, and DAH is a severe disease manifestation for which avacopan is thus indicated and now considered part of the standard of care. 9 Most recently, the study by Chalkia et al 33 described eight patients presenting with hypoxemia secondary to DAH who received avacopan. The demographics and AAV characteristics of these eight patients are similar to our cohort, except for our cohort having a higher proportion of PR3‐ANCA–positive patients. Four patients (50%) in the UK cohort experienced respiratory failure requiring NIV or IMV compared to three patients (20%) in our cohort. Twelve patients in our cohort (80%) received either rituximab or cyclophosphamide, as compared to four (50%) patients in the UK cohort, who received both rituximab and cyclophosphamide in combination. Given the retrospective nature of these data sets, one cannot account for between‐group differences in disease manifestations, illness severity, clinical course, and practice variation that may have accounted for these differences. However, our patients received standard remission induction most consistent with most recent evidence and guidelines. 9 , 13 , 24 We also sought to replicate the outcomes reported in the ADVOCATE trial aiming for complete remission (BVAS/WG of 0, with complete discontinuation of prednisone), which was observed in 10 (66%) patients in the follow‐up period. 8

Although avacopan was given strictly according to the label at a dosage of 30 mg, twice daily orally to all patients in our cohort, two patients from the UK cohort who required mechanical ventilation received avacopan applied off‐label via a nasogastric tube. 34 This illustrates the need for a US Food and Drug Administration–approved liquid formulation of avacopan either for pediatric administration or for the application of the drug by nasogastric feeding tube to patients receiving mechanical ventilation. There is currently no protocol for emulsification of the drug, and pharmacokinetic studies assessing the bioavailability of the drug in critically ill patients have not been completed. 6 , 7 , 8 , 34 Alternative formulations of therapy targeting complement inhibition in the field of AAV are on the horizon and may address this current limitation in parenteral complement blockade. 35 Vilobelimab, an intravenous anti‐C5a antibody, has demonstrated efficacy as a glucocorticoid‐sparing agent in a phase 2 trial assessing the efficacy of this agent as compared to glucocorticoids with traditional remission induction therapy in AAV. 36 Although vilobelimab has yet to be studied in AAV with DAH, both vilobelimab and avdoralimab (another monoclonal antibody targeting the C5a receptor) have been studied in randomized controlled trials in patients with respiratory failure secondary to COVID‐19, with reassuring safety outcomes. 37 , 38

Our cohort, being the largest observational cohort describing the real‐world application of avacopan in patients with AAV and DAH to date, enriches our understanding of the potential and limitations of the administration of this novel agent in this subset of patients with the most acutely life‐threatening disease manifestation of AAV. Follow‐up in this cohort was limited to the remission induction phase, and during this period of observation, avacopan appears safe and effective as an adjuvant to standard treatment in patients with AAV and DAH.

Our report has limitations inherent to observational studies, including selection bias. Despite the limitations, this study highlights several issues that should be addressed and monitored in the future as avacopan becomes more widely administered. First, starting avacopan as early as possible in addition to standard remission induction strategies is supported by the current understanding of the underlying pathophysiology of AAV, supportive data from randomized clinical trials, and the relative safety of early initiation observed in our patients. This observational cohort study reveals the lag time in initiating avacopan in clinical practice and provides some insight into the etiology of this delay. Although avacopan cannot currently be crushed and administered enterally for administration to patients undergoing IMV, this was not cited as a primary rationale for delayed avacopan initiation in this study. Second, our study illustrates that, currently, there is no clear consensus about the approach to glucocorticoid tapering when taking avacopan, despite a protocol provided by the ADVOCATE trial. 8 The fact that two patients in our cohort received no oral prednisone and that an additional four received no prednisone beyond four weeks after starting treatment with avacopan illustrates that when avacopan is started early, progression of DAH to respiratory failure is unlikely, even when no or very little oral prednisone is given. This is consistent with the significant glucocorticoid‐sparing effect documented by the large phase 3 trial in patients with severe AAV. 8 Third, our work draws attention to relevant questions pertaining to the duration of avacopan administration and pragmatic barriers prescribing this medication beyond the initial 30 days in clinical practice.

Given its rapid onset of action and consistent with our current understanding of the pathogenesis of capillaritis in AAV, consideration should be given to early implementation of this adjuvant therapy in patients with DAH caused by AAV. 4 , 5 Consistent with the results from the large phase 3 trial in patients with severe AAV, taking oral prednisone can also be minimized in patients presenting with DAH. 8 Finally, avacopan was well tolerated by the patients, and none of the serious adverse events were attributed to avacopan. Additional prospective and protocolized studies to further evaluate the precise role, timing, and duration of avacopan administration, as well as the standardized reduction of concomitant glucocorticoids in patients with DAH caused by AAV, should be conducted.

Overall, avacopan holds promise as an adjuvant for treating AAV with DAH. We describe the largest cohort of patients with AAV and DAH treated with avacopan, who were observed closely for clinical response and adverse effects. Despite the limitations of being an observational study, our data support the administration of avacopan and the potential for glucocorticoid sparing in patients with this potentially life‐threatening disease manifestation of AAV. These data can help inform the design of future randomized trials in AAV.
