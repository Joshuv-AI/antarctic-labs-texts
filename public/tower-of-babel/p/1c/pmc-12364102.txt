Moving beyond gender: a systematic review of diversity data gaps in prospective trauma research
Authors: Stephanie Haering, Jule M. Kaeselau, Christine Knaevelsrud, Sinha Engel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: To better understand heterogeneity in PTSD development and to ensure that knowledge of risk and intervention mechanisms is available for a diverse group of trauma exposed individuals, psychotraumatology needs to consider diversity in sample characteristics.Objective: In a comprehensive overview, we evaluated how nine diversity domains including age, socioeconomic status (SES), race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, care responsibilities, religion/worldview, mental health, physical health, and disability status were considered in prospective PTSD research. In in-depth analyses, we further investigated the assessment and representation of population groups with regard to age, SES and race/ethnicity.Method: Secondary analysis of a meta-analysis project that examined sex and gender considerations among k = 45 studies that prospectively assessed PTSD symptoms in trauma exposed individuals. In this study, additional information on diversity assessment and characteristics was systematically extracted and analysed.Results: While age was assessed in all studies, consideration of the remaining diversity domains was insufficient, ranging from 57.8% of studies for SES to 0.0% regarding sexual orientation and care responsibilities. The number of considered diversity domains did not increase over years. Our in-depth analyses showed that middle-aged, well-educated and racially non-marginalised individuals were overrepresented.Conclusions: Our study revealed diversity data gaps in reporting and representation, limiting the generalisability of research findings. Recommendations for increased diversity considerations are discussed.

Introduction

Diversity in science has long been neglected. For example, women have been historically underrepresented in clinical trials, resulting in a gender data gap in biomedical research (Liu & DiPietro Mager, 2016). A gender data gap also exists within psychotraumatology. Although women are at increased risk for developing PTSD, they are underrepresented in research on PTSD risk factors (Haering et al., 2024). Specifically, prospective studies on PTSD development that used gold standard diagnostic criteria examined men twice as often as women (68.3% vs. 31.7%; Haering et al., 2024). Furthermore, none of these studies considered gender minorities (Haering et al., 2024). While on the one hand there is increasing evidence on the neglect of sex and gender aspects in health research, awareness for these issues has also gained more and more attention. As such, relevant stakeholders have started to address these shortcomings. The European Journal of Psychotraumatology has pioneered the field by introducing a gender policy to stimulate sex and gender-sensitive research (Olff, 2016) and thus has contributed towards refined knowledge on sex and gender aspects in the development, course and treatment of trauma-related disorders (Langeland & Olff, 2024). Recently, further stakeholders have recognised the importance of sex and gender on health and encourage scientists to report data disaggregated by sex/gender (nature portfolio, 2022; Peters & Woodward, 2023).

However, in order to address existing inequalities in trauma research and beyond, a broader perspective on diversity is necessary. Minoritised or marginalised individuals such as those from a lower socioeconomic status (SES), those identifying as sexual minority (e.g. lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer or questioning; LGBTQ+), or members of an ethnic minority or racialised group are, for instance, at increased risk for PTSD as well (Brewin et al., 2000; Marchi et al., 2023; Paradies et al., 2015). In addition, intersectional theory (Crenshaw, 1991) acknowledges that domains of privilege and marginalisation do not act independently, but interactively, and therefore must be examined in combination. Indeed, novel research from register-based data shows that sex differences in mental disorders widen under the magnifying glass of social inequality and vary across the lifespan (interaction of sex, age, SES; Yang et al., 2024). In line with this, further approaches describe the specific intersectional stressors and related negative mental health consequences faced by, for instance, LGBTQ + Asian Americans (intersection of gender, sexual orientation and race/ethnicity; Ching et al., 2018), or by women who provide unpaid care (intersection of gender, SES and care responsibilities; Seedat & Rondon, 2021).

In order to accurately understand and address diversity-related social disparities as well as intersectional effects, psychotraumatology must consider information about the specific attributes and intersections that characterise trauma-exposed individuals. Neglecting this information might result in inadequate care and reinforcement of power privileges and marginalisation. For instance, while artificial intelligence algorithms hold the potential to advance the diagnosis, risk prediction and treatment of traumatised individuals, they might also reproduce or even perpetuate existing health inequities, if trained with data that is inherently biased (Obermeyer et al., 2019; Timmons et al., 2023). Furthermore, applying a social equality lens towards health care provision reveals that even in countries where mental health treatments are covered by statutory health insurance, marginalised individuals such as those with lower SES experience more difficulties in access to mental health services (Epping et al., 2017). On the other hand, greater recognition of diversity characteristics holds the potential to identify and address existing biases, and advance our understanding of patient heterogeneity. Such knowledge is necessary to produce a more rigorous and relevant mental health research and care, and develop refined targeted interventions that are tailored to the specific needs of the diverse population affected by trauma-related sequelae.

To this end, the first step would be to comprehensively collect and report data on various diversity domains (e.g. gender, age, SES, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, care responsibilities, religion/worldview, mental health, physical health, and disability status, as proposed by Stadler et al. (2023)). In a second step, potential sample bias must be assessed, to ensure that data adequately represents affected individuals. To date, however, it has not yet been evaluated to which extent this is the case in research on PTSD aetiology.

In the present systematic review, we aimed to investigate potential diversity data gaps in a sample of prospective studies on PTSD risk factors. The study sample had previously been investigated with regard to sex and gender considerations (Haering et al., 2024). In this secondary data analysis, we now extended our perspective beyond sex and gender to explore the following research questions: (1) Did the sample descriptions include information about the following nine diversity domains: age, SES, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, care responsibilities, religion/worldview, mental health, physical health, and disability status? Zooming in more closely on age, SES, and race/ethnicity, we further explored (2) how those domains were assessed and reported; and (3) which age, SES and racial/ethnic groups were represented in included samples?

Methods

This systematic review was a secondary data analysis of a larger meta-analysis project that investigated sex and gender aspects in PTSD risk factor literature (Haering et al., 2024). It was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). Our extended analysis was preregistered (https://osf.io/hf7ba/) and the data and analysis scripts are openly available at https://osf.io/jq2s4/.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

As this is a secondary data analysis of a larger meta-analysis project, the inclusion criteria were defined according to the primary study’s objective. Detailed information are available in (Haering et al., 2024). In brief, studies that investigated trauma exposed individuals with a mean age of ≥ 18 years; employed a prospective design (i.e. conducted a risk factor assessment before, during or within one month post-trauma); and assessed PTSD symptoms using the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS; Blake et al., 1995; Weathers et al., 2018), i.e. the gold standard for PTSD diagnosis within one month and five years after the traumatic event were included. We included reports of studies published in English language in any year.

Studies were excluded if participants received an intervention to reduce PTSD symptoms, if they were included based on (subthreshold) PTSD symptoms rather than trauma exposure per se, if trauma exposure was not determinable, or if PTSD symptoms were not measured at a specified timepoint. Further, reviews, meta-analyses and other studies without original data, qualitative and case studies (n < 10) were excluded.

Literature search and study selection

The literature search was based on the primary study’s objectives (Haering et al., 2024) and includes all reports published until October 16, 2022. The following electronic data bases were searched: PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and PSYNDEX, using the terms (ptsd OR ‘post traumatic stress disorder’ OR ‘posttraumatic stress disorder’ OR ‘post-traumatic stress disorder’) AND (trauma OR traumatic OR disaster OR accident OR combat OR war OR abuse OR assault OR rape OR crime OR survivor* OR emergency) AND (longitudinal OR prospective OR ‘long term’ OR ‘time factor’ OR ‘follow up’ OR ‘follow-up’) NOT (kids OR adolescents OR teenager OR ‘child’) to search titles and abstracts. Additionally, we contacted experts in the field, searched ClinicalTrials.gov for eligible completed studies and screened conference abstracts, as well as reference lists of relevant review articles. The full search strategy is presented in Supplement 1.

The studies were selected as follows: A trained researcher (SH) screened titles and abstracts of all records. The screening quality was determined as a second trained researcher (JMK) screened a randomly selected subpool 20% of all records in duplo, with substantial interrater agreement (κ = .65). In case of disagreement, the record was transferred to the full-text screening, which was also conducted by (SH), with (JMK) screening a randomly selected subpool of 20% of the records in duplo. Interrater agreement was high (κ = .90). If raters disagreed, a third trained researcher (SE) decided about inclusion. Lastly, we analysed the included studies for sample overlap. In case of overlap, the study’s report that met the following criteria (in this order) was included: highest number of CAPS follow-up evaluations, largest sample size, or with earlier time of publication. Data extraction and analysis

A standardised coding form was used to extract the data of interest. Extraction of bibliographic information as well as sample size, trauma context and country where the study was conducted was extracted as described in (Haering et al., 2024).

Additional data extraction for the research questions related to this manuscript was performed by (JMK). The selection of the nine diversity domains was based on the Diversity Minimal Item Set by Stadler et al. (2023). To determine whether the respective diversity domains had been considered (research question 1), studies were positively evaluated if information on the respective diversity domain was reported in numbers (e.g. percentage or number of participants by racial/ethnic group; or mean educational years of the sample). The operationalisation for the nine diversity domains is presented in Supplement 2. Due to the health-related focus of the studies included, we further rated whether exclusion criteria regarding mental health, physical health, and disability aspects were defined among included studies. To provide an overview of diversity consideration, we calculated frequencies and percentages indicating how many studies considered the respective diversity domain. To assess, whether diversity reporting increased over time, we conducted a linear regression of the number of considered diversity domains on publication year.

For the in-depth analysis of assessment and representativeness regarding age, SES and race/ethnicity (research questions 2 and 3), we extracted M and SD of age in years, and the size of the respective sample (n), in order to calculate the weighted mean and pooled standard deviation of participants’ age for statistical analysis. Regarding SES, we extracted the main category of reporting (e.g. occupation, financial situation, degree, years of education, etc.) for descriptive analyses. If years of education were reported, we extracted M, SD and n, in order to calculate the weighted mean and pooled standard deviation. If educational degrees were reported, we extracted the number of participants without high school degree, with high school degree, with high school degree and some further education (e.g. some years spent in college without formal degree, apprenticeship) and with college or university degree for descriptive analyses. Regarding race/ethnicity, we extracted the main category of reporting (e.g. ethnicity, race) as well as the corresponding sub-categories as reported in the manuscript (e.g. ‘Black’, ‘Hispanic’, ‘White’ etc.; or ‘White British’, ‘Non-White British’), and the number of participants for each sub-group for descriptive analyses. Notably, ethnic/racial identities and their categorisation is country – and culture-specific. To summarise information across studies, we therefore decided to distinguish between historically marginalised versus non-marginalised groups. As such, for each study we assessed which groups would be considered marginalised or not with regard to the specific study context.

Analyses were performed in R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2023).

Results

Reporting of diversity-related information

A total of 45 reports, representing data from n = 11,959 trauma survivors, were included in this study. Table 1 provides a general description of the reports, as well as an overview of the results for research question (1). Participants’ age was reported in all (100%) studies. The second-most frequently described diversity domain was SES, with 58% of the studies (k = 26) reporting at least one financial, occupational or educational indicator. Participants’ ethnicity/race was described in 40% of the studies (k = 18). 64% (k = 29) of the studies considered mental health, at least by defining some aspect of it as an exclusion criterion; and 18% (k = 8) of included studies explicitly provided information about their participants’ mental health status (beyond PTSD) in the manuscript. Likewise, 22% (k = 10) and 7% (k = 3) of the studies included physical health and respectively disability aspects at least as an exclusion criterion, but for each, only 4% (k = 2) reported related information in the manuscript. Equally, 4% (k = 2) of the studies described their participants’ religion/ worldview. Sexual orientation and care responsibilities were described in none (0%) of the studies. In-depth analysis of age, SES and race/ethnicity consideration

Age consideration

The weighted mean age for all participants (n = 11,959) was 31.21 years with a pooled standard deviation of SD = 10.3 years. Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of the mean age scores across studies. The distribution is moderately left-skewed, with the tail of the curve extending towards the younger ages. This indicates that the weighted mean is slightly lower than the median. Overall, the plot shows a unimodal distribution, with a single peak around the late 30s. SES consideration

While 58% (k = 26) of all studies reported at least one aspect of SES, the majority of this subgroup (88%, k = 23) described their participants’ education. Another 62% (k = 16) provided data about their occupation and 19% (k = 5) about their financial situation. 58% (k = 15) described multiple aspects of SES.

Taking a closer look at education specifically, Figure 4(a) shows that almost half of the included studies (49%, k = 22) did not provide any information about their participants’ education. Of those who did, 65% (k = 15) reported education levels, 30% (k = 7) reported education in years and only 4% (k = 1) reported on both aspects. Figure 4(b) indicates the distribution of mean education years, with a weighted mean of 12.61 years of education and a pooled standard deviation of SD = 2.48 years. The distribution appears slightly skewed to the left, indicating that the weighted mean is slightly lower than the weighted median (Md = 13.12). Overall, the distribution appears unimodal, with a single peak at around 13 years of education. As Figure 4(c) illustrates, among those participants with available information about education levels, most had a high school degree (n = 2,987, 43%), some with additional years spent in college, apprenticeship or similar (n = 2,367, 34%). 1,088 participants (16%) did not have a high school degree and n = 432 (6%) had a college or university degree. Race/ethnicity consideration

Among the 40% of studies (k = 18) that provided information on their participants’ race/ ethnicity, 28% (k = 5) used the terms race and ethnicity, respectively. Additionally, 22% (k = 4) used a joint expression (e.g. race/ethnicity). Four other operationalisations were identified, used by 6% (k = 1) of the studies each. These include the use of ethnicity and race as two separate categories, nationality, ancestry, as well as description of individuals as White, African American, or Latino without using a category name to describe the diversity domain. An overview or terminology used in included manuscripts is presented in Supplement 3. The terms used to describe specific racial/ethnic groups also varied substantially across studies. Following the guidelines of the AMA Manual of Style (Frey & Young, 2020) on how to report ethnicity/ race in medical and scientific papers, we therefore decided to distinguish between historically marginalised versus non-marginalised groups. Notably, this categorisation is country – and culture-specific. The specific terms that led to categorisation as marginalised versus non-marginalised are listed in Supplement 4. Table 2 provides an overview of how racially marginalised vs. non-marginalised participants were represented in prospective research on PTSD development, per country. It indicates large differences between countries; on one hand with respect to whether information on marginalised vs. non-marginalised group membership was available, and on the other hand, to which degree those groups were represented. Discussion

Against the background of well-documented social disparities in PTSD prevalence and severity, the present study examined how nine selected diversity domains were considered in prospective studies on PTSD development. Our findings highlight a diversity data gap in PTSD research that unfolds in two specific ways. On the one hand, some diversity domains are rarely assessed. For instance, with the exception of age, all diversity domains investigated in this systematic review were insufficiently considered. Furthermore, the majority of studies did not report any information about more than three of the nine domains, thereby preventing a comprehensive understanding of sample diversity. On the other hand, besides a lack of reporting diversity-related data, we further observed a diversity data gap with regard to the representation of included individuals. Our in-depth examination of age, SES, and race/ethnicity showed that those situated at the margins were not adequately represented. Rather, samples disproportionately included middle-aged, well-educated and racially non-marginalised individuals.

The limited assessment and availability of diversity-related information is in line with previous concerns about lacking inclusion, representativeness, and external validity in clinical psychological study samples (Henrich et al., 2010; Kennedy-Martin et al., 2015; Ledgerwood et al., 2022; Medin et al., 2017). Importantly, in order to address existing disparities, we need empirical visibility in the data; because ‘no data, no problem’ (Krieger, 2004).

One important aspect of diversity is mental health. Here, we observed that, while details on participants’ mental health were not commonly described in the demographics, many included studies considered mental health by excluding those with predefined mental health conditions. This has important implications, since pre-existing mental disorders have been identified as risk factors for developing PTSD after a traumatic event (Brewin et al., 2000; Hapke et al., 2006). To better understand the development and manifestation of PTSD following trauma and to produce more externally valid research results, it is important that samples represent the complex reality of lived experiences.

Similarly, while age was the only domain that was consistently reported across studies, our in-depth analysis suggests that older individuals were underrepresented among included studies. The majority of studies examined participants with a mean age between 30 and 40 years. The lack of samples focusing on older adults might be related to excluding individuals with pre-existing mental or physical health conditions, which become more common throughout the lifespan (Crimmins, 2015). However, while PTSD rates are lower among older adults (60 + years, Kessler et al., 2005), a significant number of older individuals does develop PTSD, with 6-month prevalence rates being estimated at 1% (Van Zelst et al., 2003). Against the background of recent demographic developments in industrialised countries, where the majority of included research was conducted, the lack of research including older adults does not adequately reflect the health care needs of the fastest growing age segment (Caplan, 2023).

Given that SES is generally considered a critical variable in health research (Adler & Ostrove, 1999), as well as an established risk factor for PTSD (Brewin et al., 2000), it is surprising that nearly half of included studies did not consider any indicator of SES (financial, occupational or educational aspects) at all. With lower SES being related to higher risk for trauma exposure and PTSD (Breslau et al., 1998; Yang et al., 2024), e.g. through living in less secure neighbourhoods and encountering more severe injury (Popal et al., 2021), but also through changes in brain development and threat-related neurobiology (George et al., 2023), deliberately including those at the lower spectrum of SES seems imperative for relevant and rigorous PTSD research.

Among the studies that reported at least one of the three SES aspects, education was the most frequently used indicator for SES, which seems to match the general picture of SES indications in mental health research (Burnette et al., 2022). However, educational reporting remained quite heterogenous, with some studies focusing on education levels whereas others focused on participants’ years of education. Our in-depth analysis of years of education suggests that well-educated individuals were over-represented in the included samples. While the average years of schooling in industrialised countries is 11.30 years (Barro & Lee, 2013), the present samples, mostly from industrialised countries, had an average of 12.61 years of schooling. Among the studies that reported educational levels, however, the included samples appear to be quite similar to the estimates for industrialised countries presented by Barro and Lee (2013). Yet, we should keep in mind that among studies included in this systematic review, the reporting of educational degrees was quite heterogenous, sometimes lacking further specification. For the present analyses, participants were allocated to the lower educational level, when in doubt. This might have resulted in an underestimation of actual education levels.

With regard to race/ethnicity, 60% of included studies did not report this information. Moreover, reporting of race/ethnicity-related information varied greatly between countries. While respective information was commonly reported by studies conducted in the US, other countries did not include respective information at all. In this regard, it is important to understand the cultural differences in assessing race/ethnicity. While the US have established a culture of routinely reporting participant characteristics with regard to race/ethnicity, researchers in other countries might face cultural difficulties in assessing race or ethnicity. In France, for instance, assessment of race – or ethnicity-related information is legally limited (Yordanov et al., 2023), whereas in Japan the construction of race is largely tied to citizenship and language (Yamashiro, 2013), resulting in classification of ‘Japanese’ and ‘foreign’ participants (Saeki et al., 2023; Saeki & Kusumoto, 2023). Importantly, however, the lack of race/ethnicity-related information does not mean that there is no racial discrimination (Kurade, 2023). Yet, it hinders comparative research with regard to race and ethnicity-related disparities. Realising the downsides of this ‘color-blindness’, researchers are now pushing for increasing collection of race and ethnicity-related data across countries (Boytchev, 2023).

Among participants for whom race/ethnicity-related information was available, more than two-thirds (72%) belonged to a non-marginalised group in their country. A closer look at the studies conducted in the US – the country where the majority of research included in this systematic review was produced, and where reporting of racial/ethnic information is common – allows a more nuanced perspective on the inclusion of marginalised individuals into PTSD risk factors research. Among respective studies, 32% of participants were identified as members of an ethnic minority or marginalised group. In contrast, according to current population estimates, 41% of the US population identify as non-White (e.g. as Black, African American, Hispanic, Latino, Asian, American Indian, etc. United States Census Bureau, 2023). Thus, inclusion of ethnic minorities and racially marginalised groups into prospective PTSD research in the US does not reflect its current population. Importantly, marginalised individuals such as African Americans are at increased risk for PTSD (Roberts et al., 2011), along with lower rates of receiving treatment (Roberts et al., 2011) – once more highlighting the need for consideration of ethnic/racial disparities.

Finally, our analysis of race and ethnicity-related terminology revealed great heterogeneity in descriptive categories. While specific descriptors and definitions of race and ethnicity vary among contexts and over time (Lewis et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2022), contemporary approaches emphasise the nature of race as a social construct (Bryant et al., 2022). To prevent misinterpretation of findings and reinforcement of racial biases, researchers should always display the underlying causes of racial disparities when engaging in race/ethnicity-related research (nature, 2023).

Importantly, increasing diversity within the scientific workforce is necessary (Medin et al., 2017) to foster equitable scientific practices that challenge biases, question assumptions and address blind spots that more homogenous teams might overlook. For example, studies have shown that female researchers are more likely to include sex and gender-related reporting (Sugimoto et al., 2019) and geographically more diverse journal editorial boards have been shown to publish more diverse research with regard to the first authors’ origins and the countries where data was collected (Goyanes & Demeter, 2020).

Strengths and Limitations

The results of this systematic review must be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First of all, due to the secondary nature of this analysis, the inclusion criteria were defined in relation to the original study’s objectives. As such, only prospective longitudinal studies that assessed PTSD symptoms with the CAPS interview were included in the present investigation. The study sample thus represents a specific segment of trauma research that is characterised by resource-intensive investigations on PTSD prediction that were predominantly conducted in high-income countries. Studies focusing on complex traumatisation (e.g. torture, trafficking or displacement) or conducted under more constrained resources are underrepresented in this sample. Future research should aim to take a broader perspective on psychotraumatology to reflect a more global perspective on diversity. Nevertheless, the results of this study reveal data gaps, which might be an indication of unfair resource allocation within the studies included in our study sample and the research field more generally in our study sample but importantly, also within the research field more generally. Relatedly, while the assessment of trauma exposure based on DSM-5 criteria allowed for a clearly defined understanding of traumatic events, this definition excludes studies using the ICD criteria as well as events such as racism, sexism or homophobia which have been empirically shown to not only amplify trauma-related symptoms but to produce posttraumatic stress symptoms themselves (Gradus & Galea, 2022; Holmes et al., 2016). Accordingly, it has been argued to move from an event-based definition of trauma towards a definition that also includes chronic experiences of oppression-related stress (Gradus & Galea, 2022; Holmes et al., 2016). Such an approach might better capture the population of people suffering from distressing stress and trauma reactions (Gradus & Galea, 2022). Furthermore, the assessment of diversity domains was limited to nine selected candidates. This selection is not exhaustive and further diversity-related data, such as body type, were not considered in this study. However, compared to similar analyses from related fields, which examined three (Nalven et al., 2021), four (Medina et al., 2021) or five (Burnette et al., 2022) variables, our study appears to be quite comprehensive. Similarly, the constraints with regards to the data base selection as well as inclusion of literature available in English only represent another limitation of this research. Finally, we were not able to examine intersectional representations within this review.

A major strength of this systematic review is its dual examination of reporting and representation gaps. On the one hand, we provide a comprehensive analysis of which diversity-related data is reported in the included literature, while on the other hand, we engage in an in-depth examination of who is represented within resource-intensive PTSD risk factor research. Furthermore, while prior literature has predominantly focused on the US (Burnette et al., 2022), our systematic review included studies conducted around the globe. Finally, this study used an established framework to engage in a comprehensive analysis of diversity domains.

Implications

Our systematic review highlights diversity data gaps with regards to diversity reporting and fair representation of diverse individuals among prospective studies on PTSD risk. The following steps may help to increase diversity-sensitive approaches to mental health science: (1) Consider. Researchers should reflect on diversity-relates aspects specific and relevant to both their research topic as well as the context the research is conducted in. (2) Collect. Where applicable, researchers should assess diversity-related information. To facilitate data collection, future research should further develop and establish diversity-sensitive instruments that balance brevity and inclusion to increase assessments of diversity-related data in quantitative studies (Stadler et al., 2023). Furthermore, against the background of the sensitive nature of diversity-related data, it is important to explain the intention of collecting such personal information within the research project (e.g. increased PTSD risk among marginalised groups) to participants. Importantly, researchers collecting data on diversity-related aspects must ensure that ethical and data safety regulations are in place and safeguard against the misuse of these data. Moreover, given that current samples are not representative for the population of trauma-affected individuals, researchers should deliberately reflect on their sampling strategy to attract and retain currently underrepresented groups. (3) Communicate. Routine reporting of diversity-related data can help to understand which populations are represented in psychotrauma research and who is missing. In addition to reporting of summary statistics researchers now increasingly ask to disaggregate data beyond sex and gender to adequately reflect information by diversity characteristics such as race (Siegel, 2024). (4) Contextualise. Providing contextual information can help readers to better understand and assess the sample characteristics. Contextual information may, for instance, include a statement on how the study sample compares to the general population in this specific context (e.g. what is the income distribution in this country; what is the average for years of schooling; etc). Furthermore, authors might explain which groups would be considered marginalised or non-marginalised given the specific study context. For example, in Germany, to date disparities exist among the former ‘East’ and ‘West’ of the country (Kasinger et al., 2023), and many individuals who formerly lived in the German Democratic Republic are still severely impacted by the ‘historical trauma’ (Maercker, 2023) inflicted through the Stasi regime. Such explanations might make it easier for readers to contextualise demographic information. Among a more general level, the differentiation between marginalised (e.g. East Germans) and non-marginalised (e.g. West Germans) helps to aggregate and compare information across varying study contexts. At the same time, however, it is important to keep in mind that statistical categories will never be able to display the complex lived experiences beyond the data.

In order to achieve the ultimate goal of inclusive trauma research, a whole range of measures beyond these ‘four Cs’ must certainly be taken. Therefore, we hope that our review will inspire a more comprehensive plan of action. Considering, collecting, communicating and contextualising diversity information can be seen as a first step of such a plan, which must be followed by further, targeted research on the biopsychosocial risk processes underlying marginalisation. A general barrier that needs to be acknowledged in this regard is that, unless strategies such as targeted oversampling are employed, study samples will usually only include small numbers of individuals belonging to minoritised (although not necessarily marginalised) groups. This problem of small sample sizes is amplified by the high heterogeneity within each diversity domain – although both, individuals who use a wheelchair and such with severe visual impairments can be assigned to the group of individuals living with a disability, their lived reality, risk of PTSD development after trauma and access to healthcare is impacted by different factors. Nevertheless, the use of diversity-sensitive instruments will make it possible for study participants to at least more accurately report on their reality. Further, if diversity information is reported, meta-research can use it to overcome the problem of small sample sizes and increase the statistical power across several studies.

Conclusion

Our systematic review for the first time examined diversity considerations in prospective PTSD research, a field where health-related disparities are well known. It revealed diversity data gaps in variable reporting and representation of individuals situated at the margins of society. Improving diversity in data collection, reporting and representation is necessary to understand to whom previous results do, and do not generalise to. It is further needed to detect specific, potentially even intersectional pathogenic processes that cannot be targeted on an individual level, but must be addressed on a societal level. In light of the prominent trauma-related disparities among social groups, greater attention towards sample characteristics can thus enhance the relevance and applicability or research findings, and moreover foster diversified innovations that meet the unique health care needs of different demographic groups. Only then will we be able to conduct more equitable, reproducible and rigorous trauma research and translate existing findings to a clinical practice that meets the needs of a diverse population.
