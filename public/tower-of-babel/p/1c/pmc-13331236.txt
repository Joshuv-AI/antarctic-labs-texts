Relation between socio-economic deprivation and chronic pain-related burden: a cross-sectional evaluation in a secondary care pain rehabilitation clinic
Authors: Kevin E Vowles, Katie Witkiewitz, Clearisse Del Rosario, Francis Agnew, Maura Mccarron, Claire Briggs, Danielle Rainey, Jason Brooks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDeprivation is an established risk factor for the presence of chronic pain. Less is known about the relation between deprivation and the impact of chronic pain on functioning. The aim of the present study was to evaluate differences in functioning across people with chronic pain based on deprivation and evaluate if the risk of clinically significant disruption increases proportionally with greater deprivation.MethodsThese issues were examined in a cross-sectional analysis of 491 treatment-seeking individuals with chronic pain. Participants completed measures of pain interference, social functioning, depression, anxiety, physical function, sleep disturbance, cognitive functioning, fatigue intensity and pain intensity. Deprivation was quantified using the Northern Ireland Multiple Deprivation Measure, which rank orders all postcodes in the province across seven domains. Three deprivation categories were derived (low, middle and high), which were used to evaluate sample differences in pain-related functioning.ResultsSignificant between-group differences were indicated across all measures with the lowest deprivation group reporting higher functioning in relation to the other two groups across most measures. The only exceptions were for depression and anxiety which increased significantly across each level of deprivation. A similar pattern was found when examining clinically significant scores, particularly for social functioning, depression and anxiety.ConclusionDeprivation is more than a risk factor for chronic pain. Deprivation is a risk factor for more distressing, disabling and disruptive chronic pain. These findings indicate that redirection of clinical efforts is warranted to allow interventions to more adequately address the complexities of those seeking treatment.

Introduction

Chronic pain is one of the most dominant and costly public health problems in the world.13 While it is highly prevalent, affecting about 25% of adults globally,4 5 its negative impact on quality of life is perhaps its most profoundly concerning characteristic. For a clinically significant number of people with chronic pain, there are substantial life-altering restrictions in independence, productivity, social functioning, mental health and purpose in life.68 Given these wide-ranging impacts, a primary challenge of chronic pain is that it is a fundamentally biopsychosocial problem;911 therefore, the causes and potential solutions require an analysis and consequent treatment approach broader than that offered through mechanistic models based in biological reductionism.

In particular, socio-economic deprivation, defined as material or social disadvantage relative to one’s community, has been identified as a reliable risk factor for the presence of chronic pain12 13 and likely increases clinical complexity. Several large-scale epidemiological studies show unequal societal distribution of chronic pain based on deprivation level.1416 It is less clear whether high deprivation is a risk factor for more negative effects associated with chronic pain. There are, however, some suggestive findings in this regard. For example, some aspects of personal finance, such as low overall wealth and income below government-defined poverty levels, and some aspects of education, such as low literacy or health literacy, are related to greater chronic pain impact.1721 In addition, food insecurity (unstable or absent access to nutritious food) appears to be a risk factor for greater chronic pain impact, while food assistance programmes may offset this risk for some.22 Finally, a statistical modelling study which estimated small area data (eg, neighbourhood) indicated that there were inequities in frequency of disabling chronic pain present based on local deprivation.23 It is therefore plausible that deprivation is more than a risk factor for the presence of chronic pain, it may also be a risk factor for more burdensome and life-restricting chronic pain.

The present study aimed to evaluate this possibility and expand on what is known about chronic pain and deprivation in several ways. First, a multifaceted assessment of emotional, social and functional aspects of chronic pain was performed in a large sample of treatment-seeking individuals and evaluated in relation to low, middle and high neighbourhood deprivation. Second, scoring profiles were evaluated relative to one another to determine if the risk of clinically significant scoring differed across the three deprivation categories. Third, the relative risk (RR) of clinically significant scores in relation to the general population was calculated to evaluate whether there were clear differences in the risk of elevated scores based on neighbourhood deprivation. These issues were evaluated in a large sample of individuals with chronic pain in a series of cross-sectional analyses.

Methods

Participants

Participants were treatment-seeking individuals referred to a specialty chronic pain treatment service located in Belfast, Northern Ireland. The service is located in a large health and social care trust and has a wide catchment population (approximately 130k individuals) in terms of those seeking specialty chronic pain care through the UK’s National Health Service, a publicly funded healthcare system.

Between November 2023 and January 2025, a total of 519 individuals were referred to the service and completed the intake assessment. Of these, 491 (94.6%) provided consent to use their information for research purposes. In terms of demographic characteristics, 63.1% noted female gender and the remaining 36.9% noted male gender. The average age was 50.9 years (SD=14.6), and the majority noted being of white European descent, 94.5%, with 1.3% noting African descent, 0.8% Asian descent, 2.6% mixed or other descent, and 0.8% declining to answer. The most frequently noted relationship status was either married/civil partnership, 36.6%, or single, 35.0%, with 11.0% being divorced, 9.0% separated, 5.1% widowed and 3.3% declining to answer. The average years of education were 13.8 (SD=3.1) and only 22% were employed in part-time or full-time work.

Pain duration averaged 12.1 years (SD=10.4). The most frequent pain location was low back, 84.7%, followed by hip/upper leg, 77.4%, lower leg, 76.6%, neck/upper back, 70.7%, arm/shoulder, 69.7%, abdomen/pelvic, 47.9% and head pain, 38.0%. The average number of pain locations was 4.7 (SD=1.9) out of the seven locations assessed with a minority of individuals, 7.5%, noting a single location.

Measures

Area deprivation

Area deprivation was assessed using the Northern Ireland Multiple Deprivation Measure (NIMDM, https://deprivatin.nisra.gov.uk/)24 which includes all Northern Ireland postcodes in rank order based on deprivation. In the UK, postcodes are small clusters of households, with an average of 18.3 (SD=14.9) per code according to the most recent UK census.25

The NIMDM uses seven domains of deprivation including income, employment, health/disability, education, skills/training, access to services, living environment and crime/disorder. These domains are weighted to provide an overall deprivation ranking, which ranges from 1 to 890 (the number of postcodes in Northern Ireland in 2017). Rankings range from highest to lowest deprivation, therefore, lower numbers are indicative of high deprivation rank/greater deprivation. Details regarding specific definitions of the seven deprivation domains and methods used to determine each domain’s weighting in the calculation of overall deprivation can be found in the NIMDM technical report.24

In order to protect participant confidentiality, deprivation ranking was entered by clinic staff, transformed to percentile (ie, individual rank/890 (the total number of postcodes in 2017)) and then into decile deprivation rank (ie, 1–10), prior to being viewed by any non-clinical research staff. Both participant postcode and NIMDM ranking were also deleted prior to being viewed by non-clinical research staff.

Self-report measures

Following referral to the pain rehabilitation service, individuals were asked to provide information regarding demographic characteristics, pain-related information and complete a battery of standardised self-report questionnaires regarding psychosocial and physical functioning. The majority of patients provided this information using a secure online data collection portal (ie, a QR code was scanned that opened a secure webpage that included all questionnaire information). When patients were unable to provide this information (eg, due to reading difficulties or lack of online access), clinic nurses assisted in questionnaire completion either via telephone or in person and data were entered by clinic staff. The service requires that this information be provided prior to initiation of care, as it provides details that are key to assessment and initial formulation of a treatment plan.

Pain related functioning

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)-29+2 was used to quantify participant functioning. The PROMIS initiative was intended to develop ‘state-of-the-science’ measures to evaluate self-reported functioning across social, mental and behavioural health domains.26 The PROMIS-29+2 is a composite of several PROMIS measures that assess domains specifically relevant to chronic pain,27 including Pain Interference, Social Functioning, Depression, Anxiety, Physical Function, Sleep Disturbance, Cognitive Functioning, Fatigue Intensity and Pain Intensity. Most of these domains are assessed using four item scales, with the exception of Cognitive Functioning, which included two items, and Pain Intensity, which included a single item assessing usual pain in the past week on a 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain) scale. Except for the pain intensity item, all PROMIS items are responded to on a 1–5 scale with anchor descriptors differing across each domain. Raw scores are converted to t-scores (mean: 50, SD: 10). Higher scores indicate greater symptoms or burden for most domains, including Pain Interference, Depression, Anxiety, Sleep Disturbance, Fatigue Intensity and Pain Intensity. For the remaining subscales, lower scores indicate worse functioning, including Social Functioning, Physical Function and Cognitive Functioning.

The PROMIS domain scores have been widely used and have strong psychometric support.2629 In the present data, internal consistency across all multi-item PROMIS domain scores was acceptable, Cronbach’s α range: 0.76 (Sleep Disturbance) to 0.96 (Pain Interference and Social Functioning).

Analytical approach

Initially, data distribution patterns were evaluated with regard to skewness and kurtosis for both PROMIS domains and deprivation percentile. In preparation for multivariate analyses, normality and homogeneity of the covariance matrices were evaluated for PROMIS domain scores. We also inspected score distributions to identify those with outlying data. Regarding the NIMDM, frequency distributions were calculated within each decile to enable a descriptive comparison with the Northern Ireland population. We then divided the deprivation decile clusters into three groups (ie, high, medium, low deprivation) as evenly as possible within the sample to enable between-group comparisons.

Next, a series of analyses were conducted to evaluate differences in the PROMIS domain scores across the three deprivation groups. We first examined between group differences via a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). It was planned that a significant omnibus effect would be followed by univariate analyses of variance (ANOVA) for individual domain scores and, following significant ANOVA results, pairwise comparisons between the three groups would be performed using the Holm procedure to control for multiple comparisons.30

Subsequent analyses focused on the fact that our measure of deprivation was based on the population of Northern Ireland and that PROMIS domain scores were standardised to t-scores based on population norms. Thus, while conducting the MANOVA allowed us to evaluate differences in pain-related functioning relative to deprivation level within this sample of people with chronic pain, there was also the opportunity to evaluate scoring profiles of the three deprivation groups relative to the general population.

In order to facilitate these analyses, PROMIS domain t-scores were categorised based on the number of SD units away from the mean in the direction of greater severity (eg, 0 SD indicated average or better functioning; 1 SD indicated a score 1 SD in the direction of worse functioning; 2 SD indicated a score 2 SDs in the direction of worse functioning). Using these SD categorisations, the second set of analyses included a series of Fisher’s exact tests to determine whether there were proportional differences in risk of a clinically significant score between the three deprivation groups. Initially, an omnibus test was performed across the three groups and, when significant, followed by pairwise tests.

The final set of analyses involved the calculation of two sets of RR ratios including the RR of having a PROMIS domain score that was: (1) 1 or more SD above the mean and (2) 2 or more SD above the mean. These analyses used the lowest deprivation group as the reference.

Data management and the calculation of descriptive statistics were done using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences.31 All other analyses used RStudio (V.4.4.3).32

Results

Preliminary analyses

Regarding data integrity, there was no evidence of problematic skewness or kurtosis in the PROMIS domain scores, absolute values for skewness all <0.98; absolute values for kurtosis all <2.1. The distribution for deprivation percentile also did not appear to be problematic, skewness=0.31 (SE=0.11) and kurtosis=−1.5 (SD=0.22). See Kline33 and Bryne34 for extended discussions of evaluating data distributions.

For most measures, there were no missing data. The clinic’s data collection system is online, emphasises the importance of complete information which is used for clinical triage decisions and alerts patients to missing responses to request item completion. The only exception was for deprivation as nine individuals lived in a postcode that had no NIMDM ranking. For these individuals, it appeared that they lived in postcodes that had been created after 2017 (eg, for a new housing development) when ratings for the NINDM were assigned. Thus, data from 482 (98.2%) individuals were used for all remaining analyses.

Multivariate normality was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk’s test and homogeneity of covariances with the Box’s M test. Both were significant, Shapiro-Wilk’s=0.87, p<0.001, Box’s M=93.2, p<0.05; therefore, we used Pillai’s Trace for the multivariate test statistic in MANOVA and Welch’s one-way ANOVA for univariate tests as both yield robust results when there is evidence of non-normality and covariance heterogeneity.35 36

We also evaluated demographic and pain-related characteristics between the three deprivation groups via ANOVA for continuous variables (ie, age, years of education, pain duration) and χ² analyses for the categorical variable of gender. The only significant difference indicated was for age, F (2, 481) = 12.9, p<0.001. Pairwise comparisons indicated that the highest deprivation group was younger than the other two groups, who did not differ from one another. The highest deprivation group averaged 47.2 (SD=13.3) years of age, the middle deprivation group averaged 52.4 (SD=14.1) years of age and the lowest deprivation group averaged 54.7 (SD=15.6) years of age.

Deprivation group comparisons

Table 1 displays descriptive information regarding the PROMIS for each of the three deprivation groups. The MANOVA examining between group differences across the nine PROMIS domain scores indicated a significant omnibus effect, Pillai’s=0.12, F (18, 944) = 3.4, p<0.001. Follow-up Welch’s ANOVA were significant for all nine PROMIS domain scores, with results ranging from F=3.5, p<0.05 for Sleep Disruption to F=16.0, p<0.001 for Anxiety.

The results of the pairwise comparisons are indicated in table 1 with different superscripts indicating statistically significant differences using Holm-controlled alpha. The pattern of findings generally indicated individuals in the lowest deprivation group had significantly lower PROMIS domain scores, indicating greater functioning, in comparison to the other two groups across almost all measures. The middle and highest deprivation groups did not differ from one another on the majority of measures, with the exception of Depression, Anxiety and Cognitive Functioning domain scores. Depression and Anxiety scores were significantly higher, and Cognitive Functioning scores were significantly lower in the highest deprivation group, as compared with the middle deprivation group. Additionally, for Sleep Disturbance, the highest deprivation group had significantly higher Sleep Disturbance than the lowest deprivation group, but neither group was different from the middle deprivation group.

The results of the sensitivity analysis, which excluded data from individuals identified as multivariate outliers, were identical to MANOVA using the full sample. Specifically, there was a significant omnibus effect, Pillai’s=0.12, F (18, 922) = 3.2, p<0.001, significant Welch’s ANOVA results for all measures, F=4.0, p<0.05 for Sleep Disruption to F=14.8, p<0.001 for Social Role Functioning and an identical pattern of pairwise differences.

Proportional differences in clinically significant scores

As noted, individual PROMIS domain scores were categorised in relation to the number of SDs in the direction of greater severity and Fisher’s exact tests were conducted to evaluate whether there were significant differences in the proportions of each severity group among the three levels of deprivation. For most PROMIS domains, scores fell between 0 and 2 SDs of the mean. The only exceptions were for anxiety and pain intensity, where scores fell between 0 and 3 SDs of the mean.

Significant differences were indicated across all but one Fisher’s exact test, range p<0.05 for Sleep Disturbance and Pain Intensity to p<0.005 for all other significant findings. The only non-significant difference was indicated for Cognitive Functioning, p=0.08. Figure 2 displays the proportional differences across the three deprivation groups with significant pairwise differences indicated with different superscripts. Overall, the proportional differences between the groups were driven by the fact that more clinically significant scores, including scores 1 SD of greater severity and especially scores 2 SD of greater severity, were greater in the middle and highest deprivation groups in comparison to the lowest deprivation group.

Proportional differences among deprivation groups in relation to SD in the direction of severity. Different superscripts indicate significant pairwise differences.

Specifically, for three of eight comparisons, Social Functioning, Depression and Anxiety, there were significant proportional differences between all three groups with a greater proportion of clinically significant scores (ie, 1 or 2 SDs) as deprivation increased. For an additional three of eight comparisons, Pain Interference, Physical Functioning and Fatigue Intensity, the highest deprivation group significantly differed from the other two groups, who did not differ from one another. There were no pairwise differences for Sleep Disruption. For Pain Intensity, the highest deprivation group differed significantly from the lowest deprivation group and neither of these groups differed from the middle deprivation group.

Relative risk of clinically significant scores

The final set of analyses categorised each individual’s score as being 1 or more SDs above the mean and 2 or more SDs above the mean, and RR ratios were calculated using the lowest deprivation group as the reference. Figure 3 contains details regarding these analyses, including 95% CIs. Those in the highest deprivation group were at elevated risk in eight out of the nine PROMIS domain scores. The sole exception to this pattern was for Cognitive Functioning, for which there were no differences indicated. The RR 95% CI ranged from 8% to 290% across domains. Average increase in risk for having a score 2 SDs above the mean was 162% (SD=29%) in the highest deprivation group. The most substantial differences for both 1 SD or more and 2 SD or more categories were for the measures of mental health, Depression and Anxiety, for which there were stepwise increases in risk across the deprivation groups in almost every case. There were also noteworthy increases in RR for Pain Interference for both the middle and highest deprivation groups.

Discussion

The current study shows marked differences in the burden of chronic pain based on geographical area characteristics, such as material resources, social circumstances and health profiles. Across almost all domains measured here, higher deprivation was associated with more severe symptoms and disruption of functioning in comparison to those living in areas with the lowest deprivation. These differences were observed both within people who have chronic pain and between those with chronic pain and the general adult population, on whom the measures used here were normed. Furthermore, it is striking that almost half of this sample lived in the most deprived areas of the service’s catchment area. Thus, a primary implication of these data is that those living in the highest deprivation are most at risk of having substantial negative impacts of chronic pain—as with some other health conditions, living in deprivation seems to put one in double jeopardy,39 where both risk of having chronic pain and risk of having significantly burdensome chronic pain is increased. Deprivation combined with likely medical comorbidities among these patients may result in further compounding of risk of harm.

Deprivation is a complex phenomenon that has inherent limits in terms of its interpretation and generalisability. For example, the NIMDM is a measure of relative deprivation ranking across the postcodes in Northern Ireland, thus, it does not assess absolute deprivation in a manner allowing direct comparisons with other regions in terms of deprivation severity or ranking. Furthermore, there are few direct comparisons of deprivation between the four nations of the UK, nor of the UK as a whole in comparison to other countries. It is possible, therefore, that these findings are specific to our data and the region within which they were collected. Studies that use similar methods as the ones used here are required to adequately evaluate the generalisability of these findings. That being said, there are patterns within the broader literature in this area that are comparable to these findings, including high rates of deprivation in chronic pain samples and evidence that some aspects of deprivation, such as financial and educational resources, are related to greater pain-related distress and interference.40 41 To our knowledge, this study is unique in its use of a comprehensive measure of pain-related functioning and a multifaceted measure of deprivation across an entire region.

There are additional limitations to consider. These data were collected within a publicly funded healthcare service where treatment has no fee at the point of care. We do not know how these results apply to those receiving private healthcare for chronic pain, nor whether there are more individuals in the lowest deprivation group receiving such care. For example, it is possible that the distribution of deprivation may differ when data are collected from those seen in both private health insurance clinics and public health services. We did not collect data regarding private health insurance, therefore, we do not know how well these findings represent those who pay for private healthcare. Finally, these were cross-sectional data, thus causal or directional conclusions are not possible, and all pain-related burden information was assessed via self-report.

For healthcare providers, the strong influence and significant complexity of deprivation and related social determinants of health can be disheartening and overwhelming. It can feel as if these factors lie far outside our sphere of influence. For example, addressing much of the problematic aspects of deprivation likely requires widespread economic and healthcare system change, as well as significant investment in community and social infrastructure.42 43 With regard to deprivation and chronic pain specifically, there are some areas where healthcare providers can help to mitigate the challenges and complexities in those living in significant deprivation. First, given that chronic pain is a problem where psychosocial components, not biological, are the primary determinants of chronic pain burden,44 it is likely time for clinical practice to pivot away from mechanistic and structural explanations for chronic pain, given their longstanding history of weak effect4549 and towards those that more adequately capture pain’s complexity, specifically biopsychosocial explanations. Second, data in the field have consistently indicated that wise investment of community and healthcare resources into adequate psychosocial interventions for chronic pain improves quality of life, incidence of illness and saves healthcare money. Specifically, clinical investment in psychosocial interventions that are adequate to the problem of clinically significant levels of pain interference, disruption in social role functioning, depression, anxiety and physical impairment, to reiterate the strongest problem areas in these data, are likely to pay health and financial dividends.5052 Third, education that normalises the contribution of psychosocial factors in chronic pain and overall health, including their relation to deprivation, can be empowering and effective, at least over the short term.5356 While it seems plausible that many people with chronic pain require more than education alone, it is a feasible starting point that can be actioned in community and primary care.56 57 Barriers to care can likely be reduced, with the utilisation of technology, such as telehealth appointments and access to web-based resources.5860 Finally, the provision of care can effectively and efficaciously prioritise patient engagement in actions that improve quality of life and facilitate valued actions, not symptom reduction alone.61 62
