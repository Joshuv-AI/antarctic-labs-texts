Taurochenodeoxycholic acid ameliorates liver fibrosis by regulating gut microbiota and bile acids via hippo-YAP pathway
Authors: Lisheng Zhang, Yujing Li, Junyi Zhang, Ming Zhang, Wei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCholestatic liver disease is characterized by gut microbiota dysbiosis and excessive toxic hepatic bile acid accumulation. Regulation of gut microbiota and suppression of bile acid synthesis are potential strategies for the treatment of cholestatic liver disease. Fetal liver bile acids demonstrate cytoprotective properties during hepatic development, prompting investigation into their therapeutic potential in extrahepatic biliary atresia (EHBA) rats. Comparing bile acid profiles, taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA) most effectively reduced hepatic bile acids and fibrosis, while altering gut microbiota and increasing Lactobacillus abundance. Additionally, TCDCA treatment significantly decreased liver Yes-associated protein (YAP) expression, thus suppressing ductular reaction during cholestatic liver fibrosis. These findings highlight that TCDCA might be a promising functional bile acid for treating liver fibrosis by modulating gut microbiota and the Hippo-YAP pathway.Subject areas: pharmacology, molecular physiology, microbiology

Introduction

Bile acids (BAs) are synthesized in the hepatocytes and then entered the intestine. The gut microbiota-BA interaction plays a key role in regulating cholesterol and BA homeostasis.1 Primary BAs are synthesized from cholesterol in the liver, conjugated with either glycine or taurine and further metabolized by the gut microbiota into secondary BAs.2 The first step in secondary BA metabolism is the hydrolysis of the amino acid moiety by bile salt hydrolase (BSH). Therefore, BSH activity is required for the formation of the BA pool and the maintenance of its multiple functions, and BSH activity might be a critical mechanism by which many intestinal bacteria form the gastrointestinal tract niche where they inhabit. There is growing evidence that gut dysbiosis exacerbates liver disease via the gut-liver axis.3,4,5 BAs, especially conjugated BAs, can reshape the gut microbiota composition through direct modulation of the bile-sensitive and bile-metabolizing bacteria.6 Hepatic accumulation of BAs is central to the pathogenesis of cholestasis-induced liver injury, and an excess of cytotoxic BAs in the liver can lead to liver fibrosis and cirrhosis and even liver carcinogenesis.7 Therefore, modification of gut microbiota is a potential strategy for the treatment of liver diseases caused by excessive BA accumulation.

So far, there has been no effective therapy to inhibit liver fibrosis. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), as a BA, has been reported to increase the survival of patients with advanced primary biliary cirrhosis, but as many as 40% of patients do not respond satisfactorily to UDCA therapy, and adjunctive therapies are needed.8 A number of studies have indicated that high dosage of UDCA can alleviate liver inflammation, probably by modifying the composition of gut microbiota and serum BA profiles in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis mice.9,10 In addition, the effects of UDCA on the treatment of hepatic fibrosis via the gut microbiota, especially on BSH activity, have rarely been reported.

In recent years, BAs have been identified as upstream regulator of the Hippo pathway.11 This pathway and its target Yes-associated protein (YAP) have been recently identified as crucial drivers for bile duct proliferation after cholestatic injury.12,13,14 The ectopic activation of YAP can convert mature hepatocytes into the cells with hepatic progenitor properties in the adult liver15 and promote the progression to liver fibrosis or cholangiocarcinoma.16,17 Thus, the inhibition of abnormal biliary epithelial cell (BEC) activation and proliferation by suppressing YAP activity may partially or even completely reverse biliary fibrosis.

In this study, we selected several high-level BAs in the fetal liver (FL) and found that taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA) treatment increased the BSH-related microbial abundance and BSH activity, thus enhancing BA excretion. Our data also demonstrated that TCDCA treatment inhibited liver YAP signaling, thereby decreasing ductular reaction, eventually preventing liver fibrosis in rats with extrahepatic biliary atresia (EHBA).

This study unravels a mechanistic link between TCDCA and changes in the gut microbiota, YAP signaling, or BA level during alleviating liver fibrosis.

Results

TCDCA alleviates EHBA-induced liver fibrosis and liver injury in rats

We hypothesized that high concentrations of BAs (TCDCA) might have protective effects on EHBA-induced cholestatic liver disease (Figures S1A and S1B). To verify this hypothesis, we supplemented rats with BAs daily starting from the 7th day after EHBA surgery for 6 weeks. First, we measured serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels to assess the degree of liver injury. The results showed that ALT levels were significantly elevated in the EHBA group compared to the Sham group (rats without EHBA surgery), indicating increased liver damage. However, the serum ALT levels in the TCDCA-treated EHBA group were significantly reduced (Figure 1A), indicating that TCDCA effectively alleviated EHBA-induced liver injury. Then, we assessed the content of hydroxyproline (HYP, a unique amino acid in collagen) in the liver to reflect the degree of liver fibrosis. The results showed that liver HYP levels were significantly elevated in EHBA rats, suggesting the occurrence of fibrosis. In contrast, TCDCA treatment significantly reduced HYP levels (Figure 1B). These findings suggest that TCDCA plays a crucial role in preventing the progression of EHBA-related liver injury.

Liver histology analysis also showed significant differences. Compared to the Sham group, the livers of EHBA group rats exhibited obvious necrosis, severe diffuse ductal proliferation, and portal vein edema. However, these pathological changes were significantly improved in the livers of TCDCA-treated rats (Figures 1C and 1D). Additionally, Sirius Red staining and Masson’s trichrome staining, along with corresponding positive area statistical analysis, showed significant fibrosis in EHBA rats, but the degree of fibrosis was significantly reduced in the TCDCA-treated group (Figures 1C and 1D). These results further support the inhibitory effect of TCDCA on EHBA-induced liver fibrosis.

To explore the molecular mechanism by which TCDCA alleviates liver fibrosis, we examined the mRNA and protein expression levels of liver fibrosis markers α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β). The results showed that the expression of these genes was significantly elevated in the EHBA group, whereas TCDCA treatment significantly decreased the expression levels of α-SMA and TGF-β (Figures 1E–1G). This further confirms that TCDCA can alleviate liver fibrosis by inhibiting the expression of these fibrosis-related genes. In short, these results indicate that TCDCA exhibits significant therapeutic effects in alleviating liver injury and inhibiting the expression of fibrosis markers.

TCDCA restores BA balance and metabolism in EHBA-induced cholestasis

To explore the mechanisms through which TCDCA alleviates EHBA-induced liver fibrosis, we analyzed the BA ratios in feces to investigate the enzymatic activity changes in the gut. The TCDCA/CDCA and TDCA/DCA ratios were compared to determine whether the BA imbalance observed in EHBA rats was linked to differences in taurine dissociation enzyme activity. In the EHBA group, the TCDCA/CDCA ratio, along with the TCDCA/α-muricholic acid (αMCA) and TCDCA/β-muricholic acid (βMCA) ratios, was significantly reduced. However, TCDCA treatment effectively restored these ratios (Figures 2A–2C), suggesting that TCDCA can reverse the BA dysregulation induced by EHBA. Conversely, the TDCA/DCA ratio showed an opposite trend (Figures 2A–2C), further supporting the hypothesis that taurine dissociation is involved in the altered BA metabolism seen in EHBA.

To further understand the shift in BA synthesis pathways, we calculated the CA/CDCA ratio to analyze whether EHBA caused a shift from the primary to alternative BA synthesis pathways. The results showed that the CA/αMCA and CA/βMCA ratios were significantly lower in the EHBA group, while TCDCA treatment significantly increased these ratios (Figures 2D–2F). Moreover, the absolute concentrations of fecal BAs showed consistent directional changes with the alterations in BA ratios (Figures S2A–S2F).

TCDCA modulates gut microbiota structure

We investigated the effect of TCDCA on the structure of gut microbiota. Using principal-component analysis (PCA), principal co-ordinate analysis (PCOA), and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on weighted UniFrac and Jaccard distances, we observed that the gut microbiota composition of the TCDCA-treated group closely resembled that of the Sham group, while showing significant divergence from the EHBA group (Figures 3A–3C). PCOA results revealed a notable separation along the PC1 axis, which explained 38.6% of the total variation, and the PC2 axis, accounting for 20.2% of the variation (Figures 3A–3C). Analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) confirmed significant differences in bacterial composition between the groups (Figure 3D), indicating that EHBA-induced gut dysbiosis was mitigated by TCDCA treatment.

To further compare the gut microbiota between the groups, we utilized linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), which revealed significant differences in microbial composition across six phylogenetic levels, from kingdom to genus (Figure 3E). Several biomarker taxa with an Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score greater than 2 were identified (Figure 3F). Most notably, the abundance of Lactobacillus, a genus known for its BSH activity that deconjugates glycine- or taurine-conjugated BAs, was significantly increased in the TCDCA group (Figures 3G and S3A). In summary, TCDCA treatment effectively reshaped the gut microbiota toward a more favorable composition, particularly by increasing BSH-producing bacteria.

TCDCA enhances BA excretion and reduces serum BA levels

Metagenomic analysis further demonstrated that TCDCA treatment significantly upregulated the expression of BSH-related proteins (Figure 4A). The increase in BSH activity promoted the deconjugation of BAs, thereby enhancing the excretion of BAs into feces. This was reflected in a significant increase in fecal BA excretion in the TCDCA-treated group, along with a corresponding reduction in serum BA levels (Figures 4B–4D). Additionally, deconjugated BAs, such as lithocholic acid (LCA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA), which are known for their cytotoxic properties, were significantly elevated in the feces of TCDCA-treated rats without normalization to fecal TBA. With normalization, DCA levels remained significantly higher in the TCDCA group, while LCA levels were comparable to those in the control group (Figures 4E and 4F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that TCDCA treatment not only modulates the gut microbiota but also enhances BA metabolism, promoting the excretion of BAs and reducing their systemic accumulation.

TCDCA suppresses hepatic stellate cell activation through the hepatic Hippo-YAP signaling pathway

To explore the molecular mechanism by which TCDCA alleviates EHBA-induced liver fibrosis, we analyzed the expression of genes related to the Hippo-YAP signaling pathway. Liver RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis revealed that (Figures S3B–S3F), compared to the Sham group, the expression levels of Hippo pathway genes were significantly reduced in the EHBA group, while the expression of downstream target genes of YAP was significantly increased. However, TCDCA treatment led to a marked recovery of the Hippo-YAP signaling pathway in the EHBA group (Figure 5A). To further validate these gene changes, we performed qPCR analysis. The results showed that the mRNA levels of YAP and its downstream target genes were significantly elevated in the EHBA group, whereas their expression was significantly reduced in both the TCDCA-treated group and the Sham group (Figure 5B). Western blotting results corroborated these findings, confirming the inhibitory effect of TCDCA on YAP and its downstream target genes (Figure 5C). These results suggest that TCDCA inhibits EHBA-induced YAP activation by activating the Hippo pathway.

Additionally, MST1, a key component of the Hippo pathway, is a protein kinase that controls cell proliferation by inhibiting YAP. Our data indicated that the expression level of MST1 in the EHBA group was significantly lower than that in both the TCDCA-treated and Sham groups, and the expression of its active form (phosphorylated Mst1, pMst1) was also significantly decreased in the EHBA group (Figures 5B and 5C). TCDCA treatment significantly restored the expression of MST1 and pMst1, suggesting that TCDCA can inhibit YAP by activating the Hippo pathway.

Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed the localization of YAP and α-SMA (a marker of hepatic stellate cell [HSC]) in liver tissues. In the EHBA group, strong YAP and α-SMA staining was observed mainly in the bile duct cells around the portal vein, while YAP expression was significantly weakened in the TCDCA-treated and Sham groups (Figure 5D). Immunofluorescence staining also showed a significant increase in the co-staining of YAP’s downstream target gene connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) and α-SMA in the EHBA group (Figure 5E). Taken together, TCDCA treatment significantly inhibited YAP activation, thereby reducing EHBA-induced HSC activation and alleviating liver fibrosis.

TCDCA alleviates liver fibrosis by suppressing ductular reaction through a YAP-dependent pathway

To explore how TCDCA alleviates liver fibrosis via the YAP signaling pathway, we analyzed the proliferation of BECs and the transdifferentiation of hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs) induced by EHBA. Co-immunostaining revealed that, in the EHBA group, proliferating BECs showed almost complete overlap of EpCAM and CK19, as well as Sox9 and CK19, indicating that these cells exhibited characteristics of proliferating BECs. In contrast, no co-staining of EpCAM and Sox9 was observed in hepatocytes marked by HNF4α, suggesting these markers were not expressed in hepatocytes (Figure 6A). However, TCDCA treatment significantly reduced the expression levels of Sox9 and EpCAM, which correlated with a marked reduction in liver fibrosis compared to the EHBA group (Figure 6A). Further immunoblotting analysis confirmed these changes. Compared with the EHBA group, the mRNA levels of CK19, Sox9, and EpCAM gradually decreased in the TCDCA-treated group (Figure 6B), with similar trends observed at the protein level (Figure 6C).

Functional maturity of cholangiocytes and YAP inhibition by TCDCA

To further investigate the functional maturity of cholangiocytes derived from hepatic stem cells, we examined the expression of the bile duct functional marker somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2). Mature cholangiocytes are known to express SSTR2. However, in the EHBA group, SSTR2 expression was extremely low, indicating that the newly formed bile ducts were functionally immature (Figure 6D). Immunoblotting and qPCR results also confirmed significantly reduced SSTR2 protein and mRNA expression in the EHBA group (Figures 6C and 6D). TCDCA treatment notably restored SSTR2 expression levels, suggesting that TCDCA promoted the functional maturation of cholangiocytes by inhibiting YAP signaling, thus reducing the progression of liver fibrosis. Additionally, immunohistochemical co-staining demonstrated that, in the EHBA group, there was an expansion of genistein (GS)-positive areas, with multiple layers of hepatocytes surrounding the central vein exhibiting aberrant GS expression. This finding indicates that EHBA activation of YAP disrupted the zonal characteristics of hepatocytes, promoting their transdifferentiation into cholangiocytes. However, in the TCDCA-treated group, GS expression was restored to its normal zonation pattern, indicating that hepatocyte function had recovered (Figure 6E). In conclusion, these results suggest that TCDCA attenuates the progression of EHBA-induced hepatic fibrosis by inhibiting YAP signaling, promoting the maturation of cholangiocyte function, and restoring the normal zonation characteristics of hepatocytes.

Discussion

Toxic BA accumulation in liver is mainly responsible for cholestatic liver disease, and it is associated with liver fibrosis. Reducing hepatic BA overload is a therapeutic strategy for BA-associated liver fibrosis.7 Current cholestasis treatments include the application of several BAs and their derivatives. UDCA works by inhibiting intestinal BA absorption and eliminating toxic BA from hepatocytes.18 Obeticholic acid (OCA) and bezafibrate have been applied for patient intolerant or poorly responsive to UDCA therapy.19 One previous study has indicated the therapeutic function of microbiota, and the BSH secreted by microbiota has been used for treating cholestatic liver disease in human and animal models.20 However, the mechanisms by which BA improves liver injury and fibrosis through microbiota have not been fully studied.

In the current study, we evaluated the effectiveness and the potential mechanisms of BA TCDCA in the treatment of EHBA-induced liver injury/fibrosis. We found that TCDCA could promote BSH to deconjugate BAs, thus increasing BA excretion and rebalancing liver Hippo-YAP signaling pathway, eventually inhibiting ductular reaction caused by the differentiation of HPCs.

BSH is secreted by gut microbes, and BSH can protect some bacterial species through BA deconjugation.21 Gut commensal microbe Lactobacillus has been reported to have BSH activity.22,23 At the genus level, the proportion of Lactobacillus was decreased in the EHBA group. Importantly, TCDCA treatment significantly decreased LCA and DCA levels in feces, and these two BAs massively deposit during cholestasis and exhibit high cytotoxicity.24,25,26 Since deconjugated BAs are less hydrophilic and less likely to be reabsorbed, the deconjugation increases BA fecal excretion.27 These findings suggest that TCDCA may enhance BSH production and restore BA homeostasis by modulating Lactobacillus populations. While the observed correlation between BSH gene abundance and functional activity strongly supports this mechanism, future studies incorporating direct enzymatic assays would provide more definitive evidence to establish this causal relationship.

Normal physiological or modestly elevated concentrations of BAs do not result in YAP activation, but chronically elevated concentrations of BAs tend to cause cholestatic pathological change.28 We analyzed the expression of YAP in the liver and found that the liver expression of YAP was upregulated after EHBA, but it was downregulated after TCDCA treatment. Consistently, serum BA concentration was increased in the EHBA group, compared to that in the Sham group and TCDCA group. These results indicated that YAP was widely expressed in the liver and was greatly affected by enterohepatic BA homeostasis. Furthermore, YAP has been reported to be important for bile duct and hepatocyte proliferation, and Yap can inhibit the differentiation of HPCs into cholangiocytes.29 The pathophysiology of EHBA involves impaired BA transport mediated by key regulators including bile salt export pump (BSEP) and farnesoid X receptor (FXR),30,31 where FXR dysfunction as the master transcriptional controller of BA metabolism exacerbates liver injury, evidenced by FXR−/− mouse models showing toxic BA-induced sterile inflammation and fibrosis.32 Although our study’s focus on Hippo signaling precluded direct assessment of BA regulators (FXR and BSEP), our phenotypic observations concord with established mechanisms, while the known FXR-BA crosstalk suggests potential TCDCA-FXR synergism meriting future investigation.

Hepatic fibrosis induced by EHBA is unique in that the primary pathological lesions occur in the area surrounding the bile duct epithelium. This pathological change is referred to as bile ductular reaction.33 The increasing evidence has shown the overexpression of SOX9, EpCAM, and CK19 during EHBA-induced liver fibrosis and the improvement of liver fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma by the inhibition of YAP.34,35,36 To further explore the relationship between ductular reaction and YAP, the expressions of SOX9, EpCAM, and CK19 proteins in liver were detected. The results showed that the expressions of all three proteins were decreased in the TCDCA group, relative to the EHBA group. Based on these findings, we speculated that, in EHBA rats, significant increase in the expressions of SOX9, EpCAM, and CK19 might result in the metaplasia of HPCs into cholangiocytes. Our results showed that TCDCA treatment specifically downregulated the YAP expression in rats, thus alleviating the EHBA-induced liver fibrosis. Recently, it has been shown that the upregulation of YAP expression in hepatocellular carcinoma patients overrides the zonation program in pericentral hepatocytes.15 TCDCA intervention rebalanced the expression of HNF4α and restored zonal GS expression, indicating the restoration of differentiation and function of hepatocytes.

The present study indicates that TCDCA treatment increases the abundance of BSH-secreting gut bacteria, thus promoting fecal BA excretion, ultimately alleviating EHBA-induced liver injury and fibrosis. In addition, TCDCA inhibits YAP activation, thus suppressing ductular reaction during liver fibrosis. Our findings suggest that TCDCA may be used for the treatment of liver disease associated with high-level hepatic BA through YAP signaling pathway and gut microbes.

Limitations of the study

Our findings are on TCDCA’s modulation of Hippo signaling and gut microbiota. While we observed enrichment of BSH-containing Lactobacillus strains and associated BA profile changes, direct measurement of fecal BSH enzyme activity was omitted, leaving the microbial-metabolic link incompletely characterized. Additionally, the biologically plausible involvement of FXR signaling was not experimentally addressed due to our primary focus on the Hippo pathway. These limitations point to needed investigations into the broader network of microbial and signaling interactions in liver fibrosis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal studies

Male SD rat (6-8 weeks old) were purchased from Huazhong Agricultural University Experimental Animal Center (Wuhan, Hubei, China). These rats were housed in a standard 12-hour light/dark cycle with free access to diet and water. Hepatic fibrosis was induced by extrahepatic biliary atresia (EHBA). These rats were given gavage of TCDCA (15 mg/kg) once a day for 6 weeks (The dose of TCDCA (15 mg/kg) was selected based on pilot experiments identifying it as the minimal effective dose required to suppress liver fibrosis without adverse effects).

All procedures were carried out in accordance with the principles of Declaration of Helsinki. All animal protocols conformed to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals prepared and approved by the animal ethics and welfare committee of Huazhong Agricultural University, with the corresponding animal ethical approval reference number provided as HZAURA-2022-0034.

Method details

EHBA procedures

EHBA Rats were anesthetized with diethyl ether and maintained with an intraperitoneal injection of 10 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium. A midline laparotomy was performed, and using microsurgical techniques, a ductotomy was made in the common bile duct above the pancreatic duct junction. A silicon catheter was inserted and secured with 8-0 nylon, leaving the biliary branches to the caudate and right lateral lobes intact. The common bile duct was ligated distal to the cannulation site. Ethanol was injected into the catheter for 30 seconds, followed by a 5-minute clamping period before release. Postoperatively, animals were given free access to food and water.

Liver histopathological examination analysis

For the histologic assessment, the livers were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 24 h and embedded in paraffin. In all experimental groups, the 5-μm-thick sections of the formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded livers were processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson and Sirius Red staining to estimate the degree of hepatic fibrosis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was extracted using RNAiso plus (TaKaRa, Japan), and the cDNA synthesis was conducted using a cDNA synthesis kit (TOYOBO, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR GREEN qPCR mix (monad, China). The data were detected by using the ABI CFX Connect TM Real-Time PCR Detection System (ABI, USA). The ΔΔCT method was calculated to obtain the fold expression levels. The primer pairs for quantitative real-time PCR were listed in Table S1.

Western blotting

Liver tissues and cells were lysed with lysis buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) to obtain whole-protein extracts. The protein samples (20 mg) were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE and then were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After being blocked with 5% skimmed milk in Tris-buffered saline/Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and then incubated with the respective HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h. Finally, the membranes were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (Advansta, USA).

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

For immunofluorescence analysis, the livers from Sham, EHBA and TCDCA rats were fixed in 4% formaldehyde-PBS solution, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm, and paraffin sections were then prepared and stained with antibodies. After being counterstained with DAPI, the slides were observed under a fluorescent microscope.

For immunohistochemistry analysis, the livers from Sham, EHBA and TCDCA rats were fixed in 4% formaldehyde-PBS solution, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm, and paraffin sections were then prepared and stained with antibodies. After being counterstained with hematoxylin, the slides were observed under a microscope.

Software-intensity measurement

Image Pro Plus (Image Pro Plus v.7: Media Cybernetics; Bethesda, MD), as an analysis program, was used to analyze and quantify data from photomicrographs. In this study, the analyses were performed as follows: Integrated Optical Density (IOD) Image Pro Plus was used to quantify the intensity of probes binding to the structures. We used the confocal series to calculate the total binding intensity of the probes (IOD-intensity value).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad). Data are expressed as mean +/- SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-tailed Student's t-test. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using ordinary one-way ANOVA with the Dunnett's multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was presented at the level of ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
