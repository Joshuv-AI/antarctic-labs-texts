Translation, cross-cultural adaptation and preliminary psychometric evaluation of the Victoria Respiratory Congestion Scale into Spanish
Authors: Jaume Canal-Sotelo, Antonio Martin-Marco, David Bottaro-Parra, Rosa Blasi-Martínez, Lina Nitola-Mendoza, Adrià Oller-Bonache, Carla Camí, Eva Barallat-Gimeno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeath rattles are a frequent clinical sign in patients in an end-of-life situation, despite the lack of consensus on how to address them. The Victoria Respiratory Congestion Scale (VRCS) is an internationally utilized scale, but it has yet to be translated to Spanish. The aim of the present study was to translate and cross-culturally adapt the VRCS into Spanish and to assess selected psychometric properties, namely criterion validity and inter-rater reliability, in patients at the end-of-life.MethodsA systematic translation and cross‑cultural adaptation methodology was applied to ensure semantic, conceptual, and cultural equivalence of the instrument for Spanish‑speaking patients at the end-of-life. A cross-sectional study was conducted between May and September 2024 in home-care patients and patients in the palliative care unit at Hestia Balaguer Hospital and Hospital de la Ribera, to assess the criterion validity and inter-rater reliability of the Spanish version of the VRCS. Criterion validity was evaluated by calculating the correlation between the sound level measurements obtained using a smartphone sound level meter app and the VRCS scores using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Inter-rater reliability was calculated using weighted Cohen’s kappa by comparing the VRCS scores independently assigned by two observers.ResultsThe first outcome of the study was the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the Spanish VRCS (Sp-VRCS). The validity and reliability were assessed in a sample of 47 patients aged between 54 and 98 years old, with 82.98% being men. In addition, 76.60% of them had a primary diagnosis of cancer. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were ρ = 0.758 (maximum dB), ρ = 0.649 (mean dB), and ρ = 0.569 (minimum dB), indicating moderate to acceptable correlations. All correlations were statistically significant (p < 0.01, two-tailed). The inter-rater reliability analysis revealed that the weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient was 1.00, indicating a perfect inter-rater agreement in this sample.ConclusionsThe Sp‑VRCS demonstrated good criterion validity and high inter-rater reliability agreement. The present study provides preliminary psychometric evidence for the Spanish version of the VRCS. The results support its use as a feasible and clinically useful scale for assessing respiratory congestion in palliative care patients at the end-of-life. Further multicentre validation studies addressing additional psychometric properties are recommended before broader implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02209-7.Keywords: Death rattles, End-of-life, Palliative care, Psychometric properties, Smartphone, Validation study, Victoria Respiratory Congestion Scale.

Background

Death rattles are respiratory sounds caused by the presence of secretions in the upper respiratory pathway. They are relatively frequent in patients who are dying or at the end-of-life [1], although their etiology has been studied little. In most cases, they are mainly related to the inability of the patient to eliminate secretions [2, 3], although other possible causes have also been described, such as infections, tumors, or excess fluids [4, 5]. Their frequency of appearance is very variable, with an incidence between 12% and 92% of the patients, and an approximate mean of 35% [4, 6, 7].

Despite the frequency with which death rattles appear, there is a certain controversy about the need for treatment. Diverse strategies have been proposed, both pharmacological and non-pharmacological, but there is neither consensus about what drug to use if opting for pharmacological treatment, nor when the optimum time is to start its use [8, 9]. On the other hand, some studies [10, 11] suggest that drugs could be more effective is they are administered early or as a preventive measure. However, there is a general agreement about the scarce efficacy once the respiratory sounds are present [12–14]. In the case of the pharmacological treatment, antimuscarinic drugs are habitually utilized. According to the systematic review by Lokker et al. [8], the most used drugs are scopolamine, glycopyrronium, hyoscine butylbromide, and atropine. The use of octreotide has also been described, although this drug is not an antimuscarinic, but a somatostatin analogue. As for the non-pharmacological measures, especially with respect to artificial hydration, consensus does not exist either. Some studies [15, 16] have concluded that it is not necessary to limit the intake of liquids to prevent the appearance of death rattles. However, another study [17] points out that a high volume of fluid administration can be associated with a higher risk of developing this symptom. Another important non-pharmacological intervention is communication with families. Many authors [18–20] underline that death rattles can create emotional distress in both family members and the care team. Thus, a recommendation is given to take some time to explain to the patient’s environment that when death rattles appear in an end-of-life situation, and with a low level of consciousness, they normally do not imply suffering for the patient [21–24].

Thus, given the lack of consensus about the most adequate strategies, there is an evident need for the availability of validated tools to clinically assess death rattles [25]. Thus, to objectively assess the presence and intensity of death rattles, diverse scales have been developed. One of the first was the one by Back et al. [13], who used a scale with scores ranging from 0 to 3. Later, the Victoria Hospice Society of Canada adapted it and created the Victoria Respiratory Congestion Scale (VRCS) [26], also using a scale from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates the complete absence of respiratory sounds, and 3 the presence of respiratory sounds that can be heard at the door of the room. This version includes precise instructions to facilitate its use. Although the VRCS has been cited in many studies, mainly in the English language, no versions that have been translated or validated to Spanish have been found.

For this reason, the Victoria Hospice Society was contacted to ask for their permission to perform the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the VRCS to Spanish. Information was provided about the study, and they were asked about the validity and reliability of the scale, verifying that it had never been formally evaluated on these aspects. Thus, the aim of the study was to translate and cross-culturally adapt the VRCS into Spanish, and to assess selected psychometric properties, namely criterion validity and inter-rater reliability, for measuring the intensity of death rattles in patients at the end-of-life situation.

Methods

The methods section provides an overview of the process involved in translating and cross-culturally adapting the VRCS into Spanish, followed by the psychometric evaluation of its criterion validity and inter-rater reliability. The section is organized into the following subsections: Instrument; Process of translation, adaptation, and psychometric evaluation; Ethics; Inclusion and exclusion criteria; Data collection; and Statistical analysis.

Instrument

The VRCS is a clinical tool used to objectively assess the intensity of the death rattles of patients in an end-of-life situation. The scale is based on the distance to which the sound can be heard and allows monitoring the changes in the symptoms and assessing the response to the treatment. The score ranges from 0 to 3: a score of 0 indicates the absence of congestion; 1 audible congestion at 12 inches (30 cm) from the thorax of the patient, but not farther; 2, audible congestion at the foot of the bed; and 3 audible congestions from the door of the room. To apply the scale, the health professionals place themselves at the side of the bed, bending the thorax towards the patient, and listen to various breaths at 30 cm. Then, they move to the foot of the bed, and lastly, to the door of the room, listening at each position. The score is assigned according to the distance at which the congestion is audible. During the assessment, environmental sounds must be minimized, such as radios or televisions, and the measurements must be repeated under similar conditions to guarantee compatibility. In the case of large or shared rooms, the distance must be modified to simulate an individual room [27].

The research team asked the Victoria Hospice Society for permission to translate and culturally adapt the scale to Spanish.

Process of translation, adaptation, and psychometric evaluation

The translation and cross‑cultural adaptation of the VRCS followed the standardized procedure described by Beaton et al. [28] including forward translation, synthesis, back‑translation, expert committee review, pretesting, and submission and appraisal of all written reports by developers/committee.

The translation of the scale and the instructions for its use, from English to Spanish, was carried out by two independent healthcare professionals. The first version created was reviewed by a panel of experts, which included an invited linguist. A harmonization process was necessary to reach consensus on the terms related to ‘congestion’. Two different Spanish expressions emerged: ruidos respiratorios and secreciones. To standardize the terminology, a specialist in internal medicine was invited to join the expert panel, and the term ruidos respiratorios was ultimately adopted. The first Spanish version was then subjected to back-translation into English, again by two independent healthcare professionals who were not the original translators and who were unfamiliar with the original scale. Although the back-translators had no prior knowledge of the original scale, they were physicians and nurses with large experience in palliative care and therefore had a good understanding of the clinical concept being assessed. The version obtained after back-translation (the second version) was evaluated by the expert panel to verify its equivalence to the original, resulting in the third version (Fig. 1).

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation process

Once the entire process outlined by Beaton et al. [28] was followed, a dedicated training session was organized for the nurses responsible for administering the scale at the two participating centers (hereafter referred to as the Sp-VRCS). The Sp-VRCS was then tested with a sample of patients in an end-of-life situation, who were admitted to palliative care at Hestia Balaguer Hospital (Catalonia, Spain) and in the unit of palliative care and home care at Hospital de la Ribera (Valencia, Spain) between May and September 2024.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committees from the University Hospital Arnau de Vilanova located in Lleida, and the Hospital de la Ribera located in Alzira. The guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki were followed. Both patients and their families were given information about the nature of the study and its voluntary character, and the written informed consent form was obtained before the start of data collection.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients older than 18 years of age were included, who, due to their clinical situation, had a prognosis of less than a week of life. To objectively and precisely determine the proximity of death, the presence of various clinical signs of imminent death were assessed: periods of apnea, Cheyne-Stokes breathing, peripheral cyanosis, absence of pulse in the radial artery, breathing with jaw movements, and decreased urine output [29, 30]. This assessment was always performed by the same healthcare professionals of the palliative care units mentioned.

Data collection

The anonymity of the data was guaranteed through an anonymization process, in which each participant was identified through an alphanumeric code related to their clinical history number. To collect the information, a specific form was used, in which the following variables were collected: age, gender, main diagnosis, and presence of comorbidities (high blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, congestive heart failure, dementia, other neurodegenerative diseases, and oncological or hematological disease). In the cases of an oncological diagnosis, the presence and location of the metastasis was also recorded. When the patient presented multiple pathologies, the main diagnosis was the one that was directly related to the cause of death; the remaining ones were included as comorbidities.

Other clinical variables were also retrospectively collected, such as the maximum body temperature 24 h before death, the use of antibiotics, diuretics, and anticholinergic drugs, as well as the volume and the route of fluid administration during this period. The presence of clinical signs of imminent death was determined prospectively.

Once the informed consent was obtained from the patients or the family members, two independent observers (nurses) applied the Sp-VRCS and measured the acoustic intensity of the sounds in decibels (dB), collecting the maximum, minimum, and mean intensities. To guarantee the homogeneity of the measurements, a smartphone with the Sound Meter (version 1.2.0) application was used. The application was administered using an iPhone 11 device for all measurements.

Statistical analysis

Following the procedure described by Tantiwatniyom and Nagaviroj [31], the characteristics of the participants were described through frequencies and percentages for the categorical variables and means and standard deviations for the continuous variables when the distribution was normal. Otherwise, the median with minimum and maximum values (ranges) was reported.

The sample size was calculated following the methodology proposed by Bonett and Wright [32] assuming a normal distribution of the correlation coefficient. Using a statistical power of 80%, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and an expected alternative correlation value (ρ) of 0.4, the estimated required sample size was 45.91 participants.

Criterion validity of the Sp-VRCS was evaluated by examining the correlation between the sound level measured with a sound level meter app and the VRCS scores using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Correlation coefficients were interpreted according to Chan’s classification [33], which defines the following categories: 0.80–0.99, very strong correlation; 0.60–0.79, moderate correlation; 0.30–0.59, fair correlation; 0.10–0.29, very low correlation; and 0, absence of correlation.

The inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s weighted kappa by comparing the VRCS scores assigned by two independent observers. To evaluate inter-rater reliability, the weighted kappa statistic was used. Following the criteria proposed by Landis and Koch [34], kappa values were interpreted as follows: (a) 0.81-1.00, almost perfect agreement; (b) 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; (c) 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; (d) 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; (e) 0.00-0.20, slight agreement; and f) < 0, poor or no agreement.

The statistical analysis was performed with the R software.

Results

Sample characteristics

Following the distribution of the patients by group according to the VRCS classification, as described in the study by Lokker et al. [8], the number of participants for each group was established. The decimal values were rounded to the nearest whole number, obtaining the following distribution: (a) Group 0: 26.45 patients, rounded to 26 patients; (b) Group 1: 5.75 patients, rounded to 6 patients; (c) Group 2: 10.35 patients, rounded to 10 patients, and (d) Group 3: 3.45 patients, rounded to 4 patients. Although the estimated sample size was 46 patients, the modifications derived from the rounding resulted in the final inclusion of 47 participants.

The study included a sample of 47 patients aged between 54 and 98 years old, with 82.98% being men. Table 1 shows the characteristics of study participants. The most frequent main diagnosis was oncological disease with 36 cases (76.60%), with a location in the lungs being the most observed with 10 cases (21.3%). The most frequent main diagnosis among the non-oncological patients were neurodegenerative alterations, with a total of 5 cases. The third diagnosis was septicemia. As for the most prevalent comorbidities in our sample, the most frequent was high blood pressure, followed by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and heart diseases, while the most observed comorbidities in the sample were respiratory and heart diseases (40.42% and 34%, respectively) (Table 2).

Criterion validity of Sp-VRCS

The results obtained showed a moderate correlation between the VRCS score and the maximum (ρ = 0.758) and medium (ρ = 0.649) dBs, while the correlation with the minimum dB (ρ = 0.569) was considered acceptable, according to the classification criteria by Chan [33].

The dispersion diagram (Fig. 2) shows the linear correlation between the Sp-VRCS score and the sound level in dB. The corresponding trend lines are shown: a linear trend line for the maximum level of sound, a quadratic correlation for the medium level in dB, and a cubic correlation for the minimum intensity of the sound. The results obtained were (a) Sp-VRCS vs. minimum intensity in dB: 0.569; (b) Sp-VRCS vs. medium intensity in dB: 0.649; and (c) Sp-VRCS vs. maximum intensity in dB: 0.758. In all cases, the level of significance was p < 0.01 (two-tailed).

Inter-rater reliability of Sp-VRCS

Inter‑rater reliability showed perfect agreement in this sample. The weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient was 1.00, indicating complete concordance between observers across all assessments in the present sample. The expected agreement by chance was Pe = 0.3843, and the standard error associated with the statistics was SE = 0.0955. The Z value of 10.47 (p < 0.001) indicates that the observed agreement was significantly greater than would be expected by chance.

Discussion

The present study presents, for the first time, the translation, cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric assessment of Sp-VRCS. This scale includes instructions that facilitate its homogeneous application, which improves the compatibility between different studies. In addition, a later study [18], adds data that further contributes to this homogeneity, especially with respect to the proposal of using 4 m as a consensus measurement instead of using the door of the room as the location.

As for the characteristics of the study participants, the death rattles are also a frequent clinical sign in patients with terminal stage cancer (76.60%), as also affirmed in the study by Davies et al. [35]. As for the distribution of patients according to the VRCS classification, it is coherent with the observations by Tantiwatniyom and Nagaviroj [31].

The criterion validity of the scale showed a moderate correlation between the Sp-VRCS scores, and the maximum levels of sound measured in dB (rho = 0.758, p < 0.01) and with the medium values (rho = 0.649, p < 0.01). These findings are comparable with the results obtained by Tantiwatniyom and Nagaviroj [31], who validated the Thai version of the scale, in which a very strong correlation was observed between the VRCS and the sound levels measured with a standard sonometer (rho = 0.8822, p < 0.05). The scores with the best correlations between dB and VRCS were found with the maximum and medium dBs, with this correlation being better than with the minimum dB. The difference in the magnitude of the correlation could be related with environmental conditions of the respective clinical settings, or with variations in the methodology used to measure sound.

Another important element of this study is the simultaneous use of a quantitative measurement (dB levels with a smartphone) to corroborate the subjective scores of the scale. Nevertheless, to minimize bias, all the measurements were performed with the same smartphone model and the same application. In this sense, the inter-rater agreement was perfect in this sample (Cohen’s kappa coefficient was 1.00), having already been good in previous studies. With this improvement with respect to published data attributed to the experience of the staff who collected the data, as both observations were performed at the same time, thus minimizing the variations that the intensity of the death rattles could present over time, and the reduction of the interference of the environmental noise. The inter-rater reliability in the study by Tantiwatniyom and Nagaviroj [31] showed an inter-evaluator concordance of 95%, with a weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.92, which is considered near-perfect agreement. In the present study, these results are consistent with previous reports on the VRCS [36], which demonstrated concurrent validity (p < 0.001).

The present study provides preliminary evidence of criterion validity and inter-rater agreement for the Spanish version of the VRCS but does not constitute a comprehensive psychometric validation according to COSMIN standards. The high inter-rater agreement observed in this sample (Cohen’s kappa = 1.00), supported by the Z-statistic (10.47) and the associated p-value (p < 0.001), suggests that the Sp-VRCS offers a consistent and reproducible interpretation across different observers, supporting its potential usefulness in both clinical and research settings.

Limitations

Despite the use of standardized protocols for measuring respiratory sounds, the possible influence of uncontrolled environmental sounds, which are part of the clinical setting, cannot be completely discounted. However, it should be noted that most patients in an end-of-life situation typically have their own room, which significantly reduces the risk of external sound interference, and thus, the margin of error. In addition, the study was conducted across different centers and care contexts (hospital and home care), which may help reduce potential biases related to background noise and strengthen the external validity of the findings. The inclusion of patients receiving home care also highlights the feasibility of using the scale in this setting. Nevertheless, further studies with larger samples of home-care patients are needed to confirm these results, as only 12 participants from this setting were included in the present study.

Another limitation concerns the number of nurses involved in the pretesting stage, as only six nurses (three per center) participated. Although this number is lower than the 30 healthcare professionals recommended in the standardized procedure described by Beaton et al. [28], increasing the sample was not feasible due to the very low nurse‑to‑patient ratios characteristic of Spanish long‑term care centers. This restricted pool of eligible professionals likely reduced heterogeneity among raters and may have contributed to the perfect Cohen’s kappa value observed.

The gender distribution of the sample was unbalanced, with 82.98% of participants being men. However, current evidence [16] does not suggest clinically meaningful differences in the manifestation or experience of death rattle between male and female patients. Although one observational study reported a higher incidence of death rattle in men than in women, this difference was not attributed to sex‑specific physiological mechanisms but rather to population characteristics.

Content validity was not reassessed in this study because it had already been established in the original English version of the VRCS and was not re-evaluated in the subsequent Thai adaptation; the aim was to obtain a Spanish version that could be readily implemented in clinical practice and support future research.

Another methodological consideration relates to the measurement device used. A smartphone-based application was employed instead of a clinical-grade sound level meter. Clinical-grade devices require periodic calibration, typically every 6–12 months, in accordance with international standards such as IEC 61,672, to ensure measurement accuracy and traceability. However, there is no evidence that acute-care hospitals in Spain routinely possess Class 1 or Class 2 clinical sound level meters. In this regard, the use of a smartphone may also be considered a practical strength of the study, as it allowed measurement technology to be brought directly to the bedside using a tool that is widely accessible to healthcare professionals.

Finally, although the psychometric evaluation of the Sp-VRCS provides a clinically useful and reliable scale for assessing death rattles in patients at the end-of-life, this study does not address the ongoing controversies regarding the effectiveness of treatments for this symptom, as this was not its objective. Nevertheless, the availability of this preliminary validated tool may help facilitate future research and contribute to the generation of new evidence in this area.

Conclusions

The Sp-VRCS demonstrated good criterion validity and high inter-rater reliability agreement. However, it is important to note that the present study provides preliminary psychometric support for the Spanish version of the VRCS, rather than a comprehensive psychometric validation according to COSMIN standards. For this reason, further multicentre validation studies are recommended to strengthen the generalizability and robustness of the scale’s application in diverse settings, and to address additional psychometric properties such as construct validity and test-retest reliability.

Despite this, the results support the use of the Sp-VRCS as a feasible and clinically useful scale for assessing respiratory congestion in palliative care patients at the end-of-life, including both home-care patients and patients in the palliative care unit. Following this study, a Spanish-language version of the scale is now available for detecting death rattles in Spanish-speaking palliative care settings, allowing physicians and nurses to assess respiratory congestion in a more systematic and consistent manner.

Lastly, it is important to underline that this psychometric evaluation does not signify an endpoint, but rather the start of a process of dissemination and implementation. It is essential to promote a Sp-VRCS dissemination plan across various healthcare units and to work towards achieving consensus on the equipment and procedures used for its implementation.
