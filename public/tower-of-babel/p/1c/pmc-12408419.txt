Supportive Care Needs From Mid‐to Long‐Term Follow‐Up Among Head and Neck Cancer Survivors: A Longitudinal Cohort Study
Authors: Femke Jansen, Dominique Molenaar, Öykü Zarsat, Birgit I Lissenberg‐Witte, Robert P Takes, Remco de Bree, Johannes A Langendijk, Jose A Hardillo, Femke Lamers, C René Leemans, Irma M Verdonck‐de Leeuw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThere is limited knowledge on the supportive care needs (SCNs) of head and neck cancer (HNC) survivors during long‐term survivorship.AimsTo investigate SCNs from 2 to 5 years after treatment among HNC survivors, and its association with demographic, personal, clinical, physical, psychological, social and lifestyle, and cancer‐related quality of life factors.MethodsSCNs were measured at 2, 3, 4 and 5 years using the supportive care needs survey (SCNS‐SF34 and SCNS‐HNC) in 403 HNC survivors. Linear mixed model analyses were performed to investigate changes in SCNs (continuous outcome) over time and factors associated with SCNs (continuous). Also, the proportion of unmet moderate‐high SCNs (dichotomous) was calculated.ResultsSCNs on the health system, information and patient support and lifestyle domain decreased from 2 to 5 years, whereas physical and daily living, psychological, sexuality and HNC‐function needs were stable. At 5 years, 33% of HNC survivors had unmet SCNs, in particular lack of energy/tiredness (9.2%) and dry mouth/sticky mucus (8.1%). Demographic (male, higher education), personal (personality, coping, self‐efficacy), clinical (advanced tumor stage, tumor recurrence), physical (low handgrip strength), psychological (anxiety, depression, fear of cancer recurrence (FCR)), lifestyle (smoking, drinking, being underweight) and cancer‐related factors (lower levels of functioning, lower levels of nausea and vomiting, higher levels of fatigue, constipation, financial, speech, social eating, dry mouth and sexuality problems, and painkiller usage) were associated with SCNs. Specifically FCR and painkillers usage were very consistently associated with higher SCNs.ConclusionsSCNs of HNC survivors decrease over time. A third experiences SCNs at long‐term follow‐up.Keywords: head and neck cancer, head and neck cancer functioning needs, head and neck oncology, information needs, lifestyle needs, needs, physical and daily living, psychological needs, sexuality needs, supportive care needs, survivorship

Background

Supportive care targeting head and neck cancer (HNC) survivors entails the provision of information and care for daily living, physical and psychological functioning, sexuality and lifestyle [1], and aims to prevent and alleviate cancer‐generic and HNC‐specific symptoms such as pain, fatigue, symptoms of anxiety or depression, problems with swallowing, speaking and eating, and to improve health‐related quality of life (HRQOL). Previous longitudinal research by Chen et al. (2013) [2] investigated supportive care needs (SCNs) among 82 oral cavity cancer survivors from before start of (chemo)radiation up to 6 months after start of (chemo)radiation and showed that SCNs peaked around 2 months, after which SCNs reduced to below baseline values. Molenaar et al. (2023) [3] investigated the longitudinal course of SCNs among 563 HNC survivors from cancer diagnosis up to 2 years after the end of treatment and found, except for a small peak in physical and daily living needs at 3 months, that SCNs reduced up to 2 years after treatment. At 2 years after treatment, 43% of HNC survivors experienced at least one moderate‐high unmet SCN.

So far, no study has investigated longitudinal changes in SCNs during the mid‐to long‐term survivorship phase (> 2 years), while 48% (hypopharynx) to 81% (larynx) of HNC survivors are still alive at 2 years follow‐up and 32% (hypopharynx) to 70% (larynx) become long‐term survivors (> 5 years) [4]. A previous cross‐sectional study described SCNs among HNC survivors 2–3 years, 3–4 years and 4–5 years after treatment and found no difference in SCNs between these 3 groups in the final multivariable model [5]. A cross‐sectional study by O'Brien et al. (2017) [6] compared HNC survivors < 5 years and > 5 years after treatment, and showed that SCNs on the psychological and health system and information domain were lower among those > 5 years after treatment, whereas groups did not differ with regard to physical and daily living, sexuality and care and support needs.

Based on these cross‐sectional results, no clear conclusion can be drawn on whether SCNs are likely to improve, stabilize or even worsen during the mid to long‐term survivorship phase. Also, it is unknown which mid‐term follow‐up factors are associated with high levels of SCNs at a later timepoint, and thus which groups of survivors are at risk for SCNs. Therefore, the aim of this study, was to investigate changes in SCNs among HNC survivors from mid‐to long‐term follow‐up (i.e. 2–5 years after treatment). In addition, this study aimed to identify demographic, personal, clinical, physical, psychological, social and lifestyle factors and cancer‐related symptoms as measured at 2 years follow‐up in relation to the absolute level of SCNs at mid‐to long‐term follow‐up. Based on previous research on SCNs up to 2 years after treatment in the same cohort of Dutch HNC patients [7] it is hypothesized that male gender, higher education, personality, coping behavior, higher tumor stage, poorer scores on anxiety and fear of cancer recurrence (FCR), smoking, poorer role, emotional and social functioning and poorer scores on cancer‐related symptoms (e.g. those with swallowing problems and those who use painkillers) are associated with higher levels of SCNs. Also, it is hypothesized that patients with tumor recurrence or a second primary cancer diagnosis experience higher levels of SCNs.

Methods

This study used data of the NETherlands QUality of life and BIomedical Cohort study (NET‐QUBIC), a longitudinal cohort study among 739 HNC survivors in the Netherlands. As described in the protocol paper [8], HNC patients were included in this study in case they were 18 years or older; newly‐diagnosed with a tumor in the oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, or larynx, or with an unknown primary site; treated with curative intent; and were able to write and read Dutch. Exclusion criteria were having severe psychiatric comorbidities, or being unable to understand the questions, test instructions or informed consent.

HNC survivors were asked to complete patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) before start of treatment (baseline), at three and 6 months, and at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years after treatment. Fieldwork visits were conducted at baseline, 6 months and 1, 2 and 5 years after treatment, and encompassed an interview, physical tests and biobank sampling. Clinical data was extracted from clinical files just after primary treatment (on baseline clinical factors and primary cancer treatment) and at 2 and 5 years after treatment (on tumor recurrence and second primary tumors). For this study NET‐QUBIC data on SCNs measured at 2, 3, 4 and 5 years after treatment were used, as two previous NET‐QUBIC studies already focused on the SCNs from respectively cancer diagnosis to 2 years after treatment and 6 months following treatment to 2 years after treatment [3, 7]. All patients included in the NET‐QUBIC cohort signed informed consent. The local ethical committee approved the study (Amsterdam UMC, location VUmc, document number: 2013.301‐NL45051.029.13).

Supportive Care Needs Survey (SCNS‐SF34) and Head and Neck Cancer‐Specific Module (SCNS‐HNC)

SCNs were measured using the 34‐item Short‐Form Supportive Care Needs Survey (SCNS‐SF34) [9, 10, 11] and the head and neck cancer‐specific module (SCNS‐HNC) [10]. The SCNS‐SF34 consists of 4 domains, namely physical & daily living (PDL), psychological (PSY), sexuality (SEX) and health system, information & patient support (HSIPS) needs [10], and one single item (more choice about which hospital you attend). The SCNS‐HNC consists of two domains, namely HNC‐specific functioning needs (HNC‐function) and lifestyle (HNC‐lifestyle) needs, and one single item (care of your stoma and/or voice prosthesis). All items have a five‐point response scale: ‘1 = not applicable’ for issues that are no problem; ‘2 = satisfied’ for issues on which the survivor needs support but the support is already satisfactory fulfilled; and ‘3 = low unmet need’, ‘4 = moderate unmet need’ and ‘5 = high unmet need’ for issues on which a survivor reports respectively a low, moderate or high unmet SCNs [12]. Per domain a total continuous score was calculated ranging from 0 to 100 score (a higher score indicates a higher level of SCNs) and a dichotomised outcome was calculated indicating whether survivors had at least one moderate‐high unmet SCN, that is a score of four or 5 (yes/no) (further called unmet need). In case of missing data in less than 50% of the SCNS‐SF34 or SCNS‐HNC items of a particular domain a total score was calculated assuming that the mean score of the missing items was the same as the completed items. The SCNS‐SF34 and SCNS‐HNC have good validity and reliability among HNC survivors [10] and showed, except for lifestyle (Cronbach's α of 0.55–0.68 per timepoint) a good to excellent internal consistency in this study (Cronbach's α between 0.84 and 0.97).

Factors Investigated in Relation Supportive Care Needs From 2 to 5 Years After Treatment

Detailed information on all collected data is provided in the data catalog [11]. Factors measured at baseline (for fixed factors such as gender) and factors measured at 2 years follow‐up were investigated in relation to the absolute level of SCNs from 2 to 5 years after treatment. Baseline factors encompassed fixed demographic, personal, and clinical factors. Two years follow‐up factors encompassed personal, clinical (i.e. cancer recurrence), physical, psychological, social and lifestyle factors, and cancer‐related quality of life symptoms. Demographic factors were age, gender, living situation (living alone or living together) and educational level (lower, middle or higher education) [13]. Personal factors consisted of personality (NEO Five Factor Inventory, a higher score indicates a higher level of neuroticism, extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness or openness to experience) [14], coping (Utrecht Coping List, a higher score indicates more active coping, palliative coping, avoidance coping, seeking support, passive coping, expression of emotions, and comforting thoughts) [15] (both measured at baseline) and self‐efficacy (Generalized Self‐efficacy scale, a higher score indicates better self‐efficacy) (measured at 2 years). Clinical factors consisted of treatment (single or multimodality treatment), ICD‐10 tumor stage (I, II, III or IV), tumor site (oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx or unknown primary), comorbidity (Adult Comorbidity Evaluation 27 (ACE‐27) categorized into no, mild, moderate or severe comorbidities [16]), WHO performance score (WHO 0 or WHO I/II/III) (all measured at baseline) and having had a tumor recurrence or second primary cancer diagnosis in between the primary cancer diagnosis and the 2 years follow‐up assessment (further called recurrence (yes/no)).

Physical factors encompassed handgrip strength as measured using a JAMAR handgrip dynamometer [17]. Out of two measurements, the highest handgrip strength was dichotomized into low (< 10th percentile) versus normal (≥ 10th percentile) handgrip strength based on gender and age‐based reference values [18]. Psychological factors consisted of symptoms of anxiety and depression measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxiety or depression [19], and FCR measured by the Cancer Worry Scale (CWS), with higher scores indicating higher levels of FCR [20]. Social factors consisted of social support as measured by the Social Support List – Interactions (higher scores indicate better social support) [21]. Lifestyle factors consisted of smoking as measured by study‐specific questionnaire on daily smoking (yes/no), alcohol usage (study‐specific questionnaire on excessive drinking (yes/no), excessive drinking was defined as > 14 glasses per week for women and > 21 glasses for men), and body mass index (BMI, categorized into underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal weight (BMI of 18.5–25.0) and overweight (BMI > 25.0).

Cancer‐related quality of life symptoms were measured using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) core and HNC‐specific quality of life module (EORTC QLQ‐C30 and EORTC QLQ‐H&N35). The EORTC QLQ‐C30 consists of 15 domains, namely global quality of life, physical, role, emotional, cognitive and social functioning, nausea and vomiting, fatigue, pain, dyspnea, insomnia, loss of appetite, constipation, diarrhea and financial problems [22, 23]. The EORTC QLQ‐H&N35 comprises 18 domains, namely oral pain, swallowing, senses, speech, social eating, social contact, sexuality, problems with teeth, dry mouth, opening the mouth, sticky saliva, cough, feeling ill, weight loss, weight gain, use of nutritional supplements, feeding tubes, and painkillers [24]. All EORTC domain scores ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores on the global quality of life and all functioning domains indicate better functioning, whereas a higher score on all other domains indicate higher symptom burden.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics, independent t‐tests and chi‐square tests were used to describe the study population, to compare it to the group of NET‐QUBIC HNC survivors without SCNs data at 2, 3, four or 5 years after treatment (while still alive) and to describe the proportion of HNC survivors with unmet SCNs. Linear mixed models with fixed effects for time (as categorical variable) and a random intercept per HNC survivors were built to describe changes in SCNs from 2 to 5 years follow‐up. Multivariable linear mixed models were built (compound symmetry matrix) adding the potential factor to the model to investigate its association with the absolute level of SCNs from 2 to 5 years after treatment. Factors were added to the multivariable mixed model using forward selection (p < 0.05). As multiple factors were investigated in relation to SCNs we distinguished factors associated with SCNs using a p‐value of < 0.05 (i.e. a more exploratory p‐value) and those with a more strict p‐value of < 0.01 (i.e. factors significant after correction for multiple testing). Multicollinearity of associated factors was evaluated by means of the variance inflation factor (VIF), which showed no signs for problematic multicollinearity. All analyses were performed using the IBM Statistical package for the Social Science (SPSS) version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY USA).

Results

In total 607 of 739 (82%) HNC survivors included in NET‐QUBIC were still alive 2 years after the end of treatment. Of these 607 HNC survivors, 403 (66%) HNC survivors had data on SCNs at 2, 3, 4 and/or 5 years follow‐up and were included in this study. The remaining 204 HNC survivors dropped‐out of the NET‐QUBIC study before 2 years follow‐up due to logistic reasons (n = 40), psychological reasons (n = 30), refusal to participate in follow/up assessments (n = 17), physical reasons (n = 16), treatment/follow‐up in another non NET‐QUBIC center (n = 5) or unknown reasons (n = 10). In addition, 86 patients were still actively participating in the NET‐QUBIC study but did have missing data on SCNs (see Figure 1).

Flow diagram.

HNC survivors included in this study differed from the non‐participating HNC survivors in terms of education, living situation, comorbidity and WHO physical performance status. Included HNC survivors were more often higher educated, living together, more often had no comorbidities and no physical limitations, compared to the non‐participating HNC survivors. Included HNC survivors were often male (74%), lower educated (40%), lived together (81%), had an oropharynx tumor (36%), had an advanced tumor stage (stage III/IV, 55%), were treated using single modality treatment (57%), had mild comorbidities (41%) and had no physical limitations (76%) at time of diagnosis (see Table 1).

The Need for Supportive Care and the Proportion of Unmet Needs Among HNC Survivors From 2 to 5 Years After the End of Treatment

SCNs on the HSIPS and HNC‐lifestyle domain decreased from 2 to 5 years, whereas SCNs on the PDL, PSY, SEX and HNC‐function domain remained stable (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 1 and Table 2). Over all timepoints SCNs were highest on the HSIPS, PDL and HNC‐function domain, followed by the PSY, SEX and HNC‐lifestyle domain. At 2 years, most prevalent unmet SCNs were lack of energy/tiredness (11.4%), having one member of hospital staff with whom you can talk to about all aspects of your condition, treatment and follow‐up (10.7%) and problems with dry mouth and/or sticky mucus (10.2%) (Table 3). At 5 years, most prevalent unmet SCNs were lack of energy/tiredness (9.2%) and problems with dry mouth and/or sticky mucus (8.1%). A third (33%) of HNC survivors had unmet SCNs at 5 years after treatment.

Mid‐Term Follow‐Up Factors Associated With Supportive Care Needs From 2 to 5 Years After Treatment

Baseline (fixed) and 2 years follow‐up factors were investigated in relation to the absolute level of SCNs from 2 to 5 years after treatment (see Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2 and Table 4 for univariable and multivariable findings). Factors associated with higher levels of PDL needs in a multivariable model were middle or higher level education (vs. lower education), higher levels of reassuring thoughts, symptoms of depression and FCR, lower levels of role functioning and lower levels of nausea and vomiting, higher levels of fatigue, financial difficulties, troubles with social eating and sexuality, the use of painkillers and not using nutritional supplements. Factors associated with higher levels of PSY needs were higher levels of neuroticism, extraversion, symptoms of anxiety and depression, FCR, lower levels of social functioning, higher levels of insomnia, trouble with social contact and the use of painkillers. Factors associated with higher levels of SEX needs were male gender, lower levels of agreeableness, higher levels of reassuring thoughts, FCR, pain and sexuality problems. Factors associated with higher levels of HSIPS needs were higher age, higher levels of neuroticism and extraversion, lower levels of openness, cancer recurrence within 2 years, higher levels of FCR, being underweight, lower levels of social functioning, higher levels of fatigue, constipation, speech problems and sticky saliva, lower levels of dry mouth, the use of painkillers and no weight loss.

Factors associated with higher levels of HNC‐function needs were lower levels of openness and agreeableness, higher levels of reassuring thoughts, lower levels of self‐efficacy, being diagnosed with a stage IV tumor (compared to stage I), cancer recurrence within 2 years, low handgrip strength, lower levels of emotional functioning, lower levels of nausea and vomiting, and more problems with appetite loss, constipation, finances, swallowing, speech and teeth. Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate changes in SCNs among HNC survivors from 2 to 5 years follow‐up, and to identify demographic, personal, clinical, physical, psychological, social and lifestyle and symptom‐specific factors associated with the level of SCNs. This study is a follow‐up study of the previously published NET‐QUBIC studies by Molenaar et al. [3, 7] which showed that SCNs on all domains lowered from cancer diagnosis up to 2 years after treatment (except for a small peak in PDL needs shortly after treatment). At 2 years follow‐up SCNs on all domains were lower than the SCNs at time of cancer diagnosis. In this study, we found a further reduction in SCNs on the domains on HSIPS and HNC‐lifestyle, whereas all other SCN domains remained stable from 2 to 5 years follow‐up. About a third of HNC survivors experienced at least one unmet SCN at 5 years follow‐up, especially concerning lack of energy/tiredness and dry mouth and sticky saliva. Demographic, personal, clinical, psychological, lifestyle and symptoms‐specific factors as measured at 2 years were found to be associated with higher levels of SCNs from 2 to 5 years after treatment.

Our findings on changes in SCNs from 2 to 5 years follow‐up are somewhat in line with the results of Wells et al. [5] who found no difference in total number of SCNs when comparing HNC survivors 2–3 years, 3–4 years or 4–5 years after treatment. Our results are also in line with O'Brien et al. [6] who found no difference in SCNs on the PDL, PSY and SEX domain and lower SCNs on the health system and information domain (slightly different from our HSIPS domain) among HNC survivors > 5 years after diagnosis compared to HNC survivors < 5 years after diagnosis. In contrast to our findings, however, O'Brien et al. [6] did found lower PSY needs among those > 5 years after diagnosis compared to those < 5 years after diagnosis. A reason for this discrepancy may be that the group of HNC survivors < 5 years also encompassed patients shortly after treatment, whereas we focused on changes from 2 to 5 years after treatment only.

The finding that demographic (being male, higher age and higher education level), personal (personality, coping, and higher self‐efficacy), clinical (advanced TNM stage and tumor recurrence), psychological (symptoms of anxiety and depression and FCR), physical (low handgrip strength), lifestyle (being underweight, smoking and drinking), and symptoms‐specific factors (functioning levels as well as generic and HNC‐specific factors) were associated with higher levels of SCNs from 2 to 5 years after treatment was largely in line with previous NET‐QUBIC results of Molenaar et al. [7] who investigated factors assessed at 6 months follow‐up in relation to the level of SCNs at 2 years follow‐up. Our study showed consistent evidence (i.e. associations in 4–five out of 6 SCNs domains) that HNC survivors with higher levels of FCR and use of painkillers had higher levels of SCNs. These factors may be used in clinical practice to identify HNC survivors at risk for SCNs up to long‐term follow‐up. Besides being used as factor to identify HNC survivors at risk for SCNs, FCR may be targeted to improve both FCR and SCNs. A previous study showed that 7% of HNC survivors have persistent FCR [25]. Psychological interventions, such as blended cognitive behavior therapy, has been found to be effective in reducing FCR [26, 27].

There were also some discrepancies in findings between this study and Molenaar et al. [7]. In our study lower levels of agreeableness, higher levels of coping using reassuring thoughts, higher levels of symptoms of depression, lower levels of nausea and vomiting, and higher levels of financial difficulties and speech problems were associated with two or more SCN domains, whereas Molenaar et al. [7] did not found such an association. Also, tumor recurrence within 2 years was associated with SCNs in our study (not investigated in the previous study). An explanation why financial difficulties do matter in the long run, but less at 6 months after treatment, may be that some types of supportive care (psychologist, physiotherapist, dietician) are reimbursed by the HNC survivors' health insurance when care is provided in the hospital as part of HNC treatment and recovery, while these types of care are mostly no longer reimbursed by the basic insurance at long‐term follow‐up. Also, HNC survivors who are employed at diagnosis, but are no longer able to work, may face further income loss after 2 years, when sickness leave paid for by their employer ends [28], which may influence their financial situation and ability to pay for different types of supportive care.

Remarkably, higher levels of nausea and vomiting and dry mouth were found to be associated with lower levels on SCNs on respectively the PDL, HNC‐function and HNC‐lifestyle domain. The association between less problems with dry mouth and a higher level of lifestyle needs is in line with Molenaar et al. [7]. The association between nausea and vomiting and PDL and HNC‐function needs has not been found previously. A potential explanation for this seemingly counterintuitive association might be that troublesome symptoms such as nausea and vomiting must be managed first before HNC survivors can recognize and prioritize other SCNs.

Clinical Implications

This study aimed to identify supportive care needs as perceived by HNC survivors and did not take into account actual received supportive care. As such, we cannot provide clear recommendations on referral of HNC survivors to specific types of supportive care. What we do know based on our study and previous studies [3, 5, 6, 7] however is that some SCNs (e.g. needs related to the provision of information) decrease from mid to long‐term follow‐up, whereas other SCNs are more stable over time. We do not know whether these SCNs are all related to the HNC diagnosis and treatment or whether part of the SCNs can be explained by normal aging. Further research is needed on this matter. Based on the most prevalent SCNs, that is lack or energy/tiredness and dry mouth and/or sticky saliva, and the identified associated factors (e.g. tumor recurrence, speech problems) it, however, seems likely that part of the SCNs are a direct result of HNC diagnosis and/or treatment. HNC survivorship care might benefit from further attention for these SCNs, for example by providing tailored information and self‐care advice regarding experienced SCNs or by better referral of HNC survivors to certain types of supportive care (e.g. psychological care) [26, 27, 29]. Also, we found that especially fear of cancer recurrence and painkillers usage at 2 years after treatment were associated with supportive care needs, warranting increased attention to these HNC survivors in clinical practice.

Limitations

A potential limitation of this study is that our findings may not be generalizable to all HNC survivors. HNC survivors included in this study more often lived together, were higher educated, had less comorbidities and had a better physical performance score, compared to HNC survivors not included. Consequently, it is likely that the identified SCNs are an underestimation of the actual SCNs. Also, not all included HNC survivors completed the SCNS‐SF34 and SCNS‐HNC on all four timepoints. Missing data per timepoint were not imputed in this study. However, linear mixed model analyses were used, which enables the use of all data (instead of data from complete cases only), including for example data from those who died during follow‐up. Another limitation is that due to the design of NET‐QUBIC, the number of completed SCNS‐HNC questionnaires was quite low in comparison to the SCNS‐SF34 questionnaire. Finally, potential limitations of this study are that we investigated many potential associated factors and that we conducted an association model rather than a prediction model with a potential risk for overfitting. Our findings therefore do not reflect causal associations and further research is needed to validate our model. To facilitate interpretation of the many statistical findings, we, however, added insight into factors that would have been significant in case a more strict p‐value of < 0.01 (i.e. p‐value corrected for multiple testing) would have been used.

Conclusions

This study showed health system, information and patient support and lifestyle needs decreased from 2 to 5 years after treatment, whereas physical and daily living, psychological, sexuality and HNC‐function needs were stable. At 5 years after treatment, a third of HNC survivors still experiences SCNs. Most prominent unmet SCNs relate to lack of energy/tiredness and dry mouth/sticky mucus. Demographic (being male, higher age and higher education level), personal (personality, coping, and higher self‐efficacy), clinical (advanced TNM stage and tumor recurrence), psychological (symptoms of anxiety and depression and FCR), physical (low handgrip strength), lifestyle (being underweight, smoking and drinking) and symptoms‐specific factors (functioning levels as well as generic and HNC‐specific factors) were found to be associated with higher levels of SCNs. Of these factors higher levels of FCR and use of painkillers were very consistently associated with higher levels of SCNs across the domains, and may need increased attention in clinical practice.
