Brucella abortus interferes with the inflammatory response and tissue repair in mice
Authors: M. F. Silva, D. F. A. Batista, C. Eckstein, F. B. Costa, P. P. Campos, M. A. N. D. Ferreira, M. S. S. Araújo, O. A. Martins-Filho, R. M. Tsolis, R. L. Santos, Tatiane A. Paixão
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Brucellosis caused by Brucella spp. is considered a debilitating chronic zoonotic disease. B. abortus, which is endemic in many countries, is responsible for chronic bovine and human infections. Interestingly, there are joint and breast implant infections caused by Brucella spp. Although Brucella spp. induce an insidious inflammatory response, little is known about the influence of bacteria on the establishment of early inflammation in vivo, particularly in a synthetic sponge model. B. abortus 2308 was able to survive and replicate in this model, whereas B. abortus Δ virB2 was attenuated, confirming its inability to cause persistent infection. Compared with Δ virB2, B. abortus 2308 is able to modulate the inflammatory response in sponge. We observed that B. abortus 2308 induced a lower influx of inflammatory cells and inflammatory mediators and the formation of fibrovascular tissue at 14 days postimplantation than the control or ΔvirB2-infected mice. Compared with the uninfected sponge, infection by B. abortus in the sponge prolonged the percentage of Mф and M1 macrophages, in addition to increasing the percentage of CD8 + T lymphocytes. B. abortus 2308 can inhibit inflammation in vivo, and the T4SS influences the course of the acute inflammatory process in a synthetic matrix model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-03837-4.

Introduction

Brucellosis is a zoonotic infectious disease that affects animals and humans. It is caused by bacteria of the genus Brucella1,2. Brucella abortus is one of the most pathogenic species in the genus and is the main cause of bovine brucellosis. Brucellosis causes economic losses due to reproductive failure and culling of infected animals. It also poses a public health risk due to its zoonotic nature since it may result in undulating fever that may progress to chronic inflammatory lesions in human patients1,3. Human brucellosis is widely distributed worldwide, with more than 500,000 new cases annually, with more than 1000 cases per million being identified in endemic areas4, although this estimated incidence is likely underestimated5. The main clinical manifestations in humans are associated with nonspecific signs and symptoms, such as fever, fatigue, headache, and myalgia, which may progress to secondary clinical complications, including endocarditis, meningoencephalitis, or musculoskeletal disorders, such as arthritis and osteomyelitis3. Interestingly, clinical cases of brucellosis have been associated with surgical implantation of joint6 and breast prostheses7,8.

Brucella spp. cause a systemic disease, with preferential tropism to the liver, spleen and lymph nodes, where they survive and multiply in macrophages, persisting and causing chronic disease2. The chronicity of B. abortus infection depends on the ability of bacteria to survive and replicate in eukaryotic cells, mainly phagocytic cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells9, although several different cell types and organs may be infected by Brucella spp10.

Several virulence factors contribute to Brucella spp. evasion of the immune system and persistence in the host, contributing to the establishment of chronic infection2,11. An important Brucella virulence factor is the virB operon-encoded type IV secretion system (T4SS), which secretes effector bacterial proteins into the cytoplasm of the host cell. Once in the host cell, T4SS effectors trigger changes in the maturation of the Brucella-containing vacuole (BCV), prevent fusion with lysosomes, and cause the BCV to acquire endoplasmic reticulum (ER) markers, thereby generating an intracellular niche that favors the intracellular multiplication of Brucella12. A functional T4SS is also required for the induction of a mild acute inflammatory response in a mouse model of infection13–15. Importantly, previous studies have also demonstrated that the virulence factors of Brucella sp. may modulate the host acute inflammatory response, favoring the establishment of a chronic course of disease11,16.

In vitro and in vivo models have been used for studying virulence factors and their influence on the host immune response against Brucella spp2. However, the initial inflammatory response triggered by Brucella during the early phases of infection is still not completely understood. The implantation of synthetic polyester‒polyurethane sponges is a well-established model for studying acute and chronic inflammatory responses, foreign body granulomatous inflammation, fibroplasia, and angiogenesis17–20. However, this model has not yet been employed to evaluate inflammation in response to bacterial pathogens. Therefore, we reasoned that the sponge model could be a suitable tool for assessing the dynamics of inflammation at the site of infection. Additionally, this model may shed light on the interaction between Brucella spp. and surgical wound healing and implantation sites. A better understanding of the establishment and kinetics of inflammatory lesions elicited by Brucella may eventually lead to the development of more efficient therapies.

Thus, the aim of this study was to evaluate the initial stages of the inflammatory process induced by B. abortus and the role of the T4SS in this process in a synthetic matrix implant model in mice.

Results

Brucella abortus lacking the virB-encoded T4SS is attenuated in the sponge model

At 24 h post implantation in the subcutaneous cervical region, sponges were inoculated with 104 colony-forming units (CFUs) of the wild-type (WT) B. abortus strain 2308 or the ΔvirB2 B. abortus mutant strain (ΔvirB2), which lacks a functional T4SS. Uninfected controls (mock) were inoculated with the same volume of sterile PBS. Bacterial loads and inflammatory responses were evaluated over a period of two weeks post infection. B. abortus was recovered from the spleen of mice infected with WT B. abortus at 6 and 13 days post infection or 7 and 14 days post sponge implantation (dpsi), demonstrating that inoculation of the WT strain into the implanted sponge resulted in dissemination and establishment of systemic infection (Fig. 1A and B). In contrast, there was no bacterial recovery from the spleens of the mice infected with the ΔvirB2 strain, indicating that this strain was attenuated in these mice, as previously reported21 (Fig. 1A and B). Bacterial colonization of the sponge (inoculation site) was detected in the mice infected with both strains, but the bacterial loads were significantly greater in the mice infected with WT B. abortus than in those infected with the ΔvirB2 strain (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C and D). Indeed, sponges inoculated with the WT B. abortus had approximately 1,000-fold more CFUs than those inoculated with the ΔvirB2 strain at both 7 and 15 dpsi. Immunolabeling of Brucella sp. revealed that it was located predominantly within the cytoplasm of cells morphologically compatible with infiltrating macrophages, and immunolabeling was more prominent in sponges infected with WT B. abortus than in those infected with the ΔvirB2 mutant strain (Fig. 1E).

Brucella abortus changes the type of inflammation that occurs in the implanted sponge model

Subcutaneous sponge implants are good models for assessing the progression of inflammation and repair. After subcutaneous implantation, the sponge stimulates fluid accumulation (inflammatory edema), followed by the influx of inflammatory cells, predominantly neutrophils and macrophages and subsequently multinucleated giant cells, and the formation of granulation tissue filling the sponge spaces17,22. In mock sponges, there was a scarce inflammatory infiltrate composed of neutrophils and macrophages at 7 dpsi, whereas at 14 dpsi, there was increased cellularity and accumulation of granulation tissue in the center of the sponge (Fig. 2A). The spaces within the sponge were filled with granulation tissue associated with an inflammatory infiltrate composed of neutrophils, macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, and rare mast cells. On the periphery of the sponge, there was a thin capsule of connective tissue associated with multifocal mild neutrophilic inflammatory infiltrate, microvacuolar (immature) adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle tissue (Fig. 2B). A similar pattern of inflammation was observed in all three groups at 7 dpsi. However, B. abortus-infected sponges had a lower histopathological score (based on inflammatory infiltrate and conjunctive capsule formation) than the other sponges did at 14 dpsi (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C, D).

Brucella abortus does not significantly change the influx of neutrophils to the site of inflammation

The inflammatory process elicited by sponges is initially composed of neutrophils and macrophages, and at later stages, there is a predominance of macrophages, with multinucleated giant cells and granulation tissue17,20,23. Considering that neutrophils and macrophages are important inflammatory cells at the B. abortus infection site24, we assessed the infiltration of neutrophils by measuring myeloperoxidase (MPO), which is an indirect indicator of neutrophilic activity and recruitment to the site of the lesion20,25. No significant differences in MPO activity were observed among the three groups (Fig. 3A). The concentration of CXCL1, which is a neutrophil chemoattractant, was also similar between all groups at 7 and 14 dpsi (Fig. 3B). Together, these data suggest that WT B. abortus does not change the influx of neutrophils to the site of inflammation.

Brucella abortus infection modulates macrophage activity at the site of inflammation

The enzyme N-acetyl-b-D-glycosaminidase (NAG), which is present in activated macrophages, can be easily quantified as an indirect indicator of macrophage infiltration20,25. At 7 dpsi, NAG activity was significantly greater in sponges infected with WT B. abortus than in those in the other two groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3C). In contrast, at 14 dpsi, there was a significant reduction in NAG activity in sponges infected with WT B. abortus compared with sponges inoculated with the ΔvirB2 strain or sterile PBS (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3C). CCL2, a chemokine that recruits macrophages, was present at higher concentrations in sponges infected with WT B. abortus than in the other two groups at 7 dpsi (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). In contrast, at 14 dpsi, there was no significant difference in the CCL2 level between sponges infected with WT B. abortus and the other two groups (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, multinucleated giant cells that usually appear in implanted sponges at 14 days and are typical of a foreign body granulomatous response20,23 were observed at 14 dpsi but only in the ΔvirB2 and mock groups. In sponges infected with WT B. abortus, there were no multinucleated giant cells (Fig. 3E and F). These data demonstrated the ability of B. abortus to modulate macrophage activity at the site of inflammation in a T4SS-dependent manner.

Brucella abortus delays granulation tissue formation in sponge implants

The subcutaneous implant of the sponge induces, in addition to a granulomatous inflammatory response, the proliferation of fibrovascular tissue known as granulation tissue26. Therefore, we analyzed the levels of tumor growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), which is a cytokine responsible for controlling cell proliferation and differentiation and is related to chronic inflammation and tissue repair17. No significant differences were observed among the groups at 7 dpsi (Fig. 4A). However, at 14 dpsi (Fig. 4B), the sponge in the mouse subcutaneous tissue induces the production of TGF-β and the production of TGF-β in mice inoculated with PBS or the ΔvirB2 strain is similar and both higher than the sponges inoculated with WT B. abortus (p < 0.05). Additionally, we evaluated the formation of granulation tissue in sponges via morphometric analysis to determine the percentage of the area of the sponge occupied by the capsule and the degree of inflammation associated with vascularized immature connective tissue in relation to the total area of the sponge. At 7 dpsi, the proportion of granulation tissue was similar among all the groups (Fig. 4C). However, at 14 dpsi, the ΔvirB2- and mock-inoculated groups presented similar proportion of granulation tissue in the sponge, but greater proportion than did the sponges inoculated with WT B. abortus (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4D), indicating that ΔvirB2 strain does not alter the type of inflammatory response in the sponge model and B. abortus with a functional T4SS may impair or delay the proliferation of granulation tissue in infected sponges.

Brucella abortus infection triggers interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production but not tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) or interleukin-10 (IL-10) production in subcutaneously implanted sponges

To evaluate the influence of B. abortus infection on the production of inflammatory mediators in the sponge model, the levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and interleukin-10 (IL-10), which are known to be induced by Brucella, were measured. When TNF-α, which is a proinflammatory cytokine induced in the acute phase of inflammation, or IL-10, a regulatory cytokine that decreases the ability of the host to induce inflammation, was analyzed, no significant differences were detected among all the groups at 7 or 14 dpsi (Fig. 5). However, B. abortus infection induces the production of IFN-γ, which is considered the most important proinflammatory and protective cytokine against intracellular pathogens27. IFN-γ production did not differ between the groups at 7 dpsi (Fig. 5E). However, at 14 dpsi, sponges inoculated with WT B. abortus had higher concentrations of IFN-γ than did the ΔvirB2 or mock sponges (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5F), indicating that IFN-γ, but not TNF-α or IL10, was induced by B. abortus with a functional T4SS in infected sponges.

Brucella abortus infection alters the phenotypic features of infiltrating leukocytes in sponges

Considering that the inflammatory processes in mock-infected sponges or sponges inoculated with the ΔvirB2 strain were similar but distinct from those in sponges infected with WT B. abortus, we evaluated the inflammatory exudates in mock sponges and sponges inoculated with WT B. abortus via flow cytometry at 14 dpsi. A gating strategy was used to identify and differentiate the population of leukocytes (Figure supplementary 1 A) and cell types involved in innate (Figure supplementary 1B) and adaptive (Figure supplementary 1 C) immune responses.

After sponge implantation, there was initially an influx of neutrophils, but at 14 dpsi, macrophages were the predominant inflammatory cells. There was no significant difference in the percentage of neutrophils (Ly6GHigh) between the mock (control) and infected groups (24% and 32%, respectively) (Fig. 6A). A greater proportion of macrophages was observed in the control sponges than in the sponges infected with B. abortus (74% and 61%, respectively; p < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 6B. Macrophage subpopulations were characterized as Mф (F4/80 + CD11c- CD206–), M1 (F4/80 + CD11c + CD206–), M2 (F4/80 + CD11c– CD206+) or M1/M2 (F4/80 + CD11c + CD206+) on the basis of their profile of receptor expression as indicated (Fig. 6B). Compared with the infected sponges, the uninfected sponges had a greater proportion of the M1/M2 mixed macrophage subtypes (63.3% and 13.3%, respectively, p < 0.05), whereas the infected sponges had a greater proportion of Mф than did the mock sponges (60.1% and 17%, respectively, p < 0.05). M1 macrophages were also more abundant in the infected group than in the uninfected group (18.6% and 14.1%, respectively, p < 0.05).

An effective immune response against intracellular infections, such as brucellosis, is characterized by a predominance of a T helper 1 (Th1) cellular response that is associated with IFN-γ production. Both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells are responsible for producing IFN-γ in response to B. abortus infection28,29. In the analysis of the lymphocyte subpopulation (Fig. 6C), no difference in B lymphocytes was found between the groups, but the infected sponges had a greater proportion of T lymphocytes than the uninfected sponges did (4.1% and 2.5%, respectively, p < 0.05). No difference was observed in the proportion of CD4 + lymphocytes, but there was a significantly greater percentage of CD8 + lymphocytes in sponges infected with WT B. abortus than in the control sponges (9.6% and 2.1%, respectively, p < 0.05) (Fig. 6D).

Together, these results indicate that B. abortus infection modulates the host immune response, inducing IFN-γ production, which drives an M1 macrophage phenotype, the production of inflammatory cytokines and increased microbicide activity. Therefore, the inflammatory pattern in the sponge model was altered as a result of Brucella infection. In these model, Brucella modules the host reacts to the implanted material and delays the remodeling phase, leading to a lower fibrous encapsulation of the sponge. In addition, this modulation of the inflammatory response depends on the viability and multiplication of the bacteria in the tissue, since the ΔvirB2 strain loses the ability to modulate inflammation and sponges inoculated with mutant strain have a similar pattern of inflammation to the group with uninfected sponges.

Discussion

Brucella is known as a stealth pathogen because it elicits a weak innate immune response and, consequently, a low acute inflammatory response11, but the influence of infection during the course of inflammation has not yet been investigated. This study advances our understanding of how B. abortus interacts with host inflammatory processes by demonstrating that B. abortus can modulate the inflammatory profile in a synthetic biomaterial platform and that a functional T4SS significantly interferes with inflammation and tissue repair responses in this model.

Subcutaneous implantation of sponges in mice as a synthetic matrix induces a foreign body response with an influx of inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and macrophages, proliferation of granulation tissue, and granulomatous inflammation. The inflammatory profile observed in sponges inoculated with PBS was similar to that reported in other previous studies using this synthetic biomaterial model17,23,30, even when two sponges were implanted instead of one sponge per animal.

In the synthetic matrix model in mice, infection with B. abortus initially attracts macrophages, but the properties of the cellular infiltrate change over time, demonstrating the ability of B. abortus to modulate the inflammatory response by preventing or delaying the establishment of granulomatous inflammation and granulation tissue elicited by the synthetic matrix. A foreign body response caused by the synthetic matrix induced the formation of multinucleated macrophages that were not present in sponges infected with B. abortus in this study. In mice, infection with Brucella sp. induces focal inflammation involving mainly epithelioid macrophages and neutrophils surrounded by lymphocytes, which are favorable sites for bacterial persistence in macrophage-rich organs such as the spleen and liver11,31,32. However, multinucleated giant cells are rarely described in the murine brucellosis model16. Granulomatous hepatitis is recognized as a lesion observed in brucellosis patients; however, fewer than 30% of people present with lesions containing multinucleated giant cells33.

This study contributes to the concept that Brucella sp. is a pathogenic bacterium that induces a weak or silent initial inflammatory response34–36. The stealthy profile of Brucella involves factors that prevent recognition by the host innate immune system31,35: Brucella lipopolysaccharide is a poor agonist of TLR437,38; its flagellin is a poor TLR5 agonist39; and it expresses proteins containing TIR domains, i.e., BtpA and BtpB, which are capable of interfering with the signaling of various TLR receptors, reducing the inflammatory response16,40–43.

The formation of multinucleated macrophages in the foreign body response is induced by IL4, IL13, and IL10 (Th2 profile). Conversely, the formation of multinucleated macrophages in the context of infection with intracellular pathogens such as Mycobacterium sp. is induced by IFNγ, TNFα, and LPS (Th1 profile)44. The presence of Brucella does not change the initial inflammatory response to the sponge implant; however, it alters the macrophage profile and foreign body response, which may be associated with the IFNγ cytokine profile.

Macrophages play a central role in B. abortus infection. The interaction with and regulation of macrophages by bacteria result in the long-term persistence of bacteria in the host and the establishment of chronic infection2,11. Macrophages can polarize into different functional states on the basis of the type of response generated by the immune microenvironment45,46. Brucella can prolong the life of macrophages (Mф) and prevent autophagy9,35,47. This effect was observed via cytometry, which revealed an increase in Mф macrophages in the infected group compared with those in the mock group. In the Th1 response, the production of IFN-γ favors polarization into classically activated macrophages (M1). In contrast, in a Th2 response, the differentiation of alternately activated macrophages (M2) occurs, which is associated with the production of anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1045,48. Owing to the increase in the production of IFN-γ in the infected group, greater staining of M1 macrophages than in the mock group was expected. In the mock group, the M1/M2 profile was observed, which was previously described for macrophages adhered to biomaterials49.

In the sponge model, the neutrophil is the first inflammatory cell to arrive at the site, along with plasma exudation, which is the earliest event of acute inflammation50. Neutrophils are also the first cells to phagocytose B. abortus after infection, releasing cytokines and forming extracellular neutrophil traps51. Interestingly, no difference was observed in the neutrophil profile between sponges infected with or without B. abortus. This finding indicates that the induction of the acute inflammatory response, with plasma exudation and neutrophil influx, was similar under both conditions, indicating that Brucella does not favor a greater influx of neutrophils to the site of infection. The entry of Brucella into the body is silent, without inducing inflammation in the mucous membranes or intestine, and even its spread to lymphoid organs does not induce acute local inflammation10,34. In a mouse model, B. abortus does not induce significant changes in leukocytes or recruitment of neutrophils, in addition to hindering the degranulation of these cells after intraperitoneal inoculation35. The same study also revealed insignificant levels of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 after intraperitoneal infection; however, this level of cytokine induction was dependent on the initial infective dose35. The manifestation of acute inflammation in brucellosis is only observed in pregnant animals in which abortion is a relevant clinical manifestation associated with acute, necrotizing and neutrophilic placentitis but is associated with a myriad of bacteria. The environment of the pregnant uterus seems to favor the multiplication of bacteria in trophoblasts, and the induction of the inflammatory response is well evidenced, unlike other affected organs10,52.

In addition to neutrophils and macrophages, lymphocytes are also present but in smaller proportions. B. abortus infection increased the proportion of T lymphocytes, mainly CD8 + T lymphocytes, in sponges. B. abortus was able to induce a specific adaptive immune response favoring the influx of CD8 + T lymphocytes, which did not occur in the uninfected sponge. The adaptive immune response against brucellosis is characterized by a T helper 1 (Th1) cell response, which includes the production of IFN-γ produced by CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, which activates the bactericidal functions of macrophages to limit the intracellular survival of B. abortus53–55.

In this study, we showed that B. abortus induced the production of IFN-γ, but not IL-10, at 14 dpsi. The immune response triggered by Brucella infection is related to the production of IFN-γ, which helps in the T helper 1-type immune response, a response considered protective against intracellular bacteria such as Brucella sp14,56. IL-10 is considered an immunoregulatory cytokine that is capable of inhibiting macrophage and dendritic activation and is known to affect the production of Th1 cytokines, including IFN-γ27,57. Therefore, the absence of IL-10 favors the control of B. abortus infection, reducing the bacterial load in the liver and spleen of mice; however, these organs present more severe inflammatory lesions2,27. TGF-β1 is a cytokine related to chronic inflammation, as it favors the repair process2. Increase in TGF-β1 production occurs in response to the sponge19. Interestingly, ΔvirB2 strain that does not interfere with this response, unlike what we observed in the sponge inoculated with Brucella abortus WT, where TGF-β production is reduced. A lower concentration of TGF-β1 in sponges from mice infected with WT B. abortus may be associated with impaired granulation tissue proliferation.

This study also demonstrated that the interference of B. abortus in the inflammatory response is dependent on a functional T4SS. Wild-type B. abortus was able to survive and replicate in the sponge implant and disseminate systemically, as demonstrated by bacterial recovery from the spleen. However, ΔvirB2 B. abortus was unable to persist with the infection in this model, which is in good agreement with other in vivo models of infection in which the T4SS is essential for the persistence of B. abortus in the host21,56. Although lower bacterial loads may partially account for the differences in the inflammatory profile between the WT B. abortus and ΔvirB2 groups, they do not explain the same inflammatory profile observed in sponges from the ΔvirB2 and mock groups. Previous studies have demonstrated that the T4SS is important for triggering the Brucella-elicited inflammatory response. Indeed, B. abortus can induce the expression of genes related to the IFN-γ-mediated immune response in the spleens of mice during the first days after infection, whereas T4SS-deficient mutants cannot13. In addition, increased IFN-γ production in mice infected with B. abortus with a functional T4SS- can influence the course of granulomatous inflammatory lesions14,15. Notably, BtpA and BtpB, which are Brucella effector proteins secreted by the T4SS into the cytosol of host cells, are important for chronic granulomatous inflammation in the liver in a brucellosis mouse model16.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that B. abortus 2308 interferes with the inflammatory profile and that it depends on the functional T4SS to alter the migration of inflammatory cells and the establishment of granulation tissue in a murine synthetic implant model.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture

The wild-type virulent strain B. abortus 2308 and a previously described isogenic mutant strain, ΔvirB221, were grown on TSA plates (Becton-Dickson, USA) for 4 days at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The selected colonies were resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the concentration was estimated via spectrophotometry at OD600 (SmartSpec, Bio-Rad, USA). To estimate the concentration of the inoculum, 1 OD was equivalent to 3 × 109 CFU/mL. Inocula containing 2 × 105 CFU/mL B. abortus 2308 or B. abortus ΔvirB2 were prepared. Dilutions of bacterial cultures were plated in TSA media to confirm the concentration of CFU in the inoculum. All the experimental procedures were performed in a biosafety level 3 vivarium.

Experimental design

Female BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks provided by the central vivarium (Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais – UFMG) were used in this study. The mice were housed in individual cages on a ventilated rack with an exhaust system with food and water ad libitum and maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 24 °C in a biosafety level 3 vivarium. All procedures were approved by the animal research ethics committee (CEUA-UFMG, Brazil, protocol 91/2018). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

The inflammatory response was evaluated in three experimental groups at seven and 14 days postsponge implantation (dpsi), which corresponded to 6 and 13 days after infection with B. abortus. The experimental groups included those infected with WT B. abortus 2308 (n = 10), those infected with B. abortus ΔvirB2 (ΔvirB2n = 10), and those in the mock (control) group inoculated with PBS (n = 10).

On day 0, two sponges were subcutaneously implanted in the cervical lateral region (bilaterally) of each mouse. One dpsi, the mice were manually restrained and inoculated directly into the sponge with 50 µL of PBS (mock group) or 50 µL of inoculum containing 1 × 104 CFU of WT B. abortus 2308 or B. abortus ΔvirB2 in each sponge. Five mice were euthanized at 7 or 14 dpsi, one sponge and the spleen were collected for bacteriological analysis, and the second sponge was collected for histological and biochemical analysis of the inflammatory response.

A second experiment was performed with two groups, infected with B. abortus 2308 (n = 5) or the control group inoculated with PBS (n = 5), and 14 days after implantation of the synthetic sponge (dpsi), the inflammatory cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Preparation and implantation of synthetic sponges

Discoid sponges 0.8 cm in diameter and 5 mm thick were obtained from a polyester‒polyurethane plate (Vitafoam, UK) via a surgical punch. The disk was prepared and implanted as previously described, with modifications22. The sponges were placed in 70% v/v ethanol for a minimum of 24 h and autoclaved in distilled water. For sponge implantation, the mice were anesthetized with 2% xylazine and 10% ketamine solution (16 and 100 mg/kg, respectively) intraperitoneally and subjected to trichotomy and asepsis of the dorsal cervical region for the allocation of synthetic implants in the subcutaneous area. After the incision was made, the tissue was gently separated, and two sponges were deposited, followed by surgical suturing (4–0 needled suture thread). The animals were kept under postsurgical observation and finally housed in individual cages with food and water ad libitum.

Sponge extraction

After 7 or 14 dpsi, five mice per group were euthanized with an overdose of anesthetic (2% xylazine solution, 30 mg/kg + 10% ketamine, 300 mg/kg), which was administered intraperitoneally. The implants were removed and weighed; one sponge was destined for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses, and the other sponge was destined for bacteriological and biochemical analyses.

Bacteriology

The sponges (one implant per animal) were macerated in 2 mL of sterile PBS in a 50 mL Falcon tube and then serially diluted and plated in TSA medium (Becton-Dickson, USA). After 4 days of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, colonies were counted, and the CFU/g ratios of sponge and spleen were estimated.

Histopathology

The sponges (one implant per animal) were processed via paraffin embedding, and serial sections of 4 μm were obtained and evaluated via hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining. All histopathological changes were described, and the type and intensity of the inflammatory infiltrate, as well as the formation of fibrovascular tissue, were determined.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed to detect Brucella sp. or neutrophils in the implants. Histological sections on slides were deparaffinized with two 15-minute baths and rehydrated through 3-minute baths in alcohols with decreasing concentrations, and three baths were performed with PBS for 5 min. Antigen recovery was performed with a pH 6.0 citrate buffer solution in a water bath at 95 °C for 20 min for neutrophil immunohistochemistry. The slides were placed in an endogenous peroxidase blocking solution with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 1 h and then in 6% powdered milk (Molico, Nestlé, Brazil) for 1 h to block nonspecific bonds. Then, the slides were incubated with the polyclonal primary antibody anti-Brucella sp. at a 1:1000 dilution. After washing, the slides were incubated with secondary antibodies from the Kit EnVision™ FLEX + system (Dako, USA) or biotinylated anti-mouse IgG diluted 1:50 (Zymed, USA) followed by streptavidin (Zymed, USA). The immunostaining was revealed with DAB chromogen (Dako, USA), and the slides were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. As a negative control, the primary antibody was replaced with PBS.

Morphometric analysis

Photomicrographs at 25X magnification were obtained from sections of HE-stained sponges from each mouse for morphometric analysis via a BX40 microscope (Olympus Company, Japan). The area of granulation tissue in the sponge was measured via ImageJ (SciJava). Initially, the total area of the sponge was measured three times, and then the area of the sponge filled with granulation tissue was measured three times. The percentage of the granulation tissue area of each sponge was calculated as the granulation tissue area × 100/total area.

Biochemical analysis of myeloperoxidase and N-acetylglucosaminidase activity

Neutrophil and macrophage activities were indirectly assessed in sponges by measuring myeloperoxidase (MPO) and acetylglucosaminidase (NAG) levels, respectively. After the implant was macerated in 2 mL of PBS, 1.5 mL of the macerate was centrifuged, 500 µL of the supernatant was separated for ELISA, and 250 µL of the sediment was homogenized with 750 µL of 80 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.0, for MPO analysis, and another 250 µL of the sediment was homogenized with 750 µL of saline‒Triton X‒100 solution for NAG analysis.

MPO was measured by quantifying the absorbance (optical density, OD) at 450 nm using tetramethylbenzidine (1.6 mM) and H2O2 (0.3 mM). The reaction was terminated by adding 50 mL of H2SO4 (4 M), as described by Socarrás et al.20. The NAG activity was evaluated by measuring the absorbance (OD) via p-nitrophenyl-N-acetyl-beta-D-glucosamine, as described by Socarrás et al., 201420. The results are expressed as the OD/g of sponge.

Measurement of cytokines and chemokines

The levels of TNF-α, TGF-β1, CXCL1, CCL2, IFN-γ and IL-10 were measured in this study. The implants were homogenized in 2 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 1200×g for 10 min, after which 500 µL of the supernatant was stored at – 20 °C. Dosing was performed with 50 µL of the supernatant via Immunoassay Kits (R&D Systems, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Fifty microlitres of capture antibody was added to the plates in each well, and the plate was sealed, placed in a dark, humid box at 4 °C and incubated overnight. The next day, the plates were washed with washing buffer. Subsequently, 150 µL of blocking buffer was added to each well, and the plate was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. The samples were thawed and centrifuged at 1200×g for 10 min, and the blanks, standards and samples were subsequently added to the plates and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The next day, after the plate contents were removed with washing buffer, 50 µL of biotinylated detection antibody was added to each well. Then, the conjugated streptavidin-HRP mixture was added for 20 min, followed by washing and the addition of the substrate, OPD (o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride). The reaction was stopped with stop solution (H2SO4 1 M), and the color intensity was measured at 450 nm in a spectrophotometer (E max - Molecular Devices, USA). The results are expressed as pg of cytokine/mg of sponge.

Flow cytometry

Analysis via flow cytometry to detect inflammatory cells present in sponges infected with or without B. abortus was performed. The sponges were slowly macerated in 2 mL of complete RPMI (CM: RPMI 1640, 0.1 HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1 mM nonessential amino acids and 10% fetal bovine serum [FBS]), and the obtained cells were centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 1200×g. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of complete RPMI. A total of 1 × 106 cells were homogenized with a mixture of antibodies for 30 min. To differentiate the population of leukocytes, an anti-mouse CD45 antibody conjugated with APC (cat. 101516; clone MB19-1; Biolegend) was used. Cells not considered leukocytes, debris or other cells were excluded. To differentiate the macrophage population and its subtypes, antibodies against CD45 conjugated with V500 (cat. 561487, clone 30-F11, BD Bioscience), CD11c conjugated with PE (cat. 117308, clone N418, Biolegend), APC-conjugated CD206 (cat. 141708, clone C068C2, Biolegend) and PE/Cy7-conjugated F4/80 (cat. 123114, clone BM8, Biolegend) were used. To differentiate the neutrophil population, an anti-mouse CD45 antibody conjugated with APC (cat. 101516, clone MB19-1, Biolegend) and a Ly6G antibody conjugated with APC-780 (cat. 47.5931.82, clone RB6-8C5, eBioscience) were used. FITC-conjugated CD19-conjugated anti-mouse antibody (cat. 101516, clone MB19-1, Biolegend) and CD3-conjugated anti-mouse antibody (cat. 77830, clone 17A2, Biolegend) were used to identify B cells and T cells, respectively. We used anti-CD4-conjugated PERCP Cy5.5 (cat. 77831, clone RM4-5, Biolegend) to detect helper T cells and anti-CD8-conjugated A700 (cat. 557959, clone 53–67, Biolegend) to detect cytotoxic T cells. A total of 100,000 events were analyzed by an LSRFortessa cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data analysis was performed via FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA).

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed via GraphPad Prism version 6 software. ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons was used to compare the CFU values, biochemical analysis results and morphometric measurements. The Kruskal‒Wallis test was used to compare the histopathological scores. Student’s t test was used for flow cytometry analyses. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
