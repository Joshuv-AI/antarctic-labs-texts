Antimicrobial, antioxidant, anticancer, and phytochemical analysis of South African medicinal plants collected from the Senwabarwana area, Limpopo province
Authors: Nkoana Ishmael Mongalo, Nontokozo Magwaza, Maropeng Vellry Raletsena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe antimicrobial resistance (AMR) of many microorganisms to conventional antibiotics poses a serious threat to human health. The situation is further compounded by the development of tumours arising from complex infections and vice versa due to an immunocompromised immune system. The objective of the study is to assess the antimicrobial, antioxidant, anticancer, and phytochemical components of the selected medicinal plants’ extracts.MethodsThe microdilution broth assay was used to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of the organic extracts against food-borne and human-infecting pathogens. Additionally, the anti-proliferative effects of the extracts were evaluated in vitro using a tetrazolium-based calorimetric (MTT) assay against a line of human colorectal carcinoma (Caco-2), human breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7), and human cervical cancer cells (HeLa). Additionally, 2,2-Diphenyl-1-Picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS), and possible iron chelation were used to measure the extracts’ antioxidant activity. Gas chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF-MS) was used to evaluate the phytochemical analysis.ResultsTalinum caffrum (Thumb. Eckl. & Zeyhr.) extract exhibited the lowest minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) value of 0.05 mg/ml against Salmonella arizonae, while extracts from Cassia abbreviata Oliv. subsp. beareana (Holmes) Brenan and Peltophorum africanum Sond. exhibited a similar MIC value against Shigella flexneri. P. africanum extracts exhibited a potent inhibition of the MCF-7 cell line, yielding an IC50 value of 1.80 µg/ml. In comparison, T. caffrum extracts exhibited an LC50 value of 5.66 µg/ml against the HeLa cell line and a notable LC50 value of 1.12 and 1.11 µg/ml against DPPH and Fe2+, respectively. The organic extract from T. caffrum contains 4-methyl-2,4-bis(p-hydroxyphenyl) pent-1-ene (20.803%), 2-furancarboxylic acid, 2-methylbutyl ester (16.909%), and 2-methylbutyl ester (13.702%).ConclusionThe selected medicinal plants exhibited a potent antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anticancer activity. The observed results, in a way, support the use of the selected medicinal plant species in treating various infections, as complementary and alternative medicines, particularly against opportunistic microbes and cancer by improving and supporting the immune system through many means, such as quenching free radicals, alleviating tumour and microbial growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05325-2.Keywords: Ethnomedicine, antimicrobial, GC-TOF-MS, anticancer, free radicals

Introduction

The use of African medicinal plants in treating various human and animal diseases remains relevant, even in the fourth industrial revolution era [1]. This relevance is due to the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) of many microbes, which can develop resistance genes to antibiotics used in many healthcare facilities. The supply chain system may be a source of resistance, resulting from improper prescription practices, poor patient education, lack of diagnostic facilities, sale of antibiotics without proper authorization, ineffective drug regulatory systems, and the use of antibiotics for purposes other than human health, such as in animal production [2]. It is estimated that AMR attributed 1.27 million deaths in 2019 worldwide [3]. Elsewhere, other authors estimated about 700,000 deaths worldwide on an annual basis and refer to AMR as a major threat to many health care facilities, hence needs urgent attention [4]. It is important to note that many AMR microbes have been isolated from cancer patients, and this is due to the immunosuppression arising from cancer, thereby making it easier for microbes to manifest [5]. These plants, used alone or in combination, combat disease progression through different mechanisms, such as inhibiting cell wall or protein synthesis, causing leakage from cells by disrupting membrane functions, interfering with intermediary metabolism, or affecting DNA/RNA synthesis and function [6].

Normal cells only procreate in response to signals from nearby cells. Each tissue maintains a size and architecture suitable for the body’s requirements. Cancer cells, on the other hand, defy this normal body functioning and follow their own internal reproduction agenda and become deaf to the normal restrictions on proliferation [7]. Their capacity to spread from the original location, infiltrating neighbouring tissues and accumulating into masses at other body parts inside the body, relocating to new metastatic sites that lead to circulating tumour cells (CTCs), resulting in multiple types of cancers [8]. Bacterial strains such as Helicobacter pylori have been recently pointed out as some facilitators for the movement of these cancerous cells to other body parts, particularly in gastric ulcer and breast cancer patients [9]. The infected individual rapidly becomes prone to various infections arising from various sources that include microorganisms and free radicals, which enter the body from the environment or hereditary means [10]. These infections may cohabit in a patient, hence the need to explore the anticancer, antimicrobial, and antioxidant activity of medicinal plants is paramount to curbing coinfections rather than one infection at a time.

Free radicals are unstable, transient, and extremely reactive substances due to one or more unpaired electrons in their valence shell. Overproduction of these free radicals eventually results in oxidative stress, which damages and oxidizes important macromolecules in the biological system and fundamentally upsets the balance of antioxidants and signal transduction pathways [11]. Free radicals can raise the risk of cancer by triggering the transcription of the original cancer genes [12]. Changes in cancer-related genes and proteins, which are linked to free-radical stress, are more common in people with inflammatory disorders that are linked to cancer, such as ulcerative colitis, hemochromatosis, and viral hepatitis [13]. In the current work, the selected medicinal plant extracts are evaluated for antimicrobial activity against selected microbes. Furthermore, both the anticancer and antioxidant activities are evaluated in vitro. The phytochemical constituents are detected using gas chromatography mass spectrometry.

Materials and methods

Plant collection and identification

The selected medicinal plants (Table 1), showing the sampled plant parts, were randomly selected from a large plant species gathered through our ethnomedicinal survey projects. Our Chief Conservation, George Tladi Mongalo, granted permission to collect the medicinal plants from 2017 to 2020 at the Broekman tribal authority. The voucher specimens were collected and identified by Mr. Paulus Nkurwana Sebothoma, head of Plant ID at the National Botanical Institute in Pretoria, South Africa, and stored in the Herbarium at the Horticulture Centre, University of South Africa, Science campus in Florida Park, Johannesburg.

Medicinal plants extraction

The plants were separately ground into fine powder using a Scientec Hammer mill (Scientec, Germany) to 2 mm particles, which were then extracted with 70% ethanol at a 5:1 v/w ratio, using LM-600RD Orbital shaker, Yihder Technology CO., LTD (Xinbei city, China) at 30 °C overnight (12 h) shaken at 100 RPM. The resulting extracts were filtered through Whatman’s No. 1 filter paper (Merck, South Africa) and evaporated using the Genevac EZ-2 Plus (Warminster, USA) at 40 °C overnight.

Microorganisms and culture conditions

A total of 7 pathogenic microbes causing various illnesses were selected for the current study and were purchased from Davies Diagnostics (Randburg, South Africa), as pellets. Fungal strains such as Candida albicans (ATCC 24433) and bacterial strains such as Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCC 13883), Proteus mirabilis (ATCC 29906), Shigella flexneri (12022), Salmonella arizonae (13314), and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 6538) were also selected. The fungus C. albicans was maintained on Potato Dextrose agar (PDA), while all the bacterial strains were maintained on Muller-Hinton agar (MHA). All the growth media used in the study were obtained from Sigma Aldrich, Germany.

Antimicrobial activity

Antifungal activity

The method adopted from Masoko et al. [25] was used to determine the antifungal activity of the extracts from the selected medicinal plants against Candida albicans. As in the antibacterial activity, the extracts were serially diluted, and a similar amount of the fungal inoculum concentration was added. Each well then received 40 µl of freshly prepared iodo-nitro-tetrazolium chloride (Fluka) at a concentration of 0.2 mg/ml, after which the plates were incubated for 24 h period. Amphotericin B served as a control. The MIC was then determined as in the antibacterial assay.

Antibacterial activity

With minor modifications, the broth microdilution test as outlined by Eloff [26] was used to examine the antibacterial activity of the chosen extracts. Fresh Muller-Hinton broth (MHB) was used to dilute the overnight cultures of independently grown microorganisms to approximately 1.1 × 107 CFU/ml. In a 96-well microtiter plate containing 100 µl of sterile distilled water, 100 µl of extracts (50 mg/ml in 5% DMSO) were added and serially diluted. Each well received 100 µl of a known concentration of bacterial culture. Gentamycin and streptomycin served as positive controls. Plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight. Each well then received 40 µl of freshly prepared iodo-nitro-tetrazolium chloride (Fluka) at a concentration of 0.2 mg/ml, and the wells were incubated for 30 min at the same temperature. The extract’s minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), as determined by interpreting the data, was defined as the lowest concentration at which microorganism growth is inhibited.

Antiproliferative effects

MTT assay

The tetrazolium-based colorimetric (MTT) method, adapted from Mosmann [25], was used to investigate the anticancer activity of the selected medicinal plant extracts [27]. Wells in columns 1 and 12 received only 200 µl DMEM to minimize the edge effect and maintain a relative humidity in a sterile laminar flow cabinet. After that, the plates were incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator (Listed Laboratory Equipment, USA) for 24 h at 37 °C until the cells reached their exponential growth phase. After aspirating the MEM from the cells into a microplate, it was washed with 150 µl of fresh phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Whitehead Scientific) and then filled with 200 µl of the chosen test plant extracts in quadruplicate at different concentrations. All test extracts were serially diluted in new MEM. The cells were not disturbed as much as possible when the media was aspirated, and test extracts were added. The medicinal plant extract was incubated on the microtiter plates for 48 h at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2. Included as negative and positive controls, respectively, were untreated cells and doxorubicin chloride from Pfizer Laboratories. All plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C after 30 µl of MTT (Sigma, stock solution of 5 mg/ml in PBS) was applied to each well in accordance with the incubation schedule. Following MTT incubation, the medium was carefully withdrawn from each well without causing any disruption to the MTT crystals in any of the wells. 50 µl of brand-new DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the MTT formazan crystals. A shaker (IKA KS 130B, Laboratory Equipment, Germany) was used to gently shake the plates until the MTT solution was fully dissolved. By measuring absorbance at a wavelength of 540 nm in a microplate reader (Chromate 4300), the degree of MTT reduction was promptly determined. The percentage of cell viability was calculated using the formula below:

%Cell viability=[Mean absorbance of sample/Mean absorbance of control]×100 [28].

The concentration of each test plant extract that caused a 50% decrease in absorbance when compared to untreated cells was determined by calculating the LC50 values, after plotting the % cell viability against the concentrations of plant extracts used. Only R2 values > 0.9 were acceptable.

Antioxidant activity

2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)

The extracts from the four medicinal plants were tested for their ability to scavenge free radicals against 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) using a slightly modified version of the procedure previously reported by Opoku et al. [29]. Two milligrams of DPPH per 100 milliliters of solution were obtained by dissolving DPPH purple powder in methanol (AR grade, Sigma Aldrich, Germany). In summary, 96-well plates were serially diluted with equal volumes of methanol containing 150 µl of each extract at a concentration of 5 mg/100 ml. Following the addition of 150 µl of the DPPH solution to each well, the plates were wrapped in foil, shaken for ten minutes, and then set on an IKA KS laboratory equipment shaker (Germany) set at 80 rpm. The dishes were then wrapped in foil and left to incubate for an hour in the dark. As a negative control, wells with a combination of 150 µl of DPPH and 150 µl of methanol were employed. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader (Varioshskan Flash, Thermo Scientific Amsterdam).

2,2'-azino-di-[3-ethylbenzthiazolinesulfonate (ABTS) assay

The free radical scavenging activity of extracts from four plants was tested against 2,2'-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS) using a method adapted from Re et al. [30]. The 7 mM ABTS tablet was dissolved in 3.45 mM potassium persulfate and 3 ml of sterile distilled water. The solution was incubated for 16 hours at room temperature. Like the DPPH assay, the extracts were serially diluted and mixed with 150 μl of ABTS solution, then incubated for 6 minutes in the dark while being vigorously shaken at 80 rpm (IKA KS, Laboratory equipment, Germany). The absorption at 734 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (Varioshskan Flash, Thermo Scientific Amsterdam).

Chelation of iron

Extracts from the four plant species were tested for iron chelation activity using a slightly modified Decker and Wielch [31] technique. The plant extracts were serially diluted with methanol, just like in the DPPH experiment. About 100 µl of sterile distilled water, 10 µl of FeCl2 (2 mM), and 20 µl of disodium-4-[3-pyridin-2-yl-6-(4- 4-sulfonato phenyl)-1,2,4-triazin-5-yl] benzenesulfonate (5 mM) were used to further dilute the extracts in each well. A microplate reader (Varioshkan Flash, Thermo Scientific, Amsterdam) was then used to measure the absorbance at 562 nm after the plate mixture had been covered with foil and shaken at 80 rpm (IKA KS, Laboratory equipment, Germany) for roughly ten minutes.

Each of the three antioxidant assays’ percentages of inhibition was then computed using the formula.

Scavenging Inhibition = [1-At/A0]X 100 [32],

At represents the absorbance of the test sample, and A0 represents the absorbance of the control solution. Graphs made with the GraphPad Prism software were used to extrapolate the IC50, or inhibitory concentration of 50% of the test radical, which is the concentration of the plant sample that inhibits 50% of the test radical. Ascorbic acid and azelaic acid were used as positive controls in all antioxidant experiments, while untreated wells were used as negative controls.

Gas chromatography mass spectrometry analysis

Talinum caffrum organic extract demonstrated potential antimicrobial and antioxidant activity, along with selective inhibition of the HeLa cell line. Consequently, it was chosen for phytochemical analysis using GC-TOF-MS. The analysis followed the method used in our previous studies [33, 34], with minor modifications. About 0.01 mg of the extract was completely dissolved in acetonitrile (GC-MS grade, Sigma Aldrich, Germany) to prepare a 1 mg/ml solution. Phytocompounds were separated using gas chromatography (8890 GC-TOF-MS, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) equipped with a LECO Pegasus BT 4D Flight Mass Spectrometer (TOF-MS) from LECO Corporation, Michigan, USA. The prepared sample was loaded into the preheated Gerstel MPS2 Liquid/HS/SPME autosampler. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an AJ & W Rxi-5Sil MS capillary column (30 × 0.25 mm I.D., about 0.25 μm film thickness). The sample (1 µl at 1 mg/ml) was injected at 250 °C with a splitless injector, and the GC oven was programmed to start at 70 °C for 0.5 min, then ramped at 10 °C per minute to 150 °C and held for 2 min. It was then ramped again at 10 °C per minute to 330 °C, and held for 3 min, for a total run time of 31.5 min. Helium (purchased from Afrox, Johannesburg, RSA, with 99.99% purity) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow of 1 ml/min until separation was complete. The GC-TOF-MS interface temperature was maintained at 250 °C, and mass spectra were acquired in full scan mode at 70 eV (m/z scan range from 50 to 600). Data collection was performed using ChromaTOF 5.55.29.01.1187, which incorporates the NIST MS 2.4 library with 95% similarity matching.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were carried out in triplicate. The results for antimicrobial assays were recorded as the mean of three independent replicates. In contrast, the antioxidant and anticancer results were analyzed using Prism 7.05 and Microsoft Excel 2017, and the data are presented as IC50 ± SE. P-values of ≤ 0.05 were referred to as significant. ChromaTOF 5.55.29.01.1187, which incorporates the NIST MS 2.4 library with 95% similarity matching, was used to analyse the data from GC-TOF-MS phytochemical analysis.

Cell culture and culture conditions

Human breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7) cells, human cervical cancer cells (HeLa), and human colorectal carcinoma cells (Caco-2) as ATCC (American Type Culture Collection) strains obtained from Thermofischer Scientific, South Africa, were incubated separately in 96-well microtiter plates with 70% ethanol extracts redissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, VWR, Life Science, USA). Each cell line’s cells were aseptically extracted from a subconfluent culture, spun for 5 min at 1000 rpm, and then resuspended in a new growth medium at a density of approximately 5 × 104 cells per milliliter. The growth medium used was Minimal Essential Medium (MEM), which was supplemented with 0.1% gentamicin (Whitehead Scientific, South Africa) and 5% foetal calf serum (Highveld Biological).

Results

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of the selected medicinal plants was tested in vitro against various pathogenic microbes (Table 2). Shigella flexneri, Proteus mirabilis, and Staphylococcus aureus were the most susceptible strains to the extracts compared to other microorganisms. Cassia abbreviata extracts showed the lowest minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.5 mg/ml against S. flexneri and Salmonella arizonae. In contrast, Talinum caffrum and Peltophorum africanum extracts had similar MIC values against S. arizonae and S. flexneri, respectively. Additionally, T. caffrum demonstrated an MIC of 0.20 mg/ml against both S. flexneri and S. aureus. P. africanum extract exhibited an MIC of 0.20 mg/ml against microbes such as Candida albicans, Klebsiella pneumoniae, S. aureus, and S. arizonae. Sclerocarya birrea exhibited an MIC value of 0.20 mg/ml against three microbes, such as P. mirabilis, S. flexneri, and S. aureus. Candida albicans was susceptible to Amphotericin B (control drug). At the same time, the bacterial strains were susceptible to both neomycin and streptomycin, which were used as control drugs against the selected bacterial strains.

Anticancer activity

The anticancer properties of the selected medicinal plant extracts are summarized below (Table 3). Peltophorum africanum and Doxurubucin significantly (P-value ≤ 0.005) showed the lowest anticancer activity against the MCF-7 cell line, with an IC50 value of 1.80 ± 0.001 µg/ml and 1.88 ± 0.001 µg/ml, respectively. Furthermore, Sclerocarya birrea and Talinum caffrum significantly (P-value ≤ 0.005) exhibited IC50 values of 44.14 ± 0.001 and 35.66 ± 0.004 µg/ml against HeLa cells, while Cassia abbreviata and P. africanum extracts insignificantly showed IC50 values of 488.82 ± 8.64 and 230.84 ± 6.83 µg/ml, respectively, against a similar cell line (P-value ≥ 0.005). C. abbreviata extracts did not show significant inhibition of Caco-2 cells, with an IC50 greater than 1000 µg/ml, while P. africanum and S. birrea had IC50 values of 112.03 ± 0.12 and 121.11 ± 0.08 µg/ml against the same cell line. Furthermore, the Caco-2 cell line was found to be unsusceptible to the extracts from the selected medicinal plant extracts compared to other cell lines.

Antioxidant activity

In the antioxidant assays (Table 4), T. caffrum significantly (P-value ≤ 0.05) exhibited a notable inhibition of ABTS and Fe2+, yielding IC50 values of 1.12 ± 0.001 and 1.11 ± 0.001 µg/ml, respectively. In contrast, both C. abbreviata and S. birrea significantly exhibited notable IC50 values of 1.44 ± 0.01 and 1.40 ± 0.004 µg/ml against ABTS free radical, respectively. S. birrea further exhibited the lowest IC50 value of 2.62 ± 0.04 µg/ml against DPPH free radical, compared to other medicinal plants. Except for C. abbreviata extracts, the extracts from the selected medicinal plants exhibited potent inhibition of Fe2+ compared to the control drug, azelaic acid. Besides Peltophorum africanum extracts against DPPH (IC50 = 4.80 ± 0.066 µg/ml), all the extracts from the selected medicinal plants significantly (P-value ≤ 0.05) inhibited DPPH, ABTS, and Fe2+.

Phytochemical analysis of Talinum caffrum

The organic extract from Talinum caffrum showed strong antibacterial activity, especially against Shigella flexneri, Salmonella arizonae, Proteus mirabilis, and Staphylococcus aureus. Additionally, the extract demonstrated potent anticancer activity by selectively inhibiting the growth of HeLa cell lines and exhibited antioxidant activity through ABTS and iron chelation, leading to its selection for phytochemical analysis. A total of 127 compounds from various groups were identified in the organic extract of T. caffrum (Table 5). The most abundant compounds by percentage area include 4-Methyl-2,4-bis(p-hydroxyphenyl) pent-1-ene, 2TMS derivative (20.893%), 2-Furancarboxylic acid, 2-methylbutyl ester (16.909%), 1,2,4-Benzenetricarboxylic acid, 1,2-dimethyl ester (13.702%), Glycolamide (2.103%), and Methyl acetoxyacetate (2.103%).

Discussions

In recent years, antibiotic resistance has become widespread in both developed and underdeveloped countries. Enzymatic degradation, target modification, decreased absorption, and overexpression of efflux pump proteins are the most common mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance [35]. The extracts from the selected medicinal plants showed varying levels of antimicrobial activity against the studied pathogens (Table 2). Besides Escherichia coli, all the other pathogens were susceptible to at least one plant extract, with Staphylococcus aureus, Shigella flexneri, and Proteus mirabilis being more susceptible to the tested plant extracts. Recently, these microbes have been frequently found in wounds and urinary tract infections (UTI) in clinical settings and are resistant to numerous antibiotics, including Penicillin, Ampicillin, Tetracycline, Gentamycin, Chloramphenicol, Streptomycin, Erythromycin, Kanamycin, Oxacillin, Norfloxacillin, Ciprofloxacin, Amikacin, and Imipenem [36–38]. Talinum caffrum extract showed the lowest minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.05 mg/ml against Salmonella arizonae, while extracts from Cassia abbreviata and Peltophorum africanum exhibited similar MIC values against Shigella flexneri. It is important to note that there are no established standards for the antimicrobial susceptibility of plant extracts [39–42]. However, in this study, plant extracts with MIC values of ≤ 0.20 mg/ml were considered potent inhibitors of microbial growth, while those with MIC values ≥ 0.20 mg/ml were considered inactive [43]. Additionally, extracts with MIC values of ≤ 0.10 mg/ml were classified as strong inhibitors of the selected microorganisms [44]. Judging by these standards, C. abbreviata exhibited a strong antimicrobial activity against both S. flexneri and A. arizonae, while T. caffrum and P. africanum exhibited stronger antimicrobial activity against S. arizonae and S. flexneri, respectively. Although these plant extracts exhibited stronger antimicrobial activity, their MIC values were relatively higher compared to the control drugs used in the study. However, it should be noted that the use of plant extracts may have multiple binding sites for microbes, compared to control drugs [45]. It is further important to note that potency (0.05 mg/ml) remains highly relevant, as this is 50 µg/ml of crude extract, indicating significant activity of the bulk material. It is important to note that the mode of action of these plant species still needs to be explored and further determine the safest and effective dosages.

Recently, Mongalo and Raletsena [14], comprehensively documented the antimicrobial activity of Peltophorum africanum from the literature. Interestingly, the 1:1 methanol: dichloromethane extract exhibited an MIC value of 1.0 mg/ml against Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus, while the methanol extracts exhibited an MIC value of 0.02 mg/ml against Cryptococcus neoformans. Elsewhere, the 70% methanolic stem bark extracts exhibited an MIC value of 1.6 mg/ml against both Neisseria gonorrhoea and Oligella ureolytica [46–48]. Recently, Mnisi et al. isolated compounds such as 3-octadecanoyl stigmasterol and Betulinic acid-3-3,4-dihydroxybenzoate from P. africanum stem bark, with potent antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [49]. The antimicrobial efficacy of Cassia abbreviata has been summarized [50]. The roots extracted with hexane, ethanol, methanol, dichloromethane, and both tap and distilled water exhibited MIC value of 0.02 mg/ml against Candida albicans, while the stem bark extracted with acetone exhibited an MIC value of 0.5 mg/ml against the drug-sensitive Mycobacterium tuberculosis (H37Rv) strain and a further 0.5 mg/ml against Bacillus cereus, Bacillus pumilis, Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis. Various extracts from the stem bark of Sclerocarya birrea exhibited some potent antimicrobial activity against various drug-resistant strains, including gastrointestinal strains [51–53]. The aqueous and acetone extracts from the stem bark exhibited potent antimicrobial activity against C. albicans and Mycoplasma hominis, yielding MIC values of 0.1 and 0.05 mg/ml, respectively [54, 55]. Elsewhere, Cassinidin A, isolated from the roots of C. abbreviata, exhibited an MIC value of 0.5 µg/ml against both Bacillus subtilis and Staphylococcus aureus and a notable MIC of 0.1 µg/ml against Candida mycoderma, while Cassinidin B yielded a MIC value of 0.5 µg/ml against C. mycoderma [56]. The aqueous extracts from Tallinum caffrum bulbs exhibited an MIC value of 2.50 mg/ml against various Mycobacterium strains such as Mycobacterium bovis, Mycobacterium smegmatis, Mycobacterium gordonae, and Mycobacterium aurum [57]. Although there is a difference in terms of microbes used, plant collection site, and other environmental factors, these reported bioactivity supports the findings of the current study.

The anticancer activity of the extracts from the selected medicinal plants has been explored (Table 3). P. africanum extracts exhibited a significant anticancer activity against the MCF-7 cell line, yielding an IC50 value of 1.80 µg/ml, while both S. birrea and T. caffrum extracts exhibited noteworthy inhibition of HeLa cells, yielding an IC50 value of 44.14 and 35.66 µg/ml, respectively. All three plants selectively inhibit the said cancerous cell lines in vitro. It is important to note that there is no clear-cut off point for the anticancer effects of various botanicals. However, some authors proposed that a good anticancer extract is thought to have an IC50 value of 20 µg/ml, while others consider an IC50 value of 30 µg/ml serves as a cutoff threshold for cytotoxic plant extracts to be considered for further purification and mode of actions in the in vitro anticancer screenings [13, 48]. Elsewhere, Kuete and Efferth [58] proposed significant or strong cytotoxicity: IC50 < 20 µg/mL; moderate cytotoxicity: 20 µg/ml < IC50 < 50 µg/ml; low cytotoxicity: 50 µg/mL< IC50 < 200 µg/ml. Judging by this standard, both T. caffrum and S. birrea extracts had a moderate inhibition of HeLa cells, while P. africanum extracts exhibited a strong inhibition of MCF-7 tumour cells. Furthermore, C. abbreviata extracts did not reveal any cytotoxicity to the selected cancer cells.

Earlier, the ethyl acetate extract from the P. africanum stem bark was evaluated for anticancer effects against some cancerous cell lines. The stem bark ethyl acetate extract reduced the viable cell counts in the human breast (MCF-7), colon (HT-29), and cervical (HeLa) to 48.38, 62.36, and 76.10%, respectively, at a dose of 25 µg/mL after 24 h of treatment [59, 60]. It is significant to highlight that because of the difference in cell lines, the solvent of extraction used, and the LC50 values, which were not documented, these results could not be comparable to our study.

Nevertheless, in their study, the altered control shriveled with membrane blebbing, loss of flattened morphology, and separated apoptotic bodies due to cytoplasm retraction around the nucleus were observed in the MCF-7 cells, which might be the possible mode of action in our study against a similar cell line. Although these plants, particularly P. africanum, exhibited excellent anticancer effects, their mode of action needs to be explored. According to literature sources, cell cycle arrest and promoting apoptosis are the most common modes of action against tumour cell lines [61]. Elsewhere, the aqueous root bark extracts from C. abbreviata exhibited IC50 values of 1.49 µg/ml against the hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell line [62].

To act as natural antioxidants and prevent oxidative stress brought on by reactive oxygen species, plants produce significant and/or abundant amounts of a variety of compounds during biotic and abiotic stress. This protects plant cells from oxidation and cell damage [63]. The antioxidant activity of the selected medicinal plant extracts against 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2ˊ-azino-di--[3-ethylbenzthiazoline sulfonate (ABTS), and chelation of iron (Fe2+) have been studied (Table 4). Talinum caffrum extract significantly exhibited the lowest IC50 values of 1.12 and 1.11 µg/ml against ABTS and Fe2+, respectively, while Slerocarrya birrea extracts exhibited the lowest IC50 value of 2.62 against a stable DPPH free radical. According to Nwachuku et al [64], a good antioxidant should be highly effective in at least two to three different assays. The lower the number, the more effective and potent the antioxidant property of the extract is considered [65]. According to Nacer et al. [66], plant extracts yielding IC50 values of ≤ 20 µg/ml are considered excellent antioxidants that could easily destabilize the free radicals in the body, thereby curbing the onset and or progression of many different devastating illnesses. Judging by these standards [67–69], T. caffrum extracts exhibited a potent antioxidant activity compared to the other medicinal plants. However, the extract exhibited antioxidant activity less than that of ascorbic and azelaic acid, used as positive control drugs in the study. Comparatively, the experimental study of all extracts revealed the following rank order in terms of IC50 value for all plant extracts examined: DPPH (3.7 µg/ml), Fe2+ (2.57 µg/ml), and ABTS (1.54 µg/ml). Elsewhere, other authors reported a contradictory trend to our findings [70–72].

Besides selectively inhibiting the growth of HeLa cells (IC50 = 35.66 µg/ml), T. caffrum exhibited potent antioxidant activity compared to other selected medicinal plants, hence selected for gas chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF-MS). The extract yielded major compounds such as 4-Methyl-2,4-bis(p-hydroxyphenyl) pent-1-ene, 2TMS derivative (20.893%), 2-Furancarboxylic acid, 2-methylbutyl ester (16.909%), 1,2,4-Benzenetricarboxylic acid, 1,2-dimethyl ester (10.684%), 1,2,4-Benzenetricarboxylic acid, 1,2-dimethyl ester (3.026%), Glycolamide (2.103%), and Methyl acetoxyacetate (2.103%) as bold faceted in Table 4. Earlier, 4-methyl-2,4-bis(p-hydroxyphenyl) pent-1-ene (2TMS derivative) was recently identified using GC-MS and reportedly linked with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) biomarkers that are lacking in clinical practice [73]. Furthermore, many authors report the compound to possess enormous anticancer activity through inducing apoptosis in different cancer cells [74–76]. Other authors reported a similar compound to yield potent antimicrobial and antioxidant activity in vitro [77–79]. The biological activity, particularly of T. caffrum may well be explained by this major compound, individually or in combination with the other compounds that yielded a higher percentage area.

Limitations of the study

The current work was limited only to the antiproliferative effects of the plant extracts not the normal cell lines, with the prospects of isolating and characterising the anticancer compounds from the promising plant extracts. Furthermore, the MBC and MFC were also a limitation for the study.

Conclusions and prospects

The selected medicinal plants showed varying levels of antimicrobial activity against specific microorganisms, especially against Salmonella arizonae, Shigella flexneri, Proteus mirabilis, and Staphylococcus aureus, with MIC values as low as 0.05 to 0.20 mg/ml. The data indicate that these medicinal plants could potentially be used as complementary and alternative medicines for treating various infections, including gastrointestinal and foodborne diseases. Additionally, the extracts, particularly those from Talinum caffrum, demonstrated strong antioxidant activity and may be useful against radicals that can cause multiple infections in humans. The extracts could also be used to alleviate a plethora of various forms of cancers and their opportunistic infections associated with AMR due to immunosuppression in patients. Furthermore, besides AMR, various forms of cancers, the plant extracts could well boost the immune system due to their potent antioxidant activity, particularly against ABTS and Fe2+. However, the mechanisms of action, toxicity, and appropriate dosages still need further research. The observed biological activities, especially those from T. caffrum, may be linked to phytochemicals identified through GC-TOF-MS analysis. In our future studies, we will be isolating and characterising potent anticancer compounds from the plant species with promising anticancer activity. Such anticancer activity will be compared to the cytotoxicity of the compounds against the normal cell lines.
