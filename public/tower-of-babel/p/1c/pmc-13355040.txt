A review of reconfigurable metasurfaces for radar applications
Authors: Boyang Qian, Hui Chu, Yong-Xin Guo, Zhenyu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryReconfigurable metasurfaces (RMSs) have emerged as powerful platforms for programmable manipulation of electromagnetic waves. Although widely studied in communications, imaging, and information security, RMSs have recently gained growing attention in radar systems. This growing interest calls for a systematic and application-oriented synthesis of metasurface-enabled radar technologies. In this review, we present a comprehensive overview of RMS-enabled radar applications from both functional and architectural perspectives. Recent advances are organized into five major domains: radar signal generation and modulation, radar deception and jamming, scattering control and stealth, direction-of-arrival estimation, and joint radar–communication systems. For each domain, we summarize the operating principles, functional roles, and representative system implementations of RMSs, highlighting how programmable electromagnetic interfaces are reshaping conventional radar architectures. Finally, we discuss key challenges related to performance limitations and outline future research directions. This review provides a structured and application-driven perspective on the evolving role of RMS in next-generation radar systems.

Introduction

Metasurfaces, regarded as two-dimensional analogs of metamaterials, have emerged as powerful platforms for manipulating electromagnetic waves at subwavelength scales.1 Composed of periodic or quasi-periodic arrays of engineered subwavelength scatterers, these planar structures enable precise control over the amplitude, phase, and polarization of electromagnetic waves, offering unprecedented flexibility in wavefront engineering.2,3 Compared with their three-dimensional metamaterial counterparts, metasurfaces feature an ultrathin profile, low weight, and compatibility with modern electronic and photonic fabrication technologies.4,5 These characteristics have stimulated extensive research and practical interest in beam steering, focusing, imaging, wireless communications, and electromagnetic stealth.6,7,8,9,10

In recent years, metasurfaces have evolved from static, passive devices11 into dynamic and reconfigurable systems capable of real-time electromagnetic control.12 Traditional metasurfaces are defined by fixed geometric parameters that determine a single electromagnetic response, limiting their adaptability to changing operational or environmental conditions. Reconfigurable metasurfaces (RMSs), in contrast, integrate tunable elements within their unit cells, such as PIN diodes,13,14,15,16,17,18 varactor diodes,5,19,20 liquid crystals,21,22,23,24,25,26 and phase-change materials including VO2.27,28,29,30 Under electrical,8,9,12 optical,24,31,32,33,34,35,36 or thermal excitation.37,38,39,40,41,42 These elements dynamically modulate surface impedance and scattering characteristics, enabling programmable control over the phase, amplitude, and polarization of reflected or transmitted waves. This evolution transforms metasurfaces from passive wave manipulators into actively reconfigurable electromagnetic interfaces.

The emergence of RMS has opened new opportunities for radar systems, where adaptability, rapid response, and multifunctionality are increasingly critical. Modern radar platforms generally operate in dense electromagnetic environments, detect fast-moving or stealthy targets, and integrate sensing, communication, and electronic countermeasure functionalities within compact hardware architectures. Conventional radar systems43,44 achieve such capabilities through mechanical beam steering, phased-array control, or frequency-agile transmitters, often at the expense of increased hardware complexity and power consumption. In contrast, RMS provides a compact, lightweight, and digitally controllable alternative capable of agile manipulation of radar waveforms, scattering responses, and spatial radiation characteristics in real time.45,46,47 Their planar and programmable nature offers an effective pathway toward miniaturized and multifunctional radar systems that combine agility, intelligence, and cost efficiency.

RMSs have demonstrated significant potential across a broad range of radar applications, offering unprecedented capabilities in dynamic beam control, target detection, and multifunctional system integration. One of the most actively explored directions involves radar signal generation and modulation, as shown in Figure 1A, where RMSs can directly synthesize or modulate electromagnetic waveforms on their surfaces. By dynamically programming the surface phase distribution, RMS enables the generation of diverse radar waveforms, including frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW), linear FMCW (LFMCW), and chirp signals.48 This enables flexible waveform synthesis without conventional high-frequency sources or mixers, allowing compact and energy-efficient transmitter designs. Furthermore, RMS can implement on-surface signal processing functions, such as dechirping and frequency translation,49,50 thereby simplifying the radar receiver chain and alleviating the computational burden of signal demodulation. Collectively, these waveform-engineering capabilities not only enhance radar performance in range and velocity measurement but also enable adaptive modulation schemes that respond to changing target or environment conditions.Figure 1Reconfigurable metasurfaces for radar applications(A) Signal generation and modulation.(B) Radar deception and jamming.(C) Radar stealth and scattering control.(D) Radar DOA estimation.(E) Joint radar sensing and communication.

Beyond waveform synthesis, RMSs introduce new physical-layer mechanisms for radar deception and jamming. As illustrated in Figure 1B, through programmable control of reflection phase, amplitude, and frequency, metasurfaces can synthesize deceptive echoes and generate controllable false targets.51 One-dimensional deception strategies allow manipulation of target observables such as range-shifting, velocity-based false target generation, and high-resolution range profiles (HRRP).52 These concepts have been extended to two-dimensional imaging domains, including synthetic aperture radar (SAR) and inverse synthetic aperture radar (ISAR) image domains,53,54 allowing entire radar scenes to be synthetically reconstructed or distorted in real time. In addition, RMS can realize spectrum spreading and waveform perturbation to reduce the coherence of enemy radar returns,55 functioning as agile and low-power jamming systems. These features establish RMS as a key technology for next-generation electronic warfare systems that require real-time, multifunctional deception and interference capabilities.

Another major research direction lies in radar stealth and cloaking, as shown in Figure 1C, where RMS enables adaptive management of radar cross-section (RCS) and scattering patterns. Traditional stealth technologies rely on fixed geometric shaping or broadband absorptive materials,56,57,58,59,60,61 which provide limited adaptability in dynamic or complex electromagnetic environments. In contrast, RMS can dynamically modulate reflection amplitude and phase distributions, enabling real-time RCS reduction tailored to specific operational scenarios. Recent studies have demonstrated programmable stealth metasurfaces capable of dynamically adjusting their absorption performance to minimize detectability.62,63,64 Such adaptive stealth mechanisms are highly desirable for modern aircraft, maritime, and ground-based radar systems that require sustained low observability under rapidly changing conditions.65,66,67

As illustrated in Figure 1D, reconfigurable metasurfaces also provide new opportunities for direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation, a critical function in radar and wireless sensing.68 By reprogramming their phase and amplitude distributions, reconfigurable metasurfaces can function as dynamically tunable apertures or coding arrays, enabling angularly selective scattering responses for spatial information extraction.69,70 This dynamic spatial control enhances angular resolution and robustness to interference while reducing the complexity and cost of traditional multi-element arrays. RMS-based DOA systems can be further combined with artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms for intelligent beam scanning, environmental sensing, and adaptive target localization.71,72

Beyond conventional radar functionalities, as shown in Figure 1E, RMS plays a central role in advancing joint radar sensing and communication (Rad-Com) systems.73,74 As wireless technologies evolve toward integrated sensing and communication in the 6G era,75 RMS offers a shared hardware platform for both functions. Their programmable nature enables the combination of sensing and communication modes, dynamic resource allocation, and the creation of intelligent electromagnetic environments that support cooperative perception and data exchange. This integration not only improves efficiency but also establishes the foundation for cognitive Rad-Com networks in intelligent transportation, environmental monitoring, and autonomous systems.76,77

Despite recent advances, several factors continue to limit the practical deployment of RMS in radar systems. Current tunable elements often suffer from narrow modulation bandwidths, slow response times, and signal distortion, making them less suitable for broadband, high-speed applications. Scalability and integration are also critical issues, as expanding RMS to large apertures requires intricate biasing networks, dense control circuits, and close co-design with antennas and radio frequency (RF) front-end.36,78 Meanwhile, the push toward intelligence and adaptivity is driving integration with AI, e.g., machine learning (ML), for real-time control, enabling context-aware electromagnetic optimization.79,80,81 Looking ahead, the application prospects of RMS span both defense and civilian domains, ranging from adaptive stealth and electronic countermeasures to automotive radar, air traffic management, and smart infrastructure sensing. Ensuring long-term reliability, electromagnetic robustness, and environmental durability will be vital for translating RMS from laboratory prototypes to practical and field-deployable radar systems.82,83

This review provides a comprehensive overview of recent developments in reconfigurable metasurfaces for radar applications. We organize the discussion around five key functional areas: radar signal generation and modulation, radar deception and jamming, radar stealth and scattering control, direction-of-arrival estimation, and joint radar sensing and communication. Through an integrated examination of these domains, the review highlights how reconfigurable metasurfaces are redefining radar architectures and paving the way toward intelligent, adaptive, and multifunctional electromagnetic platforms for the next generation of sensing technologies. To provide a clearer technical framework, the review first summarizes the fundamental principles and enabling capabilities of reconfigurable metasurfaces, including wavefront manipulation, dynamic beamforming, spatiotemporal modulation, harmonic generation, and modulation-waveform-dependent control. Building on this basis, the subsequent sections discuss how these capabilities are employed in the five radar-oriented application areas, followed by a final discussion on system-level challenges, intelligence and adaptivity, and future application prospects.

Results

Fundamental principles and capabilities

Wavefront manipulation and dynamic beamforming

Reconfigurable metasurfaces were initially developed as planar platforms for manipulating electromagnetic wavefronts through spatially engineered phase, amplitude, and polarization responses. By introducing programmable phase gradients across the aperture, an RMS can tailor the reflected or transmitted wavefront in a flexible manner, thereby enabling anomalous reflection, beam deflection, focusing, splitting, and more general wavefront shaping functions. In this sense, wavefront manipulation constitutes one of the most fundamental capabilities of RMS and serves as the physical basis for many higher-level radar functionalities discussed in the subsequent sections.1,3

Among these capabilities, dynamic beamforming is particularly important. Unlike static metasurfaces with fixed responses, RMSs can reconfigure the phase distribution of their constituent meta-atoms in real time through electrical, optical, or material-state tuning, allowing the radiation beam to be steered, reshaped, or switched on demand. This mechanism is conceptually analogous to phased-array beam control, but it can often be realized with a significantly reduced hardware burden, lower profile, and greater integration flexibility. As a result, RMS-based beamforming has attracted broad interest in wireless communications, imaging, and radar sensing, where directional transmission, adaptive reception, and agile spatial control are highly desirable.13,20,21

For radar-oriented systems, dynamic beamforming should be regarded as an enabling mechanism rather than an isolated application category. On the transmit side, programmable beam steering allows the radiated energy to be concentrated toward selected angular sectors, thereby improving illumination efficiency and supporting adaptive scene probing. On the receive or scattering-control side, dynamic aperture coding can reshape the reradiated field and provide flexible spatial selectivity, which is beneficial for target detection, tracking, and interference suppression. Furthermore, the ability to switch among different beam states, or even synthesize multiple beams, provides a practical route toward multifunctional apertures that can support sensing, communication, and countermeasure tasks within a common metasurface platform.29,41

It is also worth emphasizing that dynamic beamforming in RMSs is not limited to simple beam steering. Depending on the implemented coding strategy and tuning mechanism, the metasurface can support beam scanning over a wide angular range, beam splitting for multi-directional coverage, and adaptive beam shaping for improved field distribution control. These features are particularly relevant to radar systems that require agile angular coverage, compact hardware implementation, and reconfigurable electromagnetic responses in complex environments. Therefore, in the context of this review, wavefront manipulation and dynamic beamforming are more appropriately classified as fundamental capabilities that underpin several radar application domains, including signal generation and modulation, deception and jamming, direction-of-arrival estimation, and joint radar-communication integration.

Spatiotemporal modulation and harmonic generation

Spatiotemporal modulation extends reconfigurable metasurfaces from static wavefront shaping to the dynamic manipulation of the spectral content of electromagnetic waves. When the reflection or transmission response of a metasurface is varied in time, the surface no longer acts as a time-invariant boundary, but rather as a programmable electromagnetic interface that redistributes incident energy across different frequency components. In this sense, temporal modulation introduces an additional degree of freedom beyond conventional spatial phase engineering, enabling the metasurface to jointly control wave propagation in the spatial and spectral domains.45,84,85 This capability has become a central mechanism in many radar-oriented functionalities, including signal generation, deceptive scattering, spectral spreading, and harmonic-domain information encoding.

From a signal-processing perspective, the physical origin of harmonic generation can be understood naturally in the Fourier domain. If the metasurface reflection coefficient is periodically modulated in time, the time-varying response can be expanded into a Fourier series. As a result, multiplication of the incident wave by the periodic modulation function in the time domain is equivalent to convolution between the incident spectrum and the modulation spectrum in the frequency domain. Consequently, a monochromatic incident wave at the fundamental frequency is redistributed into a series of discrete harmonics whose frequencies, amplitudes, and phases are directly determined by the Fourier coefficients of the modulation waveform. Therefore, harmonic generation is not an incidental by-product, but a direct consequence of time-periodic boundary modulation.84,85

An important advantage of spatiotemporal modulation is that the generated harmonics can be controlled not only in amplitude but also in direction. By jointly applying temporal coding and spatial phase gradients, different harmonic orders can be selectively shaped and radiated toward different angles, thereby linking frequency conversion with beam manipulation. This mechanism is particularly evident in the space-time coding digital metasurface (STCM) discussed in the manuscript, where directional radiation of the ±1st-order harmonics was demonstrated with a conversion efficiency exceeding 88%.84 In more advanced asynchronous space-time coding metasurfaces, different subarrays are driven by different temporal sequences, allowing multiple harmonic channels to be generated, shaped, and directed simultaneously.45 Such architectures provide a compact route to multi-frequency emission, spectral decoupling, and frequency-division multiplexing without relying on multiple local oscillators or additional filtering hardware.

More broadly, harmonic generation serves as a unifying physical mechanism across several radar applications of RMSs. In radar signal generation and modulation, harmonic synthesis enables frequency translation, multi-tone generation, and direct waveform construction from monochromatic illumination.48,49,84,85,86 In radar deception and jamming, temporally controlled harmonics create artificial Doppler components, false spectral responses, and receiver-dependent scattering signatures.51,55,87,88,89 In stealth and scattering control, higher-order harmonics and spectrum redistribution can be used to suppress the carrier-frequency signature or reroute energy away from the observing radar.55,88 In harmonic-domain DOA estimation, angle information can be encoded into spectral asymmetries or separated across non-overlapping harmonic channels.69,72,90,91 Therefore, harmonic generation should be viewed not merely as one specific effect, but as a foundational bridge between time-varying metasurface physics and system-level radar functionalities.

It is also worth noting that the usefulness of spatiotemporal modulation is not determined solely by whether harmonics are generated, but by how precisely they are engineered. Pseudorandom or actively varying temporal modulation can spread scattered energy over a broader spectral range and suppress the residual carrier component, which may be exploited for camouflage and broadband interference. Likewise, random phase modulation may induce spectral spreading and matched-filter loss, revealing that the waveform-dependent Fourier spectrum of the modulation directly affects radar observables and subsequent signal processing performance. These results further confirm that Fourier domain harmonic engineering provides the theoretical basis for both constructive functionalities and undesired distortion effects in metasurface-enabled radar systems.

Influence of modulation waveform

Although harmonic generation is a general consequence of time-varying metasurface modulation, the actual application performance is strongly dependent on the specific modulation waveform. From the Fourier domain viewpoint discussed above, the temporal waveform directly determines the spectral distribution of the generated harmonics through its Fourier coefficients. Therefore, waveform design is not merely an implementation detail, but a primary factor governing frequency conversion efficiency, harmonic selectivity, spectral purity, and the resulting electromagnetic functionality of the metasurface.48,84,85 Different temporal profiles can thus lead to markedly different outcomes, even when the modulation frequency and hardware platform remain the same.

A first important aspect is the effect of waveform shape on spectral structure. Continuous or smoothly varying phase modulations generally produce more controllable harmonic distributions and are particularly suitable for direct radar waveform synthesis. Nonlinear periodic phase modulation can be tailored to generate a variety of FMCW signals, including polynomial, sinusoidal, dual-sinusoidal, and S-shaped frequency sweeps.48 In these cases, the modulation waveform directly determines the instantaneous frequency evolution of the reflected signal and therefore controls waveform linearity, bandwidth, and sweep characteristics. More recently, a fully digitized nanosecond-level time-domain coding metasurface has further demonstrated that high-speed digital coding can support high-quality FMCW generation with enhanced modulation bandwidth and practical applicability.86 These results indicate that the temporal coding waveform serves not only as a means of harmonic excitation, but also as a direct design variable for shaping radar waveforms at the physical layer.

A second key factor is the waveform precision and coding format. In digitally programmable metasurfaces, the modulation sequence is often implemented using discrete phase states, and the number of available bits strongly influences the quality of harmonic control. Higher-bit coding provides finer phase quantization, improved phase linearity, and more accurate spectral allocation, whereas low-bit modulation may introduce coarse phase transitions, stronger side harmonics, and larger spectral distortion.84,85 This issue is especially relevant in radar systems, where low-precision or strongly nonlinear modulation can create a waveform mismatch between the echo and the reference template. Under random phase modulation conditions, such distortion may lead to spectral spreading in the frequency domain and substantial reduction of the matched-filter peak in the time domain, with the degradation becoming more severe for 1-bit and 2-bit modulation.92 These findings suggest that modulation waveform design must be considered jointly with radar signal processing requirements, rather than being treated as an isolated metasurface parameter.

The influence of modulation waveforms is particularly pronounced in radar countermeasures and deceptive scattering. Pseudorandom temporal modulation can redistribute scattered energy over a broad spectral range and suppress the residual carrier component, thereby reducing detectability for non-cooperative radars.55,88 By contrast, approximately linear or waveform-matched temporal modulation is more suitable for controlled frequency shifting and false-target generation, especially in broadband radar systems such as FMCW and LFM radars.48,93,94 In this sense, different waveforms correspond to different countermeasure mechanisms: Pseudorandom modulation is more closely associated with spectral spreading and camouflage, while nearly linear or intentionally shaped modulation is more effective for controlled frequency translation, deceptive ranging, and HRRP spoofing. Therefore, the waveform not only affects harmonic amplitudes but also determines how the manipulated spectrum is interpreted by the victim radar.

Waveform dependence is also important for multifunctional sensing and communication scenarios. Programmable metasurfaces can synthesize not only standard chirp signals but also artificial micro-Doppler signatures through properly designed time-varying phase sequences.95 In such cases, the temporal waveform determines the detailed time-frequency trajectory of the generated signal and thus affects target classification, motion emulation, and training-data realism. Moreover, in metasurface-enabled radar-communication integration, the modulation profile governs spectral occupancy, channel separation, and the trade-off between sensing compatibility and communication efficiency.49,96 These examples further confirm that waveform engineering is a central design dimension in reconfigurable metasurfaces, linking low-level temporal coding with high-level system performance. Overall, the influence of modulation waveforms can be summarized from three interconnected aspects: spectral distribution, functional selectivity, and processing compatibility. The waveform determines how energy is distributed among harmonics, which harmonic channels are emphasized or suppressed, and how the resulting scattered signal interacts with radar or communication receivers. Consequently, careful waveform design is essential for maximizing useful functionalities such as waveform synthesis, beam-controlled frequency conversion, and cooperative communication, while mitigating undesired effects such as spectral distortion, false sidelobes, and matched-filter loss. For reconfigurable metasurfaces, this makes modulation waveform engineering a foundational problem that directly connects electromagnetic boundary control with practical system performance.

The fundamental principles and capabilities discussed above provide the basis for understanding the diverse radar applications of reconfigurable metasurfaces. Building on these common mechanisms, the following sections further examine their roles in radar signal generation and modulation, radar deception and jamming, scattering manipulation, DOA estimation, and joint radar sensing and communication.

Radar signal generation and modulation

In modern radar systems, signal generation and modulation not only determine the resolution and accuracy of target detection but also play a pivotal role in the efficiency of system architecture and functional flexibility. While conventional radars typically rely on frequency-stabilized oscillators, mixers, and digital signal processing modules to construct complex RF front ends, the emergence of reconfigurable metasurfaces as a novel electromagnetic control mechanism has brought about a paradigm shift in the design of radar signal chains. These metasurfaces offer a unified platform for harmonic generation, multi-frequency control, and time-varying waveform synthesis, while also exhibiting promising advantages in terms of system-level integration, power efficiency, and real-time reconfigurability.

Originally developed for static or quasi-dynamic beam shaping, metasurfaces have evolved significantly with the introduction of temporal modulation mechanisms, which expand their functionality into active manipulation of harmonic spectra. As radar systems increasingly demand simultaneous multi-frequency modulation, particularly in applications such as target classification, multi-beam imaging, and frequency-diverse sensing, conventional approaches based on multiple local oscillators and frequency synthesis circuits encounter significant challenges in scalability and hardware complexity. As illustrated in Figure 2C, a multi-partition asynchronous STCM (ASTCM) architecture has been introduced,45 where the metasurface aperture is divided into multiple sub-arrays, each modulated with distinct temporal coding sequences. This enables real-time generation and independent control of multiple carrier frequencies from a single metasurface.

More importantly, inherent harmonic interference is substantially mitigated, allowing frequency-division multiplexing to be achieved without requiring additional filtering modules. Such a strategy significantly reduces hardware overhead while supporting advanced radar functions such as frequency-agile sensing and broadband feature extraction.

Beyond frequency control, recent efforts have explored the direct synthesis of modulated radar waveforms using programmable metasurfaces, as shown in Figure 3. A representative case is the generation of FMCW signals, a widely used waveform in modern radar systems for high-resolution range measurement. Traditionally, FMCW generation relies on voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs) or direct digital synthesizers (DDS). However, with the development of nonlinearly periodic phase-encoded STCMs, it becomes possible to generate time-varying carrier frequencies directly from metasurface reflections under monochromatic illumination.48 Moreover, by introducing spatial phase gradients across the metasurface, the direction of the time-varying beam can be flexibly controlled, enabling joint programmability of waveform and beam steering. Current studies mainly show that reconfigurable metasurfaces can realize waveform-generation functionalities analogous to conventional DDS- or VCO-based sources at the proof-of-concept level. However, rigorous quantitative comparisons in terms of phase noise, frequency linearity, and other source-level metrics are still lacking and require further investigation. More recently, nanosecond-level TDCMs have been reported to significantly enhance the modulation bandwidth and practical applicability of metasurface-based FMCW generation.86 In particular, a fully digital PIN-diode-based TDCM achieved a phase tuning speed as fast as 20 ns over a full 360° cycle, enabling the generation of high-quality FMCW signals with a bandwidth of 10 MHz at C-band frequencies. This corresponds to a range resolution of approximately 15 m in a compact radar prototype while maintaining an SNR and sweep linearity comparable to those of conventional electronic sources. Notably, the demonstrated maximum effective modulation speed reached 50 MHz, substantially outperforming varactor-based metasurfaces, which are typically limited to megahertz-level bandwidths. At the architectural level, programmable metasurfaces also open new pathways toward radar system simplification and integration. Conventional radar signal chains typically consist of distinct transmitter, receiver, and digital processing stages, involving multiple components such as mixers, filters, analog-to-digital converters (ADCs), and matched filters, all contributing to system complexity and power consumption. In contrast, an information metasurface-based radar architecture is proposed in Figure 4B, where the metasurface performs both modulated waveform generation at the transmitter and dechirp processing at the receiver directly at the RF layer.49 This material-layer modulation and demodulation approach allows previously distributed RF functionalities to be physically embedded within a single metasurface structure, thereby reducing interconnect overhead and potentially eliminating the need for high-speed ADCs. Experimental validation demonstrates that such metasurface-based radar systems can achieve comparable performance in target detection, ranging, and velocity estimation while significantly reducing hardware footprint, power consumption, and system cost. This presents the metasurface as a compelling candidate for highly integrated radar platforms.

However, alongside these functional advances, metasurface-based modulation also introduces new interference dimensions to radar signal processing. In particular, nonlinear phase modulations induced by actively or pseudo-randomly modulated metasurfaces can lead to mismatches between the echo signal and the reference waveform, degrading the effectiveness of conventional pulse compression techniques. Recent theoretical work has modeled these effects under random phase modulation (RPM) conditions and revealed that RPM can cause spectral spreading of radar echoes in the frequency domain, as well as significant amplitude reduction of the matched filter output in the time domain, especially under low-bit modulation precision (e.g., 1-bit and 2-bit).92 To quantitatively assess these impacts, analytical models of the average energy spectrum (AES) and the amplitude loss ratio (ALR) were developed, providing metrics for evaluating radar performance under modulation-induced distortion. Furthermore, optimization-based deconvolution algorithms have been proposed to recover the time-varying phase sequences of the metasurface from echo data, enabling modulation pattern identification and suppression strategies in the presence of smart electromagnetic interference.50

Radar deception and jamming

Radar deception and jamming are undergoing a fundamental transition from power-dominant suppression to perception-oriented manipulation. Traditional electronic countermeasures primarily rely on noise injection or signal replay to overwhelm radar receivers, which typically require high transmission power and complex RF architectures. In contrast, reconfigurable metasurfaces enable a fundamentally different approach by directly manipulating the electromagnetic scattering behavior of targets at the physical layer. Through subwavelength control of reflected waves, metasurfaces provide compact, low-profile, and often passive platforms capable of synthesizing deceptive radar signatures, suppressing detection, or reshaping target observables in a coherent and programmable manner.88,89

By dynamically modulating reflection phase, amplitude, frequency, or polarization, metasurfaces can interfere with multiple stages of the radar processing chain, including velocity estimation, imaging formation, range profiling, and multistatic data fusion.51,87 Besides this, scattering-hotspot manipulation has recently emerged as another promising route to radar deception. For example, fluidic-accessible metasurfaces can dynamically reconstruct strong-scattering-point patterns over a wide 9–14 GHz band, thereby enabling broadband and reconfigurable electromagnetic camouflage for different target profiles.97 These effects are achieved without active signal transmission, significantly reducing electromagnetic exposure and enhancing survivability in contested environments. As a result, metasurface-enabled countermeasures are increasingly regarded not merely as jammers, but as programmable electromagnetic interfaces that reshape how radar systems perceive targets.

This section reviews recent advances in metasurface-based radar deception and jamming from a radar-centric perspective. The discussion is organized into five categories: (1) Doppler and micro-Doppler manipulation, (2) SAR and ISAR image deception, (3) HRRP spoofing, (4) multistatic and angularly adaptive deception, and (5) spectral spreading for broadband interference.

Doppler and Micro-Doppler manipulation

Velocity estimation in radar systems fundamentally relies on Doppler-induced frequency shifts, whereas micro-Doppler signatures arise from fine-scale micromotions such as rotation, vibration, and articulated dynamics. These micro-motion features encode rich kinematic and structural information and have become key enablers for radar-based target classification and recognition. Time-modulated metasurfaces offer a direct and programmable means to manipulate such Doppler-related signatures by imposing controlled temporal variations on the amplitude and phase of the echoes. As shown in Figure 5A, initial metasurface-based Doppler deception studies primarily focused on non-imaging radar scenarios, where artificial Doppler frequency shifts were generated through periodic phase or amplitude modulation of metasurface unit cells.51,87,98 While effective for velocity spoofing, these approaches are largely limited to single-parameter Doppler manipulation. To move beyond RD deception, recent efforts have explored the joint manipulation of Doppler and micro-Doppler signatures, enabling recognition-level radar deception. A critical step toward this capability is the physically consistent synthesis of micro-motion features. In this regard, scattering-center-based modeling provides a rigorous framework for analyzing micro-Doppler characteristics by decomposing complex targets into multiple dominant scattering centers,100 each associated with distinct time-varying phases and micro-Doppler frequencies. Based on this principle, time-modulated metasurfaces have been designed to reproduce the phase evolution of individual scattering centers through spatially partitioned and temporally programmed modulation regions. Both numerical simulations and experimental measurements demonstrate that the generated micromotion false targets exhibit high similarity to real targets in time–frequency representations, with reported similarity metrics exceeding 0.9.

Building upon scattering-center-guided micro-motion synthesis, metasurfaces with independent amplitude–phase co-control capabilities further enable comprehensive micro-Doppler and flicker feature deception.101 As Figure 5B demonstrates, a representative study reports a transmission-reflection-type metasurface capable of independently manipulating scattering amplitude and phase over a full 360° phase cycle with minimal mutual coupling, which is essential for accurately reproducing micro-motion dynamics. By designing time-varying coding sequences that simultaneously encode Doppler shifts and micro-motion-induced amplitude-phase variations, the metasurface can emulate micro-Doppler signatures associated with rotating blades and multi-rotor aerial targets. Moreover, by incorporating amplitude fluctuations linked to blade occlusion and scattering intermittency, flicker mechanisms are also successfully mimicked. Experimental results show that the metasurface-generated echoes closely match those of real targets in both spectral distribution and temporal evolution, enabling effective deception against radar recognition systems and inducing confusion among different target classes and rotor configurations.

Overall, the evolution from isolated Doppler spoofing to scattering-center-guided, joint Doppler-micro-Doppler-flicker deception represents a significant advancement in metasurface-enabled radar countermeasures. By enabling programmable and physically grounded synthesis of micro-motion features, time-modulated metasurfaces provide a compact and versatile platform for next-generation radar deception targeting not only detection and tracking but also high-level target recognition.

As shown in Figure 6A, two-dimensional SAR image camouflage is commonly achieved using time-modulated metasurfaces mounted on or near the protected target. By applying periodic or segmented phase modulation during illumination, artificial Doppler components are introduced into the slow-time signal.102,103 After SAR processing, these components manifest as displaced or duplicated scatterers in both range and azimuth dimensions. Subsection-shift Doppler frequency modulation is a representative strategy, where phase-tunable metasurfaces loaded with varactor diodes provide continuous phase control and enable flexible adjustment of false target spacing and intensity. Satellite SAR experiments demonstrate that multiple false targets with realistic spatial distributions can be generated without active signal retransmission.104 Related approaches further extend this idea to moving targets by using cosinusoidal or composite phase modulation, which synthesizes multiple Doppler histories and corrupts SAR-GMTI processing results.105,106

Figure 6B highlights the extension of SAR image deception to polarization-diverse and wide-angle scenarios. Conformal dual-polarized time-modulated metasurfaces are designed to maintain stable phase modulation under arbitrary polarization states and curved mounting conditions. By independently controlling the reflected phase of two orthogonal polarizations, the metasurface preserves deception performance even when the incident polarization varies. Sectionalized phase modulation applied in both fast-time and slow-time domains enables two-dimensional jamming, allowing false targets to be placed simultaneously in range and azimuth. Experimental results based on simulated SAR data and spaceborne measurements confirm that realistic two-dimensional camouflage can be achieved across wide viewing angles and polarization configurations.54

Figure 6C focuses on ISAR image deception, where the objective is to emulate the angularly dependent scattering behavior of rotating or maneuvering targets. In this case, metasurfaces are designed directly from ISAR scattering data rather than from conventional far-field beam shaping principles. Multi-layer metasurface architectures tailor broadband and wide-angle scattering responses, enabling stable ISAR illusion images across frequency ranges such as 8–12 GHz and observation angles spanning tens of degrees. By engineering phase dispersion across layers, customized illusion targets can be produced in ISAR images, including misaligned, split, bent, or tilted structures.107 Practical considerations are further addressed by studies demonstrating that complete target coverage is not mandatory; partial coverage by time-modulated metasurfaces can still introduce sufficient coherent phase perturbations to blur or duplicate ISAR images, improving deployability on realistic platforms.53

Overall, Figure 6 demonstrates the evolution of metasurface-enabled deception from SAR image camouflage to polarization-robust two-dimensional jamming and ultimately to broadband ISAR illusion synthesis. These approaches indicate a clear trend toward image-aware and programmable manipulation of radar perception, where reconfigurable metasurfaces act as adaptive interfaces between physical scattering mechanisms and radar image formation.

HRRP spoofing and countermeasures

HRRP radars characterize targets based on the coherent amplitude and phase distribution of echoes across fine range bins. Spoofing such systems requires precise control over spatially distributed scattering responses rather than broadband disruption. Programmable metasurfaces with independently controllable amplitude and phase responses have been demonstrated to synthesize predefined HRRP signatures.

In Figures 7A and 7B, recent studies demonstrate that reconfigurable metasurfaces can effectively manipulate HRRP by combining amplitude–phase synthesis with time-dependent frequency control. Guo et al. introduced a time-domain equivalent amplitude and phase synthesis method that overcomes the limited modulation states of low-bit metasurfaces by vectorially combining unit-cell responses within each time slot, enabling high-fidelity HRRP imitation under stepped-frequency and LFM radar illumination using 1-bit and 2-bit phase-coded metasurfaces.52 Building on time modulation, Sun et al. proposed a flexible and conformal multifunctional time-modulated metasurface capable of simultaneous amplitude, frequency, and polarization control, where fast periodic modulation generated multiple discrete harmonic components that appeared as multiple false peaks in the HRRP after pulse compression.93 The metasurface employed miniaturized spiral unit cells with a size of approximately 0.12λ and was implemented in a cylindrical conformal configuration, enabling effective spoofing over a wide angular range. At the same time, recent studies reveal that HRRP spoofing based on time-modulated metasurfaces remains vulnerable to waveform-level countermeasures. As illustrated in Figure 7C, Fang et al. proposed an electronic counter-countermeasure strategy using pseudorandom-coded (PC) radar signals to suppress false targets.108 Compared with LFM signals, PC waveforms exhibit significantly reduced range–Doppler coupling. Consequently, frequency shifts induced by time-modulated metasurfaces produce much less pronounced apparent range displacement after matched filtering. Experiments conducted at 30 GHz using a 1-bit phase-reconfigurable metasurface showed that PC signals substantially reduced false peaks in the HRRP while preserving the response of the uncovered real target. This suppression remained effective under both normal and oblique incidence conditions. More recently, ECCM strategies have been further extended to multiphase coding time-modulated metasurfaces, where phase-domain decoupling was used to separate metasurface-induced modulation from the target echo, thereby suppressing deceptive false targets while recovering the real target in the range–Doppler profile under more general fast-time, slow-time, and multibit modulation conditions.109

In summary, reconfigurable metasurfaces enable effective HRRP spoofing by providing programmable control over echo amplitude, phase, and frequency. Time-domain and time-frequency modulation allow the generation of multiple false scatterers and realistic range profile signatures. However, spoofing performance strongly depends on the radar waveform, and signals with improved range-Doppler decoupling can significantly suppress false targets. This interaction indicates that HRRP deception and suppression should be treated as a coupled system-level problem. Future research is expected to focus on the joint design of metasurface modulation strategies and radar waveforms, as well as on adaptive and intelligent schemes capable of responding to evolving radar counter countermeasure techniques.

Multistatic and angularly adaptive deception

Multistatic radar configurations and off-boresight illumination conditions pose two coupled challenges for metasurface-enabled deception. First, distributed receivers observe the target from different angles, breaking the single-view assumption underlying many monostatic deception designs. Second, most programmable metasurfaces exhibit inherent angle sensitivity, so their modulation performance may degrade significantly under oblique incidence. Recent work addresses these limitations by introducing architectures and control strategies that adapt to incidence angle and remain effective without requiring precise knowledge of receiver geometry.

One representative approach employs a dual-metasurface deception architecture to enhance angular adaptability and scattering strength, as illustrated in Figure 8A. In this system, a receiving metasurface estimates the incident angle through beam scanning, while a transmitting metasurface applies time-varying phase modulation to encode false target information into the reflected echo.89 Experimental results show that under oblique incidence of approximately 30°, a single metasurface can experience more than 10 dB degradation in echo strength, whereas the dual-metasurface configuration preserves effective modulated backscatter and maintains deception performance. Another line of work targets multistatic radar systems by exploiting space-time-coding metasurfaces to disrupt joint localization across distributed receivers. Experiments at X-band frequencies confirm substantial degradation in multistatic localization accuracy under space–time modulation.

In summary, angularly adaptive deception and multistatic deception are converging toward two complementary design principles. The first is architecture-level adaptation, in which a metasurface system senses incidence direction and actively preserves backscatter and modulation performance under oblique views. The second is space-frequency joint control, where dense harmonic engineering and fundamental suppression are leveraged to disrupt coherent multistatic localization across receivers and angles. Together, these works suggest that future metasurface countermeasures will increasingly be developed as adaptive electromagnetic systems rather than isolated reflective sheets, with explicit robustness to angle diversity and receiver diversity.

Spectral spreading and broadband interference

Spectral spreading provides an alternative interference mechanism that does not rely on coherent false target synthesis but instead suppresses radar perception by redistributing the scattered energy over a wide frequency range. By temporally modulating the reflection properties of metasurfaces, an incident narrowband or moderately broadband radar signal can be transformed into a noise-like spectrum with significantly reduced power spectral density at the original carrier frequency.

As shown in Figure 9, a representative approach is spread-spectrum camouflaging based on time-modulated metasurfaces. In this scheme, the metasurface periodically switches between two reflective states with approximately opposite reflection phases, such as quasi-perfect electric conductor and quasi-perfect magnetic conductor states, under the control of a pseudorandom modulation sequence.55 This temporal modulation effectively convolves the incident signal spectrum with the modulation spectrum, spreading the scattered energy into a dense set of frequency components. As the modulation sequence length increases, the spectral power density at the carrier frequency decreases significantly. Experimental demonstrations around 10 GHz show that for a pseudorandom sequence length of 127 and modulation frequencies on the order of several megahertz, the residual spectral component at the original frequency can be substantially suppressed. Beyond narrowband camouflaging, time-varying metasurfaces have also been exploited for broadband radar interference and deception. By enforcing a continuous and approximately linear phase variation over time, the metasurface performs efficient frequency translation of the incident waveform.110 For broadband waveforms such as FMCW signals, this frequency shift directly translates into range displacement after radar processing. Experiments in the 4–6 GHz band demonstrate that modulation frequencies on the order of a few kilohertz can induce apparent shifts of tens of meters, with reported frequency conversion efficiencies exceeding 90%. Unlike conventional active jammers, this approach manipulates the spectrum of the reflected signal without transmitting additional waveforms, enabling lightweight and energy-efficient broadband interference. Therefore, spectral spreading and broadband frequency manipulation based on time-modulated metasurfaces offer a complementary route to radar countermeasures. By redistributing spectral energy or inducing controlled frequency shifts, these methods degrade detection and ranging performance across wide bandwidths while avoiding the strict coherence requirements of image-based deception techniques.

Overall, metasurface-enabled radar deception and jamming have developed into a coherent physical-layer countermeasure framework that emphasizes manipulation of radar perception rather than receiver saturation. Across different radar modalities, the reviewed studies consistently demonstrate that time-varying and programmable metasurfaces can manipulate key radar observables, including Doppler signatures, image formation, range profiles, angular consistency, and detectability. Although these techniques target different stages of the radar processing chain, they share a common foundation in temporally controlled scattering, enabling highly coherent and waveform-compatible deception under low-power or passive conditions. Importantly, recent progress reveals a clear shift from single-purpose jammers toward multifunctional and reconfigurable metasurface platforms capable of switching between deception, suppression, and camouflage modes in response to operational requirements.

It is worth noting that the deception mechanisms for pulse radar differ from those for FMCW radar. For FMCW systems, time-varying metasurfaces can exploit frequency shifting to induce apparent range displacement after dechirping and beat-frequency processing. By contrast, for conventional pulse radar, especially direct time-of-flight ranging, phase-only modulation does not readily translate into an equivalent delay of the echo, and therefore, simple range spoofing is generally more difficult to realize in the same manner. In this case, metasurface-enabled deception is more appropriately implemented at the level of pulse compression and coherent echo formation. A practical route is HRRP spoofing, where the radar target is characterized by the coherent amplitude and phase distribution across fine range bins. As reviewed in this paper, reconfigurable metasurfaces can synthesize deceptive HRRP signatures through amplitude-phase synthesis and time-frequency modulation, enabling controllable false peaks and waveform-adaptive range-profile manipulation under stepped-frequency and LFM radar illumination. Another route is to exploit modulation-induced mismatch in pulse compression. Random or nonlinear phase modulation can reduce the correlation between the received echo and the reference waveform, leading to spectral spreading, matched-filter degradation, and distortion of the pulse-compressed response. More generally, for coherent pulse radar modalities such as SAR and ISAR, metasurfaces can manipulate the temporal phase history of the scattered field, thereby introducing false scatterers, range-azimuth distortions, or customized illusion images after image reconstruction. Therefore, although direct range deception for simple pulse radar is challenging through phase shifting alone, effective deception remains feasible for pulse-compressed, range-profile-based, and imaging pulse radar systems by acting on their coherent processing chain rather than on pure propagation delay alone.

To address multi-sensor detection environments, passive metasurface stealth was further extended to radar–infrared bi-stealth applications. Ultralight and thin metasurfaces were developed to simultaneously suppress microwave scattering and infrared emission, achieving radar RCS reduction exceeding 10 dB while maintaining infrared emissivity values below 0.3 across the mid-infrared band.96 Flexible and transparent checkerboard metasurfaces demonstrated dual-band stealth performance, enabling broadband microwave radar suppression while remaining optically transparent and mechanically conformal.57 These advances significantly enhanced practical applicability; however, the absence of tunability limited adaptability to dynamically changing environments.

The transition to reconfigurable metasurfaces marked a pivotal shift from static stealth toward actively controllable scattering. Electrically programmable metasurfaces employing PIN diodes or varactors enabled dynamic switching between multiple scattering states, allowing tunable RCS reduction, polarization control, and transmission windows within a single platform.111 By introducing programmable logic and information-driven control, metasurface antennas and systems began to exhibit adaptive electromagnetic responses, demonstrating the feasibility of stealth architectures capable of real-time reconfiguration.112

Reconfigurable metasurfaces were subsequently leveraged to realize dynamic cloaking and electromagnetic illusion functionalities. By actively modulating phase distributions, metasurfaces were shown to switch between cloaking and illusion states, thereby modifying the perceived scattering signature of an object.60 Dynamic electromagnetic illusion cloaks further demonstrated the ability to reshape scattering signatures in real time, enabling a physical object to emulate different virtual targets under radar interrogation.64 Increased control resolution was achieved using multi-bit programmable metasurfaces, where 3-bit architectures enabled finer phase quantization and multifunctional operation, including adaptive invisibility and beam manipulation with improved fidelity.113

Environmental adaptivity introduced an additional dimension to radar stealth. Metasurfaces responsive to ambient stimuli such as temperature or polarization exhibited chameleon-like electromagnetic camouflage, allowing passive adaptation of scattering characteristics without external control circuitry,59 as illustrated in Figure 10B. More advanced self-adaptive radar stealth systems emerged when sensing and programmable metasurfaces were integrated into unified systems. These architectures autonomously switched between transparency and invisibility modes based on detected electromagnetic signals, enabling real-time mode selection within the same frequency band,63 as shown in Figure 10C. Fully integrated intelligent metasurface cloak systems further demonstrated closed-loop operation, incorporating sensing, decision-making, and actuation to dynamically reduce radar detectability under varying illumination conditions.114

In Figure 10D, a fundamental extension of stealth concepts appears in the Doppler domain, targeting velocity perception rather than scattering magnitude alone. Time-modulated metasurfaces enabled active manipulation of Doppler signatures, allowing moving objects to appear stationary or to exhibit altered apparent velocities. Broadband Doppler cloaks demonstrated operation across multiple polarizations and wide frequency bands, effectively suppressing velocity information used by moving target indication filters.115 Time-dependent metasurfaces further enabled compensation of real Doppler shifts, rendering moving targets less distinguishable from stationary clutter over bandwidths approaching 20% of the carrier frequency.62 Doppler-domain stealth has also been extended to platform-scale implementations. Three-dimensional cloaked vehicles integrated with large-scale reconfigurable metasurface arrays demonstrated simultaneous manipulation of spatial scattering, polarization, and velocity-related signatures, enabling multi-dimensional radar signature control under realistic motion scenarios.66 Complementary studies confirmed compatibility with broadband and FMCW radar systems, indicating that Doppler cloaking principles remain effective under modern radar signal processing frameworks.65

Recent work (Figure 10E) has further broadened radar stealth into application-oriented directions. Time–space coding metasurfaces with amplitude–phase co-modulation enabled the integration of radiation and stealth, allowing metasurfaces to radiate or communicate while maintaining reduced radar observability by redistributing energy into higher-order harmonics.67 Along this direction, integrated radiation–scattering reconfigurable intelligent metasurfaces have shown that multifunctional electromagnetic regulation can be achieved within a single hardware platform, where radiation–scattering mode switching, polarization selectivity, and programmable phase control are jointly supported.116 In parallel, metasurface-assisted synthetic aperture radar imaging cloaking demonstrated that stealth can be achieved at the image formation level, suppressing or distorting target signatures in SAR reconstructions rather than solely reducing backscattered power, with structurally reduced structural similarity indices reported in experimental demonstrations.61

Beyond electromagnetic stealth alone, cross-modal intelligence has expanded stealth capabilities. Intelligent camouflage metasurfaces incorporating visual perception and ML-enabled adaptive electromagnetic responses informed by scene recognition, suggesting that stealth performance could be guided by non-electromagnetic environmental cues.117 At a system level, as shown in Figure 10F, reinforcement-learning-controlled metasurface swarms enabled the construction of open and dynamic invisible spaces. By coordinating large numbers of reconfigurable metasurfaces through learning-based control, high similarity between target and background scattering patterns was achieved in complex and dynamic scenarios, extending stealth strategies from object-level concealment toward environment-level electromagnetic control.118,119 More recently, this direction has been further advanced by meta-reinforcement-learning metasurfaces, which realized an adaptive transparent cloaking tunnel for diverse moving targets in an open corridor by combining sensor-in-the-loop control, task-adaptive policy generation, and millisecond-scale reconfiguration to suppress object-dependent scattering in real time.120

Direction-of-arrival estimation

A major conceptual transition arises with space–time modulated metasurfaces, where temporal modulation maps angular information into alternative domains, most notably frequency harmonics. In this paradigm, DOA estimation does not rely solely on spatial sampling but instead exploits harmonic signatures generated by time-varying metasurface elements. Early demonstrations showed that asymmetries between positive and negative harmonic orders are directly related to the incidence angle, enabling DOA retrieval from a small set of spectral measurements.69 Subsequent developments structured the modulation scheme across the aperture to improve accuracy and dimensionality. A representative example in Figure 11B is the active metasurface with nonuniformly periodic time modulation,91 where different sub-arrays are assigned distinct modulation frequencies to avoid spectral overlap and synthesize multiple virtual channels within a single receiver chain. In that work, a 5 × 5 active metasurface operating at a carrier frequency of 5 GHz was demonstrated, with sub-array modulation frequencies in the MHz range, a sampling rate of 20.48 MHz, and 500 snapshots per estimate. Using harmonic-domain processing combined with subspace-based algorithms, a maximum two-dimensional DOA error of approximately 0.31° was achieved. These results indicate that time-modulated metasurfaces can approach array-level angular resolution while substantially reducing RF hardware complexity. Related studies further investigated harmonic-domain DOA estimation under various conditions, including coherent sources and sparse recovery formulations,70,122 demonstrating that metasurface-induced measurement diversity can alleviate rank deficiency and enhance angular resolvability.

Beyond narrowband scenarios, time-modulated metasurfaces have also been adapted to radar-relevant wideband and interference-rich environments. For LFM radar signals, the interaction between waveform bandwidth and modulation-induced harmonics introduces additional processing considerations. As illustrated in Figure 11C, an anti-interference DOA framework based on a 1-bit space–time modulated metasurface and a single receiving antenna was proposed,90 where matched filtering in the pulse-compressed domain, followed by blind source separation techniques, was employed to recover angular information in the presence of multiple LFM interferers. Within an angular range of approximately −50° to +50°, the reported absolute DOA errors remained below about 2°, demonstrating that metasurface-based DOA estimation can be extended to practical pulse radar waveforms. At the system level, metasurface-enabled DOA estimation has also been integrated with other radar functionalities. A multifunctional space–time modulated metasurface capable of simultaneous DOA estimation and RCS manipulation was demonstrated,123 highlighting that a single reconfigurable aperture can support both angular sensing and electromagnetic signature control, an attractive feature for adaptive and stealth-aware radar platforms.

In parallel with harmonic-domain approaches, another research direction emphasizes extreme hardware simplification through compressive and single-pixel sensing. In these schemes, a dynamically reconfigured metasurface generates a sequence of spatially and temporally diverse radiation or reception patterns, effectively projecting the incident wavefront onto a low-dimensional measurement space. ML-assisted implementations have demonstrated that DOA can be inferred directly from metasurface-coded measurements without explicit physical modeling. A representative example in Figure 11D is the artificial neural network (ANN)-based framework,72 in which harmonic amplitudes generated by a space–time coding digital metasurface were used as inputs to a compact neural network. The reported prototype achieved a mean absolute error of approximately 0.12° at an SNR of −2 dB, with more than 98% of estimation errors below 0.5°, and maintained sub-degree accuracy down to SNR levels of −12 dB. The microwave prototype operated near 9.5 GHz with an aperture size of approximately 120 mm × 120 mm, demonstrating the feasibility of learning-assisted, reduced-complexity DOA estimation from metasurface-encoded signals. Related learning-driven or data-assisted metasurface DOA schemes further investigated pattern diversity, training efficiency, and robustness to measurement noise.71

Compressive sensing concepts have also been extended to retrieve richer wavefront information beyond angle alone. A representative example is the single-pixel polarimetric DOA estimation framework,68 which employs a cavity-backed programmable coding metasurface composed of dual-polarized meta-elements. Operating in the 20–22 GHz band, the system applies multiple binary coding masks and frequency samples to generate a diverse set of wave-chaotic measurement modes, enabling joint estimation of DOA and polarization state from a single RF channel. By encoding polarization information directly at the physical layer, this approach effectively increases the dimensionality of the sensing space without increasing receiver complexity. Additional refinements, including conformal implementations and optically transparent configurations, further demonstrate the adaptability of metasurface-based DOA concepts to nonplanar or multifunctional radar platforms.124

More recently, metasurfaces have evolved from programmable apertures to analog computing media. Stacked intelligent metasurfaces (SIMs) represent a paradigm in which multiple metasurface layers are jointly optimized to perform specific mathematical operations as electromagnetic waves propagate through them. In the context of DOA estimation, SIM architectures have been designed to approximate a two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform (DFT) directly in the wave domain, such that the angular spectrum of an incident signal is formed at the output plane without conventional digital processing.125 By optimizing the phase shifts of each metasurface layer through gradient-based methods, the end-to-end transfer matrix of the SIM can closely approximate the desired DFT operator. Numerical studies report mean square DOA estimation errors on the order of 10−4 under simulated conditions, using only energy detection at the output plane. This wave-domain computing framework suggests a fundamentally different radar receiver architecture, in which DOA estimation is performed intrinsically by the electromagnetic hardware, potentially enabling ultrafast and energy-efficient angular sensing.

From a broader radar-system perspective, it is also useful to briefly compare metasurface-based DOA estimation with conventional MIMO-based DOA systems. MIMO radar typically relies on multiple transmit and receive channels to construct a virtual aperture, and therefore benefits from well-established array models, mature subspace or sparse-recovery algorithms, and potentially high angular resolution. However, these advantages are usually accompanied by increased RF-chain count, higher hardware complexity, stricter synchronization and calibration requirements, and greater system cost. By contrast, metasurface-based DOA systems shift part of the sensing burden from the digital backend to the physical layer. Through programmable spatial or space–time coding, the incident wavefront can be encoded into compact measurements, harmonic signatures, or low-dimensional projections, thereby reducing the reliance on dense antenna arrays and multi-channel receivers. As discussed above, such architectures can approach array-level angular resolution while using fewer RF channels, and can further support compressive sensing, single-pixel reception, learning-assisted inference, and even wave-domain computing. Nevertheless, metasurface-based DOA estimation is not simply a substitute for MIMO radar. Its performance depends more strongly on coding diversity, modulation design, calibration accuracy, and the robustness of the subsequent inversion or learning process. Therefore, MIMO and metasurface-based DOA systems should be viewed as complementary architectures: The former emphasizes virtual-aperture expansion and mature array processing, whereas the latter emphasizes physical-layer coding, hardware simplification, and multifunctional integration. This comparison highlights the unique role of reconfigurable metasurfaces in enabling compact and computation-efficient angular sensing systems.

Joint radar sensing and communication

Joint radar sensing and communication (JRSC), also referred to as integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) or dual-function radar-communication (DFRC), aims to unify radar sensing and wireless communication within a shared platform by reusing spectrum, hardware, and signal-processing resources. This paradigm is particularly attractive for radar-centric systems where aperture reuse, beam agility, and multifunctionality are essential, yet conventional phased-array implementations remain costly, power-intensive, and hardware-complex. Reconfigurable metasurfaces, composed of subwavelength programmable unit cells, offer an alternative route toward JRSC by enabling flexible wavefront manipulation through electronically controlled scattering responses, potentially reducing RF chain count and front-end complexity.

Early metasurface-enabled JRSC efforts focused on shared-aperture front ends, in which a single metasurface-based antenna structure supports both radar and communication functions. In such configurations, metasurfaces primarily act as high-gain reflectors or transmit arrays, offering compact form factors and frequency agility suitable for millimeter-wave radar platforms. Representative designs demonstrate that dual-band operation for automotive radar and millimeter-wave communication can be achieved within a single aperture while maintaining stable radiation characteristics and manageable structural complexity.73 Although these architectures primarily address hardware-level coexistence rather than deep functional integration, they establish a practical foundation for JRSC by validating that radar and communication requirements can be simultaneously satisfied at the antenna level.

Beyond shared-aperture operation, metasurfaces enable a deeper level of integration by embedding information processing directly into the electromagnetic aperture. The concept of the information metasurface illustrates how a single programmable surface can simultaneously perform electromagnetic sensing and wireless communication without requiring large-scale conventional antenna arrays.126 By applying spatially random or time-varying coding patterns, the metasurface samples scattered fields for imaging or direction-of-arrival estimation while concurrently forming directive beams to transmit the sensed information via phase-encoded modulation. In this framework, sensing and communication are not merely functions but are intrinsically intertwined at the physical layer, leveraging the same programmable aperture for both perception and data transmission.

A critical step toward practical JRSC is the realization of simultaneous, rather than time-multiplexed, sensing and communication. For radar applications in dynamic environments, adaptability is essential. Adaptive reconfigurable metasurfaces extend the JRSC concept by integrating sensing and control within the surface architecture. By incorporating embedded detectors and local control circuitry, these systems can monitor incident wave characteristics and adjust their reflection profiles in near real time.127 Such closed-loop operation enables beam stabilization and adaptive alignment under angular variations, which is particularly relevant for mobile radar platforms and radar-assisted communication links. While full autonomy remains an active research topic, these demonstrations highlight the feasibility of feedback-assisted JRSC at the aperture level.

Beyond spatial multiplexing and adaptive control, interference management becomes a fundamental challenge when radar and communication share the same spectral resources. Space-time-coding metasurfaces offer an elegant solution by introducing temporal modulation to generate harmonic frequency components. As shown in Figure 12B, communication signals can be transmitted at the fundamental frequency, while radar sensing exploits higher-order harmonics for echo analysis.76 This harmonic-domain separation enables frequency-domain multiplexing within a single metasurface platform, reducing mutual interference provided that harmonic isolation and filtering are properly engineered.

The extension of metasurface-enabled JRSC to sub-terahertz frequencies further demonstrates the scalability of this paradigm. In Figure 12C, phase-change-material-based metasurfaces illustrate that high-gain beam steering, high-speed communication, and fine-resolution sensing can be integrated within a unified surface at sub-THz frequencies.128 These implementations highlight the synergy between material-level reconfigurability and JRSC, supporting increased data throughput alongside enhanced angular and spatial resolution. Although practical challenges such as loss, switching speed, and thermal management remain, these studies indicate promising directions for next-generation high-frequency JRSC systems.

For clarity, the multiplexing strategies adopted in metasurface-based JRSC systems can be broadly categorized into three dimensions: time-domain multiplexing, frequency-domain multiplexing, and aperture-domain (or spatial-domain) multiplexing. Although all of them aim to enable the coexistence of sensing and communication within a shared platform, they differ in how the available physical degrees of freedom are allocated, as well as in their achievable simultaneity, implementation complexity, and function coupling. Overall, the development of reconfigurable metasurfaces for JRSC reflects a clear progression: from shared-aperture coexistence, through aperture-level information fusion and spatial multiplexing, to adaptive control and space-time-frequency-domain decoupling. As summarized in Figure 12, these experimental and system-level advances align with broader perspectives identifying metasurfaces as a key enabling technology for integrated sensing and communication. At the same time, important challenges remain, including accurate system modeling, control overhead, scalability of large apertures, harmonic leakage, and hardware nonidealities.75,77 Addressing these issues will be essential for translating metasurface-enabled JRSC from laboratory demonstrations into robust radar systems for automotive, aerospace, and future perceptive communication networks. It should also be noted that metasurface-enabled JRSC generally involves an inherent performance trade-off between communication and sensing when the two functions share the same aperture, spectrum, and coding resources. On the communication side, higher data rate and lower bit-error rate usually favor stable beamforming, sufficient spectral efficiency, and communication-oriented coding patterns. On the sensing side, however, high detection probability, fine angular/range resolution, and robust parameter estimation often require wider effective bandwidth, richer waveform diversity, longer coherent integration, or more sensing-oriented aperture coding. As a result, allocating more degrees of freedom to communication may reduce the resources available for radar waveform design and echo discrimination, whereas prioritizing sensing performance may constrain communication throughput, beam flexibility, or transmission reliability.

These trade-offs are manifested differently in different JRSC architectures. In shared-aperture systems, the radar and communication functions are mainly coupled through common hardware and radiation resources, so the design challenge lies in balancing antenna gain, beam pointing, and spectral occupancy within a unified aperture. In spatial-domain multiplexing, independent beams can reduce direct interference between the two functions, but the overall performance is still limited by the finite aperture size, coding resolution, and modulation speed. In harmonic-domain or space-time-coded JRSC, communication and sensing can be separated into different frequency components, which helps alleviate spectral conflict, but harmonic leakage, conversion efficiency, and filtering complexity introduce new constraints on both radar observability and communication quality. Therefore, different metasurface JRSC schemes should be understood as offering different ways to balance the sensing–communication trade-off, rather than completely removing the underlying resource competition.

Discussion

RMSs have introduced a paradigm shift in radar systems by enabling electromagnetic wave manipulation, signal generation, modulation, and even information processing directly at the physical interface. As reviewed in the preceding sections, RMS-based radar platforms demonstrate versatile capabilities across waveform synthesis, radar deception and jamming, scattering control and stealth, DOA estimation, and joint radar–communication systems. Despite these significant advances, several fundamental challenges must be addressed before RMS-based radar architectures can achieve widespread practical deployment. These challenges, and the corresponding opportunities, are discussed below from the perspectives of performance limitations, scalability and system integration, intelligence and adaptivity, and application prospects.

Performance limitations

The performance of RMS-based radar systems is intrinsically constrained by response speed, modulation bandwidth, and signal quality. Unlike conventional digital radar architectures, where waveform agility is primarily governed by digital signal processing capability and RF front-end bandwidth, RMS-based approaches depend on the physical reconfiguration of subwavelength meta-atoms. The switching speed of active components, such as PIN diodes, varactors, or MEMS devices, together with the latency of biasing networks and control circuitry, directly limits the achievable temporal modulation rate. Consequently, implementing high-order modulation schemes, ultra-wideband waveform synthesis, and rapid real-time reconfiguration remains challenging, particularly in high-resolution radar applications or scenarios involving fast-moving targets.

Possible solutions may be considered from two main directions. The first is to explore new switching mechanisms that can support faster temporal modulation and thus enable broader-band waveform synthesis. The second is to employ higher-speed switching elements and driving circuits so as to improve the effective modulation rate of the metasurface. Meanwhile, this limitation does not rule out all practical applications. RMS-based FMCW generation remains valuable in scenarios where compactness, low cost, low power consumption, and multifunctional integration are prioritized over ultrafine range resolution. In addition, for some applications such as radar deception and countermeasure-oriented waveform generation, the main purpose is often frequency manipulation or false-target generation rather than high-precision ranging, so moderate bandwidth may still be acceptable.

In addition, time-modulated RMS systems exhibit an inherent trade-off between modulation bandwidth and signal fidelity. While harmonic generation and frequency translation enable compact and hardware-efficient signal synthesis, they also introduce spurious spectral components, intermodulation products, phase noise, and amplitude distortion. These nonidealities can degrade SNR, elevate sidelobe levels, and reduce parameter-estimation accuracy in applications such as micro-Doppler synthesis, high-resolution range profiling, and DOA estimation. Furthermore, amplitude and phase quantization errors, mutual coupling between adjacent meta-atoms, bias-line parasitism, and fabrication tolerances impose additional constraints on achievable performance, especially for large-aperture implementations and operation at millimeter-wave or sub-terahertz frequencies.

Scalability and integration

Scalability and system-level integration remain major challenges for the practical deployment of RMS-based radar systems. As aperture size and the number of meta-atoms increase, the complexity of control networks, bias routing, power distribution, calibration, and thermal management grows rapidly. Large-scale RMS arrays require densely integrated biasing circuits and high-speed control interfaces, which can substantially increase hardware overhead, electromagnetic interference risks, and failure probability. Ensuring uniform amplitude–phase response, minimizing mutual coupling, and maintaining long-term stability across thousands of reconfigurable elements are nontrivial engineering tasks, particularly under wide-angle illumination and dynamic operating conditions. These challenges become even more pronounced at millimeter-wave and sub-terahertz frequencies, where fabrication tolerances and parasitic effects critically influence performance.

From a system perspective, the integration of RMS with antennas, RF front-ends, and signal processing units has yet to be comprehensively optimized. Most existing studies emphasize proof-of-concept demonstrations of isolated RMS-enabled functionalities, while comparatively limited attention has been devoted to holistic system co-design. In practical radar architectures, antenna radiation characteristics, metasurface modulation strategies, and backend signal processing algorithms are tightly coupled. Consequently, antenna–RMS co-design and cross-layer optimization spanning electromagnetic modeling, circuit implementation, and signal processing will be essential for achieving robust performance gains. Moreover, in multifunctional systems such as integrated sensing and communication, the hardware simplification afforded by RMS may shift complexity toward control logic, scheduling, and resource management. This redistribution of complexity necessitates new system architectures that balance electromagnetic programmability with computational efficiency and real-time coordination.

Conformal metasurfaces for practical scattering manipulation

Although many existing demonstrations of reconfigurable metasurfaces are developed on planar platforms, practical radar targets and carrier structures are often characterized by curved and irregular surfaces. In this sense, conformal metasurfaces are of particular importance for practical scattering manipulation, because they provide improved geometric compatibility with realistic platforms and enable radar-signature control, stealth design, and feature manipulation directly on nonplanar surfaces.

In fact, conformal metasurfaces have already been exemplified in this review. As shown in Figure 6B, a conformal dual-polarized metasurface was employed for wide-angle SAR deception, demonstrating the feasibility of radar imaging manipulation under curved-surface conditions. Likewise, Figure 7B presents a flexible conformal multifunctional time-varying metasurface implemented in a cylindrical configuration, which enables wide-angle HRRP spoofing through joint control of amplitude, frequency, and polarization. These examples indicate that conformal metasurfaces are not only relevant to deception-oriented scenarios but also highly promising for practical scattering manipulation and adaptive radar-signature management on realistic targets.

Compared with planar implementations, conformal metasurfaces offer several practical advantages. First, they can be more naturally integrated with vehicles, aircraft, missiles, and other nonplanar platforms, thereby reducing geometric mismatch between the functional surface and the protected or manipulated object. Second, they provide a more realistic route toward wide-angle scattering regulation, since the local electromagnetic response can be distributed over curved surfaces rather than concentrated on a single flat panel. Third, conformal configurations may facilitate the integration of multiple functionalities, such as stealth, scattering shaping, and radar feature transformation, within the structural envelope of the platform itself.

At the same time, conformal metasurfaces introduce additional challenges. The curvature of the supporting surface may alter the local electromagnetic response of the meta-atoms and complicate phase compensation, calibration, and system-level optimization. In addition, flexible or conformal implementations often involve trade-offs among electromagnetic performance, mechanical robustness, fabrication complexity, and long-term stability. Therefore, future progress in this direction will require joint advances in conformal electromagnetic design, flexible materials and devices, deformation-aware calibration, and system-level integration.

Overall, conformal metasurfaces represent an important step from planar proof-of-concept demonstrations toward practical radar applications of reconfigurable metasurfaces. Their ability to combine scattering manipulation with realistic platform compatibility makes them a particularly valuable direction for future research and deployment.

Intelligence and adaptivity

One of the most promising directions for RMS-based radar systems lies in their potential for intelligence and adaptivity. By integrating RMS with AI frameworks, radar platforms can evolve from static or preprogrammed configurations toward environment-aware, context-driven, and goal-oriented operation. In such architectures, the metasurface no longer acts merely as a programmable reflector, but as an adaptive electromagnetic interface capable of dynamically reshaping wave–matter interactions in response to sensed conditions. Representative directions include adaptive scattering control based on environmental feedback, intelligent waveform synthesis tailored to specific targets or interference patterns, and cognitive radar deception strategies that respond to adversarial signal-processing schemes. These developments point toward a new class of radar systems in which electromagnetic programmability and algorithmic intelligence are deeply intertwined.

Despite this promise, achieving real-time intelligent control of large-scale RMS arrays remains highly challenging. The control space grows exponentially with the number of meta-atoms and modulation states, rendering exhaustive search or traditional optimization methods computationally prohibitive under strict latency constraints. Learning-based approaches must therefore operate under limited training data, dynamic environments, and potential model mismatch between simulation and hardware implementation. Issues of generalization, stability, robustness to noise, and resilience to adversarial perturbations are particularly critical in radar applications. Furthermore, purely data-driven control risks violating physical constraints such as power conservation, bandwidth limits, or stability margins. Consequently, embedding electromagnetic priors, physical constraints, and model-based regularization into learning frameworks will be essential for ensuring interpretable, reliable, and safe RMS control in real-world radar systems. Hybrid physics-informed and data-driven strategies are likely to play a central role in enabling scalable and trustworthy intelligent metasurface-enabled radar platforms.

A further issue that deserves explicit attention is the real-world robustness of AI-assisted RMS control. At present, many learning-based studies are still validated mainly in simulation environments or static laboratory conditions, where clutter statistics, propagation paths, target dynamics, and hardware responses are relatively controlled. In realistic radar environments, especially under battlefield conditions, the electromagnetic scene may be affected by dynamic clutter, multipath propagation, platform motion, target maneuvering, interference, and adversarial perturbations. Under such conditions, the generalization capability of trained models remains difficult to predict, and their reported performance should therefore be interpreted with caution. For large-scale practical deployment, several prerequisites must be satisfied. First, model development must move beyond simulation-dominated training toward more realistic datasets and validation protocols that include environmental diversity, hardware nonidealities, and time-varying operating conditions. Second, robust sim-to-real transfer, domain generalization, and online adaptation mechanisms will be essential for maintaining performance when the operating environment deviates from the training distribution. Third, purely data-driven control is unlikely to be sufficient on its own; electromagnetic priors, physical constraints, and hardware feasibility must be embedded into the learning process to ensure safe and reliable control. Finally, practical deployment will also depend on system-level support, including closed-loop calibration, real-time feedback, control-network reliability, and long-term operational stability. Only under these conditions can intelligent RMS-enabled radar systems move from laboratory demonstrations toward scalable and trustworthy real-world applications.

Application prospects

RMS-based radar technologies demonstrate substantial potential across both defense and civilian domains. In defense applications, RMS platforms can support low-probability-of-intercept radar, adaptive electronic countermeasures, programmable stealth skins, and distributed sensing networks, thereby introducing new degrees of freedom for information-centric and perception-dominant operations. Their ability to dynamically manipulate waveform, scattering, Doppler signatures, and angular responses enables agile electromagnetic control in contested environments. In civilian scenarios, RMS-enabled radars may facilitate compact and cost-effective sensing solutions for autonomous vehicles, smart transportation infrastructure, unmanned aerial systems, environmental monitoring, and emerging 6G integrated sensing–communication paradigms. The hardware simplification and multifunctionality offered by metasurfaces align well with the growing demand for lightweight, energy-efficient, and reconfigurable sensing platforms.

Nevertheless, large-scale practical deployment requires rigorous consideration of electromagnetic compatibility (EMC), environmental robustness, and long-term operational reliability. The performance of reconfigurable metasurfaces can be sensitive to external electromagnetic interference, temperature fluctuations, humidity, mechanical deformation, and device aging. Moreover, large-aperture arrays introduce additional challenges in calibration stability, synchronization accuracy, and control-network reliability. Ensuring consistent and repeatable performance under harsh and dynamic conditions remains an open problem. Addressing these challenges through robust electromagnetic circuit co-design, adaptive calibration algorithms, redundancy-aware architectures, and comprehensive system-level validation will be essential for translating RMS-based radar systems from laboratory prototypes into dependable field-deployable technologies.

A critical issue for the practical deployment of reconfigurable metasurfaces in advanced radar systems is their behavior under high-power operation. While many existing demonstrations are developed and validated under laboratory-scale excitation conditions, practical airborne or spaceborne radar platforms may involve much stronger incident power, larger apertures, and more demanding requirements on long-term reliability. Under such conditions, commonly used active elements such as PIN diodes and varactors may suffer from thermal accumulation, nonlinear distortion, saturation, and even burnout, making thermal management and power-handling capability major engineering bottlenecks for real-world RMS applications.

To address this challenge, the existing literature suggests three representative directions. First, material-level thermal engineering can significantly improve heat spreading: Diamond-based metasurfaces and metasurface mirrors have been proposed for harsh or high-power environments because diamond provides exceptionally high thermal conductivity, mechanical robustness, and reduced temperature rise under intense irradiation.129,130 Second, structural heat-dissipation design offers a promising route for future RMS implementation: Integrated graphene-based metasurfaces have shown that replacing conventional metallic parts with thermally conductive materials can simultaneously support electromagnetic functionality and lower operating temperature, while broadband heat-dissipation metasurfaces further indicate that radiative-cooling-oriented designs may suppress thermal buildup at the system level.131,132 Third, loss reduction inside the functional region is also important for extending power tolerance: improving the thermal transport of silicon metasurfaces through crystallization was reported to markedly reduce temperature rise without sacrificing optical performance,133 and active conducting-oxide metasurfaces have experimentally demonstrated stable operation under continuous-wave irradiances up to the kW/cm2 level, indicating that careful active-material and device-stack design can enlarge the usable power range.134 Although most of these studies were conducted in optical or infrared regimes rather than radar-band RMS, they provide useful design guidance for future high-power radar metasurfaces, especially in substrate/material selection, integrated heat-spreading structures, and the mitigation of overheating in PIN-diode- or varactor-loaded architectures.

Conclusion

This review has provided a systematic and application-oriented overview of RMS in modern radar systems. By organizing recent advances into five core domains: radar signal generation and modulation, radar deception and jamming, scattering control and stealth, DOA estimation, and JRSC, we have highlighted how RMS fundamentally reshape radar architectures at the physical layer. A common theme across these domains is the use of programmable metasurfaces to directly control electromagnetic waves. This allows part of the functionality normally handled by complex RF front ends and digital signal processing to be implemented in the electromagnetic domain.

In radar signal generation and modulation, RMS demonstrates the capability to synthesize harmonics, generate FMCW and micro-Doppler signatures, and even embed dechirping operations directly into the aperture. In radar deception and jamming, time-varying and space–time coding metasurfaces enable coherent manipulation of Doppler signatures, HRRP profiles, SAR/ISAR images, multistatic consistency, and spectral distributions, shifting countermeasures from power-centric suppression toward perception-oriented manipulation. In stealth and scattering control, RMS evolves from static RCS reduction to adaptive, Doppler-aware, and even image-domain concealment strategies, extending radar signature control into spatial, spectral, temporal, and imaging dimensions. For DOA estimation, metasurfaces transition from engineered apertures to harmonic-domain encoders, compressive sensing platforms, learning-enabled inference engines, and ultimately wave-domain computing media, integrating sensing and computation within the propagation process. In JRSC systems, RMS support shared apertures, spatial multiplexing, harmonic-domain separation, and adaptive control, enabling deeper physical-layer integration of sensing and communication.

Collectively, these developments reveal a broader paradigm shift: RMS-based radar systems no longer treat electromagnetic structures as passive radiators or reflectors, but as programmable electromagnetic processors capable of manipulating waveform, spectrum, angle, motion signature, and even image formation. This capability opens new degrees of freedom in radar design, offering compactness, multifunctionality, and dynamic reconfigurability within a single, digitally controlled aperture.

Despite these transformative opportunities, several challenges remain before RMS-based radar platforms can achieve widespread deployment. Performance limitations arising from switching speed, modulation bandwidth, harmonic distortion, quantization errors, and mutual coupling constrain achievable signal fidelity and agility. Scalability issues related to biasing networks, control overhead, calibration stability, and thermal management become increasingly critical for large-aperture and high-frequency implementations. Moreover, integrating RMS with antennas, RF front ends, and signal processing backends requires cross-layer co-design to fully realize system-level benefits.

Looking forward, the convergence of RMS with artificial intelligence, adaptive control, and integrated sensing–communication architectures is expected to further expand the functional boundaries of radar systems. Intelligent metasurface platforms that incorporate environmental sensing, feedback control, and physics-informed learning may enable cognitive radar behaviors, dynamic electromagnetic environment shaping, and robust operation in complex and contested scenarios. Achieving this vision will require coordinated advances in materials, device engineering, electromagnetic modeling, circuit integration, control algorithms, and system architecture.

In summary, reconfigurable metasurfaces represent a powerful and transformative technology for next-generation radar systems. By bridging electromagnetic hardware and information processing, RMS redefines how radar signals are generated, manipulated, and interpreted. Continued interdisciplinary research and system-oriented innovation will be essential to unlock their full potential and translate laboratory demonstrations into reliable, scalable, and intelligent radar platforms for future defense and civilian applications.

Limitations of the study

Due to the rapid development of this field, some newly emerging studies may not yet be fully covered. In addition, many reported metasurface-based radar applications remain at the proof-of-concept stage, making direct quantitative comparison and practical evaluation difficult.
