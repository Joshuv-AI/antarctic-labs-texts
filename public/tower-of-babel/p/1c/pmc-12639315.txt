Feeding leghorn chicks during simulated transport as an early feeding strategy and the benefits of a single spray probiotic application
Authors: S Jhetam, T Shynkaruk, T Hamaoka, TG Crowe, A Jeffery, MB Rogers, AG Van Kessel, K Schwean-Lardner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined early, interrupted, or no feeding, with (P) or without (N) a spray-on probiotic (Lactobacillus reuteri), on chick stress, growth, gastrointestinal tract (GIT) development, microbiota, and gene expression during and after simulated transport (ST; 24 Hz vibration, 25°C, uncontrolled humidity). Lohmann LSL-Lite chicks (n = 1520) underwent 24 h of ST (5 h post-hatch) with either 24, 16, 8, or 0 h of feed access (FA) during ST with P or N treatment (trt). Following ST, birds were housed in pens for 14 d (5 replications/trt). Body weight, blood chemistry and heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H/L) ratio, relative length, weight and histomorphology of the GIT, quantitative PCR for ileum expression of genes related to inflammation and gut-barrier function, and microbiome analysis were assessed. At d 7 and 14, P birds were heavier. On d 1 (post-ST), H/L ratios were higher in the P trt, possibly from immunostimulation. Blood pH was lowest, and ionized calcium was highest in the 8-h trt. Glucose was higher in the N trt, and more dehydration occurred in the 0- and 8-h trt. The 0-h trt had reduced small intestine length and weight, and jejunum weight was highest in the P trt on d1. On d 7, duodenum and ileum weight were lowest for 8 h and N, respectively. Histomorphology revealed interactions in the ileum and cecum, suggestive of P preventing delayed gut development with interrupted FA. On d 1, ileum IL-6 expression was greater for 0 compared to 24 h. The P trt had a lower microbiome diversity but may have improved gut development with interrupted FA and prevented dehydration in the 0- and 8-h trt. Interrupting FA after 8 h had more negative effects than 0-h FA. With 0-h FA during ST, GIT development was delayed and could have contributed to ileum inflammation. Early FA had a positive effect on leghorn chick health, gut development, and growth. One post-hatch spray application of P may improve chick well-being over long transport durations.Keywords: Lactobacillus reuteri, Gene expression, Microbiota, Blood chemistry, Delayed feeding

Introduction

The post-hatch period is a critical phase for commercial poultry, significantly influencing health, productivity, and welfare (Decuypere et al., 2001; Mitchell, 2009; Ven et al., 2013). Standard industry practice in North America is to provide chicks with first feed when they arrive on farm. In Canada, the distances from hatcheries to farms are highly variable, and as a result, the time from hatch to placement can range widely. The residual yolk sac provides 90 % of the chick’s energy during the second half of incubation and up to 72-h post-hatch (Speake et al., 1998; Sato et al., 2006), allowing for delayed access to feed. The size and composition of the residual yolk at hatch and the metabolic rate of the embryo influence the utilization of the available yolk (Nangsuay et al., 2013). The Canadian Codes of Practice for the Care and Handling of Hatching Eggs, Breeders, Chickens, and Turkeys recommends that gel pucks or alternative sources of hydration be provided if the time between hatch and placement on farm exceeds 24-h (National Farm Animal Care Council, 2016).

Recent studies have investigated the impact of early or delayed feed access (FA) using various methods to provide early FA, with the majority focusing on broilers. Some compared housing chicks with immediate FA to various holding times at the hatchery before housing with FA, and others compared immediate housing with immediate or delayed FA (e.g. Tamboli et al., 2018; Cardeal et al., 2020; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2020, 2022; Qu et al., 2021; Hassan et al., 2023). Other studies compared on-farm hatching models (early FA) to conventional hatching in a commercial hatchery (delayed FA) or compared traditional systems to the HatchCare system (HatchTech B.V., Veenendaal, The Netherlands) (e.g. Sirsat et al., 2018; Giersberg et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2021; Özlü et al., 2022; Molenaar et al., 2023; Broekmeulen et al., 2024). The HatchCare technology is a type of incubation and chick transport system that provides chicks with immediate access to feed and water after hatching in the hatcher. They remain with access to feed and water in the baskets used to transport them to farm. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have examined early and delayed FA as it relates to production, stress, or gut health in layer pullet chicks (Selim et al., 2021; Broekmeulen et al., 2024); however, these studies used a non-commercial leghorn-cross strain. To the best of our knowledge, the current research regarding early FA has not been examined in the context of the added environmental stressor of transport, nor has the impact of FA interruption been examined before arrival on farm. Interrupted FA could occur when chicks start on feed and then either run out or can’t find it before arrival on farm. The poultry industry has suggested that the GIT may be negatively impacted by the interruption of early post-hatch feed FA; however, there is no scientific evidence to support this claim.

When chicks ingest exogenous feed, rapid development of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) occurs (Uni et al., 1998). Delayed FA may lead to a decrease in intestinal enterocyte length and villus surface area, which can negatively affect growth and nutrient utilization (Sklan, 2001; Jha et al., 2019). A meta-analysis of available literature on the effects of delayed post-hatch feed and water access for up to 48-h found significantly less development of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum in broiler chicks (Jong et al., 2017). It was evident that delayed post-hatch feed and water access for up to 48-h led to reduced final body weight and greater mortality up to 6 wk of age. However, the meta-analysis results show that the adverse effects of delayed feed and water access on GIT development were short-term (up to 2 wk post-hatch).

The gut barrier and immune system are not fully developed post-hatch; therefore, early feeding strategies may help protect the gut against pathogenic bacteria and toxins post-hatch from the possible maturation of goblet cells when exposed to exogenous feed or bacteria (Filho et al., 2019; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2020). Thus, the upregulation of genes related to gut barrier mucosal development, such as Mucin 2 (MUC2), could occur (Kim and Ho, 2010; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2020). The outer mucus layer protects the intestinal epithelium from pathogens and damage, provides a site for commensal bacteria (Linden et al., 2008), supports brush border enzymes, and aids in nutrient absorption (Smirnov et al., 2006). Research has demonstrated that the regulation of genes related to gut barrier function and immune response mainly occurs in the ileum (Palamidi and Mountzouris, 2018; Barekatain et al., 2019; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2020). Another strategy for enhancing gut development and barrier function post-hatch could be the provision of probiotics. Lactic acid bacteria, such as Lactobacillus reuteri, occur naturally in the gut of poultry and produce an acidic environment that is unfavorable to pathogens which can help maintain a balanced gut microbiome by promoting beneficial bacteria (Xie et al., 2019; Sureshkumar et al., 2022). The positive effects of probiotics on performance, immunity, microbiome composition, and antimicrobial effects have been widely studied in poultry (as reviewed by Halder et al. (2024)). Therefore, its application in early feeding strategies may be beneficial when FA is delayed or interrupted.

The objective of this study was to assess the effects of early post-hatch feeding, interrupted early feeding, or no feeding during simulated transport (ST) on health, gut development, microbiome composition, and expression of genes related to small intestinal absorption, development, and gut barrier function in leghorn chicks raised to 14 d of age. This study also examined the effects of a commercial spray-on probiotic (L. reuteri) provided prior to ST.

Materials and methods

The experimental procedures for this study were approved by the University of Saskatchewan Animal Care Committee (AUP 20220079), and all birds were cared for as specified in the Guide to the Care and Use of Experimental Animals (Canadian Council on Animal Care, 2017).

Experimental design

A total of 1520 Lohmann LSL-Lite chicks were incubated and hatched at the University of Saskatchewan Poultry Research and Teaching Unit. Hatching eggs were collected from a commercial hatchery and were sourced from a 48-wk breeder flock. At hatch, birds were quality-checked and feather sexed (Lohmann Tierzucht, 2018). Sex was balanced for each treatment, with 19 males and 19 females randomly placed in 1 of 5 transport boxes (replicate unit) for a total of 38 chicks per box (5 replications/treatment; 190 chicks/treatment). A total of 20 transport boxes (56×43 cm) were divided in half by a cardboard divider with holes allowing for ventilation. Therefore, each half of the box was considered its own transport box replicate, resulting in 40 boxes that more closely met stocking density recommendations during transport in Canada (National Farm Animal Care Council, 2016). Plastic feeders (with or without feed, depending on treatment) measuring 30×7 cm (210 cm²) were placed in each transport box. Therefore, all birds had 27.6 cm2 of floor space in each box (Figure S1).

Eight treatments (trt) were tested in this study. Exposure to trt occurred during the ST period only. Birds were given FA for various durations either with or without a probiotic (Lactobacillus reuteri at 1.0 × 107 CFU/mL; FINELACT® Chick, Asahi Biosciences Inc., Peachtree City, GA), abbreviated as P or N, respectively. This included FA for 24 h (full duration of ST), the first 16 or 8 h (interrupted FA), or no FA (0 h). The same amount of commercial starter feed (290 g dry weight; Table S1) was provided to the chicks in the 24P, 24 N, 16P, 16 N, 8P, and 8 N treatments for the designated duration within the 24-h ST period. To stimulate feeding, the feed was moistened with 120 mL of distilled water. The P was stored at −20°C and brought to room temperature 3 h prior to application. Fifty grams of P was mixed with 1950 mL of room-temperature distilled water until it was fully dissolved, and then it was placed in a sterile spray bottle. Before the ST period, birds in the P trt were sprayed evenly until their fluff was dampened in a separate enclosed area to prevent P contamination of birds in the N trt. Birds in the N trt were sham-treated with distilled water only until their fluff was dampened.

Simulated Transport. The ST period took place 5 h after removing chicks from the hatcher. Chicks were placed on a vibration table for 24-h and were exposed to horizontal harmonic oscillations with a frequency of 24 Hz and an amplitude of displacement of 0.07 mm to simulate vibration that occurs during highway transport. Birds in the P trt were placed on one side of the vibration table with a polyethylene plastic partition between the N trt birds to prevent cross-contamination. Light (3 lux at each box level, LED bulbs) was provided to ensure chicks could identify feeders. The room temperature was set at 26°C with uncontrolled humidity. After 8 and 16 h, feeders were removed and weighed from the boxes in the 8 N and 8P trt, and 16 N and 16P treatments, respectively. The empty feeders were returned and remained in the trt boxes for the duration of the ST period to maintain stocking density. At the end of the 24-h period, feeders were removed and weighed from the 24 N and 24P trt. The average feed consumed during ST was 1.67, 1.32, 0.89, and 0.00 g/bird for the 24, 16, 8, and 0 h trt, respectively.

Day 1 to 14. After 24-h ST, chicks not euthanized for data collections were housed in 40 floor pens (1.5 × 3.0 m = 4.5 m2) with 32 chicks/pen (pen as replicate unit; 5 replications per trt) to 14 d of age (d of hatch was considered d 0). Birds were provided with 1 aluminum tube feeder, 1 supplemental feeder, and 2 bell drinkers. Wood shavings (7-10 cm depth) were supplied as the bedding source. Birds in the P trt were kept on one side of the facility, and cross-contamination was limited by wearing different shoes and personal protective equipment in that area.

Data Collection

Body Weight. Chicks were weighed (group BW) at hatch, post-ST (d 1), and at d 7 and 14 to calculate average BW.

Heterophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio. Blood was collected (2 chicks/replicate; 10 chicks/trt) to assess the heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H/L) ratio at hatch, post-ST, and d 7. Blood was collected from the jugular vein into K2 EDTA S-monovette tubes. Birds were euthanized immediately after blood collection. Blood smears were prepared on the day of blood collection. After drying, the slides were stored in slide boxes until stained with PROTOCOLTM Hema3TM (Fisher Scientific; Ottawa, Canada). The number of heterophils and lymphocytes was counted under 100x oil magnification until a total of 100 cells was reached (microscope B-290 TB; Optika; Bergamo, Italy) to determine the H/L ratio.

Blood Chemistry. Pre-ST, 5 control birds, and post-ST, 10 chicks/trt (2 chicks/replicate) had blood collected to assess blood pH, electrolytes, glucose, and hematocrit (packed cell volume; PCV). The jugular vein was exposed, and a 21 G needle was inserted. The needle was then removed, and a lithium heparin Microvette tube was placed at the puncture site. Blood was collected then transferred via pipette into an i-STAT cartridge (CG8+) before being placed into a portable blood analyzer (Abott VetScan i-STAT Alinity; IL, USA) for analysis.

Yolk Sac Weight. At hatch, post-ST (d 1), and d 2, the 10 chicks/trt (2 chicks/replicate) used for blood collections were euthanized via cervical dislocation and yolk sacs were removed and weighed.

Tissue Collections. Small intestines were collected from the same 10 chicks/trt used for blood and yolk sac collections post-ST (d 1). On d 2 and 7, 10 chicks/trt (2 chicks/replicate) were euthanized via cervical dislocation. Tissue collections at all ages included the length and full weight of the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and ceca, as well as histomorphology, gene expression, and microbiome analysis.

Histomorphology. From the tissues collected on d 1 and 2, 3-cm sections of the jejunum, ileum, and whole cecum were placed in formalin. 48-h after formalin fixation, samples were transferred to 70 % ethanol. Tissue samples were submitted to an external laboratory (Prairie Diagnostic Services, Saskatoon, SK, Canada) for paraffin embedding, sectioning, and staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). An individual blinded to treatment identified 5-10 well-oriented villi and crypts per cross-section per bird, which were measured using an Axiostar plus light microscope and AxioVision 4.1 measurement software (Carl Zeiss Canada Ltd., Toronto, ON). Mean values were recorded for villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD), and villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was calculated.

Gene Expression. From the same tissues collected on d 1 and 7, a 3-cm section of tissue from the ileum was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Analyses included assessment of the following genes of interest: IL-6, IL-8, MUC2, Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA), Sodium Glucose Co-Transporter-1 (SGLT-1), and Peptide Transporter-1 (PEPT-1). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA was extracted from frozen ileal tissue using TRIzol™ Reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). cDNA was synthesized from 2 µg of RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems®, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples with a 260/280 ratio between 1.80 and 2.00 were retained for gene expression analysis. For quantitative PCR, each 20-µL reaction contained 2 µL of cDNA and 10 µL of KAPA SYBR PCR Master Mix (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA) with 0.4 mM primer. The qPCR (Step One Plus thermocycler, Applied Biosystems®) conditions were 95°C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles with denaturation at 95°C for 15 s and annealing at 52-61°C for 30 s (Table S2). Samples were run in duplicates, and specificity was confirmed by melt curve analysis. Primer efficiency ranged between 90 % and 120 %.

Standard curves were prepared using a 10-fold dilution series of pooled cDNA in duplicate, assigning an arbitrary value to the highest concentration standard. Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH), Ribosomal Protein L30 (RPL-30), Succinate Dehydrogenase Complex Subunit A, and Transferrin Receptor Protein 1 were analyzed as reference genes. Only GAPDH was unaffected by treatment. The mean of the arbitrary value for the housekeeping gene, which was interpolated from the standard curve, was divided by the arbitrary value of the gene of interest to normalize expression values (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001; Hamaoka, 2021).

Microbiome. Contents from one cecum were collected from the birds euthanized on d 2 and 7 for tissue collection. On d 2, the whole cecum was collected and on d 7, contents were collected by squeezing and emptying the cecum. The cecum or contents were placed in cryotubes with 1.5 mL of buffered peptone water and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total genomic DNA was extracted by bead-beating method using the MagMAX Microbiome Ultra Nucleic Acid Isolation kit (Applied Biosystems®, Waltham, MA, USA). Universal primers corresponding to the V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene were employed for PCR amplification (Kapa HiFi HS RM; Roche Holding AG, Basel, Switzerland), and sequence sizes were selected using the NucleoMag® NGS Clean-up and Size Select kit (Takara Bio USA, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). The resulting amplicons were sequenced on the MiSeq Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Samples were multiplexed, aiming for an average sequencing depth of 25000 to 30000 sequences per sample.

Paired-end 16S amplicon sequence reads were demultiplexed using the QIIME 2 package (Bolyen et al., 2019). The first 22 base pairs of the reads were trimmed, and both forward and reverse reads were truncated at 230 base pairs at which point a decline in the average Phred score of randomly sampled reads was observed. The DADA2 algorithm (QIIME 2 v2024.10) was used to predict amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). ASVs were aligned using MAFFT (Katoh and Standley, 2013), and a phylogenetic tree was generated using Fasttree. Lastly, a naïve-Bayes feature classifier was trained on the V3-V4 region of 16-rRNA using the Greengenes database (v13.8). The ASV table, sample metadata, phylogenetic tree, and taxonomic table were converted to a Biom format table and imported into R as a Phyloseq object using the Phyloseq library. Samples with total amplicon abundances less than 1000 were removed from the analysis. After this filtering step, 69 samples remained for analysis, comprising 2655 ASVs.

Alpha diversity and beta diversity calculations were conducted using both Vegan (Oksanen et al., 2017) and Phyloseq (McMurdie and Holmes, 2013). Alpha diversity was calculated using both the observed species richness index and the Shannon index, and differences between groups were tested using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Beta diversity (between-sample distances) was calculated using Bray-Curtis and Weighted-Unifrac distances implemented, and a principal coordinate (PCoA) ordination was calculated using the Vegan library. Group differences were plotted using the Vegan Adonis2 multivariate analysis, using d, time on feed, and P trt as variables. Differences in composition at the Genus level were tested using the ANCOM2 R library and the MAASLIN2 R library.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed as a 2 × 4 factorial arrangement to identify interactions between P trt and time on feed during ST. A two-way ANOVA was performed using the MIXED procedure in SPSS 29 (SPSS Inc, IBM Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA) to investigate the interactions between P trt and time on feed and a Tukey’s range test was performed. If interactions were not significant, the main effects of time on feed and/or P trt were presented. Data were checked for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and equal variances (Levene’s test) prior to analyses. Relative gene expression data were log+1 transformed to meet normality before performing the two-way ANOVA, and the data were back-transformed when reporting means. Differences were considered significant when P < 0.05.

Results

The main effect results are presented for time on feed (24, 16, 8, or 0 h) and P trt (P or N) when the interaction was not significant. For significant interactions, the trt will be referred to as time on feed by P trt (24P, 24 N, 16P, 16 N, 8P, 8 N, 0P or 0 N).

Body weight

Pre-ST (d 0) and on d 1 (post-ST) BW did not differ between time on feed or P trt (Table 1). At d 7 and 14, there was no effect of time on feed on BW. However, birds in the P trt had a higher BW compared to the N trt at d 7 (P = 0.04) and d 14 (P = 0.04).

Heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio

Pre-ST, H/L ratios did not differ between time on feed trt (1.03, 1.17, 1.19, and 0.91 for 24, 16, 8, and 0 h, respectively; P = 0.52) nor P trt (1.06 vs 1.09 for P and N, respectively; P = 0.87). On d 1, H/L ratios did not differ between time on feed (1.36, 1.23, 1.33, and 1.33 for 24, 16, 8, and 0 h, respectively; P = 0.86); however, birds had a higher ratio of 1.51 in the P trt compared to the N trt of 1.11 (P = 0.002). At d 7, H/L ratios did not differ between time on feed (0.33, 0.32, 0.38, and 0.33 for 24, 16, 8, and 0 h, respectively; P = 0.70) nor P trt (0.31 vs 0.37, for P and N, respectively; P = 0.12).

Blood chemistry

Sodium and potassium were unaffected by time on feed and P trt. Blood pH was lower in the 8-h trt compared to the 16- and 24-h trt (P = 0.05), but did not differ from the 0-h trt (Table 2). Blood ionized calcium (iCa) was higher in the 8-h trt compared to the 24-h trt (P = 0.03) but did not differ from the 16- and 0-h trt. There was a lower concentration of iCa in the P trt compared to the N trt (P = 0.05). Blood glucose was lower in the P trt compared to the N trt (P = 0.04) but was not affected by time on feed. There was an interaction between time on feed and P trt for hematocrit (P = 0.02) and hemoglobin values (P = 0.02) (Fig. 1). For both parameters, the mean values were higher in the 24P, 8 N, and 0 N trt.

Yolk sac weight

There were no differences between time on feed or P trt, and no interactions were observed for yolk sac weight pre-ST at d 1 or 2 (Table S3).

At d 1 (post-ST), duodenum, ileum, and ceca length did not differ between time on feed nor P trt (Table 3). Jejunum length was longer in the 16-h trt compared to the 0-h trt (P = 0.03), with no differences observed between the 24- and 8-h trt. Jejunum weight was heavier in chicks in the 24- and 16-h trt compared to the 0-hr trt (P < 0.01), but did not differ from the 8-h trt. Jejunum weight was also heavier in birds in the P vs the N trt (P = 0.01). Ileum weight was heavier in the 24-h trt compared to the 0-h trt (P = 0.02).

Histomorphology

In each small intestine segment on d 1(post-ST), CD (Table 4) was affected by time on feed. In the jejunum, the 24-h trt had a smaller CD compared to the 16-h trt (P = 0.03), and in the ileum, the 24- and 8-h trt had a smaller CD compared to the 0-h (P = 0.002). Cecum CD was greater in the 24- and 16-h trt compared to the 8-h trt (P < 0.001) and in the N trt compared to the P trt (P = 0.001). The jejunum VH:CD was smaller in the 0-h trt compared to the 16- and 8-h trt (P = 0.01), and ileum VH:CD was smallest in the 0-h trt compared to all other trt (P < 0.001). Jejunum VH:CD was greater in the P vs N trt (P = 0.02). On d 1, interactions were observed between time on feed and P trt for VH in the jejunum and ileum (Table 4). Discussion

The results of this study have highlighted a nuanced response across multiple tested parameters, where time on feed during ST influenced early small intestine development, and probiotic spray supplementation was associated with positive effects on performance, blood chemistry, and small intestine development, especially when FA was interrupted.

Contrary to previous studies reporting adverse effects of delayed FA (comparable to the 0-h trt) on BW in broiler chicks (Lingens et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Özlü et al., 2022; Hassan et al., 2023), we observed no effect of early, interrupted, or no FA on BW up to 14 d. However, comparisons to previous studies using broiler chicks, on-farm hatching, or varying times of feed deprivation prove difficult due to differences between experimental designs and the genetic growth performance and metabolism of leghorn chicks. One study examined interactions between immediate post-hatch feed and water access or delayed access to feed and water for 56 h in broilers with or without a 1-h transport period (Hollemans et al., 2018). The authors found no interactions between FA and transport on performance, but immediate post-hatch feeding resulted in higher BW until 28 d compared to the delayed trt. This study design differed significantly from ours as the authors used an on-farm hatching method using HatchCare baskets with immediate water and FA, followed by a 1-h transport period before returning to the farm. It was not stated whether feed was available during the transport period, making comparisons difficult.

As transport is considered a stressor, H/L ratios were used as a measure of chronic stress. However, no effect was observed between time on feed post-ST or at d 7. Madej et al. (2024) observed an increase in H/L ratios 1 d post-hatch when chicks were given early FA (HatchCare) compared to standard hatching with no effect on d 7. However, the authors did not state whether FA was interrupted during transport to the farm. Interestingly, in the present study, H/L ratios were higher post-ST in the P trt compared to the N trt. This was likely not a stress response but caused by the upregulation of heterophils from the immunostimulatory effects of early P exposure (Koenen et al., 2004a; b; Farnell et al., 2006; Kogut, 2019). Farnell et al. (2006) found a significant upregulation of heterophils 24 h post-hatch in chicks given Bacillus subtilis, Lactococcus lactis, or Lactobacillus acidophilus via oral gavage at hatch. This suggests that early gut colonization with a spray application of P (L. reuteri) is also effective at eliciting an immunostimulatory response in newly hatched chicks.

Blood chemistry results further support the systemic effects of P. Gut health was optimized with yeast probiotic supplementation, resulting in enhanced blood calcium absorption and retention in the GIT in studies with laying hens (Tapingkae et al., 2018; Attia et al., 2022). Lactobacillus spp. were found to rapidly hydrolyze starch and metabolize glucose (Chichlowski et al., 2007), as well as increase glucose transporter gene expression (Jahromi et al., 2016). Consistent with other studies, P application improved calcium and glucose absorption after 24 h. Interrupted FA after 8 h caused a lower blood pH and higher iCa compared to continuously fed birds. As sodium and potassium concentrations were numerically higher in the 8-h trt, it may suggest that interrupted FA could lead to acidosis causing increased iCa levels due to reduced calcium binding capacity to albumin (Goyal et al., 2023). Interactions between time on feed and P trt for hematocrit (PCV) and hemoglobin suggest that interrupted FA after 8 h or no FA without P caused more dehydration, similar to previous studies with delayed FA (Tamboli et al., 2018; Hassan et al., 2023). However, the addition of the spray P may have mitigated these effects during ST. Although the mechanism is not known, it may be related to reduced intestinal water loss resulting from improved gut development and function. The opposite effect on hydration was observed in the 24P trt post-ST, indicating the mechanisms are interactive with feed supply and interruption.

Post-ST jejunum length responded positively to both early and interrupted FA and P trt. Increased jejunum weight with continuous and interrupted FA after 16-h or P application, and increased ileum weight in the 24-h trt, suggest that early FA supports GIT growth and development (Uni et al., 1995; Sklan, 2001; Ganjali et al., 2015; Lamot, 2017; Jha et al., 2019; Sözcü et al., 2020; Hassan et al., 2023). Interestingly, ileum weight was also greater in P-trt birds at d 7, reinforcing the positive effects of P on distal gut regions where microbial density is increased. Histomorphology of VH and VH:CD ratio d 1 (post-ST) suggested that interrupted or no FA induced increased epithelial replacement in the jejunum and ileum as indicated by CD. Sözcü et al. (2020) had similar findings in chicks with delayed FA. The authors suggested that delayed feeding may have caused rapid consumption of the residual yolk sac, which can stimulate functional development and maturation of the GIT, such as increased VH and CD (Uni et al., 1995, 1998; Noy et al., 1996; Wagt et al., 2020). However, we found no impact of time on feed nor P trt on yolk sac utilization, similar to other studies (Gonzales et al., 2003; Brand et al., 2010). It is important to note that there have been conflicting results on yolk sac utilization with early feeding strategies in earlier studies (Noy et al., 1996; Noy and Sklan, 2001; El-Husseiny et al., 2008; Bhanja et al., 2009). By d 2, chicks given continuous or interrupted FA and N had a greater VH:CD, indicative of absorptive and digestive capacity, than 0 N birds. However, interactions showed that the ileum VH:CD was improved in birds in the interrupted FA (8- and 16-h) trt that were given the probiotic compared to continuous (24-h) or delay-fed birds (0-h). This suggests that a single spray application of P may prevent functional gut development delays if early FA is interrupted, which is supported by the higher BW results observed in the P trt up to 14 d.

Post-ST expression of IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine, was upregulated in birds not provided with feed during ST compared to continuous-fed birds, and this was also evident in previous early FA studies (Shinde et al., 2015; Tamboli et al., 2018; Miska et al., 2022). Thus, providing feed to chicks during ST and the early post-hatch period may prevent inflammation in the small intestine. Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al. (2020) found a delay in MUC-2 expression in chicks not provided with feed for 48-h and suggested that this could lead to a deterioration in gut barrier function. The small but statistically significant increase in MUC-2 expression observed in the 24- and 16-h trt in this study suggests that early FA may improve gut barrier function and immune defence, particularly during periods of stress and exposure to pathogens, such as during transport from the hatchery to farm.

There is evidence that the GIT of chicks at hatch is not sterile in some cases (Ding et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2019; Miska et al., 2022), however, early colonization is contributed primarily by environmental, non-avian sources of bacteria, resulting in a highly variable colonization of a chick’s GIT (Stanley et al., 2013). This likely occurs more often in chicks from a commercial hatchery. No effects of time on feed were observed on the cecal alpha or beta diversity in this study. Some studies have found that early or delayed FA had no impact on microbial diversity in the jejunum (Jong et al., 2022; Marcato et al., 2024). Contrary to our findings, other studies have reported that delayed FA is associated with lower alpha diversity in both the ileal and cecal microbiota (Cengiz et al., 2012; Miska et al., 2022; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2022). However, the age effects observed in this study on both alpha and beta diversity were consistent with previous literature (Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2022). Alpha diversity was lower in the P trt, which might be expected when inoculating the gut with a P formulation comprising a single bacterium (L. reuteri). Most of the dominant genus species in the cecum at d 2 were Escherichia Shigella, with a greater abundance in the P trt, specifically with interrupted feeding. Escherichia Shigella is a Proteobacteria and has been identified as one of the most abundant bacteria in leghorn chicks between d 0 and 3 (Videnska et al., 2014; Ballou et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2021). The GIT of neonatal animals contains some oxygen; thus, facultative anaerobic Proteobacteria can rapidly colonize the gut (Wenzl et al., 2001). Few studies have assessed early FA on post-hatch microbiome development and the majority have assessed broiler microbiota at older ages. Similar to our findings, delayed FA did not alter cecal microbiota species in broiler chicks post-hatch (Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2022; Marcato et al., 2024). Although Lactobacillus and overall microbiota abundance were not affected by L. reuteri P trt at d 2 or 7, several studies have identified that age is the major contributor to shifts and stabilization of the microbiota (Ballou et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2021; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2022; Sureshkumar et al., 2022). Xiao et al. (2021) determined that Lohmann Brown Lite leghorn gut microbiota were only stabilized at 50 d of age. Although minimal effects of time on feed and P trt were observed on the microbiota in this study, distinct effects of age on composition may reflect increased complexity of the gut microbiome.

This study demonstrated that early continuous FA during transport, such as from the hatchery to the farm, increased early intestinal development in leghorn chicks. Additionally, a single spray application of the probiotic L. reuteri before transport, such as what could be applied at the hatchery, had positive impacts on BW, immunostimulation, and gut development. Contrary to previous broiler-focused studies, early, interrupted, or no FA during ST did not affect BW through 14 d of age, potentially due to genetic differences and study design. The single spray application of P may have enhanced small intestine morphology and mitigated dehydration associated with interrupted feeding after 8 h and delayed FA. The improved small intestine development, reduced ileal inflammation, and enhanced absorption were due to both P supplementation and early FA, even when FA was interrupted after 16 h. Interrupting FA after 8 h, followed by 16 h of no FA, caused more negative effects on Ca absorption, small intestine weights, and dehydration post-ST than no FA, especially without L. reuteri application. Future research could examine this early feeding strategy during standard commercial truck transport to determine the extent of FA interruption. While time on feed and P did not affect cecal microbiota, likely due to the unstable microbiome of young chicks, administering a spray P at the hatchery prior to transport may protect chicks from GIT developmental and morphological disruptions if chicks are given FA before arrival on farm and subsequently run out of feed or can’t find it. Overall, providing leghorn chicks with feed during transport as an early feeding strategy can have a positive impact on their health and gut development.
