Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease and Reflux-like Phenotypes: Using Lyon Consensus 2.0 to Guide Clinical Approach
Authors: Matthew K Schroeder, C Prakash Gyawali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is common worldwide, but troublesome symptoms alone are not sufficient for a conclusive diagnosis. The Montreal Consensus introduced a symptom-based definition of GERD and defined mucosal injury phenotypes of the disease. The Lyon Consensus adapted mucosal injury findings as well as defined abnormal reflux burden criteria for the diagnosis of conclusive GERD. The diagnosis of esophageal disorders of gut–brain interaction (E-DGBI) using Rome V criteria requires exclusion of GERD, which is also described in the Lyon Consensus 2.0 criteria. Collectively utilizing these various criteria, distinct phenotypes can be identified in patients with troublesome reflux-like symptoms. The mucosal injury phenotype, initially defined by the Montreal Consensus, requires reflux management for healing of esophagitis to improve symptoms, and surveillance to identify and prevent GERD complications including Barrett’s esophagus and esophageal adenocarcinoma. The abnormal reflux burden phenotype requires reflux management for symptom improvement. The E-DGBI phenotype requires specific management of each of the functional disorders for symptom relief. Hypervigilance and symptom-specific anxiety can overlap with all the phenotypes, and E-DGBI can coexist with conclusive GERD. Therefore, there are intricate interrelationships between Montreal, Lyon and Rome criteria that aid evaluation of troublesome symptoms, and concepts from each of these contribute to accurate phenotyping and optimal management of patients.

INTRODUCTION

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is common worldwide, with prevalence of 8%-33%.1 Two decades ago, the Montreal Consensus introduced a symptom-based definition of GERD, where the reflux of gastric content causes troublesome symptoms, disease-specific complications, or both,2 but troublesome symptoms are not always indicative of GERD.3 The Lyon Consensus was conceived to identify features from esophageal testing that define GERD, as distinct from absence of GERD.4 When findings are inconclusive, the Lyon Consensus recommends further differentiation towards or away from conclusive GERD using adjunctive metrics from esophageal testing.4,5

Concurrent with these advances, Rome diagnostic criteria have evolved to non-erosive reflux disease (NERD) requiring symptoms, a normal endoscopy and abnormal reflux burden (Table 1).6,7 Esophageal disorders of gut–brain interaction (E-DGBI) require exclusion of GERD, making the Lyon Consensus directly relevant to Rome diagnoses.5 Hypervigilance and symptom-specific anxiety are prevalent across all reflux phenotypes,8 and E-DGBI can overlap with conclusive GERD.9 Thus, GERD and related symptom-based phenotypes constitute a family of disorders with distinct but related pathophysiology that includes disruption of the esophagogastric junction (EGJ), mucosal injury and complications, esophageal clearance, esophageal perception, and esophageal hypervigilance.10 This review addresses the interrelationships between Montreal, Rome and Lyon criteria in the clinical application of Lyon Consensus 2.0.

TROUBLESOME SYMPTOMS

Troublesome typical symptoms of heartburn, acid regurgitation and esophageal chest pain introduced by the Montreal Consensus are the starting point for empiric GERD treatment trials.2,5,9 Qualification and quantification of symptoms remain central to the approach to GERD, which directly impact health-related quality of life.2,11,12 Validated patient reported outcome instruments offer valuable insights into the likelihood of GERD,13 but are not reliable to diagnose GERD.14,15 Resolution of symptoms is an important therapeutic target, although prevention and treatment of mucosal inflammation, esophageal strictures, Barrett’s esophagus (BE), and esophageal adenocarcinoma are important considerations in both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients (Figure).16

Typical symptoms have a higher likelihood of response to antisecretory therapy compared to atypical symptoms,17 hence empiric treatment trials are offered in the absence of alarm symptoms. In randomized controlled trials, over 70% of patients with heartburn respond to short-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy, but placebo response can be as high as 25%.18 Approximately 50% of patients with regurgitation also report improvement with PPIs, but regurgitation can persist despite improvement of concurrent heartburn,19,20 and similar placebo response is reported.21 After ruling out cardiac etiologies, GERD is identified in up to 60% of patients with esophageal chest pain (Table 2), where PPI response can match that of heartburn (56-85% with at least 50% improvement) in contrast to patients without objective GERD where PPI response is not significantly better than with placebo.22 Using this data, PPI responsiveness has a sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 76% in discriminating GERD from non-GERD presentations in esophageal chest pain.23 However, not all PPI responders have underlying conclusive GERD. A study of 312 endoscopy negative patients with heartburn found that of 144 patients who experienced ≥ 75% symptom improvement with PPIs, only 55% had abnormal reflux burden on pH-impedance monitoring, while the remainder had functional syndromes including reflux hypersensitivity (RH, 26%) and functional heartburn (FH, 19%).3 Therefore, troublesome typical symptoms are a starting point for empiric therapy where response predicts presence of GERD with modest sensitivity (78%) albeit with low specificity (54%).17 The Lyon Consensus 2.0 requires conclusive GERD evidence on objective testing prior to escalation of management.

Laryngopharyngeal symptoms (LPS) encompass several atypical symptoms that are poorly responsive to PPI, including chronic cough, hoarse voice, throat clearing, sore throat, and excessive phlegm in the throat.24,25 Retrograde movement of gastric contents is rarely demonstrated, making laryngopharyngeal reflux disease infrequent in LPS presentations (Table 2).26 and alternative mechanisms are more likely.27 In randomized controlled trials, PPI therapy has shown no significant benefit over placebo in undifferentiated LPS as well as uninvestigated chronic cough,28,29 and abnormal reflux is more likely when typical reflux symptoms coexist with LPS.30 Both Lyon Consensus 2.0 and the San Diego Consensus for LPS/laryngopharyngeal reflux disease recommend objective upfront esophageal testing prior to anti-reflux therapy for isolated LPS,5,25 which is further supported by cost benefit analysis.31

In contrast to other LPS, globus is negatively associated with conclusive GERD (OR, 0.5; 95% CI, 0.30-0.86) and has been validated as a negative GERD predictor in a weighted scoring system.32 Belching may associate with reflux events, but can also be part of behavioral syndromes, making upfront pH-impedance monitoring a useful initial approach (Table 2).33 Other isolated foregut symptoms such as nausea, dyspepsia, abdominal pain, and dysphagia have very low likelihood of objective GERD, and reflux testing serves primarily to rule out a reflux based mechanism.5 The more atypical the symptom, the higher the need for documentation of GERD prior to management, as non-GERD etiologies including DGBI and behavioral syndromes are likely to contribute to symptom burden more often than reflux mechanisms.

Therefore, given competing mechanisms and substantial overlap with non-reflux etiologies, troublesome symptoms alone do not define phenotypes with mucosal injury or abnormal acid burden.34

DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

Diagnostic evaluation becomes necessary in patients with typical symptoms who fail to respond to empiric treatment trials, in patients with LPS or other atypical symptoms, and in the presence of alarm symptoms, where a high-quality upper endoscopy is typically the first test performed (Table 2).5,24,35

Although endoscopy is an important tool for the evaluation of esophageal changes from reflux, erosive esophagitis can be identified in only a third of treatment naïve patients with typical reflux symptoms, and the diagnostic yield decreases to < 10% in patients on PPIs despite persistent symptoms.36 Since erosive esophagitis is expected to heal in > 70% treated with PPIs and > 90% with potassium competitive acid blockers,37 endoscopic evaluation is recommended off therapy for 2-4 weeks if GERD diagnosis is uncertain. 38,39 Screening for BE is recommended in high-risk populations including patients with chronic GERD symptoms and risk factors including older age, Caucasian race, obesity, family history of esophageal cancer, and tobacco/alcohol use.40-43

Reflux monitoring technology has advanced beyond 24-hour pH measurements alone to include impedance monitoring that can quantify mucosal integrity, and prolonged wireless pH monitoring that records acid burden for up to 96 hours. In patients with unproven GERD and negative endoscopy, ambulatory reflux monitoring off antisecretory therapy differentiates NERD from non-GERD syndromes including RH and FH (Figure), using either prolonged wireless pH monitoring or 24-hour catheter based pH/pH-impedance monitoring.5 The wireless pH probe can be placed during initial diagnostic endoscopy, overcomes day-to-day variability in reflux burden and does not interfere with the patient’s daily activities that might confound 24-hour studies.44 However, wireless pH monitoring may not be universally available, comes with higher cost, and lacks impedance-based metrics that improve sensitivity and specificity of reflux monitoring.5 Catheter based pH/pH-impedance monitoring requires high-resolution manometry (HRM) for identification of the proximal margin of the LES for proper placement. This has higher sensitivity in detection of not just acidic but also weakly/non-acidic reflux events, adding to the value of reflux-symptom association. Baseline impedance, a longitudinal marker of esophageal mucosal integrity, can be measured as an adjunctive metric of reflux burden.5 Further, pH-impedance monitoring on optimized antisecretory therapy can help determine the mechanism of persisting symptoms despite therapy in patients with conclusive GERD.45

Although insufficient for a conclusive GERD diagnosis by itself, HRM can assess esophageal peristaltic performance, motility patterns associated with reflux and EGJ morphology.46,47 Intact primary esophageal peristalsis helps promptly clear refluxate from the esophagus after reflux events, and brings neutralizing saliva for recovery of mucosal acidification.16 Hypomotility disorders such as ineffective esophageal motility and absent contractility can prolong contact of the noxious refluxate with esophageal mucosa, potentially causing reflux symptoms and mucosal complications.48 HRM is an essential component of pre-operative evaluation prior to anti-reflux intervention,46 when achalasia and obstructive motor disorders mimicking GERD were found in approximately 3% of > 1000 patients being evaluated for anti-reflux surgery.49

Barium esophagography lacks diagnostic accuracy in identifying conclusive GERD and does not replace ambulatory reflux monitoring in GERD diagnosis. A recent retrospective evaluation of 182 patients who underwent both barium esophagram and ambulatory pH-impedance monitoring found low sensitivity (37.6%) and specificity (71.6%) of barium radiography in diagnosing conclusive GERD according to Lyon Consensus 2.0 criteria.50 The role of barium esophagography in GERD presentations is primarily in characterizing EGJ morphology, and as an adjunctive study in defining achalasia or other obstructive processes.46,47

MUCOSAL INJURY PHENOTYPES

The Montreal Consensus first outlined syndromes of esophageal injury including reflux esophagitis, peptic stricture, and BE to define end organ complications from GERD (Figure).2 The Lyon Consensus adopted these mucosal injury phenotypes as endoscopic criteria for conclusive GERD (Table 1).4

Visible erosions in the distal esophageal mucosa remain the most reliable endoscopic findings of acid injury. The Los Angeles (LA) classification assigns higher grades to more profound mucosal injury, which in turn associate with higher acid exposure time (AET) on reflux monitoring, worse symptom severity at baseline, and greater symptom improvement with antisecretory therapy.51,52 While endoscopic healing with antisecretory therapy occurs more often in lower LA grades of esophagitis,38,53 recurrence of esophagitis after withdrawal of therapy is more likely in higher LA grades.54 The updated Lyon Consensus 2.0 elevated LA grade B esophagitis to conclusive GERD evidence, in light of studies demonstrating similar reflux evidence on pH-impedance testing and response to PPI compared to LA grade C esophagitis, but markedly higher reflux burden than LA grade A esophagitis.52,55 However, interobserver variation in assigning LA grades can confound Lyon Consensus designation of conclusive GERD,56,57 and borderline LA grade B with limited erosions may need to be interpreted similar to LA grade A esophagitis.58

Peptic esophageal strictures remain an important mucosal injury phenotype, although prevalence has decreased with widespread PPI use.59,60 Peptic strictures are short (< 2 cm), manifest with or without concurrent esophagitis, and typically present with troublesome dysphagia.61 Biopsies can to rule out alternative mechanisms such as eosinophilic esophagitis or neoplasia. Acid suppression alters the natural history of peptic strictures, and PPIs have been shown to be superior to histamine-2 receptor blockers for resolution of dysphagia, prevention of recurrence, and need for repeated dilation.62

Salmon colored mucosa in the distal esophagus raises suspicion of BE, and biopsies showing intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells is required to confirm BE in most but not all international guidelines. 41,63 Patients with BE have higher reflux burden compared to healthy volunteers, as well as patients with NERD and erosive esophagitis. 64,65 Long-segment BE (> 3 cm) associates with higher AET, greater number of total reflux episodes, more proximal reflux, and longer acid clearance time.64,66,67 Professional societies vary on inclusion of ultrashort segment (< 1 cm) BE and irregular Z line in the BE diagnostic pool, since large retrospective studies show negligible risk of progression to high grade dysplasia or esophageal adenocarcinoma.68,69 However, a recent retrospective study of 167 US veterans with ultrashort segment BE on index endoscopy found persistence of BE in over half of patients, and definitive dysplasia in 8.4% on follow up70; thus exclusion from diagnostic criteria is controversial.71 Given these discrepancies, the Lyon Consensus 2.0 requires biopsy proven BE for conclusive GERD diagnosis, but makes no specifications regarding BE length, and leaves this designation to the treating physician or surgeon.5

ABNORMAL REFLUX BURDEN PHENOTYPES

When endoscopic evidence of mucosal injury is not evident, further investigation using ambulatory reflux monitoring can segregate NERD from non-GERD phenotypes (Table 1).6 Meta-analysis of patients with heartburn treated with PPI found similar rates of complete symptom response between erosive esophagitis (72.0%) and NERD with abnormal reflux burden (73.5%), while negative endoscopy without further evaluation had a lower heartburn response (50.5%).70

Rome V criteria and Lyon Consensus 2.0 both require abnormal AET on either wireless or catheter-based pH/pH impedance monitoring to define NERD.5,9 Cohort studies have consistently demonstrated total AET as the most reproducible metric from ambulatory reflux monitoring, with high sensitivity (77-100%) and specificity (85-100%) for differentiation of patients with esophagitis from healthy controls.72-75 and for predicting symptom improvement after medical or surgical management.76 The 95th percentile AET threshold lies between 5.3% and 6.3% in healthy controls.77-79 Based on these and other studies comparing acid burden across the GERD spectrum to healthy controls, the AET threshold for pathologic GERD was set at 6% for Western populations, while AET < 4% is considered physiologic, and values 4-6% are inconclusive and require additional supportive data.4,72 In Asian countries, a lower AET threshold of 4% is used to define conclusive GERD.80

The Lyon Consensus 2.0 suggests prolonged wireless pH testing as the preferred ambulatory modality for evaluation of typical symptoms and negative endoscopy, especially perceptive symptoms (heartburn, esophageal chest pain) (Table 2). In a retrospective study of PPI refractory patients, 85% of patients with discordant results on days 1 and 2 had a predominant physiologic, pathologic or borderline pattern evident with additional days of analysis.81 Utilizing an AET threshold of < 4% on all days of a 96-hour wireless study, a negative predictive value of 99% was demonstrated in discriminating healthy controls from patients with erosive esophagitis.52 Additionally, the cumulative number of days with total AET < 4% has added prognostic value in predicting PPI discontinuation.82

In patients with borderline total AET (4-6%), presence of adjunctive criteria can increase confidence in the likelihood of GERD (Table 1). While various reflux episode thresholds have been reported,83 ≥ 80 total reflux episodes off acid suppression on pH-impedance monitoring provides the strongest evidence for escalating reflux management.84 High numbers of total reflux episodes can differentiate PPI dependent GERD patients from healthy controls and a reduction of reflux episodes to below physiologic thresholds post intervention is associated with satisfaction with therapy.84,85 Post hoc analysis of 123 patients randomized to twice daily PPI versus magnetic sphincter augmentation in patients with troublesome regurgitation found that > 80 total reflux episodes predicted satisfaction (≥ 50% reduction of symptoms) with intervention in 31 PPI non-responders who crossed over to magnetic sphincter augmentation.84

Baseline impedance on pH impedance monitoring is an indicator of esophageal mucosal integrity that can segregate GERD from healthy controls and provide discriminative value in patients with borderline AET.86-88 Mean nocturnal baseline impedance (MNBI) is the average at the 3 cm or 5 cm impedance channel over three 10-minute overnight intervals (1 AM, 2 AM, and 3 AM). Low MNBI (< 1500 Ω) is found in many patients with pathologic or borderline AET but is rare in healthy controls.81 In a retrospective study of 371 patients who underwent pH impedance monitoring off therapy, symptomatic improvement was similar between patients with pathologic AET and those with borderline AET with low MNBI (73% vs 76%) but similar to physiologic AET when MNBI was normal (33.3% vs 38.6%).89

Reflux symptom association (RSA) using symptom association probability ( > 95%) and symptom index ( > 50%) assesses temporal relationships between reported symptoms and reflux events.76 Rome V utilizes RSA in the diagnosis of RH irrespective of response to anti-secretory therapy (Table 1). RSA may provide insights into response to anti-reflux therapy,90 however, interpretation requires caution and should be used in conjunction with other supporting evidence prior to escalation of anti-reflux therapy.91,92

Mucosal biopsies have a limited role in the diagnosis of GERD. Small studies have shown that microscopic esophagitis is less common in healthy controls and patients with FH (13% and 15% respectively) compared to patients with erosive esophagitis, NERD, and esophageal hypersensitivity (95%, 77%, and 65%, respectively). 93 Dilated intracellular spaces on electron microscopy are more frequent in patients with increased reflux burden (60%) compared to patients with FH (9%), and correlate negatively with MNBI (r = -0.230), however, this technology is not readily available in most clinical settings.94 Overall, diagnostic performance of histopathology to differentiate conclusive GERD from non-GERD phenotypes is suboptimal and not recommended.95

NORMAL REFLUX BURDEN PHENOTYPES

Rome Diagnoses

When AET is physiologic (< 4%) in symptomatic patients, E-DGBI, particularly functional chest pain, FH and RH, are diagnosed using ROME criteria (Figure).9 Patients with heartburn or esophageal chest pain for at least 6 months, without a structural, mucosal or motor disorder on esophageal testing and physiologic acid exposure with no RSA fulfil criteria for FH and functional chest pain. If RSA is present, RH is diagnosed.

Diagnosis of E-DGBI could be confounded by day-to-day variation in reflux burden if reflux monitoring is performed using 24-hour pH-impedance, and with lack of impedance-based markers if wireless pH monitoring is utilized. Some patients with physiologic AET could have adjunctive pH-impedance based metrics that are not within physiologic range. For instance, very low MNBI and/or high reflux episode counts in the context of physiologic AET could raise suspicion of GERD, and indeed, patients fulfilling these criteria demonstrate PPI response.96 In contrast, patients with AET < 4% and no adjunctive GERD feature (normal baseline impedance, normal reflux episode counts) have psychological profiles and PPI response similar to true functional disorders, which are treated with neuromodulators and adjunctive measures.

Behavioral Phenotypes

Patients with regurgitation and belching should be carefully evaluated to discriminate between reflux-related mechanisms, behavioral phenotypes, and overlap between the 2 mechanisms.

Distinction between supragastric belching (SGB), gastric belching, and aerophagia can be made with a combination of HRM with impedance and pH impedance monitoring.97 Gastric belches result from transient relaxation of the LES, in contrast to SGB where air sucked or pushed into the esophagus and rapidly expelled, never reaching the stomach.98 Aerophagia describes air swallowing and typically presents with bloating and increased flatulence rather than belching as the primary symptom.97 A single center retrospective cohort study determined > 13 SGB over 24 hours on pH impedance monitoring as abnormal,99 with patients reporting both bothersome belching and other upper GI symptoms, rather than insolated SGB. Reflux events associated with SGB are more likely non-acidic and are often seen in PPI non-responders.100,101 Diaphragmatic breathing, cognitive behavioral therapy, and neuromodulators reduce symptoms and improve quality of life, and antireflux surgery should not be offered for isolated SGB.102

Rumination syndrome refers to the effortless regurgitation of recently ingested food from the stomach to the oral cavity followed by expectoration or re-mastication and re-swallowing, in the absence of nausea or retching.103 Rumination episodes, indistinguishable from reflux events, occur in the immediate post prandial period but stop when the regurgitate becomes acidic. While rare in the general population, rumination can be identified in PPI-non responders using post prandial HRM with impedance monitoring, or pH impedance monitoring.101,104 Management consists of patient education, behavioral therapy, and diaphragmatic breathing rather than anti-reflux therapies.105,106

Behavioral disorders can also overlap with GERD, where reflux metrics may be pathologic despite symptom presentations and test findings suggestive of SGB or rumination syndrome.107

ROLE OF HYPERVIGILANCE

Perception of esophageal stimuli occurs from the complex interplay between visceral afferent nerves in the luminal gut and peripheral nervous system as well as central cognitive and psychological processes.100 Disturbances in mood or emotional arousal can impair and amplify interpretation of esophageal stimuli.108,109 Hyperawareness of esophageal sensation leads to the perception of stimuli as frightening, and patients can develop a disproportionate behavioral response from catastrophic thinking about potential consequences. The periodic improvement of symptoms may be inappropriately attributed to close selective attention and avoidance of perceived triggers, further reinforcing the behavior pattern. This cognitive-affective pattern is collectively referred to as “esophageal hypervigilance” and can add to PPI refractory symptom burden and significantly impaired quality of life.13

While understanding of esophageal hypervigilance has evolved over the last decade, the concept of hypervigilance is well established in both mental health and pain disorders.110 The esophageal hypervigilance and anxiety scale (EHAS) is a 15-item patient reported outcome adapted to the esophagus from validated hypervigilance questionnaires.111 In the initial validation cohort of 982 patients, EHAS demonstrated excellent internal consistency, reliability, and both total and sub-scores for symptom-specific anxiety and hypervigilance positively correlated with symptom severity and negatively correlated with quality of life.111 Subsequent studies evaluating patients with reflux symptoms found that EHAS predicts symptom severity regardless of acid burden on ambulatory testing with either pH impedance or prolonged wireless pH monitoring across different reflux phenotypes.8,112 Higher EHAS scores are associated with more severe LPS despite no difference in esophageal reflux metrics.113 In a study of 102 patients who underwent anti-reflux surgery for GERD, pre-operative EHAS was an independent predictor of both symptom severity before surgery and symptom improvement after surgery.114

Esophageal hypervigilance offers a novel mechanism to explain discordance between reflux symptoms and physiologic testing and provides additional targets for therapy including cognitive behavioral therapy and gut-directed hypnotherapy.115,116

CONCLUSIONS

While GERD evaluation starts with characterization of troublesome symptoms suggested by the Montreal consensus, symptoms alone do not define actionable GERD, although typical symptoms without alarm features may warrant empiric acid suppressive therapy. Mucosal injury phenotypes are identified in a minority of symptomatic patients on upper endoscopy, especially when studied off therapy. When endoscopy is normal or demonstrates LA grade A esophagitis, GERD remains unproven, and ambulatory reflux monitoring can identify or rule out NERD, and potentially diagnose RH, FH and behavioral disorders. Diagnosis of E-DGBI requires exclusion of mucosal injury and abnormal reflux burden phenotypes, identified using Lyon Consensus criteria. Hypervigilance and symptom-specific anxiety, as well as E-DGBI diagnosed using Rome criteria can coexist across all the symptomatic reflux phenotypes. Therefore, Montreal, Lyon and Rome are intricately linked together in patient evaluation, and concepts from each of these contribute to accurate phenotyping and optimal management.10
