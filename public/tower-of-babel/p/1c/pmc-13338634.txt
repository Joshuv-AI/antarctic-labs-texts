Utility of Hair Toxicology in Detecting Child Abuse or Neglect in the Burn Unit: A Quality Improvement Project
Authors: Shady Al Hayek, Jack Bullis, Tessa A Davis-Walz, Colette Galet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren represent 20% of all burn admissions annually; 20%-30% are related to abuse or neglect. Our institution’s Child Protective Services recently decided to stop following and interpreting hair toxicology results for pediatric patients with burn injuries. Drug use/exposure is an important risk factor for child abuse/neglect. The goal of this study was to evaluate the value of hair toxicology testing to identify child abuse or neglect. Medical records of pediatric patients ≤14 years admitted between January 1, 2019 and February 29, 2024 were reviewed. Demographics, burn injury information, suspicion of child abuse/neglect on admission, routine urine drug screening tests, hair toxicology results, and reporting to the State Health and Human Services (HHS) were collected. Descriptive statistics were obtained. Univariate analyses were performed to assess the utility of hair toxicology with P < .05 considered significant. Two hundred ninety-eight patients were included; child abuse was suspected in 31.5%; hair toxicology was performed for 75.2% of patients and was positive for 35.7%. Hair toxicology was more likely to be performed when child abuse was suspected on admission (81.9% vs 72.1%, P = .044), and to be positive (40.4% vs 20.6%, P < .001). Suspicion on admission was associated with higher HHS involvement (95.7% vs 24%, P < .001). Health and Human Services was more likely to be involved when hair toxicology was performed (54% vs 24.3%, P < .004) and positive (96.3% vs 28.4%, P < .001). Hair toxicology screening for pediatric patients with burn injuries is still a valuable tool to help clinicians report suspected abuse/neglect not otherwise detected on admission using other tools.

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric burn injuries, particularly those related to abuse or neglect, pose significant clinical and social challenges. Scald injuries, often resulting from hot liquids, are the most common form of burns seen in young children, particularly those aged 0-4 years.1 Beyond the immediate physical harm, burn injuries associated with child abuse tend to involve larger total body surface areas and cause more severe medical consequences compared to accidental burns.2

Research indicates that, when abuse is suspected, burnt children are generally younger, with an average age of about 2.4 years compared to 3.9 years for accidental burn victims. These children are also found to have greater areas of their bodies affected by the burns. This demographic group is notably more susceptible to scald injuries.2 Statistically, when abuse is suspected, children with burn injuries demonstrate a higher risk of mortality. For instance, the odds of death for these victims are significantly higher, necessitating more extended intensive care and hospitalization.2

Burn unit medical professionals need to be equipped with adequate knowledge and techniques to identify and manage cases of child abuse presenting as burn injuries. The assessment in such cases should begin with stabilization procedures followed by a comprehensive examination and evaluation of the injury’s extent.1 Because the outcome of these cases depends on making an accurate diagnosis as early as possible, it becomes very important to develop tests and techniques that will help in the process.

In recent years, hair toxicology screening has emerged as a valuable tool in detecting child abuse, especially in cases involving exposure to drugs.3,4 Several factors make this test highly effective in detecting child abuse as compared to other traditional biological samples such as blood or urine.3,5–7 For example, hair analysis can document exposure to a wide range of substances over extended periods, from weeks to even years, depending on the length of the hair sample.7,8

In addition, hair sample collection is noninvasive, easy to perform, and has a low risk of sample adulteration or substitution; these factors make hair an ideal matrix for forensic investigations. Moreover, this method can retrospectively monitor drug use, providing a chronological account of substance exposure. This is particularly significant in child abuse cases, where the ability to ascertain long-term exposure to harmful substances is crucial.3,9,10

Recent advances in analytical techniques, particularly mass spectrometry, have enhanced the sensitivity and reliability of hair testing, thereby increasing the accuracy and reliability of these evaluations.11,12 These methods allow for successful detection of various substances with minimal sample amounts, making it feasible to incorporate hair analysis into routine forensic toxicology practices.11,12

It is worth noting that interpreting results from hair analysis should be exercised with caution as external contamination can lead to false positives. Therefore, ongoing efforts focus on developing more sensitive and selective analysis methods to improve accuracy and mitigate factors such as melanin content or age, which can influence drug incorporation into hair.9

From May 28, 2024, our institution’s Child Protection Program decided to no longer perform asymptomatic urine and hair testing after feedback from national experts in child abuse pediatrics and drug endangered children, stating that hair and urine toxicology is of limited clinical utility, not in line with standard national practice in the majority of child protection groups, and the fact that our institution did not have an effective medical referral pathway for pediatric patients with positive results beyond engaging Health and Human Services (HHS) to offer family services. We conducted this retrospective review over the past 5 years on our burn unit to assess the utility of hair toxicology screening in detecting child abuse in pediatric burn cases.

METHODS

Ethical statement

This quality improvement project was deemed exempt from Institutional Review by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board.

Patient population

All burn pediatric cases age ≤ 14 years admitted to our burn center between January 1, 2019 and February 29, 2024 were included. Mechanisms of burn included in the study were thermal (flame, contact, and scald), chemical, electrical, and others (inhalation injury without cutaneous burn, frostbite, and friction burns).

Child abuse protocol for burn patients 0-14 years old

Our institution has a specific child abuse protocol for burns. Our providers are trained to identify injury patterns such as tap water injuries or delay in seeking care that indicate potential maltreatment. The team also gathers the story regarding the burn from the person present on admission on 3 separate occasions by the burn surgeon, social worker, and a nurse. They then meet to discuss suspicion and whether the story lines up each time it is told. They also assess whether the child is developmentally able to do what was said to have happened. If there is disagreement in any part of the story, then it is reported, and our institution’s Child Abuse Service is involved. This specialized team at our Children’s Hospital works with the Burn Treatment Center to evaluate child maltreatment cases. Detailed documentation is required regarding injuries and developmental milestones to differentiate accidents from potential abuse. Head to toe clinical examination is performed, photos are taken, and laboratory tests are ordered including urine and hair toxicology until May 28, 2024. Skeletal surveys are obtained in all cases of suspected abuse/neglect in children under 2 years of age or those with disabilities, and only in highly suspicious cases involving children aged 2-5 years. For children older than 5 years, X-rays are obtained only for areas of specific concern.

Any suspected physical abuse, including unusual or unexplained burns (eg, cigarette burns, immersion burns), must be reported immediately to our state Child Abuse Hotline or local law enforcement. Our State HHS service gets involved from that point on. However, HHS does not follow up with the burn center on their findings postdischarge unless the child requires follow up.

Data collection

Demographics, routine urine drug screening tests, and burn injury information were obtained from our burn registry. Medical records were reviewed to collect suspicion of child abuse/neglect on admission, hair toxicology results, skeletal surveys, and reporting to the State HHS.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for all continuous variables. All non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range. Descriptive statistics were obtained. Univariate analysis was performed to assess the utility of hair toxicology. Chi-square test and Fisher exact were used for categorical variables, Mann–Whitney test was used for continuous variables. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 28.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). P values < .05 were considered significant. Graphs were prepared using GraphPrism (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the study population was mostly male. The median age was 2 years, and 84.6% of the patients were transferred from a referring hospital. Most suffered thermal burns. The median TBSA was 5% and only 4.7% of our population suffered inhalation injuries. The median length of hospital stay was 2 days. Abuse/neglect was suspected on admission for 31.5% of our population. Hair toxicology was performed for 75.2% of our population and positive in 35.7% of our patients. Hair toxicology was not performed in 74 patients (24.8%). There was no suspicion of child abuse for 58 of them. Child abuse was suspected in 16 patients who did not have hair toxicology done; 13 were discharged home within 3 days post-admission; 1 did not have enough hair for toxicology. Health and Human Services was contacted or already involved for all 16 cases to provide family support with resources to ensure patients received proper care postdischarge.

Suspicion of abuse/neglect on admission and hair toxicology

Abuse/neglect was suspected in 93 cases (31.2%). Children with suspected abuse and neglect were significantly younger (1.5 [1-5] vs 2 [1-8], P = .001), with a higher proportion of children being 1 year old or less in the abuse/neglect suspicion group (49.9% vs 32.1%; P = .007). The etiology of the burn was as follows: scald (61.7%: flame 23.7%; contact 9.7%; and other 5.4%). Children with suspected abuse and neglect were more likely to have suffered scald burns (61.7% vs 44.1%, P = .006).There were no significant differences in %TBSA and second-degree %TBSA, but the third-degree TBSA % was significantly higher in the abuse/neglect suspicion group (3.1 [1-8.3] vs 1 [0.5-3.8], P = .004), suggesting a more severe burn. Patients in the abuse/neglect suspicion group stayed longer in hospital (3 [1-9] vs 1 [1-5], P < .001). Skeletal surveys were obtained in 45.2% of suspected child abuse or neglect cases. With only 2 (4.8%) positive skeletal survey findings. Looking at the final diagnosis of child abuse, 19 cases were confirmed child abuse or neglect, both cases with positive skeletal survey findings had a final diagnosis of child abuse cases representing 10.7% of the cases with a final diagnosis of child abuse. Both were discharged home, one with their mother, the other with another family member under DHS supervision. Both had positive urine toxicology for opioids, hair toxicology was not performed for one due to a head burn and lack of hair, the other had a negative hair toxicology.

Hair toxicology testing and HHS involvement

Hair toxicology was performed on 75.2% of our population and positive in 34.8%. Urine drug testing was performed in 79.9% of our population and positive in 31.2%. Drugs detected were amphetamine/methamphetamine, cannabinoids, opioids, and benzodiazepine. Although urine and hair toxicology cannot be compared because they have different windows of detection and hair can pick up environmental (passive) exposure that would not be detected in urine, the proportions of cases with amphetamine/methamphetamine, cannabinoids, and/or opioid detected in the hair were similar to those detected in the urine. Benzodiazepines were only detected in urine toxicology. The proportion of cases with more than one drug detected in the hair was similar to that detected in the urine (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective review sheds light on the potential utility of hair toxicology screening in pediatric burn cases in which abuse or neglect is suspected. Our findings indicate that hair toxicology screening was performed in most pediatric patients and yielded positive results in just above one-third of cases. There was a strong association between positive results and involvement of HHS. Importantly, hair toxicology identified substance exposure even in patients not initially suspected of abuse (28% of our pediatric burn admissions), suggesting its value as an adjunct tool for uncovering hidden risk factors.

The demographic trends observed in our population of pediatric patients with burn injuries such as younger age and higher third-degree TBSA in suspected abuse cases is well described in the literature indicating that inflicted burns often occur in very young children and involve more severe injuries.1,2 These findings reinforce the need for heightened vigilance and comprehensive evaluation in this vulnerable population.

Hair analysis offers several advantages over traditional matrices like blood or urine, including its ability to detect long-term exposure and its noninvasive collection process.13 It can be used not only for drug exposure detection but also for micro organic contaminants such as organophosphates, organochlorines, and other pollutants.14 However, interpretation challenges remain.15 Such challenges as external contamination, variability in drug incorporation due to hair color or cosmetic treatments, and the absence of standardized thresholds for pediatric populations can complicate result interpretation.9,15–17 These limitations stress the importance of integrating toxicology findings with clinical judgment and multidisciplinary assessments rather than relying on the results of the hair toxicology in isolation.

The discontinuation of hair toxicology by the child abuse team at our institution reflects concerns about overinterpretation and unclear impact on case outcomes.18,19 Our data suggest that positive hair toxicology results frequently trigger HHS involvement; however, more in-depth research is needed to determine whether this translates into improved child safety or legal outcomes. In addition, prospective studies examining the downstream effects of toxicology-driven interventions in collaboration with the HHS would provide valuable insights.

Finally, while hair toxicology appears promising as a screening tool, its role should be considered complementary rather than definitive. Developing standardized protocols for sample collection, analysis, and interpretation for adult and pediatric patients combined with training for clinicians and social workers on interpreting these results will be critical to maximizing its utility while minimizing potential harms.20

This study has several limitations. First, this is a single-center retrospective review which may limit generalizability to other institutions or regions. Second, hair toxicology was not performed uniformly across all patients (only 75.2% of our pediatric admission received the hair toxicology screening test); hence subjecting the study to potential selection bias. Third, the interpretation of hair toxicology results can be influenced by external contamination, hair treatments and individual variability in drug incorporation which were not reported in our test results and thus not controlled for in this analysis. Fourth, the study did not assess the downstream impact of positive toxicology results on legal outcomes or child safety, leaving uncertainty about the ultimate effectiveness of this screening tool. Finally, the absence of standardized protocols for hair sample collection and analysis may affect reproducibility and thus limit the applicability to other institutions or populations.

CONCLUSION

Hair toxicology screening appears to be a valuable adjunct in identifying potential child abuse among pediatric patients with burn injuries, particularly when abuse or neglect is suspected. Positive results were strongly associated with involvement of child protective services, suggesting their role in triggering further investigation. However, given the limitations and interpretive challenges, hair toxicology should complement, not replace, clinical judgment and multidisciplinary evaluation. Future prospective studies are needed to establish standardized protocols, assess long-term outcomes, and clarify the impact of toxicology-driven interventions on child welfare.
