The impact of donor and recipient age on post-transplantation clonality in murine hematopoiesis
Authors: Lars Thielecke, Kalpana Nattamai, Aishlin Hassan, Ingmar Glauche, Hartmut Geiger, Kerstin Cornils
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe sustained production of blood and immune cells is driven by a pool of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and their offspring. Due to the intrinsic heterogeneity of HSCs, the composition of emergent clones changes over time, leading to a reduced clonality in aging mice and humans. Theoretical analyses suggest that clonal conversion rates and clonal complexity depend not only on HSC heterogeneity, but also on additional stress conditions. These insights are particularly relevant in the context of stem cell transplantations, which still remain the only curative option for many hematologic diseases, increasingly considered viable for elderly individuals. However, age-related clonal changes post-transplantation are not well understood.MethodsTo address this, we conducted a barcode-based assessment of clonality to investigate post-transplantation changes in both homo- and hetero-chronic settings, combined with low- and high-intensity pre-conditioned recipients.ResultsA robust and polyclonal engraftment was observed across all groups, but with distinct differences in barcode diversity. In particular, transplanted aged HSCs showed no changes in clonality, regardless of recipient age or pre-conditioning. Young HSCs transplanted into severely pre-conditioned old hosts as well as under reduced pre-conditioning, allowed for full lymphoid reconstitution, but showed substantial differences in clonality. Also, myeloid lineage bias, a hallmark of aged HSCs, was confirmed at a clonal level across all experimental groups. Overall, we found that aged HSCs generally maintain clonal diversity similar to young HSCs, but notable differences emerge under hetero-chronic conditions and varying pre-conditioning regimens.ConclusionThese findings challenge current paradigms and underscore the complex interactions between aging and transplantation conditions.

Stem cell transplantations remain the only curative option for many hematologic diseases, with a growing interest in their application for elderly patients. Changes in the function of aged HSCs or aged niches might limit eligibility of elderly patients for transplants. This study provides novel insight into clonal dynamics of murine aged HSCs post-transplantation in young or aged niches. Aged HSCs are able to perform similar to young HSCs with respect to clonality post-transplantation, although different age-combinations and pre-conditioning influences clonal diversity. Our findings in this model system imply that neither donor nor recipient age is the only limiting factor for HSC transplantations.

Introduction

The formation of blood and immune cells is driven by a pool of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) that are able to self-renew and to differentiate. Due to an intrinsic heterogeneity of individual HSPCs, the composition of the hematopoietic cell pool will undergo changes over time.1–4 While the progeny of some HSPCs may have a stronger tendency for self-renewal, their clonal descendances become more prevalent while other clones with a more pronounced tendency for differentiation diminish in size and number. During aging, this process results in a 30%-60% reduction of clonality in mice from 2 to 26 months of age.5 A rapid loss of the overall clonal diversity is often linked to the acquisition of mutations and a resulting advantage of the effected clones. There is increasing evidence that also in humans the number of self-maintaining clones in hematopoiesis is reduced upon aging, even without the acquisition of prominent clonal driver mutations.6 Such a behavior, more technically understood as a neutral drift, is a direct result of the underlying stochasticity of the clonal competition process, and is well documented in other stem cell systems, such as intestine7,8 or epidermis.9

Theoretical analyses also show that the speed of clonal conversion and the resulting level of clonality depends not only on the intrinsic heterogeneity of the stem cell system but also on the turnover rate of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs).10,11 Challenges of the hematopoietic system, such as transplantation, should consequently accelerate such clonal conversion processes. At the same time, stem cell transplantations continue to be the only curative option for many hematologic diseases. Interestingly, there’s a notable shift toward considering this approach viable even for elderly individuals.12 Unfortunately, the extent of age-related changes in clonality and clonal dynamics in murine hematopoiesis post-transplantation (post-tx) in combination with HSC niches of different ages still remains poorly understood.5,13–18 Therefore, it is imperative to gather additional experimental information on clonal dynamics of aged HSCs upon transplantation in various experimental settings, in order to better understand the system’s complexity and to further improve treatment availability and successes.19 Additionally, also the potential influence of different conditioning regimen needs further investigation.

In order to address such questions, we performed an annotated barcode-based assessment of clonality to investigate in-vivo changes post-transplant for both homo- and hetero-chronic transplantation settings, in combination with low- and high-intensity pre-conditioned recipients. This allowed us to determine the extent to which changes in clonality may occur upon transplantation and whether they are influenced by aged HSCs, aged niches or distinct conditioning regimens. The use of our previously established and fully annotated barcode library (ABC-library,20,21 Figure S1) enabled us to identify and count small and rare clones, which are usually hard to detect against a noisy background by any other method.19 Our data suggest that while donor age in general seems to have a greater impact on HSPC clonality and hematopoiesis compared to recipient age, the most pronounced effects emerge from the interplay between HSC age, niche age, and pre-conditioning intensity.

Methods

Barcode library

We developed an annotated barcode library (ABC-library) with 343 different barcodes.21 In brief, we exchanged the SFFV-promotor of the LeGO-G2-BC3221 by an EFS-Promotor and generated a barcoded plasmid library as described (Figure S1A).21,22 To obtain a reasonable number of unique barcodes with sufficiently large hamming distances (HD),20,21 we selected 672 individual bacterial clones after transformation and performed Sanger-sequencing. Only barcodes with correct backbones and error-free index adapter sequences were chosen to be included into the curated library.21 A set of 343 different barcodes with a minimal HD = 12 to any other chosen barcode were selected (Figure S1B). To generate a balanced library, we first combined 68 or 69 uniquely barcoded plasmids to five initial stocks. After transformation and isolation of plasmids from E. coli, we combined the five plasmid preparations to obtain the final annotated barcode library. During the procedure, we performed quality controls by sequencing 1010 plasmids of the five stocks, the subsequently generated plasmid preparations and the entire ABC-library to ensure maintenance of this complexity within the library (Figure S1C). Production of viral supernatant was performed as described earlier.23

Flow cytometry/antibodies/cell type identification

BM cell immunostaining was performed according to standard procedures (LIT) and analyzed on a LSRII flow cytometer or BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences). Antibodies: anti-Ly5.2 (clone 104), anti-Ly5.1 (clone A20), anti-CD3e (clone 145-2C11), anti-B220 (clone RA3-6B2), anti-Mac-1 (clone M1/70) and anti-Gr-1 (clone RC57BL/6-8C5), anti-CD11b (clone M1/70), anti-B220 (clone RA3-6B2), anti-CD5 (clone 53-7.3), anti-Gr-1 (clone RB6-8C5), anti-Ter119 and anti-CD8a (clone 53-6.7). After lineage depletion by magnetic separation (Dynabeads, Invitrogen), cells were stained with anti-Sca-1 (cloneD7), anti-c-Kit (clone 2B8), anti-CD34 (clone RAM34), anti-CD127 (clone A7R34), anti CD16/CD32 (clone 2.4G2) (BD Biosciences), anti-Flk-2 (clone A2F10) or anti-Ter119 and anti-CD71+ (clone R17217) and streptavidin. All antibodies were obtained from eBioscienes, except if stated differently. Cells were defined by the following marker combinations: LT-HSCs: LIN−, c-KIT+, Sca-1+, CD34−, flk2−; ST−HSCs: LIN−, c−KIT+, Sca−1+, CD34+, flk2−; MPPs: LIN−, c−KIT+, Sca−1+, CD34+, flk2+; CLPs: LIN−, CD127+; CMPs: LIN−, CD127−, c−Kit+, CD16/32−, CD34+; MEPs: LIN−, CD127−, c−Kit+, CD16/32−, CD34−; GMPs: LIN−, CD127−, c−Kit+, CD16/32+, CD34+; Ery Prog: CD71+, Ter119+; Myeloid cells; Mac1+ and/or Gr1+; B−cells: B220+ and T-cells: CD3+.

Transplantations

LT-HSCs (Lin−, c-Kit+, Sca-1+, CD34−, Flt3−) were isolated from young (10-12 weeks young) or aged (17-18 months old) C57BL/6 animals and transduced (for 24 hours) with a lentiviral supernatant that contained the ABC-barcode library (Figure S1) linked to GFP according to published protocols.23 On average 3000 HSCs were then either homo-chronically or hetero-chronically transplanted into irradiated (11 Gy, high intensity regimen) young and aged C57BL/6 CD45.1 (BoyJ) recipients along with 1.5 × 105 total BM supporter cells, and also into young C57BL/6 RAG1−/− recipients (irradiated with 2.5 Gy, low intensity regimen). The frequency of transduced HSCs was estimated by measuring the percentage of GFP-positive cells among an aliquot of ex vivo expanded transduced HSCs from each batch designated for transplantation. We obtained on average a transduction efficiency of 22% in young and 27% in aged HSCs, allowing for a successful subsequent analysis of barcode contribution in transplanted animals.21 We analyzed a total number of 43 mice. Within the wildtype cohort, we transplanted HSCs from young donors into young recipients (YY, n = 6) and into old recipients (YO, n = 8), from old donors into young recipients (OY, n = 8) and also into old recipients (OO, n = 8). Within the RAG1−/− cohort, we transplanted HSCs from young (YRag, n = 5) and old (ORag, n = 8) donors into young RAG1−/− recipients (Figure 1). At 20 weeks post transplantation, animals were subsequently euthanized to harvest bone marrow (BM). Cells were isolated from BM via cell sorting according to already published standard protocols (Figure S2A-C). DNA was harvested from these cells and processed for targeted sequencing of the barcodes as previously described.21 All experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. No formal ethics approval was required for this study.

At 20 weeks post transplantation, animals were euthanized to harvest bone marrow (BM). Cell types isolated by cell sorting were B-cells, T-cells, myeloid cells, erythroid progenitors (EPs), myeloid-erythroid progenitors (MEPs), common lymphocyte progenitors (CLPs), common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (GMPs), multipotent progenitors (MPPs), short-term HSCs (ST-HSCs), and long-term HSCs (LT-HSCs) according to published standard protocols. DNA was harvested from these cells and processed for targeted sequencing of the barcodes as previously described.2

Bioinformatic data extraction, error correction, and analysis strategy

Primary sequencing data was analyzed to check data validity and to extract barcodes and their distribution among the cell types analyzed. The NGS-derived FASTQ files underwent a quality filtration process based on the associated Phred quality score. For each sequence, an average quality score was computed across all nucleotide positions, and sequences with an average score below 30 were excluded (Figure S3). Subsequently, the remaining reads were scanned for the fixed triplets of the barcode backbone, allowing for a single mismatch. The actual barcode sequences were then extracted from these reads. The acquired barcodes were counted, sorted and saved in a raw data table featuring two columns: read counts and nucleotide sequences. The error correction algorithm is based on pairwise HDs and utilizes the aforementioned raw data tables along with a list of known BC sequences from the ABC-library. After conducting a sensitivity analysis to determine the impact of the chosen HD threshold21 and considering the minimal HD differences within our library, we used the already established threshold of HD = 8 to define BC similarity, corresponding to one-fourth of the total barcode length. Any BC sequence falling within this distance to a known BC was “assimilated” and its associated reads were added to the orresponding original BC sequence. Our decision to use HD = 8 as a suitable threshold was verified by the fact that we only found sporadic examples of BCs (with a maximal read count of one) that were shared between two known BC sequences from our library. After error correction, we compiled a list of all unique BCs which have at least three reads in at least one cell type, for each sacrificed mouse. Subsequently, those BC lists served as mouse-specific BC white lists. Therefore, for all downstream analyses we considered all those BCs as valid even if they accumulated less than three reads in certain cell types.

Our choice of a relatively small but, annotated BC library with the inherent feature to also identify particularly small clones, comes at the risk of multiple cells receiving the same barcode. However, we reasoned that among the ∼3000 transplanted HSCs only a smaller fraction is actually transduced and will finally engraft. Simulating the respective sampling process indicates that, on average, 88% of barcodes are integrated into only one engrafted cell, whereas the remaining ∼12% of clones are generally not uniquely labeled.

Downsampling approach

To mitigate potential biases caused by the inherent randomness in the distribution of NGS read counts with respect to BC resolutions, we adopted a downsampling approach targeting the top 20% (146 files) of NGS files with the highest read counts (Figure S4). Consequently, we randomly selected reads from the raw and unfiltered FASTQ-files, ensuring that the total number of sampled reads for each file was also randomly selected within a range spanning from a minimum of 1.000 reads up to 379.482 reads, which marks exactly the middle between the size of the smallest FASTQ-file within the top 20% and the size of the largest file outside of this subset.

Regression analyses

To assess the influence of recipient and donor age on the number of detected genetic barcodes (BCs) following HSC transplantation, we performed a linear regression analysis using the R-function lm(). The model included recipient and donor age (young vs. old) and mouse type (C57BL/6 CD45.1 vs C57BL/6 RAG1−/−) as categorical predictors. Model residuals were inspected to confirm normality and homoscedasticity. To investigate the influence of recipient and donor age on the frequency of T-cell and M/G-biased clones, we applied a beta regression using the betareg() function of the R-package “betareg.” This approach is suitable for modeling proportional data constrained between 0 and 1, ensuring appropriate handling of non-normality and hetero-scedasticity. To ensure appropriate handling of boundary values and improve model stability, we applied the Smithson & Verkuilen transformation.24 Two separate Beta regression models were fitted for the proportion of T-cell- and M/G-biased clones, with recipient and donor age (young vs. old) and mouse type (C57BL/6 CD45.1 vs C57BL/6 RAG1−/−) as categorical predictors. A logit link function was used for the mean model, and a separate precision (phi) model was included to estimate dispersion effects. The exceedance parameter (nu) was estimated for both models, accounting for potential boundary effects in the proportion data. Both models were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation, and residual diagnostics were performed to assess the quality of the model fits (Figure S6).

Results

Robust and polyclonal engraftment of HSC clones in all age groups

Lethal irradiation of recipients is a strong pre-conditioning treatment regimen that eradicates most of the naïve hematopoiesis and profoundly remodels the BM microenvironment. The high levels of cytokine secretion in response to irradiation-induced damage, coupled with the urgent demand for hematopoietic recovery, drive a state of stress hematopoiesis. In this context, we focused our investigation on identifying changes in clonality and clonal dynamics in murine hematopoiesis following HSC transplantation. In order to determine in more detail, how the combination of donor and host age, along with the level of pre-conditioning, influences clonality, we conducted a series of transplantation experiments. We not only transplanted young barcoded LT-HSCs (10-12 weeks) into young recipient animals (YY) and old barcoded LT-HSCs (17-18 months) into old recipients (OO), referred to as homo-chronic transplants (Figure 1A), but also conducted hetero-chronic transplants (YO & OY, Figure 1B) and additionally preformed transplants of young and old LT-HSCs into sub-lethally irradiated young Rag1−/− recipients (YRag & ORag, Figure 1C).

To evaluate the engraftment capabilities of the transplanted HSCs across the experimental groups, we analyzed the number of unique BCs identified in all hematopoietic populations (from stem and progenitor populations down to terminally differentiated monocyte/granulocyte, B- and T-cells, Figure 1) at 20 weeks post-transplant. Overall, all age combinations exhibited stable polyclonal engraftment, though with varying complexities. On average, mice transplanted with older HSCs exhibited a higher number of detected BCs compared to those receiving younger HSCs (Figure 2A, B). A linear regression analysis (Figure S6A) confirmed the significant influence of donor age (β = 39.8, P < .001), indicating that HSCs from older donors led to increased clonal diversity. In contrast, recipient age had a more subtle effect, with younger recipients displaying a slightly more versatile BC assortment than older ones, although the effect was not significant (β=−14.8, P = .12).

In more detail, the total number of unique BCs across all cell types analyzed showed no substantial differences between YY and OO set-ups, suggesting a similar engraftment potential of young and old HSCs in homo-chronic transplants (Figure 2C). In contrast, in hetero-chronic and RAG1−/− transplant settings, transplanting young HSCs resulted in a lower number of engrafted clones compared to their older counterparts, which exhibited clonal diversity levels similar to those observed in the homo-chronic settings. However, when comparing the relative clonal contributions after transplantation, we also found comparable values across all different donor and recipient age combinations and conditioning regimes (Figure S5), further supporting the notion of a robust and polyclonal HSC engraftment regardless of age (compare Figure 2). The highest overall number of distinct BCs and the highest relative abundance can be observed within the mature compartments, especially in monocytes/granulocytes (M/Gs). In general, we observed no confounding correlations between the level of engraftment and barcode abundance (Figure S7).

As a surrogate measure of clonal dominance and hematopoietic skewing, we analyzed the relative clone size of the most prevalent BC per animal and cell type. Despite a large inter-individual variability, the median clone size of the most prevalent BCs remained relatively consistent across most immature cell types (LT-HSCs, ST-HSCs) and all transplantation settings (Figure 2D), with the exception of the YY group. We only observed a trend toward more dominant clones within CMPs, EPs, B- and T-cells in OO animals. Interestingly, this trend was almost absent in YO and OY animals, while it became even more apparent in almost all cell types analyzed in YRag animals. Noticeably, the maximum clone size within the M/G compartment (excluding YRag) was distinctly lower (∼15%) than in all other cell types analyzed (30%-50%), including LT- and ST-HSCs. This difference likely correlates with the substantially higher number of unique BCs within the same compartment. Moreover, the contrast between the M/G compartment and the immature cell types could suggest that, unlike lymphoid and other cells, clonality in the M/G compartment may be less directly linked to that of the stem and progenitor compartments.

Hematopoiesis is driven by HSC-derived clones

After lethal irradiation, transplanted HSCs should be the only source of all mature cell types after hematopoietic recovery post-transplant, implying that at least a large number of BCs detected in more differentiated cell types should also be present within the HSC populations. To this end, we analyzed in which cell types which BCs can be detected. For each BC, we identified the “most primitive” subpopulation within the hematopoietic hierarchy in which it appeared, and provide the accumulated BC numbers for each cell type in Figure 3. The results indicate that indeed most BCs “first” appear in either the LT- or ST-HSC population with only a small fraction of BCs that were only detected downstream in more mature cell types.

For visual convenience, the bar plot is superimposed with the hierarchy structure of hematopoietic differentiation where the node sizes reflect the median number of “first-appearing” BCs averaged over all six transplantation settings.

To achieve a higher resolution of the stem-cell-derived clonality, we quantified the extent to which these BCs, which are present within the HSC populations, overlap with those found in more mature cell types, both regarding the number of BCs and the amount of reads occupied by these BCs. To do so, we combined LT- and ST-HSC clones and compared them to the clones detected in all other, more mature cell populations (see examples in Figure 4A). Figure 4B-G shows these analyses for all animals and all transplantation schemes. In all groups, a similar qualitative pattern of BC occurrence and read count occupation can be observed. On average, nearly all BCs found in the more mature cell types (Figure 4B-D, left side of the barplots) could potentially have originated from a subset of BCs present in LT- and ST-HSCs (Figure 4B-D, right side of the barplots), and these BCs account for the vast majority of all reads within the respective samples (Figure 4E-G, left side of the barplots). In more detail, roughly 40-70% of HSC-BCs are shared across all other measured cell types, for all transplantations settings. The proportion of shared HSC-BCs within each of the other cell types gradually declines with increasing maturation (following the hematopoietic hierarchy from naïve to mature cells), with M/G and T-cells exhibiting the lowest overlap. Nevertheless, all mature cell types are almost entirely composed of reads occupied by shared HSC-BCs (Figure 4E-G, left side). Within the limitations of such snapshot data, this type of analysis also served as an additional level of quality control, once again illustrating that the majority of clones originated within the HSC compartment and that those clones are significant contributors to hematopoiesis due to their high overall abundance in the downstream read counts.

Lineages bias in aged individuals translates to the clonal level

Myeloid lineage bias is a hallmark of aged HSCs. Flow cytometric (FC) analyses of cells in PB confirmed that aged animals transplanted with aged HSCs (OO) showed pronounced myeloid skewing especially when compared to their YY counterparts. The OO-recipients presented with a lower percentage of T-cells (Figure 5A) and an elevated percentage of M/Gs (Figure 5B). Next, we analyzed whether this bias also manifests on the level of individual clones and whether different experimental settings influence the extent of manifestation. In particular, we ask whether biased clones rather increase their output, or whether there is truly an increase in the abundance of biased clones. To address these questions, we investigated all BCs present in any HSPCs population and determined the ratio of myeloid to lymphoid contribution per clone, particularly evaluating the M/G contribution vs. T-cell contribution. We classified each clone according to the majority class as either M/G or T-cell biased. Figure 5C, D shows that there was a consistent age-dependent increase in M/G-biased clones across experimental settings, and a respective decline in T-cell biased clones. These results closely mimic the aging phenotype seen in the FC data of YY and OO recipients (Figure 5A, B), suggesting that the overall, age-related myeloid bias also translates to the clonal level. Therefore, we conclude that lineage bias is not driven by the preferential expansion of myeloid cells within myeloid-biased clones but also by an overall enrichment of such biased clones. Furthermore, also the extent of the observable lineage bias seems to be more correlated with the age of the graft rather than the age of the recipient. To also statistically investigate the influence of recipient and donor age on lineage biases, we applied a beta regression, suited to address proportion data. Two models were fitted for the fraction of T-cell- and M/G-biased clones, with recipient age, donor age and mouse type as categorical predictors. For M/G-biased clones, we found a significant effect of donor age (β = 0.86, P = .005), indicating a higher proportion of myeloid-biased clones in recipients transplanted with old HSCs (Figure S6B). In contrast, the recipient age had no significant effect (β=−0.007, P = .98). For the T-cell-biased clones, neither donor age (β=−0.43, P = .17) nor recipient age (β=−0.11, P = .76) had a significant influence on lineage bias.

Differences in the clonal dynamics

Next, we assessed the presence of individual BCs across multiple cell types (Figure 5E), as a measure of clonal outgrowth and the underlying lineage potential. In all experimental groups (except YRag), the majority of BCs were detected across all 11 cell types, indicating a widespread distribution. BCs present in one up to ten cell types exhibited a stable yet lower median relative clone size of approximately 10%, with little variation among groups, except for YRag and YO. YRag recipients displayed an inverse pattern, showing a 2-fold increase in BCs unique to a single cell type and a 2-fold decrease in the median relative clone size of BCs present across all 11 cell types. Notably, YO transplants deviated from this pattern, instead of having most BCs restricted either to one or all 11 cell types, these transplants showed a 2-fold increase in both groups. While intra-group variance is considerable, this pattern suggests that under stress conditions, aged HSC clones are just as likely as their younger counterparts to contribute to a broad range of mature cell types.

Limited correlation between the clonal contributions in different cell types

The previous analyses focus either on BC identity or clone size distribution within individual compartments. In order to analyze potential correlations between both, we examined the Pearson correlation of BC abundances between all cell types in a pairwise fashion (Figure 6). Although we know from the previous analysis that there is a substantial overlap of clones between adjacent compartments (indicated by black squares in Figure 6), the clone sizes are only poorly correlated. Moreover, we did not detect substantial differences between homo- and hetero-chronic transplantation settings.

Correlation of BC occurrences and read counts for all transplantation settings. We report median Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) based on barcode occurrences and abundances of all 11 cell types for all three transplantation settings: (A) homo-chronic transplants, (B) hetero-chronic transplants, and (C) low intensity pre-conditioned recipients. The black squares highlight adjacent cell type connections which are part of the traditional hematopoietic hierarchy structure (LT-HSCs/ST-HSCs, ST-HSCs/MPPs, MPPs/CMPs, etc.).

Interestingly, the data shows a higher level of correlation between the hematopoietic cells from YRag animals compared to all other transplanted animals. In these transplants, a small number of larger clones appear to dominate the majority of compartments. This observation is supported by the relatively low clonal diversity among HSCs in YRag animals (Figure 2B) and the even lower number of unique BCs present across all 11 cell types (Figure 5E). This pattern may reflect a more selective engraftment process in young Rag1−/− mice, where only a subset of young donor HSCs successfully establishes long-term hematopoiesis. This could possibly be due to a higher proliferative and differentiation potential, resulting in a broader representation of those few contributing clones across all cell types.

Discussion

Stem cell transplantations remain the primary treatment option for various hematological disorders, emphasizing the importance of gaining further insights into the age- and conditioning-related impact on engraftment dynamics, clonal diversity, and long-term hematopoietic reconstitution. In this study, we investigated the clonality of homo-chronic and hetero-chronic HSC transplants, as well as transplants under a low-intensity pre-condition regimen. Our results demonstrate stable polyclonal engraftment across all age combinations, albeit with varying degrees of complexity. Notably, recipients of aged HSCs exhibited a higher number of detectable barcodes than those which received younger HSCs. However, clonal dominance and hematopoietic skewing appeared largely independent of recipient or donor age, with the exception of the YRag group, which, on average, exhibited a more oligoclonal pattern. Furthermore, our findings align with previous studies, supporting the notion that aged HSCs exhibit a stronger myeloid bias than their younger counterparts. While age influences the number of engrafted clones and the extent of the myeloid bias, our results suggest that it does not inherently preclude successful transplantation, even in an OO setting.

Interestingly, our findings seem to question the neutral drift model, which suggests that aging is accompanied by a progressive decline in clonality. However, this apparent inconsistency may be resolved by considering clonality as a context-dependent property shaped by the timing of clonal marking and experimental design. In steady-state hematopoiesis, the pool of active HSC clones gradually diminishes, whereas transplantation into a highly stressed environment, such as after lethal irradiation, necessitates rapid and broad HSC activation. Artificial labeling of equal numbers of young and old HSCs may create the impression of increased clonal diversity in aged grafts. Since aged HSC populations are intrinsically less diverse, functionally similar clones may receive distinct labels, leading to an overestimation of clonal complexity. In contrast, young HSCs exhibit greater inherent heterogeneity, making their labeled clones functionally more distinct. Thus, the seemingly paradoxical findings are not necessarily contradictory but rather a consequence of how clonality is measured and interpreted within different biological contexts.

A detailed analysis of BC occurrence and read abundance over all transplantation groups revealed significant differences between hematopoiesis driven by young versus old donor HSCs, irrespective of the recipient age. These differences manifested as higher clonal diversity in recipients of aged cells. One may speculate that an increased number of engrafting clones after transplantation into irradiated hosts may be necessary to compensate for the moderately decreased fitness of aged HSCs. In other words, a higher number of aged HSCs may be required to effectively reconstitute the hematopoietic system. Alternatively, this observation might be explained by an altered selection pressure on transplanted aged HSCs, either due to competition with equally competent host HSCs or as a consequence of radiation-induced disruption of the microenvironment. Such an explanation is supported by the data from the low-intensity pre-conditioning groups, where the transplantation of young HSCs resulted in the lowest clonal complexity across our experimental groups. Such an outcome would then likely be linked to the minimal physiological stress associated with this particular transplantation setting. Considering previous evidence on the differential effects of the BM microenvironment on aged HSC engraftment,25 our data further support the notion that the age of the microenvironment may also influence, at least to a certain extent, the number and diversity of HSC clones contributing to hematopoiesis after transplantation. Overall, while these findings may inform clinical practice, further studies are needed to determine the extent to which the pre-conditioning intensity might affect clonality in different transplantation settings.

Furthermore, we identified up to 30%-40% BCs among the M/G and T-cell compartments that were absent from the combined LT- and ST-HSC pool (Figure 4B-D). Although their overall contribution is small (Figure 4E-G) their upstream source remains unclear. Additionally, the maximum clone sizes within the M/G compartments were substantially lower (except for YRag) compared to all other cell types (Figure 2D). This combination of lower clone sizes and a limited amount of shared BCs might indicate a moderate decoupling of clonality of the M/G compartment from that in other hematopoietic cells types, especially under stress conditions.

In general, our data shows that aged murine HSCs and their age-matched niches have the capacity to effectively replenish and maintain a clonal repertoire post transplantation similar to that of their younger counterparts. This observation aligns with a growing body of evidence confirming clonal diversity in HSC transplants irrespective of donor age.5,22,26,27 While murine hematopoiesis may not perfectly mimic human biology, data from mice might provide valuable insights for the design of studies on clonality in humans. Nonetheless, the full extent and the implications of age-related changes in clonality still remain incompletely understood, necessitating further exploration especially with respect to additional co-factor influences like conditioning. Certain functional properties of HSC seem to only manifest under specific environmental or experimental conditions, while others appear independent of age or conditioning. This intricate interplay between aging, transplantation conditions, and clonal dynamics not only challenges prevailing assumptions but also highlights the complex interactions between aging, transplantation conditions, and clonal dynamics.
