Retrospective analysis of platelet to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio in obese children with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease
Authors: Gang Li, Chen Wang, Weixia Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe platelet-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (PHR) is an emerging biomarker for identifying systemic inflammation and a hypercoagulable state. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) encompasses a spectrum of liver conditions closely associated with metabolic dysfunction and is recognised as the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome. In 2020, an international consensus panel proposed “metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease” (MAFLD) as an alternative nomenclature for NAFLD, better reflecting the disease’s pathophysiology. MAFLD development is closely linked to inflammation and hypercoagulability, and this study aimed to investigate the association between PHR levels and MAFLD in children with obesity.MethodsA total of 209 obese children aged 6 to 14 years who visited the Children’s Medical Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University were enrolled in this study. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥95th percentile (P95) for the same age and sex. All participants underwent ultrasound to determine the presence of hepatic steatosis, and data on physical examination and biochemical parameters were collected. The platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (PHR) was calculated based on platelet (PLT) count and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) using the following formula: PHR = PLT (1,000 cells/µL)/HDL-C (mmol/L). Multivariable logistic regression analysis was applied to evaluate the association between PHR and MAFLD. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to characterize the dose-response relationship.ResultsChildren with MAFLD had significantly higher PHR levels than obese children without MAFLD (289.48±81.15 vs. 233.86±60.57, t=−5.67, P<0.001). Multivariable regression analysis demonstrated that the highest quartile of PHR (Q4) was significantly associated with an increased risk of MAFLD after adjustment for gender, age, BMI-standard deviation score (BMI-SDS) and multiple metabolic indices [odds ratio (OR) = 6.28, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.14–18.45, P<0.001]. The dose-response curve indicated a linear positive association between PHR and the risk of MAFLD (overall P<0.001, non-linearity P=0.77).ConclusionsElevated PHR level is an independent risk factor for MAFLD in obese children and may serve as a potential biomarker for the early screening of pediatric MAFLD.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), obese, children, platelet-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (PHR)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Children with obesity complicated by metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) had higher platelet-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (PHR) levels, and higher PHR levels were associated with an increased likelihood of having MAFLD.

What is known and what is new?

• The PHR is an emerging biomarker for identifying systemic inflammation, which is closely related to MAFLD.

• For the first time, this study demonstrates that high PHR levels are independently associated with MAFLD among obese children.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The PHR, as a simple and cost-effective clinical indicator, is promising for predicting the prevalence and disease risk of MAFLD in obese children.

Introduction

In recent years, the incidence of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) has increased progressively among obese children and adolescents. The global prevalence of MAFLD in children is approximately 3–10%, whereas the prevalence among obese children and adolescents can reach up to 42.4% (1,2). MAFLD encompasses a wide spectrum of liver injury, ranging from simple steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, which may lead to severe complications such as liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (3).

Pediatric MAFLD is usually insidious at onset and presents with non-specific clinical manifestations. Liver biopsy, the gold standard for diagnosis, is invasive and thus difficult to implement in routine pediatric physical examinations.

The pathogenesis of MAFLD is associated with multiple metabolic factors, including dyslipidemia, inflammatory status, a prothrombotic state, and insulin resistance (4-6). Significant changes occur in neutrophils, platelets (PLTs), lymphocytes, and acutephase proteins during inflammation. PLTs play a critical role in inflammation through aggregation and cytokine release, and are also involved in hemostasis and the promotion of coagulation (7). Mouse experiments have demonstrated that hypercoagulability can exacerbate MAFLD, which may be related to pro-inflammatory macrophages recruited to sites of hepatic steatosis by fibrin deposition. The thrombin/fibrin(ogen) axis may serve as a pharmacologically and genetically tractable driver of MAFLD (8).

PLT count and related parameters, such as mean PLT volume and PLT distribution width, are frequently elevated in children with MAFLD and show a positive correlation with the severity of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis stage (9,10). Recent studies have also reported significantly increased levels of PLT activation markers, including P-selectin and CD40 ligand, further supporting their roles in promoting inflammation and fibrogenesis during disease progression (11,12). High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) participates in reverse cholesterol transport, which shuttles excess cholesterol from peripheral tissues to the liver for metabolic clearance. HDL-C also exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects and alleviates hepatic steatosis (13,14). A study by Lu et al. supported the use of the PLT-to-HDL-C ratio (PHR) as a useful biomarker for MAFLD in adults (15).

However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have investigated the relationship between the PHR and MAFLD in obese children, and it remains unclear whether the PHR index is equally effective in this pediatric population. We hypothesized that the PHR is associated with MAFLD in obese children. A retrospective analysis was performed using data from the Children’s Medical Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University between 2022 and 2025 to explore the relationship between the PHR and MAFLD in obese children. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0416/rc).

Methods

Study population

A retrospective study was performed among obese children aged 6–14 years who were treated at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University between January 2022 and December 2025. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥95th percentile (P95) for age and gender according to the BMI Growth Curves for Chinese Children and Adolescents Aged 0–18 Years. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University (Approval No. 2024-K140-01; Date: July 20, 2024), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ parents or legal guardians.

Hepatic steatosis confirmed by liver imaging [ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)] or histology;

Measures

All physical examinations and laboratory biochemical data in this study were retrospectively extracted from Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University’s core information system (IIH system). The height and weight were measured by standard methods. All subjects were fasted for more than 8 hours before collecting peripheral venous blood samples. Blood samples are collected into dedicated blood collection tubes and sent to the Clinical Laboratory Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University for the determination of PLT count, HDL-C, and other related indicators. Imaging diagnosis of hepatic steatosis was independently made by two physicians with intermediate and higher professional titles. In this retrospective analysis, all subjects were incident cases of MAFLD identified at their initial outpatient consultation, and none had a prior documented diagnosis of MAFLD at study entry. Abdominal ultrasound examinations and blood samples of the same individual were both completed on the same day.

Sample size

This was a retrospective study with no predetermined sample size. The sample included all eligible cases treated at Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University between January 2022 and December 2025. A total of 209 obese children aged 6 to 14 years were enrolled.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). Participants were divided into four subgroups according to quartiles of PHR. For baseline characteristics, normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and compared using the Chi-squared test. A P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Four logistic regression models were constructed to determine the association between PHR and MAFLD, with the first quartile (Q1) as the reference group. Odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used to evaluate the dose-response relationship.

Results

A total of 209 obese children were enrolled in this study, including 95 cases in the non-MAFLD obese group and 114 cases in the MAFLD obese group. Intergroup comparison revealed statistically significant differences in PHR and multiple clinical indicators between the two groups, while no significant differences were observed in fasting blood glucose, albumin, total bilirubin, and other indicators (all P>0.05). The PHR in the MAFLD obese group was significantly higher than that in the non-MAFLD obese group (289.48±81.15 vs. 233.86±60.57, t=−5.67, P<0.001). In terms of general indicators, the MAFLD obese group had significantly higher age and BMI (11.11±1.77 vs. 10.05±2.00 years, 31.21±5.16 vs. 27.86±4.83 kg/m2; all t<0, P<0.001), and there was a significant difference in gender distribution between the two groups (χ2=8.68, P=0.003), with a higher proportion of males in the MAFLD obese group (67.54% vs. 47.37%). For glucose and lipid metabolism indicators, the two groups had no significant difference in fasting blood glucose (t=−0.05, P=0.96), but the levels of insulin and C-peptide in the MAFLD obese group were significantly elevated (50.55±71.98 vs. 29.50±41.58 mIu/L, 5.61±3.81 vs. 3.94±3.15 µg/L; all t<0, P<0.01). The MAFLD obese group showed significantly higher triglyceride (1.41±0.71 vs. 1.15±0.46 mmol/L, t=−3.20, P=0.002), lower HDL-C (1.13±0.22 vs. 1.29±0.26 mmol/L, t=4.88, P<0.001) and lower lipoprotein(a) (118.81±127.14 vs. 167.29±174.66 mg/L, t=2.32, P=0.02) compared with the non-MAFLD obese group. In terms of liver function, the activities of alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in the MAFLD obese group were significantly higher than those in the non-MAFLD obese group (66.06±73.01 vs. 31.82±50.05 U/L, 43.34±35.67 vs. 31.54±33.54 U/L; all t<0, P<0.05), with no significant intergroup difference in total bilirubin (t=−0.90, P=0.37). For renal function and uric acid, the two groups had no significant difference in creatinine (t=−1.72, P=0.09), while the serum uric acid in the MAFLD obese group was significantly higher (426.66±94.61 vs. 377.11±88.73 µmol/L, t=−3.88, P<0.001). Regarding blood routine indicators, the PLT count in the MAFLD group was significantly higher than that in the non-MAFLD obese group (313.49×109/L ± 59.21×109/L vs. 291.28×109/L ± 50.81×109/L, t=−2.92, P=0.004), and there were no significant differences in hemoglobin and neutrophil ratio between the two groups (all P>0.05). In addition, the incidence of diabetes showed no significant intergroup difference (χ2=1.69, P=0.19), with an overall low prevalence in the study population (3.35%) (Table 1).

We also used logistic regression to examine the link between HDL-C and MAFLD in children, with the lowest HDL-C quartile (Q1) as the reference. The unadjusted Model 1 showed that HDL-C was inversely related to MAFLD risk. Kids in the top HDL-C quartile (Q4) had far lower odds of MAFLD than those in Q1 (OR =0.13, 95% CI: 0.06–0.32, P<0.001), while Q2 and Q3 showed no meaningful protective impact (all P>0.05). After adjusting for sex and age in Model 2, the protective effect for Q4 stayed significant (OR =0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.46, P<0.001). We then added BMI-SDS to account for adiposity in Model 3; the association weakened slightly but remained statistically significant (OR =0.23, 95% CI: 0.09–0.59, P=0.002). Model 4 included all metabolic confounders: sex, age, BMI-SDS, apolipoprotein A, uric acid, insulin, triglyceride, ALT, AST, and C-peptide. After full adjustment, only Q4 still offered independent protection, though the effect size was clearly diminished (OR =0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.79, P=0.02). None of the intermediate quartiles (Q2, Q3) demonstrated a meaningful drop in MAFLD risk versus Q1 in any model. These data suggest that only high HDL-C levels independently reduce MAFLD risk in children, and this protective influence fades as more metabolic variables are controlled for in regression (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first attempt to systematically evaluate the association between the PHR and MAFLD in a cohort of obese children. What we found was that PHR concentrations were significantly higher in obese children with MAFLD compared with those without MAFLD, and elevated PHR was independently and linearly associated with MAFLD risk in a dose-response fashion.

Studies in adults have shown that PHR may serve as a potential predictive biomarker for MAFLD (15,16). The possible mechanism can be interpreted as follows: MAFLD is a complex, multifactorial, multiple-hit disease centered on metabolic dysfunction. Its pathogenesis is mediated by a combination of insulin resistance, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammatory fibrosis, gut-liver axis disturbance, and genetic susceptibility (4,5,6,17,18). Insulin resistance and alterations in the gut microbiota can induce hepatocyte steatosis (19). Lipotoxicity and oxidative stress further cause mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum damage, and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species activates inflammatory pathways, driving the progression from simple fatty liver to steatohepatitis (20). Gene polymorphisms such as PNPLA3 and HSD17B13 significantly affect disease susceptibility and progression risk, which contributes to individual differences (21). Children are in a period of growth and development, characterized by high basal metabolic rate, active glucose and lipid metabolism, strong insulin sensitivity, and an incompletely established gut microbiota, all of which differ substantially from adults (22,23). As the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, MAFLD is much more reversible in children. Whereas adult MAFLD can progress to cirrhosis at advanced stages, cirrhosis rarely occurs in the pediatric population (24).

PHR has emerged as a novel biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and metabolic status. It has shown predictive and diagnostic value in hypertension, hyperuricemia, depression, kidney stones, and other diseases (25-29). PLT activation and low HDL-C levels are both involved in chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and lipid metabolism disorders, which represent the core mechanisms of MAFLD. Therefore, as a combined indicator of PLTs and HDL-C, PHR can more comprehensively and sensitively reflect the inflammatory and metabolic abnormalities associated with MAFLD.

The study further found that after gradual adjustment for confounding factors, the associations of MAFLD with the Q2 and Q3 groups of PHR were no longer significant, whereas the Q4 group still exhibited an independent risk effect.

RCS analysis indicated that there was no obvious threshold effect between PHR and the risk of MAFLD; instead, they showed a continuous, linear positive correlation.

This phenomenon may be explained as follows: mild elevations in PHR in the Q2 and Q3 groups only reflect early, subclinical, mild inflammation and metabolic disturbances in obese children, with weak effects that are easily masked by traditional risk factors such as age, sex, and BMI. In contrast, marked elevations of PHR in the Q4 group represent more severe inflammatory imbalance and metabolic dysfunction, which exceed the body’s compensatory capacity and thus serve as an independent risk factor for MAFLD. In addition, we adopted consistent quartile stratification and stepwise covariate adjustment to compare the associations of PHR and HDL-C with pediatric MAFLD. After full metabolic adjustment (Model 4), the top PHR quartile remained strongly and significantly linked to MAFLD (OR =6.28, P<0.001) with steady statistical performance. In contrast, only the uppermost HDL-C quartile showed modest protective effects (OR =0.27, P=0.02). This comparison indicates that PHR retains more stable independent predictive value after adjusting for multiple metabolic confounders and performs better than HDL-C alone.

This study was a retrospective analysis of children aged 6–14 years based on the database of the Children’s Medical Center, Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University. It explored the feasibility of predicting the risk of pediatric MAFLD using novel inflammatory and metabolic indicators. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a significant association between PHR and MAFLD in the pediatric population, suggesting that PHR can serve as a predictive biomarker for MAFLD risk in children.

However, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center cross-sectional study, it is unable to establish a causal relationship between PHR and MAFLD. Second, due to the lack of corresponding laboratory data from normal-weight children, the study population was limited to obese children, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to the general pediatric population. Third, abdominal ultrasound was used as the primary imaging modality for MAFLD assessment in this study. This method can only qualitatively or semi-quantitatively detect diffuse hepatic steatosis, and cannot distinguish simple steatosis from steatohepatitis, nor can it determine the presence or absence of hepatic fibrosis. Consequently, we were unable to calculate the proportion of patients with steatohepatitis or identify subjects with concurrent hepatic fibrosis in the current cohort, which may lead to an underestimation of disease severity. Finally, the present study did not include gold-standard examinations such as liver elastography or liver biopsy, and thus the predictive value of PHR for steatohepatitis and hepatic fibrosis could not be further validated. Future longitudinal studies remain warranted to clarify whether elevated PHR truly predicts the progression of MAFLD and to more fully elucidate the clinical utility of PHR in obese children with MAFLD.

Nevertheless, given that PHR is convenient and inexpensive to measure, and shows an independent and linear positive correlation with MAFLD risk in obese children, it can be recommended as a simple and effective biomarker for the early screening of MAFLD. Routine PHR measurement is recommended in obese children to identify those at high risk of MAFLD at an early stage and implement timely interventions.

Conclusions

This retrospective analysis demonstrates that elevated PHR levels are an independent risk factor for MAFLD in obese children and may serve as a potential biomarker for the early screening of pediatric MAFLD. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.
