Adaptive robustness through incoherent signaling mechanisms in a regenerative brain
Authors: Samuel R. Bray, Livia S. Wyss, Chew Chai, Maria E. Lozada, Bo Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAnimal behavior emerges from collective dynamics of neurons, making it vulnerable to damage. Paradoxically, many organisms exhibit a remarkable ability to maintain significant behavior even after large-scale neural injury. Molecular underpinnings of this extreme robustness remain largely unknown. Here, we develop a quantitative pipeline to measure long-lasting latent states in planarian flatworm behaviors during whole-brain regeneration. By combining >20,000 animal trials with neural network modeling, we show that long-range volumetric peptidergic signals allow the planarian to rapidly restore coarse behavior output after large perturbations to the nervous system, while slow restoration of small-molecule neuromodulator functions refines precision. This relies on the different time and length scales of neuropeptide and small-molecule transmission to generate incoherent patterns of neural activity that competitively regulate behavior. Controlling behavior through opposing communication mechanisms creates a more robust system than either alone and may serve as a generalizable approach for constructing robust neural networks.

INTRODUCTION

Given its high interconnected complexity, the nervous system is seemingly vulnerable to major neuronal losses such as injuries, stroke, and degeneration.1,2 However, many animals can regenerate large sections of their nervous system after severe injury while maintaining high levels of motor function and sensitivity to various stimuli.3–7 The extreme robustness of their nervous system allows them to sense and escape from harmful environmental cues such as predators and UV irradiation during regeneration. Neural robustness is generally thought to be built in the topology of synaptic connectome using redundant links to remove nodes of high centrality and reduce dependency on any given neuron.1,2,8–10 Examples include distributed nerve nets of cnidarians3,4 and duplicated neural circuits in segmented animals such as annelids and insects.5,6 However, duplication of network components may be limited by high metabolic costs of neural maintenance.11

Here, we present evidence for an alternative strategy of neural robustness: in addition to synaptic connections, volumetrically transmitted long-range signals can increase effective connectivity of the network without adding new structures to the system (Figure 1A). Volume transmission is common in the nervous system and occurs at multiple scales. Besides transmitting across synapses, small molecules such as monoamines and acetylcholine can leak out of the synaptic cleft and function as neuromodulators. However, due to rapid reuptake and extracellular degradation, their diffusion is limited to fast timescales (~100 ms) and short distances (~μm), thereby targeting immediately adjacent neurons.12–14 In contrast, neuropeptides can be secreted throughout the entire neuronal body and diffuse for up to minutes over hundreds of micrometers, transmitting their signal to potentially large numbers of neurons with matching receptors.15–17 The large length scale of neuropeptide communication and its independence from synaptic connections may reduce sensitivity to disruptions such as missing neurons, axons, or connections.

We demonstrate neural robustness based on long-range diffusion through both experimental and computational model systems. Experimentally, we study the planarian flatworm Schmidtea mediterranea, a basal cephalized animal with the ability to regenerate its entire central nervous system from small tissue fragments.18–22 This regenerative ability is key to the survival and reproduction of planarians, which undergo asexual fission by ripping off tail fragments that then develop into new individuals.23 The planarian nervous system contains diverse neural cell types and complex structures including a bilobed brain, ventral nerve cords, and peripheral projections.24,25 The number of neurons may fluctuate between ~1,000 and ~100,000 in a single animal during growth and degrowth, requiring dynamic scaling of the neural architecture.18 At the functional level, planarians show complex behavior that integrates information from chemical, light, temperature, and mechanical stimulations.26 In particular, UV light is detected through sensory cells distributed throughout the body and can stimulate reflex-like responses in decapitated animals,7,27 although it is unclear whether more complex behaviors such as sensory integration are similarly independent of the brain on longer processing time-scales. The cellular and molecular underpinnings of the planarian’s behavioral robustness remain largely unexplored.

By developing a long-term high-content imaging platform, we observed thousands of planarians during homeostasis and regeneration and quantified their behavior over millions of time points through timescales ranging from seconds to days to study slower, integrative information processing of the nervous system, including memory, which can be measured as altered behavioral distributions in response to past experiences (e.g., prior stimulation).28,29 Through this approach, we identified previously unknown behaviors including signal integration and short-term memory after UV stimulation, revealing a long-lasting latent state in the planarian nervous system. We discovered that the maintenance of this excited state is mediated by neuropeptides and more robust than inhibition, which is controlled by locally acting small-molecule neurotransmitters. Using a dual-channel neural signaling model, we showed that the different time and length scales of neuropeptide and small-molecule transmission created interfering patterns of neural activity that competitively control neural population dynamics. Through genetic and surgical perturbations of both transmission mechanisms, we demonstrated that long-range diffusion allowed peptide-mediated dynamics to better persist than those driven by small-molecule neurotransmitters. This caused peptide function to dominate, generating robust behavior during regeneration. Emerging through dynamically balancing contributions of the two signaling mechanisms, this mode of “adaptive robustness” achieves more consistent control of behavior after injury than either system would alone.

RESULTS

High-content imaging reveals an excitable latent state in planarian behavior

To uncover complex behaviors of planarians such as sensory integration and memory, we imaged freely behaving planarians for extended periods, which has been challenging due to their strong preference for solid edges and photophobic responses.30 We developed quasi-2D fluidic chambers31 to contain animals (Figure 1B) and used infrared (IR) for non-perturbative illumination,32,33 as well as programmable UV (365 nm) to drive photophobic behavior.26,27,34 We confirmed that planarians could be continuously imaged on this setup over multiple days without significant changes to their behavior (Figures S1A and S1B).

Continuous imaging with a subsecond resolution allowed us to track animals and quantify changes in their positions over time (i.e., speed) (Figures S1C and S1D; Video S1). To better quantify responses with high sensitivity and precision across a broad range of UV doses, we defined a scalar activity measurement based on the rate of change in the planarian’s shape (Figure 1C; see STAR Methods). The variations in UV responses were statistically consistent whether comparing between individual animals over multiple trials or across different animals (Figure S1E), allowing us to combine data from biological and technical replicates to enhance statistics (Table S1).

Beyond known immediate reflexes,7,27 short UV pulses (<10 s) resulted in exponentially decaying post-stimulus activity, whereas longer stimulation saturated reflexive response but caused persistent high activity post stimulus for several minutes before decay (Figure 1D). The duration of high activity, rather than the peak activity, extended proportional to the dose of stimuli, implicating an ability to both integrate stimulus and differentiate the subsequent behavior. This led to a broad power-law scaling between stimulus duration and total post-stimulus activity—measured as the integrated activity over 10 min after stimulus—consistent with Stevens’ law35 (Figure 1E).

In addition to the activity measure, we used a hidden Markov model (HMM) to characterize different types of movements by comparing the distribution of changes in parameterized animal shapes in each Markov state (see STAR Methods). We found that these states can be assigned to roaming, twitching, nodding, and being stationary (Figures S2A–S2D) and the relative probability of roaming increased upon UV stimulation in correlation with the activity score (Figure 1F). The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the distributions of the Markov states after stimulation and under control conditions had a trajectory similar to that of activity (Figure 1G), suggesting that the scalar measurement of activity contains the necessary information to describe the high-dimensional behavioral data.

Planarians exhibit extended UV responses at intermediate regeneration stages

To study how information processing within the planarian nervous system changes after neural injury, we bisected planarians to completely remove the brain and measured the UV response of tail fragments every 2 h (Figures 2A and 2B; Video S2). After long UV pulses (30 s), total response was significantly decreased in the first 2 days but then increased to >50% beyond the baseline of the intact animals between 2 and 6 days post amputation (dpa). More surprisingly, the response extended in time after injury, measured by response half-life (i.e., the time needed for median activity to fall to half its peak), reaching twice as long after the stimuli at 2 dpa compared to intact animals.

Analysis of the Markov states revealed an initial reduction and then extension in the roaming state, aligning with the activity measure, whereas the reflexive twitching behavior substantially increased in the first 2 days before gradually returning to the baseline (Figures 2C and S2E). This observation suggests that the reflexive and long-lasting behaviors are controlled through distinct mechanisms.

Response to shorter pulses (i.e., 5–10 s) followed similar trends, with initially reduced responses followed by the emergence of secondary “resonant” post-stimulus peaks during 4–6 dpa, before gradual recovery (Figures 2A, 2B, S2F, and S2G). Notably, brain regeneration occurs in a similar time frame, with a primordial brain forming at ~3 dpa and undergoing structural development over the following week.19–21

Peptidergic and small-molecule signals maintain and inhibit behavioral states, respectively

To identify the neural mechanisms determining the extent of UV responses, we began by disrupting the core SNARE complex, including syntaxin36 and snap25,37 which mediates synaptic vesicle release and fusion38 (Figure 3A), using RNA interference (RNAi). Although RNAi is a standard technique that has been used for large-scale gene function studies in the planarian nervous system,21,39 we first validated the reproducibility of behavior measurements after RNAi treatments (Figures S3A and S3B). Both syntaxin and snap25 RNAi resulted in loss of the UV response (Figure 3B), demonstrating that synaptic network is required for producing these measured behaviors in planarians. In addition, RNAi of vesicular glutamate transporter, vglut,40 fully abolished the UV response (Figure 3C) and caused uncoordinated muscular contractions (Video S3), suggesting that glutamate is a key synaptic transmitter in planarians.

We further knocked down previously characterized synthesis enzymes of various small-molecule neurotransmitters/neuromodulators and neuropeptides.41–45 Our results showed that the disruption of octopamine (i.e., knockdown of tyramine β-hydroxylase, tbh41), GABA (glutamic acid decarboxylase, gad43), and acetylcholine (choline acetyltransferase, chat44) syntheses all led to excess post-stimulus activity in response to UV. Similar to the excess activity observed during regeneration, these knockdowns resulted in secondary resonant peaks in activity after 5-s UV stimulation, significantly longer responses after 30-s UV pulses after tbh and gad RNAi, quantified by total response and half-life, and increased activity levels after chat RNAi (Figures 3D and 3E; Video S4). Double knockdown of chat and tbh led to a more severe phenotype than either single knockdown (Figures 3D and 3E). Consistently, HMM analysis showed that these RNAi treatments mostly extended the roaming state (Figure S3C). Together, these results suggest that inhibition of post-stimulus activity depends on the aggregate function of multiple small-molecule neurotransmitters.

In contrast, reduction of post-stimulus activity was only observed when knocking down prohormone convertase 2 (pc2), which is required for the maturation of many planarian neuropeptides.45 Planarians have densely packed peptidergic neurons collectively expressing a suite of over 60 neuropeptides, most, if not all, of which are coexpressed with small-molecule neuromodulator synthesis genes.24,45,46 Long-range peptide transmission could create a densely connected network, as almost every planarian neuron expresses some neuropeptides or neuropeptide receptors.24 While peptides are known to often act in parallel redundantly, synergistically, or competitively,12,47,48 disrupting pc2 allows the study of the net effect of this transmission network on behavior through reduction of overall peptide concentrations.45,49

While previous studies have noted that pc2 knockdown severely impairs coordinated movement in planarians,39 our findings indicate that pc2 RNAi animals under UV stimulation could activate the full range of behavior seen in control animals, albeit with lower frequency (Figure 4A and Video S5). Consistently, their UV responses matched the levels of controls for weak UV stimuli (Figures 4B and 4C). However, unlike controls that maintained high activity after long stimulation (30 s), responses of pc2 knockdown animals decayed immediately (Figures 4B and 4C). HMM analysis showed that, while these animals were capable of initiating roaming, they reverted to the resting stationary state more rapidly compared to controls (Figure 4D), whereas the probability of the twitching state remained mostly unaffected, indicating that peptidergic signal is required specifically for maintaining the roaming state. These effects prevented the animals from differentiating their responses to long stimuli and caused saturation in the dose-response curve (Figure 4E), suggesting that, while pc2 RNAi animals can detect and respond to UV, they fail to maintain the latent state corresponding to the integrated signal. Double knockdowns of pc2 and small-molecule neurotransmitters led to phenotypes similar or slightly more pronounced than pc2 RNAi alone (Figures 4F, 4G, S3D, and S3E), implicating potential synergistic effects between peptidergic and small-molecular modulators in regulating neural activity (see discussions below). Collectively, these RNAi experiments suggest that small-molecular neurotransmitters need to act upon the latent state maintained by the peptides.

Finally, when we amputated pc2 knockdown animals, they failed to show either an extended response or a resonant peak after 30-s and 5-s UV pulses, respectively, throughout the course of regeneration (Figures 4H and S4). Altogether, these results led us to conclude that long-range peptide signaling may control the rapid return of response maintenance after injury, whereas small-molecule signals mediate the more fragile inhibitory functions.

Neuropeptide signaling maintains short-term memory

We hypothesized that peptides could also mediate short-term memory at the minute timescale and that this memory may be similarly robust to injury. Here, we consider the memory as the encoding of information about stimulation history and the functional retrieval of this encoded state by the motor system leading to a change in behavioral output.28 To test this, we exposed planarians to pairs of 5-s UV pulses separated by a time delay. We observed that the response to the second pulse was significantly stronger than that to a single pulse with no prior stimulation (Figure 5A), with enhanced roaming state but diminished twitching state according to the HMM analysis (Figure S5A). This result demonstrates sensitization, defined here as the change in total post-stimulus activity, indicative of a form of short-term memory.50 This effect could be amplified by stronger first pulses (Figure 5B). To measure how the memory of the first pulse changes over time, we varied the delay between pulses and found a non-monotonic decay of the sensitizing effect with a secondary peak at ~3 min delay (Figure 5C). This contrasts the approximately exponential decay in activity after a single 5-s stimulation, indicating that the observed sensitization is driven by more than the additive effects between pulses.

While pc2 RNAi did not affect response to single 5-s UV pulses (Figure 4B), it eliminated sensitization (Figures 5D and 5E), suggesting that neuropeptides are required for maintaining short-term memory. Importantly, sensitization was initially reduced in amputated planarians but increased beyond that of whole-animal controls at 1 dpa and peaked at 2 dpa, demonstrating rapid recovery of sensitization memory but a lack of inhibition after injury (Figures 5F and S5B). This observation parallels the trend in the single-pulse responses during regeneration with matched timescales, suggesting that the same peptide-dependent excitable latent state may encode both signal integration and short-term memory.

Peptide-mediated functions are more robust to surgical and genetic perturbations to the nervous system

If long-range neuropeptide transmission is less reliant on the intact network structure than small-molecule signaling, lost inhibition and excess behavioral activity should be a common outcome of perturbing the planarian nervous system. To test this prediction, we performed several different surgical cuts causing partial brain damage, including severing anterior commissures (i.e., “triangle corpus”), amputating anterior to the eyespots (“pre-eyespot”), and removing posterior tissue to the left eyespot (“biopsy”). Even though these injuries affected distinct neural structures, they all led to similarly exaggerated UV responses within the first day after injury (Figures 6A and 6B).

We also perturbed neural structures genetically by performing RNAi to disrupt a set of nine transcription factors (TFs). These TFs, including tcf1, nkx2.1, pknox, and otxA, are known to play important roles in neural regeneration in the planarian.21,24,51 For example, knockdown of tcf1 and nkx2.1 leads to dysregulation in the specification of multiple neural populations,20,22 pknox RNAi disrupts brain regeneration,52 and otxA knockdown disturbs neural development, particularly for the photoreceptor neurons.53,54 Consistent with the distinct functions of these TFs, RNA sequencing of RNAi animals revealed no common differentially expressed genes after these knockdowns (Figures S6A and S6B). In stark contrast, almost all TF RNAi experiments led to similar excess activity (Figures 6C, 6D, and S6C; Video S4) and extended roaming state in response to UV (Figure S6D). Double knockdowns of TFs and pc2 led to phenotypes that resembled that of pc2 RNAi, implying that the excess activity induced by TF knockdowns is peptide dependent (Figures 6E and 6F). The strikingly consistent effect across surgical and genetic perturbations implies that the differential robustness of maintenance and inhibition of the latent state is unlikely caused by asynchronous regeneration of specific neural populations or consequences of wound responses. Instead, the two processes may be encoded through distinct patterns in population-scale neural dynamics, with peptide-mediated dynamics more robust to structural changes independent of specific neural circuits.

Long-range volumetric transmission explains the robustness of peptide function

We then asked whether differential signaling ranges are sufficient to explain the observed difference in the robustness of peptide- and small-molecule-mediated processes. For this, we developed a neural network model in which neurons interact through both volumetric and synaptic signals. We constrained the model to regulate behavior in the same manner as in planarians and then tested whether the peptide communication maintains the behavioral output after neural injury.

Specifically, we modeled population-scale neural dynamics using a custom recurrent neural network (RNN), which simulates neuronal firing coupled through sparse synaptic links and produces behavioral output by passing the neural firing state through a trained motor output layer (Figure 7A).55 This output layer contains negative or positive weights, meaning that individual neurons can either activate or inhibit behavior and that behavior is not assumed to be directly correlated with total neural firing rate. This type of model has been shown to reproduce key aspects of animal neural dynamics when trained on similar tasks.55–57 Because of the relatively fast and targeted scales of small-molecule transmission, we model their contribution as instantaneous action through synaptic links and assign subsets of neurons as octopaminergic, dopaminergic, GABAergic, and cholinergic. To account for long-range volumetric signals, we augmented the model by allowing neural firing to trigger the release of both excitatory and inhibitory neuropeptides.58 These peptides diffuse and persist extracellularly, dissipate via a first-order decay reaction, and modulate neural firing potentials based on local concentrations in a paracrine-signaling manner (Figure S7A; see STAR Methods).

Because the model is implemented in a deep-learning framework, we were able to constrain its dynamics to produce experimentally observed behavior. Simply training on wild-type response is insufficient to ensure that long-range peptide and targeted small-molecule signals maintain and inhibit behavioral activity, respectively. Therefore, we also trained on pc2 RNAi data while blocking peptide transmission in the model, allowing it to learn the effective contribution of the volumetric signal. Similarly, to constrain the roles of small-molecule neurotransmitters, we trained on RNAi data blocking their production while allowing neural firing and peptide release to be sustained. We also included double-knockdown data in the training set to constrain the epistatic interactions between neurotransmitters/neuromodulators in the model. This protocol was sufficient to train models that each fit all the experiments simultaneously, independent of initial conditions and RNN connectivity (Figure 7B). We note that the fit for chat RNAi data was poorer than other conditions, likely due to the high-amplitude activity caused by frequent U-turn movements after chat RNAi (Video S4). As the model did not extend to types of movements, this biological feature was not captured.

We then tested whether these constraints were sufficient to explain the differential robustness of the two transmission mechanisms. We simulated the effects of neural injury by ablating regions at various locations and of different sizes (Figure S7B). Matching experiments, large ablations reduced the total response, corresponding to early regeneration time points post decapitation when the network is highly disrupted. Moderate ablations resulted in exaggerated responses similar to those observed in regenerating animals or those with a surgically or genetically perturbed nervous system (Figure 7C). This consistency between experiments and model predictions demonstrates that the robustness of peptide signaling and the early return of peptide-mediated maintenance of behavioral state after injury can be explained by the difference in transmission mechanisms, regardless of circuit details.

Adaptive robustness through incoherent signal competition

This dual-channel model also allowed us to study why maintenance and inhibition of behavior segregate by mechanisms of neural transmission. It is worth emphasizing that neither system is mechanistically constrained to excite or inhibit neural firing in the model—both synaptic transmission and peptides can have excitatory and inhibitory actions. Nor do they directly inhibit the transmission of the other signal; rather, stimulating neural firing increases synaptic transmission and peptide release proportionately. This was clearly seen in the model where pc2 knockdown decreased neural firing (Figure S7C).

Therefore, the competition between volumetric and synaptic transmission in regulating behavior must be an emergent property at the neural population level. Due to long decay time and long-range diffusion, peptide signaling generates slowly varying, spatially correlated patterns of neural activity. In contrast, targeted synaptic connections and local neural modulations produce rapidly changing, spatially uncorrelated neural firing. Because both mechanisms are broadly coexpressed in planarian neurons,24 these different patterns of neural activity propagate in a shared medium, creating incoherent correlation structures that effectively compete for control. By aligning motor output to patterns of neural dynamics driven by peptide signaling (e.g., spatially continuous patterns), peptide function can stabilize and maintain behavior activity, while high-frequency targeted signals disrupt these patterns, driving the neural activity to new states and terminating the active state (Figure 7D).

We demonstrated the notion of competing neural patterns by measuring the spatial correlation of neural firing within the trained model. We detected peaks corresponding to the diffusion lengths of the neuropeptides, which were lost after peptide knockdown (Figure 7E), consistent with the idea that the spatial correlation was driven by peptide diffusion. To reveal the effects of synaptic competition on this firing pattern, we removed 10% of the synaptic connections and observed an increase in spatial covariance due to the decrease in interfering signals, supporting the notion that the two systems compete (Figure 7E). We also noted that the population activity was in a high-dimensional space with patterns of activity generated by the two transmission mechanisms not strictly orthogonal. Indeed, removing large factions of synaptic transmission (~50%) caused a loss of spatial covariance in the model (Figure 7E), suggesting that some level of synaptic connectivity was needed to amplify the initial firing and peptide communication. This result is consistent with our double RNAi experiments in which the knockdown of small-molecule neurotransmitters or TFs along with pc2 resulted in further reduction of activity beyond pc2 RNAi alone (Figures 4F and 6E).

This concept of competing neural patterns also explains our observations during neural regeneration. The reduced competitive interference from synaptic transmission on peptide-mediated firing patterns may be weakened upon injury. We quantified this effect by quantifying the spatial correlation in firing dynamics, which became more pronounced after moderate ablations (Figures 7F and S7D).

To further validate that differential robustness between signaling mechanisms determined the behavioral output after injury, we varied the topological robustness of synaptic connectivity by training over 150 independent RNN models with different network structures (Figure S7E). More robust synaptic networks maintained their contribution to neural dynamics and prevented ablation-induced excess activity even though the peptide transmission was kept identical (Figure 7G). This provides direct evidence that the excess activity induced by neural injury is caused by the different capacity of the peptidergic and small-molecule signaling to maintain their functions in disrupted neural networks.

Finally, this ensemble of models allowed us to perform regression analysis between the model parameters and the ablation-induced excess activity to identify the determining factors of neural robustness. This analysis revealed a strong and significant correlation (p < 0.01) with maximum peptide diffusion length (Figure 7H). The next largest contributor was the decay rate of the synaptic potential, which separated the timescales of peptide and small-molecule actions. To further test the importance of peptide transmission range on the model’s performance, we attempted to refit models after restricting the diffusion length of neuropeptides to only one cell body radius. Even after retraining, models with restricted diffusion failed to replicate the experimental data as closely as those with longer peptide transmission, demonstrating the importance of long-range diffusion in explaining the experiments.

DISCUSSION

Unlike neurogenesis during embryonic development that occurs in protected environments, neural regeneration must proceed while responding to threats from the surrounding world. In particular, avoiding UV irradiation is critical because it induces DNA damage, which can become particularly harmful during regeneration due to elevated cell proliferation.59 UV avoidance behavior requires not only immediate escape responses but also signal integration and short-term memory, which allow animals to evaluate changes in stimuli across space and time and perform phototaxis at length scales much larger than their body size.60,61 Our results demonstrate that both of these behaviors are mediated by neuropeptides. The volumetric diffusion of peptides makes them largely robust to structural injury, allowing for recovery early on during regeneration, which can provide a selective advantage in regenerating animals, especially those that reproduce through repeated fission and regeneration.

For these long-lasting behaviors over the timescale of several minutes to persist after injury, neural computing must be robust to neural network structural perturbations in order to sustain internal neural states. Based on the behavioral data and computational analysis, we propose a conceptual model of “adaptive neural robustness” (Figure 7I). Following structural injury, both communication channels, mediated by peptides and small molecules, respectively, are perturbed in a correlated manner, making their competitive output more robust than either channel alone.

This model was largely motivated by the observation that a wide range of perturbations to the nervous system can trigger similarly exaggerated post-stimulus activity. Some of these disruptions were designed to non-specifically affect multiple neural populations in the central and peripheral nervous systems simultaneously, as demonstrated in the RNAi experiments. Other experiments that targeted distinct parts of the nervous system (e.g., partial brain damage experiments) still consistently yielded similar behavioral phenotypes, implying that the phenomenon is unlikely governed by specific neuroanatomy or neural circuitry. These observations led us to the interpretation that the observed neural robustness is an emergent property regulated at the neural population level, with the long-range diffusion and slow timescales of neuropeptide transmission offering a molecular basis for sustaining the latent neural states.62,63 To link the microscopic processes such as the diffusion of neurotransmitters and the emergent macroscopic effects on behavior, we adopted a systems neuroscience approach, using computational modeling to test which features and constraints of neural mechanisms are sufficient to control behaviors through population-level dynamics.56,57,64 The broad agreement between the model prediction and experiments (Figures 7B and 7C) lend strong support to our proposed mechanism. In addition, by solely considering the essential biophysical properties of neurotransmitters without reliance on any biological features specific to the planarian, we argued that this mechanism for neural robustness may be generalizable.

This work also links the maintenance of a long-lasting roaming state in planarians with neuropeptides, whereas the initiation of the UV responses and the reflexive twitching state, which is amplified at early stages of regeneration, both appear to be peptide independent. The length of the roaming state is regulated by small-molecule neurotransmitters, as blocking their syntheses only extended roaming without affecting the twitching state. The function of prolonging the roaming state is likely an aggregate effect of multiple neuropeptides, as individual neuropeptide knockdowns did not replicate the phenotype of pc2 RNAi, indicating redundancy among peptides, which typically act in combinations.47 Previous studies have suggested that certain peptides may regulate the phototaxis in planarians65; however, considering the role of peptides in governing the roaming state, which could in turn influence phototaxis outcomes, these behaviors may be worth revisiting.

It has become increasingly recognized that neuropeptides can function as neuromodulatory signals, stimulating specific behaviors in diverse animals, including nematodes, flies, annelids, and even mammals.15,66–70 In certain cases, peptides operate in opposition to small-molecule signals such that the interactions between the two drive the switching between behavioral states, such as roaming and dwelling.15,70 The parallel across different organisms implies that the division of labor between the two transmission mechanisms may be a common feature of many neural circuits, given that peptide-mediated communication can form a “wireless” network throughout the entire nervous system, operating alongside the synaptic connectome.71,72 Examining whether these circuits possess similarly high robustness is an important avenue for future research.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of this study is the inability to experimentally measure neural dynamics for direct comparison with the model, due to the lack of transgenic tools that can express neural activity reporters in planarians.73 However, this constraint might be overcome in the future by using genetically tractable regenerative models that are amenable for live imaging. On the computational side, we modeled the action of volumetric and synaptic transmission in isolation, neglecting several complex factors74 such as synaptic fatigue, influence from other active neural processes, longer-term plasticity, and neural circuit architecture. Although this simplification was chosen in preference for interpretability and generalizability, it did limit our ability to explain complex behaviors such as the secondary peak in the UV response post injury (Figure 2A) and the time dependence in short-term memory (Figure 5). Rather than the amorphous structure used in our model, it is plausible that integrating neuropeptides in an effective feed-forward loop in time could create a transient activated state75,76 and recapitulate these behaviors. Additionally, the current model only considers the diffusion of neuropeptides, as planarians lack a circulatory system,77,78 although the release of peptides into coelomic fluid or the circulatory system could potentially extend the range of their activity. Importantly, our model architecture and training scheme do not exclude feedback mechanisms or neuroendocrine transport, both of which could be explored within the existing framework.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

Animal care and maintenance

Asexual S. mediterranea were maintained in the dark at 20 °C in water containing 0.5 g/L Instant Ocean Sea Salts and 0.1 g/L sodium biocarbonate. Planarians of ~4 mm in length were used for whole-animal behavior experiments and fed once or twice a week. For amputation experiments, we selected animals of ~8 mm in length so the regenerating fragments were approximately the same size as those used in whole-animal experiments.

METHOD DETAILS

Imaging chamber

To create the imaging chambers, 150 mm petri dishes were plasma treated for 2 min at high power to create a hydrophilic surface. Ten circular templates with 20 mm in diameter were traced in each dish with a permanent marker to pattern a hydrophobic mask. Each region was first loaded with a single planarian in 800 μL of Instant Ocean water to ensure complete wetting and then reduced to ~300 μL. This flattened the top surface of the droplet and reduced reflections during imaging. After loading, the dish was gently filled with ~50 mL of silicone oil until the top of all droplets were covered. The oil phase both reduced evaporation and increased surface tension at the droplet boundary to prevent planarians from escaping. Planarians remained active for at least ~7 d at 20 °C under this condition. All animals were starved at least 4 d prior to imaging and all imaging sessions lasted no longer than 7 d. For imaging regeneration time courses, animals were loaded at 8 hpa and rinsed before loading to reduce the accumulation of material ejected from the wound within the droplet. To cover the complete span of regeneration, a second cohort of animals was imaged starting 3 dpa for ~7 d. Activity responses at matching regeneration time were merged from both datasets and used for all analyses.

Imaging setup

Animals were illuminated with an IR light source (850 nm) from the side, and images were recorded at 2 frames per second using a Raspberry Pi NoIR camera, except for a higher resolution CMOS camera used to generate the videos shown in Videos S1 and S3. UV light (365 nm) stimuli were delivered by a custom-built ring of 36 LEDs mounted above the camera to illuminate the entire dish uniformly and controlled by an Arduino Uno to adjust intensity and pulse duration. In this setup, the total power that each liquid droplet received was <10 mW. Given the volume of water in the droplet (300 μL) and heat capacity of water (~4 J/g), the rate of heating from the UV source was negligible (~10−6 K/s) even assuming there is no dissipation of energy to the environment. To eliminate glare from the stimulation light source, an 800 nm long-pass filter was mounted in the camera tube. For all stimulation experiments, repetitions of the stimulation protocols were separated by 2 h of unstimulated, dark time to prevent influence between trials.

RNAi

Gene knockdowns were carried out by feeding double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) to induce RNAi-mediated gene silencing. The dsRNA was synthesized using the established protocol45 and fed to animals by mixing in a liver homogenate at a concentration of approximately 100 ng/μL. All clones for dsRNA synthesis were generated using oligonucleotide primers (Table S2) and cloned into vector pJC53.2 (Addgene plasmid ID: 26536).45 For the RNAi control condition, we fed dsRNA matching the ccdB and camR-containing insert of pJC53.2.

For all experiments, we fed dsRNA to animals 5–7 times every 4–5 d, except pc2 RNAi for which we fed 3 times when the animals stopped feeding. Animals were starved for 4 d prior to amputation, after which tails were allowed to regenerate and imaged between 10 and 20 dpa, except for pc2 RNAi animals, which were imaged 5 d after the last feeding without amputation. These treatments alone did not cause significant behavioral changes without gene knockdowns (Figure S3A), allowing us to lump all the control conditions to compare against knockdowns to maximize statistical power. Similarly, behaviors were largely consistent between RNAi batches (Figure S3B), which we also combined to enhance statistical power (Table S1). Knockdown of syntaxin caused lysis after ~20 dpa, but no gross morphological phenotypes were observed during the time window of imaging.

RNA sequencing

We performed RNAseq experiments on tcf1, nkx2.1, otxA, and pknox RNAi animals along with controls, using three replicates each containing 10 animals. After RNAi, we amputated the animals pre-pharyngeally and allowed the tail fragments to regenerate for 10 days. We then excised the heads and extracted RNA using Trizol. The RNA was cleaned up using the RNeasy Micro Kit, which includes a DNase treatment step. For RNAseq library preparation, we used the TECAN Universal Plus mRNA-Seq kit, starting with 100 ng of total RNA. mRNA was isolated using the poly T oligo magnetic beads and fragmented to ~200 bp. cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification followed the kit instructions. We used the single-ended barcode sequences from the Universal Plus UDI 96-Plex Adaptor plate (S02480) for multiplexing. The libraries were pooled, and sequenced on a NextSeq 500/550 to obtain approximately 10–15 million reads per library. For the analysis, we aligned the reads to the planarian reference transcriptome84 using Bowtie2.79 Differential gene expression analysis was then performed using DESeq2.80 Heatmap shows Z score of normalized read counts generated using the seaborn library.

Dual-channel RNN model

We modified a well-established RNN model55 by adding long-range volumetric signals. All neural models were implemented, trained, and run using Keras architecture with Tensorflow 1.14 backend in a Python environment with CUDA GPU acceleration.81 For a graphical diagram of the model architecture, see Figure S7A.

Models trained within this work contained 2,500 neuron nodes arranged in a 50 ✕ 50 square grid (nneuron) and 2 neuropeptides (npep). These values were chosen to maximize flexibility in model training while maintaining loss gradients that could be stored in working memory. The RNN cell had 5 state tensors passed between time points: a nneuron vector of neural states (Xsyn), an array of neuropeptide concentrations at each neuron location (Xpep), a binary variable indicating whether neurons are capable of responding to neuropeptides (gpep), a vector describing the modulatory potency of each neuron (gsyn), and a vector describing the ablation status of each neuron (A).

The model had one fixed parameter, a nneuron×nneuron connectivity matrix (C), which is sparse and binary. This matrix was defined using the Watts-Strogatz method.85 By varying the average node-degree (k) and rewiring probability (β), this method can create networks with topologies ranging from a completely regular ring (β=0), to small-world networks (0<β<1), to a random network (β=1). As previous studies have demonstrated “rich club” small-world networks in a variety of neural systems,86 we defaulted to using a network with k=8, β=0.001. For the models shown in Figure 7G, triplicate models with different initializations and C were trained with node degrees logarithmically spaced from 2 to 64, and β logarithmically spaced from 0 to 1 to average the effects of the sparse weighted connections. To compare the robustness of the synaptic connectivity topology, we calculated the graph connectivity of the network using the Python package NetworkX. This measure describes the smallest number of nodes that must be removed to separate the graph and corresponds with our simulated perturbation of node ablation.

The RNN cell had 7 trainable parameters: a nstimulus×nneuron input vector transforming stimulus condition into influence on neural states, a nneuron×nneuron weight matrix (Wsyn), a decay rate of neural potential (δneuron), the production rate of each neuropeptide upon neural firing (μpep), the decay rate of each extracellular neuropeptide (δpep), the diffusivity of each neuropeptide (D), the scaling co-efficient of peptide action on local neural potential (Wpep), and a nneuron vector Win which defines the transformation of the current stimulus state (Ut) into change in neural potential. The action of neurons was modeled through continuous firing rates rather than discrete firing events.55 The rationale underlying the treatment of the small-molecule mediated processes as instantaneous is that the action timescales of small molecule neurotransmitters are all significantly shorter than the model timescale resolution and that the diffusion and degradation of neuropeptides happen on time scales orders of magnitude slower.

At each time step, firing rates were sampled from the neural state according to: F=relu(Xsyn+N(0,10−3))

The non-linear rectifier maps the state of the cell into a positive firing rate.55 Extracellular neuropeptides were updated according to: Xpep(t+1)=Xpep(t)+μpepF−δpepXpep(t)+D(Xpep(t),D) where D applies a diffusivity kernel: D(Xpep(i,j,t),D)=D[−4Xpep(i,j,t)+Xpep(i−1,j,t)+Xpep(i+1,j,t)+Xpep(i,j−1,t)+Xpep(i,j+1,t)] to the peptide concentrations according to their arrangement on the square array of neurons. For calculating spatial diffusion, the array was treated with periodic boundary conditions to remove the effect of system size. Neural state updates were given by: Xsyn(t+1)=[Xsyn(t)−δneuronXsyn(t)+(Wsyn⊙C)(gsyn⊙F)+Wpep(gpep⊙Xpep)+WinUt]⊙A

Multiplication of modulatory weights and connectivity enforces a specific, sparse network of connections throughout training. Multiplication of gsyn and the firing rate masks output from genetic knockdowns of small-molecules. Multiplication of gpep and Xpep masks contributions of neuropeptide signaling. Multiplication of the entire state with A completely masks all contributions of ablated neurons in a sample.

Model inputs were the stimulus vector across the simulation time range U, gsyn, gpep, and A, which were concatenated in the model with X0, a trainable variable providing the neuron and peptide states as initial conditions fed to the RNN. The RNN then simulated 1,200 time steps (10 min) of zero-stimulus response. This allowed the neural states to evolve to a stable state, which was given the knockdown and ablation input. The final equilibrated state was then passed with U to the RNN, which simulated the dynamics and returned the complete neural state at each time point. This was rectified into the firing rate, and convolved with a single time step kernel, which effectively applied a trainable output matrix Wout to the firing rates at each time step to generate the simulated activity (Z). Z was returned as the output of the complete machine learning model.

For initialization, elements in large weight matrices were independently sampled according to: Wsyn(i,j)∼N(2×10−5,2×10−4), neural states of X0(i,j)∼N(0,10−2), peptide states of X0(i,j)∼Exp(10−2),Win(i,j)∼N(0,5), and Wout(i,j)∼N(0,0.05). Other parameters were set to: μpep=[1,1]; δpep=[0.2,0.2]; D=[3,10]; Wpep=[0.9,−0.6]; δneuron=1.25. These values were chosen to produce non-diverging neural dynamics from which we began training.

Models were trained on the median activity following stimulation and under knockdown conditions. To prevent overfitting to the timing of UV pulses, each of these median traces was used to create 20 training data samples with start times randomly selected between 1 and 4 min before the start of stimulation. Samples each had a duration of 26 min (except for Era 1, in which duration was set to 13 min in order to reduce the size of gradient operations and to speed the initial training for obtaining a rough shape of the response). The training was performed without ablation (A=1). In samples of pc2 RNAi data, gpep=0. To account for small-molecule neurotransmitter knockdowns, neurons were randomly assigned as GABAergic (12.5%), octopaminergic (12.5%), dopaminergic (12.5%), or cholinergic (50%), or generic (12.5%). The relatively high fraction of cholinergic neurons was chosen to match their abundance in the planarian nervous system,24 but the model result was only weakly dependent on the neuronal type fractions. We did not specify spatial patterns for these neural types to keep the model general and species agnostic. In samples of gad, tbh, th, or chat RNAi data, gsyn was set to zero for the corresponding neurons to prevent their activity. All neurons in the model were competent for both neuropeptide release and detection regardless of synaptic transmitter identity.

The loss (L) was defined as the mean square error between the median activity and simulated activity Z, and was optimized using ADAM gradient descent. Models were trained in 4 eras:

Era 1: learning rate=10−4+10−3exp(−epoch15), epochs = 10, batch size = 6.

Era 2: learning rate=10−4+10−3exp(−epoch15), epochs = 40, batch size = 4.

Era 3: learning rate=3×10−5, epochs = 10, batch size = 4.

Era 4: learning rate=3×10−5+10−4exp(−epoch3), epochs = 10, batch size = 4.

In Era 4, training was stopped early if the change in average loss was less than 5×10−4 for 3 consecutive epochs.

Each model was trained using responses to 5-s and 30-s pulses for control animals, as well as pc2, gad, tbh, th, and chat RNAi animals. To further constrain the functional fitting within the model, we performed double knockdowns of gad:pc2, tbh:pc2, th:pc2, and chat:pc2 and included them within the training set. Additionally, to stabilize the long-term behavior of the simulated model, we stimulated animals with UV for 30 min at reduced intensities and included this data within the training set.

After training, the rate parameters in the model shown in detail in Figure 7 were: D: [2.9952657 10.002745]; μpep: [1.006925 0.99327475]; δpep: [0.2064426 0.19140989]; Wpep: [0.90302687, −0.59036015]; δneuron: [1.2581608]. These parameters gave the range of peptide diffusion to be 3–10 cell bodies and their half-life to be ~5 min, which are biologically realistic based on previous measurements.47

To simulate responses after ablation, the ablation vector (A) was generated to mask out neurons corresponding to a spatially contiguous rectangular region of minimum aspect ratio with the desired fraction of neurons (Figure S7B). The location of the ablated region within the array was randomly chosen for each sample. For each ablation fraction, responses were generated from 200 different ablated regions and averaged to remove dependencies on ablation location.

The spatial covariance was calculated by averaging over all pairs of neurons at a given distance and across all time points on simulations lasting 11 min with a single 3 s pulse. This was sufficient to drive neural activity without dominating the signal with spatial correlation embedded within the input matrix Win. Ablated neurons were excluded from the calculation. For each ablation fraction, we simulated results from 200 ablated regions and averaged covariance at each distance.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Behavioral activity quantification

Planarians were segmented using binary thresholding. For each frame, the center of mass (COM) of the animal was determined and the perimeter pixel locations were identified and interpolated to give 100 evenly spaced anchor points. Radial distances were calculated as the distance from the COM to each of these points. The radial measurement vector was L1 normalized and aligned such that the maximum radial distance is at position 0. We then reduced the dimensionality of the radial measurements using principal component analysis (PCA) and kept the first 10 PCs (97% explained variance) for further analysis.87 PCs calculated using data from intact animals responding to 5-s UV stimulation were used to analyze all datasets. These PCs correspond to “eigen-shapes” of the animal body during movement, e.g., elongation (PC1), turning (PC2), and scrunching (PC3).87 For PCs that are symmetric, we took the absolute value of the component to prevent unnecessary separation of similar behaviors.

Because stationary and moving animals occupied overlapping shape space, incorporating temporal information into the feature vector significantly improved our ability to quantify behavioral activity. To do so, we convolved the PCA-transformed shape measurement timeseries data with Haar wavelet filters88 at scales corresponding to 2.5, 7.5, and 15 s to obtain the rate of shape change for each PC at each time point. This also served to filter out noise from the segmentation steps. Finally, we fit a one-dimensional stochastic linear dynamic system (LDS) model to the wavelet-filtered data using the state space models (SSM) Python package. This model is a continuous analog of the discrete HMM and infers a single latent scalar variable (activity) at each time point which maximizes the likelihood of the observations.

Besides activity, we also fit an HMM model to the wavelet-filtered shape component data at the scale of 7.5 s. The model was fit on data collected from intact animals under 30-s UV stimuli using the hmmlearn Python package. HMM state sequences were inferred from data using the viterbi algorithm implemented within the same package. Annotation of the HMM states was determined based on the fit Gaussian emission distributions of each of the states (Figure S2A). Movements with broad distributions of high amplitude shape changes were labeled as roaming; movements with moderate amplitude elongation were labeled twitching; movements with little elongation change but deflections in turning were labeled nodding; movements with little change in both shape and turning were labeled stationary. Because states showed symmetric pairs in their emission distributions and the symmetric states had identical responses across all experimental conditions, we focused our analysis on one state from each set.

To validate that the HMM model captured distinct behavioral states, we measured the magnitude of change in the turning PC over time after entering a specific state. Using data collected from intact animals under 30-s UV stimuli, we found that the differences between states were statistically significant (p < 0.01) during the first 15 s, despite potential state transitions occurring within this period (Figure S2D). The same model was used to extract states from data collected under various conditions, and the probability of each state at each time point post-stimulation was quantified by the fraction of trials in which the animals were in a specific state. We observed that these Markov states exhibited distinct trends during regeneration and following RNAi treatments, suggesting that these states are separately regulated.

Statistical analysis

Planarians displayed an asymmetric multimodal distribution of activities at any given time point. Therefore, we used non-parametric bootstrap statistics89 to test for significant differences between population averages. When comparing time trace data, each bootstrap sample was performed by choosing n responses with replacement from the collected data, where n is the number of responses within the given dataset (Table S1). This created new samples matching the size of the experimental dataset. The median value from these samples at each time point was recorded. This procedure was repeated >1,000 times to form a sampling distribution of the median population activity at each time point from 5 min before to 30 min after the stimulus. All time traces plotted are the mean estimate of median activity with 99% confidence intervals (CIs).

To test for significant differences between two conditions (e.g., RNAi and control), paired sets of samples were taken from each dataset and the difference in median activity between each sample was recorded. This procedure was repeated to form a sampling distribution of the difference in median activities at each time point. Significance at a time point was declared if zero fell outside the 99% CI, i.e., p < 0.01.

We also used two summary statistics. First, total response was quantified as the average median population activity during 10 min post-stimulation. This interval was sufficiently long to capture the dynamics of long-lasting responses while maintaining sensitivity to short pulses. We also defined half-life as the time for median activity intensity to fall to half its peak value. In cases where activity showed less than a 2-fold variation in the trial, we set half-life as 1 s. To compare these values between conditions, we used non-parametric bootstrap estimation. We calculated the measurement on 1,000 paired sample sets from control and experimental conditions and recorded the difference. Significance was declared if zero difference fell outside the 95% CI, i.e., p < 0.05.

Sensitization in the short-term memory experiments was calculated as the differences in the total responses to the second pulse and to a single pulse with no prior stimulation, additionally subtracting the remaining response of a single pulse above the baseline after the delay time between pulses.

To quantify the contributions of model parameters, we performed regression using a Gaussian linear model and the “statsmodels” Python package.82 The design matrix was constructed by extracting the parameters of interest from each trained models and standardizing their mean and variance using the “scikit-learn” Python package.83 These were used to calculate regression coefficients between each parameter and maximum excess response, defined as the difference between the maximum ablated total activity in response to 30-s UV stimulation and intact response for each model.
