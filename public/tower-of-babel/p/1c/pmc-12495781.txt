Development and validation of the healthcare access barrier scale (HABS)
Authors: Min Hu, Yusheng Jia, Xiaoyu Wang, Jinsui Zhang, Danyang Wei, Wen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeasuring healthcare access barriers is crucial for improving service equity. However, existing tools often lack a holistic, process-integrated approach. This study addresses this gap by developing and validating the Healthcare Access Barriers Scale (HABS), a new instrument that holistically assesses barriers before, during, and after medical visits.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 2,009 Shanghai residents. HABS was developed based on Penchansky and Thomas’s Theory of Access and Saurman’s modification. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses (EFA, CFA) were used to assess its structure. Reliability and validity were tested using Cronbach’s alpha and model fit indices.ResultsEFA and CFA supported a six-factor structure of the HABS, corresponding to six key dimensions of access barriers: unawareness, unavailability, inaccessibility, inadaptability, unaffordability, and unacceptability. The HABS demonstrated strong reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.974) and a good fit for the six-factor model (CFA: Comparative Fit Index [CFI] = 0.937, Tucker-Lewis Index [TLI] = 0.930, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation [RMSEA] = 0.069). The two most pronounced barriers were financial constraints (mean of unaffordability dimension = 3.26) and lack of awareness (unawareness = 3.19), highlighting cost burdens and health literacy gaps in this population.ConclusionsThe HABS is a valid and reliable instrument for measuring perceived healthcare access barriers in urban populations, offering a more holistic, process-integrated assessment by capturing obstacles across the full continuum of care – before, during, and after healthcare visits. This unified scale can facilitate assessment of systematic barriers, comparisons across settings, and identification of target areas for intervention. Introduction

Background

Healthcare access continues to be a critical global issue, influencing both healthcare experiences and health outcomes. While extensive research has explored the barriers to accessing healthcare, much of it has relied on qualitative methods, resulting in a lack of standardized theoretical frameworks and validated quantitative tools. Without reliable and validated instruments, it remains difficult to assess healthcare access barriers systematically, identify population needs, and design targeted interventions. Addressing this gap, the present study develops and validates the HABS, a multidimensional instrument designed to measure perceived barriers to healthcare access. Rooted in Penchansky and Thomas’s theory of access [1], this study also applies the HABS in a population-based survey of residents in Shanghai, China, to evaluate its psychometric properties and identify key determinants influencing healthcare access barriers.

Several instruments have been developed in recent years to assess healthcare access and quality, yet gaps remain in capturing the full complexity of access barriers. The Access to Health Care instrument developed by Zandam et al. [2] measures access from the user’s perspective, focusing on approachability, availability, accessibility, affordability, acceptability, and accommodation. Similarly, Wijesinghe et al. [3] proposed a scale to assess access to medical care, highlighting availability, affordability, and acceptability as central dimensions. However, these measures tend to treat barriers as static factors and may overlook the continuum of barriers encountered at different stages of the care-seeking process. In practice, obstacles arise before reaching a provider (e.g., lack of awareness of services, fear of costs), during the medical visit (e.g., long wait times, communication difficulties), and after the visit (e.g., difficulty obtaining follow-up care or reimbursement) [4, 5]. Most prior studies do not explicitly distinguish when these barriers occur, making it challenging to address the process of obtaining care holistically. The present study, instead, captures barriers at each stage of the care-seeking process, adopting a process-oriented perspective that reflects the dynamic nature of access barriers.

Previous studies have examined healthcare access in specific contexts, such as mobile health and patient-centered care. Akter et al. [6] developed an instrument to measure perceived service quality in mobile health, emphasizing the hierarchical and multidimensional nature of service quality in technology-driven healthcare settings. Similarly, Johnson et al. [7] introduced the Health Care Empowerment Inventory to assess patient engagement, highlighting dimensions such as informed decision-making, collaboration, and tolerance of uncertainty. Recent efforts to measure primary healthcare service quality have focused on dimensions such as interaction, efficiency, timeliness, accuracy, consultation, and accessibility [8]. While these models offer valuable insights, they primarily address service quality and patient participation rather than the full spectrum of structural and systemic barriers to care. The need for a comprehensive, multidimensional, and psychometrically sound scale remains particularly pressing in contexts like China, where healthcare access is shaped by institutional structures, financial constraints, and sociocultural factors.

Given these gaps, the HABS is designed to provide a comprehensive assessment of healthcare access barriers across the full continuum of care, before, during, and after healthcare visits, incorporating six key dimensions: unawareness, unavailability, inaccessibility, inadaptability, unaffordability, and unacceptability. By integrating both theoretical and empirical insights, this study aims to contribute to the standardization of healthcare access measurement, offering a validated tool that can be used for policy development, healthcare planning, and targeted interventions.

This study’s contribution is twofold. First, we introduce a theoretically grounded framework for conceptualizing healthcare access barriers, drawing on established access theory and extending it to include an often-overlooked dimension (awareness). Second, we present the development and validation of the HABS as a comprehensive, multidimensional measure of access barriers. By incorporating six key dimensions of barriers and situating them in the care continuum, the HABS aims to facilitate better assessment of access issues and support evidence-based strategies to improve healthcare accessibility. Ultimately, this research seeks to advance the standardization of healthcare access measurement, offering a validated tool that policymakers, healthcare planners, and researchers can use to identify barrier patterns and inform interventions for more equitable health services.

Concept and definition

The theoretical foundation of the instrument is rooted in the access model originally articulated by Penchansky and Thomas [1], which defines access as the degree of fit between the healthcare system and the patient. In this model, better fit equates to better access. Penchansky and Thomas grouped the characteristics of health services and patient needs into five key dimensions: availability, accessibility, accommodation, affordability, and acceptability. Building on this foundational theory, Saurman [9] proposed an important expansion of the access model by adding a sixth dimension: awareness. Taken together, these six dimensions provide a comprehensive conceptualization of healthcare access. Access to healthcare is not solely determined by the physical availability of services but is also influenced by individual, institutional, and systemic factors that create obstacles to timely and appropriate care [10]. Access can be seen as a multifaceted construct: it is achieved only when services are adequately available and reachable; organized in a way that patients can use (adaptable to their needs); priced affordably relative to patients’ resources; acceptable in terms of cultural and personal preferences; and when patients are aware of both their health needs and the services that exist to meet those needs [9]. A detailed elaboration of previous theories can be found in the Appendix (Supplementary Note 1).

In the context of our scale, drawing from existing healthcare access models [1, 4, 9], we frame each dimension in terms of the barrier experienced when that aspect of access is misaligned or lacking. Thus, the HABS measures the perceived lack or mismatch in each domain between the healthcare system’s resources and patients’ needs – effectively capturing the “negative” of the six A’s (for clarity in wording, each dimension is labeled with an “un-” or “in-” prefix to denote the barrier). The six core dimensions of the HABS were identified: unawareness, unavailability, inaccessibility, inadaptability, unaffordability, and unacceptability. Below, we define each of these dimensions and illustrate how they manifest as barriers to care.

Unawareness refers to patients’ limited knowledge about their health status and healthcare resources. It covers low health literacy, poor awareness of when to seek care, and lack of understanding of insurance benefits, which can result in delayed care-seeking behavior or inappropriate healthcare utilization [11]. It also encompasses poor health literacy, which has been linked to worse health outcomes and increased hospitalizations [12].

Unavailability reflects a shortfall in the supply of healthcare services relative to the population’s needs. This barrier arises when medical facilities, personnel, or critical resources are insufficient or not readily accessible to meet demand. Previous studies have highlighted that insufficient hospital capacity, provider shortages, and long appointment wait times contribute significantly to unmet healthcare needs [13].

Inaccessibility refers to the physical or logistical challenges that patients encounter when trying to reach healthcare services. Even if services exist (availability) and patients know about them (awareness), they might still face difficulties in getting to the point of care. Geographic distance, lack of transportation, and urban-rural disparities are well-documented barriers that disproportionately affect marginalized populations [14].

Inadaptability addresses inefficiencies and inflexibilities in how healthcare services are organized and delivered. This barrier emerges when health systems do not or cannot adapt to patients’ circumstances, including rigid scheduling systems, inconvenient operating hours, complex administrative procedures, and a lack of patient-centered care models [15]. Healthcare systems that fail to accommodate patients’ work schedules, linguistic needs, or digital access can create significant barriers to effective care utilization [16].

Unaffordability captures the financial constraints as a key determinant of healthcare access. This dimension considers whether the cost of care (including direct fees, co-payments, medications, and indirect costs like transportation or lost wages) exceeds the patient’s ability or willingness to pay. High out-of-pocket costs, inadequate insurance coverage, and medical debt are significant contributors to healthcare inequities [17, 18]. Studies have consistently shown that patients with lower socioeconomic status are more likely to delay or forgo medical care due to cost concerns [19].

Unacceptability reflects mismatches between patients’ expectations and healthcare providers’ and institutions’ characteristics or behaviors, including cultural, linguistic, and interpersonal barriers. Even if a service is available, accessible, adaptable, and affordable, patients might hesitate to use it if they perceive it to be unsatisfactory or alienating. This includes experiences or fears of discrimination, lack of provider empathy, and mistrust of medical institutions, which have been linked to reduced healthcare utilization, particularly among racial and ethnic minorities [20, 21]. Patients’ perceptions of provider competence and fairness also influence their willingness to seek care (Malat, 2001).

By conceptualizing healthcare access in these six dimensions, we use a comprehensive framework for understanding healthcare access barriers from a demand-side perspective. By integrating insights from previous healthcare access models and empirical findings, the HABS aims to offer a standardized and validated tool for measuring the multifaceted nature of healthcare barriers.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to develop and validate the HABS and to analyze healthcare access barriers among residents in China. Data collection was conducted in 2022 using a structured questionnaire administered online to a sample of Shanghai residents. The study followed a rigorous multi-step framework for measurement development, including theoretical conceptualization, item generation, and psychometric testing. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board at Fudan University prior to participant recruitment. All participants provided informed consent before taking part in the study. Participation was voluntary, and respondents had the right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Participants

Participants were recruited using a multistage stratified sampling method to ensure a sample representative of Shanghai’s residential population. In Stage 1, four municipal districts (Xuhui, Putuo, Jiading, and Songjiang) were selected using stratified equidistant sampling based on economic development levels, guided by the 2021 Shanghai Statistical Yearbook. In Stage 2, each district’s streets/towns were grouped by population size into three strata, from which one street per stratum was randomly selected, yielding three representative streets per district. In Stage 3, 2–3 larger communities (with more than 1,000 residents) were randomly selected from each street. Within these communities, residents was sampled proportionally based on the street’s share of the district population and age structure of Shanghai. This approach yielded approximately 330–420 participants per district, targeting a total sample size of 1,440–1,680.

Eligible individuals were permanent residents aged 18 to 89 years who had lived in Shanghai for at least six months. Additional inclusion criteria required participants to have no cognitive or sensory impairments that would interfere with their ability to complete the survey.

The final sample consisted of 2,009 respondents, yielding a high response rate of 93.57%. Collected demographic information included age, gender, education level, employment status, health insurance status, and whether the individual was a local Shanghai resident or a migrant resident.

Measures

The HABS was developed as the primary measure of perceived barriers to healthcare access. The HABS encompasses six dimensions corresponding to the theoretical framework discussed above, and each dimension was theoretically defined based on established access concepts from Penchansky and Thomas’s model and Saurman’s modification.

We generated an initial pool of 32 candidate items to reflect these six domains of access barriers. Item generation was informed by an extensive literature review of healthcare access issues, findings from semi-structured interviews with patients about their care-seeking difficulties, and consultations with healthcare experts to ensure content validity. Items were iteratively refined through two rounds of expert review and cognitive testing. Of the original 32 items developed for the instrument, one item was excluded after exploratory factor analysis due to weak or cross-loading patterns, resulting in a final scale of 31 items. A detailed description of the development process can be found in the Appendix (Supplementary Note 2), and the original factor loadings for the 32 items are presented in Appendix Table 4.

Each item is a statement describing a potential barrier (e.g., “Before the visit, I usually feel that the medical cost is too expensive.” or “I usually don’t know enough about my health to decide if I should go to the doctor”), and items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores on an item indicate a greater perceived barrier in that aspect of access. Several items explicitly reference the timing of the barrier (before, during, or after the visit) to capture the process-oriented nature of access barriers (for example, an item in the unaffordability domain asks about financial burden after insurance reimbursement, representing a post-visit barrier).

In addition to the HABS items, the survey collected information on participants’ health status and healthcare utilization (e.g., number of doctor visits and hospitalizations in the past year), which can be used to explore known-groups validity and to describe the context of barriers.

Data analysis

We conducted a psychometric evaluation of the HABS. First, we assessed reliability and validity. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for the overall scale and each subscale (dimension). We also computed split-half reliability to examine the stability and consistency of the scale’s results.

Next, we examined the factor structure of the HABS using both EFA and CFA. EFA was conducted on a randomly selected half of the sample to identify the underlying dimensional structure, using principal axis factoring with Promax (oblique) rotation. We anticipated around six factors based on the theoretical model, but the data-driven EFA results were used to confirm this. Any items that did not load well on their intended factor or that cross-loaded heavily on multiple factors were considered for removal or revision.

On the second half of the sample, CFA was performed to validate the factor structure suggested by the EFA and theory. We specified a six-factor model corresponding to the six hypothesized barrier dimensions, allowing the factors to correlate. Model fit was evaluated with standard fit indices: CFI, TLI, RMSEA (with 90% confidence interval), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). We also tested an alternative one-factor model (all items loading on a single general “barrier” factor) to verify that the multidimensional model provided a significantly better fit to the data. Chi-square difference testing and comparison of fit indices were used to contrast the models.

All quantitative analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.4.2). For factor analyses, we used the psych package for EFA and the lavaan package for CFA [22, 23]. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the sample

The study sample included 2,009 participants, with a nearly equal distribution of males (49.23%) and females (50.77%). The majority (80.39%) were long-term Shanghai residents, defined by household registration (Hukou), and 54.75% were covered by employee health insurance, while 33.7% were enrolled in the resident insurance program (URBMI), and 2.44% were uninsured. The average age of participants was 43.1 years, and most respondents reported moderate healthcare utilization, with an annual mean of 2.95 outpatient visits and 0.09 inpatient admissions.

Description of the HABS items

The overall mean HABS score was 3.03, indicating moderate healthcare access barriers among Shanghai residents. Among the six dimensions, unaffordability emerged as the most significant barrier, with the highest mean score of 3.26 (SD = 0.84). This was followed by unawareness (3.19, SD = 0.79), inadaptability (3.13, SD = 0.81), unavailability (2.89, SD = 0.76), unacceptability (2.87, SD = 0.74), and inaccessibility (2.84, SD = 0.77).

Table 3 presents a detailed breakdown of the 31-item HABS, including mean scores and standard deviations for each item. Notably, items related to affordability (e.g., “Before the visit, I usually feel that the medical cost is too expensive”, mean = 3.508, SD = 1.258) had the highest individual item scores, further reinforcing the prominence of financial barriers. Items reflecting uncertainty about healthcare options (e.g., “I usually do not understand the health insurance policies”, mean = 3.384, SD = 1.280) also scored high, emphasizing the role of limited health literacy in restricting access. Additionally, a structural barrier item under the Inadaptability dimension, i.e., “I usually have to wait a long time (for registration, tests, medication dispensing, etc.) to see a doctor”, had a similarly high mean score (mean = 3.547, SD = 1.283), highlighting inefficiencies in healthcare delivery. In contrast, items related to provider attitudes and trust (e.g., “I usually feel that my doctor treats me badly”, mean = 2.807, SD = 1.183) had lower scores, indicating that perceived discrimination or provider distrust may not be as significant a barrier as financial and informational constraints. These findings highlight that financial barriers, lack of awareness, and structural inefficiencies in healthcare delivery are the most significant access challenges for Shanghai residents.

Reliability of the HABS

The correlation between individual HABS items is presented in Appendix Table 1, demonstrating the internal consistency of the scale. The Cronbach’s alpha for the full scale was 0.974, indicating that the items collectively have very high interrelatedness. Each of the six subscales (dimensions) also showed strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values all above the 0.8 threshold (specific subscale alphas ranged from approximately 0.85 to 0.93). Additionally, the split-half reliability was 0.915, further confirming the scale’s stability and internal coherence. These reliability coefficients suggest that the HABS provides a consistent and reliable measure of healthcare access barriers, with strong inter-item correlations supporting the construct validity of the instrument. Together, these reliability metrics suggest that the HABS provides a stable and internally coherent measure of healthcare access barriers. Respondents’ answers are not erratic or random; rather, there is a clear pattern that the scale is capturing reliably.

Factor structure of the HABS

We conducted EFA and CFA to validate the factor structure of the HABS. Following best practices [24], we randomly split the sample into two halves and conducted EFA in one half and CFA in the other half to determine the stability of the structure of the HABS. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was high (above 0.9), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. The scree plot from the EFA showed a noticeable inflection after the sixth factor, and parallel analysis likewise suggested six factors as optimal. We initially extracted factors without fixing the number and found six factors with eigenvalues > 1 before a sharp drop-off, reinforcing a six-factor solution (Fig. 1).

Scree plot for the HABS 31 items

Using principal axis factoring with Promax rotation - an oblique rotation strategy that allows factors to correlate in the first sample half, the factor loadings indicated that each item loaded highly on one factor (generally corresponding to its intended dimension) and showed minimal cross-loading on others, strongly suggesting a 6-factor structure. Table 4 presents the rotated factor loadings for each item. For example, all unawareness items had their highest loadings on one factor (Factor 1, for example), all unavailability items on another, and so forth, effectively replicating the six theorized groupings. The inter-factor correlations ranged from 0.62 to 0.75, indicating moderate to strong associations among the six HABS dimensions (Table 5). This presence of moderate to strong correlations supports the use of an oblique rotation (like Promax) in factor analysis, which allows factors to correlate. This also suggests that while the factors are distinct, they are not independent and share some common variance, which is reasonable given that all are aspects of access barriers and thus not entirely independent.

In addition, when a seventh factor was forcibly extracted, it did not yield a meaningful factor as no items loaded strongly on a distinct seventh factor (Appendix Table 2 and Appendix Table 3). This evidence strongly pointed to a six-factor structure, aligning with our theoretical expectation.

We ran CFA in the second sample half to verify the six-factor model. As shown in Table 6, the six-factor model testing 31 items forming six subscales that can be combined into a single higher-order factor (supporting the occasional use of a total score) offered a good fit to the data ((419) = 2423.540, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.937, TLI = 0.930, SRMR = 0.034, RMSEA = 0.069, 90% CI: 0.066–0.072). Given the large sample size, a significant chi-square was expected, so we rely more on the CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR. Referring to previous practice [25–27], a CFI in the mid-0.90s and RMSEA around 0.07 suggest that the hypothesized model reproduces the covariance structure of the data quite well, with only minor discrepancies. These values are consistent with the idea that while the dimensions are related (someone facing one barrier often faces others), they are not redundant, and each captures a unique aspect of access. In addition to the theorized six-factor structure, we estimated and compared alternative CFA models specifying two- through five-factor solutions; model-fit statistics for all alternatives are presented in Appendix Table 5, where the six-factor model clearly showed the superior overall fit.

To obtain the most stable parameter estimates and to confirm generalizability, we refit the six-factor model in the full sample (Table 6). Fit indices were excellent (χ² (419) = 4006.28, p < 0.001; CFI = 0.942, TLI = 0.936; SRMR = 0.032; RMSEA = 0.065, 90% CI 0.063–0.067). We also tested a one-factor model for comparison, in which all 31 items loaded onto a single latent factor. This simpler one-factor model yielded a poor fit in both the half-sample (CFI = 0.683, RMSEA = 0.152) and the full sample (CFI = 0.696, RMSEA = 0.147).

The CFA results cross-replicated the EFA findings in the second sample half and validate a coherent six-dimension structure of the HABS. Strong model fit indices and substantial item loadings indicate that each dimension (i.e., unawareness, unavailability, inaccessibility, inadaptability, unaffordability, and unacceptability) captures a distinct aspect of healthcare access barriers while contributing to an overarching barrier construct. Therefore, these results reaffirm the six-dimensional structure of the HABS and support the use of both the sub-scale scores and an overall barrier score in subsequent analyses.

Discussion

This study developed and validated the Healthcare Access Barriers Scale, a comprehensive tool for assessing access barriers in China. The HABS addresses a significant gap in health services research by providing an instrument that integrates multiple dimensions of access and considers the full patient care-seeking continuum. The six-factor structure identified through EFA and confirmed via CFA aligns closely with existing theoretical models of healthcare access (Penchansky and Thomas framework and Saurman’s modification), reinforcing the validity and applicability of the HABS as theory-driven measure. Notably, our findings demonstrated that financial constraints (unaffordability) and lack of knowledge or information (unawareness) were the most significant barriers reported by respondents. This is consistent with prior research on healthcare inequities in China and other middle-income settings, where out-of-pocket costs and health literacy are often key determinants of whether individuals obtain needed care [28, 29]. By contrast, barriers related to physical accessibility and acceptability, while present, were less pronounced on average in this well-resourced urban setting [30, 31].

The prominence of financial barriers in our results underscores the ongoing need for policy interventions to reduce the cost burden of healthcare. The unaffordability dimension had the highest mean score (3.26 on a 5-point scale), indicating that many participants feel healthcare is too expensive. This finding aligns with China’s healthcare context, where despite insurance expansions, patients can still face substantial out-of-pocket expenses, particularly for specialized care or medications [17, 32]. It echoes studies that show high medical costs and inadequate insurance coverage lead to delayed care or financial strain on households [33, 34]. Notably, these financial challenges often emerge before care is even sought, and the HABS framework uniquely captures it as barrier before medical visits (i.e., item unaffordability 1, “Before the visit, I usually feel that the medical cost is too expensive.”). Addressing such barriers may require policy interventions such as broadening insurance benefit packages, implementing caps on out-of-pocket spending, and providing targeted medical financial assistance to low-income families [35].

Similarly, the high perceived barrier of unawareness (mean 3.19) points to gaps in health literacy, patient education, and uncertainty about how to navigate the health system. Many respondents expressed difficulty understanding insurance policies or knowing where to seek care, suggesting that better public health education and communication are needed. Interventions might include community health workshops, patient navigators, or improved dissemination of information about how to seek care and utilize insurance [36]. Enhancing awareness could empower patients to seek care promptly and appropriately, thereby improving health outcomes.

Structural barriers related to the healthcare delivery process were also evident. The inadaptability dimension (mean 3.13) suggests that inefficiencies like inconvenient appointment systems or rigid clinic hours are affecting patients’ ability to obtain care. This finding is consistent with qualitative reports of patients frustrated by early morning queues, short registration windows, or complicated referral processes in many hospitals [37, 38]. Healthcare administrators and providers should consider reforms to make services more patient-centered: for instance, introducing online appointment scheduling to reduce waiting, extending clinic hours into evenings or weekends, simplifying paperwork, and adopting one-stop services that guide patients through different departments. The unavailability score (2.89), while lower, still highlights concerns about capacity – even in Shanghai, issues like overcrowding in top-tier hospitals and uneven distribution of resources can create feelings of scarcity. Efforts to increase staffing, open new facilities in underserved districts, or improve primary care referral systems could mitigate these availability issues [39, 40].

An important strength of this study is its rigorous psychometric validation process. By splitting the sample for EFA and CFA, we ensured that the factor structure was not a result of overfitting to one dataset but rather generalizable and stable. The large sample size (over 2,000 respondents) and the representativeness of the sample to a major urban population lend confidence that the HABS can be applicable to similar settings. The internal consistency of the scale is exceptionally high, which is often the case for a broad-ranging survey instrument, but we have shown that it does not simply reflect redundancy – each subscale captures a distinct facet of access barriers. Another strength is the content validity ensured by our item development process: incorporating qualitative input and expert reviews means the scale items are grounded in real-world patient experiences and theoretical constructs. The HABS is also comprehensive yet concise – with 31 items, it is feasible to implement in large surveys or health system assessments, and it covers more dimensions than most prior instruments.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the study means we captured a snapshot of perceived barriers at one point in time. We cannot infer causality or track changes in barriers as health policies or personal circumstances evolve. Second, our study was conducted in Shanghai, a relatively affluent, urban setting with a specific healthcare infrastructure. The barrier profile observed in this study may not generalize to less-resourced or non-urban populations, where the nature and intensity of barriers could differ (for example, unavailability of services and inaccessibility due to distance might stand out in rural areas). Notably, even within this urban sample we observed heterogeneous barriers across subgroups, suggesting that different segments of the population face different primary obstacles. We therefore recommend that the HABS be validated in other contexts, including rural communities and different cultural settings, to ensure its reliability and validity across diverse populations and to capture context-specific barriers. Third, while we included a broad age range, our sample only included adults; barriers faced by children or adolescents (as perceived by parents) were beyond our scope and might require adaptation of some items. Additionally, being a self-reported measure, the HABS is subject to respondents’ perceptions and recall. It is possible that some might overestimate or underestimate barriers due to personal attitudes (e.g., individuals with critical conditions might rate every item higher). We tried to mitigate this by phrasing items as neutral statements of experience, but response bias remains a consideration.

Despite these limitations, the HABS offers a valuable new tool for research and practice. For researchers, the scale can be used to quantify access barriers in different populations, to evaluate interventions (e.g., does a new policy reduce barrier scores in a community?), or to correlate barrier levels with health outcomes. For healthcare administrators and policymakers, HABS results can highlight which areas to prioritize. In our study, for example, the clear signal on financial and informational barriers suggests that improving insurance coverage and health education should be high on the agenda for improving access equity. Additionally, because HABS is multidimensional, it allows a more nuanced evaluation of specific programs. Such future research will extend the tool’s applicability beyond our current setting and reinforce its relevance for addressing healthcare access issues in varied environments, ultimately supporting global health equity efforts.

Finally, the study contributes to the theoretical discourse on healthcare access. Our successful integration of the awareness dimension empirically supports Saurman’s argument that awareness is indeed a distinct and crucial component of access. In settings like ours, awareness-related items were among the top barrier items, underscoring that any model of access (and efforts to improve access) must consider patients’ knowledge and informational needs. Likewise, the interplay we observed among barriers (with moderate inter-factor correlations) suggests that interventions may need to tackle multiple barriers simultaneously; addressing one barrier in isolation could be insufficient if other barriers remain high.

Conclusion

The HABS is a validated, reliable instrument for measuring healthcare access barriers from the patient’s perspective. The HABS has the potential to serve as a valuable instrument for public health officials and researchers in other settings, facilitating the diagnosis of access issues and the monitoring of improvements over time. By identifying six key dimensions of access barriers, the HABS provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the complex, systemic, and individual challenges that affect whether people obtain needed healthcare. Unlike previous instruments that might focus on one aspect of access, the HABS covers awareness, availability, accessibility, adaptability, affordability, and acceptability in one unified tool. It also uniquely incorporates the full continuum of care by assessing barriers encountered before, during, and after healthcare visits, reflecting a more process-oriented view of access. The findings from our study underscore the need for multi-pronged policy interventions targeting financial affordability, health literacy (awareness), and healthcare service adaptability to enhance equitable access to care. For instance, policy efforts should aim to reduce out-of-pocket costs and expand insurance coverage (to alleviate unaffordability), invest in public education and navigation support (to reduce unawareness), and improve healthcare delivery processes (to reduce inadaptability and inaccessibility).
