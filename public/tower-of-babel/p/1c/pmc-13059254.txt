Healthcare workers’ perspectives on burnout complaints: exploring underlying dynamics using a complex systems approach
Authors: Ruben Heuven, Saskia van den Berg, Karin Proper, Sandra van Oostrom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare workers (HCWs) report burnout complaints more often than the general workforce, leading to reduced quality of life and delivered care, high absenteeism and associated costs. Addressing the complex issue of burnout complaints requires a shift from linear thinking to a systems perspective focused on interaction and feedback. This study explores the dynamics behind burnout complaints by approaching it as a complex system, from the perspective of Dutch HCWs.MethodsThirteen HCWs from eight different professions participated in two Group Model Building sessions to develop a Causal Loop Diagram (CLD). Participants identified contributing factors related to living conditions, working conditions, and societal developments, and mapped their assumed relationships into causal feedback loops.ResultsThe CLD contains 24 factors, 55 assumed causal relationships, and 417 feedback loops. Within these loops, two reinforcing and two balancing key dynamics were identified. The perceived reinforcing dynamics illustrate how burnout complaints and staff shortages amplify each other, and how rising burnout impairs care quality, prompting patient dissatisfaction that further increases burnout complaints. The perceived balancing dynamics reflect how this cycle is partially mitigated by supportive leadership and rising societal awareness, which improves working conditions and employment terms.ConclusionsAccording to HCWs, these dynamics may help explain the rising trend in the prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs. Understanding these dynamics offers valuable insights for developing more effective strategies to shift the system towards more favorable outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14261-3.

Background

Burnout has been defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) in the latest edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) as a syndrome resulting from unmanaged chronic workplace stress [1]. Since most studies assess burnout via questionnaires, the term ‘burnout complaints’ is preferred to distinguish self-reported symptoms from a clinical diagnosis [2]. Exhaustion is considered the core complaint, leading to psychological withdrawal and cognitive and emotional impairment [2–4]. While these complaints are not exclusively due to workplace stress [2], notable variations exist across different work sectors. Data from the United States and the Netherlands demonstrate that healthcare workers (HCWs) report significantly higher rates of burnout complaints compared to the overall working population [5, 6].

The higher prevalence in HCWs can be largely attributed to the intensive and complex demands of healthcare work [7, 8]. High levels of burnout complaints are observed across different healthcare professions, and persist across different stages of healthcare workers’ careers [9, 10]. In the Netherlands, burnout complaints among HCWs have risen over the past decade (Fig. 1), prompting significant attention from researchers, policymakers, and the media.

Among HCWs, burnout complaints have been linked to reduced quality of life [11], decreased productivity [12], absenteeism [13–15] and staff turnover [16]. In the Netherlands, the healthcare sector consistently reports the highest rate of absenteeism compared to other sectors [6]. Stress-related complaints, including burnout, are the second most frequently reported reason for this absenteeism, following flu or the common cold [6]. These consequences of burnout complaints extend far beyond HCWs. Employers deal with significant costs, since stress-related sick leave is typically long, averaging 202 days [17]. Of all stress-related sick leave, burnout is responsible for the longest absence and the highest costs [17]. Among patients, HCW burnout complaints has been associated with decreased quality and safety of delivered care [18]. Combined with the anticipated increased pressure on the Dutch healthcare system, including staff shortages [19], this calls for urgent attention to prevention.

Most explanatory research on burnout complaints in HCWs examines socio-demographic, individual, and work-related determinants in isolation [20]. While insightful, it pays little attention to how these factors are interconnected in complex ways that give rise to non-linear and unpredictable outcomes [21]. Similarly, most intervention research on HCWs’ burnout complaints follow an individually focused, symptom-oriented model, prioritizing stress management over structural change [22]. Yet, their effectiveness appears highly dependent on contextual factors such as workload and work schedules [23]. These working conditions are, in turn, shaped by broader developments within the healthcare sector and society at large.

To avoid reductionist approaches focusing on single determinants and ‘magic bullet’ interventions [24], the prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs can better be conceptualized as an emergent phenomenon, i.e., arising from a complex interplay of determinants in HCWs’ living and working conditions, as well as broader healthcare sector and societal developments. Crucially, this interplay is shaped by feedback processes, known as the ‘dynamics’ of a system. Conceptual models rooted in systems thinking are well suited to capture these dynamics [25].

Although these models have been applied in previous burnout research [26–29], the dynamics that shape the prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs remain underexplored, particularly from the standpoint of HCWs themselves. This is a crucial omission, as HCWs contribute essential, situated knowledge that is unlikely to surface through external or expert-only analysis.

The present study therefore aims to explore HCWs’ perspectives on the dynamics that contribute to burnout complaints among Dutch HCWs.

Methods

Design

Group model building

Group Model Building (GMB) is a participatory approach that engages stakeholders in systems mapping [30]. It is based on the premise that individuals hold mental models—cognitive representations of the systems and problems they encounter—which consist of networks of cause-and-effect relationships [31]. GMB aims to make these networks explicit and, through structured interaction, arrive at a shared understanding of a complex problem.

Causal loop diagram

To visually present HCW’s mental models of the system, a Causal Loop Diagram (CLD) was developed during the GMB sessions. These diagrams consist of factors and assumed causal relationships between the factors. Relationships are characterized by a direction (from cause to effect) and a polarity (positive when changes in the cause produce changes in the same direction in the effect, and negative when the changes are in the opposite direction). CLDs allow these connections to form cyclic chains known as feedback loops, which either work to stabilize the system and drive it towards a particular state (balancing, labeled as “B”) or amplify change and lead to exponential growth (reinforcing, labeled as “R”) [21, 31]. It is in the interplay of these feedback loops, rather than the individual links or factors in the CLD, that the explanatory power for the system’s problematic behavior resides [31, 32]. CLDs therefore offer an endogenous explanation of the problem, meaning that it emerges from interactions within the system rather than from external inputs [21].

Participants

The selection process began by identifying various healthcare professions and settings to ensure a diverse representation of Dutch HCWs. Subsequently, Dutch-speaking HCWs were recruited through the researchers’ network (convenience sampling) and asked to involve additional HCWs from their networks whose profession (e.g., midwife) or work setting (e.g., hospital) was not yet represented in the group. (snowball sampling).

This approach resulted in a group of 13 HCWs: a general practitioner (MD), three nursing assistants, a physiotherapist, a public health nurse, a gastroenterologist (MD) resident, two medical assistants, a midwife, an oncology nurse, a psychologist, and a nurse-reintegration advisor. Of these participants, five were employed in primary care and eight worked in secondary care. Although not a requirement for participation in the sessions, many of the HCWs had personal or observed experiences with burnout complaints, and four HCWs had a history of sick leave related to clinical burnout.

All HCWs provided written informed consent. The Centre for Clinical Expertise of the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment determined that this study was not subject to the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). As a result, formal ethical approval was not required. The HCWs were compensated with a gift voucher for their participation.

Procedure

The modeling process started from a dynamic problem framing, that describes how the problem changes over time [21]. In this study, this was defined as the prevalence of HCWs reporting burnout complaints—specifically emotional exhaustion, the core dimension of burnout [2, 4] —multiple times a month to daily over the past decade (Fig. 1).

Trends in this measure are a relevant policy indicator, as they are linked to important factors such as care quality [18] and appear to predict long-term absenteeism more strongly than depressive symptoms [13]. Consequently, building the CLD started with ‘Prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs’ as the central factor.

The subsequent steps, identifying key variables and mapping the system [21], were carried out together with the HCWs during two three-hour workshops to identify a set of feedback loops theoretically capable of explaining this trend. To structure and guide the sessions, small-group exercises (known as ‘scripts’) from Scriptapedia were used, available at https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Scriptapedia (for a summary of the procedure, see additional file 1). The workshops were facilitated by four researchers who fulfilled various roles, including facilitator, modeler, wall builder and note taker (script ‘roles in GMB’). To capture the reasoning behind every part of the model, sessions were audio-recorded, and written notes and photos were collected during the exercises. Any uncertainties during the workshops were addressed through facilitated group discussion until a shared interpretation was reached.

The first session, held in person, began with an introduction to the problem, including a metaphor about complex systems (script ‘elephant’). Participants then selected and prioritized factors (scripts: ‘variable elicitation’ & ‘dots’), were introduced to the concept of feedback (script ‘chicken and eggs’), and constructed initial CLDs in two small groups (script: ‘causal mapping in small groups’). Both groups constructed these initial CLDs using the same ten highest-prioritized factors, which ensured comparability between the two structures. To focus on determinants beyond the individual level, participants were able to select factors belonging to living conditions, working conditions, and societal developments, in line with the outer layers of the Dahlgren-Whitehead model of health determinants [33]. To avoid priming participants, no initial model structure was provided [30].

After the first session, the researchers consolidated the two group CLDs into a single model using Vensim PLE (version 10.2.1). Since both groups had constructed CLDs using the same set of ten prioritized factors, all links that appeared in either group’s CLD were initially retained in the combined model. The research team then reviewed the model for consistency in factor naming (relative to definitions), justification of assumed causal links, and potential omissions. Links that had been proposed by only one group, or whose justification was unclear based on the session notes, were flagged and communicated to the participants in a summary report one week prior to the second session, with a request to review it in advance.

The same 13 HCWs attended the second session, which was held online (via MS Teams and Mural, an online whiteboard tool, available at: https://www.mural.co/)) to reduce the burden of participation. The workshop began with a joint review of the combined model. All researcher-made revisions were discussed and validated by the participants. It was noted that only reinforcing feedback loops had been identified thus far. Participants were asked to reflect on possible balancing loops that could stabilize the system (script: ‘creating CLD from connection circles’). The session concluded by allowing participants to add factors from (i) the previously unprioritized factors (elicited during the small-group ‘variable elicitation’ and ‘dot voting’ exercises), and (ii) additional variables that had emerged during the two sessions and were noted by the facilitators. Participants could then vote on which of these factors they considered most relevant to include in the model.

The research team checked the CLD for consistency, resolved discrepancies, and invited participants’ feedback through a summary report. To enhance visual clarity, the final CLD was transferred to the software Kumu.

Analysis

All feedback loops affecting the central factor were extracted using automated loop detection in Vensim and ordered by length (in terms of number of variables). Loops were clustered into reinforcing and balancing types and subsequently grouped into key dynamics when they shared a causal pathway and supported a coherent causal narrative. Longer loops did not introduce additional key dynamics but extended those already identified; this saturation effect was reached at six variables per loop. As the loops reflect HCWs’ perspectives, participants were approached after the sessions and asked to enrich specific loops with concrete experiences.

Results

The CLD comprises 24 factors (for definitions: see Table 1) and 55 assumed causal relationships, that reflect unanimous agreement among HCWs (Fig. 2). The automated loop detection in Vensim identified 417 unique feedback loops that include the central factor. The process of identifying key dynamics resulted in two reinforcing dynamics (based on 22 reinforcing loops; Fig. 3) and two balancing dynamics (based on 22 balancing loops; Fig. 4).

Staffing levels under pressure

Starting from the increasing trend in the prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs over the past decade (Fig. 1), most reinforcing feedback loops (R1 - R21, Fig. 3) are centered around a subsequent decrease in staffing levels and a corresponding increase in workload magnitude. At the level of working conditions, this can be explained by increased absenteeism. At the level of societal developments, it operates through the declining attractiveness of the healthcare sector—both for current and potential new HCWs. It was perceived that growing workload and increasing burnout complaints have driven many HCWs to leave the sector. As these trends are widely covered in the media, healthcare professions were perceived less attractive to potential new HCWs.

This increased workload, in turn, amplifies the prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs through various pathways. For instance, the remaining HCWs experience a heavier workload, reduced job satisfaction, diminished decision latitude, and an increase in non-care-related tasks. This domino effect of absenteeism (highlighted in red in Fig. 3) is illustrated by the experience of a psychologist:

[…] a few colleagues dropped out, which meant the others had to take over the patients. On top of that, they got extra tasks because the waiting lists increased, and those had to be distributed among other teams. Job satisfaction decreased, and within a few months, the whole team fell apart. Some were at home with burnout, and the rest went to work elsewhere.

Decreases in decision latitude, particularly in terms of control over work schedules, is perceived as putting pressure on their social life. Additionally, HCWs who begin to experience burnout-related complaints may encounter a higher threshold for taking sick leave, as they are reluctant to let down their colleagues and patients, especially given the existing staff shortages. Whereas this response temporarily mitigates absenteeism (represented as ‘B1’ in light grey in Fig. 3), it is not a sustainable coping strategy and may ultimately contribute to a long-term increase in perceived workload. […] It also plays a role that you constantly have in the back of your mind that otherwise, your colleagues will have to work even harder. […] If this becomes structural, the risk of burnout will increase significantly.

Challenges to care quality

Finally, participants observed that rising burnout complaints among HCWs deteriorate healthcare delivery. This is partly explained by previously described dynamics: reduced staffing levels limits the time and attention available for patients. In addition, higher burnout levels may contribute to emotional and cognitive impairment, mental detachment, and more medical errors—ultimately lowering care quality and patient satisfaction (R22, Fig. 3). As dissatisfied patients voice their concerns, HCWs’ job satisfaction declines, contributing to a further increase in burnout complaints.

Societal response to signals of HCWs under pressure

At the societal level (B1-15 on the left in Fig. 4), indicators of increasing burnout among HCWs and its consequences generate a sense of urgency to improve working conditions, employment terms, and support for healthcare personnel. As a result, the HCWs in this study reported experiencing investments that led to improved staffing levels, enhanced financial compensation, greater decision latitude and higher quality of delivered care. An example of such a societal response relates to a recent amendment1 to the collective labor agreement (CLA) (highlighted in blue in Fig. 4) that was explained by a medical resident:

This meant that our clinical working hours were capped at 38 per week, plus 10 hours of self-study and preparing your outpatient consults. Before the alteration of the CLA, we worked 48 hours in the clinic, which meant that self-study and preparations should be performed in the evening/weekend. With this new CEA we have the time to do study/preparations during working hours which leaves more time for leisure, but also attending to your responsibilities in your private life. […] And my experience is also that it really does increase your work enjoyment.

Regulation through supportive leadership

At the level of working conditions (B16-22 on the right in Fig. 4), supervisors play a crucial balancing role in regulating the prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs. When burnout rates rise, they mitigate the impact by managing workload, redistributing responsibilities, and fostering job satisfaction. A nurse from a large elderly care organization explains what supervisors within their organization can do (highlighted in red in Fig. 4):

We have observed an increase in the number of burnout-related complaints. […] To address this, we have implemented training programs to equip supervisors with the skills to recognize early warning signs. Additionally, we conduct an annual ‘My Plan Employee’ meeting, in which the manager and employee discuss five key pillars: Self and Care, Mental, Emotional, Meaning, and Physical. For each area, employees can indicate whether they feel in balance. If there is an imbalance, need for support, or development request, each pillar outlines what can potentially be implemented or requested by the supervisor.

Although operating at different levels, both key dynamics stabilize burnout prevalence by improving perceived workload, job satisfaction, and quality of care.

Exogenous drivers

In addition to feedback loops that help explain the phenomenon from an endogenous perspective, three prioritized factors were perceived by the participants as exogenous (not part of a feedback loop).

The first, negative life events, was perceived as so disruptive that it can cause burnout complaints and absenteeism, regardless of workplace dynamics. The second is social support from colleagues, which can counteract burnout complaints by decreasing perceived workload and enhancing job satisfaction. The final factor, patient individualization, was perceived to be on the rise. Participants described it as a symptom of declining social cohesion leading to rising healthcare demands, as issues that were previously managed within the social community now turn into medical requests (e.g., parenting questions addressed to the general practitioner). This directly amplifies HCWs’ workload, a central factor in both reinforcing and balancing feedback dynamics.

Discussion

This study explored the dynamics behind burnout complaints from the perspective of HCWs. The perceived reinforcing dynamics illustrate how burnout complaints, staff shortages and impaired care quality amplify each other. Increased supportive leadership and rising societal awareness, which lead to investments in HCWs, were perceived to act as balancing dynamics. However, available trend data (Fig. 1) suggest that these efforts may not have been sufficient in stabilizing the rise of burnout complaints among HCWs over the past decade.

Positioning within existing research

Compared to working conditions and societal developments, significantly fewer living condition factors were selected, which is interesting given the ongoing debate around the ‘work-relatedness’ of burnout complaints [2, 3, 34, 35]. While this may indicate that HCWs view their work environment as a more significant contributor, it can also reflect greater homogeneity in participants’ work environments compared to their living conditions. This greater homogeneity may have led to more consensus within that category. Many of these workplace factors, such as workload, supervisory recognition, responsibility, and work-life balance, resemble those that have been found to be associated with burnout complaints in HCWs in a recent review [20]. At the healthcare system and societal level, the participants pointed to developments such as regulatory pressure, staff shortages, care quality concerns, increasing demand, and administrative burdens. These factors echo the main contributors to HCW workload, absenteeism, and turnover highlighted in a study by the Dutch Social and Economic Council [19].

Taken together, the convergence of variance-based research, which identifies statistical dependencies, and qualitative research, which uncovers assumed causal mechanisms and dynamics, can provide more comprehensive insight into complex problems. Mechanisms identified through participatory group model building help clarify the processes that give rise to the statistical dependencies observed in variance-based research [36]. Another example is a meta-analysis showing that the relationship between doctors’ working conditions and patient care outcomes is mediated by emotional exhaustion [37], which the present study extends by specifying the working conditions HCWs perceive as most relevant and the pathways through which they shape burnout prevalence and quality of care.

Moreover, the true value of CLDs lies in identifying dynamics [25], such as the reinforcing dynamic ‘staffing levels under pressure’. This “cyclical self-perpetuating mechanism” has previously been hypothesized from correlations between workload, burnout, and sick leave in nurses [15], and has been mentioned by experts in a previous CLD examining burnout prevalence in the general workforce [29]. Furthermore, in a large cross-sectional survey, nurses and physicians reported that improving nurse staffing levels was more important to their mental health than interventions targeting mental health directly [16].

The other key reinforcing dynamic centered around quality of care (R22, Fig. 3). While associations are widely reported, a review and meta-analysis found few rigorous studies linking HCW burnout to reduced quality of care, concluding that reported effect sizes may be smaller than indicated [38]. Nevertheless, the included longitudinal studies indeed suggest bi-directional causality [38], which provides temporal evidence for the cyclical nature of the relationship between these factors.

Regarding the balancing dynamics, the relation between supervisor support and HCW burnout complaints has been supported by various cross-sectional studies [20] and was also described in the previously mentioned CLD on burnout prevalence in the general workforce [29]. The dynamics around investments in HCWs aligns with the idea that policy interventions do not operate from outside the system (exogenous), but instead are part of the system’s own feedback processes (endogenous) [39]. However, the outgoing arrows from these investments —representing perceived policy interventions in the Dutch healthcare context— are highly context-dependent, making this part of the model potentially the least generalizable to other contexts.

Theoretical grounding for these dynamics can be found in the Job-Demands-Resources (JD-R) model, a widely applied framework for examining how job demands and resources contribute to HCW burnout complaints [20]. More recent extensions of this model incorporate feedback dynamics: persistent high demands can lead to exhaustion, which undermines cognitive functioning and emotional regulation, increasing the likelihood of self-undermining behaviors (e.g. making more mistakes). These behaviors, in turn, raise demands and intensify burnout [40]. This self-reinforcing loop is also reflected in the CLD in this study, particularly in feedback loop R22 (Fig. 3), where exhausted HCWs become less productive and more error-prone, leading to a decline in care quality. This triggers negative patient reactions, further increasing emotional demands. The JD-R model’s buffer hypothesis—that job resources mitigate the impact of demands on exhaustion [40] —is also present: participants described how supervisor and colleague support affected perceived workload and job satisfaction, which in turn shaped burnout complaints. It is worth noting that systems methodology, as used in this study, is particularly suited to generating evidence at the dimension of dynamics—identifying and clarifying the reinforcing and balancing processes that sustain complex problems over time [25].

Strengths, limitations and further reflections

This study contributes to the literature by explicitly modeling perceived population-level dynamics, showing how burnout in one HCW cascades to others. These effects are not captured in person-centered models (e.g. [26, 27]), or models that treat factors beyond the individual level as exogenous (e.g. [28]). As Geoffrey Rose argued, understanding prevalence requires studying populations, not individuals [41].

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the goal was to understand burnout prevalence as an emergent outcome, it was intentionally pre-defined and modeled as a factor to anchor the diagram to a specific outcome. This may have shaped its structure, potentially underrepresenting dimensions or relations that might have emerged if burnout had been explored more inductively. Second, this study did not stratify findings by professional subgroup (e.g., nurses versus physicians), which overlooks nuances between subgroups, such as nurse-doctor hierarchies and differences in income security, that may be contributors to burnout complaints among some professions but not others. Consequently, the findings may be interpreted as pertaining to an aggregated level only, as profession-specific mechanisms may not have been fully captured or may have been averaged across professions. Finally, sessions did not include the graphing-over-time script, which could have limited participants’ ability to identify and prioritize key dynamic variables. Overall, extending session length, incorporating additional professional perspectives or including more than one group of HCWs could have revealed further factors or relationships. Moreover, the convenience and snowball sampling strategy may have favored participants who were more engaged or system-aware, potentially narrowing the range of perspectives represented. These limitations may have influenced the inclusion of certain variables or mechanisms, while the key dynamics remain likely robust to moderate variation in the included factors.

Implications for future research

The CLD developed here offers multiple practical uses such as identifying leverage points, developing quantitative simulation models, and informing policy [42]. While these can be pursued independently, the greatest value lies in a coherent pathway from system mapping, through informing policy, to evaluation. This process includes developing CLDs with other stakeholders such as academic experts and policymakers, and translating these integrated insights into a computational system dynamics model [21]. This enables exploration of various ‘what-if’ scenarios to measure their impact on primary outcomes but also anticipating unintended consequences elsewhere in the system.

Conclusion

This study highlights how HCWs perceive the broader system driving burnout complaints, beyond individual-level factors. According to these insights, reinforcing dynamics involving shortages of staff and declining care quality have contributed to the increasing prevalence of burnout complaints among HCWs, while current investments in HCWs’ employment conditions and the support provided by supervisors worked to stabilize system change. Future research should explore this system from more perspectives and systematically identify policy scenarios to inform about effective interventions.
