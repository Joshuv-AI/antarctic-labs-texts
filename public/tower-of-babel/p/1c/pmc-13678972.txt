Causal effect estimation for ordinal outcomes: a performance comparison of G-computation, IPW, and TMLE-based methods
Authors: Aeran Park, Hyonggin An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrdinal outcomes are frequently used in clinical and observational studies. However, because ordinal variables do not have clearly defined intervals between categories, causal effect estimation for ordinal outcomes is more challenging than that for binary or continuous outcomes. Therefore, accurate causal inference for ordinal outcomes is important, as inadequate covariate adjustment may lead to biased estimates due to confounding. In this study, we defined the average treatment effect (ATE) for ordinal outcomes and compared the performance of various causal inference methods for estimating the ATE.MethodsA total of nine methods were compared, including the adjusted estimator, parametric- and Super Learner–based G-computation, inverse probability weighting (IPW), and both threshold-based and continuous-score Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE). Various simulation scenarios were constructed according to whether the outcome and treatment models were correctly specified. The methods were evaluated in terms of bias, empirical standard error (ESE), and root mean squared error (RMSE). In the real-data analysis, the Primary Biliary Cirrhosis (PBC) dataset was used to estimate the average treatment effect of D-penicillamine treatment, and the results were compared across methods using confidence intervals. Confidence intervals were constructed using model-based, bootstrap-based, or influence function–based variance estimation according to the characteristics of each estimator.ResultsSimulation results showed that, under the specific data-generating mechanisms considered in this study, TMLE-based methods generally exhibited the most stable overall performance. In particular, threshold-based TMLE maintained relatively low bias and RMSE under single-model misspecification scenarios. In contrast, G-computation– and IPW-based methods were sensitive to misspecification of the outcome and treatment models, respectively, and IPW-based methods exhibited substantial variability. When both models were simultaneously misspecified, the performance of most methods deteriorated. The Super Learner library, consisting of a generalized linear model and a random forest, improved the performance of several outcome model–based methods but did not fully mitigate the variability observed in IPW-based methods. For TMLE-based approaches, the Super Learner–based and parametric implementations showed comparable performance.In the real-data analysis, most methods yielded negative treatment effect estimates, suggesting a potential reduction in disease severity associated with treatment. However, because most 95% confidence intervals included zero, evidence for a statistically significant treatment effect was limited. Methods based on the Super Learner library generally produced narrower confidence intervals, suggesting more stable estimation performance. Nevertheless, differences in treatment effect estimates across methods were small, which may reflect the limited role of confounding in this randomized clinical trial dataset.ConclusionsUnder the specific data-generating mechanisms considered in this study, threshold-based TMLE demonstrated relatively stable performance for estimating the average treatment effect with ordinal outcomes, particularly under single-model misspecification scenarios. Threshold-based TMLE implemented using the Super Learner library performed comparably to its parametric counterpart. In the real-data analysis, methods based on the Super Learner library tended to produce smaller standard errors and narrower confidence intervals, suggesting the potential for more stable estimation in this setting.

Introduction

Ordinal outcomes are widely used in clinical and observational studies to classify patients’ disease status into categories with a natural ordering. For example, disease stage in liver disease, pain severity, and functional status are discrete variables with an inherent order, requiring statistical analysis and interpretation distinct from those used for binary or continuous outcomes [1, 2]. Various approaches have been proposed for causal effect estimation for ordinal outcomes, including methods that directly utilize cumulative distribution structures and approaches that transform ordinal outcomes into continuous scores for analysis [3, 4].

In particular, causal effect estimation for ordinal outcomes is an important methodological issue in observational studies. In real-world clinical data, randomization is often infeasible, leading to potential confounding, and inadequate adjustment for covariates may result in biased causal inference [5, 6].

G-computation has been widely used as a representative method for causal effect estimation. However, it provides consistent estimates only when the outcome model is correctly specified [7]. In contrast, inverse probability weighting (IPW) can adjust for confounding through the treatment model, but when the propensity score is close to 0 or 1, unstable weights may lead to increased variance [8]. To address these limitations, Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE), a semiparametric approach that simultaneously utilizes both the outcome and treatment models, has been proposed. TMLE possesses the double robustness property, providing consistent estimation when either the outcome model or the treatment model is correctly specified, and achieving the semiparametric efficiency bound when both models are correctly specified [9].

In addition, Super Learner is an ensemble-based machine learning approach that combines multiple candidate algorithms, including generalized linear models, penalized regression, tree-based models, and nonparametric methods. In the present study, the Super Learner library was intentionally restricted to a generalized linear model and a random forest to facilitate transparent comparisons across estimators. Consequently, the findings related to Super Learner should be interpreted as reflecting the performance of this specific learner library rather than the performance of Super Learner implementations in general. By evaluating predictive accuracy using V-fold cross-validation and learning the optimal weighted combination of candidate models, Super Learner reduces reliance on specific model assumptions and enables data-adaptive estimation. Theoretically, it is guaranteed to perform asymptotically at least as well as the optimal weighted combination of candidate learners in the library [10, 11].

Therefore, this study aimed to compare and evaluate causal inference methods for ordinal outcomes, including data-adaptive approaches using Super Learner, in order to address limitations of conventional parametric approaches. Specifically, nine methods were compared: the adjusted estimator, parametric- and Super Learner–based G-computation and IPW, as well as threshold-based and continuous-score TMLE. For most methods, the ordinal outcome was transformed into a continuous score on the interval [0,1], and the corresponding average treatment effect (ATE) was estimated on that scale. In contrast, threshold-based TMLE employed a cumulative binary decomposition of the ordinal outcome, with a separate TMLE fitted at each threshold.

In the simulation study, various scenarios were considered according to whether the working outcome and treatment models were aligned with the data-generating mechanism, and the performance of each method was evaluated using bias, empirical standard error (ESE), and root mean squared error (RMSE). In addition, data from Primary Biliary Cirrhosis (PBC) were used to estimate and compare the average treatment effect of D-penicillamine on liver disease stage (stages 1–4) across methods. All methodological comparisons were conducted under a fixed-time treatment framework.

Theoretical background of G-computation, IPW, and TMLE for causal effect estimation

Causal parameter of interest

The causal estimand of interest in this study is the average treatment effect (ATE) of a binary treatment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A \in \{0,1\}$$\end{document} on an ordinal outcome \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y \in \{1, \ldots , K\}$$\end{document}. Under the potential outcomes framework, the potential outcome for individual i is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_i(a)$$\end{document}.

In most of the methods considered in this study, the ordinal outcome is mapped to a continuous score on the [0, 1] scale using the following linear scoring transformation (except for the threshold-based TMLE described in Estimation methods section):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y^c = \frac{Y - 1}{K - 1}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Accordingly, the causal parameter of interest is defined as the average treatment effect on the transformed outcome:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \psi = \mathbb {E}[Y^c(1)] - \mathbb {E}[Y^c(0)]. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Consistency:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y = Y(A). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Consistency:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y = Y(A). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Estimation methods

G-computation

G-computation is a method for estimating causal parameters by modeling the outcome regression and evaluating counterfactual expected outcomes [7]. In this study, we defined the following outcome model using the transformed outcome \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^c$$\end{document}:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q(A,W) = \mathbb {E}(Y^c \mid A, W). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Using the fitted model, predicted potential outcomes were obtained for each individual by setting the treatment variable to A = a. The expected outcome under each treatment level was then estimated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{n} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \hat{Q}(a, W_i), \quad a \in \{0,1\}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The average treatment effect (ATE) was estimated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\psi }_G = \frac{1}{n} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \left[ \hat{Q}(1,W_i) - \hat{Q}(0,W_i) \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

G-computation provides an efficient estimator when the outcome model is correctly specified; however, it may be biased under model misspecification [7].

The standard error for G-computation can be calculated using a plug-in influence function–based approach; however, this approach may not fully account for the uncertainty associated with estimation of the outcome model. Therefore, in this study, bootstrap-based methods were used to construct confidence intervals to better account for uncertainty in estimation [5].

For Super Learner–based G-computation, the same estimation procedure was applied, except that the outcome regression model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Q}$$\end{document} was estimated using the Super Learner algorithm [10, 11]. Standard errors and bootstrap-based 95% confidence intervals were calculated in the same manner as in the parametric G-computation.

Inverse probability weighting

Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) is a method for estimating causal effects by directly modeling the treatment assignment mechanism and constructing a weighted sample [8]. The propensity score is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} g(W) = P(A = 1 \mid W). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Using the transformed outcome \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^c$$\end{document}, the average treatment effect can be expressed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \psi = \mathbb {E}\left[ \frac{A Y^c}{g(W)} - \frac{(1 - A) Y^c}{1 - g(W)} \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The corresponding estimator is given by:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\psi }_{\text {IPW}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \left( \frac{A_i Y_i^c}{\tilde{g}(W_i)} - \frac{(1 - A_i) Y_i^c}{1 - \tilde{g}(W_i)} \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

For numerical stability, the estimated propensity score was truncated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{g}(W) = \min \{\max (\hat{g}(W), \epsilon ), 1 - \epsilon \}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{g}(W)$$\end{document} denotes the truncated estimated propensity score.

IPW has the advantage of not requiring explicit specification of the outcome model, making it insensitive to misspecification of the outcome regression model. However, if the propensity score model is misspecified or if g(W) takes extreme values close to 0 or 1, the variance of the estimator can increase substantially [8].

The standard error was computed using the plug-in influence function approach. The estimated influence function for each observation is given by [12]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{IF}_i = \frac{A_i Y_i^c}{\tilde{g}(W_i)} - \frac{(1 - A_i) Y_i^c}{1 - \tilde{g}(W_i)} - \hat{\psi }_{\text {IPW}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The standard error was estimated as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{SE}(\hat{\psi }_{\text {IPW}}) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{n^2} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \hat{IF}_i^2 }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

A 95% Wald confidence interval based on the normal approximation was constructed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\psi }_{\text {IPW}} \pm z_{0.975} \cdot \widehat{SE}(\hat{\psi }_{\text {IPW}}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

For Super Learner–based IPW, the same estimation procedure was used, except that the propensity score model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{g}$$\end{document} was estimated using Super Learner [10, 11]. Standard errors and 95% Wald confidence intervals were calculated in the same manner as in the parametric IPW approach, based on the empirical variance of the estimated plug-in influence function and the normal approximation.

Targeted maximum likelihood estimation

In this study, we considered two TMLE approaches. The first is a threshold-based TMLE, which directly accounts for the cumulative structure of the ordinal outcome. The second is a continuous-score TMLE, which is applied after transforming the ordinal outcome into a continuous score.

Both approaches combine the outcome regression model and the propensity score model, and perform estimation based on the efficient influence function (EIF). Under appropriate regularity conditions, these estimators achieve the semiparametric efficiency bound [9].

Threshold-based TMLE

Threshold-based TMLE decomposes an ordinal outcome into multiple cumulative binary outcomes, applies TMLE at each threshold, and then combines the results. In this study, for each k = 1, … , K-1, we defined the following cumulative binary variable [13]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y^{(k)} = \mathbb {I}(Y \le k), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^{(k)}$$\end{document} is an indicator of whether the outcome is less than or equal to the threshold k. A separate TMLE was fitted at each threshold, and the results were subsequently combined to obtain the final causal effect estimate.

(1) Initial outcome model

For each k, the outcome model is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {Q}^{(k)}(A,W) = P(Y^{(k)} \mid A, W). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

For each threshold, the binary outcome \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^{(k)}$$\end{document} was constructed and a logistic regression model was fitted.

(2) Treatment model

The treatment model (propensity score) is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} g(W) = P(A = 1 \mid W), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and is shared across all thresholds.

(3) Clever covariate

The efficient influence function (EIF) at each threshold has the form:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D^{(k)}(O)&= H(A,W)\{Y^{(k)} - {Q}^{(k)}(A,W)\} + {Q}^{(k)}(1,W)\\ &\quad - {Q}^{(k)}(0,W) - \psi ^{(k)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The clever covariate is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H(A,W) = \frac{A}{{g}(W)} - \frac{1 - A}{1 - {g}(W)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

(4) Targeting step

The initial estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Q}^{(k)}(A,W)$$\end{document} is updated to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Q}^{*(k)}(A,W)$$\end{document} such that:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_n \left[ \hat{H}(A,W)\{Y^{(k)} - \hat{Q}^{*(k)}(A,W)\} \right] = 0. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

(5) Threshold-specific causal effect

Using the updated outcome model, the causal effect at each threshold is estimated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\Delta }_k = \frac{1}{n} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \left[ \hat{Q}^{*(k)}(1,W_i) - \hat{Q}^{*(k)}(0,W_i) \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

(6) Final estimator

The final threshold-based TMLE estimator is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {bin}} = -\frac{1}{K - 1} \sum \limits _{k=1}^{K-1} \hat{\Delta }_k. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The negative sign arises from the relationship between the cumulative probability definition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^{(k)} = \mathbb {I}(Y \le k)$$\end{document} and the score transformation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^c = \frac{Y - 1}{K - 1}$$\end{document}.

The standard error is computed using the empirical variance of the combined efficient influence function [9]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{SE}(\hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {bin}}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n^2} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \widehat{EIF}_i^2}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

A 95% Wald confidence interval is constructed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {bin}} \pm z_{0.975} \cdot \widehat{SE}(\hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {bin}}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Threshold-based TMLE has the advantage of directly incorporating the cumulative structure of ordinal outcomes. Compared with continuous-score TMLE, it directly incorporates the ordinal structure of the outcome. However, because a separate TMLE must be fitted for each threshold, the computational burden may increase.

For Super Learner–based threshold-based TMLE, the same estimation procedure was employed, except that both the outcome model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Q}$$\end{document} and treatment model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{g}$$\end{document} were estimated using Super Learner [10, 11]. The targeting step and efficient influence function (EIF) calculation were then performed using the same procedure as in the parametric threshold-based TMLE. Standard errors and 95% Wald confidence intervals were calculated based on the empirical variance of the estimated efficient influence function and the normal approximation.

Continuous-score TMLE

After transforming the ordinal outcome onto a continuous score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^c$$\end{document}, TMLE was applied by treating it as a continuous outcome [14] .

(1) Initial outcome model

In continuous-score TMLE, the outcome model is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {Q}(A,W) = \mathbb {E}(Y^c \mid A, W). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

(2) Treatment model

The treatment model (propensity score) is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} g(W) = P(A = 1 \mid W), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which is identical to that used in threshold-based TMLE.

(3) Clever covariate

The efficient influence function (EIF) for continuous-score TMLE is given by:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D(O) = H(A,W)\{Y^c - {Q}(A,W)\} + {Q}(1,W) - {Q}(0,W) - \psi , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the clever covariate is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H(A,W) = \frac{A}{{g}(W)} - \frac{1 - A}{1 - {g}(W)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

(4) Targeting step

The initial estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Q}(A,W)$$\end{document} is updated to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Q}^*(A,W)$$\end{document} through the targeting step, such that the EIF estimating equation is satisfied:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_n \left[ \hat{H}(A,W)\{Y^c - \hat{Q}^*(A,W)\} \right] = 0. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

(5) Final estimator

The final estimator based on the updated outcome model is given by:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {cont}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \left[ \hat{Q}^*(1,W_i) - \hat{Q}^*(0,W_i) \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The standard error was calculated using the empirical variance of the efficient influence function [9]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{SE}(\hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {cont}}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n^2} \sum \limits _{i=1}^n \widehat{EIF}_i^2}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

A 95% Wald confidence interval was constructed using the normal approximation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {cont}} \pm z_{0.975} \cdot \widehat{SE}(\hat{\psi }_{\text {TMLE}}^{\text {cont}}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Continuous-score TMLE has the advantage of being straightforward to implement and computationally efficient. In particular, it allows the application of TMLE algorithms by transforming the ordinal outcome into a continuous variable. However, this transformation may not fully preserve the ordinal structure of the outcome, potentially resulting in some loss of information relative to threshold-based TMLE.

For Super Learner–based continuous-score TMLE, the same estimation procedure was employed, except that both the outcome model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Q}$$\end{document} and treatment model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{g}$$\end{document} were estimated using Super Learner [10, 11]. The targeting step and efficient influence function (EIF) calculation were then performed using the same procedure as in the parametric continuous-score TMLE. Standard errors and 95% Wald confidence intervals were calculated based on the empirical variance of the estimated efficient influence function and the normal approximation.

Simulation study for comparative evaluation of methods

Data generation

In each simulation replication, the observed data were generated as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} O = (W, A, Y), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and were assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ A \in \{0,1\} $$\end{document} denotes the treatment variable, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ Y \in \{1,2,\dots ,K\} $$\end{document} is the ordinal outcome, and W is a vector of covariates.

The covariate vector consists of a mixture of binary and continuous variables,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W = (W_1, W_2, W_3), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}generated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W_1 \sim \text {Bernoulli}(0.5), \quad W_2 \sim \mathcal {N}(0,1), \quad W_3 \sim \mathcal {N}(0,1). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

All covariates were generated independently and served as confounders for both the treatment and the outcome.

Treatment assignment mechanism

The treatment variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ A \in \{0,1\} $$\end{document} was generated according to the following logistic model:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P(A = 1 \mid W)&= \text {logit}^{-1} \left( -0.3 + 1.5W_1 - 2.5W_2 + 1.2W_3\right. \\ &\qquad \left. + 1.0 W_2 W_3 + 0.4 \log (|W_2| + 1) \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

This specification mimics an observational study setting in which treatment assignment depends on covariates, while ensuring that treatment probabilities remained bounded away from 0 and 1 for all covariate values, thereby satisfying the positivity assumption.

To mitigate instability caused by extreme propensity scores close to 0 or 1, all estimated propensity scores were truncated to the interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\varepsilon , 1-\varepsilon ]$$\end{document}. This truncation was applied identically to the treatment models in both TMLE and IPW, and a pre-specified value of ɛ = 0.05 was used in the primary analysis. In the sensitivity analysis, ɛ = 0.01 was applied, as shown in Appendix A: Table 3.

Ordinal outcome generation

The linear predictor for the outcome model was defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \eta = 1.5A + 1.0W_1 - 2.0W_2 + 2.0W_2^2 + 0.8W_3 + 0.5 \sin (W_3). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The term involving A represents the treatment effect, whereas The remaining terms represent the effects of the covariates on the outcome.

Cumulative probabilities were then generated using a cumulative logit model:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {logit} \left( P(Y \le k \mid A, W) \right) = \theta _k - \eta , \quad k = 1, \dots , K-1. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The cut-points were specified as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \theta = (-1.2,\, 0,\, 1.2). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Category-specific probabilities were obtained from the cumulative probabilities, and the ordinal outcome Y was sampled from a multinomial distribution. For numerical stability, each probability was bounded below by a small positive constant and subsequently normalized to ensure that the probabilities summed to 1 [2].

True causal effect

The true average treatment effect (ATE) was evaluated using Monte Carlo simulation. To this end, 200,000 synthetic observations were generated following the same data-generating mechanism, with covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ W_1, W_2, W_3 $$\end{document} generated in the same manner. For each covariate realization, the treatment was fixed at A = 1 and A = 0 , and the cumulative category probabilities of the potential outcomes were computed using the cumulative logit model.

Based on this, the conditional expectation under each treatment level was calculated, and the potential outcome mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {E}[Y^c(a)]$$\end{document} was estimated by averaging over all simulated samples. The ordinal outcome was then transformed to a [0,1] score scale, and the average treatment effect was defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \psi = \mathbb {E}[Y^c(1)] - \mathbb {E}[Y^c(0)]. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The resulting true ATE was estimated to be 0.146 , corresponding to an average increase of 0.146 on the transformed score scale. The Monte Carlo standard error was approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ 2 \times 10^{-4} $$\end{document}, indicating that the variability of the estimate is negligible due to the large simulation sample size.

Simulation study design

Estimation methods

In this study, Monte Carlo simulations were conducted to evaluate the performance of nine estimators the average treatment effect (ATE) for ordinal outcomes. To assess the impact of model flexibility, both parametric and Super Learner–based approaches were applied to major estimation methods, including G-computation, IPW, and TMLE. This allowed us to reduce dependence on specific functional form assumptions of the nuisance models while enabling a more direct comparison of the characteristics of each method.

(1) Adjusted estimator

After transforming the ordinal outcome into a linear score, a covariate-adjusted linear regression model including the covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_2$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_2^2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_3$$\end{document} was fitted. The regression coefficient for the treatment variable A was used as the estimate of the average treatment effect (ATE). This approach was implemented using a fixed partially misspecified outcome regression model across all scenarios, thereby allowing comparison with more complex causal inference methods.

(2) G-computation (Parametric)

After transforming the ordinal outcome into a linear score, the outcome model\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q(A,W) = \mathbb {E}(Y^c \mid A, W) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}was estimated using a linear regression model.

(3) G-computation (Super Learner)

The same framework as parametric G-computation was used, but the outcome model Q(A,W) was estimated using the Super Learner.

(4) Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW, Parametric)

After transforming the ordinal outcome into a linear score, the treatment model\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} g(W) = P(A = 1 \mid W) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}was estimated using a logistic regression model.

(5) Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW, Super Learner)

The same framework as parametric IPW was used, but the propensity score g(W) was estimated using the Super Learner.

(6) Threshold-based TMLE (Parametric)

The ordinal outcome was decomposed into cumulative binary variables defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y^{(k)} = \mathbb {I}(Y \le k), \quad k = 1, \dots , K-1. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

TMLE was applied to each binary outcome, and the results were combined to estimate the ATE. Both the outcome model and the treatment model were estimated using logistic regression.

(7) Threshold-based TMLE (Super Learner)

The same framework as the parametric threshold-based TMLE was used, but both the outcome model and the treatment model were estimated using Super Learner.

(8) Continuous-score TMLE (Parametric)

The ordinal outcome was transformed into a linear score, and TMLE was applied to estimate the ATE. The outcome model was estimated using linear regression, and the treatment model was estimated using logistic regression.

(9) Continuous-score TMLE (Super Learner)

The same framework as parametric continuous-score TMLE was used, but both the outcome model and the treatment model were estimated using the Super Learner.

In this study, Super Learner was used as an ensemble learning approach to improve prediction accuracy by combining algorithms with different characteristics. A generalized linear model (SL.glm) and random forest (SL.randomForest) were included as candidate learners in the Super Learner library. The generalized linear model provides a simple and interpretable approach based on linear relationships among variables, whereas random forest is a tree-based nonparametric method that can flexibly capture nonlinear relationships and complex interactions. Because the learner library was intentionally restricted to these two algorithms, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the performance of this specific learner library rather than that of Super Learner implementations in general.

To evaluate the predictive accuracy of each candidate learner, V-fold cross-validation was applied. Specifically, the entire dataset was divided into V folds, and the model was trained using V-1 folds while prediction accuracy was evaluated on the remaining fold; this procedure was repeated across all folds. In the primary analysis, V = 10 was used, and the average prediction error across folds was used to compare candidate algorithms. In addition, a sensitivity analysis using V = 5 was conducted, and the results are presented in Appendix A: Table 6.

Finally, the weights for each algorithm were estimated to minimize the cross-validation–based prediction error, and the final prediction model was constructed as a weighted linear combination of the candidate learners [15, 16].

Model scenarios

To evaluate the robustness of each estimator to model misspecification, four scenarios were considered based on whether the outcome model Q and the treatment model g were correctly specified.

S1 (Reference model specification)

The working outcome model was specified as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q(A,W)&= \beta _0 +\beta _A A +\beta _1 W_1 +\beta _2 W_2 +\beta _3 W_2^2\\ &\quad +\beta _4 W_3 +\beta _5 \sin (W_3), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and the working treatment model was specified as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} g(W)&= \text {logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha _0 +\alpha _1 W_1 +\alpha _2 W_2 +\alpha _3 W_3 \right. \\ &\quad \left. +\alpha _4 W_2W_3 +\alpha _5 \log (|W_2|+1) \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Therefore, except for the adjusted estimator, the working nuisance models included the same covariates and functional forms as those used in the data-generating mechanism. However, because different estimators rely on different modeling assumptions, this reference specification does not imply that every estimator was correctly specified.

S2 (Outcome Model Misspecification)

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q(A,W) = \beta _0 + \beta _A A + \beta _1 W_1 + \beta _4 W_3. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In the working outcome model, the covariate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ W_2 $$\end{document}, the quadratic term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ W_2^2 $$\end{document}, and the nonlinear term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \sin (W_3) $$\end{document} were omitted.

S3 (Misspecified treatment model)

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} g(W) = \text {logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha _0 + \alpha _1 W_1 + \alpha _3 W_3 \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In the working treatment model, the covariate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ W_2 $$\end{document}, the interaction term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ W_2 W_3 $$\end{document}, and the nonlinear term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \log (|W_2| + 1) $$\end{document} were omitted.

S4 (Double misspecification)

Both the outcome and treatment models were misspecified as in S2 and S3, respectively.

Performance measures

Simulations were conducted with B = 1000 repetitions and a sample size of n = 1000 for each scenario. To evaluate the effect of sample size, additional settings with n = 200 and n = 500 were also considered. The corresponding results are presented in Appendix A: Tables 4 and 5.

The performance of each estimator was evaluated across repeated simulations. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \hat{\psi }^{(b)} $$\end{document} denote the estimated causal effect in the b -th simulation ( b = 1, … , B ), and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \psi _0 $$\end{document} denote the pre-specified true causal effect. The performance measures were defined as follows.

Bias

Bias was defined as the average difference between the estimated and true causal effects across Monte Carlo simulation replications:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Bias} = \frac{1}{B} \sum \limits _{b=1}^{B} \left( \hat{\psi }^{(b)} - \psi _0 \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Empirical standard error (ESE)

The empirical standard error (ESE) was defined as the variability of the estimator across Monte Carlo simulation replications:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {ESE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{B-1} \sum \limits _{b=1}^{B} \left( \hat{\psi }^{(b)} - \bar{\psi } \right) ^2 }, \quad \bar{\psi } = \frac{1}{B} \sum \limits _{b=1}^{B} \hat{\psi }^{(b)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Root mean squared error (RMSE)

The RMSE combines both bias and variability and is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{B} \sum \limits _{b=1}^{B} \left( \hat{\psi }^{(b)} - \psi _0 \right) ^2 }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

S1: reference model specification

In Scenario S1, TMLE-based estimators demonstrated the greatest overall robustness. In particular, the parametric continuous-score TMLE achieved low bias and RMSE (bias = 0.0016, RMSE = 0.0324). The parametric threshold-based TMLE also showed stable performance with low RMSE (bias = 0.0105, RMSE = 0.0293), while the Super Learner–based threshold TMLE yielded comparable results (RMSE = 0.0401).

Although Scenario S1 was designed as a reference specification scenario, this does not necessarily imply that every working model used by each estimator was fully compatible with the corresponding estimation target. In particular, the ordinal outcome was generated from a cumulative logit model, whereas some estimators, including parametric G-computation, were implemented using regression models for transformed score outcomes. In addition, estimated propensity scores were truncated for numerical stability, which may have contributed to residual bias even when the treatment model included the correct covariates and functional forms.

Consistent with these considerations, parametric G-computation exhibited substantial bias and RMSE (bias = 0.1470, RMSE = 0.1496). In contrast, Super Learner–based G-computation reduced both bias and RMSE (bias = 0.0484, RMSE = 0.0544). The adjusted estimator showed performance comparable to that of parametric G-computation.

For IPW, the parametric approach yielded moderate bias and variability (bias = 0.0823, RMSE = 0.0955), whereas the Super Learner–based IPW exhibited greater variability (RMSE = 0.1231).

S2: outcome model misspecification

In Scenario S2, G-computation–based methods were highly sensitive to outcome model misspecification. Parametric G-computation showed substantial bias and RMSE (bias = 0.1604, RMSE = 0.1619). Although Super Learner–based G-computation reduced both bias and RMSE (bias = 0.1094, RMSE = 0.1110), considerable bias remained. The adjusted estimator showed performance comparable to that of parametric G-computation (bias = 0.1491, RMSE = 0.1516).

In contrast, TMLE-based approaches showed relatively stable performance under this scenario. Both threshold-based and continuous-score TMLE maintained low bias and RMSE. In particular, the parametric threshold-based TMLE achieved a bias of 0.0047 and an RMSE of 0.0338, whereas the parametric continuous-score TMLE yielded a bias of 0.0062 and an RMSE of 0.0343. The Super Learner–based threshold TMLE showed comparable performance (RMSE = 0.0378).

Compared with G-computation, IPW-based methods were less affected by outcome model misspecification. However, Super Learner–based IPW continued to exhibit relatively large variability.

S3: treatment model misspecification

In Scenario S3, IPW-based methods were sensitive to treatment model misspecification. Parametric IPW yielded substantial bias and RMSE (bias = 0.1794, RMSE = 0.1813), while Super Learner–based IPW also exhibited considerable bias and variability (bias = 0.1722, RMSE = 0.1752). The adjusted estimator showed somewhat lower bias and RMSE than parametric IPW.

For G-computation, the parametric approach continued to exhibit substantial bias (bias = 0.1472, RMSE = 0.1496); however, Super Learner–based G-computation reduced both bias and RMSE (bias = 0.0488, RMSE = 0.0542).

Among the TMLE-based methods, threshold-based TMLE maintained stable performance under treatment model misspecification. Parametric threshold-based TMLE achieved low bias and RMSE (bias = 0.0141, RMSE = 0.0250), while the Super Learner–based threshold TMLE showed comparable performance (RMSE = 0.0255). In contrast, continuous-score TMLE exhibited larger bias and RMSE than threshold-based TMLE. Parametric continuous-score TMLE yielded a bias of 0.1343 and an RMSE of 0.1366, whereas Super Learner–based continuous-score TMLE reduced both measures (bias = 0.0671, RMSE = 0.0712).

These findings suggest that the asymptotic double robustness property of TMLE may not be fully realized, particularly in finite samples when the treatment model is misspecified. One possible explanation is that continuous-score TMLE relies on a single global targeting update based on a continuous outcome regression model, allowing misspecification in the propensity score model to influence the targeting step through the clever covariate. In contrast, threshold-based TMLE performs separate targeting updates for cumulative probabilities at multiple thresholds, which may reduce the influence of treatment model misspecification. This feature may partially explain the greater robustness of threshold-based TMLE under Scenario S3.

S4: double misspecification

In Scenario S4, increased bias and RMSE were observed across most methods. Parametric G-computation yielded substantial bias and RMSE (bias = 0.1606, RMSE = 0.1620), while the adjusted estimator also showed considerable bias (bias = 0.1492, RMSE = 0.1516). Super Learner–based G-computation reduced both bias and RMSE (bias = 0.1102, RMSE = 0.1118); however, considerable bias remained.

IPW-based methods exhibited larger bias and RMSE than other approaches. Parametric IPW yielded a bias of 0.1793 and an RMSE of 0.1811, whereas Super Learner–based IPW showed similar results (bias = 0.1722, RMSE = 0.1750), indicating unstable estimation performance.

TMLE-based methods also showed deterioration in estimation accuracy when both the outcome and treatment models were simultaneously misspecified. Parametric threshold-based TMLE yielded a bias of 0.1411 and an RMSE of 0.1425, while Super Learner–based threshold TMLE showed similarly degraded performance (bias = 0.1402, RMSE = 0.1416). Continuous-score TMLE likewise showed similar performance between the parametric and Super Learner–based approaches, with RMSE values of 0.1459 and 0.1443, respectively. These findings suggest that although TMLE is theoretically double robust, its robustness advantages may be limited when both the outcome and treatment models are simultaneously misspecified.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying the propensity score truncation level, sample size, and cross-validation setting. Smaller truncation levels (ε = 0.01) increased variability, particularly for continuous-score TMLE, whereas larger truncation levels (ε = 0.05) produced more stable variance behavior, reflecting the usual bias–variance tradeoff associated with propensity score truncation. Across different sample sizes, the relative behavior of the estimators was largely preserved, while smaller sample sizes were associated with increased variability and RMSE. Changing the number of cross-validation folds from V = 10 to V = 5 resulted in only minor differences, although slightly lower RMSE values were occasionally observed with V = 10. Estimation methods

To estimate causal effects, the same nine methods and analytical settings used in the simulation study were applied. For Super Learner, a library consisting of generalized linear models (SL.glm) and random forests (SL.randomForest) was used, with V = 10 and ɛ = 0.05.

To evaluate estimation uncertainty, standard errors and confidence intervals were constructed according to the procedures described in Estimation methods section. For the adjusted regression estimator, model-based standard errors for the treatment coefficient were used. For both parametric and Super Learner–based G-computation, the bootstrap-based confidence interval procedure described in Estimation methods section was employed. Specifically, bootstrap samples of size n were generated from the original data using sampling with replacement, and this procedure was repeated B = 1000 times. The same estimation procedure was applied to each bootstrap sample to re-estimate the average treatment effect (ATE), and 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals were constructed using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap estimate distribution. For both parametric and Super Learner–based IPW, standard errors and confidence intervals were calculated using the empirical variance of the estimated IF. In addition, for both parametric and Super Learner–based TMLE methods, standard errors and confidence intervals were constructed based on the empirical variance of the estimated EIF.

Conclusion and discussion

In this study, we compared the performance of various causal inference methods for estimating the average treatment effect (ATE) with ordinal outcomes.

Overall, the simulation results were largely consistent with theoretical expectations. Under the simulation settings considered in this study, TMLE-based estimators generally exhibited the most favorable overall performance. In particular, the threshold-based TMLE maintained relatively low bias and RMSE under scenarios involving misspecification of either the outcome model or the treatment model (S2 and S3). In contrast, the continuous-score TMLE appeared more sensitive to misspecification of the treatment model. As expected, G-computation and IPW-based methods were primarily affected by misspecification of the outcome and treatment models, respectively, with IPW-based approaches exhibiting substantially greater variability across scenarios. When both nuisance models were misspecified simultaneously (S4), estimation performance deteriorated for most methods, including TMLE.

The Super Learner library used in this study, consisting of a generalized linear model and a random forest, was associated with reductions in bias and RMSE for several outcome-model-based approaches. However, it did not fully mitigate the instability observed in IPW-based estimators. Furthermore, the Super Learner-based TMLE achieved performance comparable to that of the corresponding parametric TMLE. These findings should be interpreted within the context of the specific Super Learner library considered in this study and should not be generalized to all possible Super Learner implementations.

Additional sensitivity analyses showed that the main estimation patterns remained consistent across different truncation levels, sample sizes, and cross-validation settings, supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

In the clinical data analysis, all estimators yielded negative ATE estimates, suggesting a potential reduction in disease severity associated with treatment. However, most 95% confidence intervals included zero, providing limited evidence for a statistically significant treatment effect. The adjusted estimators and G-computation approaches produced similar estimates, whereas IPW-based methods exhibited greater variability, as reflected by larger standard errors and wider confidence intervals. In contrast, TMLE-based estimators generally demonstrated more stable inference, and only the Super Learner–based continuous-score TMLE produced a 95% confidence interval that excluded zero.

Methods incorporating the specific Super Learner library considered in this study also tended to yield narrower confidence intervals. Nevertheless, because the data were obtained from a randomized clinical trial with limited evidence of residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting the behavior of the estimators within this particular dataset rather than evidence of the general superiority of any specific method. Overall, differences in the estimated treatment effects across methods were relatively small, suggesting that the practical impact of estimator choice was limited in this setting. This may reflect the fact that confounding was largely controlled through randomization, thereby reducing the extent to which different adjustment strategies influenced the results.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations.

First, the methods compared in this study differed in how ordinal outcomes were utilized. Some approaches transformed the ordinal outcome into a continuous score for analysis. Although this approach simplifies estimation and implementation, interpretation of the resulting effect measures requires caution because it relies on the assumption of equal spacing between ordinal categories. In contrast, threshold-based approaches directly utilize the cumulative probability structure of ordinal outcomes and therefore more directly preserve the ordinal structure of the outcome variable. Accordingly, further methodological research is warranted to develop approaches that more effectively reflect the inherent characteristics of ordinal variables [3, 4].

Second, different variance estimation approaches, including model-based, bootstrap-based, and influence function–based methods, were used according to the characteristics of each estimator. Although this allowed confidence intervals to be constructed using procedures appropriate for each method, differences in variance estimation approaches may have influenced comparisons of confidence interval widths and statistical significance across methods [5, 9, 17].

Third, the Super Learner implemented in this study included only two candidate learners (a generalized linear model and a random forest). Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the performance of this specific learner library rather than the performance of Super Learner or machine-learning–based approaches in general. The inclusion of additional learners, alternative tuning strategies, or more diverse learner libraries may affect estimation performance and the resulting comparative conclusions [10, 11].

Fourth, the simulation study assumed a simplified data-generating structure based on specific functional forms. The covariates were generated independently, and severe positivity violations were not considered. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence under the specific data-generating mechanisms considered in this study rather than as general evidence that one estimator is uniformly superior. Further studies incorporating correlated covariates, stronger positivity challenges, and more complex data-generating mechanisms are warranted [16].

Finally, this study was conducted under a fixed-time treatment framework. In practical applications, however, treatments and confounders often vary over time, and more complex longitudinal causal inference methods would therefore likely be required [18].
