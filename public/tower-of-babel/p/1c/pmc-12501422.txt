Individual, social, and environmental predictors of insomnia symptom trajectories in a population cohort of Australian adolescents aged 13 to 17 years
Authors: Kate Maston, Malcolm B Gillies, Helga Zoega, Philip J Batterham, Helen Christensen, Aliza Werner-Seidler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to identify heterogeneous insomnia symptom trajectories in a population cohort of adolescents during a developmentally sensitive window (ages 13–17), and to examine the influence of individual, social, and environmental predictors on these different trajectories.MethodsInsomnia symptoms were assessed in a population-based sample of 6377 adolescents via online questionnaires administered annually at school from year 8 (age 13–14) to year 11 (age 16–17). Measures assessing individual, social, and environmental predictors were administered in year 8 only. We used latent class growth analysis to identify insomnia symptom trajectories and multinomial logistic regression to examine individual, social, and environmental predictors associated with each trajectory class.ResultsWe identified four insomnia symptom trajectories: one “low risk” trajectory (low-stable 55.73%), two “elevated risk” trajectories (increasing 27.49% and high-stable 10.95%), and one “remitting” trajectory (high-decreasing 5.83%). Predictors common to elevated risk trajectories (vs low-stable) were female gender, greater internalizing or externalizing symptoms, and greater negative family interactions. Adolescents with high-stable trajectories were also more likely to be gender diverse or diagnosed with ≥1 disabilities, while adolescents with increasing trajectories were more likely to have ≥1 adverse childhood experiences or lower school connectedness. Male gender was the only predictor significantly associated with a remitting trajectory (vs high-stable).ConclusionsThese findings inform future research about the developmental course of insomnia symptoms during adolescence and can guide screening and intervention efforts aimed at improving sleep health for youth at risk of insomnia. Statement of SignificanceThis large population-based study identified four distinct insomnia symptom trajectories across adolescence and a range of individual, social, and environmental predictors associated with different trajectories. Key risk factors, including marginalized identities, internalizing and externalizing symptoms, negative family interactions, adverse childhood experiences, and low school connectedness, were linked to persistent or increasing insomnia trajectories. These findings provide new evidence about the developmental course of insomnia symptoms during adolescence and point to several modifiable factors that could inform insomnia screening and intervention efforts. Future research is needed to determine whether altering these factors can shift insomnia symptom trajectories and improve long-term sleep outcomes for adolescents at risk of insomnia.

Introduction

Insomnia, characterized by difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, is a common sleep disorder in adolescence. Clinical insomnia is estimated to affect 10-13% of adolescents [1–3] with up to a third reporting subthreshold symptoms [4–7]. Insomnia is associated with numerous adverse outcomes for adolescents [8], including for mental health [3, 6, 9–11], physical health [4, 12–14], and school performance [15–17]. Accordingly, sleep health has been recognized as a global public health priority [18] with adolescents identified as an at-risk population [19].

The transition from childhood to adolescence is marked by increasing sleep difficulties linked to biological and psychosocial changes that occur during puberty [20]. Many adolescents will experience general sleep complaints during this period [21, 22], yet there is considerable variability in adolescent sleep disturbances, and a much smaller proportion will develop clinically significant insomnia [1]. This distinction is important, as general sleep complaints often lack the intensity and associated functional impairment that characterize insomnia [23]. As sleep patterns established during adolescence typically persist into adulthood [7, 24], understanding the developmental course of insomnia for different subgroups of adolescents could strengthen preventive efforts during this formative period.

Recently, research has examined trajectories of insomnia symptoms across the lifespan including age of symptom onset, persistence of symptoms, and subgroup variations [14, 25–29]. However, relatively few studies have examined trajectories of insomnia during adolescence [30] despite the increased risk of insomnia during this period. Collectively, these studies emphasize the variability in adolescents’ experiences of insomnia, identifying different trajectories with symptoms that persist, remit, or worsen over time. For example, a Swedish study identified four distinct trajectories of insomnia symptoms in a population cohort of 1294 adolescents from ages 13 to 16. Using latent class growth analysis (LCGA) to model trajectories based on total scores of the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [31], two of the identified trajectories showed increasing symptoms over time (22% of the sample), one trajectory showed remitting symptoms (9%), and a final trajectory showed consistently healthy sleep (69%) [5]. In contrast, a school-based Chinese study examined insomnia symptom trajectories in a sample of 910 adolescents between the seventh and ninth grades [4]. Also using ISI scores and LCGA, this study identified just two trajectory groups: one group with consistently healthy sleep (71.8%) and another group with persistent insomnia symptoms (28.2%). As these conflicting results may be driven by differences in sampling, measurement, or cultural context, further research is needed to bolster understanding of the progression of insomnia symptoms during adolescence, including from the Australian region, where no such studies have been conducted.

Furthermore, characterizing individual, social, and environmental risk factors for higher-risk trajectories may facilitate targeted insomnia screening and intervention. A recent review involving data for 9949 children and adolescents from five countries (United States, New Zealand, Norway, China, Japan), identified that the most commonly found risk factors for adolescent insomnia were female sex and poor mental health [32]. A range of other individual, social, and environmental factors have been implicated in adolescent sleep problems; including peer relationship difficulties [33, 34], poor family functioning [35, 36], adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) [37], bullying behavior and victimization [38, 39], school stress [5], evening screen use [40, 41], racial/ethnic minority status [14], socioeconomic and neighborhood disadvantage [42–44], diverse sexuality [45, 46], and physical, cognitive, or neurodevelopmental disabilities [47, 48]. Despite this extensive list, the evidence supporting these factors in the development of insomnia is inconclusive. Much of the research is drawn from cross-sectional studies, making it difficult to ascertain directionality in the associations [32], and many of these studies have examined risk factors associated with general sleep complaints rather than insomnia symptoms specifically [23].

Adolescents are subject to many influences from peers, school, home, and within their communities [49] which may contribute to the development and maintenance of clinical insomnia. Accordingly, the aim of the current study was to identify heterogeneous insomnia symptom trajectories in a large population cohort of adolescents during a developmentally sensitive window (ages 13–17 years), and to understand the influence of individual, social, and environmental factors on these different trajectories. Leveraging data from an Australian longitudinal cohort study of adolescent mental health (the Future Proofing Study) [50], we investigated a broad range of potential risk and protective factors for adolescent insomnia trajectories, selected based on the evidence outlined above linking these factors to sleep problems. Risk and protective factors were organized according to Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [51] which emphasizes that human development is shaped by the interaction between individuals and their surrounding environmental systems. We categorized predictors into individual-level factors, including gender identity, sexual orientation, internalizing and externalizing symptoms, diagnosed disabilities, recreational screen use, and maladaptive social media use; social-level (microsystem) factors, including positive and negative family interactions, school and peer relationships, and bullying victimization and behavior; and environmental-level (macro and exosystem) factors, including socioeconomic status (SES), cultural and linguistic diversity (CALD), ACEs, and geographic remoteness. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study to examine heterogeneous insomnia symptom trajectories in a population cohort of Australian adolescents during a critical developmental stage, and the first study that provides the opportunity to examine a broad range of potential risk and protective factors for distinct insomnia symptom trajectories.

Materials and Methods

Participants and procedures

Data were collected as part of the Future Proofing Study, an Australian school-based cohort study of adolescent mental health with an embedded randomized controlled trial of depression prevention [50]. As a null effect was found for the prevention program on all outcomes (depression, insomnia, anxiety, psychological distress) [52], participants in both the intervention and control conditions were included in the current study. Detailed information about study sampling, recruitment, and data collection methods are reported in the cohort profile [50]. Briefly, study recruitment was conducted via Australian secondary schools between 2019 and 2022, with all adolescents enrolled in year 8 at participating schools invited to take part. Informed consent was obtained from parents/carers and adolescents. Cohort demographics at baseline were broadly representative of the Australian adolescent population (for comparison of demographics see the cohort profile) [50]. Data were collected annually over four school years: year 8 (baseline, aged 13–14), year 9 (aged 14–15), year 10 (aged 15–16), and year 11 (aged 16–17) between 2019 and 2024. All data were collected during teaching periods of the Australian schooling year (spanning February to December). At each assessment, participants completed a battery of online questionnaires at school in sessions facilitated by trained research assistants. Adolescents were considered eligible for inclusion in the current study if they provided insomnia data on at least one of the four assessments. Overall, 6377 adolescents met eligibility criteria to be included in the analytic sample, with 6363 completing the year 8 assessment (99.8% of the eligible sample), 4812 completing the year 9 assessment (75.5%), 4117 completing the year 10 assessment (64.6%), and 3416 completing the year 11 assessment (53.6%). To determine patterns of participant attrition, a logistic regression analysis examined whether baseline insomnia, demographic characteristics (gender, SES, CALD background, sexual orientation, geographic remoteness), and other study variables (internalizing and externalizing symptoms, disability diagnoses, daily screen time, maladaptive social media use, positive and negative family interactions, school connectedness, bullying victimization and behavior, ACEs) predicted attrition at the final assessment (0 = attrition; 1 = retention). Adolescents who were male (Wald χ2(1) = 27.04, p < .001), had elevated externalizing symptoms (Wald χ2(1) = 51.91, p < .001), attended school in a regional or rural area (Wald χ2(1) = 10.25, p = .001), or reported lower positive (Wald χ2(1) = 9.55, p = .002) or negative family interactions (Wald χ2(1) = 8.91, p = .003) at baseline were more likely to drop out of the study at the final assessment.

Ethics approvals were obtained from the University of New South Wales (NSW) Human Research Ethics Committee (HC180836/iRECS4468), the State Education Research Applications Process for the NSW Department of Education (SERAP2019201), and relevant Catholic Schools Dioceses across Australia. The trial was registered with the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ACTRN12619000855123).

Measures

Participants completed measures of insomnia symptoms at all timepoints. All other measures used in the current study were collected at baseline. A complete description of all measures, including scoring and example items, can be found in Supplementary Material 1.

Insomnia

Symptoms of insomnia were assessed using the ISI, a psychometrically sound 7-item self-report measure of insomnia symptoms over the previous two weeks [31]. The ISI has been widely administered and validated in adolescent samples [53, 54]. Responses were reported on a Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very), producing total scores of 0 to 28. Although continuous scores were used in analyses, cutoff scores for interpreting insomnia levels were: 0–7 = no clinical insomnia, 8–14 = subthreshold insomnia, 15–28 = clinical insomnia [31, 54]. Internal consistency was high for all timepoints (year 8 α = .86; year 9 α = .87; year 10 α = .87; year 11 α = .86).

Individual-level factors

Individual-level factors were gender identity (male/female/gender diverse); sexual orientation (heterosexual/nonheterosexual); internalizing (emotional symptoms and peer relationship problems) and externalizing (conduct and hyperactivity problems) subscales [55] of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire [56, 57]; diagnosed disabilities (0/≥1 disability diagnoses); hours of daily recreational screen time (</>4 h); and a modified version of the Maladaptive Facebook Usage Scale [58].

Social-level factors

Social-level factors were positive family (i.e. greater levels of care and attention) and negative family (i.e. greater levels of demands, criticism, and arguments) interactions [59]; school connectedness [60]; bullying victimization (never or rarely/regularly bullied); and bullying behavior (has/has not bullied others).

Environmental-level factors

Environmental-level factors were an index of community-level SES [61]; self-reported CALD background (English/non-English speaking family); self-reported ACEs (0/≥1 ACEs) [62]; and school-level geographic remoteness (major cities/regional or rural areas) [63].

Data analysis

A two-step procedure was used to identify insomnia symptom trajectories. In step 1, a latent growth curve model was estimated to examine mean change in adolescents’ insomnia symptoms over time. School was included as a random effect in the model, reflecting the clustered sampling of students within schools. The intra-class correlation coefficient was small (.046), indicating that minimal heterogeneity in insomnia scores was attributable to school clustering. Consequently, random effects for school clustering were omitted from the remaining analyses. In step 2, LCGA was used to identify adolescents with different insomnia symptom trajectories. Intercept (initial level), linear changes, and quadratic changes in insomnia scores over the four timepoints were estimated in quadratic growth models that accounted for all available data for each timepoint. LCGA models include categorical latent variables derived from the data to identify groups of individuals (subpopulations or “classes”) based on their insomnia symptom trajectories [64, 65]. Models including one to six trajectory classes were estimated, and each model was fitted using at least 1000 random starting values to ensure replication of the best likelihood and to avoid local maxima. Model fit was compared using several criteria, including Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample size-adjusted BIC, Integrated Classification Likelihood (ICL) criteria, entropy, and mean posterior probabilities > .7 for participant class allocations [66]. To ensure the robustness of estimates, inclusion of at least 5% of the sample was required for each class, and high importance was placed on clinical and theoretical interpretability of the final class solution. LCGA was fit using all observations (n = 6377) under the assumption that missing data were missing at random [67]. A detailed description of our analytic approach including sensitivity testing for missing data can be found in Supplementary Material 2.

After the latent classes were characterized and extracted, individual, social, and environmental predictive factors associated with class membership (trajectories) were identified by multinomial logistic regression analysis. All predictors were included in the model in a single step as there was little evidence of multicollinearity, there were no a priori expectations about how to enter/cluster factors together, or about which factors might be of greater interest. Interaction terms were not included due to the number of predictors and complexity of interpretation. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted using cases with complete baseline data for individual, social, and environmental predictors, resulting in listwise deletion of n = 869 cases from the final model (n = 5508; 86% of overall sample). To assess potential bias due to missing data, complete cases were compared to those with any missing data on demographic (gender, SES, CALD background, geographic remoteness, sexuality) and outcome (insomnia trajectory class) variables. Effect sizes for all comparisons were in the weak to small range (Cramér’s V = 0.025–0.107), suggesting minimal bias due to missing data. In multinomial logistic regression analyses, the Benjamini–Hochberg (B-H) procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate at Q = 0.05. Only p-values less than or equal to their respective B-H thresholds were considered statistically significant.

Post hoc analyses explored univariate associations between each individual predictor and insomnia trajectory class to further probe the extent and direction of relationships (see Supplementary Material 3 for complete details).

Latent growth curve models and LCGA were conducted in R v4.3.2 with the lme4 and lcmm packages. Example R code can be found in Supplementary Material 4. Summary statistics and multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted in SPSS v30.

Insomnia symptom trajectories

The initial latent growth curve model showed statistically significant linear (p = .019) but not quadratic (p = .354) change in insomnia symptoms across the four measurement occasions, indicating modest linear growth in insomnia symptoms over time for the overall cohort (Supplementary Table S1). LCGA models with one to six classes were then fitted to examine whether subgroups of adolescents in the cohort followed different insomnia symptom trajectories from ages 13 to 17 years (for plots of all six models, see Supplementary Figure S1). The four-class model was selected as the best fitting solution as it showed higher entropy than the three- and five-class models, lower ICL criteria than the five-class model, and generated classes that identified changes in adolescent insomnia symptoms over time (Supplementary Tables S2–S4). Mean posterior probabilities were >.7 for all class allocations in the final model. The four identified classes were: low-stable insomnia (n = 3554; 55.73%) characterized by adolescents who consistently reported no clinical insomnia symptoms from years 8 to 11; increasing insomnia (n = 1753; 27.49%) characterized by adolescents who reported no clinical insomnia symptoms in year 8 that developed into subthreshold insomnia symptoms in years 9, 10, and 11; high-stable insomnia (n = 698; 10.95%) characterized by adolescents who consistently reported clinical insomnia symptoms from years 8 to 11; and high-decreasing insomnia (n = 372; 5.83%) characterized by adolescents who reported clinical insomnia symptoms in year 8, remitting to subthreshold symptoms in year 9 and no clinical symptoms in years 10 and 11. Comparison between high-decreasing and high-stable trajectories

As identification of the high-decreasing trajectory provided an opportunity to understand potential protective factors for adolescent insomnia, we conducted a post hoc multinomial logistic regression analysis with high-stable insomnia as the reference class to understand whether the characteristics of adolescents with a high-decreasing trajectory differed significantly from those with a high-stable trajectory. Adolescents had significantly lower odds of a high-decreasing trajectory (vs high-stable) if they were female (Wald χ2(1) = 14.933, p < .001, OR: 0.526, 95% CI = 0.380% to 0.729%) or gender diverse (Wald χ2(1) = 6.655, p = .01, OR: 0.491, 95% CI = 0.286% to 0.843%) compared to male.

Consideration of COVID-19-related school closures on insomnia symptom trajectories

By coincidence, data collection for the Future Proofing Study overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic between 2020 and 2021. While the majority of assessments took place whilst schools were open, a small proportion of years 8, 9, and 10 assessments were conducted during COVID-19-related school closures between July and November 2021 whilst students were learning from home. A total of 363 adolescents completed their study assessments during this period. Due to the staggered enrollment of schools into the study, the breakdown of participants impacted by school closures was: year 8, n = 67/6363 (1% of assessments); year 9, n = 185/4812 (4% of assessments); and year 10, n = 111/4117 (3% of assessments). Several studies have reported that the COVID-19 school closures in Australia had an impact on adolescents’ sleep. Some studies reported increases in subjective sleep disturbance [68] whereas others reported improvements in overall sleep duration [69, 70]. Given the possibility of changed adolescent sleep patterns during this period, we conducted an exploratory LCGA that included a binary covariate for COVID-19 school closure at the year 8, 9, or 10 assessment in a four-class model to investigate whether COVID-19 school closures impacted adolescents’ predicted insomnia symptom trajectories. We found no evidence to indicate that assessment completion during COVID-19 school closures influenced insomnia symptom trajectories in a four-class model (Estimate = 0.07, SE = 0.21, Wald = 0.35, p = .77).

Discussion

We identified four insomnia symptom trajectories between the ages of 13 and 17: one “low risk” group (low-stable trajectory), two “elevated risk” groups (increasing and high-stable trajectories), and one “remitting” group (high-decreasing trajectory). Most of the cohort (55.73%) followed the low-stable trajectory and reported healthy sleep patterns throughout the study. A sizable subgroup (27.49%) followed an increasing trajectory with symptoms that increased from low to subthreshold levels over the four years, potentially indicating emerging risk for insomnia disorder. A third subgroup followed a high-stable trajectory (10.95%) with consistently elevated symptoms indicative of clinically significant insomnia disorder. The final subgroup followed a high-decreasing trajectory (5.83%) reporting symptom levels at baseline comparable to the high-stable group which remitted to healthy sleep patterns by ages 15–17. While adolescents with high-decreasing trajectories showed symptom improvement across the study, severity of insomnia symptoms at age 13–14 indicates an increased risk for insomnia disorder relative to the general adolescent population.

The trajectories identified in our Australian cohort broadly align with international research which has shown that approximately one in three adolescents experience elevated insomnia symptoms, often consisting of smaller subgroups with persistent high symptoms, increasing symptoms, and remitting symptoms [5, 7, 25]. In the context of globally worsening adolescent sleep patterns [71–75] this study makes a significant contribution to the limited literature on the developmental course of insomnia symptoms during the adolescent years by identifying distinct subgroup trajectories using LCGA, a sophisticated longitudinal modeling approach. In line with previous research [32], female gender and elevated internalizing or externalizing symptoms were among the strongest predictors of a high-stable or increasing insomnia trajectory. Negative family interactions were consistently associated with elevated risk trajectories, whereas positive family interactions appeared to offer a protective effect, underlining the importance of the family and home environment on adolescent sleep. Other predictors of high-stable insomnia included being gender diverse, reporting one or more diagnosed disabilities, and bullying behavior; while an adolescent-onset (increasing) trajectory was associated with reporting one or more ACEs, lower school connectedness, and SES. While each of these factors have been separately implicated in adolescent sleep health [35–37, 47, 48, 76, 77], this study allowed for the examination of a range of potential risk and protective factors for adolescent insomnia in the same cohort, and highlights several modifiable factors that may inform insomnia screening and intervention efforts.

Individual-level predictors of insomnia symptom trajectories

We found that female adolescents had 2.4 times greater odds of a high-stable trajectory and 1.8 times greater odds of an increasing trajectory, relative to males. This accords with current evidence which shows that females experience higher rates of insomnia than males during adolescence and adulthood [78]. Female adolescents also report significantly greater impacts of sleep difficulties on daytime functioning, including greater impairments in school and social activities [79]. While gender differences in sleep disorders may be related to the hormonal changes that commence during puberty [80], another explanation could be the higher prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms in female adolescents [50] which are highly comorbid with insomnia [81], or social and cultural pressures that are unique to female adolescents [82]. Gender diverse adolescents also had 2.6 times greater odds of a high-stable trajectory than males. Research continues to show that gender diverse individuals face multiple health disparities compared to cisgender peers [45, 83], including a higher prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms [50] as well as sleep problems [46, 76], which are likely to be related to higher levels of minority stress faced by this group [84]. Given these vulnerabilities, adolescent sleep interventions could benefit from ensuring that materials use inclusive language regarding gender identities so that they appeal to diverse groups of young people and do not cause further stigmatization. More broadly, steps toward evidence-based policies and interventions that promote the inclusion and support of diverse gender identities from a young age may help to mitigate the adverse impact of minority stress on adolescent insomnia.

Odds of a high-stable, increasing, or high-decreasing trajectory were significantly greater for adolescents who reported higher levels of internalizing or externalizing symptoms. Comorbidity between internalizing symptoms and insomnia is well established [3, 8, 53, 85]. Bidirectional associations have also been found between externalizing symptoms and general sleep disturbances from late childhood to adolescence [86, 87], with childhood externalizing symptoms shown to predict adult insomnia [88], highlighting the importance of early emotional and behavioral interventions for sleep health.

Adolescents who reported one or more disability diagnoses had 1.6 times greater odds of a high-stable trajectory. This finding extends the current literature which indicates that individuals living with physical, cognitive, or neurodevelopmental disabilities experience a higher prevalence of sleep disturbance [47, 48, 89] and indicates a need for insomnia interventions that better support the sleep needs of adolescents living with disabilities and their families.

Counter to our expectations, we did not find evidence in multivariate models to indicate an association between higher daily screen time or higher levels of maladaptive social media use with any of the elevated insomnia symptom trajectories. Several large cross-sectional studies have found negative associations between greater night-time screen use and adolescent sleep [40, 90], however, longitudinal associations between screen use and social media on sleep outcomes remain unclear. A recent systematic review found that while problematic types of screen use (e.g. overuse, cyber-victimization) impacted sleep quality and duration, relationships between other types of screen use and sleep were uncertain [41]. Relationships between screen time, social media use, and sleep are likely to be complex, and further high-quality longitudinal research is needed to understand the direction and extent of these associations.

Social-level predictors of insomnia symptom trajectories

Our results showed that family interactions played an important role in all elevated insomnia symptom trajectories. Adolescents who reported family interactions with greater demands, criticism, and arguments showed significantly higher odds of a high-stable, increasing, or high-decreasing insomnia trajectory, whereas adolescents who reported family interactions with greater care and attention showed significantly reduced odds of a high-stable or high-decreasing trajectory, indicating a protective effect. Growing research links family and parenting factors to the development and regulation of sleep behaviors during adolescence [35]. Encouragingly, these factors may present useful intervention targets for addressing adolescent insomnia. A recent systematic and meta-analytic review identified several modifiable parenting factors that influenced adolescent sleep outcomes, including parental rule-setting for bedtimes, family functioning, and parental warmth [36]. These factors could be leveraged in parenting programs to support adolescent insomnia and evaluated to determine if they lead to improved sleep outcomes.

Further, we found that adolescents reporting higher levels of school connectedness, which refers to a sense of peer-group inclusion or belonging in the school context, had significantly lower odds of an increasing trajectory, indicating the protective value of school and peer relationships in adolescent-onset insomnia. These findings align with a large body of literature demonstrating bidirectional relationships between peer factors and adolescent sleep [34]. Our study extends these findings and suggests that peer factors may be particularly relevant for adolescent-onset insomnia and an important target for adolescent sleep interventions.

One unexpected finding was that adolescents who had bullied others in the past year showed significantly reduced odds of a high-stable insomnia trajectory. This is contrary to previous research, which indicates that both perpetrators and victims of bullying experience greater levels of sleep disturbance [38, 39, 91–93]. Moreover, we did not find evidence to indicate an association between regular bullying victimization and any of the elevated insomnia symptom trajectories, further deviating our findings from previous research. One explanation for this may be methodological as most studies in this area have been cross-sectional and examined general sleep complaints rather than insomnia. It is also possible that other variables in the model (e.g. internalizing symptoms) [94, 95] functioned as mediating factors or confounders, generating altered estimates. This was supported in univariate models (see Supplementary Table S5), which showed that, when examined in isolation, both perpetrators and victims of bullying had greater odds of all elevated insomnia trajectories, and indicates that associations between bullying behaviors and insomnia trajectories were influenced to some extent by other variables in the model. Closer investigation is needed to identify which factors mediate the relationships between bullying behavior, victimization, and adolescent insomnia over time.

Environmental-level predictors of insomnia symptom trajectories

Another unexpected finding was the association between higher SES and greater odds of an increasing or high-decreasing trajectory. Research consistently links lower SES with sleep disturbances [14, 42, 43] yet we did not find evidence to indicate an association between lower SES and any of the elevated insomnia symptom trajectories. It is possible that the community-level index of socioeconomic advantage/disadvantage used in this study was not sensitive to family-level variations in SES, or that there was not sufficient variation in SES in this cohort (i.e. 70.8% of the sample were in the top five deciles) to evaluate these associations. Nevertheless, large population studies have shown that neighborhood socioeconomic and built-environment characteristics (e.g. safety concerns, poor housing) are linked with increased risk of inadequate sleep [96] and insomnia [44] in adolescents. Given the complexity of these findings, future research is needed to more closely examine community- and family-level SES with insomnia symptom trajectories.

Finally, adolescents who reported exposure to ≥1 ACEs showed 1.3 times greater odds of an adolescent-onset (increasing) trajectory, relative to adolescents who had not experienced ACEs. This corresponds with previous research that demonstrates a cumulative effect of ACEs on adolescent insomnia symptoms that extend into adulthood [37]. While we did not find a statistically significant association between ACEs and increased odds of a high-stable trajectory, the association was in the expected direction, and other variables in the model (e.g. internalizing symptoms) [97] may have functioned as mediating factors, resulting in reduced estimates.

Consideration of the high-decreasing insomnia trajectory

The current study identified one subgroup of adolescents (high-decreasing trajectory) whose insomnia symptoms remitted over the study period, providing an opportunity to understand factors associated with symptom improvement. Consequently, we examined whether adolescents with high-decreasing insomnia symptoms differed significantly from those with high-stable symptoms. We found that male adolescents were more likely to have insomnia symptoms that remitted during adolescence, again underlining the importance of gender in insomnia. Interestingly, a recent longitudinal study modeled insomnia trajectories from childhood to early adulthood and found that 27% of childhood insomnia symptoms naturally remitted during the adolescent years [7]. As our baseline assessments took place at age 13–14, we were unable to explore participants’ childhood history of insomnia symptoms; however, it is possible that adolescents with high-decreasing trajectories are part of an expected trend of remitting childhood insomnia symptoms.

Clinical implications and recommendations

Insomnia was prevalent in our adolescent sample, with approximately 38% reporting high-stable or increasing insomnia symptoms between the ages of 13 and 17. Characteristics associated with high-stable or increasing insomnia trajectories included female or diverse gender, higher internalizing and externalizing symptoms, negative family interactions, diagnosed disabilities, and ACEs; while positive family interactions and higher school connectedness were associated with lower risk. Greater awareness of these characteristics could assist in targeting and tailoring screening, intervention, and preventive efforts for adolescent insomnia. Given the high prevalence of insomnia in our sample, routine screening for sleep disorders is recommended in primary care settings. This would represent a significant shift in Australian healthcare, where over half of health professionals are untrained in assessing sleep, and approximately one third report that they do not routinely screen for sleep disorders in pediatric patients, nor routinely recommend evidence-based treatments [98]. Cognitive behavioral therapy is an effective treatment for insomnia [99–101], with digital options available for youth (e.g. the Sleep Ninja® app in Australia) [102]. However, recent estimates indicate that only 30 full-time equivalent psychologists in Australia specialize in insomnia management [103]. Investment in establishing adequate clinical training in sleep health is paramount to addressing the significant personal, health, and educational burden of insomnia in youth. Moreover, as 17% of the sample were already reporting clinical insomnia symptoms by age 13–14 (i.e. adolescents with high-stable and high-decreasing trajectories), an optimal time to commence insomnia screening is likely to be in childhood, when symptoms first begin to emerge. With regard to adapting insomnia treatments to better target the familial and social influences associated with higher odds of elevated risk trajectories, insomnia interventions should consider including modules that support parents to address problematic family dynamics, as well as supporting adolescents to navigate challenges in school and peer relationships.

Strengths and limitations

This study has a number of strengths including the large and diverse adolescent sample assessed on four occasions, the administration of a validated insomnia measure at all timepoints to examine the progression of insomnia symptoms over time, and the examination of a broad range of individual, social, and environmental characteristics that may put adolescents at increased risk for clinical insomnia. Several limitations must also be considered. First, we did not include objectively measured indicators of insomnia. Although self-reported insomnia measures may be prone to reporting biases and do not objectively assess patterns of sleep activity, they are cost-effective, feasible for population studies, and necessary for assessing subjective aspects of insomnia such as sleepiness [104, 105]. Second, as an adolescent-specific cohort study, we did not have access to participant data prior to age 13–14. Given insomnia symptoms were already established for 17% of the cohort at baseline, we were unable to investigate predictors associated with symptom onset for these subgroups. Future studies should commence data collection earlier in life, ideally from birth, to more accurately map the developmental course of insomnia for those at highest risk. Third, some measurement occasions of the Future Proofing Study coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic which may have influenced usual adolescent sleep patterns [68–70]. While our exploratory analyses did not find a significant effect of the COVID-19 lockdowns on insomnia symptom trajectories, the potential impact of COVID-19 on insomnia outcomes cannot be ruled out. Fourth, due to the absence of such data in the Future Proofing Study, we were unable to include contextual information about adolescents’ school structures (e.g. school start times; distance traveled to school) with environmental-level predictors assessed in our models. Given that school structures play an important role in adolescents’ opportunity for sleep [106, 107], including these variables in future insomnia research would be beneficial. Finally, generalizability of our findings may be limited due to cultural differences between Australia and other countries (e.g. different school systems or adolescent social expectations), and a source of potential bias may have been introduced via the complete case approach in the multinomial logistic regression analyses. Although this analytical sample was relatively smaller (n = 5508), it retained 86% of the original cohort, was well powered to test the hypotheses, and showed no substantive differences from excluded cases, indicating any potential bias was minimal and unlikely to have affected the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study highlight the heterogeneity in insomnia symptom development in adolescents from ages 13 to 17 years. We identified four distinct insomnia trajectories and found that females, adolescents with elevated internalizing and/or externalizing symptoms, and those with higher negative family interactions had greater odds of elevated-risk insomnia trajectories. The overall prevalence of insomnia symptoms was considerable, with 11% of the cohort showing persistent clinical insomnia and 27% showing adolescent-onset increasing subthreshold symptoms, further supporting calls for adolescent sleep disturbance to be addressed as a public health priority. These findings inform future research about the developmental course of insomnia symptoms during adolescence and can be used to guide screening and intervention efforts aimed at improving sleep health outcomes for youth at risk of insomnia.
