Current evidence and challenges of multitarget anti-angiogenic agents for glioblastoma: Results from clinical trials
Authors: Xinliang Liu, Zhigang Chen, Xin Wang, Pengwei Yan, Dan Zong, Wenjie Guo, Xia He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlioblastoma (GBM) is a highly vascularized and aggressive brain tumor with a dismal prognosis. This systematic review critically appraises clinical evidence for multitarget anti-angiogenic agents, which inhibit pathways such as VEGFR, FGFR, and PDGFR. Most supporting data come from small, single-arm phase II trials or retrospective studies. Five challenges are highlighted: (i) translational shortcomings of current preclinical models, (ii) restriction of agent delivery by the blood-brain barrier (BBB), (iii) heterogeneity of tumor endothelial cells (TECs) driving intrinsic and adaptive resistance, (iv) angiogenic escape via vasculogenic mimicry (VM), and (v) a lack of consensus on late-line treatment for recurrent GBM (rGBM). Future research should prioritize robust biomarker development, brain-penetrant agents, strategies tailored to specific TEC subtypes, and disruption of VM to unlock the full therapeutic potential of anti-angiogenic agents in GBM. This review provides an evidence-based foundation and translational research guidance for clinicians and pharmaceutical researchers.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most frequent malignant adult glioma (approximately 50.9%). GBM is highly malignant, aggressive, and recurrent, with an extremely short lifespan. However, since 2005, only three new therapies (temozolomide (TMZ), bevacizumab (Bev), and tumor-treating fields) have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for GBM.1,2,3 The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of patients with GBM is only 13%.2 Hence, effective treatments for GBM remain an unmet need, making the exploration of new therapeutic drugs imperative.

The pathological features of significant vascularization in GBM indicate that tumor vessels can serve as important therapeutic targets. However, the treatment of GBM with single-target anti-angiogenic agents has limited efficacy. Since Bev (a monoclonal IgG1 antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A)) significantly improved short-term outcomes (progression-free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR)), the FDA accelerated its approval in 2009 as a treatment for recurrent GBM (rGBM).3 But several subsequent phase III studies did not confirm the long-term survival benefit of Bev.4 Unfortunately, aflibercept, a novel recombinant antibody targeting a VEGF-A/B ligand, has achieved similar results in treating patients with rGBM, with a 6-month PFS rate (PFS6) of 7.7% and a median PFS of 3 months5 One reason for these repeated failures may involve redundant angiogenesis signaling pathways and compensatory activation of parallel pathways, such as fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR)/FGF, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)/PDGF, and ANG/Tie pathway.6

Thus, multitarget anti-angiogenic agents could serve as promising treatment options for GBM. Moreover, compared with developing a new, expensive drug, repurposing already approved drugs may be more beneficial for patients.7 Indeed, investigators have attempted to use multitarget anti-angiogenic agents that have been successful in other cancer types to treat patients with GBM in clinical trials. However, limited efficacy is exhibited by most multitarget anti-angiogenic agents for GBM. This disappointing performance highlights an urgent need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying resistance and to refine therapeutic strategies that can overcome the complex angiogenic network of GBM.

In summary, this article aims to systematically review the current evidence and challenges associated with the use of multitarget anti-angiogenic agents for the treatment of patients with GBM, providing an evidence-based basis and translational research directions for medical practitioners and drug developers.

Recent in vitro studies support the use of anlotinib in combination with radiotherapy or TMZ in GBM.9 Through the JAK2/STAT3/VEGFA signaling pathway, anlotinib induces G2/M phase arrest in glioblastoma cells (GCs), triggering apoptosis and autophagy, which in turn inhibits GCs' proliferation, migration, and invasion.9,10 Moreover, it also enhances the sensitivity of GCs to radiation.9 Radiation disrupts the BBB, increasing the concentration of anlotinib in the brain.9 Additionally, TMZ can enhance the anti-GBM effect of anlotinib.10 Collectively, the promising results demonstrated by anlotinib in preclinical studies have facilitated its translational advancement into clinical applications.

Clinically, Lv et al. first reported a case of rGBM treated with anlotinib, achieving a PFS of approximately 2 months11 Subsequently, numerous clinical trials have been conducted to assess the efficacy of anlotinib in improving outcomes for patients with GBM. The exploration of anlotinib for treating GBM has focused mainly on rGBM, with China leading the way in this field, as most of the researchers involved are from China (see Table 2). The vast majority of studies are small-sample, retrospective studies. One phase II study included patients with GBM who experienced their first progression after standard treatment and who received anlotinib combined with TMZ.12 In terms of both response rates and survival outcomes, this study significantly outperformed EORTC 261014.12 No Grade 4–5 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were observed, and the incidence of Grade 3 AEs was 23.8%.12 Several other studies have yielded similar results.8,13,14 However, when anlotinib is compared with Bev, a few points need to be noted. First, there is a lack of head-to-head phase III studies comparing anlotinib with Bev for rGBM. Second, unlike Bev,15,16 most of the anlotinib studies included patients with GBM who were experiencing their first recurrence.12,13 Finally, with advancements in GBM diagnosis and treatment techniques, it is not appropriate to compare the current anlotinib study results with those of the Bev studies from five years ago. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the efficacy and safety of anlotinib are superior to those of Bev in patients with rGBM. Since local recurrence is the predominant pattern of treatment failure in GBM, investigations incorporating radiotherapy with anlotinib therapy have been conducted. Guan et al. conducted two consecutive studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of CyberKnife radiosurgery (25 Gy/5 f) combined with anlotinib in patients with rGBM.13,17 This combined regimen demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety profiles, achieving a median overall survival (mOS) of 19.5 months and an ORR of 95.5% (n = 22); no Grade 4–5 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were observed.13,17 The promising outcomes observed in these two studies may be attributable to two factors. First, a potential synergistic effect exists between radiation and anlotinib. This survival advantage has also been documented in patients receiving combined radiotherapy and bevacizumab therapy.18,19 Guan’s research team is currently conducting a phase II study comparing anlotinib + radiosurgery versus Bev + radiosurgery in patients with rGBM. The administration of anlotinib combined with TMZ and radiotherapy may provide more clinical benefit to patients with nGBM. A phase II study (GBM-AS, NCT04119674) conducted by Lai et al. first reported the use of anlotinib (8 mg/day) combined with the Stupp regimen as a first-line treatment for patients with nGBM.25 This study included 33 patients with nGBM, with mPFS and mOS values of 10.9 months and 17.4 months, respectively.25 Building upon these findings, a randomized phase II clinical trial (ALTN-II-01, NCT04959500) was conducted by Chen et al.26 The results of the ALTN-II-01 study (the study design see Figure 3) were presented as an oral presentation and designated a late-breaking abstract at the 2025 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Annual Meeting. The results demonstrated that anlotinib (10 mg/day) combined with the STUPP regimen yielded significantly superior mPFS outcomes compared to the placebo control group (mPFS: 9.89 vs. 5.85 months, p = 0.0043).26 This finding was consistent with the results from two phase III trials (AVAglio28 and RTOG 082529) assessing the addition of Bev to the STUPP regimen for nGBM. The OS data from the ALTN-Ⅱ-01 study remain immature, with additional follow-up being warranted to assess long-term survival outcomes. In contrast, the PFS benefit derived from Bev has not been translated into OS extension.30 Notably, mPFS in the control groups was observed to be at comparatively low levels across the above three trials. Additionally, the dosing regimens of anlotinib differed between the GBM-AS and ALTN-II-01 trials. Since a 12 mg starting dose has been established across all approved oncologic indications, dedicated dose-finding studies are warranted to determine the optimal therapeutic dose for combination therapy regimens.Figure 3Study design of the ALTN-Ⅱ-01 trialIt shows patient inclusion criteria, randomization (1:1) into two groups with different treatment regimens, and the primary and secondary endpoints of the study.

The identification and exploration of potential prognostic biomarkers for anlotinib constitute a prerequisite for patient stratification and subsequent therapeutic personalization. The subgroup analysis of the ALTN-Ⅱ-01 study revealed that patients with MGMT promoter methylation derived a significantly greater survival benefit from the anlotinib combination therapy (mOS: >36 months vs 17.97 months).26 Another phase II trial enrolled 32 patients with nGBM with unmethylated MGMT promoter, who received radiotherapy with concomitant and adjuvant anlotinib after surgical resection. The results demonstrated an mPFS of 10.5 months, while mOS remained immature.23 Anlotinib efficacy appears correlated with MGMT promoter methylation status. This phenomenon may arise from synergistic interactions between impaired DNA damage repair mechanisms and Anlotinib-induced replication stress. Additionally, several case studies have reported that patients with GBM with specific gene mutations, such as the FGFR3-TACC3 fusion and FGFR3 S249C mutation, can benefit from anlotinib treatment.31,32 In contrast, mutations in HEG1 and RP1L1 are poor prognostic factors for PFS.25 Before these molecular biomarkers can be used to guide the clinical use of anlotinib, it is necessary to validate them through prospective clinical studies. An ongoing phase II clinical trial (NCT04004975) is enrolling patients with rGBM who harbor mutations in VEGFR, KIT, PDGFR, or FGFR genes, as identified through next-generation sequencing. The results of this study will provide more reference for future patient selection.

Cediranib

Cediranib is an efficient pan-VEGFR TKI (IC50: 0.5 nmol/L). Compared with Bev, cediranib has high oral bioavailability; owing to its short half-life, the dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) of cediranib is easy to manage.33 Preclinical studies have confirmed that cediranib has antitumor and anti-angiogenic effects and increases the sensitivity of GCs to TMZ.34,35 Cediranib alleviates brain edema by reducing vascular permeability and diameter, thus extending the survival of mice in an orthotopic glioblastoma model, even though the tumor continues to grow.36 Additionally, cediranib inhibits lymphangiogenesis by blocking VEGF-C/VEGFR-3.37 VEGF-C/VEGFR-3 is associated with tumor lymph node metastasis, but its role in GBM needs further investigation.37

Batchelor et al. have conducted several phase II/III clinical studies to explore whether cediranib can be used to treat GBM. Interestingly, cediranib improved PFS in patients with nGBM but not in those with rGBM. The phase III REGAL study compared cediranib monotherapy, cediranib combined with lomustine (LOM), or LOM alone in patients with Rgbm.38 The results indicated that cediranib reduced corticosteroid use and delayed the deterioration of neurological status but did not improve survival.38 Recently, a phase II NRG/RTOG 0837 trial randomly assigned 137 patients with nGBM to receive either cediranib or a placebo.39 The findings showed that patients in the cediranib group had significantly greater PFS6 and mPFS than did those in the control group.39 However, there are several points of concern in the NRG/RTOG 0837 study: 1) The mPFS of the control group was significantly lower than that of historical phase III study controls (6.2–7.3 months).28 2) The primary endpoint of NRG/RTOG 0837 was PFS6; is this design reasonable for nGBM? First, similar to Bev, the vascular normalization effect of cediranib may also influence MRI evaluations. Pinho et al. found that cediranib significantly reduced the incidence of pseudoprogression.40 Second, this study used the MacDonald criteria for efficacy evaluation instead of the more reasonable RANO criteria. 3) Can the toxicity of cediranib treatment be accepted in clinical practice? Approximately 4 out of the 5 patients in the cediranib group experienced serious adverse events, which was higher than that reported by the control group (16.4%). In the cediranib group, 11 patients (12%) died, 5 of whom died from cediranib treatment.39

Combining cediranib with other targeted drugs for GBM also lacks efficacy (see Table 3). Compared with single agent treatment, cediranib combined with MEDI3617 (an angiopoietin-2 inhibitor) improved survival rates in mouse models of GBM.41 However, clinical studies have failed to replicate these preclinical results. Regorafenib (REGO)

REGO is an oral small-molecule multikinase inhibitor that has been approved for the treatment of various cancers, such as hepatocellular carcinoma (2nd-line, RESORCE study), colorectal cancer (3rd-line, CONCUR and CORRECT studies), and gastrointestinal stromal tumors (3rd-line, GRID study).

REGO targets signaling pathways involved in angiogenesis, tumorigenesis, and the tumor microenvironment in GBM. Preclinical studies have shown that REGO significantly improves vascular permeability, as demonstrated by a 25% reduction in the extravasation of the gadomer.47 Additionally, REGO directly inhibits the growth of GCs in a dose-dependent manner.47 The potential mechanism is that REGO prevents autophagy in GCs by stabilizing phosphoserine aminotransferase 1.48 Recently, REGO was reported to significantly reduce the transdifferentiation of GCs into tumor-derived endothelial cells (TDECs), thereby inhibiting tumor formation.49 Furthermore, Morelli et al. evaluated the drug response via an in vitro three-dimensional organoid model and reported that rGBM responded better to REGO than to TMZ.50 When combined with other treatments, REGO can restore GCs' sensitivity to TMZ by inhibiting the CXCL12/CXCR4/ERK/NF-κB signaling pathway.51

Regorafenib for the treatment of GBM

The multicenter, randomized controlled phase II REGOMA study was the first to compare the efficacy and safety of REGO versus LOM for treating rGBM. This study is one of the few to achieve positive survival outcomes in clinical research. The mOS in the REGO group was significantly greater than that in the LOM group (7.4 vs. 5.6 months, p = 0.0009).52 As a result, REGO was subsequently approved by the Italian Agency of Medicine (2019) and adopted in the NCCN guidelines (2020) for treating Rgbm.56 However, the reliability and representativeness of these study results have been widely questioned. First, the mOS for both groups was low, particularly the LOM group’s survival time, which was significantly lower than that of the control group in previous phase III studies (mOS: 8–9.8 months). Weller et al. attributed this to the poor prognosis of patients included in the REGOMA study.66 Second, there was an imbalance in baseline characteristics between the two groups, despite being a randomized study. The REGO group had more positive prognostic factors, such as a lower corticosteroid use rate, IDH1 mutations, and MGMT promoter methylation. Third, the REGOMA study did not conduct independent central radiological or pathological molecular reviews. Owing to differences in technical levels, the consistency rate between local and central reviews is poor.39 Fourth, the study excluded patients who used strong CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers, as REGO is metabolized by CYP3A4, such as antiepileptic drugs. Fifth, although the health-related quality of life reported by patients was similar between the two groups,67 the incidence of G3/4 adverse events (AEs) was higher in the REGO group than in the control group (16%). Owing to severe toxic reactions, the REGO dosage was reduced in 17% of patients. In fact, low-intensity regimens show similar efficacy to standard-dose treatments and a lower incidence of G3/4 Aes.58 This forces us to reconsider the dosing of REGO to balance the quality of life and survival. Sixth, the BBB penetration capacity of REGO appears limited, given REGO’s high protein binding rate and the distribution of some targets in GCs.68,69 Zeiner et al. reported that the concentrations of REGO and its metabolites in cerebrospinal fluid were significantly lower than those in serum but sufficient to inhibit most REGO targets.68 However, REGO failed to reach effective concentrations during treatment intervals.68 Seventh, several small-sample real-world studies have shown unoptimistic results,56,58,60 possibly because 1) these studies included late-stage patients with multiple recurrences (≥2)60 and 2) there were differences in the intervals for efficacy evaluation. Considering that the mPFS is 2–3 months,58,60 should we adjust the interval for evaluating efficacy, and what would be the best interval? 3) The patient’s physical condition is worse.59

Recently, the prospective observational REGOMA-OSS study included 190 patients with rGBM from 30 cancer centers, further confirming the REGOMA results.64 However, the results of the phase II/III Bayesian adaptive platform GBM AGILE trial indicated that REGO treatment for rGBM did not improve OS compared with LOM.70 Conducting randomized controlled phase III clinical trials is one of the most effective ways to resolve these controversies.

The REGOMA-2 phase 1 trial (NCT06095375) established the maximum tolerated dose of REGO as 160 mg during maintenance therapy and 120 mg during concurrent chemoradiotherapy in patients with MGMT-methylated IDHwt nGBM, with observed weak pharmacokinetic interactions between the agents.71 This study established the dosing foundation for clinical investigations of REGO combined with the Stupp regimen, thereby significantly facilitating multicenter phase II/III trials of REGO in nGBM.

These studies also identify predictive biomarkers for the efficacy of REGO, including clinical characteristics (age,58,63 HFR;52,53 and steroid use56), molecular factors (fT3/fT4 ratio62), and genetic markers (MGMT methylation and IDH mutations54,58,60). However, contradictory results have emerged from different studies.54,58,60,63 Large-scale studies to prospectively validate these biomarkers are necessary. Additionally, similar to Bev, REGO treatment results in two patterns of MRI recurrence.68 However, unlike patients with Bev, patients with reduced contrast enhancement after REGO treatment have a longer survival period.68 This difference may be related to the fact that REGO’s anti-edema effect is not as strong as that of Bev.56

Sunitinib

Sunitinib is a small-molecule TKI with antitumor and anti-angiogenic activity. Preclinical in situ models have confirmed that sunitinib can prolong mouse survival.72

Currently, all clinical trials of single-agent sunitinib for rGBM have ended in failure (see Table 5), whether in pediatric or adult patients or with high-dose intermittent regimens. Neyns et al. were the first to report the results of a clinical trial of sunitinib for treating Rhgg.73 The results showed a survival of less than 4 months, with no radiological responses observed in patients.73 Subsequent studies reported an mOS of approximately 9–13 months, similar to Bev treatment for rGBM; But radiological responses were rarely observed (≤10%), and the mPFS rarely exceeded 2 months74,75 Similar to that of primary brain tumors, the ORR for sunitinib in patients with brain metastases is only 0–12%.76 However, a few subgroups of patients might benefit from sunitinib treatment. The efficacy did not meet expectations, and possible reasons include the following: i) Patients enrolled had poor prognoses. In Neyn’s study, 61.9% of patients had a Karnofsky performance status (KPS) ranging from 70% to 60%, and 57.1% experienced a second recurrence.73 However, the patients in this study were relatively young, with a median age of 43 years73 In addition, the study by Janssen and colleagues included patients who progressed shortly after enrollment.75 ii) Sunitinib may have poor activity against GCs, or GCs may have inherent resistance to sunitinib. The IC50 of sunitinib in GCs is approximately twice that in other tumor cell lines.75 iii) Sunitinib has poor permeability across the BBB, or the efflux of BBB transport proteins leads to limited accumulation within the tumor. Phase I studies indicated that the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of sunitinib combined with Iri for rHGG was 50 mg/m2; the DLT was mainly hematological, which differs from that of Bev.78 On this basis, the commonly used dosing regimens for sunitinib are 50 mg/day or 37.5 mg/day, either 4 weeks on/6 weeks off or continuous daily dosing.77,79 However, studies in rabbit models revealed that the proportion of sunitinib (25 mg, PO) penetrating the BBB into the cerebrospinal fluid was less than 5%.82 Recently, a study analyzed the concentration of sunitinib in postoperative tumor samples from patients with nGBM treated with sunitinib (50 mg/day, PO, for 2 weeks).83 The results showed that the concentration in the tumor (median 1.9 μmol/L) was 10 times higher than that in the plasma, but did not reach the IC50 value in GCs (median 5.4 μmol/L).84 The concentration of sunitinib used in other tumors is five times greater than that used in GBM, which explains the differences in treatment responses and reflects the drug barrier effect of the BBB.83 A pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics meta-analysis found that exposure to sunitinib was positively correlated with prolonged survival and radiological response.85 On the basis of this, the II/III phase of the STELLAR trial evaluated the feasibility of high-dose intermittent sunitinib (300 mg, Q1W; or 700 mg, Q2W) for treating Rgbm.75 The results showed that high-dose intermittent sunitinib was ineffective compared with lomustine (LOM).75 Additionally, Tang and colleagues confirmed that BBB transport proteins significantly affect the intracranial accumulation of sunitinib.86 When genes related to transport proteins were knocked out or transport protein inhibitors were used, the intracranial accumulation of sunitinib increased 22-fold.86

Owing to the presence of the BBB, intranasal delivery (IN) might bypass the BBB. Recently, Seidkhani and colleagues reported that IN treatment with sunitinib in GBM rats resulted in tumor shrinkage (vs. IN placebo) and good safety (vs. sunitinib oral delivery).87 The rats gained weight, and no liver toxicity was observed.87 However, the tumor volume in GBM after IN was significantly larger than that after oral delivery; the study did not assess the drug concentrations within the tumor before and after administration or between different delivery routes.88 Therefore, whether IN can replace oral delivery is debatable.

Does combining sunitinib with radiotherapy or chemotherapy improve treatment efficacy? Currently, very few studies have reported results on the use of sunitinib in combination with nGBM. A phase II study (NCT02928575) is ongoing to clarify the efficacy and safety of sunitinib combined with the STUPP regimen in patients with nonmethylated MGMT promoter nGBM. The results of this study provide more evidence regarding the use of sunitinib for the treatment of nGBM.

Vandetanib

Vandetanib is an oral low-molecular-weight highly selective TKI.89 In addition to VEGFRs, vandetanib also inhibits EGFR/HER1 and is rearranged during the transfection (RET) of kinases.90 In 2011, vandetanib was approved by the FDA for the treatment of MTC.91 Half of the patients with GBM had EGFR amplification.92 The EGFR signaling pathway plays an important role in tumor development.90 Studies using animal models of glioma have shown that Vandetanib reduces tumor size by more than 50%, whether it is combined with RT or TMZ.93

Vandetanib has good safety. Phase I clinical studies indicate that Vandetanib (100 mg/day) combined with RT and TMZ is safe for treating Ngbm.90 Another phase I study found that when Vandetanib (200 mg/day) was combined with sirolimus (a mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor) for rGBM, the DLT was increased in alanine aminotransferase.94

The clinical efficacy of vandetanib is poor, and further research is not recommended.95 In a phase I study, the PFS6 for treating rGBM with vandetanib was 15.8%.94 A noncomparative phase II study randomly assigned 114 patients in a 2:1 ratio to receive either RT or TMZ with or without vandetanib.95 Compared with the control, the addition of vandetanib did not improve survival (mOS: 16.6 vs. 15.9 months; mPFS: 7.7 vs. 6.2 months) or the ORR (25.4% vs. 17.9%), leading to the premature termination of the study.95 However, this study does not appear sufficient to preclude the role of vandetanib in treating GBM. 1) When comparing therapeutic efficacy between groups, noncomparative study designs must be considered methodologically suboptimal. Implementing comparative study methodologies may yield more reliable conclusions. 2) Regarding the low efficacy of vandetanib in treating GBM, potential contributing factors include the lower dose used, limited inherent activity, or insufficient penetration of the BBB. The dose of vandetanib used during combined radiochemotherapy was 100 mg/day rather than 300 mg/day when used alone.95 Lee et al. confirmed that vandetanib (100 mg/day) has a weaker effect on VEGFR2 inhibition by comparing plasma biomarker levels during treatment with different VEGFR TKIs.95 In fact, even at a dosage of 300 mg/day, vandetanib still failed to improve survival in patients with rGBM, with a PFS6 of only 6.5%.96

Sorafenib

Two phase I/II studies in which sorafenib (Sb) was added to first-line treatment revealed that this approach did not improve the efficacy of nGBM treatment.97,98 However, these studies provide some references for future research: 1) Sb combined with TMZ is well tolerated, and Sb does not interfere with TMZ metabolism; 2) the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of Sb is 200 mg BID.97,98 Notably, Hottinger and others did not consider the use of enzyme-inducing antiepileptic drugs (EIAEDs) when studying MTD and DLT.98 Reardon and colleagues found that patients taking EIAEDs had significantly lower pharmacokinetic exposure to Sb than patients with non-EIAED did.99

For second-line treatment, several studies have combined Sb with chemotherapy or targeted therapies for rGBM, including temozolomide,100,101 temsirolimus,102,103 Bev,104 erlotinib,105 and tipifarnib.106 These studies were either prematurely terminated owing to excessive toxicity or failed to meet efficacy expectations. Owing to the low to moderate permeability of Sb across the BBB, its ability to treat GBM is extremely limited, even at maximum tolerated doses.103 Additionally, potential redundant pathways may be one reason for the failure of Sb combination therapies.102 Recently, the intranasal sorafenib-loaded poloxamer-carrageenan nanoemulgel (SPCNEG) exhibited 3.41- to 8.12-fold enhanced drug release, potent glioma cytotoxicity (IC50 2.23 μg/mL vs. 16.61 μg/mL), and 3.24-fold increased brain bioavailability over conventional therapy, offering a non-invasive GBM treatment strategy with optimized brain targeting.107

Enzastaurin

Enzastaurin is an oral serine‒threonine kinase inhibitor that blocks tumor growth and angiogenesis signaling pathways by inhibiting protein kinase C-β (reviewed by Bourhill et al.108). Enzastaurin has been widely studied for treating solid and hematologic tumors, including GBM, lymphoma, and lung cancer.

Phase I studies showed that patients with GBM tolerate enzastaurin well. When combined with RT and TMZ for nGBM, the recommended dose of enzastaurin is 250 mg/day.109 For rGBM in combination with TMZ, the recommended dose is 500 mg/day.110 Kreisl et al. found that increasing the dose from 525 mg/day to 900 mg/day did not increase the serum drug concentration, and the MTD was not reached.110 Moreover, TMZ does not affect the metabolism of enzastaurin.110 Notably, studies have shown that enzastaurin can prolong the QT interval.111

The results of phase II/III studies indicate that enzastaurin has limited antitumor activity. An open-label phase III clinical trial compared enzastaurin with LOM in patients with rGBM. Interim analysis showed no differences in mPFS (1.5 vs. 1.6 months), mOS (6.6 vs. 7.1 months), or ORR (2.9% vs. 4.3%).112 Therefore, the study was terminated early.112

Some researchers believe that combining enzastaurin with other treatments might be more effective.112 In reality, the efficacy of enzastaurin in combination therapies is also disappointing. A phase II trial investigated the use of enzastaurin combined with RT in patients with nonmethylated nGBM harboring the MGMT promoter.113 The results revealed PFS at 6 months and mOS rates of 53.6% and 15.0 months, respectively.113 However, this study did not meet its prespecified hypothesis, which was PFS at 6months ≥55%.113 Another trial revealed that combining Bev with or without enzastaurin had similar efficacy for treating recurrent malignant gliomas.114

Pazopanib

Pazopanib is a second-generation multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets VEGFR 1–3, PDGFR α/β, and c-Kit.115 It has shown single-agent antitumor activity and is approved for the treatment of metastatic renal cell carcinoma and soft tissue sarcoma.115

There are very few clinical studies on the use of pazopanib for treating GBM. This may be because early studies confirmed that pazopanib has limited activity against GBM. An early phase II study evaluated pazopanib as a monotherapy in patients with rGBM. The results revealed that the PFS6, mPFS, and mOS were 3%, 12, and 35 weeks, respectively.115 However, nearly one-third of patients show a reduction in contrast enhancement on MRI.115 The efficacy of pazopanib combined with other targeted therapies, such as lapatinib (an ErbB inhibitor)116 and dabrafenib (a BRAF inhibitor), is also unsatisfactory.117

The 2023 ASCO Annual Meeting reported safety results from an ongoing phase I/II Pazoglio trial (NCT02331498).118 The Pazoglio trial aims to provide evidence for maintaining pazopanib combined with TMZ treatment in patients with nGBM.118 The phase I results indicated that the regimen is safe and feasible, with no grade 4-5 adverse events observed.118 Phase II is currently recruiting, and its results are eagerly anticipated.118

Nintedanib

Nintedanib is an oral, small-molecule, triple angiokinase inhibitor approved for the treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and advanced/recurrent lung adenocarcinoma.119

A phase II study first assessed the feasibility of nintedanib for treating the first recurrence of GBM (second-line) or recurrence after Bev treatment failure (third-line).120 The primary endpoint of the study was the ORR.120 No grade 3-4 adverse events or radiographic response patients were observed, with mPFS and mOS of 1 and 6 months, respectively.120 This study was prematurely terminated because of the ineffectiveness of the treatment regimen.120 Interestingly, 12% of the patients with rGBM in the present study were assessed as having stable disease (SD), and one SD patient had disease control for at least 17 months120 Therefore, the authors suggested considering PFS or SD as endpoints. However, it remains uncertain whether these alternative endpoints can predict OS. Another study reached similar conclusions, regardless of whether prior treatment included Bev.121 A search at clinicaltrials.gov revealed no ongoing clinical studies.

Axitinib

Axitinib is an oral, highly potent second-generation TKI that targets VEGFR1-3 and has been approved for the treatment of advanced renal cell carcinoma. The efficacy of axitinib in inhibiting VEGFR is approximately 50–450 times greater than that of first-generation TKIs. Preclinical studies have shown that axitinib significantly extends the survival of mice with orthotopic xenografts via mechanisms such as killing endothelial cells,122 and inhibiting neovascularization,123 directly inhibiting human GBM stem cells, and reversing the tumor-suppressive immune microenvironment.124,125

Currently, there are only two clinical studies on axitinib for treating GBM (NCT03291314 [GliAvAx trial]126 and NCT01562197 [AxiGII trial]127,128), both involving patients with rGBM. The noncomparative phase II AxiGII study randomly assigned patients with rGBM into three arms: receiving best supportive care (Control-arm, n = 22), axitinib monotherapy (AXI-arm, n = 50), or axitinib combined with LOM (AXILOM-arm, n = 29).127,128 The efficacy of axitinib monotherapy for rGBM was similar to that of best supportive care with LOM or Bev.128 The addition of LOM did not improve oncological outcomes for patients with rGBM but rather increased hematological toxicity.127 Another GliAvAx study reported the successful combination of axitinib with avelumab (a PD-L1 monoclonal antibody) for the treatment of patients with metastatic renal cancer and applied this regimen to rGBM treatment.126 The primary endpoint PFS6 was 18.5–22.2%, which did not reach the preset threshold of 50%.126 Additionally, there are currently no related studies on the use of axitinib for treating GBM.

Apatinib

Other anti-angiogenic agents

Endostatins

Recombinant human endostatin (rh-ES) is an anti-angiogenic protein derived from type XVIII collagen. The antitumor activity of rh-ES has been widely reported in various cancers, such as non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma.

Research on the use of rh-ES for treating GBM has been in the basic research phase. Preclinical studies have shown that endostatin can reduce tumor volume and prolong survival in glioma animal models; some new delivery methods, such as microspheres, plasmid arterial administration, or local brain infusion, have also been explored.137,138,139,140,141 Ratel and others discovered endostatin antibodies in the serum and tumor brain tissue of patients with GBM, suggesting that conventional delivery methods may need improvement to avoid triggering a humoral immune response.142 In addition to inhibiting neovascularization, endostatin can directly inhibit T-type Ca2+ channels in GCs, exerting antitumor effects.143

Currently, clinical research on the use of rh-ES for treating GBM is in the very early stages, with only a few clinical studies reported. One retrospective study included 30 adult patients with recurrent disseminated glioblastoma who received combination treatment with TMZ, Iri, or rh-ES.144 The results revealed 6-month PFS and radiological response rates of 23.3% and 20%, respectively; the mPFS and mOS were 3.2 months and 6.9 months, respectively.144 For this group of patients with extremely poor prognoses, combination treatment with rh-ES significantly extended the survival time and had manageable toxicity.144 When comparing the results of this study with those of other anti-angiogenic treatments, such as Bev, caution is warranted, as few clinical studies on rGBM have reported on tumor dissemination. More importantly, the good efficacy of rh-ES in combination therapy for GBM must be further validated through large-scale prospective clinical studies. In fact, on the basis of the aforementioned retrospective study, a phase II clinical trial (NCT04267978) aimed at prospectively clarifying the efficacy and safety of the combination treatment of TMZ, Iri, and rh-ES in patients with rGBM is ongoing.

Challenges

To date, clinical trials using multitarget anti-angiogenic drugs approved for treating other cancers in GBM have repeatedly failed. Clearly, this simple, mechanical “replication” strategy faces numerous challenges, which will be discussed below.

Limitations of preclinical models

Laboratory findings are often poor predictors of the clinical efficacy of anti-angiogenic drugs.145 These findings indicate that preclinical models (including cancer cell lines and animal models) have limitations. First, cancer cell lines that have undergone multiple passages exhibit biological phenotypes and genetic characteristics that differ significantly from those of the corresponding primary human tumors.145 Second, the xenograft tumors used in animal models to assess antitumor drugs initially lack a tumor vascular ecology.115 Therefore, these tumors must form new blood vessels. In contrast, human primary brain tumors typically have lower angiogenesis demands due to prior vascularization.115 Finally, interspecies differences in the BBB may exist.146

In summary, the basic research results of a drug are among the key criteria for determining whether it is necessary to enter the clinical trial stage. The construction of preclinical models that reliably simulate human GBM and the BBB requires interdisciplinary collaboration. An increasing number of in vitro GBM models are being designed to incorporate vascularization, including spherical, 3D bioprinted, and microfluidic-based systems. These models more accurately recapitulate vascularized tumor tissue, such as its structural, mechanical, and biochemical characteristics, and possess advantages including reproducibility and mass production, thereby accelerating the clinical translation of anti-angiogenic therapies (AATs) and facilitating the investigation of resistance mechanisms.147

Blood–brain barrier (BBB)

Unlike other cancers, clinical trials for brain gliomas need to consider whether multitarget anti-angiogenic agents can effectively cross the BBB to maintain a good distribution volume within tumor tissue. Notably, the vasculature of brain metastases exhibits significantly higher permeability than that of primary gliomas. Evidence from a recent study indicated that brain metastases exhibit the highest levels of extravascular fibrinogen and collagen-IV—established biomarkers of vascular leakage—followed by GBM.148 Furthermore, patients with renal cell carcinoma brain metastases receiving the same agents have a poorer prognosis than those with extracranial metastases.149 These findings imply that, in addition to intrinsic properties of GBM, the BBB significantly influences treatment efficacy.

Currently, there are two differing views on the relationship between the BBB integrity and GBM treatment. 1) Restoration of pathological BBB function is beneficial for GBM treatment, a view first proposed by Zhang et al. in 2022.150 The theoretical basis is that pathological leakage of the BBB can allow harmful substances from the blood to enter the central nervous system, thereby reshaping a local tumor microenvironment conducive to the occurrence and development of GCs150; the restoration of pathological BBB function limits the ability of tumor cells to access the materials needed for growth.150 The VEGFR antagonist axitinib promotes the restoration of pathological BBB function, thereby extending the survival of tumor-bearing mice.150,151 Whether the therapeutic effect of axitinib stems from the restoration of pathological BBB function or a direct antitumor effect remains to be established. 2) The existence of the BBB integrity severely hinders the intratumoral delivery of antitumor drugs.152 This perspective is universally accepted and will be further examined in the subsequent discussion.

The physical and biochemical barriers of the BBB constitute the principal obstacles limiting drug accumulation at the tumor site in GBM. The former consists of the blood‒brain barrier, blood‒cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB), and cerebrospinal fluid‒brain barrier (CFB), which help maintain homeostasis for neurons. While the BCSFB is uniquely characterized by the presence of fenestrations, which confer a degree of permeability, the endothelial cells of both BBB and BCSFB exhibit tight junctions that impede the paracellular transport of most macromolecules. Furthermore, within the CFB, choroid plexus epithelial cells are interconnected by gap junctions. Small-molecule drugs traverse the BBB via transcellular passive diffusion or active transport, whereas large-molecule drugs primarily rely on the latter mechanism. Although many therapeutic agents possess physicochemical properties conducive to passive diffusion, their intracranial distribution is often substantially limited by efflux transporters.153 Therefore, the biochemical barriers, composed of multiple transporters and receptors, serves as a key target for regulating the entry or exit of therapeutic drugs into the brain. In fact, efflux transporters—including P-glycoprotein (ABCB1/P-gp) and breast cancer resistance protein (ABCG2/BCRP)—actively pump many TKIs (e.g., sorafenib and sunitinib) out of the brain, sharply restricting their cerebral accumulation.149,154 Their concentrations in the brain are markedly lower than in the systemic circulation; even high doses fail to sustain effective intracerebral exposure and are more likely to increase systemic toxicity. Meanwhile, accurately assessing drug concentrations within brain tumors remains challenging.

The second factor is significant heterogeneity in the integrity of the BBB or blood‒tumor barrier in GBM.146,155 GBM can be divided into three areas according to the degree of BBB integrity: (i) The gross area of GBM shows enhanced, uneven, high-signal regions on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MR images. Hydrophilic contrast agents penetrate the incomplete blood‒brain tumor barrier, where they enter the extracellular space of tumor cells.146 The mechanism by which GBM causes pathological leakage of the BBB is as follows: the GBM secretes VEGF, which dose-dependently downregulates the expression of tight junction proteins (claudin-5 and occludin) and promotes the formation of numerous abnormal new blood vessels.150,151 (ii) In T2-weighted or T2-weighted FLAIR images, high-signal regions may show milder damage to the blood‒brain barrier, presenting as peritumoral edema. This may be related to tumor cell infiltration into adjacent normal tissue, with hypoxia being a key factor driving this process.156 (iii) In addition to the high-signal regions in T1-weighted, T2-weighted, or T2-weighted FLAIR images, the remaining normal brain tissue may also hide tumor cells. The reasoning is as follows: even after high-signal areas on T1W, T2W, or T2W FLAIR images are surgically removed, patients with GBM still inevitably experience recurrence and progression; a certain proportion of tumor cells can be found in biopsy samples taken from brain tissue outside the edema area.146 Areas ii) and iii) may become critical for future tumor recurrence for the following reasons: owing to the relatively intact blood‒brain barrier, the drug distribution volume in these areas is limited.155 Clearly, traditional imaging assessment techniques need improvement; however, how to more accurately delineate tumor regions is beyond the scope of this review.

Beyond the inherent heterogeneity of the BBB in GBM, AATs have also been shown to reduce its permeability. For instance, long-term therapy with Bev significantly decreased BBB permeability in a human xenograft model.157 Similarly, Sorensen et al.158 reported that a single dose of cediranib lowered Kˆtrans within 24 h in patients with rGBM, indicating vessel tightening and reduced permeability. These studies suggeste that AATs mediate BBB stability, with imaging manifestations appearing as reduced contrast enhancement on MRI—a phenomenon often described as “pseudoresponse.” This not only improves cerebral edema in patients with GBM, but interestingly, those initially assessed as “partial responders” exhibit longer PFS.4

Therefore, mitigating the restrictive influence of the BBB is vital for improving agent penetration and delivery.159 The improvement strategies and subsequent technological development directions are as follows.(1)Enhancing permeability or regulating transporter protein expression. Focused ultrasound in combination with intravenously delivered microbubbles offers a non-invasive, targeted, and reversible strategy for transiently disrupting the BBB, thereby improving drug delivery.160 Moreover, the modulation of transporter protein expression (such as GLUT1 and GLUT3) represents a critical strategy for enhancing the delivery of therapeutic agents across the BBB. Recently, a study established GLUT3, traditionally considered a glucose transporter, as a key multifunctional transporter that directly binds to and mediates the uptake of TMZ and capecitabine in GBM upon its upregulation, thereby enhancing chemosensitivity.161 However, this method lacks clinical data support. Additionally, borneol not only transiently reorganizes tight junctions to enhance BBB permeability but also increases intracranial drug exposure by upregulating influx transporters (i.e., OAT1 and OAT3) and downregulating efflux transporters (such as P-g)162—both animal studies and early-phase clinical research have demonstrated that borneol significantly increases the brain-tissue concentrations of various chemotherapeutic and targeted agents and exhibits synergistic antitumor effects when incorporated into composite nano-delivery systems.163,164 Another approach involves osmotic BBB opening. A phase I trial demonstrates that the superselective intraarterial cerebral infusion of mannitol for BBB opening, followed by Bev and cetuximab, is well tolerated and shows promising preliminary efficacy with prolonged survival in pediatric patients with refractory high-grade glioma.165 Another phase I study yielded comparable results.166 These findings suggest that strategies aimed at the transient disruption of the BBB can effectively enhance intracranial drug distribution volumes, thereby improving antitumor efficacy when combined with systemic therapeutics. However, caution is warranted regarding potential adverse effects, including the risk of irreversible BBB injury and central nervous system toxicities, which underscores the need for careful risk-benefit evaluation in future clinical trials.(2)Bypassing the BBB through alternative administration routes or agent “disguise” techniques. The former includes nasal administration, direct intracerebral injection, and convection-enhanced delivery.167 Deshmukh et al.168 pointed out that intranasal delivery is low-toxicity, non-invasive, and exploits olfactory-trigeminal pathways to bypass the BBB. In a phase I/II CAPTIVE trial,169 investigators directly injected the oncolytic adenovirus DNX-2401 into rGBM lesions and subsequently administered intravenous pembrolizumab, resulting in a favorable safety profile and prolonged survival in a subset of patients. Parvar et al.170 highlighted that CED can infuse active pharmaceutical ingredients directly into brain tissue at therapeutic concentrations, thereby overcoming the BBB and broadening treatment options for brain tumors. The latter encompasses designing drugs that can easily pass through the BBB, including polymer nanoparticles and drug-encapsulated polymer microspheres. Recently, novel nano-delivery systems—including angiopep-2-modified framework nucleic acid nanoparticles,171 engineered outer membrane vesicles,172 ROS-responsive fusogenic liposomes,173 and biomimetic PROTAC liposomes174—have been developed to specifically regulate or degrade key drug-resistance targets such as MGMT, MDK, and BRD4. These advancements are designed to overcome GBM resistance to both TMZ and immunotherapy. Despite encouraging early-phase results, these BBB-bypass strategies still face challenges related to long-term safety, large-scale manufacturing, and precise spatiotemporal drug distribution. Future work should prioritize multipronged standardization and scalability—establish good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compliant manufacturing and long-term safety datasets to ensure reproducibility and regulatory readiness, and rigorously validate them in randomized, multicenter trials to accelerate clinical translation.

Heterogeneity of tumor endothelial cells

Conventional perspectives hold that tumor vasculature is derived from preexisting vessels; consequently, TECs are considered to possess relatively stable genomes. However, accumulating evidence demonstrates that TECs are highly heterogeneous and display diverse aberrant phenotypes. Deciphering the heterogeneity of TECs is essential for promoting vascular normalization and overcoming anti-angiogenic therapeutic resistance.

Single-cell sequencing technology offers distinct advantages for resolving cellular heterogeneity, thereby enabling the comprehensive characterization of TECs. Xie et al. constructed a molecular atlas of GBM endothelial cells using single-cell RNA sequencing of freshly isolated endothelial cells from human glioblastoma and matched peritumoral tissue. Five distinct endothelial cell (EC) phenotypes were identified, including Peripheral EC types I–III and Core EC types I–II.175 Among them, Core EC type I (Co1) is characterized by an angiogenic program involving vascular basement-membrane remodeling, endothelial tip cell formation, and sprouting pathways, and so forth. The heterogeneity of TECs holds significant prognostic value. Elevated ESM1 levels (a TEC subpopulation) are significantly associated with higher tumor grade and poorer survival.176

More importantly, studies have shown that TEC heterogeneity increases following AATs. For instance, the upregulation of Tgfb1 has been noted among tip-like TECs after aflibercept treatment.177 On the other hand, differential sensitivities among TEC subtypes: tip-like TECs and breach TECs are particularly sensitive to AATs, whereas postcapillary vein and proliferating TECs exhibit relative resistance to ATTs.178 Collectively, these studies indicate that AATs could modulate the proportional distribution of TEC subpopulations, which in turn exhibit differential sensitivities to AATs.

Recently, studies have shown that certain EC subpopulations in immune cell–infiltrated tissues exhibit specialized capacities for immune cell recruitment—a trait that may be induced by chronic inflammatory stimuli in non-lymphoid organs. For instance, in the lung and liver, these immunomodulatory ECs support immune homeostasis and contribute to maintaining a delicate balance between immune tolerance and inflammation.179 Such EC subsets may represent promising targets for future combination strategies involving anti-angiogenic and immunotherapeutic interventions.

Looking ahead, research on tumor vascular endothelial cells is anticipated to evolve into an integrated single-cell, spatial, and multi-omics approach. By simultaneously profiling transcriptional, epigenetic, and proteomic data across various organs and treatment timelines, this strategy seeks to construct multidimensional vascular atlases and to unify the nomenclature and evolutionary trajectories of TEC subpopulations. Incorporating biomechanical cues—specifically fluid shear stress and matrix stiffness—into single-cell and epigenomic profiling has emerged as a critical strategy to elucidate how mechanotransduction pathways such as Notch–UNC5B180 and VEGF–Notch–ERK181 govern the arterial-like specification of TECs, with the aim of leveraging this “arterialization” to achieve vascular normalization and enhance the efficacy of immuno- and radiotherapy.

Vasculogenic mimicry

During tumor progression, the angiogenic profile transitions from conventional endothelium-dependent vessels (EDVs) to VM, characterized by tumor cell-derived tubular networks (PAS+/CD31-). VM is observed in approximately 30–40% of GBM specimens, constituting roughly 10% of the total vascular structures.182,183 To comprehensively evaluate VM, a systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed July 2025) using the keywords “vasculogenic mimicry” and “glioblastoma” along with associated terms. This search revealed approximately 70 relevant studies published over the past five years. Current investigations have practically focused on epigenetic modifications or post-translational modifications, such as SUMOylation, phosphorylation, and m6A methylation.184,185,186 These finding suggest VM as an active area of research in neuro-oncology, which has received substantial research attention.

The precise mechanisms underlying VM formation remain incompletely understood. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is recognized as a critical prerequisite for VM formation.187 Moreover, multiple compensatory signaling pathways are involved in VM formation, such as the PI3K-Akt, JAK/STAT, TGFβ-Smad2/3, Hippo/YAP1, and MAPK, which are typically upregulated in aggressive cancer cell populations. A recent study showed that PLK4 activates the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways through the phosphorylation of EphA2 at Ser901 and Ser897 residues, which in turn promotes VM formation and tumor progression in GBM.188 In addition to directly forming vessel-like networks,182 glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) regulate phenotypic switching in differentiated glioma cells (DGCs) through the secretion of signaling molecules. Wang et al. found that exosomal circCMTM3, derived from GSCs, facilitates VM in DGCs through the stabilization of STAT5A/B proteins and activation of the STAT5A-CHI3L2/SRSF1 signaling axis.186 Overall, the endothelial-like phenotypic transformation observed in DGCs represents an increased propensity for migratory and invasive behavior, suggesting a mechanistic link between VM and tumor aggressiveness.

Admittedly, multitarget anti-angiogenic agents, including vandetanib, anlotinib, and sorafenib, have been reported to suppress VM formation.189,190,191 However, substantial evidence has established VM as a compensatory resistance mechanism against anti-angiogenic therapeutics.192,193 Sunitinib has been shown to promote cancer stem cell phenotypes and facilitate VM formation through the upregulation of the lncRNA-ECVSR/ERβ/HIF-2α signaling pathway, thereby enabling sustained tumor progression during anti-angiogenic therapy.194 In contrast, COL5A2 is a key driver of VM formation in regorafenib-resistant cells.195 Therefore, targeted therapeutic strategies directed against VM-specific molecular markers may offer superior survival benefits in VM-positive GBM. Targeting the ERβ/circDGKD signaling pathway has been demonstrated to inhibit VM formation induced by sunitinib pretreatment, consequently extending survival in orthotopic models.196 Furthermore, the administration of the VM formation inhibitor daurisoline was shown to significantly increase sensitivity to sorafenib.197 Although numerous proteins, lncRNA, tRNA, or circRNA targets have been demonstrated to regulate VM formation, only CVM-1118 has progressed to clinical trials (NCT03600233). This suggests a relatively low translational rate of basic research findings into clinical trials.

VM-targeted agents in combination with other therapeutic modalities may represent one of the promising directions for future research. Recently, Luo et al. demonstrated that nanoparticles possessing self-antigen EDV and VM capabilities (designated VE-DDP-Pro) effectively enhance the efficacy of cisplatin in inhibiting cancer growth and metastasis and prolong the median survival time of tumor-bearing mice.198 Another study showed that VM formation is one of the key mechanisms underlying TMZ resistance in gliomas.199 Additionally, VM is significantly more prevalent in PD-L1-positive patients compared to PD-L1-negative counterparts; mechanistically, PD-L1 promotes VM formation through the EMT pathway, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the combined targeting of PD-L1 and VM.187 Therefore, similar to anti-angiogenic approaches, combining anti-VM agents with chemotherapy or immunotherapy exerts synergistic antitumor effects.

Selection of drugs beyond second-line treatment for recurrent glioblastoma

There is currently no consensus on the treatment regimen for rGBM, and different guidelines show some variation. In 2022, the guidelines updated by the American Association of Neurological Surgeons (AANS) and the Conference of Neurological Surgeons (CNS) recommended the following treatment options for adult rGBM: cytoreductive surgery (Level II); monotherapy (Level III); reirradiation (Level III); and tumor treatment (Level III), among others.200 These guidelines indicate that there is insufficient evidence to support monotherapy with targeted drugs or combinations with other medications (such as cytotoxic drugs or immunotherapies) for treating rGBM. The NCCN Guidelines Version 2.2024 for adult glioma recommend Bev and REGO as the preferred systemic treatment options for rGBM.

Currently, research on multitarget anti-angiogenic agents has focused mainly on monotherapy or combination chemotherapy for rGBM. There are no large clinical studies confirming that multitarget anti-angiogenic drugs alone can significantly prolong OS, although phase II studies have reported survival benefits with REGO. However, almost no studies have reported the combination of multitarget anti-angiogenic drugs with other treatment modalities (such as TTF or immunotherapy). Therefore, it is still too early to exclude multitarget anti-angiogenic drugs from the treatment options for rGBM.

Conclusions

Over the past decade, most clinical trials investigating multitarget anti-angiogenic agents for GBM have failed to demonstrate meaningful clinical benefit. These repeated failures highlight that the simple repurposing of therapeutics effective against extracranial malignancies, despite showing significant preclinical efficacy, remains insufficient to overcome the unique pathophysiology of GBM. Drug penetration is limited by the BBB, while the heterogeneity of TECs and the tumor vasculature evolves from classical EDVs to VM, facilitating resistance to AATs. Furthermore, robust clinical evidence guiding therapeutic selection beyond 2nd-line therapy for rGBM remains limited, and validated biomarkers for patient stratification or treatment response monitoring have yet to be established. Future research should prioritize biomarker-guided precision strategies, innovative drug delivery platforms, and well-designed multimodal regimens to overcome the current therapeutic plateau.
