Development and psychometric evaluation of the stigma scale for caring for individuals with sexually transmitted infections (STISS)- student version
Authors: Dilay Açıl, Sema Üstgörül
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile stigmatization by healthcare providers is a primary barrier to effective care for individuals with sexually transmitted infections (STIs), there is currently a lack of specialized, validated instruments to assess these attitudes across the broader spectrum of STI care. This study aimed to fill this gap by developing and psychometrically validating the Sexually Transmitted Infections Stigma Scale (STISS) among future healthcare professionals. The scale provides a standardized framework for identifying and addressing discriminatory perceptions to enhance inclusive clinical practice.MethodsThis methodological study involved the development and validation of the Sexually Transmitted Infections Stigma Scale (STISS) in three stages. Content validity was assessed by a seven-member expert panel using the Davis technique to calculate the Content Validity Index (CVI). Construct validity was evaluated using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA, n₁ = 227) with Maximum Likelihood extraction and Promax rotation. The resulting structure was then cross-validated using second-order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA, n₂ = 200). Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha for the overall scale and its subscales.ResultsContent validity was excellent, with a CVI of 1.0. EFA identified a 20-item, four-factor structure (Blame, Concealment, Personal Reaction and Segregation) which explained 68.8% of the total variance, with factor loadings ranging from 0.521 to 0.965. CFA confirmed the model, yielding acceptable fit indices: χ²/df = 2.947, RMSEA = 0.059, CFI = 0.954, NFI = 0.925 and GFI = 0.912. The overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.94, while subscale reliability coefficients ranged from 0.83 to 0.92, demonstrating robust internal consistency across all analytical stages.ConclusionThe STISS is a psychometrically robust instrument for assessing stigmatising attitudes in STI care. Although it was initially validated within a student cohort, the scale’s clear factor structure and high reliability suggest that it is a suitable tool for identifying biases in infectious disease nursing. Further validation with diverse professional groups is recommended to enhance the scale’s generalisability.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Sexually transmitted infection, Public health, Quality of care, Scale development

Introduction

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are diseases caused by a large number of different bacteria, fungi, viruses and parasites and the main route of transmission is unprotected sexual intercourse. The transmission of these diseases can occur through various means, including sexual intercourse, pregnancy, blood transfusions, and contact with contaminated blood products. The most prevalent STIs include HIV, hepatitis B and C, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, trichomoniasis, HPV, syphilis and genital herpes. STIs can cause many serious health problems such as infertility, ectopic pregnancy, cancer, chronic pelvic pain, pelvic adhesions and even death. Furthermore, the infant of an infected mother can develop eye diseases, central nervous system infection and death. Beyond the individual consequences, this fear also reflects the broader public health burden of STDs worldwide [1].

STIs are a significant public health concern for adolescents and young adults all over the world. As stated in the 2019 World Health Organization report, the number of individuals who contract STIs on a daily basis exceeds one million, with an estimated 376 million new cases of curable STIs annually [2]. This increase is not only limited to biological and clinical consequences, but can also lead to strong social and psychological effects, particularly stigmatization, on individuals [3].

Stigma, a concept widely studied across various social science disciplines, is difficult to define yet rich in meaning. Socially, stigma refers to the use of a person as a means of degradation [4–8]. Goffman, who first defined the concept of stigma, described it as ‘a deeply discrediting quality’. Goffman’s theoretical standpoint posits that stigma constitutes a form of social undervaluation of the self [9]. The fact that the stigmatised individual carries a stigma that is directly socially recognisable and already known by people causes him/her to be subjected to a discrediting. For instance, those who look horrible, those who have been in prison, those who receive alcohol or drug treatment, those with infectious diseases are expressed as individuals who are ethnologically stigmatised in society [9]. At the same time, in Goffman’s stigma theory, the characterisation of stigma is emphasised as a social process depending on the social context. In Link and Phelan’s study, stigma is conceptualised as a social process involving separation, labelling, stereotyping, loss of status and discrimination [10].

Sexually transmitted diseases are among the most prevalent diseases worldwide. And the distinguishing characteristics that lead to the stigmatization of individuals or groups can vary based on historical, cultural, local, or universal values [11, 12]. People tend to label those who are different from themselves, which may lead to psychological impacts such as depression, shame, guilt, and low self-esteem among stigmatized individuals [13]. In studies examining the experience of stigma in the society, it has been reported that patients who experience discrimination and stigmatisation in the society have decreased self-esteem [14]. Patients who feel stigmatisation and have decreased self-esteem begin to isolate themselves, withdraw from the society, and treatment non-compliance increases. Positive and negative affective and cognitive (believed and felt worthlessness, stigmatisation) characteristics affect patients’ coping mechanisms and self-esteem [15]. It is known that inpatients have a fear of being rejected by the society and therefore these patients do not trust themselves and show a more defensive attitude. The care process of the patient who tries to receive treatment with fear of stigma is also negatively affected. The most important point here is to address the fear of being stigmatised by health professionals while receiving health care and discrimination from other patients in the treatment process.

Sexually transmitted diseases and stigmatisation

Every domain of life poses unique challenges for individuals with STIs and those living with them, yet stigma and discrimination are common threads. Disclosure of one’s STI status can lead to social exclusion from family, school, or workplace [16]. While similar reasons may underlie different forms of exclusion, their consequences vary, necessitating interdisciplinary examination [17].

STIs are a persistent and harmful problem that creates significant barriers to disease prevention, access to care and treatment. This stigmatisation not only affects individual health outcomes but also has important familial, social and economic consequences [18]. Studies show that social misconceptions and insufficient knowledge about STIs contribute to the development of negative attitudes towards individuals affected by these infections [19, 20].

While healthcare settings should ideally provide supportive environments, evidence suggests that they can also facilitate the reinforcement of health-related stigma.’ Biased attitudes and/or insufficient training among professionals can lead to suboptimal care and judgemental communication [21]. Mitigating clinical stigma is critical for fostering inclusive workplaces, improving care quality, and supporting better outcomes for patients. Adopting a cross-cutting approach enables common domains to be identified across various stigmas, moving beyond disease-centered approaches [22]. This enables researchers to develop more concise and comparable measures, as well as design interventions that better reflect the lived realities of vulnerable populations. Ultimately, reducing these barriers minimises perceived social risks, thereby enhancing engagement with the entire care continuum. Thus, individuals with sexually transmitted infections may be subject to blame and moral judgement not only from society but also from healthcare providers. In healthcare, stigma becomes particularly problematic when directed at individuals with socially sensitive conditions like STIs. Healthcare personnel may exhibit various reactions stemming from fear of infection, ranging from neglecting patient concerns and wishes to outright rejection or verbal abuse. Within healthcare settings, examples of stigma and discrimination may be observed in the form of judgemental comments, inadequate privacy protections which may result in patient labelling or exposure, inadequate provision of care, early discharge, or mistreatment in front of family members [14, 15, 23]. In a globalized world, healthcare employees regularly provide care to patients who have different values, beliefs and health practices [24]. Cultural diversity in healthcare presents healthcare students with challenges that require not only medical knowledge, but also sensitivity to cultural differences. Patients may have different perspectives on diagnosis, treatment and even the very concept of health and illness [25]. Research, usually on HIV/AIDS, has been conducted on preventing and reducing the transmission of sexual risk behaviours (e.g. condomless sex), which are more likely to acquire sexually transmitted infections in various populations, including lifelong ethnic, racial and sexual minorities [26]. Moreover, the transmission of a disease through sexual intercourse, which is one of the basic needs of life, causes stigmatisation in many societies [27]. Therefore, even if there are cultural differences, this may affect the dose of stigma rather than the application of stigma [28]. Another important point of this research is that health professionals who have knowledge about sexually transmitted diseases may apply more stigma due to their awareness. Because these diseases can be transmitted not only sexually but also through blood [29], the health professional who tries to protect himself/herself from contamination while providing care may unknowingly stigmatise the individual with STIs. Therefore, it is not thought that cultural or religious differences will be very different when providing care in the hospital [30]. What is important here is that the health professional should not carry the self-protection behaviour to a different dimension while providing care, that is, to provide equal care to all patients without applying stigma [31].

Despite the existence of literature pertaining to HIV/AIDS-related stigma [14], there is a notable absence of comprehensive scales that are specifically designed to assess health workers’ perceptions of stigma and discrimination related to STIs. The primary aim of this study is to develop a measurement scale that assesses healthcare workers’ stigmatising attitudes and behaviours towards individuals with not only HIV/AIDS but all sexually transmitted infections, and to validate a comprehensive instrument addressing stigma toward all STIs. It is thought that this scale, developed to address the stigma associated with all sexually transmitted infections from the perspective of healthcare providers, will fill a gap in the literature as a comprehensive instrument that can be applied across different cultures and to different healthcare professionals.

Materials and methods

Study design and sampling methodology

The population of this methodological study, which is a scale development study, consists of all 3rd and 4th grade midwifery and nursing students of a state university in western Türkiye (N = 620). The sample consisted exclusively of nursing and midwifery students (easily accessible to the researchers), and that medical or other allied health students were not included in the study, as they represent the primary pre-professional group with clinical exposure to infectious diseases. The sample consisted of students who spoke Turkish, had an internet connection and agreed to participate in the study (n1=227 (age 22.3±0.89, %85.3 female, %58.7 nursing student; n2=200 (age 21.2 ± 1.06, %87.9 female, %60.3 nursing student)). Inclusion criteria were being an active 3rd or 4th-grade student and providing informed consent, while students with incomplete survey responses were excluded. The sample size for the study was determined using a preliminary power analysis with G*Power software. Taking the effect size as medium (f² = 0.2), α as 0.05 and power (1-β) as 0.90, the minimum recommended sample size was found to be 207. More than this number of participants took part in the study. In addition, the item/participant ratio was considered, as this is frequently encountered in the literature. The aim was to have 5–10 participants per item. The researchers attempted to reach the all of the population, and the individuals we reached constituted 34.9% of the first sample and 32.2% of the second, representing a sufficient sampling frame for single-center validation.

Data collection

The research was conducted in the 2023–2024 academic year and was carried out a linear three-stage framework:

Phase I (Item Generation and Content Validity)

In the first stage, a detailed literature review was conducted on the subject, systematically searched major electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Ovid, Google Scholar, and national databases (e.g., TR Index), focusing on studies published in both English and Turkish without a specific time restriction to ensure a broad conceptual reach. The search strategy utilized key terms such as “stigma,” " stigma with sexually transmitted infections,” “stigma of healthcare providers,“stigma and care” [32–45]. Therefore a question set containing 30 questions was created and the question form was consulted for expert opinion using the Davis technique. As a result of the 7 experts’ feedback and revisions, a scale form including 28 questions was created and a pilot application was conducted with students.

Phase II (construct validity - EFA)

In the second stage of the study, validity and reliability examinations and exploratory factor analysis of the scale were conducted with the data obtained from 227 students online. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was performed using the Maximum Likelihood extraction method and Promax (oblique) rotation. Before the data collection process began, the participants were informed of the purpose, scope and voluntary nature of the study. This information was also provided in the introduction section of the online form. They were informed that participation was voluntary, that their responses would remain confidential, and that the data would be used solely for academic purposes. After obtaining their informed consent, students were asked to complete the online data collection forms via a secure web link shared by the researchers. During the explanation, it was emphasised that the answers given were not about whether they were right or wrong, but were really intended to understand their opinions. After the exploratory factor analysis, a scale structure with 20 items and 4 factors was obtained. Convergent validity was evaluated by calculating Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for each sub-dimension.

Phase III (Model Verification - CFA)

The final version of the 20-item scale was applied to Sample 2 (n = 200). Second-order Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and correlation was employed to confirm the structural integrity of the factors identified in EFA.

The STISS is a 5-point Likert scale with 1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Moderately Agree, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly Agree. The lowest score to be obtained from the scale is 20 and the highest score is 100; as the score increases, the stigmatization tendency of professionals caring for individuals with STIs increases. The scale has no reverse scored items. The validity and reliability results of the scale are presented in the findings section.

Results

Content validity of the scale

Content validity is the extent to which the scale as a whole or an item serves the purpose. Content validity index (CVI) is a method used to transform qualitative data based on expert opinions into statistical quantitative data [49–51]. Davis technique was utilized to evaluate the content validity index [49]. The draft scale, consisting of 30 items created by the researchers was submitted to the evaluation of 7 experts (infectious diseases specialist, psychiatric nurse, public health nurse, gynecology and obstetrics nurse, psychologist). The experts were asked to evaluate each item as “not appropriate”, “needs to be changed appropriately”, “appropriate, but needs minor changes”, “appropriate”. Then, the number of experts who expressed the appropriate opinion for each item was divided by the total number of experts, and the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were calculated. If the CVI is above 0.80, it is interpreted that the content validity result of the questions in the scale is appropriate [50]. While established methodological frameworks suggest that item reduction should ideally be deferred until the construct validation stage [51, 52], 2 items in this study were excluded at this phase due to a total lack of expert consensus and their identified conceptual irrelevance. Expert evaluations confirmed that the remaining items were appropriate in terms of both their conceptual structure and content. Some experts recommended minor changes to the wording, while others suggested removing items with similar wording or those that might influence responses based on social desirability. It was also recommended that attitudinal and behavioural statements be distinguished from one another, and that statements related to general stigma other than that associated with sexually transmitted infections be removed. Changes were made in the prepared draft scale form in line with the suggestions of the experts and the data collection phase was started afterwards. The content validity was found to be statistically significant since the CVI ≥ CVR (0.99). The Content Validity Index (CVI) score for the scale was computed as 1.0, indicating a high level of content validity. Moreover, the Content Validity Rate (CVR) score for each item on the scale ranged from 0.72 to 1.

Logical (surface) validity

It was made within the framework of the evaluations of researchers and people who are not related to the subject whether a scale measures the situation under investigation or not, and arrangements were made to make the items comprehensible [53, 54].

Pilot application

Pilot test application following the completion of the content validity assessment, a pilot test was conducted with six students undergoing learning with nursing and midwifery who were not part of the sample to examine surface validity [55].

Validity of the scale- construct validation

Before proceeding to exploratory factor analysis, some important characteristics of the data should be examined. One of these assessments is the Kaise-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure, which evaluates the suitability of the sample for factor analysis. The KMO measure is a statistic that measures the adequacy of the relationships between the variables in the sample. An acceptable KMO value recommended in the literature should generally be greater than 0.5, ideally close to 1 [53, 54]. Another important test is Barlett’s test of sphericity. This test evaluates whether there are significant relationships between variables and generally the p value should be less than 0.05, which indicates that the correlations between variables are statistically significant [53–60].

These preliminary analyses assess whether the data set is suitable for factor analysis and provide a reliable basis before proceeding to exploratory factor analysis. This stage is critical for understanding the structural properties of the scale and determining the correct factor structure. Exploratory factor analysis

Prior to factor analysis, multivariate outliers were assessed using Mahalanobis distance across all scale items. 12 responses of participants were excluded from further analysis. After outlier removal, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted using the Maximum Likelihood extraction method and Promax (oblique) rotation, following best practices for analyzing potentially correlated psychological constructs [61, 62] to examine the underlying structure of the stigma scale. The number of factors to retain was determined based on the eigenvalue > 1 criterion. Items with factor loadings above 0.40 and minimal cross-loadings were considered acceptable. According to the results of the exploratory factor analysis, it was determined that certain items (3rd, 9th, 10th, 11th, 13th, 14th, 19th and 28th) in the STISS did not meet the criteria in terms of item eigenvalue and factor loadings. These items were removed in order to make the scale more meaningful and reliable, and the factor structure of the scale was checked by conducting exploratory factor analysis again. The initial item pool consisted of 28 items. However, during the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), eight items were removed based on both statistical and theoretical considerations. Statistically, these items either had low factor loadings (i.e., below 0.40), exhibited cross-loadings on multiple factors, or showed low communalities, indicating poor representation by the extracted factors. In addition to these statistical issues, a theoretical review of the items revealed that they did not align well with the conceptual focus of the scale. Specifically, these items appeared to reflect other psychosocial constructs such as general stigmatisation attitudes, etiological beliefs, or professional/ethical values, rather than the intended construct. Therefore, these items were excluded to ensure the content validity, structural coherence, and parsimony of the final scale. The exploratory factor analysis for the STISS yielded a structure with 20 items and 4 factors. According to the rotated solution, the factors explained approximately 35.4%, 19.5%, 9.6%, and 4.3% of the variance, respectively (Table 1). These percentages are based on the Rotation Sums of Squared Loadings, which are recommended for oblique rotations such as Promax [61, 62]. The exploratory factor analysis revealed a clear four-factor structure. The lowest factor loadings of the STISS were 0.521 and the highest was 0.965. Factor 1 consisted of 9 items (Item 1–9) with loadings ranging from 0.605 to 0.965. Factor 2 contained 3 items (Item 10–12), with loadings ranging from 0.521 to 0.827. Factor 3 included 5 items (Item 13–17), with loadings ranging from 0.792 to 0.928. Lastly, Factor 4 comprised 3 items (Item 18–20) with high loadings between 0.805 and 0.915. All factor loadings exceeded the 0.40 threshold recommended for meaningful interpretation [62]. Also it was assessed internal consistency through Composite Reliability (CR), followed by Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for convergent validity. The analysis yielded CR values between 0.766 and 0.939, which are well above the conventional 0.70 threshold, indicating high internal consistency across all dimensions. Furthermore, the AVE scores ranged from 0.531 to 0.724; since all values exceeded 0.50, the scale demonstrated sufficient convergent validity. These results indicate that the scale has strong factorial validity and well-differentiated subdimensions and the STISS has a structure consisting of 20 items and 4 factors (Table 1).

Confirmatory factor reanalysis

Different sample group were analysed (n2=200). At this stage, Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Barlett test were used to evaluate the suitability of the data for factor analysis. A sample of 200 participants is generally considered sufficient in the literature for four-factor CFA models [63]. This goodness of fit indicators reveal that the sample size is sufficient to ensure the stability of the model.

After these initial conditions were met, the final version of the scale was administered to the second sample group with a retest, the validity and reliability of the scale were confirmed by confirmatory factor analysis, and the sub-dimensions of the scale were named according to the item content [55]. The factors were named as Blame (9 items), Concealment (3 items), Personal Reaction (5 items), and Segregation (3 items). The names given to the factors increase the scientific value of the research by expressing the basic characteristics and concepts underlying each factor, facilitating the comprehensibility of the analysis and the interpretation of the analysis results. The identification of factors was primarily based on Goffman’s (1963) conceptualisation of stigma and was further supported by contemporary frameworks [8]. Blame involves attributing responsibility for a stigmatised condition, which is consistent with attribution theory (Weiner, 1995) and the emphasis placed by Link and Phelan (2001) on labelling and status loss [10, 64]. ‘Concealment’ refers to behavioural strategies employed to hide stigmatised identities, aligning with Corrigan’s (2004) self-stigma model and Goffman’s (1963) concept of ‘passing’ [8, 65]. ‘Personal response’ captures emotional and cognitive reactions such as shame and self-blame, as described in Corrigan and Watson’s (2002) model of internalised stigma [66]. ‘Segregation’ represents exclusionary attitudes and social distancing, in line with Link and Phelan’s (2001) components of stigma and supported by structural stigma literature (Hatzenbuehler, 2016) [10, 67].

Blame can be defined as a moral judgment of responsibility to a person for an undesirable or otherwise norm-violating action or outcome. It occurs when we assign responsibility to someone else for a fault or wrong. It can lead to unhelpful emotions like hatred and resentment [68]. In this factor of the scale, there are statements that include the person’s accusatory approaches.

Concealment is the state of being hidden or the act of hiding something. Individuals with concealable stigma continually decide whether, and if so, how to disclose to others. These complex decision-making processes are evident across various stigmatized sexual health contexts, including sexually transmitted diseases and infections, and sexual trauma, among others [69]. The second factor was given this name because it contains statements about hiding the situation against the possibility of experiencing stigma.

Personal response is the expression and evaluation of one’s own ideas and feelings. It teaches the person to think about why they feel the way they do. In this factor, the responses focused on what the person’s thoughts and feelings would be like if they themselves were to contract STIs [70].

Segregation is the action of separating people, historically on the basis of race and/or gender. Segregation implies the physical separation of people in everyday activities, in professional life, and in the exercise of civil rights. The European Commission defines segregation as “the act by which a (natural or legal) person separates other persons on the basis of race, color, language, religion, nationality or national or ethnic origin without an objective and reasonable justification.” [71].

In this final factor analysis, in line with the method proposed was reported [53–56], according to the results of the second level confirmatory factor analysis conducted with the data obtained from the final sample, the goodness of fit values obtained are as follows: χ²/Sd = 2.28, RMSEA = 0.058, NFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.96, CFI = 0.98 and GFI = 0.90 (Table 2). This are within the limits accepted in the literature and show that the model has high stability [72]. Figure 2 is presented as a graphical figure showing the standardized estimated values. These values indicate that the relationships between the factors are accurate and the structural integrity of the model is appropriate [55, 56]. According to established guidelines in the literature [73], values of χ²/df less than 3 and RMSEA below 0.08 generally indicate acceptable model fit in social science research. Additionally, Shi et 0.2020 and Sharif-Nia et. 2023 emphasize that while fit indices provide important information, they should be interpreted alongside theoretical coherence and construct validity [74, 75]. Although some indices are borderline, these values fall within acceptable limits, suggesting an adequate but not perfect fit.

Confirmatory Factor Reanalysis

The internal consistency of each factor was evaluated using Cronbach’s Alpha statistic. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficients were 0.92 for the Blame (9 items) sub-dimension, 0.86 for the Concealment (3 items) sub-dimension, 0.90 for the Personal Reaction (5 items) sub-dimension and 0.83 for the Segregation (3 items) sub-dimension. The overall reliability of the scale was found to be 0.94. These high values indicate that the internal consistency and reliability of the measurement tool are high (Table 3).

Discussion

More than 1 million treatable STIs occur every day in people aged 15–49 years worldwide. STIs have a direct impact on sexual and reproductive health through stigmatisation, infertility, cancers and pregnancy complications and can increase the risk of HIV [1]. The treatment process of these infections requires the compliance of individuals and the sensitivity of care staff. Attitudes of health professionals towards individuals with STIs are important in terms of ensuring continuity of care. In this context, the measurement tool developed will serve to evaluate the negative perceptions of health professionals regarding STIs and to improve public health by taking measures against them. Although there is a stigma scale related to HIV/AIDS in the literature [56, 77], there is no scale evaluating attitudes towards the care of individuals with STIs. This measurement tool consisted of 20 items and 4 sub-dimensions: blame, concealment, personal reaction and segregation from the society in order to evaluate stigmatisation attitudes towards individuals with STIs.

In alignment with DeVellis’s scale development framework, the findings of this study support the content and construct validity of the STISS [36]. The main objective of using this scale is to raise awareness of potential barriers to care arising from the perceptions of healthcare professionals. With a training plan structured around developing this awareness, the health of society can improve multidimensionally. Students training to become health professionals must be taught the importance of adopting an equal and non-discriminatory approach to the provision of health care services. In particular, they should be made aware of the importance of avoiding stigmatizing attitudes and behaviours when providing services to vulnerable or disadvantaged groups. Such training strengthens professional ethical awareness and contributes to the development of a more inclusive, individual-centred healthcare system. Indeed, providing appropriate care services for sexually transmitted infections (STIs) improves individual health outcomes and contributes significantly to economic efficiency and the achievement of sustainable development goals (SDGs). For instance, an intervention study in Tanzania found that improving STI care services reduced the incidence of HIV-1 infection by 40 per cent, making it a highly cost-effective strategy [78].Furthermore, strengthening STI care services reduces the risk of STI transmission, fertility issues, pregnancy complications, and psychosocial effects. Thus, improving productivity and reducing the burden on health systems provides economic benefits [79, 80]. In this context, improving care processes related to (STIs) is crucial for achieving the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal of ‘Health and Well-being for All’ (SDG 3). Ensuring universal access to sexual and reproductive health services, for example, directly contributes to sub-goals such as ending infectious diseases and promoting equal access to health services [81].

The EFA and CFA results robustly confirm the structural integrity of the STISS. In the literature, HIV-related stigma scales are particularly prevalent [82–84]. The most frequently used of these evaluate the stigma experienced by HIV-positive individuals, both internalised and perceived through their environment and these scales are often used in researches. However, this scale attempts to establish a different conceptual framework to that of existing studies. The aim is to examine the stigma associated with all sexually transmitted infections from the perspective of healthcare professionals who will provide care for individuals with these infections. Most existing scales either do not address the stigmatising attitudes and behaviours of healthcare professionals caring for individuals with STIs at all, or evaluate them indirectly. In this respect, it is considered appropriate to highlight the study’s originality in terms of respect for human rights, fairness, and universal values, and its potential to contribute to the ongoing provision of care.

Validity of the scale

Validity of content

In this study, it was found that the instrument developed to measure stigma towards the care of an individual with a STIs was reliable and valid and the subscales were highly correlated with each other. The sample consisted of similar age groups, classes and different undergraduate programmes. All 20 items of the scale were evaluated as relevant and clear by the experts, which constituted evidence for content validity. The analyses showed that the STISS adequately measured the subject matter and content validity was achieved.

Construct-concept validity

Explanatory factor analysis

Before proceeding to exploratory factor analysis, some important characteristics of the data should be examined. The Kaise-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure evaluates the suitability of the sample for factor analysis and is a statistic that measures the adequacy of the relationships between the variables in the sample. The KMO value should generally be greater than 0.5, ideally close to 1 [57, 85] Barlett’s test of sphericity evaluates whether there are significant relationships between variables and generally the p value should be less than 0.05, which indicates that the correlations between variables are statistically significant [39, 86, 87]. The KMO index of 0.50 and above and Barlett’s test being significant (p < 0.05) are considered necessary for factor analysis to be performed [88–90]. In this study, KMO index was found to be 0.888 and Barlett’s test result was χ2 = 3497.651, df = 190, p < 0.001). The current data set provides the necessary conditions for factor analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis is a widely used method to understand and validate the structures of scales. The eigenvalue of each item should be greater than 1 and factor loading values should generally be above 0.40, and if these criteria are not met, the relevant items may need to be removed from the scale and the analysis may need to be reconstructed [57]. It was determined that certain items (3rd, 9th, 10th, 10th, 11th, 13th, 14th, 19th and 28th) did not meet the above criteria. These items were removed in order to make the scale more meaningful and reliable, and the factor structure of the scale was checked by conducting exploratory factor analysis again. The exploratory factor analysis for the yielded a structure with 20 items and 4 factors. This structure explains 68.843% of the total variance, which is an important validity criterion for factor analysis. [54, 91]According to the rotated solution, lowest factor loading value was 0.521 and the highest value was 0.965 and the four factors explained approximately 35.4%, 19.5%, 9.7%, and 4.3% of the variance, respectively (Table 1). The analysis revealed that all item loadings exceeded the commonly accepted lower threshold value of 0.50 [88, 89], demonstrating strong factor–item relationships and indicating a high level of internal consistency for the overall scale.

Confirmatory factor analysis

The construct validity of the scale was evaluated in order to determine both the characteristics it measures and the meaning of the scores obtained by the people to whom the scale was applied. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is a method used to assess whether the structure of a scale developed on the basis of a previous sample or exploratory factor analysis results is confirmed by new data collected [54, 75, 91]. Confirmatory factor analysis tests various measurement models, usually uncorrelated model, single-factor model, first-order and second-order multifactor model. Among these models, the model that provides the best fit is selected and the factor structure of the scale is determined. Numerous fit indices are used to assess the validity of the second-order multifactor model, including Chi-Square fit index (χ2/Sd ≤ 5), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA≤ 0.080), normed fit index (NFI≥ 0.90), redundancy fit index (IFI≥ 0.90), comparative fit index (CFI≥ 0.95) and goodness of fit index (GFI≥ 0.90) [54, 55]. The goodness-of-fit values (∆χ²= 486,236, df = 165, χ2/df = 2.947, RMSEA= 0.059, NFI= 0.925, IFI= 0.940, CFI= 0.954, and GFI= 0.912) obtained according to the results of the second level CFA, which was used to confirm whether the related factors are STISS components, show that STISS can be measured with a four-factor structure (Fig. 1; Table 2). These findings support the structural validity of STISS and show that it can be used as an effective tool to assess individuals’ awareness and attitudes about sexually transmitted diseases. This shows that hypothesis H1 is confirmed.

Reliability of the scale

The reliability of the scale was analysed in order to show that the scale can make measurements free from errors, collects the data correctly and is a repeatable scale. The reliability of the scale was analysed according to the ‘Classical Test Theory’ and ‘Internal Consistency Reliability’ were evaluated. Cronbach alpha reliability coefficients were calculated in the reliability analysis. The ability to give consistent results from application to application and the ability to show invariance over time were examined. Regarding reliability, the findings compare favorably to accepted psychometric standards. The STISS Cronbach’s α for the final combination of items for the subscales were all above 0.757,77. This shows that the scale has a robust internal consistency in all dimensions (Table 3). The difference in Cronbach’s alpha between the two samples (n1 = 0.772, n2 = 0.942) is largely due to the sample characteristics. The first sample had a more heterogeneous participant profile, whereas the second sample consisted of a more homogeneous group in a single university, possibly with a higher level of overlap with the item contents. As stated in the literature, the alpha coefficient reflects not only internal consistency but also the variance structure in the sample [47]. Therefore, the higher alpha value observed in the second sample supports the high internal consistency of the scale.

According to the literature, the item-total correlation coefficient is expected to be higher than 0.40. It is suggested that items below this value should be removed from the scale [57, 92]. Scale item-total correlations are presented in Table 1, and it is seen that the lowest item-total correlation of the items examined is 0.441 and the highest correlation is 0.760. In this case, the measurement tool developed is a reliable, is considered [93].

In order to perform confirmatory factor analysis again according to the data obtained from the second sample, the KMO value was found to be 0.908 and the Barlett test χ2 value was 3409.9 (p = 0.000), and it was determined that the data set was suitable for confirmatory factor analysis. Modification values indicate that the relationships between the items should be improved during the factor analysis process [53, 75]. According to the results of the second level confirmatory factor analysis, the goodness of fit values obtained are as follows: χ²/Sd = 2.28, RMSEA = 0.058, NFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.96, CFI = 0.98 and GFI = 0.90 (Fig. 2; Table 2). In confirmatory factor analysis, the χ²/df ratio was found to be 3.04. Although this value is slightly above the classical threshold of < 3, the range of 3–5 is accepted as ‘reasonable fit’ in the literature [72, 75, 94]. These results confirm the structural integrity of the model as stated in the literature [93, 94].

According to the regression analysis results between the questions in the scale and the dimensions formed by these questions, it is seen that the items are significantly related to each other (Table 4). This shows that hypothesis H2 is confirmed. High and positive correlations between the items in the dimensions show that the items exhibit similar behaviours and this increases the internal consistency of the test [57, 59]. The correlations obtained in this study are presented in Table 5 and it is determined that the internal consistency of the scale is high according to the literature. To prevent reinforcing harmful stereotypes, we emphasize that the scale should be employed only with appropriate ethical approval and informed consent.

The development of the STISS establishes a baseline for understanding and measuring stigma related to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among future healthcare professionals. While this study focuses on the initial validation of the instrument, its robust psychometric performance suggests that it could be a valuable tool in academic and clinical training environments. To establish the STISS as a globally applicable instrument, subsequent research should prioritise multicentre investigations across diverse healthcare disciplines and professional levels. Such extensive testing is essential for confirming the scale’s cross-cultural stability and its ability to identify implicit biases hindering equitable access to infectious disease care. Ultimately, integrating the scale into broader professional contexts will enable targeted interventions to be developed to reduce stigma and improve the quality of care for individuals with STIs.

Conclusion

The STISS was found to be a reliable and valid instrument when used with a large, diverse sample. STISS was developed with 20 items and 4-factor structure as a result of the data obtained and analyses performed. Reliability and validity of the scale were ensured and thus, stigmatisation tendencies related to STIs could be evaluated. The use of this scale in professionals providing care services may facilitate taking precautions and managing the process related to STIs. The knowledge, attitudes and behaviours of the people from whom they receive care and treatment services have a significant effect on individuals’ compliance with treatment. The needs of health professionals on this issue can be determined with this measurement tool and interventions can be planned by serving the purpose (role play, demostration, empathic skill development studies, etc.). The data obtained with this scale can guide curriculum studies in developing sensitivity to STIs during undergraduate education. At the same time, the use of this scale can be used in primary, secondary and tertiary health institutions to ensure that in-service trainings serve the purpose at every step where health care is provided. Human nature tends to protect itself without being aware of it. Moral values, perceptions of hygiene, adherence to professional responsibilities, and the tendency to limit communication may affect the care provided by health workers caring for people with sexually transmitted infections. Evaluating such situations can contribute to both professional and vocational training from a different perspective. This scale points to an important gap in terms of understanding the source of stigma and developing targeted training/intervention programmes, especially in clinical/field practice. In this respect, the scale developed in this study can contribute to the improvement of care processes both for those currently working in the profession and for those continuing their education. In addition, the provision of effective STI care services offers health and economic benefits at both individual and societal levels and should be considered as an important strategy for achieving sustainable development goals. Future studies validate the scale with a wider range of health professionals (e.g. doctors and social workers) in different sociocultural settings, in order to increase its generalisability and applicability.

Limitations

Several limitations of the study should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the sample was limited to senior nursing and midwifery students at a single university in Turkey. Although students from social work, physiotherapy and medicine were initially planned to be included, data could not be obtained from these departments during the online data collection process. Consequently, the findings represent a specific cohort and may not fully reflect the attitudes of practising healthcare professionals or students from other disciplines.

Methodologically, certain measurement properties, such as test–retest reliability, measurement invariance and responsiveness, were not assessed. Furthermore, some items were excluded during the content validation stage rather than the factor analytical phase, as a procedural choice. While this was done to maintain conceptual rigour and expert consensus, it limited the empirical observation of those items’ statistical performance. Data collection relied on online self-report measures, introducing the possibility of social desirability bias, whereby participants may have provided socially acceptable responses rather than reflecting their true attitudes. Additionally, the study was conducted within a specific Turkish cultural context. Since no ‘gold standard’ scale for measuring sexual stigma was available in the local language, criterion-related validity could not be tested.

Finally, while this study focused on self-reported attitudes, the scale’s predictive validity regarding real-world discriminatory behaviours was not evaluated. Future research should prioritise multi-centre validation with diverse healthcare providers and incorporate behavioural observation or experimental designs to further substantiate the scale’s practical utility. Despite these limitations, the STISS aligns with established stigma dimensions and offers a unique multi-STI focus that makes a significant contribution to the field.
