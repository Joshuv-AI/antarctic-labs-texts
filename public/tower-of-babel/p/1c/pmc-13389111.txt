Machine learning models for injury risk prediction in football players: A systematic review of predictors, performance, practical applications, and limitations
Authors: Mehdi Aslani, Mostafa Zarei, Zohreh Yaghoubitajani, Abolfazl Malekahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFootball is associated with a high incidence of musculoskeletal injuries because of its dynamic, high-intensity, and contact nature. Machine learning methods offer the potential to capture nonlinear relationships among diverse risk factors. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the application, accuracy, and practical utility of machine learning models for predicting injuries in football players.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines. A systematic search was performed in Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus, along with manual searches, through October 2025. Cohort and cross-sectional studies applying machine learning methods for injury prediction in football players were included (n = 22). Study quality and risk of bias were assessed using the Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool.ResultsWe performed a narrative synthesis because of substantial heterogeneity in study design, machine learning models, injury definitions, and predictor variables. Common predictors included training load metrics, previous injury history, biomechanical and neuromuscular characteristics, anthropometric variables, and psychological and biological indicators. Most studies reported moderate-to-high discriminative performance based on area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and precision metrics. Reported accuracy values ranged from approximately 55% to 96%. However, several studies relied only on accuracy as the primary metric, which is susceptible to misinterpretation in the presence of class imbalance between injury and noninjury observations; metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, or area under the curve–ROC would provide a more balanced evaluation. Models generally demonstrated better performance for noncontact and soft tissue injuries than for contact injuries.ConclusionsMachine learning models may have exploratory value for injury risk stratification in football. However, the current evidence remains heterogeneous and methodologically limited. Further studies with large-scale designs, standardized methodologies, external validation, appropriate calibration, and clinical interpretability are required before these models can be recommended for routine injury-prevention practice.Review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (ID: 1279575).

Introduction

Football is played by more than 250 million people worldwide, and its physically demanding, contact-driven nature contributes to a high prevalence of musculoskeletal injuries. 1 These injuries rarely result from a single factor; instead, they arise from a combination of previous injuries, muscle imbalances, aerobic fitness, workload, body composition, age, sleep quality, and psychological factors.2,3 The consequences extend beyond individual players, as reduced performance can lower team rankings, diminish club revenue, and increase healthcare costs. 4 Although early injury risk prediction is clearly advantageous, it could be considered essential for reducing these broader consequences. 5 Although caution is needed to avoid overestimating its power, timely risk assessment enables medical and technical staff to take action, potentially preventing injuries or reducing their impact. 6 Moreover, performance evaluation can inform training schedules and tactical decisions, complementing injury prediction to support overall athlete well-being. 7

In football, teams increasingly rely on a range of technologies to monitor injury risk, including global positioning system (GPS) and inertial sensors, functional screening tests, and biological or psychological markers of fatigue and health.8–10 Machine learning (ML) has attracted considerable attention because of its capacity to model nonlinear relationships and complex interactions among multiple predictors. However, the assumption that complex ML models (e.g. random forests or neural networks) necessarily outperform simpler regression-based approaches appears overstated, particularly given the small sample sizes, class imbalance, and limited external validation that characterize most football injury datasets. 11 The first application of ML in football appeared in 2014, analyzing team performance during the Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) Euro 2012 tournament. 12 Since then, the field has expanded, with studies exploring injury prediction, physical performance forecasting, training load management, career trajectory modeling, club performance, and even spectator attendance prediction.13,14 Despite this growing body of work, the practical benefits of ML for football clubs and experts remain somewhat unclear. 15

Several reviews have attempted to summarize these developments. Claudino et al.13 examined AI approaches for injury risk assessment and performance prediction across team sports, whereas Rico-González et al. 16 focused specifically on football, covering talent identification, performance, and injury prediction. Leckey et al.17 provided a scoping review of ML approaches to injury risk prediction, noting that only a limited number of models achieved high AUC values. Meanwhile, Herold et al.18 explored the potential of ML for refining offensive tactics in football. Pillitteri et al. 19 analyzed the relationships between internal and external load indicators and injuries, and narrative reviews by Nassis et al.14 and Rossi et al.20 examined ML-based injury prediction in football.

These reviews provided pieces of the puzzle but none offered a comprehensive, football-specific synthesis evaluating predictors, model performance, validation approaches, calibration reporting, class imbalance issues, methodological quality, and practical implications for coaching and medical staff. In other words, although some evidence exists, the operational and clinical relevance of these models remains only partially understood. 18

Considering the above, the present review sought to address this gap. Therefore, we aimed to provide a football-specific perspective on ML-based injury prediction by gathering and synthesizing the existing evidence. Our focus was on predictor domains, methodological limitations, model performance, risk of bias, and the practical application of these findings. The goal was not to claim readiness for routine implementation but to provide a grounded overview to help researchers, clinicians, and coaches understand what these models can and cannot currently deliver. We also highlighted areas where caution is warranted so that enthusiasm for ML does not exceed the available evidence. In doing so, we hoped to provide a clearer understanding of both the promise and the limitations of ML in predicting injuries in football and offering a roadmap for future research and more informed decision making.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and related recommendations for sports science. 21 The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (ID: 1279575). A PRISMA 2020 flow diagram describing the study selection process is presented in Figure 1.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection. A total of 714 records were identified (711 through database searching and 3 through manual/ResearchGate searching). After removing 54 duplicates, 660 records were screened and 625 were excluded. Of 35 reports sought for retrieval, 3 could not be retrieved. Thirty-two reports were assessed for eligibility, and 10 were excluded (6, ineligible population; 2, no machine learning model; 2, no injury outcome). Finally, 22 studies were included in the review.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they involved football players and applied ML, artificial intelligence, or related predictive algorithms to assess injury risk. Both prospective cohort and retrospective analytical studies were included. Cross-sectional studies were considered only if they used temporally structured or retrospective datasets within a predictive modeling framework; purely descriptive cross-sectional studies without temporal injury classification were excluded. Explicit labeling of the learning paradigm (supervised, unsupervised, or deep learning) was not required, provided that the algorithm and injury-prediction objective were clearly identifiable. Only original full-text articles published in English were included, whereas systematic reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, and non-English publications were excluded. No publication date restrictions were applied.

Injury definitions and classification

In this review, injury was defined according to the definitions used in the original included studies. Where studies applied the UEFA injury definition, injury was generally considered a time-loss physical complaint sustained during football training or match play that resulted in the player being unable to fully participate in subsequent training or match activities. Injury mechanisms were classified according to the terminology reported in each study. Contact injuries were considered injuries resulting from direct physical contact with another player, object, or external force, whereas noncontact injuries were considered injuries occurring without direct external contact, such as during sprinting, cutting, acceleration, deceleration, landing, or other football-specific movements. Soft tissue injuries included muscle-, tendon-, or ligament-related injuries when reported by the original studies. Because injury definitions and mechanisms were not reported consistently across studies, the present review extracted the injury type and injury definition as reported by each study and did not perform post hoc reclassification when the original classification was unclear.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted independently by two reviewers (MA and ZY) in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception through October 2025. Manual searches of reference lists and ResearchGate were also performed to identify additional relevant studies.

The search strategy followed the Patient/Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework, combining terms related to football, ML, injury prediction, workload monitoring, and artificial intelligence. The acronym “ML” was used only in the manuscript text after defining “machine learning” and was not used as a standalone search term.

Complete search strategies for each database are as follows:

PubMed: (soccer[Title/Abstract] OR football[Title/Abstract]) AND (machine learning[Title/Abstract] OR artificial intelligence[Title/Abstract] OR deep learning[Title/Abstract] OR prediction model[Title/Abstract] OR classification[Title/Abstract] OR random forest[Title/Abstract] OR support vector machine[Title/Abstract] OR XGBoost[Title/Abstract]) AND (injury prediction[Title/Abstract] OR injury risk[Title/Abstract] OR noncontact injury[Title/Abstract] OR musculoskeletal injury[Title/Abstract])

Scopus: TITLE-ABS-KEY ((soccer OR football) AND (machine learning OR artificial intelligence OR deep learning OR prediction model OR classification OR random forest OR support vector machine OR XGBoost) AND (injury prediction OR injury risk OR noncontact injury OR musculoskeletal injury OR training load OR GPS OR wearable))

Web of Science: TS = ((soccer OR football) AND (machine learning OR artificial intelligence OR deep learning OR prediction model OR classification OR random forest OR support vector machine OR XGBoost) AND (injury prediction OR injury risk OR noncontact injury OR musculoskeletal injury))

Selection process

Records identified through database and manual searches were deduplicated and then screened in two sequential stages against the prespecified eligibility criteria: first by title and abstract and subsequently by full-text assessment. Reasons for exclusion were documented at the full-text stage. Studies meeting all inclusion criteria were included in the final qualitative synthesis.

Data collection process

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (MA and ZY) using a data extraction framework developed based on recommendations from the Checklist for Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies (CHARMS). Extracted information was cross-checked between reviewers to ensure consistency and accuracy. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, consensus, and consultation with a third reviewer (MZ) when necessary. Because of the qualitative nature of the extraction process and the heterogeneity of prediction model reporting across studies, formal inter-reviewer agreement statistics were not calculated. Data items

The key data items extracted from each study included study characteristics (authors, year, country, and study design), participant characteristics and study population, sample size and number of injury events, injury definition and injury type, prediction horizon and unit of analysis, predictor variables, ML algorithms and model-development approaches, validation methods (e.g. internal validation, cross-validation, train-test split, and external validation), model performance metrics (e.g. AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision), calibration assessment where reported, handling of missing data, methods used to address class imbalance, external validation status, key findings, limitations, and the authors’ conclusions.

Prediction horizons were extracted as the time interval between predictor measurement and injury outcome assessment. Studies were categorized into three main temporal windows: long-term or season-level models, which use baseline, preseason, or early-season data to estimate injury risk over an extended follow-up period, such as a full season; medium-term or block-level models, which predict injury risk over defined periods of several weeks or months; and short-term or daily/session-level models, which estimate injury risk close to the time of exposure, such as match by match or session by session. When studies did not clearly report the prediction horizon, they were categorized as unclear. These temporal distinctions are clinically important because long-term models support preseason screening and season planning, medium-term models inform training-block adjustments, and short-term models guide day-to-day coaching and medical decisions.

Study risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of each included study were independently assessed by two reviewers (MA and ZY) using Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST), 41 with disagreements resolved through discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer (MZ). PROBAST evaluates four domains: participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis, using signaling questions to classify each domain as having a low, high, or unclear risk of bias. Key considerations included participant representativeness and eligibility, the predefinition and reliability of predictors, the objectivity of outcome assessment, and analytical issues such as overfitting, data leakage, handling of missing data, class imbalance, validation procedures, and calibration. Applicability to the review question was also assessed based on the relevance of participants, predictors, and outcomes. Detailed domain-specific ratings and justifications are provided in Table 2.

Synthesis methods

Data synthesis was performed qualitatively by analyzing key findings, with a focus on predictor variables, ML algorithms, prediction horizon, validation strategy, and model performance. Studies were grouped according to algorithm type (decision trees, random forests, support vector machines, gradient boosting, neural networks, and other methods) and predictor domains. The temporal categories defined above were used to support the narrative synthesis. Findings were summarized narratively to highlight common trends, strengths, and limitations across studies.

Results

Study selection

A total of 714 records were initially identified from Scopus (n = 624), Web of Science (n = 36), PubMed (n = 51), and ResearchGate/manual searches (n = 3). After the removal of 54 duplicates, 660 records were screened by title and abstract. A total of 625 records were excluded because they did not meet the prespecified eligibility criteria. Of the 35 full-text reports sought, 3 could not be retrieved. The remaining 32 reports were assessed for eligibility, of which 10 were excluded as not relevant (6 because of an ineligible population, 2 because of the absence of a ML model, and 2 because of the absence of an injury outcome). Finally, 22 studies were included in the final qualitative synthesis.

Study characteristics

The studies were conducted across diverse geographical regions, including Europe, North America, and Asia. Sample sizes ranged from 18 to 734 participants; 13 studies involved adult athletes, 4 involved adolescent athletes, and 5 did not report participants’ ages.

The majority of the included studies evaluated the predictive ability of ML models for injuries occurring in any region of the lower extremity. However, several studies focused on specific anatomical injury locations, including hamstring strain injuries,11,22,23 head injuries, 24 and groin injuries. 25

Most included studies applied supervised ML or predictive classification approaches, whereas Rossi et al. 20 used an unsupervised approach. In nine studies, the authors did not explicitly categorize the learning paradigm as supervised, unsupervised, or deep learning; however, these studies were retained because they reported identifiable ML or predictive modeling algorithms relevant to football injury prediction.24–33 This incomplete reporting was considered a methodological reporting limitation rather than an exclusion criterion.

Among the included studies, most used prospective longitudinal cohort designs to predict future injury occurrence using preseason or ongoing monitoring data. However, several studies applied retrospective or cross-sectional analytical frameworks using previously recorded injury and workload datasets to develop classification models. These retrospective approaches may differ methodologically from true prospective prognostic prediction models and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Predictor variables

The predictor variables most frequently collected across studies were classified into four major domains: (a) demographic and injury history variables; (b) psychological factors; (c) physical and neuromuscular performance measures; and (d) GPS-derived workload metrics.

Eleven studies incorporated GPS-derived workload variables, such as internal and external load measures, training volume, and match playing time. Nine studies included demographic characteristics, including age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and previous injury history, as predictors.23,25,28,31,33–38

Psychological and perceptual factors were examined in four studies, most commonly including sleep quality, anxiety, behavioral stress, and perceived stress.11,34,36,37 Five studies included biomechanical performance tests such as counter-movement jump (CMJ) and vertical jump performance as predictors of injury risk.11,22,32,36,39

Neuromuscular and physical fitness characteristics, including knee and thigh muscle strength, flexibility, speed, agility, balance, and endurance, were reported as predictors in 10 studies.11,23,25,28,31,32,36–39

Additional predictors included blood biomarkers such as hematocrit and red blood cell count, 20 match-related characteristics such as player position, 33 and video-based match observations for head injury assessment. 24

ML algorithms

Regarding the ML algorithms used, 10 studies applied decision tree–based models, 9 studies used Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), 7 studies used random forest algorithms, and 5 studies used support vector machine (SVM) models. Vallance et al.34 reported the highest predictive performance using an XGBoost model, achieving an accuracy of 97% for injury prediction.

Temporal prediction windows

The included studies differed in the time span over which injury risk was predicted. Long-term or season-level models generally used baseline, preseason, or early monitoring data to estimate injury occurrence over an extended follow-up period. These models may be useful for identifying players who require preventive screening or individualized season planning, but they may be less sensitive to short-term fluctuations in fatigue, workload, recovery, and contextual match demands.

To estimate subsequent injury risk, medium-term or block-level models used monitoring data collected over defined periods, such as several weeks or months. These models may help identify evolving risk profiles during a training block, but their practical value depends on how frequently data are updated and whether the prediction window aligns with coaching decisions.

Short-term or daily/session-level models, including those based on repeated workload or player-monitoring inputs, are more closely aligned with day-to-day injury-prevention decisions. For example, Freitas et al.40 investigated noncontact injury prediction in professional football players using ML approaches, illustrating the relevance of short-term risk estimation in practical monitoring contexts. However, these models may require higher-quality repeated data, careful management of within-player correlations, and validation strategies that avoid leakage between training and testing observations.

Because temporal prediction windows differed across studies, model performance metrics should be interpreted within the context of the prediction horizon. A season-level model and a daily/session-level model answer different clinical questions, and their AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity values should not be compared without considering the underlying time window, injury prevalence, and unit of analysis.

Model performance evaluation

With respect to model evaluation, AUC was the most commonly reported performance metric and was used primarily to assess model discrimination. Because several studies reported more than one model or more than one AUC value, these values were summarized at the model level rather than aggregated by study. Therefore, these categories should not be interpreted as mutually exclusive counts of included studies. Overall, reported AUC values ranged from 42% to 91%, indicating substantial variability in model discrimination across studies and models. Sensitivity was reported in 14 studies and ranged from 0.23 to 0.98, whereas specificity was reported in 8 studies and ranged from 0.68 to 0.95. Precision, or positive predictive value, was reported less consistently, ranging from 10% to 97%. Reported accuracy values ranged from 55% to 96%; however, accuracy was interpreted cautiously because many studies involved imbalanced datasets, and in such settings, high accuracy may reflect the predominant classification of noninjured players rather than effective identification of injury cases. 42

Several studies demonstrated relatively high specificity but comparatively lower sensitivity, suggesting that the models may perform better at identifying noninjured players than players at increased injury risk. From a practical injury-prevention perspective, this limitation may reduce clinical usefulness because failure to identify high-risk athletes could limit opportunities for early intervention.

Validation approaches varied substantially across studies. Most studies used internal validation procedures, such as cross-validation or random train-test splitting, whereas external validation was rarely performed. In studies using repeated player-monitoring or time-series data, random splitting may increase the risk of data leakage if observations from the same player, team, season, or adjacent time periods are included in both the training and testing datasets. 43 Calibration assessment and decision-threshold analyses were rarely reported, substantially limiting the translation of predicted probabilities into actionable clinical decisions. 44

Taken together, model performance should be interpreted cautiously, as discrimination metrics such as AUC alone do not fully capture clinical utility, particularly given the low injury prevalence, class imbalance, and near absence of calibration reporting across the included studies. 45

Across studies conducting model interpretability analyses, GPS-derived workload variables (such as rapid increases in training load, high-speed running, and acceleration) were identified as important predictors in nine studies.26,27,30,31,33–35,40,46 Anthropometric characteristics, such as BMI and age, were identified as important predictors in six studies,28,31,33,36,37,39 whereas previous injury history was identified as a key predictor in approximately eight studies.11,23,31,34–37,46 Biomechanical characteristics, particularly range of motion, muscle strength, and neuromuscular control, were also frequently identified as relevant predictors. Risk of bias and applicability assessment

Among the 22 included studies, only 1 study was rated as having an overall low risk of bias, 4 studies were rated as unclear, and 17 studies (77%) were rated as having an overall high risk of bias according to the PROBAST assessment. According to PROBAST guidance, a high risk of bias in any single domain results in an overall high-risk rating. Bias was observed predominantly in the analysis domain, with common issues including small sample sizes, inadequate handling of missing data, insufficient reporting of validation procedures, and potential model overfitting.

In addition, some studies did not clearly describe the separation procedures between training and testing datasets. As noted above, inadequate separation of training and testing data raised additional concerns regarding data leakage and optimistic performance estimation.

Regarding applicability, several studies raised concerns related to unclear inclusion criteria and the limited generalizability of the predicted outcomes to real-world injury-prevention settings. The full assessment of risk of bias and applicability is presented in Table 2.

Results of individual studies

Most studies reported moderate-to-high exploratory discrimination; however, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical or practical readiness. One exception was Kekelekis et al.,25 which reported an AUC of 0.42, indicating discrimination below chance level.

Results of syntheses

A synthesis of the studies identified consistent trends regarding the predictor variables most commonly associated with injury risk. These included physical performance metrics (e.g. sprint speed, agility, and endurance), injury history, biomechanical factors (e.g. joint stability and movement patterns), and environmental factors (e.g. playing surface and training load). Several studies suggested that incorporating multidimensional predictor variables may improve model discrimination. Predictive performance appeared to vary by injury type and mechanism; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because injury definitions and contact/noncontact classifications were not reported consistently across studies. Among studies that distinguished injury mechanisms, models generally appeared to perform better for soft tissue and noncontact injuries than for contact injuries.

A meta-analysis or statistical synthesis was not performed because of heterogeneity in ML models, outcome measures, and injury types. Overall, the findings suggest that ML approaches may have exploratory value, but the evidence remains insufficient to support routine implementation in football injury-prevention settings. 17 Models differed substantially in prediction horizon, validation strategy, unit of analysis, injury definition, and clinical actionability, and improvements in AUC or accuracy alone are insufficient indicators of clinical utility without also considering calibration, sensitivity, class imbalance, and external validation. 44 Accordingly, these findings were interpreted in the Discussion section, with emphasis on model-validation quality, the clinical actionability of predictors, limitations related to class imbalance, and the practical consequences of false-positive and false-negative predictions for coaching and medical staff.

Reporting biases

The findings from the included studies were compared with the broader literature on injury prediction in sports to identify reporting biases. Although most studies reported their primary performance metrics, comprehensive reporting of model limitations, including calibration, validation procedures, and handling of class imbalance, was inconsistent. Some studies, particularly those with small sample sizes, insufficient reporting of model-validation procedures, or lower methodological quality, appeared to emphasize positive outcomes and high accuracy rates without adequately discussing limitations or potential sources of bias. 47 This selective emphasis on favorable findings could contribute to an overestimation of model effectiveness in the literature. 17

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was evaluated by considering the risk of bias concerns identified using PROBAST, together with the consistency of findings and the relevance of outcomes for real-world injury prediction in football. The methodological quality of the included studies was limited primarily by concerns in the analysis domain, together with heterogeneity in study design, predictor selection, algorithms, injury definitions, and validation methods.

Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was judged to be low to moderate because of frequent risk of bias concerns in the analysis domain, limited external validation, inconsistent reporting of calibration and class imbalance, and substantial heterogeneity across studies.

Discussion

This systematic review identified studies applying ML to predict injuries in football players. Discrimination performance varied substantially across studies, with reported accuracy ranging from 55% to 96%. However, accuracy alone may be misleading in imbalanced datasets and does not establish clinical usefulness.15,25,34,36,39 ML algorithms can detect complex patterns among multiple risk variables, but their real-world utility requires external validation, calibration, interpretability, and deployment evaluation.11,28,35

Methodological reporting was often inconsistent. Although most studies reported discrimination metrics such as AUC or accuracy, calibration, handling of missing data, class imbalance, and external validation were frequently absent or inadequately described, limiting generalizability and clinical confidence.11,25,36,39 Although some models demonstrated moderate-to-high discrimination, interpretation is further limited by small numbers of injury events, heterogeneous injury definitions, and inconsistent validation strategies.11,25,28,34,36

ML-based predictive models have garnered attention because of the economic and performance burden of player injuries, with rehabilitation costs reaching substantial levels (e.g. €11,583 per player per season). 35

A key concern is the reliance on internal validation methods, such as cross-validation or random train-test splits, without adequate separation of repeated player or team observations, which can lead to overfitting and inflated performance metrics.25,28,36 Data leakage is a particular risk when repeated observations are split between the training and testing datasets, potentially producing overly optimistic results.25,36,39

No single algorithm consistently outperformed the others. Tree-based and ensemble methods (decision trees, random forests, and XGBoost) were commonly applied because of their ability to model nonlinear relationships and interactions, although they may overfit in small datasets.25,34,36 Simpler models can sometimes provide comparable performance and be more interpretable and easier to implement, highlighting the importance of comparing algorithms using discrimination, calibration, interpretability, external validity, and decision-making relevance.11,25,36,39

Injury-prediction datasets are inherently imbalanced because noninjury observations substantially outnumber injury events. Across the 22 included studies, the handling of class imbalance was inconsistently reported. Several studies did not explicitly describe any imbalance-correction strategy. Among those that did, the most commonly applied techniques included oversampling of the minority (injury) class, including synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE), which generates synthetic injury samples by interpolating between existing minority-class observations; random undersampling of the majority (noninjury) class; and cost-sensitive learning or class weighting, which penalizes misclassification of the minority class more heavily during training. However, most included studies either did not report the method used or handled imbalance implicitly through algorithm choice (e.g. XGBoost and random forest incorporate built-in mechanisms that partially mitigate class imbalance). The absence of explicit imbalance correction likely contributed to the pattern of high specificity but lower sensitivity observed across the review, as models tended to identify noninjured players more reliably than injured players.

Comparison with regression-based approaches requires caution; complex ML models do not automatically yield better predictions. Regression models may provide comparable performance with greater transparency. Future studies should perform direct comparisons using the same datasets, consistent predictors, prediction windows, validation procedures, calibration, and decision-relevant metrics.11,25,34

Prediction horizons influence practical application. Long-term models inform preseason screening and risk stratification, whereas short-term, session-level models are relevant to day-to-day coaching and medical decisions. Frequent, high-quality data collection is required for short-term models to minimize the risk of data leakage.11,25,34,36

Predictor domains differ in their practical relevance. GPS-derived workload variables capture short-term exposure but are context dependent; previous injury history is consistent but nonmodifiable; neuromuscular and biomechanical measures can guide actionable interventions; and psychological and wellness indicators provide important context but are assessed less consistently.11,22,25,31,33,34,36,46

Currently, ML outputs should be used only as supportive information integrated with clinical judgment, athlete history, wellness data, training context, and staff expertise, rather than in isolation for player selection or return-to-play decisions.11,25,31,34

Standardized, reliable, and frequent data collection is essential for robust prediction because the low incidence of injury limits model performance, imbalanced datasets bias predictions toward noninjured players, and generalizability is affected by variations in environment, team context, and assessment methods.1,11,25,34,48,49 Future models should integrate internal factors (demographic, physiological, and psychological) with external factors (training load and environmental conditions) and incorporate veracity metrics to improve reliability. Prospective validation, calibration, and deployment studies are required to assess clinical utility.11,25,31,34

Practical implementation for coaching and medical staff

The practical value of ML models in football injury prevention depends on both statistical performance and the ability of predictions to support safe, timely, and actionable decisions. Useful models should identify modifiable risk factors, provide interpretable information, and support decisions such as training load adjustment, recovery planning, targeted screening, or clinical referral. Models reporting high accuracy but limited sensitivity, poor calibration, or unclear decision thresholds may have limited practical utility.

Implementation requires local validation, calibration assessment, and evaluation of clinically meaningful decision thresholds. Models developed in one team, league, sex, or age group may not generalize to others and should not be applied without contextual adaptation. 17

Rather than providing definitive predictions, ML outputs can identify players who require closer monitoring, individualized load adjustment, recovery support, or further clinical assessment. Before routine implementation, prospective studies should evaluate whether ML-assisted decision making reduces injury incidence or severity and should consider the consequences of false-positive and false-negative predictions for coaching and medical staff.

Limitations

The findings of this review should be interpreted with caution. Inclusion of only peer-reviewed English-language articles may have introduced publication and language bias. Despite a comprehensive search using “machine learning” and related terms, relevant studies may have been missed if atypical terminology was used, studies were not indexed, or were published in other languages.

Most included studies involved male professional players, limiting the generalizability of the findings to female, youth, or amateur populations. Heterogeneity in study design, injury definitions, prediction horizons, and units of analysis precluded direct comparisons across studies. Direct comparisons between ML and regression-based models were few, preventing conclusions regarding the superiority of either approach.

Seventeen studies (77%) were judged to have a high overall risk of bias according to PROBAST, primarily because of concerns in the analysis domain. In addition, incomplete reporting of ML methodology, including the learning paradigm, hyperparameter tuning, validation strategy, and handling of class imbalance, was common across the included studies, limiting the robustness and generalizability of the review’s conclusions.

Conclusion

This systematic review suggests that ML models may have exploratory value for injury risk prediction in football, with several studies reporting moderate-to-high discriminative performance. However, the current evidence remains heterogeneous and methodologically limited. In addition, the reported model performance should not be interpreted as sufficient evidence to support routine practical implementation, particularly given the widespread gaps in calibration, external validation, and real-world deployment evaluation.

From a practical perspective, ML models should currently be considered decision-support tools rather than standalone injury-prevention systems. Their use by coaching and medical staff should be cautious, context-specific, and combined with clinical expertise and standardized monitoring procedures. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter prospective cohort studies, harmonized injury definitions, transparent reporting, external validation, calibration assessment, interpretability, and evaluation under realistic football deployment conditions.
