Development of the Japanese version of the Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire: A cross‐sectional validation study
Authors: Suguru Shimokihara, Hiroyuki Tanaka, Walter R Boot, Yuma Nagata, Shunsuke Nakai, Masahiro Tenjin, Takayuki Tabira
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimIn response to the growing popularity of mobile devices among older adults in Japan, this study aimed to establish a reliable and valid measure of mobile device proficiency by developing a Japanese version of the Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire (MDPQ‐J) for the Japanese population.MethodsTo evaluate the reliability and validity of the MDPQ‐J, we administered the questionnaire to 100 young or middle‐aged participants (37.78 ± 13.90 years, 57.00% women), and 62 older participants (75.90 ± 6.01 years; 59.68% women).ResultsThe MDPQ‐J showed a high degree of internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.98). Moreover, MDPQ‐J scores in older participants tended to be lower than those in young or middle‐aged participants. In terms of validity, the MDPQ‐J score of older participants was significantly associated with age (ρ = −0.31), the amount of time using a mobile device per day (ρ = 0.64), and the system usability scale (ρ = 0.39). Additionally, the MDPQ‐J scores were significantly positively associated with the importance (ρ = 0.37), performance (ρ = 0.57), and satisfaction (ρ = 0.29) associated with daily mobile device use in older participants.ConclusionsThe high reliability and validity of the MDPQ‐J in the Japanese population highlight its utility in adapting mobile devices for older adults, which is crucial in an increasingly digital society. Further research should explore the mediating role of mobile device proficiency in various health‐related outcomes. Geriatr Gerontol Int 2024; 24: 1223–1232.

Introduction

Information and communication technology (ICT) enables users to perform various tasks with minimal effort, and many older individuals have come to own their own mobile devices. A mobile device is a small, lightweight, and portable information terminal. Mobile devices, such as smartphones and tablet PCs, are essential for ICT usage in our daily lives. Additionally, 61% and 74.2% of individuals aged ≥65 years in the United States 1 and Japan, 2 respectively, own a smartphone. In daily life, mobile devices are used to make phone calls, exchange messages, check schedules, make purchases, pay bills, and/or access entertainment content, thus making a range of tasks easier. However, some older individuals who own mobile devices do not benefit from ICT. Internet literacy among older people showed considerable differences, 3 and background factors, such as social and socioeconomic factors, may influence the digital divide among older individuals. 4 Because of these societal conditions, efforts to address digital inequality among them must be increased in future.

In healthcare, initiatives to manage health through digital technology (digital health or eHealth) have expanded to include the elderly because of the risk of deteriorating health with age. 5 , 6 Although many older individuals are becoming more accepting of digital technology, 7 some are apprehensive about it. 8 Therefore, factors influencing their proficiency with mobile devices should be examined. However, few reports that objectively evaluate older adults' proficiency with mobile devices are available. Older adults are accepting of digital technology but the extent to which they are using it remains unclear. Thus, to achieve healthy aging, it is helpful to objectively express the differences in proficiency level regarding mobile devices owned by the older population. For this reason, the Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire (MDPQ) was created to accurately assess mobile device proficiency in older adults. 9 This questionnaire is a self‐report measure designed to capture objective proficiency in using mobile devices, and it is a highly reliable and valid tool. The MDPQ has been translated into Spanish. 10 Also, because the MDPQ can quantitatively assess proficiency in using mobile devices, it is expected to be a valuable tool for studying older adults' interaction with ICT. Consequently, an evaluation index that can quantitatively and easily assess mobile device proficiency in the older Japanese population could be useful.

This study aimed to investigate the adaptation of the MDPQ in Japan. The MDPQ could serve as a basic and quantitative indicator of ICT proficiency among the older population. For this purpose, we created a Japanese version of the MDPQ (MDPQ‐J) and focused on validating its reliability and validity. We expect that this new assessment tool will have the same high reliability and validity as the original version in the Japanese population. We hypothesize that years of use or frequency of use of mobile devices will be positively associated with mobile device proficiency as measured by the MDPQ‐J. Furthermore, we expect that not only the usability of mobile devices but also the subjective importance, performance, and satisfaction of using mobile devices in daily life will be positively correlated with the MDPQ‐J.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross‐sectional design.

Participants

Using nonprobability sampling, we recruited participants from Tokyo, Osaka, and Kagoshima prefectures in Japan. Data were collected from January through to December 2023. The older participants were ≥ 65 years of age, owned mobile devices, and received long‐term frailty‐prevention classes or home care provided by their local governments. For recruitment in the long‐term frailty‐prevention classes, evaluation forms were distributed and completed by older adults who attended the classes and were willing to participate in the study. For recruitment in home care services, evaluation forms were distributed to those who were willing to participate in the study and completed during home care staff visits. We excluded those requiring special physical aids (e.g., external switches) to operate the mobile device (except for stylus pens) and those with cognitive impairments that could make their responses unreliable. Young or middle‐aged participants were rehabilitation workers in general hospitals who were younger than 65 years and university students majoring in rehabilitation and their parents. The exclusion criteria were the same as those for older participants.

Procedure for translation

Similar to the translation process reported in previous literature, the MDPQ was translated to Japanese using a back‐translation process. 11 , 12 First, with permission from the original authors, 9 all original items were initially translated from English to Japanese by a native Japanese speaker with a fluent command over English, and were then back‐translated from Japanese to English by another bilingual professional. Subsequently, the results were discussed with native English speakers to identify any elements that might have been mistranslated. In translating the questionnaire, expressing the nuances of the original version was emphasized rather than a literal translation. The response items in the original version are (i) “never tried”, (ii) “not at all”, (iii) “not very easily”, (iv) “somewhat easily”, and (v) “very easily”. We were concerned that having symmetrical choices would bias the responses toward the middle, 13 so we avoided bias in the response items by having more positive choices. The asymmetry of the Japanese version was verified by the original author. Thus, all draft translations were confirmed to match the original questionnaire. Thereafter, an MDPQ‐J consisting of 46 questions that corresponded to the items in the original version was administered (see Appendix). In the original version of the MDPQ, the instructions state, “Remember, you are rating your ability to perform each of these tasks specifically using a mobile device.” This is because the MDPQ is designed to be used by the respondents themselves and to assess the respondent's ability to use mobile devices. However, it does not include the use of mobile devices with the assistance of others. Therefore, the MDPQ‐J was designed with the same cautionary text as in the original version. Finally, we conducted a cognitive debriefing with five older adults who participated in the study. We asked them about the MDPQ‐J questions and difficulties in answering the questions using options provided, and they gave us the feedback that they could understand the questions and options without any problems.

Instruments

The MDPQ‐J contains 46 questions with eight subscales, which are as follows: mobile device basics, communication, data and file storage, internet, calendar, entertainment, privacy, and troubleshooting and software management. Each question item is rated on a five‐point scale based on the individual's subjective proficiency level. The scores ranged from 8 to 40 and were scored in a similar manner to in the original version. For each subscale of the MDPQ‐J, responses for all questions were averaged. These averaged scores were then summed across subscales to come up with a total score of the MDPQ‐J. The participants were assessed using a paper‐based questionnaire.

Similar to in the original MDPQ report, we also included a short survey asking how long the participants had been using their mobile devices and how often they used them. For the number of years of use of mobile devices, participants were asked to indicate in whole numbers the number of years they had been using mobile devices up until the time of their response. For frequency of use, participants were asked to respond to the appropriate item regarding the amount of time they spent using their mobile device per day (i: <0.5 h, ii: 0.5–1 h, iii: 1–1.5 h, iv: 1.5–2.0 h, v: 2.0–3.0 h, vi: 3.0–4.0 h, vii: 4.0–5.0 h, and viii: >5.0 h). In this study, this continuum of choices was considered and treated as a quantitative variable.

To examine the relationship with a similar assessment scale, the Japanese version of the System Usability Scale (SUS) was used to assess its relevance to the MDPQ‐J. The SUS is a 10‐item survey with five responses, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. 14 The SUS enables respondents to assess a large range of instruments and services, such as mobile devices, websites, and applications. Several previous studies have shown that the SUS can measure the usability of various technologies among older adults, 15 , 16 , 17 providing valuable insights into the relationship between technology usability and the older adult population. Participants were asked to use the SUS to evaluate the usability of their usual mobile devices.

Additionally, the role of mobile device use in the participants' daily lives was assessed using indices of importance, performance, and satisfaction with reference to the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure procedure. 18 Specifically, the following questions were asked regarding their current mobile devices. (i) How important are they in your daily life? (1: not important at all, to 10: extremely important). (ii) How well do you feel you are able to use your mobile devices? (1: very poor, to 10: excellent). (iii) How satisfied are you with your daily use of mobile devices? (1: very dissatisfied, to 10: very satisfied).

All participants were instructed to complete all items of the questionnaire accompanied by the researchers. Submitted questionnaires were checked by the researchers. If any items were incomplete, participants were asked to complete them.

Sample size calculation

For this study, we targeted a total of 200 participants, equally divided between young or middle‐aged and older adults (100 participants in each age group). This sample size was determined based on feasibility considerations to ensure that the study would be manageable within the available resources and time‐frame. In addition, this sample size was considered sufficient to provide a preliminary assessment of the reliability and validity of the MDPQ‐J among Japanese community‐dwelling older adults.

Statistical analysis

First, we plotted the obtained MDPQ‐J scores by age group. The assessed variables were tabulated using descriptive statistics. Subsequently, we assessed the internal reliability of the MDPQ‐J with Cronbach's α, which was calculated by age group for all items and each of the eight subscales. In addition, to examine the validity of the MDPQ‐J, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to determine the correlation between the MDPQ‐J and SUS. Similarly, we examined the correlation of the MDPQ‐J with participants' age, years of mobile device use, and the amount of time spent using a mobile device per day. Furthermore, correlations between the MDPQ‐J and the importance, performance, and satisfaction of mobile device use were examined to determine the importance of mobile device use in daily life. These correlation coefficients were calculated for each age group of participants. We used R ver. 4.2.2 for statistical analysis and data plotting by age group. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Reliability of MDPQ‐J

Cronbach's α was calculated to assess the reliability of the MDPQ‐J. Overall, the MDPQ‐J showed excellent internal consistency (α = 0.98), and the subscales ranged from 0.86 to 0.94 (Table 2). The same trend was observed when the data were stratified into young or middle‐aged and older participants. For young or middle‐aged participants, the α of the MDPQ‐J was 0.96, with subscales ranging from 0.70 to 0.92. For older participants, the α of the MDPQ‐J was 0.97, with subscales ranging from 0.69 to 0.93.

Validity of the MDPQ‐J

Table 3 presents the results of the correlation analysis by age group performed to validate the MDPQ‐J. Specifically, Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were calculated between the basic mobile device questions and between the SUS and MDPQ‐J scores. We found a significant negative correlation between higher age and lower MDPQ‐J scores in both older and young or middle‐aged participants. Also, the amount of time using a mobile device per day, SUS, and MDPQ‐J were significantly positively associated with both young or middle‐aged and older participants. Notably, the correlation between the amount of time using a mobile device per day and the MDPQ‐J was greater for older participants than for young or middle‐aged participants. Additionally, MDPQ‐J scores were significantly positively associated with importance, performance, and satisfaction with mobile device use, and this association was stronger for older participants than for young or middle‐aged participants.

Discussion

Characteristics of mobile device proficiency in older adults

In this study, the MDPQ‐J was created through a back‐translation process to quantitatively assess the mobile device proficiency of Japanese older adults, and its reliability and validity were verified. This assessment confirmed that the MDPQ‐J has sufficient validity and reliability in the Japanese older population. Similar to previous reports on the MDPQ, 9 , 10 the present study suggests that older adults may be less proficient with mobile devices than younger or middle‐aged adults. The total and subscale MDPQ‐J scores were comparable to those of the original version reported in the United States in 2016. 9 The original version was reported 8 years ago, indicating that although mobile devices are rapidly gaining popularity among the older population, the proficiency level of this population may still be low. The scores were lower in the data and file storage subscale and the troubleshooting and software management subscale, which consider tasks that are characterized by more complex operations and require somewhat specialized knowledge. Therefore, older individuals may require assistance with file management, troubleshooting, and software management to become proficient in using mobile devices.

Reliability and validation of the MDPQ‐J

The overall internal consistency of the MDPQ‐J was excellent (α = 0.98). Considering that α = 0.99 for the original and Spanish versions of the MDPQ, this means that the MDPQ‐J had the same high internal reliability as these versions. The results of the subgroup analyses for internal consistency showed slightly lower alpha values for the entertainment and privacy subscales for older adults. Older adults may not use their mobile devices as frequently as younger adults for the entertainment activities listed on the MDPQ‐J (games, videos, music, books, and photography), and there might be significant individual differences in their use of mobile devices for these activities. While younger adults commonly engage in these entertainment activities on their mobile devices, there is more variation among older adults, with some using mobile devices for entertainment and others not as much. Older adults' privacy concerns also vary across reports. Previous studies have reported that older adults are either very concerned about their privacy 19 or not concerned at all. 20 We believe that these variations in individual perceptions of privacy and entertainment are reflected in the observed decline in internal consistency.

Correlation analysis of the MDPQ‐J showed a significant positive association between MDPQ‐J scores, age, and the amount of time spent using a mobile device per day. Interestingly, the years of use of mobile devices and MDPQ‐J scores were not significantly associated. Previous research has shown that older individuals use their smartphones primarily for social media and news reading, 21 indicating they may not be familiar with the variety of features included in mobile devices, even if they have been using them for a long time. In addition, despite its inclusion at the beginning of the MDPQ‐J, the short questionnaire used to assess the history of use of mobile devices did not include a statement regarding the definition of mobile devices. We cannot rule out the possibility that some participants had used flip phones or feature phones in the past and misunderstood that their history of mobile device use included the use of these devices. Contrary to our hypothesis, the non‐significant association between length of mobile device use and the MDPQ‐J may have reflected this misconception in participants' responses. Future studies should examine the association in more detail after clarifying the definition of mobile devices to participants. On the other hand, older participants showed a significant positive association between time spent using mobile devices per day and MDPQ‐J scores. This suggests that older adults who are more proficient with mobile devices may use mobile device features for a variety of tasks that occur throughout the day and during leisure time. Furthermore, the correlation between the SUS and MDPQ‐J was weak because the SUS is mainly a questionnaire evaluating the usability of mobile devices in general, whereas the MDPQ‐J is a scale evaluating the user's proficiency in detailed items related to mobile device control, and the evaluation of each operation of mobile devices in detail is challenging for the SUS. A better way to assess the digital literacy of older adults would be to assess both the MDPQ‐J and the SUS and consider them in terms of proficiency and usability.

The advantage of the MDPQ‐J is that it can easily identify the characteristics of older adults for detailed mobile device operation items and is expected to allow quantitative evaluation of changes in proficiency levels through panel assessment and the effects of interventions. Currently, the use of ICT to support people in their daily lives is important. 22 Life has become increasingly digitized in recent years, and the ability to use mobile devices can alleviate some of life's difficulties. As evidence, MDPQ‐J scores are related to the importance, performance, and satisfaction with using mobile devices among older adults, indicating that improving proficiency with mobile devices may contribute to maintaining independent living among older adults.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants’ cognitive function was not assessed. Cognitive function is related to the ability to use technology as well as to the ability to use mobile devices, especially in older individuals. 23 , 24 Although participants who were predicted to have potential cognitive impairment were excluded from recruitment in this study, it may have been necessary to use a more objective assessment of cognitive function. Future studies should use a simplified cognitive assessment scale to examine the relationship of cognitive impairment with the MDPQ‐J in more detail. Second, the target number of participants for this study was not based on a priori calculations. A post hoc power analysis was performed to test the validity of the number of participants in this study. From previous reports on the validity of the MDPQ, 9 , 10 the expected correlation coefficient was set at 0.30 for young or middle‐aged adults and at 0.60 for older adults. The post hoc power to detect these correlation coefficients significantly was 0.85 for young or middle‐aged participants and 0.99 for older participants. This result suggests that the sample size of this study was sufficient to detect the expected correlation coefficients. However, to improve the reliability of the MDPQ‐J, reproducibility and factor structure should be investigated in future studies. Nevertheless, our results not only reveal the level of mobile device proficiency among the older population in Japan but also make an important contribution to comparisons across different cultural and racial populations and to the identification of the key points needed to provide technical assistance to older adults.

Conclusion

We created the MDPQ‐J by using a back‐translation process, and it is sufficiently reliable and valid as a mobile device proficiency assessment for the older Japanese population. As mobile device ownership increases among older adults, this questionnaire is expected to provide the necessary information needed to adapt mobile devices for use in their lives. In the future, the MDPQ will be used worldwide as a benchmark to determine the ability of older populations to adapt to ICT.
