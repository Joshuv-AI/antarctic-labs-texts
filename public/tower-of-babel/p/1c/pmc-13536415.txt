Craniofacial and airway changes after skeletal Class III orthognathic surgery: A machine learning study
Authors: Zay Yar Linn, Johnson Hsin-Chung Cheng, Sam Li-Sheng Chen, Daniel De-Shing Chen, Muhammad Izzah Abdillah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposeMandibular setback surgery is a common orthognathic procedure for correcting Class III malocclusion. This study aimed to evaluate posterior airway space (PAS) changes in Class III patients following mandibular setback surgery, focusing on skeletal, dental, and soft tissue alterations and their association with airway changes using conventional statistics and machine learning.Materials and methodsFifty patients who underwent mandibular setback surgery were assessed using cephalometric radiographs taken preoperatively (T1) and postoperatively (T2). Measurements included PAS area and anteroposterior width, alongside angular and linear cephalometric parameters. Paired t-tests evaluated pre- and post-treatment differences. Multivariate regression, Pearson correlation and Random Forest regression were used to analyse associations between craniofacial changes and PAS alterations.ResultsSignificant postoperative changes were observed in most craniofacial and airway variables, except for SNA, overbite, lower lip to E-plane, and hypopharynx area. Multivariate regression showed that age, sex and several other variables influenced PAS measurements, but their effects were not uniform across all airway levels. Pearson correlation showed the strongest associations between PAS changes and SNB, ANB, mandibular body length, overjet, SN-GoGn, and Z-angle. Random forest regression demonstrated low overall predictive performance; however, the model identified SNB, nasolabial angle, overjet as the most influential predictors of airway changes.ConclusionMandibular setback surgery significantly reduces PAS. Skeletal changes exert the greatest influence on airway, followed by soft tissue and dental variables. Horizontal craniofacial landmarks movements are more predictive of airway changes than vertical shifts. SNB emerged as the most influential variable in both correlation and machine learning analyses.

Introduction

Patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion typically exhibit a retrusive maxilla and/or mandibular prognathism.1 Treatment for these patients may involve camouflage orthodontics or orthognathic surgery, depending on the severity and the patient’s preferences, in order to enhance both aesthetics and occlusion.2 Surgical interventions commonly include maxillary advancement, mandibular setback, or a combination of both, with LeFort I osteotomy and bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO) being the most frequently used techniques.3

The soft palate, tongue, hyoid bone, and surrounding soft tissues are interconnected with the maxilla and mandible. Consequently, repositioning these skeletal structures can lead to adaptive changes in the soft tissues.4 These adaptations may result in a narrowing of the PAS, raising concerns about the potential development of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) following mandibular setback surgery.5 While maxillary advancement generally tends to increase airway space, mandibular setback is often associated with a reduction in PAS.4 However, some studies have found no significant postoperative changes in the airway,6 and the link between PAS narrowing after orthognathic surgery and OSA development remains inconclusive.7,8

Lateral cephalometric radiographs are frequently used in orthodontic and orthognathic evaluations because they are accessible, cost-effective, and expose patients to less radiation compared to computed tomography (CT) and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).9,10 However, LCRs have limitations due to their two-dimensional representation of the inherently three-dimensional airway.7 Despite these limitations, studies show that CT and CBCT do not always provide significantly better assessments of the airway, which supports the continued use of LCRs for airway evaluation in clinical practice.9

Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) has advanced rapidly and is now widely utilized in both medical and dental fields, especially for diagnosis and treatment planning.11 A subset of AI, known as machine learning, has been applied in orthodontics to automatically identify landmarks and assist in making decisions about extraction patterns and the necessity of orthognathic surgery.12–14 However, few previous studies have employed machine learning algorithms to predict changes in airway dimensions following orthognathic surgery. Previous studies have mainly relied on conventional statistical methods, which may fail to capture the complex, nonlinear interactions among skeletal, dental, and soft tissue factors. To address this limitation, the present study integrates cephalometric analysis with a machine learning approach to identify key craniofacial predictors of postoperative airway changes in skeletal Class III patients following mandibular setback surgery. The Random Forest algorithm was selected for its ability to handle nonlinear data and determine variable importance; it has also been applied in previous research to analyze craniofacial and dental changes in Class III orthognathic surgery.15 By combining conventional statistical and machine learning methods, this study aims to improve understanding of postoperative airway alterations and support more predictive, individualized orthognathic treatment planning.

Materials and methods

This retrospective observational cohort study aimed to evaluate changes in the posterior airway space (PAS) among patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion following orthognathic surgery. The study was conducted at the Department of Orthodontics, Taipei Medical University Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan, and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee/Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University (IRB No. N20240531).

Patients who received orthodontic and orthognathic treatment at Taipei Medical University Hospital were included for the study. The data collection was carried out from August 2024 to February 2025. From an initial random sample of 75 patients, 50 (mean age: 22.1 ± 6.2 years; range: 16–56 years; 24 males, 26 females) were selected based on specific inclusion criteria: a diagnosis of skeletal Class III malocclusion, treatment involving orthodontics and mandibular setback surgery, availability of clear pretreatment (T1) and post-treatment (T2) lateral cephalometric radiographs, and consent to the use of their medical records.

Written informed consent was obtained from the subjects for the use of radiographic data for research purposes. All the data collection were followed anonymization principles of Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM) guidelines. All radiographs were taken by experienced radiologists with patients in natural head position and with the teeth in maximum intercuspation. Post-treatment radiographs (T2) were taken immediately after debonding, reflecting a postoperative interval of more than six months due to the orthodontic detailing phase.

Patients with congenital deformities, facial trauma, previous orthodontic or surgical treatment, an ANB angle greater than 0°, facial asymmetry, body mass index (BMI) below 18.5 and over 25, or those who underwent genioplasty or tonsil/adenoid surgery were excluded.

The cephalometric landmarks evaluated in this study included glabella (G), soft tissue nasion (N’), pronasale (Pn), subnasale (Sn), labrale superior (Ls), labrale inferior (Li), stomion superius (Sts), stomion inferius (Sti), soft tissue pogonion (Pog’), soft tissue menton (Me’), nasion (N), orbitale (Or), sella (S), point A (A), point B (B), point D (D), pogonion (Pog), gonion (Go), gnathion (Gn), menton (Me), porion (Po), upper incisor tip (Is), upper incisor apex (UIA), lower incisor tip (Ii), lower incisor apex (LIA), and hyoid (H) (Appendix Table 1). For each case, a total of 19 cephalometric parameters were measured, with detailed descriptions available in Appendix Table 2.

Airway assessment was carried out according to the Arnett/Gunson FAB Surgery cephalometric analysis. Four lines parallel to the Frankfort horizontal plane were drawn through point A (A), upper incisor apex (Is), point B (B), and pogonion (Pog) to the posterior pharyngeal wall to define airway segments. Linear airway measurements included PAS at points A, Is, B, and Pog, defined by the distance between the anterior and posterior pharyngeal walls at each reference line. Airway area measurements included the nasopharynx area, oropharynx area, hypopharynx area, and total airway area, measured between the defined superior and inferior reference lines and the pharyngeal walls (Fig. 1).

Lateral cephalograms were manually traced by a single operator and were rechecked by two experienced orthodontists. PAS measurements were performed using SketchAndCalc Area Calculator software (iCalc, Inc., Palm Coast, FL, USA), and cephalometric measurements were carried out using WEBCEPH digital cephalometric software (AssembleCircleCorp., Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). A constructed horizontal line (cHL) was generated by rotating the sella–nasion (SN) line 7° clockwise,16 with a constructed vertical line (cVL) perpendicular to cHL passing through sella (S) to create an X–Y coordinate system for measuring horizontal and vertical positional changes in landmarks at T1 and T2. To assess reliability, 15 cephalograms were retraced after several months, with intraclass correlation coefficients and measurement errors were calculated.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 19.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Reliability was assessed through the intraclass correlation coefficient, and an a priori power analysis performed with G*Power (version 3.1.9.7, Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf) indicated that 44 participants were required to detect a medium effect size (dz = 0.5) with 90 % power at α = 0.05 using a two-tailed paired t-test. Paired t-tests were used to compare the area and linear measurements of the PAS between pretreatment (T1) and post-treatment (T2) radiographs. Multivariate regression was performed to evaluate the influence of key confounding factors, including age, sex, amount of setback, and mandibular rotation, together with skeletal, dental, and soft-tissue variables, on changes in PAS. Pearson correlation was applied to evaluate the association between PAS changes and skeletal, dental, soft tissue, and landmark variables. Furthermore, a random forest regression model was implemented using machine learning algorithms in Python (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) to determine the factors associated with changes in the PAS.

Results

A total of 50 patients undergoing surgical correction of skeletal Class III malocclusion were included in this study. The majority of participants (94 %) were aged between 16 and 30 years, with 4 % aged 31–45 years and 2 % over 45 years with a nearly equal gender distribution (48 % male, 52 % female). All patients underwent mandibular setback surgery, with intraoral vertical ramus osteotomy (IVRO) performed in 98 % of cases, while bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO) was utilized in the remaining 2 % (Appendix Table 3).

Appendix Table 4 summarizes the preoperative (T1), postoperative (T2), and change (T2–T1) values for cephalometric measurements. All parameters showed statistically significant postoperative changes, except for SNA, overbite, and lower lip to the E-plane. Appendix Table 5 provides the corresponding vertical and horizontal landmark measurements at T1 and T2. Significant changes were observed in all measurements, except for the horizontal positions of Point A and UL, which did not reach statistical significance.

Preoperative (T1) and postoperative (T2) linear and area PAS measurements showed significant reduction in all parameters except the hypopharynx area (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A and B).

Multivariate regression showed that several variables demonstrated significant associations. Age emerged as the most consistent contributor, whereas sex, mandibular rotation, and other variables including SNB, overbite, lip lengths, nasolabial angle, facial contour angle, occlusal plane angle, and upper lip to E-plane exhibited isolated positive or negative effects that were limited to particular airway measurements (Table 1).

Pearson correlation results showed that SNB had a moderate positive correlation with upper hypopharyngeal width, while ANB and SN-GoGn were weakly negatively correlated with oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal widths. The Z-angle showed a weak positive correlation, and upper lip length showed a weak negative correlation with airway widths. No significant dental correlations were noted (Table 2). Overbite and nasolabial angle showed weak correlations with the oropharynx and nasopharynx areas, respectively; other associations were not statistically significant (Table 3).

Vertical landmarks showed limited correlations with airway widths, while horizontal measurements demonstrated several significant positive associations—most notably lower lip length with nasopharyngeal width and Me’ with upper hypopharyngeal width (Table 4). For airway area, only overbite and nasolabial angle showed weak correlations with oropharynx and nasopharynx areas, respectively (Table 5).

Random forest regression analysis revealed that craniofacial variables had limited predictive value for airway dimensions. Positive R2 values were found only for lower hypopharyngeal width, while the gonial angle, SN-GoGn angle, and the occlusal plane angle showed weak associations with upper pharyngeal width. Among the soft tissue variables, the facial contour angle and upper lip length exhibited minor predictive relevance for lower hypopharyngeal width. All other variables demonstrated negligible or negative predictive values (Figs. 3–5).

Appendix Fig. 1 indicated that vertical changes in Point A, UL, and LL had modest predictive value for airway outcomes, with the highest R2 (0.37) observed for Point A and the nasopharynx area. Appendix Fig. 2 showed that horizontal changes of Point A, Point D, Hyoid, and Me demonstrated low positive R2 values (0.01–0.26), suggesting limited predictive relevance for postoperative airway dimensions.

Fig. 6 presents the feature importance scores for all cephalometric variables. SNB, nasolabial angle, overjet, facial contour angle, and mandibular body length were identified as the most influential predictors, while ANB, L1 to mandibular plane angle, and occlusal plane angle showed moderate influence, with the remaining variables having minimal contribution.

Discussion

This study consisted of a relatively uniform group of young adults, with a balanced gender distribution, providing a stable baseline for evaluating airway changes following mandibular setback surgery. In comparison to other studies, nearly all participants underwent IVRO technique, ensuring procedural consistency and reducing confounding variables, which allowed for a more precise assessment of treatment-related alterations in PAS.

Postoperative skeletal measurements demonstrated significant modifications. Notably, reductions in SNB and mandibular body length, along with an increased ANB, indicated effective mandibular repositioning, consistent with C. U. JOSS AND U. W. THÜER findings following mandibular setback surgery.17 The observed increases in SN-GoGn and gonial angle likely reflect mandibular clockwise rotation associated with IVRO, in agreement with Yoshioka et al.18 Dental changes demonstrated the combined effect of orthodontics and surgery, including reduced maxillary incisor inclination and increased mandibular incisor proclination, as also reported by Zou et al.19 Overjet increased postoperatively, while overbite remained stable, aligning with Halvorsen et al.20 The occlusal plane angle also increased, reflecting adaptation to skeletal changes. Soft tissue analysis revealed increased upper lip length and nasolabial angle, consistent with Mobarak et al. and attributed to muscular adjustments post-surgery as suggested by Eggensperger et al.21,22 Increased upper lip to E-plane and reduced lower lip length aligned with prior report by Marsan et al.23 Decreased facial contour and Z-angles indicated reduced chin prominence and a flatter profile, as noted by Kanwal et al.24

Landmark assessments revealed significant postoperative positional changes, primarily characterized by posterior and inferior displacements. These findings align with Reyneke, who associated airway changes with vertical movement of PNS and horizontal or upward movement of the D-point.25 The posterior shifts reflected the effects of mandibular setback, while inferior displacements were likely due to clockwise mandibular rotation following IVRO,26 consistent with observations by Kimet al. and Eggensperger et al.27,28 In contrast, the vertical changes differed from those reported by C. U. JOSS AND U. W. THÜER,17 whose use of BSSO with rigid fixation often resulted in minimal vertical movement and counterclockwise rotation.29 The inferior and posterior shift of the hyoid bone observed in this study was also supported by Eggensperger et al.28

All PAS measurements in this study showed a decreasing trend after surgery, which is consistent with previous findings from lateral cephalograms2,30 and CBCT assessments.1,5 The total PAS area was reduced by 18.77 % (140.46 mm2), comparable to the 12.78 % reduction reported by Tselnik and Pogrel.31 The oropharynx area showed the greatest decrease, followed by the nasopharynx, while the hypopharynx exhibited only minor change. Linear measurements similarly indicated that the most pronounced narrowing occurred at the oropharyngeal width and upper hypopharyngeal width. These findings align with the CBCT results of Jeong et al.,32 who observed significant reductions at the CV1 and CV2 levels but no significant changes at the CV3 and CV4 levels. However, Wenzel et al. mentioned in their discussion that a decrease in airway size does not necessarily imply a corresponding increase in airway resistance.33

Age showed a consistent negative association with airway measurements and emerged as the most influential factor affecting PAS changes. This finding aligns with the well-established understanding that pharyngeal airway space decreases with increasing age.8,34 Similarly, sex showed a positive correlation supporting the fact that females are more suspectable to airway changes.34 Other significant predictors only isolated associations, suggesting that these factors do not exert a broad or consistent influence on overall airway morphology.

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between skeletal, dental, soft tissue, and cephalometric landmark changes and PAS alterations following mandibular setback surgery. Since all patients underwent mandibular setback orthognathic surgery, most changes occurred in the lower facial region. Consequently, the most correlations were found with skeletal variables, followed by soft tissue variables. Dental variables showed only minimal correlations, as they were least affected by the treatment. Similarly, for landmark changes, horizontal movements demonstrated more correlations with airway changes than vertical movements.

Random forest regression analysis further substantiated these findings, showing generally low R2 values across all variable categories, which reflects a limited ability to predict postoperative airway changes following mandibular setback surgery. Among the assessed predictors, skeletal parameters exhibited the highest predictive value for airway alterations, followed by soft tissue variables. In contrast, dental variables contributed the least to the prediction of airway morphology.

Horizontal positional changes of cephalometric landmarks demonstrated greater predictive relevance for postoperative airway dimensions than vertical changes, consistent with the results of the Pearson correlation analysis. These findings underscore the significant role of anteroposterior skeletal and soft tissue movements in shaping airway morphology after mandibular setback surgery, whereas dental variables and vertical displacements appear to have a minimal impact on airway outcomes.

Analysis of the feature importance scores suggested that skeletal variables were weighted more heavily by the model than soft-tissue or dental factors when estimating PAS-related changes. A similar pattern appeared in the Pearson correlation results, which showed comparatively stronger associations for skeletal variables, fewer for soft-tissue measures, and minimal relationships for dental components. Although these trends are exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously given the model’s limited predictive performance, they collectively imply that skeletal characteristics may have a relatively greater influence on airway morphology. This observation supports the relevance of skeletal evaluation within the broader context of orthognathic surgery planning.

This retrospective study used existing clinical data to evaluate outcomes after mandibular setback surgery. Several limitations should be noted: cephalometric analysis was restricted to two-dimensional views, limiting assessment of three-dimensional changes; and the modest sample size, although consistent with G*Power calculation, may have introduced bias. Advanced methods such as convolutional neural networks and objective OSA assessments, including polysomnography and clinical measures (e.g., body weight, neck circumference), were also not included. Future studies with larger samples, 3D imaging, and objective sleep evaluations are recommended to enhance predictive accuracy and better characterize airway risks.

Mandibular setback orthognathic surgery narrows the airway, most notably in the oropharynx, followed by the nasopharynx and hypopharynx. Skeletal changes exert greater influence on airway dimensions than soft tissue or dental factors, with horizontal landmark movements having a stronger impact than vertical, highlighting the need for careful consideration in treatment planning.

This study demonstrates that mandibular setback surgery results in significant reductions in the PAS, primarily influenced by skeletal and soft tissue factors, with minimal contribution from dental variables. The research found that horizontal displacements of anatomical landmarks have a greater effect on airway changes compared to vertical shifts. These findings highlight the importance of thorough preoperative assessment of soft tissue and skeletal structures to minimize the risk of postoperative airway compromise. Future research employing 3D imaging, larger sample, and objective sleep assessments will be valuable in providing a clearer understanding of airway changes and related risks following mandibular setback procedures, ultimately guiding safer and more predictable treatment planning.
