16S rRNA Amplicon Sequencing of the Intraoperative Surgical Site Demonstrates an Aberrant Microbiota Which Can Predict Colorectal Surgical Site Infection
Authors: Veral Vishnoi, Emily C. Hoedt, Michelle Zhao, Rosemary Carroll, Shaveen Kanakaratne, Natalie Lott, Peter Pockney, Stephen R. Smith, Simon Keely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSurgical Site Infection (SSI) of an abdominal incision is a dreaded infectious complication following colorectal surgery. Understanding the intraoperative microbiology holds the key to prevention. Genomic sequencing has revolutionised culture‐independent techniques, allowing for a more sensitive tool in microbial detection. The purpose of this study is to determine whether an aberrant microbiota is present within the intraoperative surgical site in patients that develop an SSI.MethodsThe subcutaneous abdominal surgical site of patients undergoing elective bowel resection was opportunistically sampled, following fascial closure. DNA was recovered from the swabs and used for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. The intraoperative microbiota was compared in those with and without a subsequent SSI with focus on alpha diversity and beta diversity, as well as taxonomic composition.Results16/123 patients developed an SSI. The bacterial profile within the surgical site showed a strong presence of obligate anaerobes in all patients. Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson measures of alpha diversity from the intraoperative swabs were all significantly increased in SSI patients (p = 0.05, 0.05 and 0.05, respectively). Beta diversity demonstrated no significant separation between patients (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index, PERMANOVA p = 0.394).ConclusionsTo the best of our knowledge this is the first study to demonstrate an aberrant intraoperative microbiota of the surgical site associated with subsequent infection. Pending validation studies, this provides a target for the strategic manipulation of the surgical site to reduce the burden of infection.

Introduction

Incisional surgical site infection is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients undergoing emergency and elective colorectal resection. Traditional thinking has attributed the higher rate of SSIs in this form of surgery due to contamination from the gastrointestinal tract. However, strategies to mitigate contamination intraoperatively have failed to reduce rates of SSIs convincingly. These strategies may be misguided as preventative measures to control infection have been derived from results of the postoperative microbiology when the infection has already developed. Common pathogens isolated by conventional methods (culture and sensitivity testing) in colorectal surgery include E. coli, K. pneumoniae, Proteus mirablis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and S. aureus , reflecting both gastrointestinal and skin flora [1, 2, 3].

It is clear we require more specific strategies to curb the threat of SSIs in colorectal surgery. The microbiology of the intraoperative surgical site presents a unique opportunity for investigation. As contamination is believed to be the cause of eventual infection, characterising the microbial profile of the intraoperative surgical site may be the key to tailored preventative measures. Previous studies have shown that a ‘positive’ intraoperative culture identifies a patient who is at higher risk of developing a clinical SSI, but application is limited due to its inability to correctly identify the pathogen(s) involved [4, 5, 6]. This illustrates the limitation of culture‐dependent methods as only certain microorganisms thrive in the preselected nutritional and physiological conditions used in the laboratory to culture and isolate organisms.

Utilisation of next generation sequencing through amplification of the 16 s rRNA gene is a culture‐independent technique that provides a detailed characterisation of the microbial composition of a specimen based on the genetic material identified. This is collectively termed the microbiota [7]. Based on the results from our pilot study, we used 16 s rRNA amplicon sequencing to characterise the microbiome of the intraoperative surgical site in patients undergoing elective colorectal resection [8]. In this pilot, we identified a potential aberrance in those that eventually developed infection. Here we report a powered investigation that sampled the intraoperative surgical site, in patients undergoing elective colorectal resection. The intention of this study was to sample the surgical site at the end of the operative case to firstly characterise the microbiome, but secondly to identify predictors of infection. The key components of the microbiome that were investigated in this study include alpha diversity, beta diversity and taxonomy. Alpha diversity refers to within group differences, whereas beta refers to between groups. Taxonomy refers to the different types of bacteria present within the samples. To the best of our knowledge this is the largest powered study to use 16s rRNA sequencing to sample the surgical site in colorectal surgery.

Methods

Patient Screening, Recruitment and Progression

This was a prospective observational multi‐centre cohort study conducted as a collaboration between the Hunter New England Local Health District (HNELHD), Hunter Medical Research institute (HMRI) and the University of Newcastle (UoN) over a one‐year period. This project had ethical approval by the local Hunter New England human research ethics committee (HNEHREC No: 17/05/17/4.05). Patients undergoing a bowel resection and primary anastomosis by specialist colorectal surgeons were screened. Patients over the age of 18, undergoing an elective bowel resection and primary anastomosis were included. Patients were excluded from the study if they were on intravenous or oral antibiotics for nonsurgical reasons within 4 weeks of their scheduled resection or if active infection or inflammation was found at the time of the operation.

Following written informed consent, patients progressed to have their bowel resection. The types of resections were broadly categorised into right sided (proximal to splenic flexure) and left sided (distal to splenic flexure) resections. Routine standard care proceeded unchanged. All patients underwent preoperative skin decontamination via triclosan wash. All patients received an intravenous induction general anaesthesia and prophylactic intravenous antibiotics, which included a 2nd generation cephalosporin and metronidazole. Preoperative skin preparation varied between clinicians. Following resection of the intended segment of bowel and fascial closure, specimens from the subcutaneous wound were collected prior to closure of the skin. In open cases, the midline laparotomy wound was sampled. In laparoscopic assisted cases, the lower midline/Pfannenstiel extraction site was sampled. A standard eSWAB (Copan, Italy) was used by the operating team to swab the entire length of the subcutaneous incision. The swab was immediately snap frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored in a −80°C freezer for processing later. The wound was covered with a sterile dressing and not removed until day 7 for wound review. Postoperatively, the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol was followed as per the unit's practice.

The primary outcome was the development of an incisional SSI. In this study we defined SSI as per the units' practice, which included clinical evidence of an infection, either superficial or deep, within the surgical incision (supra‐fascial). This includes the presence of erythema, cellulitis, a collection, or purulent discharge from the wound. The infection must have occurred within 30 days and required intervention. Intervention was considered conservative with oral or intravenous antibiotics or reoperation with incision and drainage.

Patients were excluded from the study if there was deviation from standard care such as a return to the operating room for reasons other than surgical site infection (haemorrhage and anastomotic leak). Patients were followed up until day 30, the end point of the study. Surveillance was performed through outpatient clinics in which patients and their wounds were reviewed. Additional surveillance was achieved via phone contact with patients and their general practitioner to ensure all patients with a postoperative SSI were identified.

Clinical Statistics

Clinical variables were documented and assessed for significance using chi‐squared and t‐test for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Multivariate logistical regression was performed to determine the adjusted odds ratios for clinical variables. All these tests were performed on R studio.

16S rRNA Amplicon Sequencing Methods

The swabs were stored in a secure biobank before being sent to Australian centre for Ecogenomics (ACE) in batches for processing. A blank eSWAB was included in each batch as a negative control. Following DNA extraction, the V3 and V4 regions were targeted and amplified before undergoing parallel sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq Sequencing System, as previously described by Vishnoi et al. [8].

Microbiota Analysis

Bioinformatic Pipeline

The raw sequence data was processed using QIIME2 and DADA2. Taxonomic assignment and further filtering was performed on R studio.

Microbiota Analytical Approach

The overall composition of the intraoperative surgical site was determined and visualised via a heat tree using the Metacoder package [9]. Metrics used for comparison included alpha diversity, beta diversity, taxonomic comparison, and differential abundance testing. The primary comparison in this study was between those with an SSI and without an SSI.

Alpha Diversity

Chao1, Shannon and Simpson were selected as measures of alpha diversity. It was calculated at an ASV level. Phyloseq [10] was used to estimate these values for each clinical sample before using R studio to perform subsequent statistical analysis to assess for significance. The Phyloseq package was also used to generate boxplots to visualise the differences in alpha diversity between groups. Means testing between groups. The Shapiro–Wilk test demonstrated a non‐normal distribution (p = 0.005) for all diversity measures, hence parametric testing was not appropriate. As such, Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests with false discovery rate (WCR FDR) for multiple corrections was implemented to assess for significant differences in the mean of alpha diversity with binary outcomes (i.e., SSI, yes or no).

Beta Diversity

Beta diversity was calculated at an ASV level using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index and visualised via boxplots. To determine if a significant difference was present between groups, permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was tested. This was performed on the microeco package on R studio [11].

Taxonomic Composition

The absolute ASV count data was transformed into relative abundance. Visual representation of the taxonomic differences between groups was achieved via boxplots and line plots through the Microeco package on R studio [11]. Due to poor assignment at the species and genus levels, comparison between clinical groups was performed only at the taxonomic level of family. Visualisation was limited to the top 8 most abundant families.

Differential Abundance Testing

WCR FDR and ANCOMBC were used to determine differentially abundant taxa between the variables of interest. Taxa were determined to be differentially abundant only if both techniques of statistical testing were satisfied. Based on the results from our pilot study, differential abundance (DA) testing at the level off family identified over 400 taxa to undergo paired analysis. Most of these taxa carried a relative abundance of 0.0001 or lower. Hence their presence is unlikely to be significant. Therefore, for clinical relevance DA testing was limited to the top 20 most abundant taxa in this study.

16S rRNA Sequencing Bioinformatic Pipeline Results

A total of 125 samples were included in two separate sequencing runs (123 clinical samples with two clean negative controls). These runs were independently processed, trimmed, paired ends merged, denoised and formed into ASVs. Two separate sequence read tables were created and merged into one as the final dataset. Taxonomic assignment against the SILVA SSU r138 reference database identified 9192 unique ASVs in the final dataset. The sequence read count in the negative controls was low and, as such, they were filtered out (Batch 1–238, Batch 2–93). Finally, clinical samples with < 500 sequence read counts were removed. This left a final dataset of 122 samples. The maximum sequence read count was 106 333 and the minimum was 703.

The Microbiota of the Intraoperative of the Surgical Site

The microbial profile of the surgical site in all patients is dominated by bacteria belonging to the phyla Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota and Actinobacteria. Further classification at a class level shows dominance with Clostridia, Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia, with very limited presence of skin flora such as Bacilli and Actinobacteria (Figure 1).

Discussion

The factors leading to surgical site infections are a combination of microbial presence, microbial load and the host‐immune response [12]. The inability to cultivate bacteria using culture‐dependent methods has resulted in an underappreciation for the role that intraoperative bacteria play in the pathogenesis of surgical site infection. Motivated by the results from our pilot study [8], we exploited 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing to characterise the intraoperative bacteria within the surgical site with the intention of demonstrating an aberrance in those with an eventual infection. The findings are significant as those with an eventual infection have a significantly different microbial diversity at the time of the operation. These findings will add to the body of literature regarding surgical site infections, but they raise further questions which require investigation.

In this study, the surgical site is characterised by the presence of obligate anaerobes Lachnospiraceae, Bacteroidacaea and Ruminococcacea. Their presence is very suggestive of endogenous gastrointestinal contamination; however, confirmation is not possible because there is no reference point to indicate what is normal or native to the abdominal subcutaneous plane. Additionally, we have identified families Rubrobacterceae, Pseudonacardaceae and Marinococcaceae which, to the best of our knowledge, have not been cultured in humans before [13]. However, we cannot confirm their origin as they may be unidentified members of the gut lumen, native skin flora or exogenous contamination from other sources. Finally, we have also seen Moraxellaceae, a gram‐negative aerobic family that colonises the upper respiratory tract in humans [14]. It is not a member of the gut lumen, and its presence in the surgical site requires clarification, as it could suggest exogenous respiratory contamination from members of the operative team. Overall, it appears that the intraoperative surgical site is an amalgamation of bacteria with many origins. However, given the paucity of studies investigating the surgical subcutaneous space, it is not possible to determine what is native to the skin and what is contamination (endogenous or exogenous). Ultimately, to determine the source of the bacteria we have identified, we need to consider dynamic sampling of the surgical site, before, during and after the operation to identify the origin and pathogenicity of the identified bacteria.

In this powered study, we can confirm that an increase in alpha diversity is associated with surgical site infection. Chao1, Shannon and Simpson were all statistically higher on means testing, highlighting the potential of the subcutaneous intraoperative surgical site alpha diversity in predicting a subsequent SSI. This is the first report of intraoperative surgical site microbiota‐derived predictors of SSI that we are aware of. Whilst this is valuable and may allow us to predict those at an increased risk of infection, it does not allow us to prevent infection as we do not have a target for intervention. We need to consider what accounts for this increase in alpha diversity in patients with an SSI. Alpha diversity may be determined using different calculations, which give weight to rare and dominant taxa alike. We have used Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson, the combination of which allows us to have a uniform understanding of the alpha diversity of the surgical site [15]. Beta diversity and taxonomic composition complement alpha diversity. In our study, there is no separation in beta diversity between patients with an SSI or those without an SSI. This is reiterated with respect to taxonomic composition as no family of bacteria was determined to be differentially abundant. Therefore, the increase in alpha diversity in SSI patients is unlikely to be related to the types of bacteria present. Instead, it may relate to the microbial load, thus providing a future avenue for investigation to determine the cause of increased alpha diversity and the role of microbial load in subsequent SSIs. This may provide a therapeutic target which would facilitate strategic manipulation of the wound.

The main strength of this study is its sample size: it is specifically powered to detect differences in alpha diversity, a key feature of the microbiota. To our knowledge, this is the largest observational study demonstrating intraoperative surgical‐site microbiota aberrations that may predict infections after colorectal resection. However, the study is not powered to assess differences in other clinical factors (e.g., skin preparation), which should be addressed in future microbial‐driven research. While 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing shows great promise, it cannot resolve species‐level identity or absolute functional activity. This limits immediate opportunities for targeted interventions but supports further investigation using higher‐resolution genomic methods such as metagenomics to clarify species composition and functional potential. Additional limitations include the inability to determine the cause of increased alpha diversity in patients who developed SSIs; if greater microbial load drives higher alpha diversity, reducing contamination load could be a viable strategy to lower SSI rates in colorectal surgery.
