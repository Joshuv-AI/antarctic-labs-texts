Silence, stigma, and sexual health: experiences of sexual dysfunction among men living with diabetes in Ghana
Authors: Mercy Adjei Adumatta, Mawuli Kushitor, Wisdom Majida Zinnya Mahama, Sophia Ayabalie Adala, David Binpi Nayiden, Linda Quainoo, Sukpen Reuben Uwumbor-Apak, Naab Joseph Kologbire, Helen K. Arkorful
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundErectile dysfunction (ED) is a common but under-discussed complication among men living with diabetes (MLWD), particularly in African contexts where sexual health is culturally sensitive. Despite major psychosocial and relational consequences, ED is often inadequately addressed within routine diabetes care. This study explored perceptions, impacts, and management practices related to sexual weakness among MLWD, alongside healthcare providers’ experiences managing the condition in Ghana.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory qualitative study across five public healthcare facilities in four regions of Ghana. In-depth interviews were conducted with 26 MLWD and 14 healthcare providers involved in diabetes care. Participants were purposively recruited, and data were analysed using thematic analysis supported by ATLAS.ti.ResultsParticipants attributed sexual weakness to multiple causes, including diabetes-related complications, medication effects, and spiritual factors. Divergent explanatory models between patients and providers shaped management decisions and care-seeking. Many men reported using herbal remedies, aphrodisiacs, energy drinks, and spiritual interventions, often delaying biomedical care. Sexual weakness was associated with distress, reduced confidence, and relationship strain, and disclosure was selective, shaped by stigma, secrecy, and trust. Providers expressed concern about misconceptions, unregulated treatment use, and structural constraints within overcrowded diabetes clinics that limited meaningful engagement on sexual health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15057-1.

Introduction

Health-seeking behaviour among men with diabetes: perspectives on sexual dysfunction

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is an increasingly common yet highly sensitive health concern among men living with diabetes (MLWD) in Africa [1–4]. Available evidence suggests that between 50% and 75% of MLWD experience ED, with prevalence rates reported to be approximately twice as high as among men without diabetes [5, 6]. As the burden of diabetes and other cardiovascular diseases continues to rise across the continent, the prevalence of ED is also expected to increase [7]; International Diabetes Federation, [8]; International Diabetes Federation, [9]). Despite its clinical and psychosocial significance, ED is frequently overlooked in routine care in many African health settings [10, 11]. Both clinicians and MLWD may avoid discussing sexual dysfunction because of its cultural sensitivity [12–14], resulting in silence that may contribute to the growing burden of ED and its associated complications.

The limited body of research available paints a concerning picture of how MLWD navigate care for ED, often seeking assistance discreetly from informal and unregulated sources [15–17]. Much of the existing literature on ED is quantitative, documenting prevalence and risk factors across settings ([18]. While such data are important, they provide limited insight into the lived experiences of men affected by ED. This study, therefore, seeks to explore the sexual experiences of MLWD, as well as the experiences of health providers involved in managing ED among this population.

Although many men may not actively seek care for ED within formal health facilities [19–21], some evidence indicates that they turn to unregulated remedies, including combinations of herbal preparations and aphrodisiacs [22–24] which may exacerbate health problems [25]. Overall, there is a limited understanding of ED among both MLWD and health providers. Longstanding evidence shows that mismatches between patients’ and providers’ understandings of health conditions can undermine adherence to treatment and management plans [26–28]. When patients perceive that their concerns cannot be adequately addressed within the health system, they may seek alternative sources of care [29]. At the same time, providers may not fully appreciate the complex ways in which living with diabetes shapes men’s experiences and treatment outcomes.

ED is not solely a physiological condition; it also carries significant social and psychological consequences [30, 31]. Studies have documented associations with reduced self-confidence and self-esteem, anxiety, depression, and relationship breakdowns [32, 33]. Diabetes itself is linked to a fluctuating and often distressing emotional burden, which ED can further intensify. Improving the management of ED, therefore, requires a nuanced understanding of both patient and provider perspectives.

In many public hospitals, diabetes clinics are overstretched, with health providers managing large patient volumes that limit the time available to address complications such as ED [34]. Clinic environments often do not provide sufficient privacy for discussions about sexuality, particularly within socially conservative contexts. Additionally, most diabetes clinics lack a full multidisciplinary team. Specialist care, such as endocrinology services, is largely confined to tertiary facilities in major urban centres, leaving many MLWD without access to specialised support [35]. Even where services are available, few health professionals receive training on men’s sexual health needs, which encompass not only clinical but also psychological dimensions. This study therefore examines how men living with diabetes and healthcare providers perceive, interpret, and manage sexual weakness within diabetes care settings in Ghana. By exploring both patient and provider perspectives, the study seeks to illuminate explanatory models, management practices, and contextual barriers that shape care-seeking behaviour and clinical engagement regarding sexual weakness.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an exploratory qualitative study to explore the lived experiences and perceptions of sexual weakness among men living with diabetes, its impact on their psychosocial wellbeing and relationships, and the strategies used to manage the condition. The study also examined healthcare providers’ perspectives on the causes, management practices, and challenges associated with addressing sexual weakness within diabetes care. This design was appropriate for capturing participants’ subjective experiences, meanings, and interpretations of sexual weakness within their sociocultural and clinical context.

Study setting

Data were collected from diabetes clinics in five public healthcare facilities across four regions of Ghana: Legon Hospital in the Ayawaso District (Greater Accra Region), Margaret Marquart Catholic Hospital in the Kpando Municipality (Volta Region), Sandema Hospital in the Builsa North Municipality (Upper East Region), War Memorial Hospital in the Kassena Nankana Municipality (Upper East Region), and Walewale Municipal Hospital in the North East Region. These sites were purposively selected to capture variation in cultural context, language, and healthcare delivery systems across urban and rural settings.

Study participants and sampling

The study involved two participant groups: men living with diabetes and healthcare providers involved in diabetes care. Eligible male participants were aged 30 years and above and had attended diabetes clinics for at least six months, ensuring sufficient experience with the condition and its management. Healthcare providers included physicians, nurses, diabetes educators, and counsellors who routinely provided care to diabetic patients.

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure inclusion of individuals with relevant knowledge and experience related to sexual weakness and its management. Among the male participants, 10 were recruited from Kpando Municipality, 8 from Ayawaso District, and 8 from Builsa North Municipality. Healthcare providers included 10 participants from Kassena Nankana Municipality and 4 from Walewale Municipality.

Sample size was guided by the principle of data saturation. Interviews continued until no new themes or meaningful insights emerged, indicating that sufficient depth and breadth of information had been obtained to address the study objectives.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth face-to-face interviews using a semi-structured interview guide adapted from the RODAM qualitative protocols developed by Aikins [36]. Interviews were conducted in English and, where necessary, in local languages (Twi and Ga), depending on participant preference, and later translated into English for analysis. The guide explored participant characteristics, experiences of sexual weakness, its impact, and communication and management practices within diabetes care. Interviews were conducted in private consulting rooms to ensure confidentiality and encourage open discussion. Probing questions were used to obtain deeper insights and clarify responses. All interviews were audio-recorded, with field notes taken to support data accuracy. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis to identify patterns and themes across participants’ narratives. All interviews were transcribed verbatim into English and reviewed repeatedly to ensure familiarity with the data. Coding was conducted iteratively by identifying meaningful units and grouping similar codes into sub-themes, which were further refined into broader themes representing shared patterns and key concepts. Themes were reviewed and refined to ensure clarity, consistency, and accurate representation of participants’ experiences. Data analysis was supported using ATLAS.ti version 7.5.7 (ATLAS.ti GmbH, Berlin), which facilitated systematic coding, organisation, and interpretation of the data. Relevant verbatim quotes were used to illustrate the findings.

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, participants were assigned unique identification codes, and no personally identifiable information was recorded. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Results

Introduction

This study explored perceptions, impacts, and management practices related to sexual weakness among men living with diabetes, drawing on in-depth interviews with both male patients and healthcare providers. Using a thematic analytic approach, the results capture convergent and divergent perspectives across participants, highlighting how sexual weakness is understood, experienced, and managed within the context of diabetes care. The findings are organized into dominant themes and subthemes reflecting perceived causes, psychosocial and relational impacts, management practices, disclosure patterns, and misconceptions surrounding sexual weakness. Where relevant, areas of agreement and contrast between patients and healthcare providers are presented. Although participants frequently described erectile difficulties and inability to sustain erection, the local expression “sexual weakness” occasionally also encompassed reduced sexual desire, diminished sexual performance, and broader concerns about masculinity and intimate relationships. However, erectile dysfunction emerged as the dominant manifestation discussed across interviews. Verbatim quotations are used to illustrate key themes and to preserve participants’ voices. Participants are identified using alphanumeric codes corresponding to their profiles in the sociodemographic tables presented in the supplementary file.

Perceived causes of sexual weakness

Participants attributed sexual weakness to multiple causes, including biomedical, treatment-related, and spiritual explanations. While healthcare providers commonly linked sexual dysfunction to diabetes-related nerve damage and vascular complications, many patients perceived it as a side effect of long-term diabetes medication. Some participants also framed sexual weakness as a spiritual issue, including curses or supernatural attacks, influencing their interpretation of the condition and subsequent care-seeking behaviours. As one healthcare provider explained,When they come, they are much particular about it. They say the medications have made them have erectile dysfunction. Some even decide not to take the medicine because of its side effects. (S2). A patient also said,And it’s like, when you take these medications for a long time, it reduces your ability to have sex. (L6)

Discussion

The study examined how men living with diabetes (MLWD) and health providers understand and interpret erectile dysfunction (ED). Divergences in these perspectives may partly explain poor adherence to diabetes management among men [27, 37]. The findings indicate that clients and providers frequently rely on fundamentally different explanatory models [38], which complicates effective ED management. Not all clients accept ED as a complication arising solely from poorly controlled diabetes, nor do they always align with the biomedical explanations offered by health professionals [39].de-Graft Aikins, [40], Kleinman, [41], Powers, [42]. ED, in particular, appears susceptible to misconceptions and unhelpful conspiratorial beliefs. Because opportunities for open discussion between clients and providers are limited, such beliefs often persist unchallenged.

Men experiencing ED often confide only in a small circle of trusted individuals due to the social consequences of disclosure [19], even though these confidants may lack accurate knowledge. The secrecy and stigma surrounding ED further limit accurate understanding of the condition. In public-sector clinics, where patient volumes are high, the sensitive nature of the ED, combined with the complexities of diabetes care, often limits opportunities for private, meaningful engagement. Disclosure also depends on clients’ trust in both the health system and individual providers. In this context, some men view ED as better addressed outside formal health facilities and seek spiritual or herbal remedies, returning to hospitals only when their condition has significantly worsened.

ED is widely perceived as emasculating [43], carrying a substantial social and psychological burden [44]. The implications of ED extend beyond the individual, affecting intimate relationships [45] and, in some cases, family stability [46, 47]. Although effective treatments for ED are available, the surrounding social, cultural, and clinical constraints often prevent their appropriate use.

Previous studies have advocated for the deliberate integration of ED management into routine clinical care to improve awareness and treatment uptake ([48]; Musape et al.,2022). However, such intentional inclusion raises important questions about feasibility within existing clinical processes and constraints. While hospitals aim to provide privacy through consulting rooms, many facilities lack adequate infrastructure, and even when space is available, time pressures and the clinical environment may not be conducive to sensitive discussions. Creating a supportive environment for ED management requires more than physical space; it also demands a consciously designed clinical culture that fosters trust and encourages patients to share deeply personal concerns [49].

To better address men’s specific needs, one possible approach is to designate separate clinic sessions for men and women. Men-only clinic days may provide a more enabling environment for men to engage openly with health professionals about ED and related concerns.

Conclusion

Sexual weakness among MLWD is shaped by intersecting biomedical, cultural, and structural factors. Mismatches between patient and provider perspectives, compounded by stigma and constrained clinical environments undermine disclosure and effective management. Integrating sexual health into routine diabetes care and strengthening supportive, trust-based clinical spaces may improve communication, care-seeking, and well-being.

Limitation

Although this study explored participants’ lived experiences of sexual weakness among men living with diabetes, it was not originally designed as a strict phenomenological study. In addition, the data collection tools were not fully developed within a phenomenological framework. This may have limited the depth of philosophical alignment typically expected in pure phenomenological research; however, the approach still allowed for rich qualitative insights into participants’ experiences and perceptions.
