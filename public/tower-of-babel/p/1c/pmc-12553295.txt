Regulation of domestic violence: a global perspective
Authors: Jane Fisher, Erica Lukas, Astghik Mavisakalyan, Jacqui True
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examines the global evolution of the laws addressing domestic violence (DV), providing insights on the number and types of laws adopted by countries around the world since early 1980s. It highlights disparities in law adoption across different regions and countries with varying levels of economic development.MethodsThe empirical analysis is based on data from Women, Business and the Law (WBL), covering 190 economies annually from 1980 to 2024. The study introduces a distinction between laws related to DV based on legal source, distinguishing between DV laws, gender-based violence (GBV) laws, family violence laws, and criminal code. The study then presents a systematic descriptive analysis of cross-country DV laws and their association with the intimate partner violence (IPV) prevalence.ResultsThere is a significant negative correlation between the presence of DV laws and the prevalence of IPV. The study finds that the type of law that regulates DV matters. Specifically, DV laws and criminal codes are more strongly associated with lower IPV prevalence. The number of laws regulating DV is also related to IPV prevalence. Additionally, the study reveals regional disparities in the adoption of laws, with higher-income countries adopting laws earlier than lower-income regions.ConclusionThe presence and types of DV laws matter significantly for IPV prevalence. Introduction

Domestic violence (DV), a form of violence against women and girls (VAWG), constitutes a violation of human rights and a major public health and social concern globally. Almost one in three women worldwide report having experienced physical and/or sexual violence from an intimate partner during their lifetime [1]. The consequences of DV for victim-survivors and their families [2–11] as well as society overall [12] are profound. Recognising the consequences of DV, international frameworks such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) have called for stronger legal protections for women and girls.

National legal frameworks play a critical role in defining the State’s commitment to preventing and responding to DV. Comprehensive DV laws can provide protection orders, establish criminal sanctions, mandate survivor support services, and create mechanisms for enforcement and accountability. While most countries have adopted legislation addressing some forms of VAWG, the scope and effectiveness of DV laws may vary considerably. However, there remains limited systematic evidence on the types of law adopted by countries to address DV and their effectiveness in addressing the issue.

This study addresses this gap by analysing national DV laws in a global sample of countries. We pursue three main tasks. First, we generate markers of DV law type and intensity. We code DV laws based on their legal source, distinguishing between DV laws, gender-based violence (GBV) laws, family violence laws, criminal codes, and other laws. While some countries regulate the DV through a single legal source, others may do so through introducing provisions on DV across multiple types of laws. Therefore, we also introduce a measure of intensity of legal regulation of DV across countries. Second, we use the data generated in the first stage to document the trends in the evolution of different types of DV laws globally. We also describe how the presence of different laws varies by region, economic development, and legal origin. Third, we study the association between the generated markers of DV laws and intimate partner violence (IPV) prevalence across countries through a regression analysis.

This paper contributes to the emerging literature on DV laws and their impacts. Most existing studies present case studies of specific national laws and their impact. The literature has documented the impacts of anti-violence legislation in reducing the suicide rates [9], improving married women’s health, self-confidence, and intrahousehold bargaining power [13] and leading to a decline in the prevalence of IPV and the proportion of women justifying violent behaviour [14]. In contrast, decriminalization of DV can lead to decreased life satisfaction, increased depression and increased alcohol intake among college-educated married women [15]. This evidence comes from distinct country settings such as Mexico, China and Russia, limiting the generalizability of findings.

A recent multi-country study by Richardson et al. [16] provides further evidence on the impact of DV legislation on societal attitudes toward IPV. Utilizing data across 61 countries, the study provides an assessment of the changes in IPV attitudes following the adoption of DV laws showing that such legislation is associated with modest reductions in the acceptance of IPV. The current paper complements this work by documenting the differences in the types of DV laws adopted by countries and examining how these laws are associated with IPV prevalence.

The nuanced classification of DV laws and the analysis of their relevance to IPV prevalence is an important contribution to the literature, particularly given the diversity in the way DV is regulated across countries. Legal frameworks differ in scope, specificity, and enforcement mechanisms, which can significantly influence their effectiveness. Distinguishing across types of DV laws is essential, as responses may differ considerably depending on whether DV is addressed through a dedicated DV law or embedded within broader legal frameworks. As discussed in Violence Against Women: What Everyone Needs to Know [17], the U.S. legal framework exemplifies a comprehensive approach, combining specialised laws with an appropriations bill that allocates substantial federal funding and provides structured guidance to state and local criminal justice systems. This includes support for temporary housing, employment initiatives, and other social services for survivors. In contrast, Russia’s 2017 legal reform, which decriminalised certain forms of DV, illustrates the adverse consequences of the absence of a specific legal offense: the lack of a standalone DV statute reinforces the perception that DV is not treated as a serious crime by authorities [17]. Building on these contrasting examples, the key innovation of our study is in the systematic coding of DV legislation across a global sample of countries, which allows us to classify the types of law regulating DV and assess how these variations matter for addressing the issue. Our contribution lies in empirically quantifying and comparing these legal frameworks cross-countries and linking them to IPV prevalence using a global database. In doing so, we move beyond existing research that focuses primarily on the mere presence of laws, showing that the nature and components of legislation are crucial for understanding their impact on public health outcomes globally.

Methods

Our analysis proceeds in three steps. First, we generate markers of DV law type and intensity. Second, we use the generated data to document the trends in the evolution of different types of DV laws globally and variations by region, economic development, and legal origin. Third, we study the association between markers of DV laws and the prevalence of IPV across countries. The description of each step follows.

Defining markers of DV laws

We use data from Women, Business and the Law (WBL) 1.0 for law presence in 190 economies annually during the period from 1971 to 2024 (calendar years 1970 to 2023) [18]. One of the indicators in the dataset is whether there is legislation specifically addressing DV in a country in a particular year. We use it as our measure of legislation presence. Moreover, as the dataset al.so specifies the legal source, we use it to assign each country a marker of a law type based on the source. Using this approach, we categorise laws into the following groups: DV law, GBV law, family violence law, criminal code, and other law. See Appendix A for more details on the definitions of law type. A country may have more than a single type of law; we therefore additionally generate a measure of legal intensity of DV regulation based on the number of law types used by a country to regulate DV.

To ensure methodological rigor in tracking legal changes over time, we rely on the longitudinal structure of the WBL database, which captures laws annually and updates each country-year observation based on legislative changes effective as of October 1 of the prior year. The WBL team compiles and validates this information annually using a standardized and transparent process (see Appendix A for a summary of approach to data collection and validation). Although there is no universally accepted typology of DV-related laws, it is useful to draw on conceptual definitions of domestic, intimate partner, gender-based, and family violence to understand the types of legislation typically associated with each.

Based on the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) report on Measuring Prevalence of Violence Against Women: Key Terminology [19, p.2]], DV refers to “abusive behaviour (physical, sexual, emotional violence and neglect) that occurs within the private, domestic sphere, generally between individuals who are related through blood or intimacy”. In many contexts, IPV, which refers to violence perpetrated by a current or former partner or spouse, is the most common form of DV. However, in some cultural contexts, abuse by in-laws or extended family members may be prevalent. The term DV should be used with care, as it can (1) overlap with both IPV and GBV, and (2) encompass violence against individuals other than women, such as children or the elderly within the household [19].

According to Violence against Women: What Everyone Needs to Know [17, p.8]], GBV is a broader term referring to “a crime committed against persons, whether male or female, because of their sex and/or socially constructed gender identity, roles, and relations, that results in or is likely to result in physical, sexual, psychological, or economic harm or suffering, including threats of such acts, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty”. For violence to qualify as gender-based, it must be motivated, at least in part, by the gender or perceived gender of the person or group targeted [17]. Laws framed under the umbrella of GBV often encompass DV but may also address violence occurring outside of the household, such as workplace harassment or sexual violence in public spaces [19].

The term family violence is sometimes used interchangeably with DV, particularly in legal or sociological contexts. It generally refers to any form of abuse occurring within the family, regardless of the relationship between the victim and perpetrator or the gender of those involved [19]. This broader framing may include child abuse, elderly abuse, and sibling violence, in addition to IPV.

Finally, general criminal laws may also apply to acts of DV, but without a targeted focus.

Evolution and differences in DV laws

We utilise the constructed dataset on DV laws for descriptive inquiries in two main directions. First, we study how different types of laws have evolved over time, and in conjunction with key international milestones related to VAWG. This analysis offers the opportunity to identify trajectories in legal evolution and whether specific types of laws appear at earlier vs. later stages of development. We also categorize countries into groups defined by relatively early vs. relatively late adopters of specific DV laws. Second, we ask whether adoption of different DV laws varies systematically by world regions, level of economic development, and legal origin.

DV laws and IPV prevalence

In the final stage of the analysis, we ask whether markers of DV laws are correlated with IPV prevalence. Our main outcome of interest is the IPV prevalence in the last 12 months sourced from WHO and Women, Peace and Security Index for year 2018, the latest year for which data on IPV prevalence is available for a large sample of countries [20].

The explanatory variables of interest are the markers of DV laws defined earlier, including: the presence of legislation; the types of laws, and the intensity of the law (number of different laws adopted).

To control for confounding country-level factors, we include several key variables, adding these progressively in the estimations. GDP per capita (in constant 2015 USD) is drawn from the World Development Indicators (WDI) [21]. To address skewness and allow proportional interpretation of coefficients, GDP per capita is log-transformed in the analysis and reported as ‘ln GDP per capita’ in all tables. This transformation allows coefficients to be interpreted as the change in IPV prevalence associated with a proportional (percentage) change in income. The democracy measure is based on the dichotomous classification provided in the Quality of Government (QoG) dataset [22]. Legal origin, categorized into English common law, French commercial code, German commercial code, Socialist/Communist law, and Scandinavian commercial code, is also taken from the QoG dataset, originally developed by [23]. Regional indicators (e.g. East Asia & Pacific, Sub-Saharan Africa) are based on classifications in the WBL dataset [18]. Additionally, we control for measures of women’s economic and political rights, sourced from the Cingranelli-Richards (CIRI) Human Rights Dataset, accessed via the QoG dataset [24].1

These controls allow us to account for structural differences across countries that may influence both the adoption of DV laws and IPV prevalence, thereby enabling a more accurate estimation of the relationship between legal frameworks and violence outcomes.

Results

Evolution of DV laws

Figure 1 presents the evolution of legal responses to DV globally over the period 1983 to 2024, and the links to key international events on VAWG. The share of countries adopting laws addressing DV has changed from close to zero in early 1980 s to over 80% in 2024.2

Share of countries adopting laws addressing DV and key international events on VAWG, 1983-2024. Data Source: Women, Business and the Law (WBL) and Timeline of Feminist Governance (O’Shanassy, 2023)

The patterns in legal recognition and regulation of DV across the globe with many countries adopting laws since the mid-1990s, can be linked to the emergence of international normative consensus. The General Recommendation 19 of the United Nations (UN) CEDAW adopted in 1992 and the UN General Assembly Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women, adopted on 20 December 1993, were passed unanimously (without a vote). Both documents introduced, for the first time, a GBV framework that addressed violence occurring in the home and family as well as community.

This was followed by what has been described as the ”Beijing effect” [26], referring to the 1995 UN World Conference on Women in Beijing. This conference universally adopted the Platform of Action, which identified 12 critical areas of concern, including VAWG. Moreover, the 1993 UN Declaration under Article 4 outlined actions that states should take to address this form of violence. While it did not explicitly mandate the creation of specialised laws, it recommended legal measures with a level of specificity that effectively necessitates such legislation.

Figure 2 presents the cumulative share of countries that have adopted DV-related legislation, disaggregated by the type of law enacted. A notable acceleration begins around 1995, with sharp increases in the adoption of specific DV laws, which by 2024 cover over 40% of countries. These are distinct from more general legal instruments such as criminal code provisions, family violence laws, and GBV laws, all of which have also expanded, albeit to a lesser extent. Reliance solely on criminal code provisions has become less prevalent over time, as more countries have moved toward specialized legislation. There is also a shifting trend where countries tend to adopt specific DV laws, instead of broader types of laws, to address issues related to DV.

Share of countries adopting laws addressing DV by type of law, 1983-2024. Data Source: Women, Business and the Law (WBL)

Figure 3 complements the previous analyses by capturing not just the presence but also the intensity of DV legislation, measured by the number of distinct legal provisions a country has enacted. The chart stratifies countries based on whether they have adopted one, two, or three types of legal instruments addressing DV. This distinction is potentially important because a single law may signal basic recognition of the issue, while multiple laws may at times suggest a more comprehensive institutionalized response.

Share of countries adopting laws addressing DV by number of law, 1983-2024. Data Source: Women, Business and the Law (WBL)

Most countries initially began with a single legal provision, but over time, many moved toward adopting multiple laws. From around 2010 onward, there is a visible increase in the share of countries with two laws, and to a lesser extent, three laws. Although only a small share of countries had reached the threshold of three laws by 2024, the trend suggests a growing recognition of the multifaceted nature of DV, which requires legal responses that span across various domains.

Figure 4 shows that the timing of adoption of different types of laws varies by countries. Hong Kong is the first country that adopted DV law in 1988, but most countries adopted DV law in the period of 2000-2015. The family violence law has been adopted by countries since the earlier period of 1983-2000. Provisions on DV in a criminal code and other law are, on the contrary, more likely to be introduced in the later periods after 2015. Differences in prevalence of DV laws

We study the differences in the prevalence of laws addressing DV by economic development, regions of the world, and legal origins.

Figure 5 explores the cross-sectional relationship between economic development and the type of DV legislation adopted by countries as of 2017. Countries are ranked along the x-axis by their GDP per capita (in 2015 constant USD), and the y-axis plots the natural logarithm of GDP per capita. Each dot represents a country, color-coded by the legal provision it uses to address DV.

Different types of laws addressing DV and GDP per capita, 2017. Data Source: Women, Business and the Law (WBL) and World Development Indicators (WDI)

The figure suggests a diverse mix of legal types observed across the income spectrum. However, some patterns emerge. Higher-income countries (toward the right end of the distribution) more frequently adopt other laws. This potentially reflects greater institutional capacity to implement specialized legislation. In contrast, lower-income countries more often have no specific legislation. Dedicated DV laws, GBV laws and family violence laws appear scattered across middle-income ranks.

Although economic development may facilitate legal expansion, the diffusion of legal norms is crucial in shaping responses to DV. Next, we consider adoption trajectories by income group, region, and legal origin.

Figure 6(a) shows the cumulative share of countries that adopted DV legislation by level of development from 1983 to 2024. High-income countries moved earliest and most comprehensively, with nearly complete adoption by the late 2000s. Upper-middle income countries closely followed, with rapid convergence after 2000. Lower-middle income countries show a slower and more gradual uptake, while low-income countries lag significantly, with just under 60% adoption by 2024. Appendix C provides further details on the timeline of adoption by type of law and country. These patterns suggest that higher income countries, potentially owing to greater fiscal capacity, stronger rule-of-law institutions, and more active civil societies may adopt progressive legislation earlier.

Diffusion of legal norms by development, region, and legal origin, 1983–2024. Data Source: Women, Business and the Law (WBL)

Considering the diffusion of legal norms by region, Fig. 6(b) suggests that a wave of legal reform begins in the early 1990s and accelerates in the 2000s. Latin America and the Caribbean led the early adoption trend, reaching over 80% coverage by 2005. This reflects the region’s strong engagement with gender rights movements and regional conventions. Europe and Central Asia, and South Asia followed with steep adoption curves post-2005.

In contrast, progress has been significantly slower in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) and Sub-Saharan Africa. By 2024, fewer than 60% of countries in the MENA region had passed relevant legislation. One contributing factor may be legal pluralism, where Sharia law often serves as the primary mechanism for resolving family disputes. This can conflict with formal legal frameworks aimed at addressing DV, potentially hindering comprehensive legal reform. Such pluralism may help explain the region’s comparatively low adoption rate of DV laws [27].

Figure 6(c) shows the diffusion of DV legislation across countries by legal origin between 1983 and 2024. Countries with German and Scandinavian Commercial Code were early and complete adopters, reaching 100% coverage by the mid-2000s and early 2010s respectively. English Common Law countries began adopting earlier, in the late 1980s, but followed a more gradual path, reaching close to 90% adoption by 2024. In contrast, countries with Socialist/Communist and French Commercial Code origins lagged behind. Both groups show slower and delayed uptake, with acceleration only after 2005.

Figure 7 presents the distribution of laws addressing DV and IPV prevalence around the world. Countries with no legislation addressing DV often have relatively higher rates of IPV prevalence in the previous 12 months (darker shades). For example, Singapore and Switzerland both have legislation addressing DV and report prevalence rates of 2%. In contrast, no legislation exists in Iraq where the IPV rate, at 45.3%, is the highest globally.

Discussion

This study provides a global assessment of DV laws and their associations with the prevalence of IPV. The findings demonstrate a consistent and statistically significant negative correlation between the existence of DV legislation and IPV prevalence across countries. This supports the growing body of literature that emphasizes the role of formal legal mechanisms in protecting women and reducing VAWG [16, 28–30].

Most significantly, we introduce a distinction between different types of laws addressing DV and show that the type of law regulating DV matters. Specifically, dedicated DV laws show a strong negative association with IPV rates compared to other types of legislation such as family violence law or GBV law. This finding aligns with UN good practice guidance, which advocates for adopting specialized, gender-specific framework, to ensure comprehensive protection and effective support for survivor [32] and is consistent with prior research indicating that comprehensive, specific legal provisions tend to have greater normative and institutional impact [29, 31].

This study also considers the role of legal intensity, specifically, the number of laws addressing DV, in IPV prevalence. We find that higher legal intensity is associated with lower IPV prevalence. However, the number of laws should not be interpreted as a direct measure of legal quality or comprehensiveness. It is possible that a single law may encompass a broad and cohesive framework, while multiple fragmented laws may yield weaker protections. Our measure of legal intensity serves as a proxy for breadth of legal coverage, but does not capture the depth, scope, or enforceability of each law. This limitation underscores the importance of distinguishing between legal quantity and legal substance, a distinction emphasized in legal epidemiology research [33]. Future research should build on this by incorporating content-based coding or scoring methods to evaluate the extensiveness and specificity of legal provisions.

Our findings offer additional insights into the existing findings on the role of laws in addressing DV. Heise and Kotsadam [30] demonstrate, through a multilevel analysis across 44 countries, that gender equality in family-related legislation is associated with lower rates of IPV however the societal norms play a critical role in the relationship. Htun and Weldon [34] emphasize that autonomous women’s movements play a central role in driving the adoption and sustainability of effective DV legislation, often more influential than government type or economic development. Klugman et al. [28] further argue that effective legal frameworks must be integrated with broader gender policy, such as enhancing women’s economic and political rights, to produce meaningful reductions in violence. Together, this body of work affirms that while legal reforms are necessary, they must be situated within broader frameworks of social transformation, institutional capacity, and women’s empowerment to effectively combat IPV.

Finally, it is important to note that the findings reported in this paper offer valuable descriptive insights, however they do not establish causal relationships. The cross-sectional nature of the data and the reliance on IPV data from a single year (2018) limits causal inference. Future research should consider using instrumental variable approaches or panel data methods to strengthen causal claims and explore the temporal dynamics of legal reform and IPV reduction. Furthermore, while our data capture the formal presence and types of DV laws, they do not reflect how effectively those laws are implemented or enforced in practice. Legal reforms may exist on paper but fall short in actual protection or justice delivery for survivors. In particular, the presence of a law does not necessarily translate into legal protection; institutional actors such as police, judiciary, and advocates play a central role in shaping how survivors experience the legal system. Data on enforcement, reporting, and adjudication are often unavailable yet critical for understanding the real-world impact of legal reforms. Future research should examine enforcement mechanisms, administrative capacity, budgetary support, and judicial practices to better understand the conditions under which laws are effectively implemented. Mixed methods approach, including qualitative case studies and implementation audits, may be especially valuable for capturing the real-world operation and effectiveness of legal protections.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant progress made in the adoption of laws addressing DV globally since the early 1980s. We document the evolution of different types of laws addressing DV over time and document the differences in adoption across various regions, levels of economic development, and legal origin. Importantly, the study establishes a strong negative correlation between the presence of laws on DV and the prevalence of IPV, suggesting that effective legal frameworks tend to report lower levels of IPV. Additionally, the study emphasizes that not only the existence of these laws but also their specific types play a crucial role in influencing outcomes. Overall, the evidence indicates that robust legal frameworks are essential for effectively addressing DV.
