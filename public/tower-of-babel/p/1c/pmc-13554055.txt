Food environment and cardiovascular kidney metabolic diseases mortality
Authors: Xiaowen Wang, Ting Zhang, Qianying Li, Heejin Lee, Jinghua Li, Peien Zhou, Jianwei Wu, Philip K. Hopke, Shao Lin, Xiaobo Xue Romeiko, Tong Wang, Hongmei Yu, Yuantao Hao, Wangjian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic (CKM) diseases are leading causes of mortality in the United States (US), with geographic and socioeconomic disparities. The community food environment, including food accessibility and security, is a modifiable determinant of CKM health. We systematically examined associations between county-level multidimensional food environment indicators and CKM-related mortality across US counties. We conducted an ecological analysis integrating data from the County Health Rankings and CDC WONDER. Generalized linear models estimated rate ratios (RR) for age-adjusted CKM mortality across quartiles of the food environment index, limited access to healthy foods, and food insecurity. Adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic factors, the most favorable quartile of the food environment index was associated with reduced mortality from cardiovascular disease (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.84–0.86), diabetes (RR 0.76, 0.73–0.79), renal failure (RR 0.73, 0.69–0.77), and overall CKM disease (RR 0.83, 0.82–0.84) compared with the least favorable quartile. Conversely, limited access to healthy foods (RR 1.11, 1.09–1.12) and food insecurity (RR 1.19, 1.18–1.21) correlated with higher overall CKM mortality. Counties with less favorable food environments are linked to higher CKM-related mortality. Improving structural food access and security may be essential for reducing disease burden and promoting health equity.

Introduction

Cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic (CKM) diseases remain major contributors to morbidity and mortality in the United States, imposing a growing burden on public health and healthcare resources1. More than a quarter of US adults have experienced at least one CKM disease in the last two decades, and the percentage of individuals with multiple CKM diseases has increased from 5.3% in 1999 to 8.0% in 20202. This interrelated cluster of conditions, which was conceptualized by the American Heart Association as CKM diseases, is driven by deeply interconnected pathophysiological pathways3. Specifically, metabolic dysfunction (such as insulin resistance and adiposity) triggers systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. These mechanisms concurrently drive microvascular damage in the kidneys and macrovascular damage in the cardiovascular system, creating a vicious cycle where renal impairment further accelerates cardiovascular decline3. Furthermore, these diseases share critical upstream behavioral and structural risk factors, particularly poor dietary habits driven by socioenvironmental disadvantages4. Despite advances in medical treatment and preventive care, disparities in CKM-related outcomes continue to exist across geographic and socioeconomic gradients5.

A critical yet modifiable determinant influencing CKM health is the community food environment. The food environment includes multiple dimensions that shape nutritional opportunity and metabolic risk, including the accessibility, affordability, and security of healthy foods. Key metrics include the food environment index, which integrates geographic proximity to food retailers and economic capacity to afford nutritious foods; the proportion of low-income residents without adequate supermarket proximity; and food insecurity, reflecting a condition in which individuals or household do not have reliable access to sufficient quantities of affordable and nutritious food6. These factors are increasingly recognized as crucial drivers of dietary quality and health behaviors and, consequently, of cardiometabolic and renal health6,7. For instance, areas with poor food environments are associated with higher consumption of processed foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, and high-sodium diets, which can in turn elevate the risk of hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease8,9. However, there is still some debate as to whether food environment can improve healthier behaviors10. In practice, consumers often purchase food at multiple locations, or their shopping activities span different stores and areas11,12. Therefore, the foods they are exposed to and actually consume may not accurately reflect the “foodscape” surrounding their residence. Individual factors such as culture influence, health awareness, and food knowledge can also influence actual food choices13.

Therefore, despite a growing body of research, the relationship between the food environment and health outcomes remains controversial. While some studies report significant associations between adverse food environments and increased risk of individual chronic diseases (e.g., cardiovascular disease [CVD], diabetes) and mortality14–16, others have failed to observe consistent or robust links17–19. Although these prior investigations have provided valuable insights into these conditions separately, few studies have systematically evaluated the contribution of these multidimensional food environment factors to the integrated CKM mortality burden at the population level. Taking an ecological approach allows us to understand how community-level factors and structural contexts shape health outcomes. Furthermore, despite spatial and socioeconomic gradients in food access and CKM disease burden, the extent to which county-level variations in food environments account for geographic disparities in mortality remains poorly characterized. Herein, we conducted a nationwide mortality database to examine the association between food environment indicators and CKM-related mortality across US counties.

Discussion

In this large, nationally representative ecological analysis, we observe that multidimensional county-level food environment metrics, including the food environment index, limited access to healthy foods, and food insecurity have strong, graded associations with CKM diseases mortality across the US Specifically, counties with more favorable food environments exhibited substantially lower rates of mortality due to CVD, diabetes mellitus, and renal failure, while counties with poorer access to healthy foods or higher levels of food insecurity faced measurably elevated risks. Importantly, these gradients persisted after comprehensive adjustment for demographic and socioeconomic confounding factors, and remained robust across subgroups, as well as in analyses of disease subtypes and under various model specifications.

Previous research provides mixed results as to whether increased access to healthy food outlets leads to better health outcomes at the community or individual level20,21. For example, a US study involving 60,775 postmenopausal women from the Women’s Health Initiative reported that a higher number of grocery stores within a 1.5-mile radius of one’s residence was linked to lower body mass index (BMI) and lower diastolic blood pressure22. Conversely, findings indicated that closer proximity to fast-food restaurants was not related to higher BMI in the Framingham Heart Study offspring cohort over 30 years follow-up23. In the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis study of adults aged 45 and above, greater access to healthy food options corresponded to a 38% decrease in the incidence of type 2 diabetes24. Based on another cohort study of 2,753,000 individuals across the continental US, of whom 82% had access to healthy food retail within their residential area, this research found that healthy food retail presence was not associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality or all-cause mortality, while the presence of unhealthy food retail was associated with a 15% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.11–1.20)17. A Chicago study of 770,000 people found low-income, Black neighborhoods are three times farther from supermarkets and have significantly higher rates of hypertension, diabetes, and CKD than affluent, White neighborhoods. After adjusting for both neighborhood and individual-level factors, the study found that greater household distance to the nearest supermarket at the zip code level was significantly associated with higher incidence of diabetes, but with lower incidence of hypertension and CKD18. Beyond food access, most studies have shown consistent associations of lower food insecurity with better dietary quality and lower prevalence of chronic conditions. For instance, the Health and Retirement Study reported that food insecurity among US adults aged 50 and above was associated with poorer diet quality, including higher intake of unhealthy foods and lower adherence to Mediterranean and Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet patterns25. Multiple cohort studies have consistently linked food insecurity to reduced life expectancy, faster memory deterioration, increased incidence of dementia, and elevated risks of cardiometabolic diseases in populations26–30. Overall, these individual-level, longitudinal analyses support the notion that both the availability and affordability of healthy foods in a local environment can influence dietary behaviors and subsequent health outcomes. Our study, leveraging comprehensive county-level data, extends the knowledge of prior results and supports the critical role of both food access and food affordability in shaping population health outcomes.

Mechanistic pathways likely underpinning these relationships are established. Greater distance to supermarkets has been shown to limit intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, and fibers, while promoting consumption of ultra-processed, calorie-dense foods that are widely available at convenience stores or fast-food outlets8,31,32. Food insecurity compounds these dietary constraints by necessitating intermittent caloric restriction, substitution of inexpensive processed food, and reduced medication adherence, all of which promote metabolic dysregulation and accelerate CKM pathogenesis9,33. Such mechanisms are also consistent with our finding that strong associations were observed between food environment and diabetes and kidney outcomes, supporting conceptual models of the food–inflammation–metabolism axis34,35. However, given the ecological design of this study, these mechanistic interpretations are inferred at the population level and may not directly represent individual-level pathways. Individuals within the same county may experience substantially different food environments and behavioral responses, which cannot be captured in aggregate data. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mechanisms at the individual level, and further studies with individual-level data are needed to validate these pathways.

Regional disparities in the distribution of food environment quality and CKM mortality rate highlight the role of underlying health inequities. The concentration of food insecurity and limited access in southern regions partially overlaps with “stroke belt” and “diabetes belt” mortality patterns in the US36,37, which underscores that place-based social determinants are crucial to improve health disparities. Previous research has shown that broader social vulnerability metrics, such as the social vulnerability index or social deprivation index, are associated with increases in cardiovascular mortality at the county level38,39. Notably, our stratified analyses by household income and rural percentage did not reveal consistent effect modification, suggesting that the impact of poor food environments may not be fully mitigated by socioeconomic status. Furthermore, when we adjusted for state-level clustering, the associations between food environment metrics and mortality were notably attenuated. This attenuation may suggest that broader state-level policies, such as Medicaid expansion, public health funding, and the administration of safety-net programs, account for a substantial portion of these geographic variations40,41. These combined findings support policy arguments that macro-level interventions to improve food environments, such as incentivizing supermarket placement and strengthening the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) by increasing participation and ensuring that benefits are sufficient to support a healthy diet, should be prioritized for all at-risk communities42.

Our study extends the existing literature, which has often focused on single exposure–outcome or relied on self-reported health data, by systematically integrating multidimensional, validated food environment indices with all-cause and cause-specific mortality across US counties. The observed attenuation of effect estimates after accounting for socioeconomic covariates is expected, given the interaction of poverty, employment, and food access, but subgroup analyses indicated that these associations remained consistent across different socioeconomic strata. These findings underscore that the food environment may represent a modifiable and upstream risk factor for cardiometabolic mortality. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant recognition. First, the ecological nature of our analysis precludes direct inference at the individual level, limiting our ability to establish causal relationships between local food environment and CKM mortality, introducing potential ecological bias and limiting our ability to establish causal relationships between local food environment and CKM mortality. Second, our analysis was conducted at the county level, without adjustments for individual-level variations and clinical risk factors like medication use. Residual confounding by unmeasured community factors also remains possible. Third, contemporaneous data were used to examine these relationships, and spatial structure hasn’t been considered by the random effect model. Our analysis may also not fully reflect the long-term effects of improvements in food access or food security. Fourth, counties with missing or incomplete data were excluded. Few counties were excluded for CVD mortality, but a larger proportion were excluded for diabetes and renal failure, which may limit the national representativeness of these outcomes. Moreover, our study focused exclusively on mortality, limiting our ability to evaluate earlier disease stages. Because standardized county-level incidence data are currently lacking, future studies utilizing electronic health records are needed to investigate the food environment’s impact on disease onset and prevalence. Last, county-level aggregation may obscure within-county heterogeneity in exposure or risk, particularly in large or demographically diverse regions.

In conclusion, multidimensional features of the food environment at the county level, including composite access indices, limited healthy food availability, and food insecurity, are independently associated with mortality from cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic diseases in the US Our results underscore the need for policy and public health interventions that address structural food access and security, not merely individual behaviors, to reduce the persistent and unequal burden of CKM diseases. Targeted investment in healthier food environments should be considered a cornerstone strategy in population cardiometabolic health improvement and health equity initiatives. Interventions aimed at improving food environments may contribute to population cardiometabolic health and health equity, although further studies with individual-level data are needed to confirm these relationships.

Methods

Study design

We performed an ecological analysis to examine associations between county level food environment indicators and mortality from CVD, diabetes mellitus, renal failure, and total CKM across the US using data from 2016 to 2019. The study period was restricted to 2016-2019 to establish a stable, pre-pandemic baseline, thereby avoiding the profound confounding effects of COVID-19 on mortality and healthcare access.

Exposures

In this study, county-level food environment indicators, specifically the food environment index, limited access to healthy foods, and food insecurity, were obtained from the County Health Rankings and Roadmaps. These data, which are publicly accessible and methodologically documented (https://www.countyhealthrankings.org), provide standardized, composite measures of health determinants at the county level across the US The platform integrates multiple nationally representative sources and has been widely cited in published studies, supporting its reliability and validity for population health research38,43–46. To ensure strict temporal alignment with the mortality data, all food environment metrics were specifically sourced for the corresponding 2016-2019 period.

Food environment index

The food environment index, originally derived from the USDA Food Environment Atlas and the Map the Meal Gap project by Feeding America, integrates indicators of healthy food access and socioeconomic constraints. It combines information on geographic proximity to supermarkets or grocery stores with measures of income and transportation barriers, generating a composite score ranging from 0 (least healthy environment) to 10 (most healthy environment)47,48.

Limited access to healthy foods

Limited access to healthy foods was defined as the proportion of the population who are low-income and reside far from a supermarket or large grocery store. This measure was obtained from the USDA Food Environment Atlas and reflects the burden of food deserts where both cost and distance constrain access to nutritious foods. According to the USDA Economic Research Service documentation, the indicator identifies census tracts where a significant number or proportion of residents live more than one mile (urban areas) or ten miles (rural areas) from the nearest supermarket, supercenter, or large grocery store, and also have low income levels. Based on U.S. Census Bureau criteria, a census tract is classified as urban if its population-weighted centroid falls within an area of more than 2,500 people, and is designated as rural otherwise. This spatially derived metric helps quantify structural barriers to food access that disproportionately affect vulnerable populations49,50.

Food insecurity

Food insecurity was defined as the proportion of the population lacking reliable access to sufficient and nutritious food. Data were originally drawn from Map the Meal Gap analysis, which captures both adequacy of food supply and affordability of balanced meals. Estimates were derived using the US Department of Agriculture’s Core Food Security Module within the Current Population Survey (CPS). Feeding America modeled the relationship between food insecurity and its determinants (poverty, unemployment, median income, disability status, homeownership, and racial/ethnic composition) at the state level, then applied those coefficients to county-level data from the American Community Survey (ACS) and Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) to generate local food insecurity rates51,52.

Data source for mortality outcome

The US county-level mortality rates and population data for the period 2016-2019 were obtained from the Underlying Cause of Death database, maintained by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) system (https://wonder.cdc.gov/). These data originate from death certificates for all US resident deaths, which constitute a complete census of mortality and are certified by medical professionals or coroners in accordance with statutory requirements53,54. Age-adjusted mortality rates (per 100,000 population) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for CVD, diabetes mellitus, renal failure, and total CKM diseases. CVD and its subtypes were identified using the following ICD-10 codes: ischemic heart disease (I20–I25), acute myocardial infarction (I21–I22), heart failure (I50), and cerebrovascular diseases (I60–I69). Diabetes mellitus was defined by codes E10–E14, and renal failure by N17–N19. Total CKM mortality included deaths from CVD (I00–I99), diabetes mellitus (E10–E14), and renal failure (N17–N19).

Covariates

Several established metabolic risk factors were included as covariates to mitigate potential confounding in the analysis. All covariate data corresponding to the 2016–2019 period was obtained from the County Health Rankings and Roadmaps and the original sources have been listed in Table S6. These confounders were considered in classifying the population or in model adjustments: (1) Demographics: percentage of female (% Female), percentage of Hispanic (% Hispanic), percentage of population over the age of 65 years (% 65 and older). (2) Socioeconomic and environmental factors: percentage of uninsured population, ratio of population to primary care physician, household income inequality, percentage of population ages 16 and older unemployed, high school graduation rates, percentage of population living in a census-defined rural area, and obesity prevalence. Rural classification is based on the U.S. Census Bureau definition, where urban areas are defined as territories with at least 2000 housing units or a population of 5000, and rural areas included all remaining territories. Obesity is defined as the percentage of the adult population (aged 20 and older) that reports a body mass index (BMI) greater than or equal to 30 kg/m².

Statistical analysis

Counties with incomplete data on food environment indicators (food environment index, limited access to healthy foods, and food insecurity) or CVD, diabetes mellitus, and renal failure mortality rates were excluded to ensure analytic robustness. The proportion of excluded counties varied significantly across outcomes; this variation is driven by the CDC WONDER’s standard protocol of suppressing mortality data in counties with small absolute death counts (fewer than 20) to protect privacy and maintain statistical reliability. No missing data were observed among covariates.

Spatial variation in food environment indicators and cause-specific mortality rates was visualized using the ggplot2 package in R software. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted among these food environment indicators. For each disease and food environment metric, we calculated mortality rates (per 100,000 adults) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each quartile group. We employed generalized linear models (GLM) with quasi-Poisson distributions to evaluate associations between food environment metrics and mortality from CKM diseases, including CVD, diabetes mellitus, and renal failure. All three metrics were categorized into quartiles for the analysis. Log(population) was used as an offset to account for varying county sizes. These methods have been widely employed in prior ecological studies14,43,55–57. Rate ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were estimated by comparing the mortality rates in the higher quartiles (Q2, Q3, and Q4) versus the lowest quartile (Q1) of each food environment metric. Model 1 adjusted for calendar year. Model 2 further adjusted for demographic covariates, including percentage of female residents, Hispanic population, and residents aged 65 years or older. Model 3 further adjusted for socioeconomic factors, including percentage of uninsured population, ratio of population to primary care physician, household income inequality, percentage of the population aged 16 and older who are unemployed, and high school graduation rates. To explore potential effect modification and understand whether associations differed across varying sociodemographic and health contexts, subgroup analyses were conducted by median household income, percentage of population living in a census-defined rural area, and obesity prevalence. Several sensitivity analyses were also performed. First, we tested the associations between food environment metrics and mortality from subtype-specific CVD (ischemic heart disease, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, and cerebrovascular diseases). Second, to address potential state-level clustering, mixed-effects models with state as a random intercept were fitted using the lme4 package. Third, we used all-cause mortality as an outcome for comparison. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1, with significance defined as 95% CIs excluding 1 or a P value < 0.05.
