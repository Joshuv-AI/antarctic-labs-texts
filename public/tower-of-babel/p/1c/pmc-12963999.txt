Identification of key quality indicators for Zigui navel orange varieties through multivariate analyses
Authors: Mingfang Tao, Peng Zhang, Tiancai Wang, Dan Zheng, Zhenzhen Xia, Xun Zhang, Yinjun Li, Youxiang Zhou, Xitian Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study comprehensively characterized five representative Zigui navel orange cultivars regarding physicochemical properties, bioactive compounds, and volatiles. Quality indicators varied among navel orange cultivars (p < 0.05, η2 > 0.78) for all parameters. Newhall Orange (NO) exhibited superiority in content of protein, vitamin C, total soluble solids, soluble sugars, total phenolics, and total flavonoids, and antioxidant activity. 14 characteristic quality indicators for Zigui navel oranges were identified through multivariate analyses (correlation analysis, HCA and OPLS-DA) and market-relevant parameters. These indicators were grouped into three principal components through PCA (KMO > 0.6 and Bartlett's test p < 0.05) with eigenvalues >1, accounting for 93.125% of the cumulative variance. A comprehensive quality score was calculated for each cultivar by weighted summation of the standardized values of these 14 indicators, with NO demonstrating the highest score. These findings provide theoretical guidance for nutritional evaluation and processing applications of Zigui navel oranges.

Introduction

China is one of the world's leading citrus producers and exporters, yielding 32.71 million metric tons annually (Chen et al., 2021). As one of the prominent navel orange production hubs in the Yangtze River basin, Zigui primarily cultivates September Red Orange (SRO), Newhall Navel Orange (NO), Brown Orange (BO), Chinese Red Orange (CRO), and Lunwan Navel Orange (LNO). Benefiting from the unique climatic conditions and superior citrus varieties, the navel orange cultivation area in Zigui spans approximately 26,667 ha, yielding over 800,000 tons annually (Hubei Provincial Department of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, 2025). Consequently, the navel orange industry has become one of the key agricultural pillar industries driving regional development in Yichang City, Hubei Province, a strategic citrus production hub in central China.

Zigui navel oranges (Citrus sinensis) exhibit fruit quality characteristics influenced by multiple factors including color, size, shape, taste (sweetness and acidity), and aroma (Goldenberg et al., 2018). Previous studies have suggested substantial variation in nutritional and bioactive compounds across citrus varieties (Costanzo et al., 2020). For instance, in a study conducted in Chongqing, China, significant differences were found in single fruit weight (SFW), titratable acidity (TA), and vitamin C (VC) across 11 diverse citrus varieties, although their total soluble solid (TSS) content showed no significant differences (Li, Hong, et al., 2019). In addition, navel oranges are particularly valued for their bioactive constituents, which exhibit diverse health benefits including antioxidant, antitumor, and anti-aging activities (Lu et al., 2023). Phytochemical analyses identify flavonoids as the predominant secondary metabolites in citrus, followed by phenolic acids and limonoids (Lin et al., 2023). More than 250 flavonoids have been identified in citrus fruits, classified into flavones, flavanones, anthocyanins, and flavonols (Karn et al., 2021). The pulp of sweet oranges predominantly contains flavanones (mainly narirutin and hesperidin), along with trace polymethoxyflavones (sinensetin and tangeretin), and minor phenolic acids such as ferulic acid (FA) (Li, Wang, et al., 2019). As the primary bitter compounds in citrus, limonoid concentrations vary widely among species, with tangerines ranging from 11.63 to 546.27 mg/kg FW, mandarins from 68 to 486.64 mg/kg FW, pomelos from 175.53 to 1587.34 mg/kg FW, and oranges from 5.74 to 245.92 mg/kg FW (Lin et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024). The aroma profile of citrus fruits is determined by the composition and concentration of volatile compounds, with terpenes and aromatic compounds being the major contributors to their characteristic fragrance (Zhang et al., 2020). The characteristic aroma compounds of sweet oranges comprise d-limonene, valencene, linalool, and methyl butyrate (Sharon-Asa et al., 2003). Polyphenols, limonoids, VC, synephrine, and terpenes contribute to the strong antioxidant capacity of navel oranges (Pieracci et al., 2022). Since navel orange quality is determined by multiple factors, establishing a scientific evaluation system is essential, in which the selection of characteristic indicators is particularly crucial.

Currently, multivariate statistical methods are applied either individually or in combination to comprehensively evaluate fruit quality, enabling the discrimination of fruit origin and variety. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) demonstrates particular efficacy in identifying critical quality indicators and improving the separation of samples among groups (Li et al., 2024; Li, Hong, et al., 2019). Khalid et al. (2023) utilized correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) to demonstrate that sweet oranges stored in zero-energy cool chambers had lower quality loss than those stored at ambient laboratory temperatures. Zacarías-García et al. (2022) applied PCA to distinguish navel orange maturity stages based on relationships among sugar and acid compounds, VC, tocopherols, phenols, flavonoids, and antioxidant activity, and further differentiated red-fleshed varieties from blond oranges using carotenoid contents. Kim et al. (2021) utilized hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) to classify 10 citrus varieties into four groups according to fruit quality characteristics and metabolite profiles, aligning with the genetic differences among citrus parents. However, despite extensive studies on the nutritional components of various citrus varieties, comprehensive analyses of the nutritional constituents in representative Zigui navel orange cultivars remain limited.

This study evaluated five representative navel orange cultivars from Zigui County via comprehensive analyses of fruit morphological characteristics, physicochemical properties, nutritional composition, bioactive compounds, volatile composition and relative abundance, and antioxidant capacity. Notably, this is the first study to integrate phenolic, flavonoid, and volatile profiling for the specific quality evaluation of these local cultivars. Furthermore, a simplified evaluation framework was established by screening 14 discriminant markers using multivariate statistics (correlation analysis, HCA, OPLS-DA) and constructing a PCA-based quality assessment model. These findings provide novel insights and practical tools for germplasm selection, breeding, and industrial quality control of Zigui navel oranges.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Five navel orange cultivars (Citrus sinensis Osbeck) with consistent cultivation cycles and field management practices were selected for this study (Table S1). Fruits were harvested at commercial maturity between October 2023 and February 2024. Sampling was conducted at representative orchards (approximately 230 m altitude) in Zigui County. Three independent sampling units (0.1–0.2 ha each) were randomly designated as three biological replicates. Within each unit, five healthy trees with uniform growth were randomly selected. To minimize intra-tree variation, two blemish-free fruits were collected from each of five positions (upper, middle, lower, inner, and outer canopy) per tree. The pulp from 50 fruits (one sampling unit) per biological replicate was pooled. The pooled pulp samples were homogenized at 4 °C, and the homogenates were stored at −80 °C until analysis. It should be noted that this extensive pooling strategy, while ensuring representativeness, inherently limits the inference of within-cultivar biological variability and may mask individual variations.

Nomilin, obacunone, limonin, eriodictyol, rutin, naringenin, hesperetin, sinensetin, tangeretin, nobiletin, eriocitrin, narirutin, hesperidin, vanillic acid (VA), p-coumaric acid (p-CA), protocatechuic acid (PA), sinapic acid (SA), FA, caffeic acid (CA), hydroxybenzoic acid (HA), were purchased from Chengdu Purify Co., Ltd., with purities exceeding 98%.

Determination of physicochemical parameters of Zigui navel oranges

The measurements of fruit physicochemical parameters were performed at the Hubei Provincial Key Laboratory of Agricultural Products Nutrition, Quality, and Safety. Moisture content was determined by the weight loss of 10 g of crushed samples dried at 101–105 °C to constant weight. Protein was measured via the Kjeldahl method. TA, expressed as citric acid equivalents, was determined by titrating 10 g of homogenized citrus sample (in 100 mL distilled water) with 0.1 N NaOH, using phenolphthalein-ethanol solution as an indicator (Pieracci et al., 2022). TSS was measured in filtered juice at 20 ± 0.5 °C using a calibrated refractometer. Soluble sugar content was determined by direct titration of the appropriately diluted filtrate with Fehling's reagent and calculated based on the glucose standardization value and the volume of sample consumed. The sugar-acid ratio (SAR) was calculated as TSS/TA. SFW and FED (10–20 fruits) were measured using a balance and digital caliper. VC was quantified by reversed-phase HPLC with UV detection at 245 nm following ultrasonic extraction with metaphosphoric acid and separation using an ion-pair reagent. To quantitatively assess peel coloration, the Comprehensive Color Index (CCI) was selected for its established utility in citrus research and its strong correlation with peel maturity and carotenoid pigments (Liu et al., 2024; López-Gómez et al., 2020). CCI was determined using a spectrophotometer (CM-5, Konica Minolta, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) with the formula: CCI = 1000×a⁎/(L⁎×b⁎), where a* indicates redness and greenness, and b* indicates yellowness and blueness, L* denotes lightness.

Determination of polyphenolic compounds, synephrine, and limonoid compounds content of Zigui navel oranges

Synephrine and limonoids were extracted from navel oranges using a modified QuEChERS method. Specifically, 5 g of homogenized sample was extracted with 20 mL acetonitrile. A salt mixture containing 4 g MgSO₄, 1 g NaCl, 1 g Na₃C₆H₅O₇, and 0.5 g Na₂HC₆H₅O₇ was added with ceramic homogenizer beads. The mixture was vortexed vigorously for 3 min and centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 5 min. Subsequently, 6 mL of supernatant was transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 900 mg MgSO₄ and 150 mg primary secondary amine for purification. After shaking and centrifugation, the final extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and diluted 100-fold with 10% (v/v) acetonitrile in ultrapure water prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Polyphenolic compounds were extracted by homogenizing 5 g of sample with 20 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and then subjected to ultrasonication at room temperature for 20 min. After centrifugation at 4000 r/min for 5 min, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and diluted 10 -, 100 -, or 1000 - fold for subsequent analysis.

A LC-TQ 5200 (HeXin, Guangzhou, China) equipped with LC pump, autosampler, system controller, triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, vacuum pump and ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, U.S.A) was employed for the identification and quantification of synephrine, limonoids, and polyphenolic compounds. Chromatographic separation was achieved at 40 °C with a 5 μL injection volume using a mobile phase comprising (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1 min, 95% A; 1–8.5 min, 95 → 30% A; 8.5–8.6 min, 30 → 0% A; 8.6–11 min, 0 → 95% A; 11–14 min, 95% A. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in negative ion mode with the following parameters: ESI needle voltage, 4500 V; curtain gas temperature, 600 °C; drying gas temperature, 600 °C; drying gas flow, 7 L/min; nebulizer gas flow, 3 L/min; sheath gas flow, 0.55 L/min; collision gas flow, 0.4 L/min. Quantitation was conducted via external calibration curves generated by linear regression of peak areas (y) versus concentrations (x).

The performance of the developed UPLC-MS/MS method was comprehensively validated. All 22 target analytes showed strong linearity (R2 > 0.991) within their respective concentration ranges (Supplementary Table S2). The method was highly sensitive, with LODs and LOQs as low as 0.5 ng/mL and 1.0 ng/mL. Precision was robust, with all intra-day and inter-day RSDs below 3%. The accuracy of the method was confirmed by recovery studies, which yielded mean values between 90.6% and 104.5% with RSDs below 12%. The method was accurate, precise, and sensitive enough for the reliable quantification of these compounds in the samples.

Determination of volatiles in Zigui navel oranges

0.1 g of navel orange pulp was weighed into a 20 mL headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) vial, with the addition of 40 μL internal standard solution (2-octanol, 41.1 μg/mL). After homogenization, the sample was equilibrated at 55 °C for 5 min in a water bath. HS-SPME was performed at 55 °C for 30 min using a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber, followed by thermal desorption at 250 °C for 5 min in the GC injection port. Analysis was conducted on a GC–MS 1000 system (Hexin Mass Spectrometry Co., Ltd., China) equipped with a DB-17 MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) using high-purity helium carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.00 mL/min. The gas chromatography temperature program was: initial hold at 50 °C for 2 min, followed by a linear gradient of 3 °C/min to 110 °C with a 2 min hold, subsequently heated at 4 °C/min to 170 °C and maintained for 3 min, and finally a rapid ramp of 10 °C/min to 230 °C held for 2 min. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted under electron ionization conditions (70 eV) with optimized thermal parameters: ion source and transfer line temperatures maintained at 230 °C and 280 °C, respectively. Quantification was performed semi-quantitatively, and the results were expressed as relative abundances normalized to the internal standard.

Determination of total phenolic and flavonoid contents in Zigui navel oranges

Total phenolic content (TPC) was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu method as described by Keawbankrud et al. (2025). Briefly, 100 μL of polyphenol extract was mixed with 150 μL ultrapure water and 250 μL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After 5 min dark incubation, 375 μL of 5% (w/v) Na2CO3 solution and 1125 μL ultrapure water were added sequentially. The reaction mixture was kept in the dark at 25 °C for 60 min before measuring the absorbance at 765 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, U.S.A). Quantification was performed using a gallic acid standard curve (y = 0.0024× + 0.0635, R2 = 0.9937), with results are expressed as milligram gallic acid equivalents per kilogram orange fresh weight (mg GAE/kg FW).

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined by the sodium nitrite‑aluminum nitrate method. Briefly, 400 μL polyphenol extract was mixed with 80 μL of 5% (w/v) NaNO2 solution and incubated for 5 min at 25 °C. Then, 80 μL of 10% (w/v) AlCl3 solution was added, followed by another 5-min incubation. Subsequently, 800 μL 10% (w/v) NaOH solution and 640 μL of 50% (v/v) ethanol solution were added. After 15 min of reaction at 25 °C in the dark, the absorbance was measured at 510 nm using Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader. Quantification was performed using a rutin standard curve (y = 0.0018× - 0.0119, R2 = 0.9987), with results are expressed as milligram rutin equivalents per kilogram fresh weight (mg RE/kg FW).

Determination of antioxidant activity in Zigui navel oranges

The DPPH radical scavenging assay was performed according to the method of Sripakdee et al. (2025) by mixing 100 μL of polyphenolic extract with 1.9 mL of 0.1 mM DPPH methanolic solution in amber vials. After vortexing, the reaction mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader. A Trolox standard curve was established by plotting concentrations (x) against absorbance (y), yielding the regression equation (y = −3.6339× + 0.6343, R2 = 0.9999). Results are expressed as the milligrams of Trolox equivalents per gram fresh weight (mg TE/g FW).

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was performed by mixing 50 μL of polyphenolic extract with 2.45 mL prepared FRAP reagent (containing 0.1 mM 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine [TPTZ] in 300 mM acetate buffer, pH 3.6). Quantification was calculated through a Trolox standard curve, yielding the linear regression (y = 5.3549× + 0.0903, R2 = 0.9989). Results are expressed as milligrams of Trolox equivalents per gram fresh weight (mg TE/g FW).

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences in each quality parameter among the five varieties were evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.2 for a one-way ANOVA (α = 0.05, 5 groups). Flavor compounds were identified utilizing the NIST 20.0 mass spectral library. Multivariate pattern recognition analysis included OPLS-DA with par scaling in SIMCA (v14.1, Umetrics, Sweden). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using SPSS PRO (Online Platform, China) with z-score scaling. The suitability of the data for PCA was confirmed with a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) > 0.7 and a Bartlett's test of sphericity p < 0.05. Correlation analyses among quality parameters were conducted using Pearson's method in SPSS Statistics (v19.0). Data visualization was performed utilizing GraphPad Prism (v9.5, San Diego, CA) and TUTU CLOUD (https://www.cloudtutu.com/). Venn diagrams were created using the Jvenn online plotting software (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).

Results and discussion

Basic nutritional and physicochemical quality analysis

Distinct variations in basic nutritional quality were observed among the navel orange cultivars (p < 0.05, η2 > 0.78, Table S4). The mean moisture content exceeded 83% across all cultivars. NO exhibited the highest protein content (1.35%), while SRO, BO, and CRO showed comparable levels (1.19–1.21%), and LNO had the lowest content (1.10%). These findings aligned with previous reports by Guo et al. (2023), who documented protein contents ranging from 0.91% to 1.39% in various citrus varieties. VC varied significantly among cultivars (26.24–52.46 mg/100 g FW), following the order: NO > CRO > LNO > BO > SRO (p < 0.05). This range was comparable to reported values for Citrus sinensis pulp (36.55–52.85 mg/100 g FW) (Zhou et al., 2018). Notably, the VC content in NO was markedly higher than that in CRO and SRO. Consistent with reports of lower VC levels in the red-fleshed Cara Cara compared to Washington Navel (De Ancos et al., 2017), our finding suggests that the superior VC accumulation in NO is likely associated with specific genotype characteristics rather than pigmentation.

TA, dominated by citric acid with minor amount of malic acid (Lado et al., 2018), is a critical determinant of the flavor profiles in navel oranges. TA content varied significantly across cultivars, ranging from 0.33 g/100 mL (SRO) to 0.63 g/100 mL (CRO), with intermediate values (0.42–0.47 g/100 mL) observed in BO, LNO, and NO. Compared to Washington Navel and Valencia Late from Spain (Zacarías-García et al., 2022), Zigui navel oranges exhibited substantially lower TA. This lower acidity is likely attributable to favorable local agro-environmental factors and may contribute to milder flavor profiles, which are often associated with consumer preference when sweetness is sufficient. TSS refers to water-soluble compounds in fruits, primarily consisting of sugars, tannins, organic acids, and small amounts of pigments, minerals, and vitamins. TSS content ranged from 9.47 g/100 g (LNO) to 14.03 g/100 g (NO and CRO), following the rank order as NO = CRO > SRO > BO > LNO (p < 0.05). These values were comparable to previous reports on TSS content (8–13%) for Lane Late, and Delta varieties (Emmanouilidou & Kyriacou, 2017). The SAR is commonly used to evaluate the flavor and maturity of fruit, as TSS increases and TA decreases during ripening (Zhang et al., 2022). Among the cultivars, SAR value ranged from 21.23 to 39.89, in the descending order: SRO > NO > BO > CRO = LNO (p < 0.05). SS content, dominated by glucose (1–2%), fructose (1–3%), and sucrose (2–6%) (Lado et al., 2018), exceeded 12% in SRO, NO, and CRO, while BO had the lowest SS content at 9.27%.

Fruit morphological characteristics including shape, weight, and color significantly influence consumer preferences and exhibit marked genetic diversity (Goldenberg et al., 2018). BO, CRO, and LNO exhibited comparable average SFW, approximately 260–280 g, while SRO and NO average around 110–120 g. FED ranged from 57.24 to 83.02 mm across orange cultivars, forming three size classes: large (LNO/CRO: 81.02–83.02 mm), intermediate (BO: 79.46 mm), and small (SRO/NO: 57.24–61.00 mm) (Table 1). The CCI, an indicator of color saturation and hue intensity, showed significant cultivar-dependent variation. As detailed in Table 1, CCI differed significantly among cultivars, with CCI values showing the order: BO > LNO > SRO > CRO > NO (p < 0.05). Composition and content analysis of polyphenolic compounds

The composition and distribution of flavonoids vary significantly among citrus varieties, existing primarily as glycosides (e.g. narirutin and hesperidin) (Michailidis et al., 2024). Ten flavonoid compounds were identified and quantified in navel orange pulp, among which hesperidin and narirutin were predominant. Fig. 1A showed significant differences in the pulp concentrations of narirutin and hesperidin among the tested navel orange varieties. Notably, NO exhibited the highest levels of narirutin (372.32 mg/kg) and hesperidin (616.60 mg/kg) among the five tested varieties. These values exceeded those reported for Washington navel orange (124.59 ± 3.27 and 314.48 ± 9.89 mg/kg) and Cara Cara navel orange (121.98 ± 5.34 and 433.30 ± 21.63 mg/kg) (De Ancos et al., 2017). These compositional differences hold practical significance. Given that 10% hesperidin formulations (Daflon) are clinically proven to mitigate diabetic risks (Ben-Assuli et al., 2022), and that an extra daily intake of 255 mg hesperidin and 13.5 mg narirutin lowers systolic blood pressure by 1.01 mmHg, the observed variation offers distinct benefits despite being sensorily neutral. Therefore, cultivars with higher hesperidin and narirutin levels exhibit superior value for developing functional foods targeting cardiovascular health (Valls et al., 2021). Additionally, rutin and eriodictyol were identified as notable flavonoids in Zigui navel oranges. Although the rutin content in these five varieties was lower than that reported for Washington navel orange (10.76 ± 0.11 mg/kg) and Cara Cara (8.43 ± 1.13 mg/kg) (De Ancos et al., 2017), the substantial abundance of hesperidin and narirutin underscores a robust antioxidant capacity in Zigui fruits. Phenolic acids, characterized by multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups on the benzene ring, mainly consist of hydroxycinnamic and hydroxybenzoic acids. Fig. 1A indicated seven phenolic compounds were detected in navel orange pulp, with significant variations in content of most phenolic compounds among varieties. Representative hydroxycinnamic acid-type phenolics in citrus include p-CA, FA, SA, and CA, while the representative hydroxybenzoic acid-type phenolic acids are PA, VA, and HA. Consistent with the findings of Li et al. (2019), navel orange pulp was predominantly composed of hydroxycinnamic acid-type phenolic acids, with SA and FA being the most abundant, together accounting for 75.62% of the total content of the seven phenolic acids quantified. Analysis of synephrine and limonoids

Synephrine, a characteristic alkaloid in Citrus species (Rutaceae), possesses metabolic-promoting properties by increasing caloric expenditure and fat oxidation and serves as a validated biomarker for orange intake assessment (Bader et al., 2017). As illustrated in Fig. 1B, significant differences in synephrine content were observed among the pulp of five navel orange varieties, ranging from 3.45 to 7.23 mg/kg, with the following rank order: NO > SRO > CRO > LNO > BO. The bitterness in navel orange juice and other processed products is mainly attributed to limonoids, which are triterpene derivatives that predominantly exist in the form of free aglycone and glycoside within citrus tissues. Limonoids exhibit a range of beneficial properties, including anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and cardioprotective effects (Lu et al., 2023). Limonoids including nomilin, obacunone, and limonin were observed in navel orange pulp. As shown in Fig. 1B, obacunone was exclusively detected in BO and CRO, with concentrations of 0.20 mg/kg and 0.22 mg/kg, respectively. CRO pulp contained the highest level of nomilin (21.84 mg/kg), while LNO pulp exhibited the highest level of limonin (43.44 mg/kg). Consistent with previous studies, significant differences in nomilin and limonin content were evident across the different navel orange varieties (Huang et al., 2019).

Composition and content of volatile flavor compounds

Aroma is a crucial quality indicator influencing consumer preference. The variety NO contained the most volatile compounds (12), followed by BO (8), CRO (7), and finally LNO and SRO (6 each) (Fig. 1D, p < 0.05). Among the six common volatiles quantified (Fig. 1C), four sesquiterpenes (chamigrene, valencene, α-selinene, and α-maaliene) exhibited a concentration order of NO > BO > CRO > SRO = LNO (p < 0.05). Notably, d-limonene and valencene were markedly more abundant than the other four compounds in all varieties, identifying them as the primary contributors to the characteristic citrus aroma of navel orange pulp. Varietal differences in volatile content reflect distinct aromatic profiles among cultivars. For SRO, the Relative Odor Activity Values (ROAVs) for D-limonene (0.50) and α-selinene (0.041) indicate negligible contributions to the overall aroma. Conversely, α-selinene in NO (ROAV = 1.11) and d-limonene in CRO (ROAV = 1.08) exceed the threshold of 1, indicating their potential as key odorants in these cultivars (Table S3) (Gao et al., 2022). D-Limonene is commonly associated with citrus aroma, while α-selinene has been linked to a black pepper-like scent (Gao et al., 2022). Valencene imparts orange-woody-citrus notes, accumulates upon ripening, and is a key flavor compound in the citrus industry (Sharon-Asa et al., 2003). It is important to note that the volatile profiling in this study was conducted using semi-quantitative methods. Furthermore, given that sensory evaluation was not conducted, the assessment of aroma relevance is constrained to chemical abundance and ROAVs, and the definitive sensory contribution of these compounds remains to be elucidated.

Analysis of antioxidant activity, total phenolic and total flavonoid content

As depicted in Fig. 2A, the TPC in various navel orange pulps ranged from 131.21 to 314.77 mg GAE/kg FW, with the ranking from highest to lowest being NO > BO = SRO > LNO > CRO (p < 0.05). In Fig. 2B, the TFC of various navel orange pulps followed the order: NO > SRO = CRO > BO > LNO (p < 0.05), ranging from 379.40 to 816.78 mg RE/kg FW. These levels are consistent with literature values for citrus pulp (Costanzo et al., 2022). Fig. 2C and D indicated that all varieties exhibited strong antioxidant activity in DPPH and FRAP assays, with NO showing the highest antioxidant capacity, followed by CRO and LNO. Antioxidant capacity also varied significantly among varieties. This variation is likely associated with the TPC, TFC, and VC content, aligning with the findings of Guo et al. Correlation analysis of quality indicators

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the indicators exhibited varying degrees of correlation. Moisture content showed significant negative correlations with protein, VC, TSS, SS, synephrine, flavonoids (naringenin, narirutin, hesperidin, eriodictyol), phenolic acids (p-CA, FA, CA), TPC, five terpenes (o-cymene, chamigrene, valencene, α-selinene, α-maaliene), and antioxidant activities (FRAP, DPPH), while showing positive correlation with tangeretin. Fruit morphological parameters (FED, SFW) were negatively correlated with protein, SS, SAR, synephrine, flavanones (hesperetin, narirutin, hesperidin), and seven phenolic acids, while positively correlated with obacunone, limonin, nomilin, and tangeretin. CCI was positively correlated with moisture, nomilin, tangeretin, FED, and SFW, but negatively correlated with protein, VC, TSS, SS, synephrine, flavanones (hesperetin, narirutin, hesperidin), phenolic acids (p-CA, SA, FA, CA), TPC, FRAP and o-cymene. TSS and SS exhibited significant positive correlations with synephrine, narirutin, hesperidin, and TPC, whereas they showed significant negative correlations with sinensetin, tangeretin, and nobiletin. SS constitutes the material basis for sweetness perception in fruit, a sensory attribute potentially influenced by a complex interplay of sugar composition, acidity, volatiles, and polyphenols. The strong positive correlation between hesperidin, narirutin, and SS may be structurally related to their rhamnosyl-α-1,6-glucose moiety. This structural feature distinguishes them from the intensely bitter naringin (rhamnosyl-α-1,2-glucose), which is associated with a virtually tasteless profile (Tripoli et al., 2007). Consequently, their high accumulation suggests a potential to preserve the intrinsic sweetness of sugars, possibly contributing to the overall perception of sweetness. TPC and TFC were significantly positively correlated with protein, synephrine, flavanones (naringenin, eriocitrin, narirutin), and phenolic acids (p-CA, PA, SA, CA, HA), but showed significant negative correlations with nomilin, limonin, and fruit morphological parameters. Antioxidant activity (FRAP/DPPH) was negatively associated with moisture and rutin, but positively linked to VC, flavanones (naringenin, eriodictyol, narirutin, hesperidin), and phenolic acids (p-CA, SA, FA, and CA). These findings align with a previous study indicating a strong association between phenolic compounds and citrus antioxidant capacity (Guo et al., 2023). Specifically, the strong positive correlation between hesperidin, narirutin and antioxidant capacity highlights their potential role in biochemical defense. Theoretically, this correlation may be explained by the unique 2-phenylchroman-4-one structure of these flavanones. Phenolic hydroxyls scavenge radicals via hydrogen donation, with stabilized phenoxy radicals preventing chain reactions. Carbonyls and adjacent dihydroxyls chelate metal ions (e.g., Fe2+, Cu2+) to suppress Fenton reactions, while conjugated double bonds quench singlet oxygen through energy transfer, effectively mitigating oxidative damage (Olszowy, 2019). Furthermore, in vivo, hesperidin and narirutin regulate oxidative stress by modulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes via the NRF2 signaling pathway (Li et al., 2021, Umar Ijaz et al., 2024). Additionally, VC acts as a hydrophilic antioxidant, scavenging radicals via electron and proton donation from its lactone ring hydroxyls (Kaźmierczak-Barańska et al., 2020). Given these distinct yet complementary roles, the observed correlations suggest a potential synergistic interaction between polyphenols and VC. Moreover, five terpenes showed significant negative correlations with moisture, yet significant positive correlations with nutritional components (protein, VC), flavanones (naringenin, eriocitrin, narirutin, hesperidin), the five phenolic acids (p-CA, SA, FA, CA, HA), TFC, TPC, and antioxidant activities. Hierarchical cluster analysis of quality indicators

To visualize varietal differences in quality parameters and identify characteristic indicators, HCA with heatmap visualization was performed. To ensure the validity of the clustering results, statistical validation was conducted using the Silhouette Coefficient, Davies-Bouldin Index (DBI), and Calinski-Harabasz Score (CH). The analysis yielded a Silhouette Coefficient of 0.839, a DBI of 0.191, and a CH score of 590.791. These metrics indicate that the clusters are well-defined and distinct, statistically validating the HCA groupings (See Fig. 4). Results demonstrated that samples of the same variety clustered together, forming distinct groups with significant inter-varietal differences, particularly showing clear separation of the NO cultivar. This separation is primarily associated with biochemical factors, such as high levels of volatiles, total flavonoids (especially flavanones), total phenolics (particularly seven phenolic acids), basic nutrients (e.g., SS and TSS), and CCI, as well as low levels of limonoids, moisture, SFW, and FED. Variations in these indicators contribute to the clustering distance, as these compounds represent key compositional markers of the chemical and morphological characteristics that distinguish NO from other navel orange varieties. The quality parameters were categorized into four groups: (1) fruit morphology (FED and SFW), limonoids, moisture, color, TA, polymethoxyflavones and d-limonene; (2) rutin, VA, SAR, hesperetin, TFC, PA and HA; (3) hesperidin, SS, TSS, TPC, synephrine, FA and p-CA; (4) protein, antioxidant properties (FRAP, DPPH), bioactive components (VC, SA, CA, eriodictyol, eriocitrin, naringenin, narirutin), and five volatiles. Screening of differential indicators of Zigui navel oranges based on OPLS-DA

OPLS-DA, a supervised discriminant analysis method, was applied to identify characteristic quality indicators in Zigui navel oranges, with the 41 parameters as independent variables (X) and cultivars as the dependent variable (Y). The goodness of fit for the independent and dependent variables is represented by R2X and R2Y, respectively, while Q2 indicates the predictive ability of the model (Liu et al., 2023). The OPLS-DA model demonstrated excellent performance across 41 navel orange quality indicators (R2X = 0.996, R2Y = 0.984, Q2 = 0.97), with statistically robust values confirming excellent model fit and predictive power. The model's validity was confirmed by a permutation test (n = 200), which yielded a significant p-value (p < 0.05) and the Q2-intercept (−0.834), indicating no overfitting. The score plot showed varietal classification consistent with HCA results (Fig. 5A). Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) was calculated to evaluate the contribution of each indicator in discriminating navel orange varieties (Ju et al., 2025). VIP values reflected the importance of each indicator in distinguishing between the different navel orange varieties, with VIP > 1.00 as the selection criterion (Liu et al., 2023). Nine indicators were identified, ranked by VIP score as TPC, SFW, TFC, hesperidin, narirutin, limonin, d-limonene, valencene, and SAR. These indicators could serve as characteristic markers for the quality differences in Zigui navel orange pulp across varieties. Comprehensive quality evaluation of Zigui navel oranges varieties

Nine characteristic indicators with VIP > 1.00 were selected and combined with citrus-related standards and common market evaluation indicators (SS, FED, CCI, DPPH and FRAP), totaling 14 indicators for PCA to evaluate the overall quality of five Zigui navel orange varieties. PCA, a linear unsupervised dimensionality reduction method, orthogonally transforms original data into new principal components (PCs), achieving data dimensionality reduction and visualization (Zacarías-García et al., 2022). The suitability of the data for principal component analysis was confirmed by a KMO measure of 0.749 and a significant Bartlett's test of sphericity (p < 0.01). The Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue >1) was applied to determine the number of PCs to retain. This criterion indicated that the PCs explained more variance than the average variance of the original variables, thus confirming the suitability of these 14 indicators for PCA. This decision was further validated by visual inspection of the scree plot (Supplementary Fig. 1), which showed a clear elbow after the third component, supporting the extraction of three components that collectively explained 93.125% of the total variance. PCA extracted three significant components (cumulative variance: 93.125%), each representing distinct quality dimensions: (1) PC1 (61.263%) had high positive loadings for TPC, narirutin, hesperidin, SS, SAR, valencene, TFC and CCI, but negative loadings for limonin, SFW and FED. The indicators positively correlated with PC1 reflect a combination of bioactive compounds, taste-related components, and color attributes, serving as the decisive factors influencing consumer purchasing decisions. (2) PC2 (22.878%) was mainly dominated by antioxidant capacity, demonstrating high loadings from DPPH and FRAP. (3) PC3 (8.985%) was primarily associated with volatile composition, characterized by d-limonene. Based on PCA of 14 quality indicators for 15 Zigui navel orange samples, factor scores were weighted and summed according to the variance contribution rates. The comprehensive quality score (CQS) was calculated using a weighted sum of the three PC scores. The weights for PC1, PC2, and PC3 were 65.785%, 24.567%, and 9.648%, respectively, reflecting their proportional contributions to the total variance (Table S5). The final CQS was computed using the following equation: CQS = 0.658 × F1 + 0.246 × F2 + 0.0965 × F3. A sensitivity analysis tested the robustness of the CQS model. Weights for PC1 and PC2 were varied by ±10% relative to original values, while PC3 was adjusted to ensure weights summed to 1. Despite variations in absolute scores, sample rankings remained stable, confirming the model's robustness (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). The quality ranking showed NO with the highest score, followed by CRO, SRO, BO, and LNO (See Table 3). Therefore, based on the optimized key physicochemical indicators, the PCA comprehensive evaluation model could simplify the data collection process and facilitate reliable overall quality assessment of different Zigui navel orange varieties. This model provides a quantitative framework for selecting varieties with balanced nutritional and physicochemical properties. By quantifying internal quality beyond visual appearance, it facilitates market grading and premium pricing. Its specificity to Zigui navel oranges supports geographical indication authentication and origin tracing, safeguarding the brand against counterfeiting. Furthermore, tracking quality dynamics facilitates the optimization of orchard management and processing suitability, promoting the preservation of nutritional value. However, it is crucial to acknowledge the limitations and scope of applicability of this model to ensure its responsible interpretation and application. The current model is constrained by its specificity to a particular harvest year (2023–2024), geographical location (Zigui County, ∼230 m altitude), and sample state (fresh, unprocessed pulp). Furthermore, the use of mixed samples, while providing variety-level averages, may mask individual fruit variability. The quality of navel oranges is highly dynamic and subject to variations arising from seasonal effects, growing conditions (including soil type, altitude, and orchard management practices), and processing states. These factors collectively limit the model's generalizability to other regions or agronomic regimes. Conclusion

Zigui navel orange cultivars exhibited distinct physicochemical profiles, characterized by prominent terpene volatiles (d-limonene, valencene) and hesperidin/narirutin. Indicators including TPC, SFW, TFC, hesperidin, narirutin, limonin, d-limonene, valencene, SAR, SS, FED, CCI, DPPH and FRAP serve as key quality evaluation indicators for different Zigui navel orange varieties, with NO achieving the highest overall score based on PCA. This study systematically evaluated the overall quality of five Zigui navel orange varieties, effectively characterizing their physicochemical attributes and providing theoretical guidance for nutritional evaluation and processing applications for Zigui navel oranges.
