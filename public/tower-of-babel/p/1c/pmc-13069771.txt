Impact of organizational climate on the execution of infection prevention and control among Chinese medical staff: a multi-center cross-sectional explorative online survey
Authors: Simin Ma, Kai Qin, Mingjun Zou, Qianning Wang, Li Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch on infection prevention and control (IPC) execution among medical staff after the full resumption of routine medical services remains limited. Organizational IPC climate is a crucial factor influencing the awareness and behaviors of medical staff within healthcare institutions. This study aims to explore the associations between organizational IPC climate and IPC execution among Chinese medical staff, thereby offering new evidence for improving the performance of nosocomial infection management.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted among 13,397 medical staff from 295 medical institutions in Hubei Province, China. The questionnaire was designed to measure IPC climate at both the hospital and department levels, as well as medical staff’s self-reported IPC execution in terms of motivation, process, and outcomes. The structural equation model was applied to examine the relationships among the variables.ResultsAt the hospital level, IPC training and publicity were positively associated with self-reported (SR)-execution motivation (β = 0.518, P < 0.05), SR-execution process (β = 0.829, P < 0.05), and SR-execution outcomes (β = 0.495, P < 0.05), whereas the management model was negatively associated with SR-execution motivation (β = −0.543, P < 0.05), SR-execution process (β = −0.851, P < 0.05), and SR-execution outcomes (β = −0.575, P < 0.05). At the department level, IPC climate was negatively associated with SR-execution motivation (β = −0.037, P < 0.05), SR-execution process (β = −0.040, P < 0.05), and SR-execution outcomes (β = −0.090, P < 0.05).ConclusionMedical institutions could implement continuous, specific, and effective IPC training and various publicity strategies, while optimizing management models by improving systems and procedures, reducing unnecessary burdens, and refining incentive mechanisms. These measures may enhance organizational IPC climate and thus improve IPC execution among medical staff.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26855-x.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are common adverse events in medical institutions, causing direct harm to patients, visitors, and medical staff and posing a major global health challenge [1]. According to the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development and the World Health Organization, nearly 3.5 million people worldwide may die from HAI annually by 2050 [2].

The execution of infection prevention and control (IPC) measures by medical staff is essential for preventing and controlling the occurrence and spread of HAIs [3]. IPC execution refers to the ability of medical staff to implement HAI-related laws and regulations, comply with relevant standards and procedures, so as to ensure medical quality and safety for both patients and themselves. Although traditional IPC measures are effective in preventing HAIs, medical staff often demonstrate suboptimal execution in clinical practice [4].

IPC execution is not merely a matter of following instructions from superiors or enforcing administrative orders and prohibitions; rather, it requires addressing the medical staff’s underlying perceptions of the organizational IPC climate. Organizational climate [5] refers to an individual’s subjective perceptions of their organizational environment. When organizational members perceive consistent environmental characteristics, a shared organizational climate gradually takes shape. This climate represents the internal style and characteristics of an organization and influences staff behaviors and psychological responses. Considering the characteristics of the organizational IPC climate in which medical staff operate is an important prerequisite for compliance with IPC measures. Due to differences in individuals’ perceptions of work tasks based on their roles, variations in climate perception may prevail among staff across disciplines [6]. Moreover, IPC strategies and their application vary across medical institutions, leading to variations in organizational IPC climate [7, 8]. A positive organizational climate can promote an improvement in staff’s behaviors and enhance work execution effectiveness. In the field of IPC, several endemic studies have demonstrated that positive organizational climate improves adherence to IPC practices and supports continuous improvement in IPC quality among medical staff [9, 10].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, medical institutions have placed great emphasis on HAI prevention, and management models involving the participation of all medical staff in IPC were widely adopted. Several studies have found that IPC climate and execution improved markedly in Hubei Province, China [4, 11, 12]. However, after the full resumption of routine medical services, medical staff face dual pressures from heavy clinical workloads and ongoing IPC responsibilities, while related research remains limited. Furthermore, existing studies on organizational IPC climate are relatively circumscribed, focusing primarily on the hospital level. As the emphasis placed on IPC varies substantially across clinical departments, it directly affects staff execution. Therefore, the influence of IPC climate in medical institutions on IPC execution among medical staff warrants further investigation. This study aims to explore their relationship through an online survey among Chinese medical staff, providing new evidence for optimizing IPC execution and HAI management performance under routine medical conditions.

Methods

Study design, participants, and quality control

A cross-sectional survey was conducted between March 5 and March 14, 2025, to collect anonymous online questionnaires from medical institutions in Hubei Province (Central China). Participants (1) aged > 18 years; (2) who had a clinical working experience of more than one year; (3) who had participated in IPC work in the past month; and (4) who had provided informed consent were included in the study. This study was conducted with the support of the Hospital Infection Prevention and Control Branch of the Preventive Medicine Association of Hubei Province. The questionnaire (Supplementary Material 1) was sent to the leaders of nosocomial infection management departments in participating hospitals, who then disseminated it to medical staff within their institutions. These leaders were responsible for survey quality control. Exclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: (1) unreasonable responses (e.g., identical answers across all items or implausibly long clinical work years); (2) excessively short response times (minimum completion time was 4 min); and (3) inconsistent responses to trap items (our research group included 2 items with the same meanings but different wordings in the questionnaire. When the responses to these items were inconsistent, we excluded the questionnaire).

Survey instruments and their modifications

The organizational IPC climate scale was revised based on the HAI safety culture scale [13] of our research team with an expert review method. The scale assesses IPC climate at two levels: hospital level and department level. The hospital-level scale includes two sub-dimensions: training and publicity, and the management model.

Based on the IPC practices after the full resumption of routine medical services, we have revised the IPC SR-execution scale [4, 14] developed before the COVID-19 pandemic using an expert review method.

Additionally, the questionnaire also collected data on the number of IPC training sessions provided by medical institutions and attended by medical staff, demographic characteristics, and included an open-ended question soliciting suggestions for IPC improvement.

To enhance internal validity, the questionnaire was reviewed by two professors specializing in IPC from tertiary hospitals and 10 experts from a teaching university. These experts completed the questionnaire and provided verbal feedback on item clarity and readability. Some items were revised according to the verbal feedback.

The reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficient. The overall Cronbach’s α was 0.89 (n = 13397), indicating good internal consistency. The Cronbach’s α values for each dimension ranged from 0.81 to 0.96, all above 0.80, suggesting satisfactory internal consistency for all subscales. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was used to examine the structural validity of the scale. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value was 0.89, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was χ² = 9792.45 (df = 218, P < 0.05, n = 13397), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. Three common factors were extracted, with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 80.20%. The factor loadings of all items ranged from 0.65 to 0.87, and the loadings on other factors were all < 0.40 with no significant cross-loadings, demonstrating good structural validity. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to verify the factor structure identified by EFA. The model fit indices were as follows: χ²/df = 15.42, RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 0.90, TLI = 0.89, and GFI = 0.89, suggesting an acceptable overall fit. The standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.52 to 0.95 (all 95% CIs did not include 0, P < 0.05), supporting reliable structural validity.

Statistical analyses

Frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SD) were used to describe IPC climate scores, IPC SR-execution scores, and demographic characteristics. The structural equation model (SEM) was employed to examine the associations between organizational IPC climate and IPC SR-execution among medical staff. This method allows for the simultaneous modeling of complex relationships between observed and latent variables, making it suitable for the study design [15]. Potential confounders, including age, gender, years of clinical experience, educational level, profession, professional title, employment type, institution level, and exposure to IPC training, were controlled. As questionnaire items were measured on a 5-point ordinal scale, mean- and variance-adjusted weighted least squares extraction estimation was applied to examine the associations among the study variables.

Descriptive analysis, reliability testing and EFA were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The SEM and CFA were conducted using the lavaan package in R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Goodness-of-fit indices were applied to evaluate SEM’s fit: root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; < 0.08 acceptable, < 0.05 excellent), comparative fit index (CFI; > 0.90 acceptable, > 0.95 excellent), and goodness of fit index (GFI; > 0.80 acceptable, > 0.90 excellent) [16, 17].

Results

Demographics description

This study surveyed 295 medical institutions, including 101 tertiary hospitals, 131 secondary hospitals, 27 primary hospitals, and 36 other hospitals. In total, 13,725 questionnaires were collected, of which 13,397 were valid, yielding an effective response rate of 97.61%. The majority of the respondents were female (84.81%, 11,362/13,397), nurses (74.67%, 10,003/13,397), and had a bachelor’s degree or above (90.00%, 12,057/13,397). Nearly half of the respondents held intermediate professional titles (48.61%, 6,512/13,397), and more than half were employed under a contracted employment system (58.36%, 7,818/13,397). The mean age of respondents was 37.77 years (SD 7.99), and the average length of clinical work experience was 11.54 years (SD 8.39). Most respondents worked in secondary Grade A hospitals (33.22%, 98/295) and tertiary Grade A hospitals (21.69%, 64/295). Measurement scores of IPC climate

As shown in Table 2, this study included 8 hospital-level IPC climate items (including 4 items related to training and publicity and 4 items related to management models) and 6 department-level IPC climate items. Among hospital-level items regarding training and publicity, the highest mean score was reported for “An appropriate number of IPC training sessions with suitable content has been conducted in your hospital” (mean = 4.75, SD = 0.562), and the lowest scores were reported for “IPC signs and posters are often displayed in the public areas of your hospital” (mean = 4.68, SD = 0.696) and “The screensavers of working computers in your hospital regularly promote IPC contents” (mean = 4.68, SD = 0.735). Regarding hospital-level items regarding management model, the highest score was reported for “Your hospital regularly organizes inspections related to IPC involving multiple departments” (mean = 4.76, SD = 0.554), whereas the lowest score was reported for “Your hospital regularly recognizes and commends outstanding staff for IPC” (mean = 4.64, SD = 0.700). At the department level, the highest score was reported for “Your department has established comprehensive systems and processes for IPC” (mean = 4.71, SD = 0.590), whereas the lowest score was for “You are relatively satisfied with the difficulty level and workload of IPC work in your department” (mean = 4.62, SD = 0.714).

Measurement scores of IPC SR-execution

SEM of the association between IPC climate and IPC SR-execution

The SEM examining the association between hospital-level IPC climate and IPC SR-execution demonstrated acceptable model fit, with RMSEA = 0.08 (acceptable), CFI = 0.91 (acceptable), and GFI = 0.93 (excellent). As shown in Fig. 1, training and publicity were positively associated with SR-execution motivation (b = 0.520, SE = 0.240, β = 0.518, P < 0.05), SR-execution process (b = 0.833, SE = 0.255, β = 0.829, P < 0.05), and SR-execution outcome (b = 0.498, SE = 0.241, β = 0.495, P < 0.05). By contrast, the management model showed negative associations with SR-execution motivation (b = − 0.545, SE = 0.240, β = −0.543, P < 0.05), SR-execution process (b = − 0.856, SE = 0.255, β = −0.851, P < 0.05), and SR-execution outcome (b = − 0.578, SE = 0.241, β = −0.575, P < 0.05).

Current status of IPC training in medical institutions

The largest proportion of medical institutions (42.88%, 5,745/13,397) conducted 9 or more IPC training sessions annually, followed by those conducting 7–8 sessions (21.37%, 2,863/13,397). In terms of individual participation, 46.29% (6,201/13,397) of respondents reported attending 7 or more IPC training sessions each year, whereas 7.35% (984/13,397) reported attending 2 or fewer sessions annually. Discussion

Analysis of the causes of HAI outbreaks in medical institutions has demonstrated that medical staff’s awareness and execution of IPC practices are critical to HAI prevention and control [18, 19]. Studies have shown that organizational IPC climate is a key factor influencing HAI management practices, and that IPC execution is closely related to medical staff’s perception of the IPC climate [9, 20, 21]. Specifically, a stronger perceived is associated with higher recognition, commitment, awareness, and execution enthusiasm among medical staff to implement IPC measures. After the full resumption of routine medical services, the IPC environment faced by medical staff has undergone marked changes, underscoring the urgent need to reassess IPC climate and its execution in medical institutions.

Our results revealed that the average scores for both IPC climate in medical institutions and IPC SR-execution among medical staff were relatively high. The primary motivations for IPC execution were to ensure medical quality, protect personal safety, and safeguard patient safety. Medical staff demonstrated a relatively high level of understanding regarding IPC objectives, contents, requirements, and high-risk links. However, their scores in setting personal IPC objectives were relatively low. Medical staff attached great importance to reflection and continuous improvement during IPC execution. The most commonly adopted improvement approaches, in descending order, were feedback from inspections conducted by the department of nosocomial infection management, suggestions from colleagues/leaders, self-review and reflection, and data-based feedback from the department of nosocomial infection management. When encountering problems in IPC work, medical staff most frequently address them by communicating with colleagues, reporting to department leaders, consulting reference materials, and reporting to the department of nosocomial infection management.

The IPC execution scores observed in this study were slightly higher than those in previous studies [4]. A possible explanation is that, following the resumption of routine medical services, medical institutions continued to regard IPC as an important component of medical safety and consolidated previous achievements.

Nearly two-thirds of medical institutions conducted 7 or more IPC training sessions per year, demonstrating a high institutional emphasis on IPC training. However, 29.17% (3,908/13,397) of medical staff participated in only 4 or fewer IPC training sessions annually, suggesting that attendance rates in IPC training remain suboptimal. Responses to the open-ended question on suggestions regarding training most frequently emphasized “continuity”, “specificity”, and “effectiveness”.

First, existing evidence has revealed that medical staff often exhibit deficiencies in both IPC knowledge and skills, as IPC encompasses a wide range of theoretical and practical knowledge from multiple fields [22, 23]. Moreover, many medical colleges and universities have not yet established comprehensive IPC-related courses or programs [24, 25]. Therefore, continuous pre-service and in-service training is essential to ensure timely updates on policies, guidelines, cutting-edge techniques, and risks of emerging and sudden infectious diseases [26, 27]. Second, varying job responsibilities across professional roles result in variations in training needs [28]. Customized IPC training courses for specific professions or departments can maximize the participation of specific audiences and optimize training effectiveness. Third, there is an urgent demand for effective IPC training among the respondents. Active, constructive, and interactive IPC training approaches are more favored than traditional passive methods [29]. In recent years, innovative training methods have drawn attention for improving learning outcomes and can be used alone or in combination. For instance, simulation-based training offers a safe and controlled simulated-environment to strengthen the competence and confidence in addressing distinct scenarios [30–32]. Virtual reality training has been found to improve skills in donning and doffing personal protective equipment [33].

Consistent with previous studies [34, 35], our study unveiled that training and publicity were positively associated with IPC execution of medical staff. Based on the β coefficients of SEM, it had a significant and strong positive effect on execution motivation and process, and a clear and moderate positive effect on execution outcome. This indicates that conducting sustained and targeted IPC training and publicity and improving training effectiveness are core factors for enhancing execution motivation and process, and important factors for improving execution outcome among medical staff.

Hospital-level management models were negatively associated with IPC execution motivation, process, and outcome, exerting a significant and strong negative effect based on the β coefficients of SEM. Department-level IPC climate has an extremely small and weak effect on IPC execution motivation, process, and outcome. Systems and procedures in HAI management may increase medical staff’s workload, requiring them to spend considerable time handling various data, reports, and records. Furthermore, strict IPC requirements may impose additional psychological pressure on medical staff, who are concerned about their inability to simultaneously manage clinical tasks and IPC work, especially when facing inspections, assessments, and potential penalties. This suggests that the hospital’s IPC management model is a core factor: the greater the medical staff’s job pressure and/or workload, the lower their execution motivation, the poorer their execution process, and the worse their execution outcome.

While the fundamental aim of nosocomial infection management is to safeguard medical quality, overly cumbersome regulatory procedures and punitive approaches may negatively affect the work efficiency, psychological stress, and job satisfaction of medical staff [36, 37]. Incentivizing employees can significantly improve their work motivation and job satisfaction [11, 38]. Although leaders have highly emphasized IPC, supporting incentive systems are lacking, which has led to limited incentive effects [39, 40]. Therefore, hospital leaders need to maintain a balance for management mode, which involves optimizing systems and procedures, reducing unnecessary burdens, and establishing strong incentive mechanisms, thereby boosting the medical staff’s willingness and enthusiasm in IPC execution.

This study has several limitations. First, this study relies on medical staff’s self-reported data regarding organizational IPC climate and their own IPC execution, thus risks of recall bias, preserving personal integrity, and social desirability bias might exist [41, 42]. Compared with other information sources, such as direct observation, self-reporting, and self-assessment results, may overestimate or underestimate the actual level [43]. Second, the high values in the questionnaires may raise concerns of ceiling effects and limited variability [44], although we tried our best to control them. Third, convenience sampling may have led to selection bias, as only those who were interested participated in the study, while those who were not interested did not [45]. Four, IPC training may be passive, active, constructive, or interactive. We did not clarify the definition for this training during the investigation, possibly leading to some ambiguity. Five, as this study conducted surveys in medical institutions within Hubei Province, a clustering effect—where medical staff within the same hospital/city tend to offer similar responses, while responses from those across different hospitals/cities vary significantly—might have occurred.

This study is the first to apply organizational IPC climate to IPC execution in medical institutions, expanding the scope of research in this field. Furthermore, it enriches the benchmark data on IPC climate and execution using a multicenter, large-sample survey. Additionally, this study is the first to explore the correlation between IPC climate in medical institutions and medical staff’s IPC execution, providing a theoretical and scientific basis for targeted intervention strategies to be adopted by medical institutions for optimizing the performance of nosocomial infection management.

Conclusions

By employing SEM, this study investigated the impact of organizational IPC climate in medical institutions on medical staff’s IPC execution. Training and publicity were positively associated with IPC execution; however, the hospital-level IPC management model was negatively associated with IPC execution. Medical institutions could prioritize the application of continuous, specific, and effective training programs and publicity in multiple ways. Additionally, management models could be optimized by improving systems and procedures, reducing unnecessary burdens and strengthening incentive mechanisms. These measures may strengthen the medical staff’s perception of the IPC climate and boost their IPC execution.
