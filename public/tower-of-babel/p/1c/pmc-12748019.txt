A Qualitative Study of Health Priorities of People With Dementia and Care Partners in Emergency Department and Hospital Care in a Public Safety‐Net Setting
Authors: Anita Chary, Sumin Yoon, Victor Lara, Annika Bhananker, Michelle Suh, Kei Ouchi, Maura Kennedy, Aanand D Naik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOver half of the people with dementia visit the emergency department (ED) each year. Little is known about how people with dementia and their care partners conceptualize or communicate about health outcome goals or care preferences during acute care episodes.Participants and SettingWe conducted interviews with 14 Hispanic people with dementia and 19 care partners recruited from a public safety net hospital system in the southeastern United States. Participants were community‐dwelling and had recently visited the ED.MethodsWe used a qualitative study design informed by phenomenology and guided by the patient priorities care (PPC) framework. Interviews elicited patients' health priorities, including their care preferences and health outcome goals, as well as their experiences communicating with ED and hospital care teams. We used a deductive content analysis to identify health priorities and inductive thematic analysis regarding communication experiences.ResultsWe identified three key themes. First, participants' health priorities were grounded in values of maintaining social connection and independence. Second, hospitalizations were distressing, disorienting, and physically burdensome for both patients and care partners, leading to preferences to avoid admission whenever possible. Third, communication with ED and hospital teams felt unidirectional, with care plans delivered rather than discussed. Participants felt that care preferences were rarely elicited or incorporated into clinical decision‐making.ConclusionsHospital admission is a critical decision point during acute care episodes for people with dementia. Incorporating brief conversations about health priorities at this juncture may improve alignment between emergency care and what matters most to patients and care partners. Adapting existing frameworks like PPC for time‐pressured acute care settings may help promote shared decision‐making about hospital admission.Keywords: dementia, emergency department, geriatrics, health priorities, qualitative research

Introduction

Over half of people with dementia (PWD) visit the emergency department (ED) each year [1, 2]. These encounters can lead to interventions, including hospital admission, that may not align with patients' and care partners' wishes [3]. Yet little is known about how to promote goal‐concordant care in the ED for this population [4].

This gap is especially salient for socioeconomically disadvantaged and racially or ethnically minoritized PWD. These patients often lack access to longitudinal primary care, making the ED their main source of healthcare [5, 6, 7]. Sociocultural factors also influence their and care partners' interactions with healthcare systems [3, 8]. At the same time, dementia is most prevalent among Black and Hispanic people compared to other racial and ethnic groups, and the Hispanic population is projected to have the largest increase in dementia cases in the coming decades [9]. As a critical point of contact, the ED offers a potential opportunity to identify what matters most to these patients and guide care accordingly.

Patient priorities care (PPC) is a structured, evidence‐based approach to aligning treatments with the health priorities of older adults. It involves (1) identifying a patient's specific health outcome goal(s), bothersome symptoms or problems interfering with the goal, and preferences about types of care they are willing to receive, and then (2) using this information to guide communication and decision‐making [10]. In ambulatory care, PPC has reduced treatment burden and improved concordance of care with patient priorities compared to usual care [11, 12, 13]. However, little is known about how to adapt PPC for emergency care settings, or about how PWD and their care partners identify and communicate priorities in the acute care context.

Recent ED‐based dementia initiatives have focused on delirium prevention, sensory modification [14], and dedicated ED units [15], with less attention to incorporating patients' health priorities into healthcare decision‐making [16]. Existing strategies such as ED‐based geriatrics specialist consultations are largely limited to EDs with Geriatric ED Accreditation [17, 18, 19], a formal designation for adopting guidelines to improve the care of older adults [20]. Practical tools to incorporate patients' health priorities in routine emergency care settings are needed.

We conducted a qualitative study to: (1) identify health priorities of socioeconomically disadvantaged PWD and their care partners using the PPC framework, and (2) understand their experiences communicating about health priorities in the ED. These insights can inform the integration of patient priorities into ED decision‐making and the adaptation of care alignment models like PPC for acute care settings.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a qualitative study using interviews with patients and care partners. The study was informed by phenomenology, which seeks to understand how people make sense of their lived experiences. Simultaneously, we used the PPC framework to explore how participants identified and articulated their health priorities. This study is reported using the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (Supplement Text S1) [21]. The Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board provided ethics approval (H‐53024).

Study Setting

The study population was recruited from a public county‐operated health system in the Southeast United States with 130,000 annual ED visits. The health system is dedicated to serving indigent and underinsured patients: 45% lack insurance, 20% have commercial insurance, 23% have Medicaid, and 12% have Medicare. Older adults who immigrated to the United States tend to use a county‐based temporary insurance program, as they do not meet the Medicare eligibility criteria of US citizenship or 5 years of lawful permanent residency. Half of patients identify as Hispanic, 25% as Black, 18% as White, and 7% as Asian and other races.

Participant Selection and Sample

We identified patients with dementia who had an ED visit between December 2023 and January 2024 using retrospective chart review performed by a physician. Patients had a structured diagnosis of any form of dementia (e.g., Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, Parkinson's disease) documented in the problem list or past medical history; individuals with other forms of cognitive impairment, for example, mild cognitive impairment or traumatic brain injury, were not included. Inclusion criteria for patients were age 55 or older, having a phone number listed in the chart, having a care partner, and community‐dwelling. We used an age minimum of 55 due to the demography of the population, specifically as related to the frequent presence of multiple chronic conditions by this age. We excluded patients who were nursing facility‐dwelling, incarcerated, or whose ED visits were for critical illness resulting in intensive care unit admissions. Inclusion criteria for care partners were age 18 or older and a phone number listed in the patient's chart. We aimed to interview patients/care partners across the health system's four dominant ethnic and racial categories (Hispanic, Black, White, Asian) until reaching thematic saturation, that is, the point where no new themes emerge [22], within each group. We sought to recruit a racially and ethnically diverse sample of PWD and care partners, rather than stratify by ED disposition or presentation severity, as the PPC process focuses on broad, longitudinal priorities rather than condition‐ or disposition‐specific concerns.

Recruitment

A medical student and an anthropologist with no involvement in ED patients' clinical care performed recruitment by phone over a 3‐month period. For PWD who expressed interest in participating, researchers used the Evaluate to Sign Consent measure to determine decision‐making capacity [23]. We obtained assent from participants without capacity and consented to their legally authorized representative.

Data Collection

We developed an interview guide with two focal areas (Supplement Text S2). The first section used established questions from PPC to identify patients' health priorities. The second section was developed by the study team and focused on communication about health priorities during ED visits. We pilot tested the interview guide with two older adults and one care partner of a person with dementia. We modified interview questions for clarity based on consultation with the Wisconsin Network for Research Supports' Community Advisors on Research Design & Strategies, a community research advisory board comprised of patients and family members from diverse ethnic and racial backgrounds that has offered over 300 consultations to make research materials more comprehensible and engaging.

The interviewer was a native Spanish speaker who was trained by a qualitative methods expert with a PhD in Anthropology and participated in online PPC workshops to develop skills in identifying health priorities. Interviews occurred in person in participants' homes, by phone, or videoconference in English or Spanish based on participant preference. Questions were directed toward the person with dementia to ensure their perspectives were elicited without being overshadowed by care partners, as can occur in clinical interactions. When care partners provided the majority of the response to a question, interviewers asked the person with dementia if they agreed or had other input and observed body language and facial expressions. We conducted interviews in dyads or triads, rather than individual interviews, to capture the dynamics of multiple decision‐makers and caregivers' involvement in navigating care. Interviewers took notes about dyadic/triadic interactions following each interview. Participants received a $100 gift card. Audio recordings were professionally transcribed in English or Spanish. A native Spanish speaker translated Spanish transcripts to English. We reviewed electronic medical record data to determine the Charlson Comorbidity Index [24] for each person with dementia. We performed the Quick Dementia Rating System [25] with care partners as a measure of dementia severity.

Data Analysis

To analyze the first portion of the interview about health priorities, we used a deductive approach [26] to identify health values, care preferences, and health outcome goals using the PPC framework (Supplement Text S3). We identified patterns using thematic content analysis. For the second portion of the interview related to experiences communicating about care preferences, we used an inductive approach [27]. We reviewed the first five transcripts to develop a preliminary codebook focused on care partner experiences, hospital experiences, communication about clinical decisions, and language (Supplement Text S3). We used Microsoft Excel to organize data into a matrix by participant versus summarized data and quotations related to each code. Each transcript was coded independently by two researchers, one non‐clinician and one clinician, at levels of phrases and paragraphs. A senior qualitative researcher led consensus discussions to resolve coding discrepancies. For example, if one analyst coded a paragraph of an interview as related to “hospital experiences” and the second analyst did not, the senior researcher requested analysts to provide rationale for their coding and asked all team members to opine about the application of the codes. Researchers iteratively reviewed coded data and quotations in the matrix for overarching themes. The research team included anthropologists, medical students, emergency physicians, and a geriatrician with diverse racial and ethnic identities. Data collection and analysis occurred in tandem. We reached data saturation by 15 interviews, at which point we terminated recruitment.

Results

We conducted 15 interviews with 33 total participants. Eleven interviews were with patient/care partner dyads, 3 with a patient and two co‐primary care partners, and 1 with a care partner alone, as the person with dementia felt unwell at the time of the scheduled interview and wished for us to proceed with the care partner alone. Fourteen interviews were in Spanish and one in English. Most interviews were performed in person in participants' homes (n = 9). The remainder occurred by phone (n = 6). Interviews lasted on average 87 min (range: 47–119 min).

Of 55 eligible patients identified, we reached by telephone potential participants representing 24 patients (either a patient or care partner), who identified as Black, Asian, and Hispanic. We successfully enrolled to study completion 33 participants representing 15 patients, all of whom identified as Hispanic. Sixty percent of PWD were female (n = 9) and 40% male (n = 6). Care partners were predominantly adult children (58%, n = 11) and female (79%, n = 15).

Health Priorities Framed Around Social Connection and Independence

Across interviews, patients and care partners articulated health outcome goals grounded in values related to maintaining social connection and maximizing functional independence. People with early‐stage dementia articulated their values and goals themselves. Care partners conveyed values and goals for those with more advanced disease.

Social connection centered on spending time with family and engaging in social hobbies, such as cooking and shopping with others. For example, one participant had previously worked in restaurants and enjoyed cooking for and eating with family:

I like cooking, but in the state I find myself in, I can't stand that long. (ID 109, person with dementia)

Deconditioned after several hospitalizations and rehabilitation stays, he developed difficulty walking and standing. The patient and care partner saw mobility as the most important health problem his healthcare should support so that he could continue cooking. Participants saw improving mobility, managing anxiety, fatigue, and pain, and maintaining memory (e.g., medications, mental exercises) as instrumental interventions for supporting social engagement.

Participants defined independence in terms of being able to engage in daily routines and mobilize themselves—that is, walk and transfer—within their homes. PWD sought to avoid burdening family members, and indeed wanted to contribute to their households whenever possible. One person with early dementia described a goal of staying physically active so that he could take care of his grandchildren. Knee pain limited him from walking and going to the gym. His spouse described:

He is always worried about being on time to pick up [the grandkids]…He makes flour tortillas for the girls…He's very involved in the chores here around the house. (105, care partner)

Participants' health outcome goals often reflected the intersection of values of social connection and independence, highlighting how functional ability (e.g., mobility, pain control) was tied to their ability to maintain meaningful roles in the family and household.

Challenging Experiences Shape Ambivalent Preferences About Hospital Admission

Participants expressed care preferences to avoid hospital admission when possible. Although not all patients had been admitted from the ED visit that led to their recruitment to the study (Table 1), all participants had had a hospital admission in the prior year and spoke about care preferences within this context.

Participants described three distinct ways in which hospitalization posed challenges both for PWD and their care partners. First, hospital stays interrupted PWD's daily rhythms, separated them from family, and triggered confusion, anxiety, and agitation. Care partners expressed that when PWD were alone in the unfamiliar hospital environment, they became disoriented and distressed. One care partner recounted:

She tears out [her IV], she tears everything out. She becomes violent, aggressive. (ID 108, care partner)

This care partner worried that any future admission would again provoke disorientation. In these ways, admission disrupted health values related to social connection, managing health, and functioning.

Second, hospital admission was logistically and physically challenging for care partners. Although care partners did not criticize hospital accommodations, several described crowded boarding conditions where PWD were admitted into shared ED rooms lacking chairs or cots for family. In such cases, care partners stood or sat and slept on floors. Rather than providing respite, hospitalization often extended the caregiving experience under physically demanding conditions. In response, some participants expressed guilt about burdening their family. One participant told his children:

Go do your jobs. You've already given me a ride here. (ID 104, person with dementia)

Third, in addition to these emotional and environmental challenges, participants highlighted that hospital admission decisions felt like a top–down process that excluded their perspectives. Although participants trusted that admission was clinically warranted if recommended, for some, alternatives felt both plausible and preferable. For example, one participant and his care partner (ID 100) recounted that during a heart failure exacerbation, they had asked whether he could return to the ED the next day for another dose of IV diuretics instead of being admitted overnight. They were told it was not possible. Although participants respected clinicians' judgment, some expressed disappointment that their care preferences—including desires for alternatives to hospitalization—could not be accommodated.

Clinical Communication Lacked Dialogue or Attention to Health Priorities

When reflecting on their experiences of communication with care teams, participants often did not distinguish between the ED and inpatient settings—a conflation likely driven by prolonged ED boarding and overlapping care environments. Participants described that information flowed from clinicians to families, rather than between them. Patients and care partners often reported learning about treatment plans after decisions had already been made. Care partners felt that physicians rarely asked what treatments care recipients “would want or not” (ID 101) and “weren't very willing to collaborate” (ID 113). For example, one patient sustained a hip fracture after a fall. A surgeon initially recommended nonoperative management with physical therapy, a plan that aligned with the family's preferences. However, after 2 days, the surgeon changed the plan without further discussion, causing significant distress for the care partner:

And then he tells me that they're going to operate on her…I started crying with him and I was very distressed because an operation, imagine. (ID 112)

Another example involved a hospital care team giving a care partner instructions about space needed for the patient's toileting transfers and prescribing a raised toilet seat after a fall. The care partner did not have the opportunity to discuss how the patient's home bathroom could not accommodate these recommendations. In these ways, care plans could leave patients and care partners feeling excluded from decision‐making. The absence of collaboration led some care partners to disengage:

There's nothing to be gained by talking to the staff, because it's a team and they already know what they're going to do. (ID 104)

No participants had ever discussed their health priorities with emergency or hospital clinicians.

Notably, participants did not describe language barriers due to widespread access to bilingual staff and interpreters. Participants generally praised Spanish‐speaking clinicians and interpreters, though some noted that they lacked access to interpreters outside of their interactions with clinicians, that is, while waiting in the ED and when interacting with technicians.

Discussion

In our qualitative study, Hispanic PWD and their care partners emphasized health priorities related to social connection and independence, described hospitalizations as challenging, and reported limited involvement in ED and hospital treatment decision‐making. Although our study was not designed to focus exclusively on Hispanic participants, the resulting sample provides insights into an underrepresented group that disproportionately experiences structural barriers in dementia care [29] and has frequent ED use compared to other racial and ethnic groups [5, 7, 30, 31]. Experiences navigating care decisions within multigenerational households and in‐hospital communication may reflect cultural values and systemic inequities that shape the acute care experience for Hispanic patients and care partners. Our study highlights that hospitalization decisions may occur without consideration of patients' care preferences and may represent a missed opportunity to align acute care with individual health priorities.

Several aspects of acute care may pose challenges to integrating patients' health priorities into decision‐making. Time constraints, lack of a longitudinal relationship with the patient, and the need to stabilize acute illness all limit clinicians' ability to elicit and act on patients' health outcome goals in real time [32, 33]. Communication may be further constrained by team handoffs, physical separation between patients and clinicians, and unclear responsibilities for goal‐setting conversations [34].

Despite these potential challenges, our findings suggest that hospital admission decisions may offer a practical entry point for aligning ED care with the priorities of PWD and their care partners. Participants' priorities of maintaining social connections and maximizing independence reflect findings from prior PPC‐based research [11, 12, 13] as well as longstanding literature in geriatrics and palliative care [35]. Yet, these priorities were disrupted by hospitalization, which participants described as disorienting, isolating, and physically taxing. Participants desired alternatives to hospitalization when possible, echoing findings from other studies [3, 8]. In cases where inpatient or outpatient management is both clinically reasonable, understanding a patient's values—particularly around independence—can inform ED decisions about hospitalization [36, 37]. Similarly, these priorities may guide inpatient teams' discharge planning, rehabilitation referrals, or care coordination to better support patients' functional goals and caregiving context.

Existing shared decision‐making (SDM) frameworks in emergency care for older adults focus on serious illness and end‐of‐life care, such as resuscitation and mechanical ventilation [38]. Although these models have improved how clinicians engage patients and families facing critical illness [39], they may be less applicable to patients like those in our study—PWD who are not critically ill. For these patients, the key questions are different: How will care decisions impact their daily lives, social connections, and ability to remain at home without overburdening care partners? Hospital admission decisions can reflect what medical anthropologist Sharon Kaufman describes as the routinization of intervention‐focused care [40]—that is, standard next steps in clinical care that move forward without explicit alignment with a patient's goals. New or adapted SDM approaches are needed to guide hospitalization decisions and align acute care with patients' functional goals and caregiving contexts.

Our findings suggest that adapting frameworks such as PPC for ED and hospital care could help bridge this gap. Full implementation of PPC in these acute care settings may not be possible. However, a brief adaptation—focused on care preferences at key decision points, such as hospital admission—could support clinicians in understanding and acting on patient priorities in real time [41, 42]. Structured questions about what matters most could inform whether hospitalization aligns with or disrupts a patient's functional goals or caregiving context. Alternative phrasings, developed based on clinical practice, are offered as adaptations with simple, concrete language for people with dementia.

These adaptations are particularly important given emerging national initiatives to promote age‐friendly health systems. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) now require hospitals to identify a patient‐centered goal for hospitalization [43]. Similarly, the Geriatric Emergency Department Accreditation (GEDA) program emphasizes communication about goal‐concordant care as part of its standards [20, 44]. Yet practical strategies to elicit and incorporate these goals—particularly for patients with cognitive impairment—are limited. Additionally, our findings resonate with the aims of CMS's new Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) Model, which seeks to reduce avoidable hospitalizations and support community‐based dementia care through person‐centered, caregiver‐focused approaches [45]. Integrating goal elicitation into ED and inpatient care could help advance GUIDE's mission by preserving continuity, supporting family caregivers, and reducing unnecessary transitions out of the home.

Limitations

This study was conducted within a single safety‐net health system and included only Hispanic participants, the majority of whom were Spanish‐speaking. Although this provides valuable insight into an underrepresented population, findings may not apply to other racial, ethnic, or linguistic groups. In addition, all data were based on retrospective accounts and may be subject to recall bias.

Conclusion

Hospital admission decisions may be the most practical area for aligning ED and hospital care with the health priorities of PWD and their care partners. Brief adaptation of PPC and other SDM models that focus on patient goals and priorities may help clinicians better honor what matters most in acute care.
