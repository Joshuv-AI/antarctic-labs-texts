Selenium nanoparticles affect chicken offspring's intestinal health better than other selenium sources
Authors: Yanhong Chen, Sasa Zhang, Xuyang Gao, Zhiqian Hao, Yanbing Guo, Yongqiang Wang, Jianmin Yuan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to assess the effects of maternal diets containing various selenium (Se) sources on the intestinal mucosal function in the jejunum of chicken offspring. A total of 630, 18-wk-old Hy-Line Grey hens and 70 18-wk-old Hy-Line Grey breeders were randomly allocated into 7 groups, with 5 replicates in each group (18 hens and tow roosters). After 4 wk of Se depletion, the birds were fed either a nonsupplemented basal diet (control) or the same basal diet supplemented with 0.15 mg/kg selenium nanoparticles (Nano-Se), 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se, 0.30 mg/kg selenocysteine (Sec), 0.30 mg/kg sodium selenite (SS), 0.30 mg/kg selenomethionine (SeMet), or 0.15 mg/kg Nano-Se + 0.15 mg/kg Sec, for 8 wk. Frtilized eggs were collected and incubated during the final week of the experiment. Jejunal tissues from embryonic d 18 and the hatch day were collected for analysis, and the 7-d survival rate of the offspring was recorded. Compared to the control, the maternal diet of 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se, 0.30 mg/kg Sec, and 0.30 mg/kg SeMet significantly increased the survival of 7-day-old offspring (P < 0.05). The maternal diet supplemented with 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se significantly increased intestinal villus height and the villus height/crypt depth ratio in chicks at embryonic d 18 and in 1-day-old (P < 0.05). The maternal diet containing 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se and Sec increased the mRNA expression levels of tight junction proteins in 1-day-old offspring (P < 0.05). Supplemental 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se significantly increased the mRNA expression levels of marker genes in intestinal enteroendocrine, stem, and Paneth cells (P < 0.05). In 1-day-old chicks, the number of intestinal goblet cells, as well as the mRNA expression levels of intestinal mucin2 (Muc2) and goblet cell differentiation factors (Spdef and C-myc), were the highest in diets supplemented with 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se. Moreover, the expression levels of intestinal Muc2 and Spdef in chicks at embryonic d 18 was the highest with 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se supplementation (P < 0.05). Supplementing with 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se significantly reduced reactive oxygen species levels and decreased the mRNA expression levels of apoptosis-related genes in 1-day-old chicks (P < 0.05). Additionally, 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se supplementation significantly down-regulated NLRP3 pathway gene expression in 1-day-old chicks (P < 0.05). In conclusion, maternal dietary supplementation with Nano-Se improved jejunal microarchitecture, antioxidant levels, and the expression of tight-junction protein in chicken offspring along with supporting goblet cell development by inhibiting the NLRP3 signaling pathway.

INTRODUCTION

The intestinal barrier is crucial for maintaining intestinal health (Bedford and Apajalahti, 2022). The primary role of the intestinal mucosa is to maintain the intestinal balance and protect against pathogenic microorganisms (Gustafsson and Johansson, 2022).

Therefore, chicks with immature intestinal mucosa are particularly susceptible to pathogens and oxidative stress, which leads to stunted growth (Li et al., 2015; Pitargue et al., 2019). Exploring new strategies to improve the intestinal health of chicks is essential.

Maternal nutrition plays a vital role in embryonic development and offspring growth in poultry (Emamverdi et al., 2019). Selenocysteine (Sec), a selenium (Se)-containing amino acid, mitigates oxidative stress by regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Barchielli et al., 2022). Broilers grow rapidly and are highly vulnerable to dietary Se deficiency, which causes tissue damage, cell death, and disruption of immune and redox homeostasis (Yang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023). Accordingly, broiler diets should be rich in high-quality Se to enhance antioxidant capacity and reduce inflammation, thereby improving the overall quality of offspring.

Recently, less toxic and more bioavailable organic alternatives, such as selenomethionine (SeMet) and Sec, have emerged as effective alternatives to conventional sodium selenite (SS) (Briens et al., 2013; Rajashree et al., 2014; Skřivan et al., 2012). Maternal supplementation with SeMet is an effective strategy for improving antioxidant performance and overall health of chicks (Asadi et al.,2017; Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021).

Selenium nanoparticles (Nano-Se), a form of Se with antimicrobial properties (Wang et al., 2007), offer superior adsorption and lower toxicity than SS (Gangadoo et al., 2020; Sarkar et al., 2015). A comparative study in mice revealed that Nano-Se is a more effective antioxidant than SS in mitigating intestinal inflammation and protecting the intestinal mucosa against oxidative damage-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction (Alkhudhayri et al., 2018). Nano-Se alleviates dextran-sulfate-sodium-induced colitis in mice by enhancing intestinal barrier function and boosting antioxidant capacity (Ye et al., 2023).

Our previous study in broilers revealed that Nano-Se can be synthesized by Bacillus subtilis S12 which notably reduces intestinal oxidative stress and inflammation by activating the NLRP3 signaling pathway, and promoting the proliferation of intestinal goblet cells (Chen et al., 2022). We also showed that Nano-Se was superior to other Se sources in improving the intestinal barrier function of broilers (Chen et al., 2024). Whether maternal Nano-Se supplementation is superior to other Se sources for improving the intestinal health of chicks requires further study. Organic Se and Nano-Se are better than inorganic Se; however, the combination of Nano-Se and Sec can achieve better results is yet to be explored.

This study aimed to compare the effects of a maternal diet of various forms of Se on intestinal histology, antioxidant status, inflammatory factors, goblet cell factors, and NLRP3 signaling pathways in chicken offspring.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and Experimental Design

A total of 630 healthy 18-wk-old Hy-Line Grey hens and 70 18-wk-old Hy-Line Grey roosters were randomly allocated into 7 groups of 5 replicates each (18 hens and 2 roosters). After 4 wk of Se depletion by feeding a basal diet without additional Se, the hens were fed either a nonsupplemented basal diet (control) or the same basal diet supplemented with 0.15 mg/kg Nano-Se, 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se, 0.30 mg/kg Sec, 0.30 mg/kg SS, 0.30 mg/kg SeMet, or 0.15 mg/kg Nano-Se + 0.15 mg/kg Sec. In the previous week, 55 fertilized eggs (per replicate) were incubated. On embryonic d 18 and 21 (1-day-old chickens), 8 chicks from each treatment group were randomly selected and euthanized to obtain jejunum tissue samples. Portions of the jejunum were used for morphological analysis, while the remaining tissue was frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for RNA and protein analyses.

Newly hatched chicks were raised in a temperature-controlled room and fed a commercial diet (Tangshan Quanqi Feed Co. Ltd, Tangshan, Hebei, China) meeting the recommended requirements for Hy-Line Grey laying hens. The chicken house temperature was initially set at 35°C and gradually decreased to 33°C by d 7. The birds had unlimited access to crumbled-pellet feed and water. Hy-Line Grey guide was used for management, and mortality rates were recorded daily.

Samples Measurement

The Histological Characteristics of Jejunum Tissues

After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for more than 24h, the intestinal tissues were embedded in paraffin wax. Subsequently, 5-μm sections of each sample were stained using Alcian Blue (AB) and periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS). Finally, the slides were examined under a Leica microscope (DMI803250593; Heidelberg, Germany) to analyze jejunal morphology and goblet cell density. Intestinal morphology, including villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and goblet cell density (under the mid-villus area, 100μm in length) was evaluated by randomly selecting 10 intact villi per bird and using a previously described method (Röhe et al., 2020).

Gene Expression Analysis by Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction

Intestinal samples (50 mg) from 1-day-old chicks were used to extract total RNA, using RNAiso Plus (9109; TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan). Sample purity and quantity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), based on a 260/280 nm absorbance ratio in the range of 1.8-2.0. RNA integrity was verified and RNA was reverse transcribed using a StarScript III One-Step RT-PCR Kit (A236-04; GenStar, Beijing, China). Measurements of Antioxidant-Related Proteins and Inflammatory Factors Using Western Blotting

Jejunal tissue (approximately 50 mg) extracts were homogenized in radio-immunoprecipitation-assay buffer and the supernatants were collected. Protein samples were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (IPVH00010; Burlington, MA). Membranes were blocked with nonfat dry milk in triethanolamine-buffered saline at room temperature (25 °C) for 1 h, followed by overnight hybridization at 4 °C along with the following specific primary antibodies: anti-SOD1 polyclonal antibody (1:10,00 dilution; 10269-1-AP; Proteintech Group, Inc., Wuhan, China); anti-catalase polyclonal antibody (1:1,000 dilution; 21260-1-AP; Proteintech Group, Inc, Wuhan, China); anti-NLRP3 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:1,000 dilution; A12694; ABclonal, Wuhan, China); anti-ASC/TMS1 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:1,000 dilution; bs-6741R; Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China); anti-TLR4 polyclonal antibody (1:1,000 dilution; BS79626; Nanjing Bioworld Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China); anti-GAPDH polyclonal antibody (1:4,000 dilution; 10494-1-AP; Proteintech Group, Inc.); or anti-beta-actin rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:1,000 dilution; PTM-5143; Jingjie PTM BioLab). The secondary antibody (HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG [H+L]; 1:1,000; AS003; ABclonal) was then added and the membranes were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were examined using BeyoECL Plus (P0018S; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to calculate the density of the protein bands.

Measurements of Reactive Oxygen Species in Jejunum Tissue by Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay

The ROS concentration was measured in the jejunal tissue of 1-day-old chicks, using a chicken ROS ELISA Kit (MM-6012001; Jiangsu MEIMIAN, Yancheng, China). Finally, the concentration was normalized to the weight of the jejunal sample and expressed in pg/mL.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software (SPSS 20.0, Institute Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and Graph Pad 8.0 Software (Graph Pad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Duncan's multiple range test was used to compare the means between different treatments. Results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 and were presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

RESULTS

DISCUSSION

The embryonic stage is crucial because maternal nutrition plays a key role in influencing development. Nutrient deficiencies in the breeder's diet can lead to similar deficiencies in the offspring (Olkowski and Classen, 1999). The results of the present study suggested that maternal Nano-Se, Sec, and SeMet intake reduced the offspring mortality rate during the first week compared with the control diet and SS supplementation groups. All offspring received the same basal diet with consistent feed intake; therefore, the differences in Se status were solely due to the maternal diet. Maternal SeMet supplementation, compared with SS supplementation, has also been reported to significantly reduce chick mortality in the first postnatal week (Wang et al., 2011), which supports our findings. Maternal SeMet outperformed SS for Se retention in eggs and 1-day-old chicks, suggesting higher Se retention in early SeMet-treated offspring, which persisted during later growth stages (Zhang et al., 2014). This intriguing finding may be explained by the fact that Nano- and organic Se convert to Se2− via selenocysteine lyase (Scly) or trans-selenation at a much higher rate than inorganic Se, which relies on the glutathione-glutaredoxin and thioredoxin pathways for conversion to selenide Se2− (Turanov et al., 2011). Therefore, we speculate that Nano- and organic Se in the maternal diet offer higher bioavailability and retention in chicks, reducing inflammation and enhancing the antioxidant status for better growth.

The 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se group revealed a higher VH, a lower CD, and a higher VH/CD ratio, indicating that maternal Nano-Se supplementation may enhance intestinal development in the offspring during the embryonic period, as evidenced by the lower first-week mortality rate. Furthermore, tight junction proteins control the permeability of intestinal cells and maintain the structural integrity of the junctions (Li and Ajuwon, 2021). The results of the present study revealed that adding 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se and Sec to the maternal diet increased the mRNA expression levels of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Claudin-1, and Claudin-5 in the newly hatched offspring compared to the control and SS diets. This result suggests that maternal Nano-Se intake leads to better intestinal integrity in chicken offspring.

Maintaining the intestinal barrier relies on a variety of specialized cells, such as absorptive, Paneth, enteroendocrine, and stem cells. A higher VH/CD ratio indicates enhanced intestinal cell development (Awad et al., 2011). We observed that maternal intake of 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se increased the mRNA expression levels of markers of intestinal enteroendocrine cells (Chga), stem cells (Lgr5), and Paneth cells (Lyz) in 1-day-old chicks compared to the control and SS diets. Maternal Sec intake also upregulated the mRNA expression levels of Chga, Lyz, and SI (epithelial cell marker) in 1-day-old chicks. Thus, maternal Nano-Se and Sec can promote the development of intestinal cells in the offspring.

In challenging environments or conditions of Se deficiency, harmful oxygen, and nitrogen free radicals are produced in chicks, causing oxidative damage to intestinal tissues and reducing cell viability (Sinha et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2015). Selenium acts as an antioxidant, boosting the body's defense against peroxidation and free radical damage, which forms selenoprotein P that prevents intestinal diseases (Short et al., 2018). Selenium may increase enterocyte viability by regulating the activity of inflammatory cytokines and increasing the intestinal antioxidant status (Safdari-Rostamabad et al., 2017). Zhang et al. (2014) and Zhao et al. (2019) reported that, compared with SS, maternal dietary SeMet improved the antioxidant capacity of chicken offspring compared with the SS diet. We first observed that, compared with the control and SS-supplemented diets, maternal dietary supplementation with 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se significantly reduced ROS levels, decreased the mRNA expression levels of Bax and Bcl-2, and increased the protein expression levels of CAT in 1-day-old chicks. These findings indicated that 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se in the maternal diet reduced free radical levels and enhanced cellular anti-oxidative abilities.

Maintaining the intestinal mucosal barrier also depends on the secretion of intestinal mucus, which aids in the growth of beneficial bacteria and shields the epithelial surfaces from pathogens (Duangnumsawang et al., 2021). Goblet cells produce intestinal mucus and are primarily located in columnar epithelial cells (Nyström et al., 2021). Secretion of intestinal mucus is also dependent on ROS production and inflammation levels (Patel et al., 2013). Intestinal bacterial infection triggers the Notch signaling pathway by increasing ROS levels, resulting in the loss of goblet cells (Xie et al., 2020). Selenium deficiency in chickens can damage the intestinal mucosa and cause apoptosis of intestinal goblet cells by activating ROS signaling (He et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2018). Spdef and C-myc are key genes in the differentiation and maturation of intestinal goblet cells, as well as mucus production (Alam et al., 2020; Konsavage et al., 2012). Our previous data indicated that Nano-Se increased the number of intestinal goblet cells and Muc2 mRNA expression levels in broiler chicken (Chen et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2022). We also observed that maternal dietary supplementation with 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se led to the highest number of goblet cells and up-regulated the expression levels of Muc2, Spdef, and C-myc in day-old chicks. Moreover, maternal diets supplemented with 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se resulted in the highest intestinal Muc2 and Spdef expression levels in 18-day-old embryos. These results suggest that maternal Nano-Se supplementation significantly enhances goblet cell differentiation and maturation at the embryonic stage.

Selenium deficiency can cause higher levels of production of pro-inflammatory factors and ROS, thus aggravating inflammation and cell death in the gastrointestinal tract (Chi et al., 2019; Patankar and Becker, 2020). Evidence suggests that NLRP3 triggers pyroptosis in response to intestinal infections (Jia et al., 2020). The NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β pathway is a key driver of inflammation and can be activated by increased ROS levels (Li et al., 2022; Sahin et al., 2019). Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome leads to the formation of a complex with ASC and pro-caspase-1, which converts ASC into active caspase-1. This releases IL-1β and caspase-1, which drive harmful cellular responses (Schneider et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2023). Selenium can reduce inflammatory damage by inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway (Huang et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2019). Few NLRPs are involved in the regulation of goblet cell-induced mucus secretion (Wlodarska et al., 2014). Pathogenic bacteria influence goblet cells, regulate Muc2 secretion and promote mucus layer formation through the NLRP3 pathway (Birchenough et al., 2016). We studied the impact of Se on NLRP3 signaling in chicken embryos and observed that maternal 0.30 mg/kg Nano-Se treatment down-regulated the mRNA expression levels of Myd88, Caspase-1, and IL-18 and the protein levels of caspase-1, NLRP3, and TLR4 in day-old chicks. Thus, Nano-Se is the most effective form of Se for promoting intestinal goblet cell development in chicken embryos by inhibiting the NLRP3 signaling pathway.

Nano-Se is superior to other Se compounds in terms of its antioxidant properties. Further investigation is required to understand if this variation is caused by differences in the metabolism, deposition, and tissue distribution of different sources of Se in the embryonic stage of offspring.
