Surface-specific performance of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles in latent fingerprint visualisation
Authors: Anuj Sharma, Mahipal Singh Sankhla, Sumeet Singh Bhati, Anugya Agrawal, Shivani Tyagi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Latent fingerprint (LFP) visualisation remains a cornerstone method in forensic science, with ongoing developments aimed at enhancing clarity, sensitivity, and substrate compatibility. Due to their ability to tailor surface chemistry and optical properties, nanoparticles present a promising avenue for fingerprint development, especially on various types of surfaces. However, there has been a lack of understanding regarding the comparative behaviour of nanoparticles across different substrates. This review aims to address this gap by critically comparing the surface-specific performance of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles. In which we consider common nanoparticles for LFP development, such as Gold, Silver, silica, zinc oxide, Titanium dioxide, iron oxide, Copper oxide, and Aluminium oxide. Our review examines how various nanoparticles influence fingerprint residue on porous and non-porous surfaces and assesses their effectiveness in terms of clarity, durability using these nanoparticles. Our key finding of comparative analysis highlights that gold nanoparticles yield promising outcomes even on historically challenging porous substrates due to their affinity for sweat and amino acids. Conversely, zinc oxide and titanium dioxide exhibit superior fluorescence-based contrast on non-porous surfaces such as glass and plastics, as well as some porous surfaces. The rest of the nanoparticles were able to achieve their success on porous and non-porous surfaces with some limitations. We also outline diverse methods employed by various researchers, including dusting, brushing, spraying, and fluorescence imaging, while emphasising the role of substrate texture and the functionalization of nanoparticles. The review provides insights using comparative tables on selecting effective nanoparticle-based methods for specific forensic contexts to achieve more stable and universal fingerprint recovery in criminal investigations.Graphical abstractIllustration of various nanoparticles in forensic science for latent fingerprint Visualisation

Introduction

Nanoparticles with various morphologies have, in the last two decades, been synthesised through the techniques of nanotechnology, which find application in forensics. In the area of LFP detection, metal nanoparticles like silver or gold have been used to specifically target the residues present in the fingerprints. Such nanoparticles are frequently used to enhance sensitivity by aiming at metabolites and explosives by conjugating with antibodies and aptamers [1]. Their attributes, high surface area, smaller particle size, and good thermal and electrical conductivities are beneficial in assisting with the identification of crime scenes [2].

Apart from this, the use of metal-oxide nanoparticles such as titanium dioxide, zinc oxide, iron oxide, europium oxide, and silica nanoparticles against LFPs while purporting their effectiveness. These metal oxide powders are preferred due to their high contrast, good adhesion to fingerprint residues, and the low diminution of the image resolution, which is especially useful when the imagery is captured during daylight. These attributes accurately map the Integrated Automated Fingerprint Identification System (IAFIS) approach to tracing LFPs for criminal investigations [3, 4].

Quantum dots (QDs) such as cadmium sulfide (CdS QDs), cadmium selenide (CdSe QDs), and cadmium telluride (CdTe QDs) have been noted to have very good fluorescing properties, which are useful in fingerprint Visualisation on both porous and non-porous substrates [5]. Quantum dots have been known to possess high quantum yield, good emission light spectrum, large surface area, small size, and are stable after exposure to light. These properties enable them to interact with fingerprint residues and provide clearer images, better visibility, and sharper ridge patterns [6].

Moreover, other functionalized nanoparticles, which include carbon dots (CDs), polymer dots, fluorescent silica nanoparticles, fluorescent mesoporous silica nanoparticles, conjugated-polyelectrolyte dots (CPEDs), aggregation-induced emission luminogens (AIEgens), and rare earth metal-based fluorescent nanomaterials, are used for detecting LFPs. The use of these nanoparticles is due to their capability of producing quality images with a high background contrast, excellent specificity, and increased sensitivity. The adaptability and effectiveness of these nanomaterials have widened the scope of tools used by forensic specialists, enabling them to reliably pinpoint persons implicated in the crime [7, 8].

It has been established that the synthesis of nanoparticles can be broadly categorised into two approaches, namely the top-down and the bottom-up approach, as depicted in Fig. 1. The Top-Down method also includes Mechanical milling, which is a method of grinding bulk materials into nanosized particles through the fracture of particles using the milling balls, and Sputtering, which involves the bombardment of particles with high kinetic energy, which are then deposited onto a substrate material. In addition, laser ablation is another technique wherein a high-power laser is directed at bulk (target) material, and as a result, metallic nanostructures are produced. Chemical etching is also applicable in this method of operation by using electively reactive chemicals to eliminate and lower the dimensions of the target material. On the contrary, the Bottom-Up approach builds nanoparticles from the smaller building blocks. Such as sol–gel synthesis, where sol-solution is allowed to polymerise into gel form, and gels can sinter into nanoparticles, and laser pyrolysis, where laser energy initiates reactions that depend on reducing agents to form nanoparticles. Using biological methods, which is a subdivision of the Bottom-Up techniques, plants, yeast, or even bacteria are used for the eco-friendly synthesis of nanoparticles. A Comparative overview of key studies employing gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) for latent fingerprint visualisation

A simplified approach was adopted by Hussain et al. [9], who proposed in situ formation of AuNPs directly on fingerprint ridges using endogenous fingerprint constituents like lecithin and potassium iodide (KI). This work diverged from previous methods by eliminating the use of pre-synthesised AuNPs and enabling Visualisation through naturally occurring redox reactions. Direct AuNP formation on fingerprint residues increases contrast and stability of developed prints. Sebaceous fingerprints were collected from volunteers on A4 paper or Whatman cellulose filter paper. Substrata with LFP were submerged in an aqueous solution of gold chloride (HAuCl₄·3H₂O, 1 mM) at room temperature. Fingerprints became visible after about 1 h, with the maximum development in 4 h. Developed prints showed red to purple colouration due to the surface plasmon resonance of formed AuNPs. Lecithin and KI, natural constituents of human sweat, were found to reduce Au3⁺ to Au⁰, enabling the in situ formation of AuNPs (15–20 nm) on fingerprint residues. Plastic surfaces, e.g., petri dishes; however, the intensity of developed fingerprints over these surfaces was less vivid than over porous substrata. Shown are characteristic surface plasmon resonance absorption bands typical of AuNP formation. In the absence of sufficient reducing agents, grooves prevented AuNP formation, increasing contrast with ridges. Formed fingerprints did not fade colour for at least 6 months, indicating stability. This Method showed better results on a porous surface than on a non-porous surface.

Shifting from purely visual to combined optical and chemical detection, Tang et al. [10] employed AuNP sputtering and imaging mass spectrometry (IMS) for dual-mode fingerprint analysis. Their approach shared similarities with Wei’s dual-imaging but went further by extracting molecular signatures, including drugs and endogenous lipids. The formation of differently sized AuNPs on ridge versus groove areas reflected earlier work on morphology-based contrast and built upon it with advanced instrumentation for spatially resolved chemical mapping. In contrast to traditional techniques, the technique obtains high-contrast images and chemically resolved spatial data from endogenous (e.g., fatty acids) to exogenous compounds (e.g., drugs). Sebum fingerprints were produced by a donor on various surfaces (e.g., glass, plastic, paper). Verapamil hydrochloride was occasionally applied to mimic contamination. Fingerprints were gold sputtered using a coater with high-purity gold at 12 mA, ~ 0.04–0.05 mbar pressure in argon, for 130 s, forming nanoparticles on residues. IMS utilised matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) for chemical imaging. IMS analysis used laser intensities of 2600–3000 arbitrary units and spatial resolutions of 60–90 μm, imaging fatty acids (e.g., palmitic, stearic, oleic acid) and other fingerprint components. Fingerprint ridges that are lipid-rich produced ~ 3 nm pseudospherical AuNPs, whereas cleaner grooves favoured larger, irregular nanoislands (~ 10 nm). Non-porous substrates such as glass coverslips and plastic films gave excellent optical and chemical imaging without additional treatments. For porous surfaces, white paper was also employed; because of insulation, additional sputtering (10 s at higher current) was required for better conductivity in IMS. Without additional sputtering on porous paper, IMS mapping was less effective. High-resolution optical images exhibited clear colour contrast: ridges pink (592 nm SPR peak) and grooves blue (660 nm SPR band). TEM verified the nanoparticle size: ~ 3 nm on ridges and ~ 10 nm on grooves. Mainly consisting of sebum, these areas accommodate smaller AuNPs as the organic matrix facilitates nucleation. The AuNPs are pinkish with luminescent emission and enhance MALDI signals. Grooves containing less organic matter produce larger, irregular AuNP clusters. Varied nanoparticle morphology causes a blue shift in plasmon resonance and enhances contrast between ridges and grooves. SPR peaks at 592 nm (ridges) and 660 nm (grooves) confirm nanoparticle size and distribution.

Where Tang’s method centred on chemical analysis, Shenawi et al. [11] focused on contrast inversion via hydrogen bonding between AuNP-capping ligands (with mercaptocarboxylic acids) and cellulose substrates. Conventional methods for the development of fingerprints on porous media, such as paper, are likely to be based on the chemical components in sweat, such as amino acids, in trace amounts, and produce weak or invisible prints. Their strategy allowed for “negative” fingerprint imaging on porous surfaces by targeting the paper substrate itself rather than fingerprint residues. This represented a paradigm shift: instead of visualising what is present (residues), it visualised what isn’t, effectively expanding AuNP utility in previously difficult contexts such as paper. Here, the background is coloured, but the fingerprint ridges are not, by exploiting the inverted visualisation through hydrogen bonding between the carboxylic groups of the capping ligands and the cellulose of the paper, allowing selective silver deposition in the regions not covered with fingerprint residue. By targeting the paper substrate itself, the method circumvents reliance on the chemical composition of sweat. Several mercaptocarboxylic acids were screened, including 11-mercaptoundecanoic acid and 6-mercaptohexanoic acid. The thiol group is anchored to the gold surface, and the carboxylic acid group is left free for hydrogen bonding to cellulose. The paper was immersed in the solution of AuNPs, and the nanoparticles bound to the cellulose of the paper in the regions not covered with fingerprint residue. After rinsing, the paper was treated with a silver enhancement solution, leading to silver deposition over the AuNP-bonded areas, and thereby forming a negative image of the fingerprint. Developed fingerprints were visually examined and exhibited excellent high-contrast negative images. The study did not use advanced imaging or spectroscopic methods; the problem was the visual quality of printed fingerprints. The fingerprint residues serve as a mask, preventing the AuNPs from binding to the cellulose underneath. Where there are no fingerprint residues, the AuNPs bind to the cellulose through hydrogen bonding. The subsequent deposition of silver produces a dark background and clear, uncoloured ridges. Best results were achieved using AuNPs capped with 3-mercaptopropionic acid. It developed fresh and 14-month-old fingermarks as negative impressions with high contrast and resolution. A similar dual-purpose approach was adopted by Cheng et al. [12] using sputtered AuNPs for both imaging and mass spectrometric analysis via surface-assisted laser desorption/ionisation mass spectrometry (SALDI-MS).

Like Tang, they demonstrated high-resolution optical contrast and molecular fingerprinting, but they also emphasised particle size distribution and the biocompatibility of gold. Cheng’s work validated AuNP versatility across substrates like paper and glass, reinforcing earlier conclusions about substrate-dependent performance. The premise of the method was to develop a clean, stable, and dual-use technique that not only could increase the optical contrast of fingerprint ridges but also enable detailed molecular analysis by SALDI-MS. Gold, because of its chemical stability and intense surface plasmon resonance (SPR) effect, was investigated independently of its alloys with silver to evaluate its potential as a single-component fingerprint development reagent. The process deposited AuNPs onto fingerprints on paper and glass substrates. Sputtering employed a gold target with argon for 60–100 s. This yielded small particle sizes (3–5 nm) on oily sebum-rich ridges and agglomerates with larger particle sizes in cleaner grooves. The nanoparticle morphologies created a contrast: ridges were pink-coloured because of an SPR peak at 530 nm, whereas the grooves emitted a purple-blue colour because of the red-shifted SPR response from larger-sized particles. Optical Visualisation of clear fingerprints without chemical dyes was enabled through this colour change. AuNPs mostly stuck to organic and lipophilic units in fingerprint ridges, i.e., fatty acid residues and amino acid residues. These units were involved in stabilising and aiding the nucleation of AuNPs, while grooves lacked such material content and hence experienced uncontrolled nucleation. Thus, there was a significant visual and morphological contrast in the ridge and groove regions. This was also supported by TEM, which reiterated the size difference between nanoparticles in both regions. The same was established by UV–Vis spectroscopy, with clear SPR peaks for ridge-deposited AuNPs around a fixed 530 nm. Palmitic, oleic, and stearic fatty acids were detected, demonstrating AuNPs' dual role as mass spectrometry matrices and imaging agents. Glass showed the best imaging performance, where fingerprints had high contrast and resolution for more than a month. On paper, fingerprint clarity was maintained, but contrast was reduced after about 12 days because of substrate absorbency. AuNPs still enabled successful visual and chemical analysis on paper, nonetheless. The research experimented with different compositions of alloys, but gold sputtered pure gold samples demonstrated that AuNPs can create stable, high-contrast LFP on a variety of surfaces. The ease of the sputtering process, combined with the biocompatibility and longevity of gold, makes AuNPs a candidate for application in forensic applications where visual identification and molecular analysis of fingerprint residue are needed. The authors did not mention the quantity of fingerprints that were tested, but results were replicable on every sample of the different surface materials, with little failure on glass or paper.

This research demonstrates the effective application of AuNPs to contemporary forensic fingerprint identification. In a shift toward fluorescent imaging, Yu et al. [13] introduced gold nanoclusters immobilised on montmorillonite (AuNCs@MMT) as a safer and more stable alternative to traditional powders. Their use of red fluorescent emission under UV light offered high visibility on both porous and non-porous surfaces. This powder overcomes the drawbacks of traditional methods, including the toxicity of cadmium-based quantum dots and operational complexity. To enhance dispersion and usability for powder-based fingerprint detection, the AuNCs were immobilised on Na⁺-MMT clay through electrostatic interaction (verified by a zeta potential of − 28.4 mV), creating the final AuNCs@MMT nanocomposite. The AuNCs synthesised were around 4.08 nm in diameter, verified by TEM and HRTEM imaging. They showed intense red fluorescence with an emission peak at 655 nm and an excitation maximum at 525 nm. The photoluminescence was stable under both UV (365 nm) and green light, which is important in avoiding background fluorescence and reducing UV exposure risks. The AuNCs were well dispersed in the MMT clay, which enhanced their fluorescence emission and avoided agglomeration. The nanocomposites maintained the structural integrity of the clay, as verified by XRD and IR spectroscopy, and did not show any signal of elemental gold in EDX due to low relative content. LFPs were collected by a single donor on a range of porous and non-porous substrates: glass slides, plastic, stainless steel (tweezers), painted metal (binder clips), porcelain enamel, adhesive tape, weighing paper, and a multicoloured ceramic mug. High-quality fingerprints were developed within one hour using conventional powdering techniques. The developed prints exhibited bright red fluorescence under UV light with minimal background staining, especially on polished surfaces like glass and stainless steel. Even third-level details such as sweat pores and second-level minutiae were visible under magnification. On porous substrates like weighing paper, the AuNCs@MMT nanocomposites produced visible fingerprint patterns, although background fluorescence from the substrate slightly reduced contrast. Despite this, the developed prints remained usable. SEM images revealed strong, selective adherence of the powders to fingerprint ridges via electrostatic interactions with oily and moist residues, while deposition in grooves was minimal. This ridge-specific binding, along with the clear red fluorescence under UV light, enabled distinct ridge-valley contrast. On non-porous surfaces such as glass, stainless steel, and plastic, the prints exhibited sharp, high-contrast ridge details with very low background staining. Similarly, on complex multicoloured surfaces like ceramic mugs, the red fluorescence provided excellent contrast even when ridge details were barely visible under normal lighting. The powder retained both its fluorescence and fingerprint-developing capability after 7 months of ambient storage, including exposure to high summer temperatures (> 30 °C), confirming the stability and practicality of AuNCs@MMT nanocomposites for in-situ forensic applications. Kolhatkar et al. [14] combined Single-Metal Deposition (SMD) with Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) to achieve high-contrast Visualisation and chemical identification. This study echoed earlier dual-imaging methodologies by Tang and Cheng but introduced a powerful vibrational spectroscopy layer for biomolecular identification, highlighting the evolving sophistication in AuNP-enabled fingerprint analysis. Leverage the plasmonic properties of AuNPs, this procedure is aimed at enhancing both the luminescence and Raman signals of fingermark residues to enhance Visualisation and chemical profiling. The fingerprints were deposited on a glass slide non non-porous nature, then Immersion in a colloidal gold solution (AuNPs synthesised from HAuCl₄ and trisodium citrate dihydrate). A He–Ne laser (632.8 nm) was used for excitation, and AIST-NT OmegaScope 1000 with a Nanofinder 30 Raman spectrometer captured the luminescence and Raman signals, then spatial mapping was performed to correlate the luminescence intensity to the fingermark ridge pattern for SERS measurements. AuNPs (15–20 nm) bound preferentially to fingermark ridges because of the presence of organic residues. Most recently, Wei and Cui [15] synthesised Au@KCC-1 nanocomposites—gold-decorated, amine-functionalized silica nanospheres—that provided strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged fingerprint residues.

Their work integrated the chemical selectivity of Shenawi’s approaches with the surface versatility and stability observed in Yu’s fluorescent powders. Their powder formulation demonstrated lasting activity and strong ridge selectivity on both porous and non-porous substrates. Wei and Cui prepared a sensitive, selective, and stable powder by in-situ growing Au NPs on a fibrous nano-silica support (KCC-1), which is first aminopropyl-functionalized (KCC-1-NH₂). Small Au NPs and accessibility of active sites of KCC 1 enhance adhesion to negatively charged fingerprint residues and show clear third-level features and ridge details on various surfaces. This research bridges the gap between conventional powders and advanced fluorescent nanocomposites. Hydrothermal synthesis was obtained by dissolving tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) in a solution of cyclohexane and 1-pentanol, addition of cetyl pyridinium bromide (CPB) and water-soluble urea under stirring for 30 min, and then heating at 90 °C for 10 h. The product was washed and calcined at 550 °C for 6 h. Refluxing was carried out for 12 h of KCC 1 (0.2 g) with 3 aminopropyltriethoxysilane (3 APTES, 0.36 g) in toluene under a nitrogen atmosphere. KCC 1 NH₂ was separated from the mixture by washing with ethanol. KCC 1 NH₂ (0.2 g) was dispersed into 100 mL of water and stirred with 3 mL hydrogen tetrachloroaurate(III) trihydrate (HAuCl₄·3H₂O, 0.0099 g) for 3 h. Excess sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) was used to reduce Au3⁺ ions. The reaction was for 3 h, immobilising Au NPs onto KCC 1 NH₂ fibres. Final Au@KCC 1 nanocomposites were filtered and dried. Analysis by ICP indicated an Au loading of 1.66 wt%. Au@KCC 1 powders were used as fingerprint powders on various surfaces: Non-Porous: Blue plastic, foundation box, glass, and smooth plastic Blu-ray discs. Porous: Red cardboard and black paper. Treated fingerprints were visualised by a VSC6000 workstation under visible light. FSSEM and TEM established the fibrous nature of KCC 1 and its spherical microsphere size (~ 313 nm) with fibres ~ 33.66 nm apart. Au NPs, of average size 3.02 ± 0.75 nm, were immobilised evenly on the fibres without agglomeration. UV Vis Spectroscopy exhibited maximum absorption at 530 nm, as expected of Au NP size. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) established bands for Si–OH, condensed silica, and C–H stretching of grafted 3 APTES. XRD established amorphous silica without prominent Au peaks (due to low loading), whereas XPS established the chemical state of Au. DLS established particle size distribution of KCC 1 (~ 314 nm) and a zeta potential of + 25.54 mv for Au@KCC 1, as would be expected from electrostatic interactions with negatively charged fingerprint residues. Amino-functionalized KCC 1 guarantees strong Au NP binding, which attracts them to negatively charged fingerprint components (amino acids, proteins). Accordingly, powders selectively attach to papillary ridges, with minimal furrow deposition. Latent prints developed with powders of Au@KCC 1 exhibited high sensitivity, selectivity, clear ridge detail, and detectable sweat pores. Grayscale analysis exhibited maximum contrast between the ridges and furrows, with glass exhibiting a D value greater than the base box. All supports produced legible fingerprints, including porous ones like black paper and red cardboard, upon which Au@KCC 1 was superior to market powders. The powders remained active for 6 months of dry storage.

A Comparative overview of key studies employing Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) for LFP Visualisation

Negatively charged nanoparticles are generated through a reduction process that draws positively charged fatty acids in fingerprint residues under acidic conditions. AgNPs were synthesised by a wet chemical process using varying concentrations of silver nitrate (0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 M), and their characterisation was done by UV–visible spectroscopy and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM). At 0.001 M, AgNPs were irregular (~ 10.66 ± 1.22 nm); at 0.01 M, spherical (~ 12.50 ± 2.64 nm); at 0.1 M, agglomerated (~ 14.44 ± 2.68 nm). UV–Vis spectroscopy revealed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks at 400–402 nm. HR-TEM confirmed nanoparticle formation and size difference.

Fingerprints were developed on porous white paper from volunteers by a spray method after acetic acid treatment to neutralise alkaline fillers that can interfere with nanoparticle interaction. Developed prints showed excellent clarity in ridge details (bifurcations, deltas, core, short ridges, ridge endings). Fingerprints remained clear for over 30 days, far exceeding AgNO₃, which faded in about 20 days. The attraction between negatively charged AgNPs and positively charged sebaceous residues ensured strong adherence mainly to ridges, not grooves. While this approach yielded promising results on paper, further refinement in synthesis and surface interaction was explored in subsequent studies.

Building upon this chemical synthesis foundation, Brandão et al. [17] introduced a greener method for AgNP production using acetylated cashew gum (ACG) as both a stabiliser and a reducing agent. This innovative approach addresses the limitations of traditional fingerprint developers like ninhydrin and silver nitrate, which are expensive, time-consuming, and unfriendly. The authors used a biodegradable, low-toxicity polymer of Anacardium occidentale (cashew tree) to design a low-cost, rapid, and easy nanoparticle synthesis method for in-field forensic use. The nanoparticles were reduced from 1 mM silver nitrate using sodium borohydride in the presence of ACG at varying concentrations. The synthesis was confirmed by UV–Vis spectra and NTA data. The lowest concentration (0.5 mg/mL) produced the smallest and most monodisperse AgNPs (mean diameter: 83.5 ± 2.3 nm, mode: 46.4 nm), while increasing concentrations yielded larger and aggregated particles. TEM and AFM analysis showed that all ACG-AgNPs were mostly spherical but displayed different levels of aggregation, particularly at high concentrations of ACG.

The zeta potential values were very negative in all samples ( − 31.2 to − 34.1 mV), showing very good colloidal stability and high electrostatic interaction potential with the positively charged fingerprint secretions residues. The 0.5 mg/mL ACG-AgNPs performed better, detecting 20 min in 1-h-old and 11 minutiae in 24-h-old fingerprints. By comparison, the 5.0 mg/mL sample only detected 6 minutiae in the 1-h-old print, and the 1.0 mg/mL sample exhibited poor contrast due to over-aggregation. The improved performance of lower ACG concentrations was attributed to reduced nanoparticle size, higher dispersion, and better surface interaction, enabling better resolution of fingerprint ridge details. Theoretical modelling and DFT simulations validated these results, showing that ACG-AgNPs exhibit local chemical reactivity and electrostatic behaviour similar to traditional reagents.

While Prasad and Brandão established the usefulness of AgNPs on absorbent surfaces, Barros et al. [18] transitioned this research to non-porous metallic substrates by utilising green-synthesised AgNPs in combination with Surface-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionisation Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (SALDI-TOF MS). The study found that AgNPs exhibit unique optical and electrochemical features, reducing environmental and health risks and improving fingerprint detection. Latent fingerprints were deposited on MALDI plates and treated with either α-CHCA or green-synthesised AgNP suspension. Mass spectra were obtained in reflector-positive ion mode, with fingerprints examined at three spots for reproducibility. The study employed a harmless and scalable strategy to biologically synthesise AgNPs, using no toxic stabilisers or reducers. The results showed that AgNP-treated fingermarks generated significantly stronger mass spectral signals than untreated samples and samples treated with α-CHCA or AgNO₃ solution. Principal silver ion peaks were at least 20 times stronger in AgNP-treated samples than in AgNO₃. The study also indicated that silver's attraction to squalene, mainly in sebaceous secretions along ridges, mirrors selective interaction with fingerprint ridges. In this study, non-porous metal substrates, such as MALDI plates and aluminium foil, were used. Fingerprint detail could be visualised on aluminium foil using the AgNP suspension without preconditioning or UV treatment, but no spectrometric data were provided. No porous surfaces were examined, and an opening for further research is mentioned.

Despite its success, both studies were limited to porous substrates, prompting the need to explore techniques suitable for metallic and non-porous surfaces. Further refining AgNP use on metallic surfaces, Alves et al. [19] introduced an electrochemical technique for fingerprint development on stainless steel, copper, and brass. This method relied on potentiostatic deposition, where AgNPs formed only in the fingerprint furrows (grooves), generating high-contrast negative images. The controlled, non-toxic procedure offers enhanced clarity and durability even on aged samples and real-world items like keys and coins.

The study collected sebaceous fingerprints from a female donor and deposited them on pre-cleaned stainless steel, copper, and brass substrates. The AgNPs were electrochemically deposited from an aqueous electrolyte containing 5 mM AgNO3 and 5 mM KNO3 under potentiostatic control at − 0.35 V vs. Ag/AgCl, with deposition times ranging from 80 to 200 s depending on the substrate. The developed fingerprints were analysed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Nikon D5500 camera imaging, the Griaule® Forensic Fingerprint software, and the UK Home Office grading system.

Substrate-dependent variation in nanoparticle morphology was observed, with stainless steel exhibiting dendritic and loosely dispersed structures, while copper and brass surfaces exhibited more compact and uniformly agglomerated nanoparticle carpets. Brass exhibited the best performance, with 97.62% of fingermarks graded as level 4 using the UK Home Office scale. Copper followed with 90.48% at level 4, while stainless steel yielded 33.33% at level 4 and 26.19% at level 2 (potentially identifiable). The method demonstrated excellent stability in long-term ageing assessments, with high-quality images obtained even from fingermarks aged up to one year under ambient conditions. Quantitative contrast analysis of the SEM images confirmed the presence of critical minutiae, including ridge endings, bifurcations, deltas, and cores.

As opposed to Divya et al., who aimed at synthetic functionalization, Rajan et al. emphasised eco-friendly, bio-based materials without compromising on imaging quality. The process was meant to enhance the clarity of fingerprints on multicoloured or patterned surfaces where traditional powders fail. SNPs (~ 200 nm) were prepared through thermochemical treatment of rice husk ash, alkaline solubilization, and acid precipitation. They were white, spherical particles that were coloured with natural (curcumin, anthocyanin, carotene) and synthetic dyes (safranin O, superb blue, Ponceau 4R), and mixed with corn starch (ideal 9:1 proportion) to enhance pigment dispersal. Characterisation through FESEM affirmed retention of morphology after doping, while FTIR indicated little chemical alteration, manifesting structural stability. Curcumin-doped SNPs exhibited the greatest fluorescence and thermal stability under UV (350–380 nm) light. Latent prints of four donors were made on ten multicoloured, non- and semi-porous surfaces—like metal cans, porcelain, leather, and glossy paper—using squirrel hair brushes and examined under Forensic Alternative Light Source (FALS). Curcumin-doped SNPs provided the highest ridge contrast, adhesion, and shelf-life (more than one year), particularly on non-porous surfaces, compared to synthetic counterparts and minimised problems such as over-powdering and irregular particle sizes encountered with commercial fluorescent powders. Nevertheless, textured leather decreased clarity in all powders. In general, curcumin-derived SNPs were cost-effective, non-toxic, environmentally friendly, and user-safe substitutes for commercial fluorescent powders. Following this environmentally friendly pathway, Jenie et al. [22] further boosted the sustainability and efficiency of fingerprint detection using silica nanoparticles (SiNPs) derived from geothermal wastes and incorporating Rhodamine 6G (R-6G) intra-nanoparticle matrix incorporation. This technology improved fluorescence brightness and lipid residue interaction compared with Rajan et al.'s natural dye-derived system. By converting silica scaling from geothermal power plants—a troublesome industrial byproduct—into a photoluminescent forensic tool, the research provided an inexpensive, non-toxic alternative to traditional powders, solving issues like toxicity, low contrast, and poor surface compatibility. SiNPs were prepared through a sol–gel process: amorphous geothermal silica was treated with different NaOH concentrations (0.375–3.0 N) at 60–90 °C to produce sodium silicate, followed by titration with HCl to give silica gel, which was dried at 80–100 °C. The best nanoparticles (at 1.5 N NaOH, 90 °C) had a BET surface area of 289.2 m2/g, a particle size of ~ 20.7 nm, and mesoporous nature. R-6G was introduced during the sol–gel process, yielding fluorescent silica nanoparticles (FSNPs) of uniform 5–10 nm diameter, as established by TEM. FTIR results established hydrogen bonding between R-6G and silanol groups, while XRF revealed a reduction in SiO₂ purity owing to incorporated dye and salts. XRD established the amorphous character of FSNPs, which enhanced reactivity and fluorescence. Optical analysis revealed FSNPs to exhibit 2.4 times greater fluorescence intensity compared to free R-6G and fluoresce at 583 nm when excited at 553 nm. Their decreased fluorescence lifetime (0.008 ms compared to 0.01 ms) indicated confinement-enhanced emission. Fingerprints from one donor were developed on glass, acrylic, aluminium, and polyurethane. On glass, aluminium, and polyurethane, distinct ridge details were seen under white light, although adhesion on acrylic was weak. Under UV (365 nm), all surfaces exhibited improved clarity and bright orange fluorescence, particularly on glass. FSNPs selectively bound to lipid residues through lipophilic interactions, allowing ridge-specific deposition with little background noise or clumping, even on difficult surfaces such as polyurethane.

Bhati et al. [23] presented mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNPs) prepared through a microemulsion templating route (MET), which provided a significantly greater surface area (941.88 m2/g) and better sweat residue adsorption than the lower surface area particles of Divya et al. and Rajan et al. This facilitated high-contrast fingerprint imaging under UV light, particularly on non-porous substrates. MSNPs were a photostable, non-toxic, and highly adsorptive substitute for traditional fingerprint powders, which overcame drawbacks such as low sensitivity, background noise, and toxicity. The nanoparticles were synthesised by a surfactant-templated sol–gel process with CTAB, TEOS, ammonium hydroxide, ethyl acetate, and methanol under controlled conditions. TEM and SEM established the presence of spherical particles (100–150 nm) with deep furrow-like mesopores, a BET surface area of 941.88 m2/g, and a pore volume of 1.30 cm3/g. FTIR, XRD, and EDX established their amorphous nature and chemical integrity, while a zeta potential of − 27 mV established good colloidal stability. MSNPs were tested on five non-porous surfaces—glass slide, stainless steel, plastic calculator cover, plastic phone cover, and silica phone cover—and on porous ones such as paper and wood. Four donors' (post-rubbing forehead and nose) latent prints were developed using a camel hairbrush and viewed with 395 nm UV light. The MSNPs yielded distinct ridge patterns (Level 1) and minutiae (Level 2) on all non-porous surfaces, although sweat pore details (Level 3) were not visible. No useful development was achieved on porous substrates because of nanoparticle absorption and deformation. Prints were stable for as long as 30 days on glass and silica phone covers, but less durable on stainless steel and plastic after 15–20 days. Transparent polybags did not generate useful prints, possibly because of low contrast and high reflectance. Most importantly, MSNPs were found to be non-toxic through MTT assays on Hela and human epithelial cells at concentrations of up to 15 µg/mL, establishing them as safe to use in forensic applications.

Unlike fluorescence-based techniques, Meng et al. [24] created a dye-free technique utilising monodisperse silica nanoparticles (MSNs) to construct photonic crystals for the Visualisation of latent blood fingermarks on dark, non-porous surfaces. The technique overcame issues of dye degradation and background noise inherent in earlier fluorescence methods. The breakthrough involves the use of photonic crystal effects, generated through self-assembled MSNs—to produce visible contrast under white or monochromatic light without chemical tagging. Prepared through the Stöber method, MSNs (~ 240 nm, < 5% size distribution) were dispersed in ethanol with 1.0% Tween 80, creating a stable suspension. Comparative testing revealed that ethanol and Tween 80 provided the best clarity and ridge hold among surfactants and solvents tested. Blood-stained fingerprints, cast onto black polypropylene and aged for up to 30 days, were developed by spraying the MSN suspension, washing with ethanol, and drying. Ridge patterns were made visible by Bragg diffraction, particularly under 540 nm illumination and yellow-green filters. MSNs preferentially attached to ridge residues, creating light-diffracting structures without contaminating furrows, providing high-contrast images even after 30 days, though some sharpness was lost with age. No surface incompatibility was noted, but the process was only tested on black plastic. Notably, it needed no fluorescent dyes or pre-treatment, providing a straightforward, non-toxic, and extremely sensitive method well-suited to quick forensic application, with long-term potential for general surface application. Oliveira et al. [25] pushed fingerprint detection further by synthesising chitosan-functionalized MCM-41 mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with dansylglycine (MCM-41@Ch@DnsGly), combining high surface area, intense fluorescence, selective ridge adhesion, and photostability in a single system. These hybrid nanoparticles performed better than previous materials from Divya et al., Bhati et al., and Meng et al., especially on intricate surfaces such as banknotes and metals, and maintained image clarity for aged prints up to 30 days. The nanoparticles were prepared in three steps: MCM-41 formation, chitosan functionalization, and dansylglycine loading, with particles sized between 68 and 113 nm. Fingerprints (fresh and aged) were developed with dusting and subsequent UV lighting, which provided high-contrast images on different surfaces. The particles selectively attached to fingerprint ridges without background noise and clumping due to stable dispersion and photostability. Even on holographic substrates, small glare was reduced by filter adjustment. On the UK Home Office grading scale, 94% of produced prints achieved Grade 3 or 4, demonstrating high identification potential and verifying the system's superior robustness and substrate compatibility for forensic purposes.

Rajan et al. [26] provided the groundwork by synthesising ZnO nanoparticles through a simple direct precipitation route, yielding tiny (~ 13 nm) phase-pure powders that facilitated straightforward, high-contrast fingerprint formation on non-porous surfaces such as dark glass. Though successful in creating visible ridge patterns, this initial process was mostly confined to flat, dark backgrounds and was not surface-versatile or optically tunable. Their application of ZnO as a white contrast powder proved to be highly clear even in the absence of photoluminescence, providing ease for high-contrast Visualisation on black surfaces. Although UV–Vis spectroscopy revealed an absorption maximum at 365 nm and a ~ 3.2 eV bandgap. The nanocrystalline ZnO particles were found to have high surface reactivity, allowing good adhesion to LFP residue through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions. Used as white powder on non-porous glass surfaces, the ZnO NPs gave clear high-contrast ridge definition even before lifting, successfully revealing minutiae without major background staining. Non-porous surfaces alone were tested, with the best performance noted.

Verma et al. [27] reported morphology-controlled ZnO nanostructures, producing cone-shaped nanoparticles of high aspect ratio (~ 85 nm base width and 225 nm height) by chemical precipitation. This breakthrough enhanced fingerprint powder-surface interactions, enabling successful fingerprint development on 12 different substrates, i.e., glass, metal, and plastics. Nonetheless, difficulty still existed for application on wet surfaces and maintaining high clarity on semi-porous surfaces. Through a water-based chemical precipitation process, ZnO nanoparticles were synthesised by mixing ZnCl₂ and NaOH at 90 °C, centrifugation, washing with ethanol, drying at 60 °C, and pulverising. XRD attested a highly crystalline hexagonal wurtzite phase (~ 25 nm crystallite size), whereas FESEM showed cone-shaped structures with an average diameter of ~ 85 nm and height of ~ 225 nm. ZnO powders were tried on 12 substrates, ranging from glass, copper, ATM cards, steel, wood, and marble, covering non-porous, semi-porous, and porous classes. Sustained physicochemical interactions, specifically hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces with eccrine and sebaceous residues (e.g., amino acids, fatty acids, uric acid), allowed selective ridge adhesion and high-resolution ridge, minutiae, and sweat pore Visualisation. Maximum development was seen on dark, plain non-porous surfaces such as copper and glass, while wood and marble displayed poor to mediocre results owing to low contrast and roughness. Yet, the powder could not be applied on wet surfaces, as too high adhesiveness resulted in smudging. Although photoluminescence was not investigated, the research showed high forensic application of ZnO nanoparticles for dry, clean, non-porous material.

In response to the requirement for field-friendly, practical powders, Bumbrah et al. [28] developed a ZnO nanoparticle-based LFP powder by mixing hydrothermally synthesised nanoparticles with additives like boric acid, talcum powder, and crystal violet. The composite formulation improved adhesion on wet and dry non-porous surfaces and demonstrated outstanding stability for up to 24 h, successfully addressing some of the durability and toxicity issues not specifically addressed in previous research. In comparison to Naik et al., their work focused on affordable formulations that could be applied for practical forensic applications. ZnO nanoparticles were also prepared using a hydrothermal procedure by treating 0.1 M zinc acetate dihydrate with 1 M sodium hydroxide in methanol and subjecting it to autoclaving at 120 °C for 45 min, followed by centrifugation, washing, drying at 60 °C, and grinding. A fingerprint powder was formulated by mixing 2.5 g ZnO with 1 g boric acid and 1 g of talcum powder, 0.45 g barium sulfate, and 0.05 g crystal violet. Characterisation established ~ 12.8 nm spherical nanoparticles with hexagonal structure (XRD), lattice fringes (HRTEM), and surface Zn2⁺ state with adsorbed oxygen (XPS). The method effectively created freshly deposited and aged (up to 24 h) fingerprints on dry and wet non-porous surfaces like aluminium foil, glass, smartphone screens, and laptop touchpads with good ridge clarity and absence of background staining. Depletion experiments indicated good nanoparticle adhesion even to the seventh consecutive impressions. ZnO nanoparticles selectively adsorbed fingerprint ridges through interactions with eccrine and sebaceous constituents such as lipids, amino acids, and uric acid. Even though porous surfaces were not being tested, impressive results on non-porous surfaces established ZnO as appropriate for LFP detection in diverse environments.

Refining the optical properties of ZnO further for forensic imaging applications, Naik et al. [29] synthesised Cu-doped ZnO nanoparticles (Zn1−xCuₓO), engineering the intrinsic characteristics of ZnO toward visible-range photoluminescence. Their Cu-doped samples had tunable violet-blue emission under UV illumination (423 and 478 nm) and greatly improved visibility of fingerprints on glass, plastic, and metal substrates. This development filled the photoluminescent sensitivity gap for improved detection under forensic UV conditions. This emphasised the necessity of surface affinity optimisation. ZnO's intrinsic non-toxicity, broad bandgap (3.37 eV), and intense UV photoluminescence were improved by Cu2⁺ doping, altering structural, optical, and electrochemical characteristics. Nanoparticles were co-precipitated with zinc acetate dihydrate and copper acetate monohydrate in an ethanol–water medium under ph-controlled NaOH (7–8), and then dried (80 °C) and annealed (500 °C). XRD established a hexagonal wurtzite structure, microstrain, and lattice deformation, crystallite sizes varying between 18.57 and 27.75 nm, which are best at x = 0.05. FESEM and TEM showed spherical to quasi-spherical shapes of nanoparticles (20–35 nm) with limited hexagonal facets. Optical characterisation revealed tunable bandgap (3.37–3.41 eV) and improved visible photoluminescence, producing violet (423 nm) and blue (478 nm) radiation caused by defect states (Zni and V_Zn). Raman analysis exhibited oxygen vacancies, whereas cyclic voltammetry reflected increased catalytic activity towards the detection of uric acid, associated with high metabolite interaction in fingerprints. Powder dusting on different substrates (glass, plastic, metal, wood, book covers, and coins), followed by Visualisation with UV light (365 nm) displayed clear high-quality ridge details (Levels I–III) on smooth substrates, but prints on porous materials such as wood and coins were less clear. Improved nanoparticle adhesion to ridges was due to hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions with lipids, amino acids, and metabolites in fingerprint residues. Redirecting attention to environmental sustainability, Chandana et al. [30] investigated green combustion synthesis by employing lemon-extracted L-ascorbic acid as a biofuel for the production of ZnO nanoparticles. Their sustainable ZnO NPs exhibited intense blue photoluminescence upon UV (365 nm) illumination and high ridge detail resolution, such as Level III (involving sweat pores and creases) fingerprint features, such as sweat pores and ridge contours. XRD, SEM, TEM, HR-TEM, and UV–Vis DRS established a hexagonal wurtzite structure, with crystallite sizes from 20.42 nm (ZA) to 44.54 nm (ZC). Morphologies were irregular to quasi-spherical, with agglomeration in ZC. In 365 nm UV light, ZnO NPs—particularly Zn-based—exhibited superior contrast and distinct level-III ridge patterns on both porous (paper) and non-porous (glass, plastic, aluminium, metal) backgrounds. Strong interaction with fingerprint residues took place through electrostatic forces and hydrogen bonding. Depletion experiments verified high sensitivity through fifth impressions, and time-dependent tests evidenced maintenance of fingerprint clarity for 24 h when stored on aluminium foil, outperforming normal powders.

Optical complexity was also enhanced by Flores et al. [31], who contrasted ZnO nanoparticles prepared using chemical precipitation and combustion routes. They showed that the size and shape of nanoparticles could be controlled to maximise multiband photoluminescence, allowing Level I to Level III fingerprint features to be captured with better contrast on diverse non-porous substrates such as aluminium, glass, and stainless steel. Precipitation gave smaller hemispherical NPs (~ 17.2 nm), whereas combustion gave larger, uniform square-like NPs (~ 73.4 nm). XRD, Raman, and SAED established phase-pure hexagonal wurtzite ZnO (JCPDS 36–1451), with UV–Vis absorption indicating optical band gaps of 3.69 eV (precipitation) and 3.59 eV (combustion). Photoluminescence investigations indicated strong UV and green-orange emissions, improving fingerprint contrast under 360 nm UV light. NPs preferentially bound to ridge residue via physisorption and chemisorption with biomolecules such as lipids, amino acids, and salts. Excellent ridge detail (Levels I–III) was observed on non-porous surfaces (aluminium, stainless steel, clear and black glass), where precipitation ZnO worked best on metals and combustion ZnO on black glass. Poor performance was obtained on porous surfaces because of absorption of biomolecules.

Copper oxide nanoparticle

Copper oxide nanoparticles have been employed in various studies due to their high versatility and performance across diverse substrates. Table 6 shows the comparative analysis for various methods where copper oxide is used for LFP development. Renuka et al. [34] synthesised a ZrO₂/CuO (Zirconium dioxide/ Copper oxide) nanocomposite using solution combustion to produce multifunctional ZrO₂/CuO nanocomposite oxide (NCO) via solution combustion using zirconyl nitrate, copper nitrate, and oxalyl dihydrazide (ODH) at 500 °C, in Zr: Cu molar ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 2:1, yielding ZrCu, ZrCu₂, and Zr₂Cu. A Comparative overview of studies using Copper Oxide nanoparticles for latent fingerprint Visualisation

Bhagat et al. [35] developed a biocompatible CuO nanoparticle synthesis using green tea extract as a reducing agent. This method eliminated the need for toxic chemicals and produced spherical nanoparticles that revealed clear second-level fingerprint features such as bifurcations and deltas on non-porous surfaces like glass and steel. The interaction between nanoparticle surface groups and biomolecules was inferred from FTIR spectra, further advancing the understanding of how CuO nanoparticles adhere to fingerprint residues. The synthesis involved boiling 1 g of dried green tea leaves in 50 mL deionised water at 70–80 °C for 20 min to obtain a phytochemical-rich extract, which was then mixed with 40 mL of 0.05 M CuSO₄·5H₂O and ultrasonicated for 8 h. The resulting dark brown-black colloid, indicating nanoparticle formation, was centrifuged to isolate the product. FE-SEM and EDX showed spherical CuO nanoparticles (500–900 nm) with Cu and O, along with biomolecular residues (C, N, S, Si). FTIR confirmed Cu–O, O–H, and CH₂ bonds, indicating high surface reactivity. The nanoparticles were applied via powder dusting to visualise LFPS on non-porous surfaces (glass, steel, butter paper, white paper). The powder adhered strongly to friction ridges, revealing second-level features such as bifurcations, islands, and deltas, without the need for UV or fluorescent light. Fingerprint residue interaction was facilitated by electrostatic and hydrogen bonding between the nanoparticle surface and biomolecules like amino acids, fatty acids, and uric acid, inferred from FTIR spectra. All tested surfaces showed high-resolution prints with no development failures, demonstrating strong substrate versatility and effectiveness on smooth, non-porous surfaces.

Recent developments by Atri et al. [36] focused on CuO nanosheets, which addressed the limitations of traditional fingerprint powders by providing enhanced surface area and high crystallinity. Their hydrothermally synthesised nanosheets demonstrated superior adhesion and high-resolution fingerprint Visualisation on non-absorbent substrates, including glass and aluminium foil, revealing sharp ridge details even on successive impressions. Using a hydrothermal synthesis approach, CuSO₄·5H₂O was reacted with NaOH under autoclaving at 150 °C for 18 h to produce CuO nanosheets with high crystallinity, enhanced surface area (~ 173.4 m2/g), and uniform morphology. PXRD confirmed the monoclinic CuO phase with an average crystallite size of ~ 42 nm, while FESEM showed rectangular nanosheets with high aspect ratios. EDS indicated a near-stoichiometric Cu:O ratio, and FTIR/Raman analyses identified Cu–O bonds and hydroxyl functionalities, suggesting surface-active sites for fingerprint residue interaction. The nanosheets demonstrated superior adhesion and high-resolution fingerprint development on ten non-porous surfaces (glass, aluminium foil, ceramic tile, etc.), revealing sharp, visible ridge details like bifurcations and deltas, particularly on aluminium foil, glass, and CDs. No performance limitations were observed across the substrates, and the nanoparticle–ridge interaction, attributed to van der Waals and electrostatic forces, was supported by FTIR and Raman results. The nanosheets maintained reproducibility, with clear ridge detail even on the seventh impression. Dubey et al. [37] synthesised copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles for LFP Visualisation on non-porous surfaces using a cost-effective precipitation method. The synthesis involved dissolving CuCl₂·2H₂O in distilled water, adding glacial acetic acid, and then adjusting pH with NaOH under ultrasonication, resulting in a colour change from blue to black, indicative of CuO formation. The product was centrifuged, washed, and air-dried. XRD confirmed monoclinic CuO with crystallite sizes averaging ~ 100 nm, and FE-SEM revealed uniform nanorod morphology. FTIR identified Cu–O stretching at 628 cm⁻1, along with surface–OH and CH stretches, indicating surface reactivity. The nanoparticles were evaluated on glass, polycarbonate CDs, and steel using powder dusting. Clear ridge features (bifurcations, ridge endings) were visible on glass and steel, while prints on CDs were fainter due to lower surface moisture. The CuO nanorods interacted with fingerprint residues through electrostatic and hydrogen bonding, particularly with amino acids, lipids, and uric acid. FTIR and FE-SEM confirmed that surface functionalities facilitated this interaction. No background interference or smudging was observed, and porous surfaces were not tested.

Grover et al. [38] developed a silica-coated copper oxide (SiO₂/CuO) nanocomposite for enhanced LFP Visualisation on both fresh and aged non-porous surfaces. The synthesis involved CuO precipitation with copper acetate, CTAB-mediated reduction, and Stöber silica coating, followed by thermal calcination at 400 °C. PXRD confirmed crystalline CuO with a crystallite size of ~ 33.68 nm, and FE-SEM/TEM revealed rod-shaped CuO particles embedded on spherical SiO₂ (506 nm). FTIR analysis showed Cu–O and Si–O–Si stretches at ~ 480 and ~ 530 cm⁻1, indicating strong bonding. The nanocomposite was tested on various non-porous surfaces (glass, aluminium foil, CDs, bakelite, black ceramic) and paper. It showed excellent ridge adherence and clear second-level features like bifurcations and ridge endings, with the best results on aluminium foil and CDs. It was also effective on aged prints (up to 60 days) and chemically altered fingerprints. However, paper and other porous surfaces showed poor development due to inadequate particle retention. FTIR suggested hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between nanoparticle surface groups (–OH, Cu–O, Si–O–Si) and fingerprint residues like lipids and amino acids, but no specific interaction with uric acid was observed. The material exhibited high reproducibility across successive impressions.

Yadav et al. [40] prepared a bio-based core–shell nanocomposite (C-dot@TiO₂), with carbon dots from pomegranate peel coated with a TiO₂ shell. Their study expanded the application of TiO₂ to concentrate on surface chemistry to realise persistent fingerprint sensing, even after 7 Days of water submersion, i.e., 7 Days, demonstrating greater environmental stability compared to the previous methods. Carbon dots (C-dots, 2–5 nm) prepared from pomegranate peel extract by hydrothermal treatment (175 °C, 12 h) were shell-coated with TiO₂ with titanium butoxide at 180 °C for 36 h in ethanol–water solvent. The formed nanocomposite was spherical (~ 500 nm shell thickness) with a clear core–shell structure (HRTEM) and anatase TiO₂ phase (XRD, Rietveld refinement). Elemental purity was verified by EDS (Ti, C, O). Bandgap shift from 3.33 eV (TiO₂) to 3.97 eV (C-dot@TiO₂) evidenced higher optical activity, and FTIR identified surface hydroxyl, carboxyl, amine, and C=C groups responsible for the surface reactivity. On both non-porous (aluminium foil, glass) and porous substrates (mica sheet, textured marbles), high-contrast finger printing Visualisation persisted even after the substrate was wetted for 7 days. SEM imaging witnessed strong ridge fixation, revealing Level I–III features (bifurcation, sweat pores, ridge endings, islands). Surface functional groups permitted hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interaction with fingerprint residues (lipids, amino acids). 80–100% prints on aluminium and glass received a "very good" (score 5) rating according to qualitative scoring, with a minor decline in clarity on mica sheets; porous surfaces were not adequately investigated. Characterisation was performed using XRD, TEM, HRTEM, FTIR, UV–Vis DRS, Raman, and SEM, which established structural, morphological, optical, and chemical characteristics of the nanocomposite.

To promote the TiO2 formation, Babu et al. [41] redirected attention towards environmentally friendly synthesis, synthesising europium-doped TiO₂ (TiO₂:Eu3⁺) hierarchical nanostructures through a green hydrothermal route with EGCG as a biosurfactant. The structures showed intense red luminescence under UV illumination and enabled rapid, non-destructive Visualisation of fine ridge details on curved and non-conventional surfaces, thereby extending TiO₂-based materials further into real-world forensic applications. Titanium(IV) isopropoxide and europium nitrate pentahydrate in ethanol, with EGCG, were hydrothermally treated at 100 °C for 6 h and annealed at 600 °C for 3 h. The flower-like nanostructures (dumbbell, stem, mushroom-like) obtained were pH- and EGCG-dependent. XRD established anatase TiO₂ with Eu3⁺ incorporation, crystallite sizes of 21–32 nm (Scherrer) and 20–38 nm (W–H), and HRTEM showed 0.27–0.31 nm interplanar spacing (101 plane). Photoluminescence exhibited intense red emission at 612 nm (5D₀ → ⁷F₂) at the peak concentration of 5 mol% Eu3⁺, with CIE coordinates and CCT ~ 2509 K. SEM and TEM established uniform morphology, and FTIR detected O–H, C–O, and N–H groups facilitating ridge adherence through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces. Fingerprints developed on plastic, metal, aluminium foil, magazine paper, and curved objects (spray bottles, cans) exhibited distinct Level I–III details (bifurcations, terminations, sweat pores) upon exposure to 254 nm UV light with negligible background noise. Pixel profiling was used to validate selective ridge adhesion, as high Eu3⁺ doping resulted in quenching of the PL quenching. The technique allowed for fast (within 30 s) and non-destructive development on different donors and substrates to make TiO₂:Eu3⁺ nanostructures environmentally friendly, highly luminescent materials for forensic fingerprint analysis.

But until this point, the focus remained largely on physical Visualisation. One of the most significant changes came with Yang et al. [42], who combined electrospray-deposited TiO₂ nanoparticles with matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-FTICR MSI). This groundbreaking dual-mode strategy enabled TiO₂ to both act as a fingerprint enhancer and chemical imaging matrix, not just permitting the development of ridge patterns but also molecule-scale detection of endogenous lipids and foreign residues in the prints. TiO₂ NPs (Sigma-Aldrich) were electrosprayed (4 kV, 50 L/h, 1 mm/s) onto LFPs on non-porous substrates (ITO glass, plastic, metal) and semi-porous ones (tape, varnished wood) before vacuum desiccation. SEM validated uniform TiO₂ deposition with ~ 2 µm aggregates. NPs selectively attached to ridges, retaining Level I–III features (arches, bifurcations, pores) without print damage. As enhancer and MALDI matrix, TiO₂ facilitated high-resolution MSI (9.4 T, 1000 Hz, 50 µm raster), imaging lipophilic compounds such as triacylglycerols (TAGs 52:2, 52:3), DAGs, cholesterol esters, and fatty acids, with MS/MS confirmation (Δ ≤ 5 ppm; HMDB, LIPID MAPS). TiO₂ selectively interacted with glycerides and sterol esters through non-covalent forces, and chemical imaging of fresh and 10-day-old prints indicated little loss of signal. High-contrast development was obtained on all substrates tested, porous materials not being tested. TiO₂ retained TAGs better than conventional DHB matrices by inhibiting fragmentation. Further, novel exogenous molecules (e.g., diethylamino hydroxybenzoyl hexyl benzoate, α-tocopherol acetate) provided chemical distinction among overlapping prints and held potential in donor discrimination under forensic analyses.

Hinners and Lee [43] made these advancements more applicable to field use by taking commercially available forensic-grade TiO₂ powders and reusing them as a dual-purpose reagent acceptable for use with lower-resolution MALDI instrumentation. Their work revealed that TiO₂ could obviate the necessity of additional matrix applications while reducing background spectral noise, rendering high-fidelity chemical imaging possible even without the use of high-end laboratory instrumentation. High surface area and photocatalytic stability TiO₂, which was originally used in forensic applications, was redirected to function as a MALDI matrix to eliminate supplementary matrix steps and reduce spectral contamination over carbon forensic powders. Groomed fingerprints were picked up on non-porous glass slides in a split print technique created with TiO₂ or carbon forensic powder (CFP) and examined through MALDI-LTQ-Orbitrap (355 nm Nd: YAG laser, 30,000 resolution at m/z 400, 100 µm raster, 10 shots/pixel, ~ 2700 pixels/condition). Overlay matrices (CHCA, DHB, silver, sodium-gold) were further tried over TiO₂-developed prints. TiO₂ selectively bound to lipid-rich ridge areas and facilitated ionisation of endogenous substances (triacylglycerols, wax esters, fatty acids, cholesterol) and exogenous residues (pseudoephedrine, procaine, personal care products) with low background noise. Particle size was not specifically determined, but forensic-grade TiO₂, augmented with proprietary additives, performed better than analytical-grade TiO₂. Positive ion mode imaging was most effective; negative mode, though, saw TiO₂ without silver sputtering underperforming due to oxygen-rich surface conditions, albeit with improved detection of fatty acids. Development did not destroy structure, retaining details even when under MALDI laser irradiation. Porous surface testing was not conducted, though TiO₂ retained high-contrast fingerprint development on non-porous glass surfaces, affirming its applicability to fast, field-deployable forensic practice.

Conclusion

This review provides an organized and evidence-based integration of the performance of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles in LFP development on different substrates. Of the materials examined, ZnO, CuO, and TiO₂ nanoparticles exhibited superior performance on non-porous substrates, specifically due to their surface energy, morphology-dependent adhesion, and photoluminescent characteristics. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) were remarkable for having a double application in visual and chemical imaging, with high selectivity towards amino acid-rich residues, demonstrating impressive success even on problematic porous substrates when functionalized or enhanced with silver. Analogously, silica-based composites exhibited strong adhesion, particularly when designed with high surface area and functional groups optimized for electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions. Still, while nanoparticles hold great forensic potential, their use in LFP detection is not without challenges. One of the largest hurdles is the unreliable performance on the porous and textured surfaces of most human skin, with nanoparticle absorption and low ridge contrast being common occurrences. Reproducibility across variable fingerprint age, depletion level, and donor likewise poses issues. Toxicity and environmental hazards associated with nanoparticle exposure, particularly with heavy metal-based or non-biodegradable types, present valid safety concerns. Additionally, there is a dearth of standardized protocols in nanoparticle synthesis, application methods (e.g., dusting vs. sputtering), and imaging conditions, which inhibits practical implementation and regulatory approval. From the forensic practitioner's perspective, material choice needs to be optimized for substrate type and investigation requirements: ZnO and TiO₂ are best suited for high-contrast imaging on non-porous glass and plastic under UV, AuNPs are best suited for dual imaging on porous substrates, and silica or CuO-based composites provide balanced performance on a variety of surfaces with low background interference. The selection of synthesis method (e.g., green synthesis, doping, or hybridization) and application method (e.g., immersion, dusting, or electrospraying) also has direct implications for effectiveness and safety. Although metal and metal oxide nanoparticles offer a revolutionary advantage in LFP detection, making this innovation routine in forensic practice requires more focus on biocompatibility, environmental influence, scalability, and compliance with regulations. Subsequent research must center on creating non-toxic, biodegradable nanoparticles with adjustable surface properties, in addition to universal protocols for cross-laboratory verification. Only through these avenues can nanoparticle-based LFP detection become a standardized and trustworthy tool in the forensic sciences.
