Naturally self-reactive B cells are poised to cross the selection barrier into autoimmune germinal centers
Authors: Danni Yi-Dan Zhu, Maya Sangesland, Vintus Okonkwo, Zhenrui Zhang, Victoria Rosado, Larance Ronsard, Caitlin McCarthy, Caroline Alexander, Ralston M. Barnes, Daniel Rohrer, Nils Lonberg, Debashree Tagore, Musie Ghebremichael, Carlos Castrillon, Joshua M. Akey, Michael C. Carroll, Daniel Lingwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYRoughly 20% of circulating B cells react with self-antigens. An open question is whether germline autoreactivities expand when immune tolerance is breached. To test this, we supplied the 564Igi mouse model of spontaneous autoreactive germinal centers (GCs) with naive B cells that were polyclonal with human-like CDRH3 diversity but were genetically constrained to one of two alleles of the human antibody VH gene IGHV1-69. This polymorphism is skewed across global ethnicities, encoding either F54 or L54 in the CDRH2 loop, with L54 endowing autoreactive B cell receptors (BCRs). L54 B cells were selectively retained within 564Igi mice, leading to their incorporation into autoimmune GCs. This advantage was lost within wild-type C57Bl/6. We also demonstrate human-like L54 IGHV1-69 usage within the geographic variation of ancestral Neanderthals and Denisovans. Collectively, our results suggest that the self-reactive B cell pool is ancestral and is positioned for expansion by autoimmune environments.

INTRODUCTION

B cells targeting self-antigens are removed by a series of tolerance checkpoints that prevent cellular activation and maturation of their autoreactive B cell receptors (BCRs) into circulating autoantibodies.1-4 During development within the bone marrow, central tolerance limits the expansion of self-reactive B cells through the combination of pathways: clonal deletion to remove autoreactive lineages; receptor editing, where further immunoglobulin variable gene rearrangement is employed to alter BCR antigen specificity; and B cell anergy, a collection of functional and epigenetic changes imposed to lower self-reactive BCR responsivity.3,5-13 Peripheral tolerance occurs outside the bone marrow and comprises functionally analogous pathways that also delete, suppress, and/or alter BCR antigen specificities of autoreactive B cell clones.6,12-15 It is estimated that ~20% of circulating B lymphocytes remain self-reactive following central and peripheral tolerance checkpoints.6,16 While the remaining autoreactive BCRs can be in part “contained” by enrichment within specialized anergic B cell subsets, including mouse T3 B cells17,18 or IgD+/IgM− B cells (BND) within human peripheral blood,6,19,20 the contribution of naturally occurring/persisting self-reactive BCRs to the development of autoimmune diseases, arising when tolerance fails or is broken,21-23 remains an open question.

We previously showed that L54 IGHV1–69, a common polymorphism in the human antibody VH gene IGHV1-69, deterministically imparts germline BCR autoreactivity within the HC2 transgenic mouse system, in which polyclonal B cell clones develop with human-like CDRH3 diversity but are fully constrained to one user-defined IGHV gene.24 Upon comparing IGHV1-69*09 and IGHV1-69*01, commonly used human IGHV1-69 alleles encoding for L54 or F54 in the CDRH2 apex, respectively,25,26 we found that L54 (but not F54) usage endowed polyreactive and autoreactive germline B cells that underwent anergic remodeling to limit self-antigen reactivity.24 Also identified here was repertoire-wide BCR receptor editing of L54 (but not F54) B cells that also potentiates the “rescue” of their expandability from the germline B cell pool.24

In humans, L54 vs. F54 IGHV1-69 usage is skewed ethnically and geographically, the causes for which are unknown.27 Both L54 and F54 IGHV1-69 hardcode for human broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) targeting the highly conserved central stem epitope displayed by the hemagglutinin (HA) spike protein of influenza A viruses24,28,29; however, unlike F54 bnAbs, L54 usage is not enriched within memory B cells targeting the central stem site, and L54 carriers exhibit lower serum titers of such bnAbs.25,27,30-36 Using our transgenic mice as vaccine models, we demonstrated that F54 and L54 IGHV1-69 provide germline-encoded substrate for targeting the bnAb site,28,29 but that immune tolerance limits vaccine expansion of L54 bnAbs within the memory B cell pool.24 In the current study, we addressed whether a self-reactive germline B cell pool, operationally provided by L54 IGHV1-69 naive B cells from HC2 mice, could be promoted to expand, as judged by the method we developed for tracking B cell recruitment into spontaneous autoreactive GC centers (GCs).37,38

Here, we adoptively transferred B cells from the naive polyclonal pools of heterozygous L54/F54 IGHV1-69 HC2 genotypes to 564Igi mice, a model of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).37-42 The 564Igi mouse was generated by knockin of BCR from an autoreactive B cell clone targeting ribonuclear complexes (e.g., RNP41,42) and forms spontaneous autoreactive B cell GCs in a TLR7-dependent manner.37-42 For IGHV1-69 B cells, we find that polyclonal L54 (but not F54) BCRs from HC2 mice harbor VH gene-encoded specificity to the RNP autoantigen, and that conversion to the F54 background attenuates this autoreactivity in vitro. Similarly, L54 IGHV1-69 B cells were preferentially retained following transfer into the 564Igi mouse, favoring their entry into the spontaneous autoreactive GCs that form within this model. This advantage was eliminated in the WT C57Bl/6 “non-autoimmune” background, potentiating autoimmune environments as expanders of the naturally autoreactive B cell pool. We further demonstrate that skewed usage of the L54 IGHV1-69 allele across various ethnic groups correlated with geographically biased IGHV1-69 polymorphism in the genomes of Neanderthals and Denisovans, archaic hominins. Together, our data suggest that naturally autoreactive B cells are an ancient ancestral feature, and that functionally, this B cell pool is poised for incorporation into autoimmune GCs once tolerance is broken.

RESULTS

Naive L54 B cells are selectively retained within 564Igi, advantaging these cells for crossing the selection barrier into autoimmune GCs

Unlike F54 IGHV1-69, the L54 variant allele provides a deterministic source of autoreactivity to the polyclonal germline B cell pool (unconstrained CDRH3 diversity) that activates immune tolerance within the HC2 mouse system24 (Figures 1A and 1B). However, L54 usage also includes repertoire-scale BCR editing,24 a pathway that can restore expandability of B cells that contain autoreactive features,43-45 and which we report here as an increase in the lambda/kappa light chain ratios of L54 B cells within the bone marrow and spleen (Figure 1B). To then compare L54 vs. F54 polymorphism as a predisposition for expanding polyclonal germline B cell pools within autoimmune 564Igi mice (CD45.1+/+), we adoptively transferred resting B cells from the heterozygous L54+/− /F54+/− HC2 genotype (CD45.2+/+), where the naive B cells are polyclonal (unconstrained human-like CDRH3 diversity) but equally divided for usage of L54 or F54 IGHV1–6924,28 (Figures 1C and 1D). This 1:1 ratio of L54/F54 in the input B cells was confirmed by G6 staining, an anti-idiotypic antibody specific to F54 IGHV1–6925,46 (Figure 1C). CD45.1+/− /CD45.2+/− B cells from wild-type (WT) C57Bl/6 mice were co-transferred as an internal control. The transfers were performed by intravenous injection, after which we evaluated downstream B cell development in the 564Igi mouse spleen 7 days post-transfer; a sampling time frame and procedure we have established for adoptive transfer of resting B cells to the 564Igi mouse37,38 (Figures 1D and 1E). We find that despite equivalent L54 and F54 B cell input, L54 B cells dominate over their F54 counterparts for retention in the model, including their participation in the autoimmune GCs and in orthogonal B cell development pathways, such as plasmablast formation (Figures 1F, S1A, S1B, and S2). When counts of L54 and F54 GC B cells, plasmablasts, and plasma cells were normalized to the respective L54 and F54 total donor B cells, no L54 dominance was observed, indicating that the advantage for L54 B cells likely occurs at the level of follicular entry and retention, and no further advantage is conferred upon GC commitment and antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) differentiation (Figure 1G). Notably, the difference in entry/retention between F54 and L54 and participation in autoimmune GCs (and orthogonal plasmablast development) was eliminated if the transfer was performed in WT C57Bl/6 mice, in which tolerance is not breached (CD45.1+/+) (Figures 1H, 1I, and S3).

L54 vs. F54 B cells within autoimmune GCs of 564Igi mice

To further resolve the advantage for L54 B cells crossing the selection barrier to autoimmune GCs, we performed immunofluorescence and confocal imaging of the recipient 564Igi spleens at day 7 post-transfer (Figures 2A-2C, S4, and S5). Using a custom-trained model for single-cell segmentation and annotation of follicular B cells and GC B cells, we found that the F54 B cells, marked by G6 reactivity, were deriched from these B cell compartments relative to their L54 counterparts (Figures 2B, 2C, S4, and S5). Similarly, the application of a custom-trained model for single-cell segmentation and annotation of total CD138+ ASCs also revealed orthogonal F54 B cell derichment with this B cell subset (Figures 3A-3C, S4, and S5).

L54 IGHV1-69 usage encodes germline BCR reactivity to RNP autoantigen

Autoreactivity of the 564Igi mice is driven by nuclear-containing antigens such as RNP, and successful engraftment and differentiation of WT B cells in the 564Igi recipients depend on signaling through the double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) sensor TLR7.37,38 Thus, we evaluated RNP reactivity to a panel of clonally diverse germline IGHV1-69 mAbs (IgG format) from the B cell repertoires of HC2 mice (n = 11 independent L54 clones, and n = 9 independent F54 clones, Figure 4) and of humans (n = 14 independent L54 clones and n = 15 independent F54 clones, Figure S6) that we previously used to demonstrate L54 encoded polyreactive affinity to diverse antigens and autoantigens24 (see also STAR Methods for list mAb names). The L54 mAbs also showed elevated reactivity to RNP in both HC2 mice (Figure 4A) and humans (Figure S6A). For the five highest reacting L54 clones (n = 5 from HC2 mice and n = 5 from humans), we evaluated VH-encoded binding to RNP by sequentially converting these L54 germline mAbs into the F54 IGHV1-69*01 background (L54F, L54F/R49G, then L54F/R49G/I56T within CDRH2).24 This VH sequence conversion attenuated RNP binding by L54 mAbs from both HC2 mice (Figures 4B and 4C) and humans (Figures S6B and S6C), demonstrating a VH-encoded germline antibody paratope for this antigen that was preserved across otherwise clonally diverse IGHV1-69 BCRs.

L54 B cells compete with the WT C57Bl/6 lineages recruited to the autoreactive GCs

Co-transfer of L54+/− /F54+/− /CD45.2+/+ B cells with CD45.1+/− / CD45.2+/− WT C57Bl/6 B cells into CD45.1+/+ 564Igi mice also shows that WT B cells are better retained throughout B cell differentiation (Figures 5A, 5B, S7A, and S7B), consistent with competition with the naturally autoreactive polyclonal pool within naive WT C57Bl/6 B cells.37,47 However, this difference in retention is eliminated if L54+/+/CD45.2+/+ B cells are used in an otherwise identical co-transfer composition (Figures 5C, 5D, S7D, and S7E). Hence, an increase in L54 copy number by homozygosity increases the competitive fitness of HC2 cells in this system.

Origins of IGHV1-69 allelic usage leading to germline encoded autoreactivity

We previously reported L54 allelic homozygosity as ~13% globally,36,48 but further analysis of the 1000 Genomes Project data reveals elevated L54 homozygosity in South Asians.24,27,48 While most humans express at least one copy of F54 IGHV1-69,24,27,48 we confirmed the skewing for L54/L54 homozygosity within South Asians within the UK Biobank (>100,000 genomes49) (Figure 6A). Furthermore, we calculated a statistic known as FST,50 to more quantitatively measure the F54 and L54 allele frequency differences in British/European, African, and South Asian populations from the UK Biobank. FST values vary between zero and one (0 ≤ FST ≤ 1), with FST = 0 when all populations have the same allele frequencies and FST = 1 when populations are fixed for alternative alleles. The FST value for alleles at the IGHV1-69 locus is 0.101, which is consistent with the average genome-wide FST of ~0.110.51 Note that an FST of 0.101 indicates that 10.1% of the total genetic variation at the IGHV1-69 locus is due to differences between populations.50 We also calculated pairwise FST between the British/European, African, and South Asian samples in the UK Biobank. The FST between British and African individuals is 0.0005, indicating that the frequencies of the F54 and L54 alleles and genotypes are very similar in these populations (Figure 6A). In contrast, the FST between British and South Asian individuals and African and South Asian individuals was 0.144 and 0.160, respectively, indicating that the levels of population structure were commensurate with the genome-wide average. Thus, the F54 and L54 allele and genotype frequencies vary modestly among populations, and surprisingly, global patterns of variation are driven by the South Asian population.

To further evaluate the history of IGHV1-69 polymorphism, we examined F54 vs. L54 allelic usage in the four archaic hominin genomes sequenced to high coverage.52-55 We find that the Chagyrskaya Neanderthal, Altai Neanderthal, and Altai Denisovan individuals from Asia are F54/L54 heterozygotes, but the European Vindija Neanderthal, which is genetically closer to humans,53 was homozygous F54/F54 (Figure 6B). Since Neanderthals and Denisovans exchanged genetic material with modern humans,52-58 these data are consistent with the F54 allele predominating in Europe for hundreds of thousands of years and an elevated use of L54 in South Asians.

DISCUSSION

Our study suggests that autoreactive B cells, which form a substantive proportion of the B lymphocytes in circulation,5-11,14,19 are advantaged for retention, and as a consequence, downstream recruitment into autoimmune GCs when B cell tolerance is broken. This points to positive feedback as a potential mechanism for advancing B cell autoreactivity into autoimmunity when tolerogenic safeguards regulating self-antigen responsiveness are disrupted.

This environmental effect was illustrated by the loss of L54 B cell selection when the adoptive transfers were performed in WT C57Bl/6 mice. Except for a minor difference in CDRH2, L54 vs. F54 B cells are otherwise genetically identical,24,28 further suggesting that autoimmune L54 selection is an antigen-driven effect. The CDRH2 loop responsible for L54 IGHV1-69 endowed BCR autoreactivity is the most hydrophobic antigen-binding loop encoded within the human B cell repertoire.59 Subtle conversion to the F54 IGHV1-69 form can eliminate germline autoreactivity, consistent with our previous findings,24 and highlight a fine line between imparting germline utility for recognizing foreign vs. self-antigen. Within autoimmune GCs, epitope spreading is a known feedforward pathway of autoimmune B cell development,40,60-62 and we could expect that downstream somatic hypermutation (SHM) and affinity selection both strengthen and further diversify autoreactive L54 BCR substrate specificity.

We previously described L54 B cells as functionally anergic due to lowered surface IgM upon exiting the bone marrow during B cell development.24 Anergic B cells harbor a range of phenotypes but classically are thought to have a significantly shorter half-lives,3,5-13 and this is consistent with our observation of lowered L54 B cell competitive fitness relative to co-transferred WT B cells, both within WT and autoimmune environments. However, increased L54 copy number appears to overcome this deficit in the autoimmune environment, potentially because L54 usage also leads to repertoire-scaled BCR receptor editing, which would enable continued B cell responsivity, including to autoantigen. Our suggestion is that this combination provides substrate for retention and downstream entry to autoimmune GCs. We recently reported a similar observation, where B cells from two novel immunoglobulin transgenic strains that express BCRs originated from WT clones activated within WT:564Igi bone marrow chimeras were recruited into 564Igi GCs and produced autoantibodies, concomitant with potent receptor editing and the absence of overt autoimmunity in naive mice.63 Thus, 564Igi provides an autoimmune niche that broadly supports activation of tolerized BCR clones.

The evolutionary history surrounding the IGHV1-69 allelic polymorphism remains unclear. Why would geographically disparate human populations skew for autoreactive vs. non-reactive forms of antibodies? The similarity in geographic variation between human and Neanderthal IGHV1-69 alleles is striking and could reflect similar environmental pressures that emphasize L54 over F54 (or vice versa). Although it is possible that the allelic variation at this locus arose in one hominin lineage and then spread through the admixture, the presence of both alleles in modern humans, Neanderthals, and Denisovans suggests that the L54 variant arose in the population ancestral to these three groups (~600 kya; where kya = thousand years ago). L54 and F54 IGHV1-69 homologs are seen within non-human primates,64,65 potentiating both variants as ancestral alleles. We have previously proposed an evolutionary framework for hardcoded specificities and germline utility within the B cell repertoire: while originally shaped by a primary adaptation to combat infectious disease, the BCR products may also harbor cross-reactivities that co-emerge as evolutionary spandrels or “unplanned” secondary consequences of the primary adaptation.66,67 In this context, L54 may enable rapid complementarity to some microbial antigens, and the “unplanned” accompanying autoreactivity is accommodated as collateral “damage.”

Limitations of the study

Entry and then survival of L54 GC B cells will require proliferation68,69; however, outside the GC, skewing of the transferred cell population toward L54 in the B220+/CD19+ population likely also involves increased survival, and our study does not resolve these distinctions. Being polyclonal (diverse LC and CDRH3), L54 B cells also had varying degrees of RNP reactivity, and this is consistent with a partial advantage over F54 B cells in the 564Igi model (~60:40). Finally, we note that the 564Igi mice provide only one environment, and the autoreactive landscape of the B cell populations could alter if placed in different autoimmune environments.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

B6.1/.2 mice were generated by crossing C57BL/6J (CD45.2) and B6.SJL (CD45.1), both of which were obtained from Jackson Laboratories and maintained in-house. 564Igi mice on a C57BL/6 background were originally provided by Dr. Theresa Imanishi-Kari (Tufts University) and crossed with B6.SJL for multiple generations to generate the CD45.1+/+ 564Igi mice.39-42 In HC2 mice, B cell development is restricted to use of single human VH genes, but diverse human DH and JH segments, generating a polyclonal CDRH3 repertoire that is similar to humans.24,28,29,66,70-73 We deployed previously CD45.2+/+ HC2 genotypes: IGHV1-69*01 (F54) or IGHV1-69*09 (L54) usage as F54+/+, L54+/+, and L54+/− /F54 ± .24,28,29,72 In all cases, antibody LC input was from the mouse repertoire.24,28,29,66,70-73 The HC2 mice were a gift to D.L. from Bristol-Myers Squibb (Redwood City, CA) and their use requires an MTA with BMS. These animals were maintained within Ragone Institute’s HPPF barrier facility and maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle with a standard chow diet. In experiments involving B cell adoptive transfer, sex-based rejection of the transplant74-76 was avoided by matched female transplants into female recipients.

Recombinant antibodies

The monoclonal antibody, G6, which is specific for the F54 position in IGHV1-69 antibodies,46 was generated as recombinant IgG.24,25 In addition, we generated recombinant IgG versions of germline L54 and F54 IGHV1-69 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), previously isolated from the antigen naive IgM+/IgD+ B cell repertoires of HC2 mice and humans24 (HC2 L54 clones: L69_70; L69_59; L69_71; L69_4; L69_8; L69_9; L69_25; L69_36; L69_80; L69_88; L69_27; HC2 F54 clones: F69_12; F69_19; F69_47; F69_60; F69_74; F69_81; F69_79; F69_34; F69_37; human L54 clones: L69_h47; L69_h124; L69_h128; L69_h164; L69_h149; L69_25; L69_h14; L69_h1; L69_h3; L69_h5; L69_h7; L69_h33; L69_h44; L69_h127; human F54 clones: F69_h105; F69_h110; F69_h117; F69_h33; F69_h41; F69_h107; F69_h127; F69_h130; F69_h133; F69_h147; F69_h163; F69_h01;F69_h02; F69_h03; F69_h20).24 For some of these L54 clones, we generated recombinant IgG versions bearing sequential conversion to the F54 IGHV1-69*01 background (L54F, L54F/R49G, then L54F/R49G/I56T within CDRH2). In all cases, plasmids expressing antibodies as human IgG1 heavy + light chain28,35 were transfected into Expi293 cells using a commercial transfection kit. The cells were grown in Expi293 expression medium for six days and the culture supernatants was incubated with Protein G Sepharose resin. The IgG was directly eluted with low pH elution buffer into 1M Tris, pH 8.0 and then purified and buffer exchanged into PBS using size exclusion chromatography (SEC) to confirm IgG size and assembly (Superdex 200 10/300 column). For purified G6, the IgG was labeled with Alexa 488 or Alexa 647 using commercial labeling kits, re-purified by SEC and then snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage. G6-A488 and G6-A647 was used as a flow cytometry tool to monitor the proportion of F54 IGHV1-69 to L54 IGHV1-69 B cells as before.24,25

Flow cytometry

Spleens were harvested into ice-cold MACS buffer (0.5% bovine serum albumin/BSA, Fisher Scientific, 2mM EDTA in 1× PBS) and mechanically dissociated using pestles in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. Samples were then filtered through 70 μm cell strainers (Fisher Scientific) and centrifuged at 1400 rpm at 4°C for 5min. LN cell pellets were resuspended in MACS buffer. Spleen cell pellets were resuspended in RBC lysis buffer (155 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM NaHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA), incubated for 5 min on ice and centrifuged as before. Splenocyte pellets were washed once with MACS buffer, centrifuged and resuspended in MACS buffer. Single-cell suspensions were added to 96-well round-bottom plates, centrifuged and resuspended in 50 μL antibody staining mix (1/200-1/400 diluted fluorophore-conjugated antibodies and 1/1000 diluted Viability dye Fixable live/dead stain Efluor 780). Monoclonal antibodies used for analysis include: rat anti-mouse CD45R/B220-Brilliant Violet 605; rat anti-mouse CD19-Brilliant Violet 650; rat anti-mouse/human GL7-Alexa Fluor 488, -Pacific Blue; rat anti-mouse CD38-APC, mouse anti-mouse CD45.1-PE, -PerCP/Cyanine5.5; mouse anti-mouse CD45.2-PerCP/Cyanine5.5; rat anti-mouse CD138-Brilliant Violet 510; rat anti-mouse IgD-PE/Cyanine7, -Pacific Blue; G6-Alexa Fluor 488, -Alexa Fluor 647; CD3 BV510; F4.80 BV510; GR-1 BV510; CD19 APC-eFluor780; Igλ1-3 – FITC; and IgK – PE. Samples were incubated for 30 min at 4°C, followed by addition of 150 μL of MACS buffer. Plates were subsequently centrifuged at 1400 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was flicked out of the plates. The samples were then fixed for 20 min at room temperature in the dark with 100 μL of fixation buffer. For surface staining, 100 μL of MACS buffer was added to each well and centrifuged 1400 rpm for 5min. The supernatant was removed, and cell pellets were resuspended with 150 μL of MACS buffer ready for acquisition. Sample acquisition was performed on a 4-Laser 17-color LSR II analyzer with HTS equipped with 405 nm, 512 355nm, 594nm, and 488nm lasers, or a 5-Laser 17-color LSR II SORP analyzer with HTS equipped with 355nm, 405nm, 488nm, 561nm and 640nm lasers, depending on the experiment. Flow cytometry data analyses were performed with FlowJo software.

B cell purification and adoptive transfer

Donor resting B cells were enriched with B Cell Isolation Kit according to manufacturer protocols. Briefly, total splenocytes single cell suspensions from whole spleen were prepared as described above. After equilibration to desired concentrations, splenocyte single cell suspension in MACS buffer was consecutively incubated for 10 min with biotinylated antibody cocktail and 10 min with streptavidin magnetic beads, both on ice. Cells were then transferred into sterile 5 mL culture tubes and pre-enriched with EasySep Cell Separation Magnets for 5 min at room temperature. Cell supernatants were transferred to MACS cell separation LS columns fixed on magnet and collected into MACS buffer. 5 mL culture tubes containing residual cells after pre-enrichment were washed 2× by with 1-3mL of MACS buffer while attached to magnet, and supernatants were added to same LS column. Cells were then centrifuged, resuspended in 0.5–1 mL of MACS buffer and counted. The desired number of cells were retrieved, pelleted and resuspended in sterile HBSS buffer with 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 2% FBS, ready for transfer. Adoptive transfer was performed by single-eye retro-orbital injection at 100 μL volume with 0.5 mL Lo-Dose Insulin Syringes with 29G needle.37,38,47,77 The composition of the transferred cells was either: L54+/− /F54+/− /CD45.2+/+ + CD45.1+/− /CD45.2+/− WT C57Bl/6 or L54+/+/CD45.2+/+ + CD45.1+/− /CD45.2+/− WT C57Bl/6; where co-transfer of CD45.1+/− /CD45.2+/− B cells provided an internal control for transfer and engraftment at 7 days post injection (1–1.25 × 107 donor B cells were transferred per genotype).37,47 In some cases, CD45.1+/+ WT C57Bl/6 mice were the recipients.

Immunofluorescence confocal microscopy and image analysis

Fresh spleens were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound after harvest, and rapidly frozen on dry ice. Tissue blocks were stored at −80°C until ready for cryosection. 14 μm tissue cryosections were mounted on SuperFrost+ slides (Fisher Scientific) and stored at −80°C until ready for staining or stained immediately. For immunofluorescence staining with surface and intracellular markers, slides with mounted cryosections were dried at room temperature for 20 min and each section on the slide was outlined with hydrophobic barrier and fixed with 4% PFA at RT for 10 min. After rinsing with PBS, slides were then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton in 1× PBS for 3–5 min at RT, washed 3 times with PBS, 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST) and incubated for 1 h in blocking buffer (3% BSA, 5% fetal calf serum/FCS in 1× PBS). This was followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated primary antibody mixture in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. Monoclonal antibodies used for analysis include: mouse anti-mouse CD45.2-PE, -APC; rat anti-mouse CD138-PE; rat anti-mouse/human GL7-Pacific Blue; mouse anti-mouse CD45.1-PE; mouse anti-mouse CD45.1-Brilliant Violet 421; G6-Alexa Fluor 488, -Alexa Fluor 647; and B220-PE, -APC. The slides were then washed 3 times with PBST, mounted in Fluoro-Gel (Electron Microscopy Sciences), applied with coverslips, and sealed with clear nail polish. Imaging was performed using an Olympus FV3000R resonant scanning confocal microscope equipped with 4 laser lines (405, 488, 514, and 633 nm) and ultra-sensitive GaAsP detectors with full spectral imaging. Images were acquired with 30× silicone oil objective and frames stitched with the Olympus CellSens software.

For confocal image analysis, single cell segmentation was performed in batch with Cellpose using the B220, GL7, or CD138 channels with custom trained models for follicular B cell, GC B cell, or ASC analyses, respectively (Figure S5). Segmentation models were trained on corresponding image stacks to be analyzed, based on the cyto3 initial model, at learning rate of 0.1, weight decay of 0.0001, and 500 epochs. Raw, merged channel tiff images were denoised and segmented in batch with custom CLI commands, and segmentation masks were exported as.png files. Based on segmentation masks created, single cell intensities of the CD45.1, CD45.2, and G6 channels were then measured using the Scikit-image RegionProps function. Cellular annotation of the single cell mask was performed in R with custom designed script, where identities of B6, 564Igi, L54 IGHV1-69 or F54 IGHV1-69 were assigned to each cell based on CD45.1, CD45.2, and G6 intensities and cell type frequencies derived from flow cytometry analyses. Cell type identities were converted into discrete color pixel values. In the final annotated image masks, cells of each identity were enumerated for specific follicles, GCs, or fields of view (ASCs) for quantification.

Germline L54 vs. F54 antibody reactivity to RNP

Germline L54 and F54 IGHV1-69 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), previously isolated from the antigen naive IgM/IgD B cell repertoires of HC2 mice and humans24 were tested in ELISA as recombinant IgG for reactivity to recombinant ribonucleoprotein (RNP). The VH encoded paratopes for L54 reactive clones were further evaluated within the ELISA assay by sequential conversion of the germline L54-IGHV1-69*09 sequence into its F54-IGHV1-69*01 (incorporation of L54F, L54F/R49G, then L54F/R49G/I56T within CDRH2).24 In all cases, Nunc MaxiSorp plates (96 well) were coated at 4°C overnight at 200ng per well with RNP and then washed with PBS and blocked in PBS containing 10% FBS for 1 h. The primary and secondary antibodies were incubated in PBS with 1% FBS. Plates were again washed in PBS and then developed using TMB solution and quenched with 1N sulfuric acid. Samples were read at 450nm using the Teacan Infinite m1000 Pro microplate absorbance reader (Mannedorf, Switzerland).

L54 IGHV1-69 and F54 allelic frequencies within the UK biobank

We accessed whole genome sequence data of 150,119 individuals from the UK BioBank (UKB),49 for the L54/F54 SNP at coordinates rs5589101024,27 (represented in GRCh38 coordinates as chr14:106714815:SG in the UKB whole genome sequence dataset). Our analysis focused on three cohorts within this dataset: British/European; African; and South Asian. We calculated FST as the variance in allele frequencies across populations as described on pages 166–167 in Weir 1996.78

L54 vs. F54 polymorphism in Neanderthal and Denisovan IGHV1-69

The genotype of IGHV1-69 at the locus of interest was inferred in archaic hominins using high-coverage whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from three Neanderthals and one Denisovan52-55 Genotype data in variant call format (VCF) was obtained from publicly available repositories: Altai, Vindija, and Denisovan data were downloaded from http://cdna.eva.mpg.de/neandertal/Vindija/VCF/, while Chagyrskaya data were retrieved from http://cdna.eva.mpg.de/neandertal/Chagyrskaya/VCF/. The genotypes of these four archaic hominins were extracted using VCFtools.79 Among the three Neanderthals, the Altai52 and Chagyrskaya54 individuals were heterozygous, whereas the Vindija Neanderthal53 was homozygous for F54. The Denisovan individual55 was also found to be heterozygous.
