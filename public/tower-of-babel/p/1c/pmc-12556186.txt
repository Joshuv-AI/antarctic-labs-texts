Association between cMIND diet adherence and frailty among Chinese older adults: A 10-year longitudinal study
Authors: Lin Yang, Mengying Li, Jing Shu, Lizheng Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoth cognitive impairment and diet are significant factors associated with frailty, however, the association between the Chinese Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (cMIND) diet and frailty remains unclear.MethodsThis longitudinal study analyzed data from 1,943 adults aged ≥65 in the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) spanning 2008–2018. Adherence to the cMIND diet was assessed using a food frequency questionnaire, whereas frailty status was determined using the frailty index. The cMIND dietary scores were categorized into three groups based on tertiles: (T1:0−4; T2:4.5–5.5; T3:6–12). The association between cMIND adherence and frailty incidence was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, while the dose-response relationship was examined with restricted cubic splines (with knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles).ResultsThe mean age was 74.9 ± 7.3 years; 50.5% were female. The restricted cubic spline model revealed a significant nonlinear association between baseline cMIND dietary scores and frailty risk (nonlinear p < 0.05). Subsequently, the Cox proportional hazards model showed that, after adjusting for covariates, participants in the highest tertile of cMIND scores exhibited a 16% reduction in frailty risk compared to those in the lowest tertile (HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72−0.97, p = 0.022). However, subgroup analyses revealed that the association varied according to baseline cognitive function. A significant inverse association was present in those with normal cognition (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.86−0.96) but absent in those with cognitive impairment (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.93–1.09; interaction p = 0.037).ConclusionHigher adherence to the cMIND diet is associated with a lower risk of frailty among older adults in China, specifically in those with normal cognitive function at baseline.Keywords: cMIND, Older adults, Frailty, CLHLS, Cohort study

Introduction

China is facing a significant population aging challenge. Currently, individuals aged 65 and above account for 14% of the total population, and this figure is projected to reach 400 million by 2050. This trend will place China among the countries with the most severe aging populations globally [1,2]. With increasing aging, frailty has become a global public health challenge [3]. Frailty is a non-specific age-related syndrome caused by multiple factors, characterized by the decline in physiological reserves of multiple organ systems with advancing age and a reduction in stress resistance [4,5]. Frailty development is influenced by a range of environmental, behavioral, and nutritional factors [6,7]. Studies have shown that different dietary patterns are associated with frailty [[8], [9], [10]]. The Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet is an emerging dietary pattern that combines the Mediterranean diet with the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, emphasizing unprocessed foods rich in antioxidant nutrients that can reduce oxidative stress and inflammation [11,12]. The MIND promotes the consumption of fresh and abundant fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds, along with moderate consumption of dairy products, poultry, and fish, while limiting the intake of red meat and sweets. This pattern can reduce the risk of hypertension, protect cardiovascular health, and prevent cardiovascular disease [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. The Chinese Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (cMIND) was culturally adjusted to form a dietary pattern that conforms to the dietary habits of the Chinese people. The cMIND diet can significantly reduce the risk of cognitive impairment in older adults, which is a known risk factor for frailty [17,18]. Although previous studies using data from the China Longitudinal Health and Longevity Survey (CLHLS) have examined associations between general plant-based dietary patterns and frailty [8], as well as between the cMIND diet and cognitive impairment or depression [11,17], the specific association between the cMIND dietary pattern and frailty risk has not yet been investigated in longitudinal studies. To date, no domestic or international studies have directly demonstrated the longitudinal association between cMIND dietary adherence and incident frailty. Therefore, this study uses data from the China Longitudinal Health and Longevity Survey (CLHLS) to explicitly examine the longitudinal association between adherence to the culturally adapted cMIND diet and frailty risk in Chinese older adults.

Methods

Participants

The data for this study were drawn from the CLHLS, a nationwide longitudinal survey of adults aged 65 years and older across 23 provinces that has included multiple follow-up waves since 1998. This study utilised data from 2008 to 2018, comprising four survey waves conducted in 2008, 2011, 2014, and 2018. All participants enrolled voluntarily after receiving full information about the study’s objectives, content, and rights, and provided written informed consent. The 2008 CLHLS cohort comprised 16,954 participants. This study excluded participants aged under 65 and over 105 years old, as these age groups lack sufficient weighting in the CLHLS. The sampling and weighting strategy of the CLHLS was not designed for these extreme age cohorts, and data reliability may be compromised. Moreover, individuals lacking cMIND and frailty-related data were excluded, as were participants exhibiting frailty at baseline. Ultimately, 1,943 participants were included in the study, as shown in Fig. 1.

Assessment of frailty

This study employed the Frailty Index (FI) scale developed by Gu et al. [19], which comprises 39 items covering chronic diseases (such as hypertension and diabetes), sensory functions (vision and hearing), physical functions (walking and picking up objects), and mental health dimensions. Each item is a binary variable, entries that meet the description received 1 point. If a participant has reported two or more serious illnesses in the past two years, they receive a score of 2. The total FI score is calculated using the following formula: FI = (sum of all deficit scores) / 39. A higher FI value indicates a more severe degree of frailty, with an FI > 0.21 representing frailty [20], as shown in Supplement Table S1.

Assessment of cMIND

Participants completed a qualitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) at baseline and follow-up periods, which assessed consumption frequency without specifying portion sizes. The cMIND dietary scoring system, developed by Huang et al. [17] and has been adapted for the Chinese population. The scoring system comprises 12 assessment dimensions: staple food variety and intake, fresh vegetables, fresh fruits, mushrooms/seaweed, cooking oil, fish, legumes, nuts, garlic, tea, and sugar/candy. Participants were asked about their current dietary habits, without specifying a timeframe such as the past year. Frequency categories included: less than twice weekly, 3–5 times weekly, more than 6 times weekly, less than once monthly, 1–3 times monthly, and more than once weekly. Different foods corresponded to different consumption frequencies, with total scores ranging from 0 to 12 points. Main staple food diversity, staple food proportion, and cooking oil usage were scored as 0 or 1 point. The remaining nine items were scored as 0, 0.5, or 1 point. Higher scores indicate greater compliance, as shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Assessment of cognitive ability

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used to assess cognitive abililty. This scale consists of 30 questions covering areas such as memory, recall, attention, calculation, and visuospatial skill. Each correct answer was awarded 1 point, and no points were awarded for incorrect answers. The total score ranged from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate better cognitive function. Participants were considered to have cognitive impairment if they scored <17 points (no formal education), <20 points (primary school education), or <24 points (secondary school education or above) [21]. Cognitive impairment is one indicator of FI. To calculate FI scores, it was necessary to assess whether participants exhibited cognitive impairment based on their educational attainment.

Assessment of covariant

Based on previous literature, this study selected the following covariates: age (continuous), sex (male/female), education level (no formal education/formal education), marital status (married/unmarried or divorced or widowed), place of residence (city/village), economic status (wealthy/normal/needy), smoking (yes/no), drinking(yes/no), exercise (yes/no), and body mass index (BMI, underweight/normal weight/overweight/obesity). BMI is calculated based on the height and weight provided by the participants. Smoking status, drinking, and exercise were assessed based on self-reported data from the CLHLS questionnaire. Smoking, drinking, and exercise were all defined as dichotomous variables (yes/no). Participants who reported currently smoking/drinking/exercising at the time of the survey were classified as “yes.”

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). Multiple imputation was used to address missing data for cMIND and FI variables (missing rate <10%) using the ‘mice’ package with chained equations. The imputation model included all analytic variables to preserve their interrelationships. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, and categorical variables were imputed using logistic regression-based methods. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies (percentages), and normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences across cMIND score tertiles (T1, T2, T3) were tested using chi-square tests for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables.

To explore the potential nonlinear relationship between the baseline cMIND score (as a continuous variable) and the risk of frailty, we fitted restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the cMIND score distribution [22]. The significance of the nonlinear association was assessed using a likelihood ratio test. Subsequently, Cox proportional hazards models were employed to quantify the association between cMIND score tertiles and frailty incidence. Model 1 was unadjusted, while Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, education level, place of residence, economic status, BMI, smoking, drinking, exercise and MMSE score. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data were weighted based on age, sex, and place of residence to ensure representativeness. The association between cMIND dietary adherence and cognitive function was assessed as a secondary analysis using a linear regression model with MMSE score as the continuous outcome.

To test the robustness of the primary findings, subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying the population based on baseline characteristics, including age (<73/≥73 years, based on the median), sex, education level, marital status, economic status, place of residence, smoking status, drinking, exercise habits, BMI categories and cognitive ability. To formally test for potential heterogeneity in the association across subgroups, an interaction term between the cMIND score and each subgroup variable was incorporated into the fully adjusted Cox model. The statistical significance of each interaction was assessed using the likelihood ratio test, comparing the model with and without the interaction term.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 1,943 participants were included in the study, with an average age of 74.9 ± 7.3 years. Among them, 982 (50.5%) were female. By the end of the follow-up period, 881 patients (45.3%) were frail. In this study, subjects were divided into high, medium and low adherence groups according to baseline cMIND diet scores: T1 was the low adherence group (0–4), T2 was the medium adherence group (4.5–5.5), and T3 was the high adherence group (6–12).The results showed that there were statistically significant differences in age, sex, education level, marital status, economic status, place of residence, drinking, exercise, and BMI among the older adults in the different cMIND groups at baseline (p < 0.05), as shown in Table 1.

Association between cMIND score and FI

As shown in Fig. 2, the restricted cubic spline model revealed a significant nonlinear association between baseline cMIND scores and frailty risk (nonlinearity test p < 0.05). Frailty risk decreased gradually with increasing cMIND scores, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the moderate-to-high score range (approximately 5–8 points), suggesting that greater adherence to the cMIND diet is associated with a lower risk of frailty among older adults. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess this association, with incident frailty (binary outcome: 1 = frail, 0 = non-frail) as the dependent variable and cMIND score tertiles (T1 as reference) as the independent variable. In the unadjusted model, compared with the T1 group, the frailty risk was not significantly different for the T2 group (HR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.83−1.11, p = 0.576). In contrast, the T3 group had a 23% lower risk (HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.66−0.89, p < 0.001). Model 2 adjusted for age (continuous variable), sex (1 = male, 2 = female), educational attainment (0 = no formal education, 1 = formal education), marital status (1 = married, 2 = unmarried/ divorced/widowed), economic status (1 = wealthy, 2 = normal, 3 = needy), place of residence (1 = city, 2 = village), smoking status (1 = yes, 0 = no), drinking status (1 = yes, 0 = no), exercise status (1 = yes, 0 = no), BMI classification (1 = underweight, 2 = normal weight, 3 = overweight, 4 = obese), and MMSE scores (continuous variable). After 10 years of follow-up the results indicated no significant association between the T2 group and frailty risk (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.86−1.14, p = 0.857), whereas the risk of frailty was decreased by 16% (HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72−0.97, p = 0.022) in the T3 group, as shown in Table 2.

Association between cMIND and cognitive ability

Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine the association between cMIND diet adherence and frailty. To assess the association between cMIND and cognitive ability, linear regression models were constructed with cognitive score (MMSE score) as the continuous outcome and cMIND tertiles as the primary exposure. Three sequential models were built: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 was further adjusted for education level, marital status, economic status, residence, smoking, drinking, exercise, and BMI. Using T1 as the reference, the study found that after adjusting for covariates, the T2 and T3 groups in 2011 showed a linear correlation with cognitive ability (p < 0.05), but no such correlation was observed in the other years, as shown in Table 3.

Subgroup analysis

To further investigate potential variations in the association, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, exercise habits, BMI categories, education level, marital status, economic status, place of residence, and cognitive ability. The results are presented in Fig. 3. In the overall cohort, each 1-point increase in the cMIND score was associated with an 8% reduction in frailty risk (HR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.88−0.96, p < 0.001). This inverse association was consistently observed across most prespecified subgroups, including different age groups (<73 years: HR = 0.91; ≥73 years: HR = 0.94), both sexes (female: HR = 0.93; male: HR = 0.92), and various lifestyle strata. Formal interaction tests identified cognitive ability as a significant source of heterogeneity (p for interaction = 0.037). A significant inverse association was observed in participants with normal cognitive function (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.86−0.96, p < 0.001) but was not observed in those with cognitive impairment (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.93–1.09, p = 0.891). For all other subgroup variables, including age, sex, education, economic status, and lifestyle factors, the p values for interaction were >0.05, indicating no statistically significant interaction.

Discussion

This study provides the first longitudinal evidence from a Chinese cohort that higher adherence to the cMIND diet is significantly associated with a lower risk of frailty among older adults. This finding provides a new direction for nutrition interventions compared to drug interventions, as dietary adjustments based on local dietary culture are more readily accepted by the older adults and hold public health value for frailty prevention. Our analysis demonstrated that higher cMIND dietary adherence was associated with a significantly lower risk of frailty. Firstly, restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a marked non-linear relationship, indicating a progressive reduction in frailty risk with increasing cMIND scores, particularly within the moderate to high score range. Subsequently, Cox proportional hazards models quantified this association. Subsequently, Cox proportional hazards models quantified this association. After adjusting for multiple potential confounders, participants in the highest tertile of cMIND adherence showed a statistically significant lower risk of frailty compared to those in the lowest tertile.

While previous studies by Huang et al. [17] and Lin et al. [23] reported a stable association between cMIND adherence and reduced cognitive impairment, our longitudinal data revealed an inconsistent relationship, with a significant association observed only in the 2011 follow-up. This intermittent pattern stands in sharp contrast to the stable, inverse association we found between cMIND and frailty risk throughout the study period. This key discrepancy suggests that the mechanism linking the cMIND diet to frailty may be linked to pathways distinct from its relationship with global cognitive function as measured by the MMSE. Although we adjusted for numerous confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., inflammation or social support) cannot be entirely ruled out.

Subgroup analyses provided crucial insights into the consistency of the association between the cMIND diet and frailty, along with its potential moderating factors. This inverse association was consistently observed across most demographic and lifestyle strata, including those defined by age, sex, economic status, and most health behaviours. However, cognitive ability was identified as a statistically significant source of heterogeneity. The lack of a significant inverse association among participants with cognitive impairment warrants further investigation. This finding may suggest that the benefits of dietary patterns on frailty development diminish once cognitive function declines, potentially due to reduced capacity to adhere to complex dietary patterns or shared neuropathological processes between cognitive impairment and frailty. Alternatively, individuals with cognitive impairment may struggle to accurately report dietary intake. Although not statistically significant, descriptive variations in the association were observed across certain subgroups. The inverse association appeared more pronounced among drinkers compared to non-drinkers, and among rural residents compared to urban residents, where the association was weaker or absent. While these findings require validation in larger studies, they point towards potential factors for future exploration in optimising the dietary benefits of cMIND.

The cMIND diet recommends moderate intake of staple foods, fresh fruits and vegetables, plant oils, mushrooms, seaweed, fish, soybeans, garlic, nuts, and tea beverages, while limiting sugars and sweets. However, this dietary plan excludes red meat (such as pork and beef). Given that red meat is a common dietary component in China, this approach may warrant reconsideration. The potential health impact of moderate, lean red meat consumption within a balanced diet for older adults remains an open question and presents an opportunity for future research to refine nutritional strategies for frailty prevention. Fresh vegetables and fruits are rich in vitamins, and previous studies have shown that a low vitamin intake is associated with a higher risk of frailty. For every 10 mg increase in carotenoid and vitamin E intake, the risk of frailty decreased by 16% and 1%, respectively [24]. Tea is a common beverage in China and is rich in polyphenols and catechins. Studies have shown that higher tea consumption can protect the older adults from muscle atrophy and reduce disability by increasing xanthine levels, thus limiting frailty [[25], [26], [27]]. Maluric acid is a metabolite of dietary polyphenols in the intestinal flora of plant foods (such as fruits, vegetables, and tea). Low levels of maluric acid are associated with cognitive impairment, muscle wasting, and frailty [28]. Vitamins, tea polyphenols and catechins have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and inflammation is one of the mechanisms of weakness; therefore, it is advisable to eat fresh fruits and vegetables to prevent weakness [29,30]. Garlic has been shown to prevent osteoporosis because of its allicin, which has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects [31].

This study used longitudinal data from 2008 to 2018 to analyze the relationship between cMIND adherence and frailty. The findings indicate that after controlling for confounding factors, high adherence to cMIND can reduce the risk of frailty in older Chinese individuals. This inverse association was robust across different age groups, sex, educational level, marital status, economic conditions, living locations, BMI, and lifestyle habits (such as smoking, drinking, and exercise). This conclusion is specific to China’ s older population. Although the study utilised nationally representative cohort data and a comprehensive frailty index to assess frailty, it still has certain limitations: participant information was collected through questionnaires, which may have introduced recall bias, and the questionnaire did not quantitatively assess dietary intake. Furthermore, only baseline BMI was used due to substantial missing follow-up data, precluding its use as a time-varying covariate. Despite adjustments for confounders, residual confounding may remain. Finally, given the unique dietary culture in China, the findings of this study may not be applicable to other regions or populations.

This longitudinal study provides supportive evidence that higher adherence to the cMIND dietary pattern is associated with a reduced incidence of frailty specifically among older Chinese adults without cognitive impairment at baseline. These findings could inform future dietary recommendations for frailty prevention in this specific population subgroup.
