Conservative Management Enabling Spontaneous Passage of an Idiopathic Primary Spontaneous Enterolith: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Lensa Million Baharu, Ragasa Getachew Bayisa, Zenebe Teklu Gebremariam, Esmael Mohamed Dawud, Nigusu Solomon Herego, Nahom Teklu Maru, Webi Alemayehu Tulu, Ahmedin Solomon Kedir, Tamirat Godebo Woyimo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnterolithiasis—formation of stone concretions within the gastrointestinal tract—is an uncommon and underrecognized cause of intermittent abdominal symptoms and acute bowel obstruction. Primary (true) enteroliths originate within the bowel lumen and are usually associated with focal stasis from anatomic or inflammatory pathology; idiopathic primary enterolithiasis (no identifiable predisposing lesion) is exceptionally rare. We present a case that illustrates diagnosis by contrast‐enhanced CT and successful nonoperative management of extensive idiopathic enterolithiasis. A 52‐year‐old woman presented with a 2‐month history of intermittent, crampy lower abdominal pain that increased in frequency over the week before presentation. She had no prior abdominal surgery, no history of inflammatory or ischemic bowel disease, and no systemic symptoms. On examination she was hemodynamically stable with a soft, non‐tender abdomen and normal laboratory tests. Contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated numerous discrete intraluminal calcifications within the large bowel consistent with multiple enteroliths but no bowel dilatation, obstruction, perforation, or underlying stricture/diverticulum. Given the clinical stability and absence of obstructive complications, she was treated conservatively with bowel rest, intravenous (IV) isotonic fluids, and analgesia. Over the following days, her pain resolved, and she passed multiple firm, stone‐like fragments per rectum. Compositional analysis of a retrieved specimen showed a predominance of calcium salts (calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate) with minor magnesium ammonium phosphate and calcium oxalate. She remained asymptomatic at the 2‐month outpatient follow‐up with no further intervention. In carefully selected, hemodynamically stable patients without evidence of complete obstruction or ischemia, conservative management (bowel rest, IV fluids, close observation) can permit spontaneous passage of even multiple enteroliths and avoid surgery. Contrast‐enhanced CT is essential for diagnosis and assessment of complications, and thorough evaluation to exclude underlying causes is recommended to guide follow‐up and prevent recurrence.

Introduction

Enterolithiasis, characterized by stone concretions in the gastrointestinal tract, is a relatively rare but increasingly recognized clinical entity, often associated with conditions like diverticular disease and inflammatory bowel diseases [1]. The documented occurrence of primary and secondary enterolithiasis among specific groups ranges widely from 0.3% to 10%, heavily influenced by factors such as clinical manifestation, causative factors, and underlying predisposing conditions [1, 2].

Intraluminal stone formation of various sizes is more common than anticipated, typically remains underreported in absence of clinical symptoms or due to its diminutive size that permits intermittent passage, and may not be visualized on conventional radiologic imaging in the majority of the cases [2, 3].

The primary enteroliths exhibit varying chemical compositions depending on where they form, how far they migrate within the intestine, and where they become impacted. The concentration of choleic acid within the enterolith inversely correlates with the distance from the proximal small bowel to the ileocecal valve, with the highest concentrations (52%–84%) observed in the duodenum and jejunum, gradually decreasing to single digits as they move distally [2].

Enteroliths can exhibit a wide range of sizes and quantities, spanning from a few millimeters to up to 10 cm in their largest dimension. They can manifest as individual stones, clusters of concretions, or be distributed throughout the entire intestine, with counts ranging from just a few to nearly a 100 stones [1, 3]. Symptoms are nonspecific, including abdominal pain and gastrointestinal disturbances, potentially leading to complications such as bleeding and perforation [1, 2].

While conservative management is indeed the preferred initial approach for stable patients without obstruction, the rarity of this case lies in the extent and idiopathic nature of the stone burden. Most reported cases of primary enterolithiasis, even when managed conservatively, are associated with identifiable anatomical predispositions (e.g., diverticula, strictures). The presentation of multiple, extensive enteroliths in the large bowel in the complete absence of any such risk factors—anatomical, inflammatory, neoplastic, or surgical—is exceptionally uncommon in the literature. Furthermore, the successful nonoperative passage of such a substantial calcific burden challenges the typical surgical narrative often associated with similar radiographic findings. The aim of this case report is to illustrate the detailed clinical presentations, diagnosis by contrast‐enhanced CT, and successful nonoperative management of extensive idiopathic enterolithiasis located within the transverse colon of large bowel.

Case History/Examination

Here, we present a 52‐year‐old female patient who presented with a two‐month history of intermittent lower abdominal pain. The pain was crampy and of moderate intensity, initially occurring about twice weekly without clear triggers, then gradually increasing in frequency and severity over the week before presentation. It was confined to the lower abdomen and was not associated with nausea, vomiting, fever, changes in bowel habits, or weight loss. She also denied any urinary symptoms or gynecologic complaints. Her review of systems was otherwise unremarkable. The patient had no significant past medical or surgical history. She took no regular medications or supplements and had no history of gastrointestinal disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or prior abdominal surgeries. She denied excessive intake of dairy, spinach, nuts, or other high‐oxalate foods. She had no family history of intestinal or renal calculi. Gynecologic history was unremarkable (no gynecologic surgeries, normal menstrual history). She was a nonsmoker and did not use alcohol or illicit drugs.

On examination, the patient was alert and in no acute distress, with stable vital signs. Blood pressure: 120/70 mmHg, pulse rate: 98 bpm, respiratory rate: 24 breaths/min, temperature: 36.9°C, oxygen saturation: 98% on room air. General examination findings were normal. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, non‐distended abdomen with normal bowel sounds. There was no tenderness, guarding, or rebound on palpation of the abdomen, and no palpable masses or organomegaly. Examination of other systems (cardiac, pulmonary, neurological) was unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis

Secondary‐enterolith formation in the intestine is almost invariably secondary to conditions that promote prolonged luminal stasis or an altered microenvironment. Anatomical abnormalities such as congenital or acquired diverticula (duodenal, jejuno‐ileal), blind loops, and surgical entero‐anastomoses (including Roux limbs), and strictures or stenoses from Crohn's disease or intestinal tuberculosis create focal areas of slowed motility that favor precipitation of calcium phosphate, calcium carbonate, and bile salt concretions. Additional predisposing factors include radiation enteritis, eosinophilic enteritis, mucosal diaphragmatic disease, intestinal duplication, fistula formation, malignancy, and kinking from intra‐abdominal adhesions or incarcerated hernias. Secondary enteroliths may also arise from migrated gallstones or renal calculi via cholecystoenteric or ureteroenteric fistulas.

In our case, exhaustive evaluation—including contrast‐enhanced CT, serological and post‐compositional assessment—revealed no evidence of diverticular disease, prior abdominal surgery or anastomosis, blind loops, stricturing inflammatory or infectious bowel disease, radiation changes, neoplasia, fistula tracts, or adhesive kinking. There was likewise no history of enteric infections (e.g., amoebiasis), ischemic enteritis, or congenital anomalies. The absence of these well‐established secondary causes supports classification as primary idiopathic enterolithiasis.

While our evaluation rigorously excluded anatomical causes of stasis, we acknowledge the potential role of functional dysmotility or dietary influences in idiopathic lithogenesis. Conditions such as chronic intestinal pseudo‐obstruction or slow‐transit constipation can promote luminal stasis without structural abnormalities. Although our patient reported no chronic constipation or symptoms suggestive of a primary motility disorder, and no specific testing (e.g., motility studies) was performed during her acute presentation, this remains a consideration. Similarly, dietary habits high in calcium or oxalate can predispose to enterolith formation, particularly of calcium‐oxalate or calcium‐phosphate composition. Our patient denied excessive intake of dairy, spinach, nuts, or other high‐oxalate foods. The compositional analysis revealing calcium phosphate/carbonate and minor struvite suggests precipitation in an alkaline environment, potentially influenced by luminal pH and bacterial action, rather than a primary dietary excess. In truly idiopathic cases, a multifactorial interplay of subtle dysmotility, luminal microbiome, and local biochemical milieu is likely at play, even in the absence of overt clinical risk factors.

Investigations and Treatment of the Case

Laboratory studies, including complete blood count, serum electrolytes, renal and liver function tests, and inflammatory markers, were all within normal limits (Table S1). A contrast‐enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis was performed. It showed numerous small, discrete calcifications scattered throughout the large intestine, more prominent in the transverse colon, consistent with extensive enterolithiasis, with the largest identified stone measuring 14 mm. There was no evidence of bowel obstruction, perforation, or significant bowel wall thickening. No masses, strictures, or other pathological findings were identified (Figures 1 and 2). All other findings on imaging were normal. Given the patient's stable condition and absence of obstructive complications, conservative management was chosen. She was treated with bowel rest, intravenous fluids of isotonic normal saline of 0.9%, and pain control with diclofenac 75 mg given intramuscularly. Over the next few days, her abdominal pain gradually resolved. The patient subsequently passed several hard, stone‐like fragments per rectum. No surgical intervention was required. One of the retrieved stones was sent for compositional analysis. It confirmed a true enterolith, composed predominantly of calcium salts. The major constituents were calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate, with minor components of magnesium ammonium phosphate (struvite) and calcium oxalate.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient was followed in the outpatient clinic for 2 months after discharge. She remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of abdominal pain or new symptoms. No additional stones were noted, and she required no further interventions. Overall, the patient made a full recovery with conservative treatment and close observation.

Discussion

Our case was found to have an idiopathic primary enterolith within the large intestine in the absence of any known predisposing conditions. Enterolithiasis, or the formation of gastrointestinal concretions, typically arises when intestinal stasis occurs—most often in association with diverticula, surgical entero‐anastomoses, blind pouches or afferent loops, incarcerated hernias, bowel neoplasms, adhesive kinking, or stenosing inflammatory disorders such as Crohn's disease or intestinal tuberculosis [1, 4]. It may be classified as primary (originating within the gut lumen) or secondary (arising from migrated biliary or renal calculi via fistulae), and its prevalence in selected cohorts ranges from 0.3% to 10% [1, 2, 3]. Proximal enteroliths generally consist of choleic acid salts, whereas those forming distally tend to be heavily calcified. Patients often present with fluctuating subacute symptoms—intermittent abdominal pain, distention, nausea, and vomiting—reflecting the enterolith's movement through the bowel [1, 2, 3, 4]. Serious complications can include obstruction, mucosal pressure injury, gangrene, intussusception, afferent loop syndrome, diverticulitis, iron‐deficiency anemia, hemorrhage, or even perforation, with reported mortality rates up to 3% for primary and 8% for secondary enterolithiasis.

Review of literature reports of idiopathic primary enterolithiasis are few and almost uniformly document focal, usually solitary, calcified stones causing bowel obstruction that required operative removal; notable examples include a primary jejunal enterolith causing acute obstruction reported by Karveli et al. and several primary true enterolith cases summarized by Jadib et al. [5, 6], all managed surgically after imaging identified an obstructing intraluminal calculus [7]. Other series and case reports emphasize that enterolith formation most commonly reflects an anatomic cause of stasis (diverticula, strictures, anastomoses) and therefore present as single obstructing stones or stones proximal to a narrowing (examples: Molla et al., Sharma et al.) [5]. Reports of multiple enteroliths are generally associated with identifiable stasis (e.g., diverticula or blind loops) rather than truly idiopathic disease [8]. Crucially, we found no case report describing extensive, multiple idiopathic (no structural/inflammatory predisposition identified) enteroliths distributed through the colon that resolved solely with conservative observation and spontaneous passage; most nonoperative successes in the literature involve partial obstruction with an identifiable cause or active endoscopic/chemical fragmentation rather than simple observation [9]. In this context, our patient differs from previously published cases by three features: [1] multiplicity and extensive colonic distribution of true, calcium‐based enteroliths without detectable anatomic stasis, [2] confirmation of idiopathic (non‐secondary), and [3] successful conservative management with spontaneous passage—a combination that appears to add a novel and clinically relevant example to the field [1, 7, 8].

Contrast‐enhanced CT is the primary modality for enterolithiasis, providing multiplanar detection of sharply defined intraluminal masses at the obstruction transition with proximal dilatation and precise localization. CT differentiates primary enteroliths (proximal, low‐attenuation, gas‐containing/honeycomb) from secondary stones (distal, high‐attenuation, calcified, often with cholecystoenteric fistula and pneumobilia), and reveals complications (bowel edema, mesenteric changes, free fluid/air), directly guiding management [1, 9].

Definitive diagnosis relies on thorough history, physical examination, and targeted imaging, and management ranges from endoscopic retrieval to surgical intervention. In contrast to the usual presentation, our case showed none of the above risk factors or clinical complaints: there was no history of prior abdominal surgery or diverticular disease; no evidence of inflammatory, infectious, neoplastic, or adhesive pathology on imaging or exploration; and the patient experienced no obstructive symptoms such as vomiting or distension. This supports classification as primary idiopathic enterolithiasis in our patient.

Management of enterolithiasis spans a continuum from noninvasive medical therapy to endoscopic clearance and definitive surgical intervention. Initial conservative measures—including bowel rest, nasogastric decompression, and solvent‐based dissolution (e.g., cola infusion)—have been shown to soften calcified enteroliths, facilitating fragmentation and spontaneous passage without procedural risks [10]. When conservative therapy is insufficient or stones persist, endoscopic approaches using balloon‐assisted or double‐balloon enteroscopy (DBE) enable direct visualization, mechanical lithotripsy, electrohydraulic lithotripsy, snare fragmentation, and retrieval of obstructing concretions; these techniques carry lower morbidity than open surgery and have been successfully employed even in altered anatomy such as Crohn's‐related strictures [11, 12]. Surgical enterolithotomy—with or without segmental resection or correction of underlying stenosis—remains the gold standard for acute bowel obstruction, perforation, or when endoscopic removal fails; it also allows definitive management of associated pathology to prevent recurrence [10, 11].

Conservative management is particularly indicated in patients who are hemodynamically stable, exhibit no signs of complete obstruction or ischemia, harbor stones ≤ 20 mm in diameter, and demonstrate spontaneous movement of the enterolith on imaging studies, making them ideal candidates for nonoperative resolution.

Strength and Limitation

This report's strength lies in its detailed documentation of an exceedingly rare idiopathic primary enterolithiasis with full clinical, radiologic, and biochemical workup. The CT images clearly demonstrated multiple large‐bowel calcifications, and stone composition analysis confirmed a true calcium‐based enterolith. Notably, the patient achieved a complete recovery with nonoperative care (no surgery was needed), illustrating a successful management approach. However, as a single case the findings have limited generalizability. The follow‐up period was relatively short (2 months) and longer‐term outcomes (e.g., recurrence) are unknown. The true etiology remains unclear (hence “idiopathic”), so we cannot determine specific risk factors or mechanisms to guide prevention.

Conclusion

This case highlights that primary enterolithiasis—even without known predisposing factors—should be considered in patients with unexplained intermittent abdominal symptoms. Prompt contrast‐enhanced CT can identify multiple calcified enteroliths, and metabolic analysis can confirm stone composition. Our patient's uneventful recovery with nonoperative care suggests that conservative management is viable in carefully selected stable cases. Clinicians should still evaluate thoroughly for occult causes (e.g., diverticula, strictures) to prevent recurrence. Increased awareness of this rare entity and its benign course in select patients may help avoid unnecessary surgery.

Learning Points

Maintain a broad differential: Although idiopathic primary enterolithiasis is very rare, it should be considered in patients with subacute or recurrent lower abdominal pain, even in the absence of obvious risk factors. Use imaging for diagnosis: Contrast‐enhanced CT is key to diagnosis, often revealing intraluminal calcified masses scattered along the bowel consistent with enterolithiasis. Plain films may miss such stones, so CT should be pursued if suspicion is high. Consider conservative therapy first: In hemodynamically stable patients without complete obstruction, initial nonoperative management (bowel rest, IV fluids, pain control) can soften and mobilize stones, allowing spontaneous passage. Endoscopic or surgical intervention can be reserved for those with failed conservative therapy or complications. Search for underlying pathology: Even when no obvious cause is found, clinicians should thoroughly exclude diverticula, strictures, fistulas, or postsurgical anatomy. Identifying and correcting any underlying stasis or obstruction (if present) is important to prevent recurrence. Stone analysis (e.g., calcium phosphate/carbonate composition) can also help classify the enterolith as primary versus secondary. Monitor for recurrence in idiopathic cases: Given the unknown etiology, patients with idiopathic primary enterolithiasis should be advised about the potential for recurrence. Follow‐up should include periodic clinical review for symptom recurrence (e.g., abdominal pain, distension) and consideration of surveillance imaging (e.g., abdominal X‐ray or non‐contrast CT) at 6–12 months or sooner if symptoms recur, as there are no established guidelines. Patient education on reporting early symptoms is crucial.
