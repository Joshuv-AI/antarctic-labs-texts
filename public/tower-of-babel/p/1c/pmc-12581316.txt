Synthesis of conceptual, methodological and pedagogical works on scaling up complex public health interventions: a scoping review
Authors: Nolwenn Stevens, François Alla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn public health, the scaling up of interventions can bring progress for the health of populations on the basis of promising experiments, by meeting major health, ethical and economic challenges. However, there is no systematic procedure, like those established for drug development, to guide development of a public health intervention with the aim of scaling it up, nor is there a benchmark approach to establish the validity of an intervention for scaling up. We performed a literature review to analyse the concept of scaling up in the public health field.MethodsThe materials included were published between 2000 and 2021 and present conceptual frameworks and models, guides and tools developed for scaling up public health interventions. Three main objects were analysed through these publications: the meanings of the concept of scaling up, success factors or barriers to scaling up, and the steps or phases of work in the scaling up process.ResultsThis research explored 24 bibliographical references: 16 scientific articles, 5 guides or tools, 2 public health science theses and 1 book chapter. Two main approaches were defined: scalability and scaling up. 29 success factors and 28 obstacles to scaling up were identified. Finally, 10 work phases in the scaling up of a public health intervention were described.ConclusionsIn order to embrace the different nuances highlighted in the existing literature, we propose a new definition of public health intervention scaling up. It concerns all levels from local to global. It is not a spontaneous process, but requires active dissemination of the intervention in space, in time and in real world conditions. Several avenues for future research are proposed. They concern the practices of the different stakeholders, specifics of the process according to the scale concerned, the arcana of the scaling up decision, and the connections between the concept of scaling up and other notions in the field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25040-w.Keywords: Scaling up, Public health intervention, Decision support, Evidence-based, Evaluation, Complex intervention

Background

In public health, the scaling up of interventions allows progress in population health on the basis of promising experiments [1]. The Medical Research Council (MRC) considers that the role of intervention research in population health is to provide interventions that are effective, implementable, cost-effective, transferable and scalable in real world conditions [2].

Thus, scaling up can be apprehended as the ultimate finality of intervention development, then becoming part of the intervention development continuum which includes, without imperative chronology: intervention design, evaluation and scaling up [1]. Despite the importance of this perspective of generalisation, there is no systematic procedure guiding the development of interventions with a view to scaling up: there are no key stages in the development of a complex public health intervention like those laid down for the development of drugs; there is no gold standard, no tool or reference approach making it possible to establish the validity of an intervention for its scaling up. The MRC, for example, made a new proposal for a framework for the development and evaluation of complex interventions in 2021, in which scaling up and its specific conditions are not spelled out [2]. The implementation phase, defined by the MRC as deliberate efforts to increase the impact and uptake of successfully tested health innovations, is described briefly but no methodology is proposed and only a few tips and caveats are presented to increase "the potential of developing an intervention that can be widely adopted and maintained in real-world settings".

However, scaling up is not self-evident, nor does it follow on naturally or spontaneously from an intervention. For instance, many interventions endure as local initiatives, sometimes for a long time; others reach scale without having been properly tested; still others fail when they are scaled up, despite promising results during their experimentation [3–8].

Public health decision-making must be based on the best available scientific knowledge [9]. Knowledge or evidence is not only about the effectiveness of interventions, it is "the available body of facts or information indicating whether a belief or proposition is true or valid" [10]. The validity of an intervention for scaling up implies a body of evidence that should then be considered to arrive at a compelling decision.

In response to these difficulties and the questions raised about the scaling up of public health interventions, several authors have put forward proposals for conceptual frameworks or models on the subject, and for guides or tools to guide the process. This article presents a review of the literature that provides a synthesis of these different works.

The main objectives of this literature review are: i) to identify conceptual frameworks and models, guides and tools developed on the scaling up of public health interventions and ii) to analyse these publications in order to describe the concept of scaling up of public health interventions, to identify the conditions for successful scaling up, to identify the barriers to scaling up and, finally, to describe the steps or phases of work in the scaling up process.

The aim is to understand how these concepts have been addressed, described and used in population health. The review provides an overview of the state of the art in terms of existing tools, models and guides for scaling up complex public health interventions.

For the purposes of this study, an intervention is defined as “a set of actions with a coherent objective to bring about change or produce identifiable outcomes” [11]. These actions may include “policy, regulatory initiatives, single strategy projects or multi-component programmes” [11]. We are interested here in so-called population-based health interventions. They correspond to non-clinical interventions that focus on programmes both within and outside the health sector, with an impact on the health of populations but not on the health of individuals as such [12]. “Public health interventions are intended to promote or protect health or prevent ill health in communities or populations”, and are distinct from “clinical interventions, which are intended to prevent or treat illness in individuals” [11]. These interventions are generally complex, as defined by the Medical Research Council. This complexity is based on: i) the number of interacting components, ii) the influence of the actors involved in the implementation and of those who receive it, iii) the number and variability of expected outcomes, and iv) the adaptations that may occur during the implementation of the interventions [13].

For the purposes of this review, frameworks or conceptual models refer to generic conceptual or methodological proposals regarding the potential for scaling up interventions or the process of scaling up interventions. Guidelines, tools or checklists are scientifically developed materials to guide the assessment of the potential for scaling up interventions, the decision to scale up, or the process of scaling up. They are therefore more operational in nature, i.e. action-oriented, although they make use of the concepts and thinking addressed in the frameworks and models.

Methods

We conducted a scoping review to conduct our literature search on existing tools, models or guides for scaling up complex public health interventions.

Our research focus corresponded to the following indications: i) clarify key concepts and definitions in the literature, ii) identify key characteristics or factors related to a concept, iii) examine how research is conducted on a certain topic or area and, finally, iv) identify and analyse knowledge gaps. We then proposed to map, present and discuss these concepts and their characteristics [14].

As stated in the scoping review methodology, we endeavoured to respect the following points: a research protocol was defined beforehand, a systematic search was carried out in the selected databases, the method was described transparently to ensure it was reproducible, measures were implemented to reduce errors and increase reliability, and the data was extracted and presented in a structured manner [14].

Finally, in accordance with the recommendations in force, we followed the PRISMA-ScR checklist for scoping reviews to carry out this study [15].

In order to meet the various objectives of our review, as set out above, we have chosen to briefly present each bibliographical reference included in our review (in line with the first objective we have set ourselves). Readers will easily be able to refer to one or other reference of particular interest. For each point of study, we have then chosen to adopt a horizontal method of analysis and therefore a horizontal presentation of the results, thus favouring the construction of summary results.

Two databases were selected: Medline via the PubMed portal and Web Of Sciences. In addition, the bibliographic references of relevant articles were also searched ("bibliographic snowball").

Sélection of articles

Before starting the article selection process, the results of the searches conducted in each database were pooled and duplicates eliminated. This was facilitated by the use of Zotero software.

Articles were selected in two stages: The first consisted in reading the titles and abstracts, and the second in reading the entire article. At each stage, relevant articles corresponding to the inclusion criteria were retained, while articles not corresponding to the inclusion criteria or corresponding to the exclusion criteria were excluded (Fig. 1).

Flow chart

The first stage of our literature search identified a total of 1615 references, of which 855 came from Medline and 760 from Web Of Science. Of these 1,615 references, 538 duplicates were eliminated. The remainder of our analysis therefore focused on 1077 references.

The second stage of our literature search was to select the relevant articles with regard to our inclusion and exclusion criteria by examining the titles and abstracts of each reference. At the end of this stage, 1038 references were excluded. The remainder of our analysis therefore focused on 40 references.

The third stage of our literature search was to analyse the full text of each reference against the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as previously defined. This stage led us to exclude a further 28 references. At the end of this stage, our documentary corpus consisted of 12 references.

The first two stages of the bibliographic reference selection process were carried out by a single reviewer. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were determined collectively by the two reviewers. During the third stage of the process, concerning the eligibility of references for full-text reading, the first reviewer was able to call on the second reviewer as needed when there was doubt about the inclusion or exclusion of a reference. By comparing the analyses carried out by each reviewer in the light of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and discussing these analyses, the reviewers reached a consensus on whether or not to include the document.

To these 12 references were added 12 additional references mentioned in the initial corpus. Our final corpus therefore contained 24 bibliographic references.

Terminology and definitions adopted in the literature review

Scale-up refers to the deliberate effort to broaden the delivery of an EBI with the intention of reaching larger numbers of a target audience. Often an EBI scale-up will target health delivery units within the same, or very similar settings, under which the EBI has already been tested.(Aarons et al.)

Scale-out refers to a deliberate effort to broaden the delivery of an EBI. Scale-out is an extension of scale-up and uniquely refers to the deliberate use of strategies to implement, test, improve, and sustain an EBI as it is delivered to new populations and/or through new delivery systems that differ from those in effectiveness trials.(Aarons et al.)

Scaling up refers to deliberate efforts to increase the impact of successfully tested health interventions so as to benefit more people and to foster policy and program development on a lasting basis (WHO and Expandnet)

Scalability of an intervention is determined by its effectiveness and by the likely reach and adoption of the intervention, the costs of operating at larger scale and the acceptability and fit of the intervention within the local policy context. (Milat et al.)

Scalability was proposed by Milat et al. in 2013 and corresponds to “ the ability of a health intervention shown to be efficacious on a small scale and or under controlled conditions to be expanded under real-world conditions to reach a greater proportion of the eligible population, while retaining effectiveness”, “scalability denotes the capacity of an individual intervention to be scaled up”, “scalability of an intervention is determined by its effectiveness and by the likely reach and adoption of the intervention, the costs of operating at larger scale and the acceptability and fit of the intervention within the local policy context” [24, 27–29, 38]. Seven years later, Milat et al. substantiated and operationalised the notion by proposing a tool to assess the potential for scaling up interventions: “the intervention scalability assessment tool” [29].

As for the scaling process, two definitions are mostly used. Some authors refer to the definition proposed by Mangham and Hanson: “the ambition or process of expanding the coverage of health interventions”, “the term has also referred to increasing the financial, human and capital resources required to expand coverage” [25, 36, 37], others to those of the World Health Organisation and ExpandNet – “deliberate efforts to increase the impact of the health service innovations successfully tested in pilot or experimental projects so as to benefit more people and to foster policy and programme development on a lasting basis”, “deliberate efforts to increase the impact of successfully tested health interventions so as to benefit more people and to foster policy and program development on a lasting basis”, “the deliberate process of enhancing the impact of an effective intervention” [7, 24, 27, 29, 32, 34, 35, 38, 39]. Paina et al. consider scaling up as follows: “Scaling up in health is more than the expansion of coverage of health services. It can be defined as a set of processes that lead to expanded and sustainable coverage of services, and involves strengthening the capacity of delivery organizations, increasing diversity and robustness of funding and management arrangements, and growing the system’s overall capabilities to add more services or to integrate services” [33, 40, 41]. Finally Nguyen et al. propose the following working definition: “the deliberate attempt to increase the impact (i.e., improve population health and reduce health inequities) of an effective population health intervention.” [30].

Scaling is thus a three-dimensional process that takes place in time, space and reality. Scaling invests the dimension of space through the appearance of interventions of interest to new sites and new populations. Scaling invests the dimension of time through its ambition to perpetuate the intervention. Scaling up takes on the dimension of reality in that it goes beyond the controlled, even optimized, conditions of the experimental framework. Scaling up only takes place in real conditions of practice and organization.»

Geographical or population expansion is the core of the concept, while temporal expansion and normalisation are not mentioned systematically. Moreover, some authors distinguish between two parts of scaling up: "implementing scale up" and "sustaining the scaled up". [30] Others choose to distinguish geographical or population extension from temporal expansion by distinguishing between “scaling up” and “sustaining” [8].

In contrast, most authors agree on the characterisation of scaling as an active process [7, 8, 17, 18, 24, 25, 27, 29–35, 38]. As such, it is distinguished from diffusion (a passive and spontaneous process), and compared to dissemination (deliberate and planned efforts) [8].

Aarons et al. also put forward the notion of "scale-out or scaling-out" as “a deliberate effort to broaden the delivery of an evidence based intervention. Scale out is an extension of scale-up and uniquely refers to the deliberate use of strategies to implement, test, improve, and sustain an evidence based intervention as it is delivered to new populations and/or through new delivery systems that differ from those in effectiveness trials.” Thus they point to the importance of the degree of similarity or dissimilarity of the populations and contexts or systems concerned. Three types of scaling are then discerned: i) fixed population, different context, ii) similar context, different population and iii) different population and context [17].

Ovretveit et al. propose the notion of "descaling", which would be the "reverse" of scaling up, or "de-implantation": “how to reduce the use of interventions providing no benefit for most patients but which steals resources from others” [42]. They emphasise the need to explore this issue of scaling up.

Finally, several authors have proposed a more detailed characterisation of the scaling variants and a typology of them. Schematically, we propose to distinguish between four categories of scaling (Fig. 2).

The four main categories of scaling

Horizontal scaling up or quantitative scaling up or replication or expansion: This type of scaling up corresponds to the spread of the intervention through replication to scale up: interventions are replicated in different geographical sites, are extended to more populations or to new population categories. As strict replication is rarely possible, interventions will generally be adapted to meet the possibilities and needs of the contexts [8, 18, 20, 23, 27, 30, 32, 34, 35, 38, 41, 43–45].

Organizational scaling up, structural scaling up or collaboration: This type of scaling up corresponds to the development of formal or informal partnerships around the intervention, the search for additional (financial and institutional) resources in order to consolidate the intervention, to improve its effectiveness, efficiency and sustainability and to favour its expansion. It implies a possible redistribution of responsibilities related to the intervention's outreach [8, 20, 30, 41, 43, 44].

Finally, one last type of scaling is mentioned: spontaneous diffusion, although all the definitions rule out the spontaneity of the scaling process, distinguishing passive diffusion processes from active processes or deliberate efforts to disseminate that constitute scaling [35].

In this visual representation, we wanted to show the form and main mechanisms of each type of scaling. Thus, the scaling up of an original intervention following the horizontal scaling up pathway will mainly rely on a mechanism of multiple adoptions, whereas the scaling up of an original intervention following the vertical scaling up pathway will mainly rely on a central decision to institutionalise the intervention as well as on the evolution of the conditions required for the scaling up of the intervention.

These different types of scaling are not mutually exclusive. This categorisation is only intended to support the identification and understanding of different forms of scaling. For example, some authors distinguish between scaling that is both horizontal and structural, or scaling that is both vertical and functional [8]. They then borrow and add together the characteristics of both categories of scaling. Other combinations are also possible.

Scaling up contexts

Among the references reviewed, most researchers have addressed the issue of scaling up in the context of low and middle-income countries [8, 19–21, 23, 25, 33, 36, 37]. Fewer authors have focused on high-income countries [24, 27–29, 32, 38]. In the body of literature resulting from this review, it is mainly Milat and his colleagues who examined the latter. Some authors seem to have worked on this concept independently of country income levels, so their work could inform scaling up in a low, middle or high-income country equally well [17, 18, 30, 31, 42].

Furthermore, many references address scaling up in an international context. The scaling up of a given intervention in such cases corresponds to the expansion of this intervention outside the borders of its original country. Scaling up has thus been of great interest to international health, global health or humanitarian aid. Although the situation is less frequently mentioned in the body of literature available to us, scaling up can be undertaken on a national or even sub-national, regional level for example.

Objects of scaling

It should be remembered that the bibliographic selection in this literature review only included references dealing with public health interventions of the complex, population-based type and which addressed health in a generic manner. Thus, references dealing with simple, individual health-oriented, clinical or pharmacological interventions or interventions dealing with a single theme were not retained in the body of literature of this review.

The literature explored deals with the scaling up of various interventional objects. Some simply refer to interventions in the health field under the terms: « global health program», « health interventions», « health services», « public health interventions», « public health programs»,« intervention de santé publique», « intervention de santé mondiale», « health promotion interventions», « health interventions for population», « population health interventions», « interventions in national and global health programs», « Global health interventions», « Health policy and programmes» and « Health systems» [8, 22–26, 26–31, 36, 37]. Some specify the complexity of the interventions concerned by their work: « complex health interventions»,« complex intervention in public health»,« complex population health interventions» et « complex adaptative systems» [18, 21, 29, 33]. On several occasions, interventions described as "innovative" are targeted with the terms « health innovations»,« innovations», « innovative health interventions» et « new program, policy or intervention» [7, 19, 20, 22, 26, 34, 36, 42]. Finally, mention is made of evidence-based interventions or interventions that have been proven by the expressions « evidence-based (population health) interventions» et « tested health service innovations» [17, 32, 35].

Reasons for interest in scaling up

The interest in scaling up public health interventions is based on a threefold observation that is widely shared: i) the availability of evidence on effective interventions, ii) a health situation that is still alarming in certain regions of the world, or certain territories, iii) the lack of any guarantee that an intervention that is successful on a small scale will be successful on a large scale [7, 8, 17–19, 23, 24, 26–30, 32–37, 42].

There is a significant gap between what is known and what is implemented. It could be described as a deficit in the translation of knowledge into action, in other words, a gap in translational research concerning public health interventions [15]. Experimentation is of very limited interest if it is not studied with a view to possible scaling up [29, 35]. The entire world population, regardless of country income levels, could benefit from scaling up the 'right' interventions in a sustainable way [10–13, 15, 27, 30, 32, 33, 43]. Scaling up in a meaningful way could help to achieve the Millennium Development Goals, among other things [33–36]. Scaling up is also a matter of health equity [23, 25]. It is a way of selecting among interventions in order to avoid unnecessary expenditure, resources and effort [29]. The challenge of scaling up is therefore a matter of health, ethics and economics, seeking to increase the impact of public health interventions by extending their dissemination to a larger territory or population, capitalise on the knowledge acquired during the experimental phases by not restricting the benefits to those in the experiment and allowing all potential beneficiaries to benefit, and finally use the resources made available to the health systems in a reasoned and efficient manner.

However, the scaling up of public health interventions and its success is not self-evident: “Scaling up ideas, tools, programs and policies is not straightforward”, “We do not know how best to scale up interventions effectively” [18, 19, 37]. Scaling up then becomes an important research object, worked on from several angles: conditions for success, obstacles, stages, etc. [29, 31, 36]. Identifying the factors for successful scaling up of interventions is crucial [19, 20, 26, 35] for many local successes show disappointing results on a larger scale, as the criteria for scaling up are rarely taken into account [7]. The aim is to inform public health policy decisions [21, 22, 28, 35].

We propose to divide them into 5 categories: conditions related to i) the intervention itself; ii) the context; iii) the populations; iv) the resources and, finally, v) dissemination. Scaling up steps

Scaling is often considered a non-linear process [18, 19, 30, 31, 36]. Rather than strictly successive stages, there are working phases that are identified and may appear at different times and at different points in the process. Many of these work phases serve to create the conditions for successful scaling up mentioned above.

However, some authors do identify key stages in scaling up [18, 24, 26, 27, 30, 31, 38]. For instance, Indig et al. define four stages, Milat et al. describe four different stages, Nguyen et al. propose a division into three phases and eleven actions, Barker et al. identify four different stages, Bradley et al. list five components and Cooley et al. present three stages and 10 tasks.

Discussion

This literature review identified 24 bibliographical references: 5 proposed conceptual models, 12 frameworks, 6 guides and 1 evaluation tool on the scaling up of public health interventions. Two main approaches were found: scalability and scaling up. 29 success factors for scaling up and 28 obstacles were identified. They can be grouped into 5 categories: those related to i) the intervention itself; ii) the context; iii) the populations; iv) the resources and, finally, v) dissemination. Finally, 10 working phases in the scaling up of a public health intervention were identified: i) development of the experimental intervention; ii) identification of the intervention; iii) evaluation and analysis of the intervention through the lens of scaling up; iv) analysis of the contexts, populations and resources involved in future deployment; v) the decision to scale up the intervention; vi) selection and planning of the scaling-up strategy; vii) preparation of the intervention, contexts and populations; viii) dissemination of the intervention; ix) ongoing evaluation or monitoring; and x) sustaining the intervention over time.

The notion of scaling up public health interventions has several meanings. Although some definitions are more frequently used, there is no consensus on any of them. In the light of the different works analysed in this literature review and in order to embrace the different nuances highlighted in the existing definitions, we propose to define scaling up as the active three-dimensional process composed of (i) geographical or population expansion, (ii) normalisation and (iii) the perpetuation of the intervention; conditioned by characteristics linked to the intervention, the context, the populations, the resources and the dissemination strategies deployed; resulting from a decision and requiring the mobilisation of dedicated resources with the aim of increasing the health impact of the intervention by making rational use of resources according to a principle of equity (Fig. 3). We propose to schematise the scaling up of public health interventions by representing the three dimensions it covers, its processual nature, its subordination to a set of conditions, its requirement for dedicated resources, its dependence on a decision, and its aims (Fig. 3).

Representation of the scaling up of public health interventions

Among the objectives pursued by the scaling-up of an intervention, the literature identifies that of increasing health equity. However, while scaling up interventions can be a public health strategy for achieving greater equity in health, it does not automatically reduce health inequity. For this reason, it needs to be handled with vigilance, bearing in mind the objective of increasing equity in health. Indeed, if no precautions are taken in this direction, if specific measures are not implemented for the most disadvantaged, scaling up may, on the contrary, contribute to increasing health inequity [25].

Notions related to the scaling up of public health interventions suffer from a similar polysemy and lack of consensus on their definition as those on scaling up. They seem to belong to a semantic grey area which implies an imprecise distinction in the use of different concepts or nuances such as dissemination, diffusion, transferability, or applicability, for example [47].

The process of scaling up is not a passive and spontaneous development of the intervention, nor is it limited to the dissemination of the intervention alone. Scaling up involves all the segments of intervention development: design of the public health intervention, evaluation(s) and dissemination. Scaling up cannot be seen as a continuous and monotonous process, nor as a momentary activity. The implementation contexts, needs and characteristics of the populations, and resources that can be mobilised may change during the different phases of the process. These changes then lead to adaptations of the intervention and the dissemination strategies to promote and maintain the success of scaling up. In addition, each of the working phases described above can be reopened and revised during the scaling-up process. For example, as the intervention evolves during scaling up, it can be re-evaluated and analysed through the lens of scaling up. Similarly, preparation or adaptation of the intervention, contexts and populations may occur throughout the scaling-up process. The decision to scale up the intervention may be revised according to the results of the preparation phase or those of the ongoing evaluation of the intervention and scaling up process.

It therefore seems difficult to identify where scaling up ends. It could be considered an unfinished process insofar as constant adaptations are required and the perpetuation of the intervention does not allow for the final completion of scaling up. It could also be considered complete when no further activity is required to ensure its perpetuation, as the intervention then becomes part of current practice. It could also be closed when the needs behind the development of the intervention are no longer present.

It is also possible to identify three groups of activities in the process of scaling up an intervention. The first group includes intervention development and implementation activities, the second evaluation and analysis activities, and the third strategic and decision-making activities. We propose to map the scaling-up process by representing the different phases of work, the types of activities to which each phase relates, and the iterations between these phases (Fig. 4).

Main working phases of the scaling up process

The different phases identified in the literature as constituting the scaling up of public health interventions, and particularly the phase of evaluating and analysing the intervention through the prism of scaling up and determining the potential for scaling up interventions, echo the reflections frequently encountered in the literature on the limitations of "classic" interventional evaluations. In particular, evaluations that focus solely on the effectiveness of complex interventions are considered insufficient to guide decision-making [48–50].

The body of literature explored in our study enables us to provide an overview of the different stages in the scaling process. At the same time, it provides us with useful insights into the scaling process. However, more details would be very useful in order to better understand what is at stake during the scaling up of an intervention, and even more would be needed to advise and support the various stakeholders in the process. This subject should therefore be explored further. There are a number of avenues to explore: from a study of what the literature can tell us about notions related to scaling up or encompassed by this concept, such as the adaptation of interventions, to a study of the experiences of the various stakeholders involved in scaling up. In addition, this literature review has enabled us to identify four typical categories of scaling: they can be described as horizontal, vertical, organisational and/or functional. It would be useful to observe whether the phases of the scaling process differ according to these categories, as well as the success factors or obstacles to the process.

Among the intervention-related success factors identified by this literature review, certain characteristics have been the subject of dedicated work. The dimension of the viability or "viable validity" of the intervention, defined as "the extent to which an evaluation provides evidence that an intervention is successful in the real world" or "viable viability refers to stakeholders' views and experience regarding whether an intervention program is practical, affordable, suitable, evaluable, and helpful in the real-world" [51] can be recognised in the condition of compatibility of the intervention with the environment and actual conditions of deployment. This dimension is also linked to the conditions of usefulness, adaptability, simplicity and testability of the intervention. The applicability dimension of the intervention, defined as "the extent to which an intervention process could be implemented in another setting" [52] can be recognised in the condition of adaptability and flexibility of the intervention to adjust to the implementation environment.

Scaling up is closely linked to a number of related notions, many of which have been the subject of dedicated work. Although the use of these different terms often remains imprecise and involves some conceptual and methodological confusion, proposals for semantic clarification are nevertheless available in the literature. For example, Chen has worked on the notion of viability, Wang on applicability and transferability, Pluye on sustainability, durability and perpetuation, and Rogers and Dearing on diffusion [6, 47, 51–55]. A lexical study could define the specificities of these different notions and the connections between them.

The typology of factors for success and hindrances identified by this literature review suggests that the intervention to be scaled up should be addressed not merely in terms of the activities it comprises, but rather as an inseparable assortment of operations-contexts-populations-resources. This consideration is in line with the proposal by Cambon et al. [56] to consider the public health intervention as an interventional system defined as "a set of interrelated human and non-human contextual agents within spatial and temporal boundaries generating mechanistic configurations—mechanisms—which are prerequisites for change in health". Public health intervention can be recognised as an interweaving of intervention, population and contextual elements, with all three interacting to produce the desired effects [1]. The scaling up of interventions—as seen in the various works analysed—respects this broader consideration by systematically considering this whole.

A literature review with similar objectives was conducted by Milat et al. [57] in 2013 "The purpose of this literature review was to synthesise evidence on scaling up public health interventions into population-wide policy and practice, with a focus on defining and describing the processes and frameworks that support the scale up of public health initiatives. The review also aimed to identify key success factors and barriers to effective scale up of public health interventions.” While adopting different research methods in terms of the databases explored and the inclusion and exclusion criteria, our two studies arrive at concordant results in terms of the scaling up process, the success factors and the barriers. However, their bodies of literature are only partially superimposable. Each study retained 24 bibliographical references, of which only 6 were common to both literature reviews. The main aspects that differentiate these studies are: i) the keywords used are not exactly the same, ii) the publication period considered: from 1990 to 2013 for the first study versus 2000–2021 for our study, and iii) the inclusion of works on a specific public health topic for the first study, whereas our study only retained generic works on scaling up. It is therefore particularly interesting to note the concordance of the results of these two studies despite the choice of pathways to shed light on the process of scaling up public health interventions. Thus, our study helps to reinforce and confirm the results obtained by Milat et al. in 2013. We believe that it also complements them in several respects. Firstly, by helping to stabilise the concept of scaling up, which, as Milat and his team explain, has many meanings. Our study proposes both to explore this plurality of approaches by describing the different uses identified, and also to identify the skeleton of this notion, shared by the different researchers in the field. We go so far as to propose a new definition of the concept by attempting to embrace the different nuances found in the corpus of literature, as well as a visual schematisation of the notion. We also propose to explore the contexts for which this concept has been borrowed and the reasons for its mobilisation in order to open up the possibility of discussing the foundations and attributes of the concept. With regard to the scaling process and the different phases of work on which it is based, we have set out to provide an overview based on the different proposals. Our work has therefore been guided by a horizontal analysis. This complements the vertical analysis proposed by Milat et al. which provides a description of the stages appearing in each bibliographic reference. In the same way, we propose a synthesis of the success factors and obstacles identified in the entire body of literature of our literature review, around five thematic poles which complete the enumeration of these elements on the basis of their occurrences in the literature as Milat and his colleagues chose to proceed.

Strengths and limitations

Aside from the analytical description of the literature, a number of points should be highlighted. While the literature review was of publications from the last twenty years, we note that 21 of the 24 references selected were published after 2010. Scaling up public health interventions, and more specifically conceptualising and theorising them, is a subject of current interest, and one that has been explored more frequently over the last ten years. It is also clear that there are few authors working on this research subject. The 24 bibliographic references reveal considerable redundancy in the authors involved, meaning that a small number of researchers are responsible for the work in this area. As a result, the review explored the work of pioneers or references in the field over the last two decades, such as Milat, Hanson, Ranson, Bauman, Mills, Redman, Yamey and Nguyen. It therefore reflects the work of relatively few research teams.

The methodology used to conduct this literature review made it possible to define scaling up precisely, as well as the scope of application of the concept, i.e. complex public health interventions. By choosing to limit the lexical field around the concept and retain only the tools or models developed, it was possible to reduce the dispersion of results and identify conceptual, methodological and pedagogical work. This review has therefore focused on very elaborate reflections on the concept of scaling up.

The terminologies used in the corpus of literature to designate the concept of interest to us mainly relate to the notion of "scale" in English or "échelle" in French, with all its ramifications: "scaling up", "scale-up", "scalability", "implementation at scale", "descaling", "process of scale up", "scale-out". The term "generalisation" in its French or English versions is not used in the references analysed, although it was part of the scope of the bibliographic research. It would appear that in the field of public health, the term 'generalisation' and its derivatives are preferred to indicate a transfer of findings, an extension of results, or the ability to predict results from a sample to a larger group (inductive inference) or from a larger group to a sample (deductive inference). The term scaling up involves action or intervention rather than data or information. It therefore refers to the process and means of dissemination to larger territories or populations, which is the subject of this research.

Our methodological choices effectively ruled out works on the same theme that do not use the target terminology. Related concepts such as expansion, dissemination, transferability, perpetuation or sustainability, for example, could be explored and would provide a complement to our review. Finally, by selecting only conceptual or methodological papers, we did not study the practices of researchers in analysing the potential for scaling up public health interventions or in implementing scaling up itself.

In addition, we have opted for a horizontal analysis and presentation of the results of this literature review, summarising the proposals of the various bibliographical references explored in our review. Our aim was to provide an overview of the conceptual and methodological aspects of scaling up complex population health interventions. However, other methods could complement our work, such as a vertical approach, presenting the results reference by reference, or a comparative approach highlighting the similarities and differences between the different bibliographical references studied.

This literature review did not explore the question of the different protagonists in scaling up. The place and role of the population, the actors involved in the intervention, its leaders and designers, the researchers and decision-makers are not specified. This question seems important to us, especially as we have seen that the scaling up of a public health intervention is based on a very wide variety of activities and therefore requires many skills.

A further line of research could be pursued concerning the influence of the complexity of interventions on the scaling-up process. The scaling-up process could be analysed in terms of the complexity of interventions: attributes of intervention complexity and degree of complexity. We could then determine whether, and if so how, the complexity of interventions influences the scaling-up process.

We have already emphasised the importance of handling the scaling up of interventions with caution in order to serve the objective of greater equity in health and avoid widening inequity. This issue could be investigated further by complementary research. To this end, a critical analysis could be carried out, comparing the ambitions or objectives of scaling up with the results of scaling up in terms of health inequitys. A more detailed analysis of the success factors for scaling up in contexts of vulnerability could also be an interesting line of research.

Furthermore, the results of this literature review mainly reflect technical and highly normative aspects of scaling up interventions. It therefore presents a smooth and undifferentiated framework for scaling up.

As for the perimeter of scaling up, it would seem to remain undefined. We have seen that all territorial scales could be concerned, from local to global, provided that the target scale is broader than the scale of origin of the intervention. Depending on the scale in question, the conditions for success, obstacles to scaling up, and scaling up activity could differ.

The decision to scale up is not documented in our literature review. The body of literature does not address the political aspect of this decision much, if at all.

Finally, another limitation of our study is that our review covered documents published up to March 2021, so we may not have taken into account emerging approaches to scaling up complex public health interventions, stemming from work that took place concurrently with or subsequent to our study.

Conclusions

An essential perspective of this work would be to compare the results of the literature review with the practices of the different actors in scaling up, and to document the gaps between theory and application.

Work on the place and role of the population, intervention actors, intervention designers, researchers and decision-makers in the process of scaling up a public health intervention would make it possible to explore the distribution of tasks and skills required to scale up an intervention, the constraints and the common or specific interests of the various protagonists in scaling up.

Work on the different scales could specify the characteristics inherent in each of them with regard to scaling.

Work on the decision-making process, on the constraints on the choice of scaling up an intervention, and on the selection of interventions designated for scaling up would complete the understanding of the phenomenon under real conditions. Similarly, systems for financing public health interventions could be analysed in order to understand the extent to which scaling up of interventions is a real objective or goal.
