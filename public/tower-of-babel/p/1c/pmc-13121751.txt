Polarity-tunable field-free room-temperature spin orbit torque switching via topological symmetry breaking in an all-vdW heterostructure for spin logic applications
Authors: Fan Gao, Zili Wang, Runyu Zhao, Jing Li, Xiaoyue Song, Peiyuan Yu, Yun Sun, Weiran Xie, Guodong Wei, Yuan Yao, Jin Zou, Jie Zhang, Shucheng Xing, Roberto Mantovan, Zongxia Guo, Nicolas Jaouen, Weisheng Zhao, Tianxiao Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo-dimensional ferromagnetic materials hold great promise for advancing low-power, high-integrated spintronic devices due to their atomic flat surfaces and versatile interfacial modulation. However, achieving a combination of room-temperature, field-free spin-orbit torque switching with tunable polarity in wafer-scale vdW heterostructures remains a significant challenge. Here, we demonstrate polarity-tunable, field-free spin-orbit torque switching in an all-vdW Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 heterostructure, grown by molecular beam epitaxy. Interfacial coupling induces perpendicular magnetic anisotropy in Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 interface, while the rest in-plane magnetic anisotropy component of Fe4GeTe2 breaks the inversion symmetry, enabling field-free switching. By modulating the direction of in-plane component, magnetic switching with different polarity could be achieved at low current density (~1.55×106 A/cm2). This allows for 16 reconfigurable Boolean logic functions in a single device, paving a pathway for energy-efficient 2D spintronic memory and logic systems. Our findings highlight the potential of all-vdW spin-orbit torque devices to revolutionize spintronics with scalable, room-temperature electronic control.Subject terms: Magnetic devices, Spintronics, Two-dimensional materials

Introduction

The emergence of spin-orbit torque (SOT) devices offers a transformative platform for constructing energy-efficient, high speed, and nonvolatile memory and logic systems, addressing the growing demands of modern information technology1–4. To enhance the performance of SOT spintronic devices, researchers have conducted extensive investigations based on heavy metals and conventional ferromagnetic material systems5–7. However, the difficulty in achieving smooth interface roughness with traditional materials results in SOT heterostructures exhibiting low spin transparency, which subsequently impacts the power consumption of SOT devices. Recently, the utilization of all-van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures has been regarded as a promising avenue to overcome the bottlenecks of power consumption and physical size limitations in integrated circuits, owing to their significant improvements in device integration and spin transparency at the interfaces8–14, including demonstrations in Bi2Te3/Fe3GeTe2 heterostructure15, (Bi,Sb)2Te3/Fe3GeTe2 heterostructure16, etc. However, an external magnetic field is still needed to break symmetry for deterministic switching, which hinders the integration of SOT devices on semiconductor chips. Therefore, the exploration of all-vdW field-free SOT heterostructures holds significant importance for further improving the performance of SOT devices and advancing their applications.

Recent studies have demonstrated field-free switching using various methods, including asymmetric wedge structures17,18, out-of-plane (OOP) spin currents19, exchange bias20–22, and tilted magnetic anisotropy23. Within all-vdW structures, researchers have combined the unique vdW material WTe₂, which exhibits OOP spin currents, with exfoliated vdW ferromagnetic materials Fe3GeTe2, successfully achieving field-free switching24. Later, by mechanically exfoliating Fe3GaTe225 and pairing it with WTe226 and TaIrTe427, researchers have fabricated heterostructures that enable deterministic field-free switching at room temperature. However, these material systems may face limitations in achieving wafer-scale fabrication of all-vdW structures, which hinders their potential for large-scale applications. Furthermore, these approaches primarily focus on realizing field-free switching through specialized SOT sources, while overlooking the critical role of the ferromagnetic layer in this process. This oversight further diminishes the scalability of SOT spintronic devices.

Previous studies have demonstrated that combining two magnetic layers, PMA and IMA, not only enables the IMA layer to provide an in-plane effective field to assist switching but also allows for further control over the polarity of field-free switching. The tunable polarity of field-free switching, as it enables the realization of complete spin logic within a single nonvolatile memory, holds significant promise in application of spin logic devices28. Therefore, integrating magnetic layers with PMA and IMA into a heterostructure, while ensuring the moderate coupling between the two layers, is crucial for enabling logic functionalities in SOT spintronic devices. In conventional materials, an interlayer with precisely controlled thickness must be inserted between the IMA and PMA layer to ensure that both maintain their magnetic anisotropy29. However, the naturally present vdW gap between monolayers inherently resolved this issue for vdW material systems. Moreover, one of the key features of 2D vdW ferromagnetic materials is that their magnetic properties can be easily modulated through applying external physical fields30. Research has shown that interface engineering can modulate the Curie temperature and magnetic anisotropy of two-dimensional magnetic materials12,31–33. Therefore, it paves a fascinating and convenient way to construct the IMA and PMA coexisted 2D vdW ferromagnet through interface engineering for achieving polarity-tunable and field-free SOT switching12,34. Notably, unlike Fe3GeTe215, Fe4GeTe2 exhibits a uniquely tunable magnetic anisotropy35, enabling the coexistence of PMA and IMA within a single layer. This PMA–IMA coexistence gives rise to an internal symmetry-breaking exchange field, eliminating the need for any external in-plane magnetic field. However, such all-vdW structure has not yet been reported, and SOT devices based on this system hold significant potential for realizing low-power, highly integrated spin logic devices.

Here, we report the polarity-tunable field-free SOT-driven magnetic switching at room temperature in a wafer-scale all-vdW Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 heterostructure grown by molecular beam epitaxy (MBE). The magnetic behavior of the heterostructure could be deconvoluted into the coexistence of IMA and PMA component, in which the IMA component provides an equivalent assisting field for field-free switching. More importantly, the polarity in field-free SOT switching can be flexibly manipulated by modulating the unidirectional IMA layer with a low switching current density of ~1.55 × 106 A/cm2 at 300 K. The PMA in Fe4GeTe2 was confirmed from the interfacial coupling with the Bi2Te3, while the IMA was from its intristinc anisotropy. Micromagnetic simulations revealed the magneto-dynamics process of polarity-controlled SOT switching. Subsequently, based on the device characteristics, we demonstrate 16 reconfigurable Boolean logic functions in a single device. Our results open a new way for the construction of field-free and polarity-controlled all-vdW SOT devices at room temperature and promote the development of 2D spintronic memory and spin logic for practical applications.

Results

In this study, we employed MBE to grow thin films on (0001) sapphire substrates, utilizing reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) for in situ monitoring during the growth process. The result of X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed the high-quality single-crystal character of Bi2Te3(12)/Fe4GeTe2(4) heterostructures shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, in which the number represents the film thickness with unit of nm. The high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) cross-section image in the inset (left) in Fig. 1a distinctly delineates two regions: Bi2Te3 and Fe4GeTe2, with evident a clean interface with no inter-diffusion between the layers and no secondary phases are detected36. Figure 1b presents a typical VSM measurement of M as a function of the OOP magnetic field. The sharp reversal and hysteretic behavior observed around μ0Hz = 0 are indicative of the PMA component. The increase in M at higher field suggests a gradual transition of magnetization from in-plane (IP) to OOP orientation, affirming the presence of an IMA component. This M-H loop distinctly demonstrates the coexistence of both IMA and PMA components within the Fe4GeTe2 layer37. The M-H curve can be decomposed into a hysteresis loop attributable to Fe4GeTe2 (PMA, adjacent to Bi2Te3) and a hard axis loop corresponding to Fe4GeTe2 (IMA, away from Bi2Te3), as shown in the inset. In contrast, the Fe4GeTe2 film, shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, exhibits only IMA at 300 K. Hall bar devices were fabricated using standard photolithography and ion beam etching, with dimensions of 100 μm (length) × 16 μm (width), as shown in Fig. 1c, where Vxx and Vxy represent the longitudinal voltage and Hall voltage, respectively. The Rxx-T curve for the Bi2Te3(12)/Fe4GeTe2(4) device, shown in Fig. 1a, demonstrates a metallic behavior. As comparison, 12 nm Bi2Te3 was also grown on a sapphire substrate, and the magnetotransport results, shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, reveal that the Hall resistance exhibits a negative slope under an OOP magnetic field, confirming the n-type Bi2Te3. To elucidate the magnetic properties of the heterostructure, temperature-dependent magnetic transport measurements are conducted. Figure 1d illustrates the anomalous Hall resistance (Rxy) curves of the Bi2Te3(12)/Fe4GeTe2(4) heterostructure with an OOP magnetic field from 100 to 300 K. Throughout the entire temperature range, the Rxy measurements yielded hysteresis loops with pronounced remanence and relatively sharp switching, and they reach saturation under modest perpendicular magnetic fields. These features clearly indicate the presence of regions where the magnetization vector is perpendicular to the film plane, meaning the existence of components with strong PMA38. Temperature dependence of normalized remnant Rxy and coercivity of Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2, shown in Fig. 1e, the ordinary Hall resistance have been deducted, reveals that both the remnant Rxy and coercivity decrease with increasing temperature. Unlike previously reported IMA in pure Fe4GeTe2 at room temperature, the heterostructure maintains notable PMA feature at room temperature, retaining approximately 70% remnant Rxy and a coercivity of 140 Oe at 300 K. Figure 1f shows the hysteresis loop of Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 with in-plane magnetic field from 150 to 300 K, which confirms its strong PMA characteristic. Leveraging these distinguished properties, we will conduct room-temperature SOT-driven magnetic moment switching based on this material system.

The SOT-driven measurement was conducted in above heterostructures, and a schematic diagram of the dynamics in SOT-driven magnetic switching is illustrated in Fig. 2a. When a writing current (Iwrite) is applied to the Bi2Te3 layer, a spin current is generated, which propagates perpendicularly to the direction of the applied current and is subsequently injected into the adjacent Fe4GeTe2 layer. Once the injected spin current exceeds a critical writing current density, it induces the switching of the magnetization (M) within the Fe4GeTe2 layer through SOT. Here, the writing current density can be defined as Jwrite=Iwrite/[w*(tTI+tFGT)], where w = 16 μm is the width of the Hall bar, tTI and tFGT is the thickness of Bi2Te3 and Fe4GeTe2, respectively. The magnetization of the Fe4GeTe2 layer can tilt up or down under the effective spin-orbit field induced by spin current, generating positive or negative Z components (Mz). For PMA samples with predominantly OOP magnetic components, an additional external magnetic field along the x-axis is required during the measurement process to break symmetry and enable deterministic SOT switching. Therefore, the magnetization switching could be achieved in the Fe4GeTe2 by the charge-spin conversion induced SOT from the current applied in Bi2Te3 with an external x-axis magnetic field.

Room-temperature SOT-induced magnetic switching is critical for the potential application. Hence, the current-driven variations of Rxy under an external magnetic field Hx along the x-axis are shown in Fig. 2d, e, where the Hx alternates with the sequence from positive to negative and negative back to positive, respectively. As the applied write current sweeps from positive to negative, obvious magnetization switching behavior of Fe4GeTe2 could be observed, and the polarity of deterministic switching is significantly affected by the direction of the in-plane external field. Notably, the switching amplitude varies with the applied in-plane field. The maximum switching ratios, calculated from the data in Fig. 2d, e, are approximately 22% for field-assisted switching and 16.8% for field-free switching. These values are consistent with reported SOT efficiencies in other two-dimensional magnetic systems (e.g., ~20%42) and reflect the practical challenge of achieving full magnetization reversal in the two-dimensional limit. Interestingly, careful examination could find that field-free switching could be achieved with its polarity adjusted by the sequence of the external field. For instance, as the external field was applied from positive to negative, as shown in Fig. 2d, the switching shows a Up to Down polarity at positive external field. When the applied field decreases to 0, the polarity of field-free switching maintains the same characteristic as the initial condition and does not change with the applied field reversed to a relatively small value. However, when the magnetic field exceeds a certain threshold, the switching polarity of the device changes, becoming dominated by the direction of the external field. Vice versa, a Down to Up polarity field-free switching was observed, when the applied field was applied from negative to positive, as shown in Fig. 2e. Such polarity-tunable field-free switching behavior may arise from the unique magnetic configuration combing both IMA and PMA components together in the heterostructure. The IMA component breaks the symmetry during the switching process, which facilitates the field-free switching. In addition, it could be calculated that the critical switching current density in the Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 at 300 K is ~1.55 × 106 A/cm2, which is significantly smaller than that in the FGT/Pt heterostructure42, and it corroborates the high charge-spin conversion efficiency in TI non-trivial origin and the high-quality interfaces of our sample15. Figure 2f further elaborates on polarity-tunable field-free switching observed at different temperatures (250, 270, 280, and 300 K), showcasing the device’s ability to switch magnetization without an external magnetic field.

Research in the previous section has confirmed that the polarity of field-free switching in the Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 heterostructure is determined by the direction of the initially applied in-plane magnetic field. To further explore the effects of the direction and magnitude of this preset field (Hpre) on field-free SOT switching, the pulse current was applied under zero field condition following initialization with different Hpre values and the switching behaviors are shown in Fig. 3a. To prevent any remanent magnetic field effects, the sample was physically removed from the magnet for each field-free measurement. The results demonstrate that field-free switching cannot be achieved without the initialization provided by the preset magnetic field. Specifically, in the absence of a preset magnetic field, the device resistance remains largely unchanged even as the write current increases, indicating the absence of deterministic magnetization switching. However, when the preset field reaches 0.5 T, distinct field-free switching is observed. Furthermore, as the preset field is progressively increased to 1, 1.5, and 2 T, the switching polarity remains unchanged. Critically, when the direction of the preset field is reversed (e.g., increased to −0.5 T), the switching polarity also reverses. Similarly, increasing the magnitude of the negative preset field does not alter this reversed switching polarity. Therefore, the preset magnetic field not only enables field-free switching but also controls its polarity. Furthermore, the switching amplitude after applying a + 2 T preset magnetic field is slightly smaller than that after a –2 T preset field, a phenomenon observed at both 250 and 270 K (Fig. S6). The underlying mechanism of this phenomenon lies in the difference in the magnitude of the interlayer exchange coupling field under the two opposite preset field directions. This difference originates from the canted layers situated between our PMA and IMA components that are insensitive to the in-plane magnetic field and their orientation is defined by the OOP magnetic field. To clarify this, we performed comparative experiments, and the results are shown in Fig. S7.

To further investigate the effect of the preset field on SOT switching amplitude, we performed field-free magnetization switching under different preset fields at 250, 270, and 300 K, and the results are shown in Fig. 3b. At room temperature, the switching amplitude under a −2 T preset field increases 2 times compared to that at −0.5 T preset field. And, this trend was maintained in other temperatures. To further examine the effect of higher preset field on switching behavior, we performed SOT switching at 250 K with the preset field of −2 and −3 T, and the results are shown in Fig. 3c. It is noteworthy that the shape of the magnetization reversal curves is nearly identical under both conditions, indicating that once the preset field exceeds a critical threshold, additional increases have negligible impact on the switching window. The summary results of the switching amplitude are shown in Fig. 3d, clearly demonstrating the switching amplitude kept enlarging with higher preset field across different temperatures. We summarized the switching ratio (Rswitch/RAHE) versus preset magnetic field at 300 K, as shown in Fig. S8. According to calculations, the switching ratio increases with increasing preset in-plane magnetic field and the maximum value of the switching ratio is around 25% at 300 K. Additionally, to evaluate the device’s resilience to external perturbations, reverse magnetic fields were applied after establishing the preset field. Figure 3e demonstrates that as the reverse magnetic field increases, the switching window progressively narrows and ultimately disappears at 0.6 T. This highlights the excellent anti-interference capability of the preset-field-induced magnetic state, reinforcing the critical role of the preset field in enabling stable, field-free switching.

As mentioned above, the magnetic behavior of the Bi2Te3(12)/Fe4GeTe2(4) heterostructure exhibited coexistence of IMA and PMA components. To understand such fascinating phenomena, different layer configurations: Fe4GeTe2(4), Bi2Te3(8)/Fe4GeTe2(4), and Bi2Te3(12)/Fe4GeTe2(6) were fabricated, and anomalous Hall resistance measurements were performed for magnetic analysis. Figure 4a shows the results of 4 nm Fe4GeTe2, which exhibits a temperature-dependent transition from PMA to IMA with increasing temperature, consistent with previous reports31. In contrast, the Bi2Te3(8)/Fe4GeTe2(4), as shown in Fig. 4b, maintain the same strong PMA characteristics across all temperatures as Bi2Te3(12)/Fe4GeTe2(4) heterostructures in Fig. 1d, which highlights the effect of Bi2Te3 for enhancing the PMA property in Fe4GeTe2. Furthermore, when the Fe4GeTe2 layer is thickened (Bi2Te3(12)/Fe4GeTe2(6)), the anisotropy gradually shifts towards IMA, indicating a weakening of the PMA, as shown in Fig. 4c. In addition, as shown in Fig. S9, the results from the X-ray Magnetic Resonance Reflectivity (XRMR) measurements indicate that the distribution of IMA and PMA is related to the distance from Bi2Te3, specifically their spatial distribution along the Z-axis. The possibility that the observed magnetic behavior originates from the Bi2Te3 layer is ruled out. Fe doping into Bi2Te3 has been reported to induce only weak ferromagnetism below 14 K, with no observable magnetic order at room temperature43. Regarding the proximity effect, previous study indicates that the resulting Curie temperatures are typically very low44. Even in the limited number of reports claiming a Curie temperature near 300 K45, the induced magnetic moment remains orders of magnitude smaller than that of intrinsic magnetic materials. Therefore, neither Fe doping nor proximity-induced magnetism in Bi2Te3 can account for the room-temperature, robust magnetic anisotropy in this work. These observations strongly suggest that the topological insulator interface contributes to the enhancement of PMA in Fe4GeTe2 itself, giving rise to the coexistence of PMA and IMA components with no evidence of measurable magnetic contribution from the Bi2Te3 layer. It has been reported that the PMA of magnetic films containing iron atoms, such as Y3Fe5O12 and Tm3Fe5O12, can be greatly strengthened by the adjacent TI layer46,47. These changes are due to the topological surface states (TSS), which can strengthen exchange coupling between Fe ions. Furthermore, theoretical calculations show that modifying the chemical potential of topological insulators can effectively tune the magnetic anisotropy of ferromagnets48. For our experiments, we propose that the enhanced PMA may originate from the strengthened exchange coupling between Fe ions in Fe4GeTe2 mediated by the TSS electrons of the Bi2Te3. As shown in Fig. S10, we have also ruled out the influence of strain on the magnetic anisotropy of Fe4GeTe249.

Subsequently, we conducted micromagnetic simulations to reveal the dynamics of the polarity-tunable field-free switching, and these simulations were performed using Mumax3. The above results indicate that Bi2Te3 introduced a PMA component into Fe4GeTe2 at 300 K. In addition, it was noted earlier that the Fe4GeTe2 layer in the heterostructure contains both IMA and PMA components at this temperature. Therefore, we constructed a three-layer magnetic model to simulate the magnetic distribution of the experimental samples, as shown in Fig. 4d. Due to interfacial engineering, the layer adjacent to the topological insulator exhibits dominant PMA, while the top layer transitions into IMA.

Based on this model, the IMA layer serves as a symmetry-breaking element required for deterministic switching of the PMA layer through interlayer exchange coupling50, while the direction of the in-plane magnetic anisotropy determines the polarity of the field-free switching. As shown in Fig. 4e, we initially set the magnetization of the IMA layer by applying an external in-plane magnetic field in two opposite directions. Following the removal of this field, current-induced switching measurements revealed distinct hysteresis loops, indicating two opposing polarities depending on the initial magnetization state. The important switching points during field-free switching under initial positive and negative 1 T preset magnetic field are marked in the figure, where TA and TE represent the starting and ending points, corresponding to the positions of the maximum positive pulse current, TB and TD represent the magnetic moment switching points, while TC corresponds to the maximum negative pulse current. The magnetic moment schematics of the three important switching points during field-free switching (TA, TC, and TE) are shown in Fig. S11. We observe that when the magnetization direction of the in-plane layer is reversed, the polarity of the PMA layer, which is the main source of the anomalous Hall resistance, also reverses under the same current polarity. This demonstrates that the effective field generated by the multilayer magnetic structure itself is the origin of the polarity-tunable field-free switching. In addition, the inset of Fig. 4e shows field-free switching after applying 0.5 and 1 T preset magnetic fields, and a comparison of the two curves shows that the switching amplitude increases with increasing preset magnetic field. This may be due to the fact that the in-plane magnetic moments of the ferromagnetic layers become more homogeneous as the preset magnetic field increases, resulting in an increase in the strength of the interlayer exchange coupling and hence a larger equivalent field. Figure 4f displays the time-resolved evolution of the in-plane magnetization component throughout the magnetization switching process, the mx under a −1 T preset magnetic field exhibits a marked enhancement compared to the −0.5 T condition, thereby generating a stronger equivalent field to facilitate magnetization switching. In addition, the results reveal that the applied pulse current is insufficient to change the polarity of the in-plane magnetization component, allowing the in-plane layer to stably provide an effective field that facilitates field-free switching.

Reconfigurable logic design offers advantages such as enhanced hardware resource utilization, reduced transistor resource consumption, and more. Below, we demonstrate the implementation of 16 reconfigurable Boolean logic functions in a single Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 heterostructure device. In this scheme, the preset magnetic field and input currents are defined as binary logic inputs. The magnetization strength of Fe4GeTe2 during the writing process is characterized by different magnetic moment states, and the final output is represented by the anomalous Hall voltage. High and low voltages correspond to logical outputs “1” and “0”, respectively.

The device structure is shown in Fig. 5a. Owing to the interfacial engineering, the Fe4GeTe2 layer adjacent to Bi2Te3 exhibits PMA properties (lower Fe4GeTe2), while the other layers maintain IMA properties (upper Fe4GeTe2).

The device operates with four binary inputs: one preset magnetic field (H) and three input currents (IA, IB, IC) applied along the x-direction. The logic output “L” is determined by the anomalous Hall voltage. As shown in Fig. 5b, each input can assume only two distinct states, representing the binary logic values “0” and “1”, with the specific configuration for the NAND function indicated in parentheses. It should be noted that the absolute values of current IA and the sum of currents IB and IC are each 15 mA. This current magnitude is chosen to ensure the switching of the PMA components in Fe4GeTe2.

Figure 5c illustrates the entire process of the NAND logic operation in the device for different inputs. As shown in Fig. 5b, four distinct states are involved in one logic operation and the inputs H, A(IA), B(IB), and C(IC) are set to “1”, “p”, “q”, and “0”, respectively. In this setup, “p” and “q” represent two input variables of the Boolean logic gates (each being “0” or “1”) for the logic operation, while H and C are held constant at “1” and “0”, respectively. Specifically, in the initial state, the Fe4GeTe2 magnetization is uncertain, with the upper Fe4GeTe2 magnetic moment along either the +x or −x direction, and the lower Fe4GeTe2 magnetic moment oriented along either the +z or −z direction. In the second state (step 1), when a 1 T magnetic field of H (H = 1) is applied along the +x axis, the upper Fe4GeTe2 magnetization is set to +x direction, following the Up to Down polarity of field-free SOT switching. Thus, in the third state (step 2), when IA is set to −15 mA (A = p = 0), the magnetization is aligned along the +z direction, corresponding to logic output L = 1. Conversely, when IA is set to +15 mA (A = p = 1), the magnetization is directed towards the −z direction, resulting in a logic output L = 0. In the fourth state (step3), IB and IC are applied simultaneously. Since IC is fixed at −7 mA (C = 0) in the NAND logic function, the total current applied in the third step is −15 mA (with B = q = 0) or 1 mA (with B = q = 1). For the −15 mA (B = q = 0, C = 0) case, the magnetization is switched to the +z direction, giving Boolean results of p = 0, q = 0, L = 1 and p = 1, q = 0, L = 1, as shown in the first and third columns of Fig. 5c. For the 1 mA (B = q = 1, C = 0) case, the magnetization remains aligned as in the third state (step 2), because the current is insufficient to switch the magnetization. Thus, it will give Boolean results of p = 0, q = 1, L = 1 and p = 1, q = 1, L = 0, as shown in the second and fourth columns of Fig. 5c. The implementation of the remaining 15 Boolean logic functions is depicted in Supplementary Figs. S13–S17.

In summary, the single Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 device is capable of implementing the complete set of 16 Boolean logic functions. This is achieved by systematically varying the input preset magnetic field H and the three input currents IA, IB, and IC in three sequential steps. The ability to fully control spin logic electrically within a single nonvolatile memory device paves the way for energy-efficient, compact, and adaptable spintronic devices.

In conclusion, we have successfully fabricated wafer-scale vdW Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 heterostructure by MBE and achieved polarity-tunable field-free current-driven SOT switching at room temperature. With an exceptionally high SOT efficiency of ~0.8 at 300 K, Bi2Te3 enables highly energy-efficient magnetic switching, achieving a critical switching current density as low as ~1.55 × 106 A/cm2 in the Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 heterostructure. Through interfacial exchange coupling effect between Bi2Te3 and Fe4GeTe2, the ferromagnet layer shows both IMA and PMA components, leading to controllable magnetic switching polarity depending on direction of the IMA component. In addition, the effect of preset magnetic field on field-free switching is experimentally demonstrated and verified by micromagnetic simulations. Based on the device characteristics, we demonstrate the practical application of polarity-controlled switching by realizing 16 reconfigurable Boolean logic functions in a single device. Our results provide the feasibility for the construction of all-electric, room-temperature vdW SOT devices and promote the development of 2D spintronic memory and spin logic system for future practical applications.

Methods

Sample growth

The samples were prepared by MBE system, and the films were grown on (0001) sapphire substrates. The substrates were annealed at 600 °C for 1 h prior to growth to obtain a clean surface, followed by cooling to 220 °C. High-purity Bi, Fe, Ge, and Te were evaporated from standard Knudsen cells, and the growth rate was ~3 nm/min. Crystal quality was monitored in situ by RHEED during growth. After growth, Te was deposited on the films as the protection layer.

Crystal characterizations

The phase and crystal structure characterization of Bi2Te3/Fe4GeTe2 heterostructure were characterised by XRD and TEM. XRD analysis was performed using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO MRD diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation. Cross-section TEM sample prepared by focused ion beam, then a JEOL-ARM 200 F Cold Field Emission Gun TEM was employed to examine the atomic structure of the samples.

Electrical measurements

Hall bar devices were fabricated by standard lithography and ion beam etching, achieving Hall bar dimensions of 100 μm (length) × 16 μm (width). The electrical measurements were carried out in Cryogenic physical property measurement system.
