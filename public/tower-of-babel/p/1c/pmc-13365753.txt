Oral health-related quality of life, food satisfaction and eating behaviours among community-dwelling older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Amani Alhazmi, Maha Ali, Manal Mohammed Hawash, Ali Mohieldin, Mahmoud Abdelwahab Khedr, Mona Metwally EL-Sayed, Wagida Wafik, Hala Awad, Eman Mahmoud Mohammed Shouker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe socio-demographic and health-related characteristics, eating behaviours, food satisfaction and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) among community-dwelling older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia; to assess how eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL vary across socio-demographic and health-related groups; and to examine how eating behaviours and food satisfaction are associated with OHRQoL, adjusting for socio-demographic and health-related characteristics.DesignA descriptive cross-sectional study.SettingPrimary healthcare centres (PHCCs) in the Abha district of the Asir region, Saudi Arabia.ParticipantsA total of 350 older adults aged 60+ were recruited from PHCCs in Abha, Asir Region, Saudi Arabia, using a non-probability convenience sampling.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome measure was OHRQoL, assessed using the Geriatric Oral Health Assessment Index. Secondary outcome measures included food satisfaction, measured by the Satisfaction with Food-Related Life scale and eating behaviours, assessed using the Eating Behaviours Scale–Short Form.ResultsMost participants showed fair-to-good eating behaviours (86.6%), moderate-to-high food satisfaction (87.7%) and fair OHRQoL (69.7%). Bivariate analyses revealed that food satisfaction was significantly positively associated with OHRQoL (r=0.517, p<0.001) and weakly negatively associated with eating behaviours (r=–0.185, p<0.001). Multiple linear regression analysis identified food satisfaction as the factor most strongly associated with OHRQoL (β=0.496, p<0.001), followed by oral care practices (β=0.180, p<0.001) and eating behaviours (β=0.177, p=0.001).ConclusionFood satisfaction showed a strong association with OHRQoL, highlighting the psychological dimension of dietary experiences in later life. Although many participants reported favourable eating behaviours and food satisfaction, OHRQoL remained moderate. These findings emphasise the need for integrated, culturally tailored interventions that incorporate nutritional counselling, oral-health promotion and chronic disease management to optimise healthy ageing in this population.

Introduction

The global population is undergoing a significant demographic shift, with the number of individuals aged 60 years and older steadily increasing. By 2050, older adults are projected to constitute more than 22% of the global population, up from approximately 14% in 2020.1 The proportion of older adults aged 65 and over is projected to steadily increase over the next few decades, reaching approximately 18.4% of Saudi Arabia’s total population by 2050.2 This demographic transition, while indicative of advances in healthcare and socioeconomic development, presents urgent challenges for ensuring the quality of life (QoL) of ageing populations.1 3 Among these challenges, nutrition, food satisfaction and oral health emerge as critical yet often interconnected factors that can negatively affect overall health outcomes in later life. The growing complexity of these interrelationships highlights the importance of examining them together rather than in isolation, particularly in rapidly ageing societies.46

Oral health status among older adults is frequently impaired by cumulative dental issues, including tooth loss, periodontal disease, xerostomia and oral cancer, all of which can profoundly affect daily functioning and overall well-being.7 These conditions often result in impaired masticatory function, which in turn limits food choices and diminishes the pleasure of eating, thereby negatively influencing nutritional status and overall QoL, a concept captured by oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), which refers specifically to the impact of oral health on daily functioning, comfort and social well-being.710 Evidence from Saudi Arabia indicates that inadequate oral healthcare utilisation and diminished oral health status are common among older adults and are linked to poor dietary practices and reduced OHRQoL.8 9 Given that oral health directly affects daily functioning, especially the ability to chew and enjoy food, understanding eating behaviours among older adults is crucial for evaluating nutritional risk and overall health outcomes.11 12

Eating behaviours play a central role in maintaining physical and psychological health among older adults. As individuals age, physiological changes, including decreased metabolic rate, altered taste and smell perception, reduced gastrointestinal function and dental issues, can significantly affect dietary patterns.11 12 These changes, along with comorbidities and polypharmacy, are often associated with reduced appetite, selective eating, inadequate nutrient intake and malnutrition.12 13 Malnutrition in older adults is a widespread concern, linked to increased morbidity, frailty, reduced immune function and higher hospitalisation rates.13 14 Importantly, these very eating behaviours, particularly reduced appetite, selective eating and inadequate nutrient intake, can serve as significant risk factors for diminished food satisfaction, creating a cyclical relationship where poor eating behaviours undermine the subjective enjoyment and contentment derived from meals.12 13 15

Alongside eating behaviours, food satisfaction, defined as the subjective contentment with the food one consumes, has gained recognition as a crucial factor of nutritional well-being.11 16 Food satisfaction encompasses various dimensions, including taste, variety, cultural appropriateness, mealtime environment and the psychological and social experiences associated with eating.1517 In older adults, food satisfaction has been linked not only to dietary intake but also to emotional health, social connectedness and overall QoL.1416 Lower food satisfaction has been associated with poorer dietary adherence, undernutrition and reduced psychosocial well-being, particularly among individuals living alone.4 15 Because food satisfaction reflects both emotional and cultural dimensions of eating, it plays an especially important role in regions with strong culinary traditions, such as Saudi Arabia’s Asir region.

This emphasis on food satisfaction is particularly relevant when considering OHRQoL, as higher levels of food satisfaction have been shown to positively influence dietary quality, nutritional status and overall well-being, factors that are closely intertwined with oral health outcomes.9 18 19 Several factors are associated with OHRQoL among older adults, including age-related declines and oral conditions such as tooth loss, xerostomia, periodontal disease and ill-fitting dentures, which can significantly impair chewing ability and limit food choices, thereby affecting OHRQoL.5 15 20 Psychosocial factors such as loneliness, limited meal interactions, oral discomfort and restricted access to dental care can worsen oral health, nutritional risks and OHRQoL.4 5 Collectively, these factors establish a bidirectional relationship in which poor oral health adversely affects eating behaviours and food satisfaction, while suboptimal eating behaviours and low food satisfaction may further contribute to declines in OHRQoL.9 21 22 These interconnected factors, including eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL, create a complex cycle conceptually guided by the Wilson and Cleary Model of Health-Related QoL, which posits that individual and clinical characteristics influence functional health, symptom perception and overall QoL outcomes.23 In the current study, socio-demographic and health-related characteristics were conceptualised as associated factors of eating behaviours and food satisfaction, which may, in turn, be associated with OHRQoL among older adults (figure 1).

Although research on oral health and nutrition among older adults has increased in Saudi Arabia, most previous studies have focused on isolated aspects, such as factors associated with OHRQoL21 or dietary practices9 independently. While these studies provide important insights into oral health or nutrition separately, they offer a limited understanding of how eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL interact simultaneously among older adults. This gap was also highlighted by Tounsi, who noted that research examining the interplay among these variables remains scarce.8 To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have examined these relationships among community-dwelling older adults in Saudi Arabia, particularly in Abha, or have used a conceptual framework to explain the interrelationships among these variables. Therefore, investigating these interrelationships may provide valuable insights for developing integrated and culturally tailored geriatric care interventions for nutrition, psychosocial well-being and oral health simultaneously, particularly within the Saudi context.9 21 22

To address this gap, the present study aimed to investigate OHRQoL, food satisfaction and eating behaviours among community-dwelling older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia. The specific objectives of this study were threefold: (1) to describe socio-demographic and health-related characteristics, eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL among community-dwelling older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia; (2) to assess how eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL vary across socio-demographic and health-related groups; and (3) to examine how eating behaviours and food satisfaction are associated with OHRQoL, adjusting for socio-demographic and health-related characteristics.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional design to examine the relationships among eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL among older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist guidelines.24 Data were collected from June through October 2025.

Study setting

The research was conducted in four primary healthcare centres (PHCCs) in the Abha district of the Asir region, Saudi Arabia, namely Almwzafeen, AlAzizya, Almansak and Almanhel. These centres were selected to ensure representation of both urban and peri-urban areas within Abha. The Ministry of Health oversees these PHCCs, which serve people of all ages. They offer a range of services, including preventive care, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation for various health concerns. Furthermore, they offer specialised programmes in nutritional counselling to help individuals maintain a healthy, balanced diet.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated based on the Cohen f² method for multiple linear regression, based on the F-test for the overall model and previous studies.9 10 25 The formula used was: n = (Z_(1 − α/2) + Z_(1 − β))² / f² + k + 1, where n is the required sample size, Z_(1 − α/2) is the standard normal deviate for the chosen significance level (two-tailed), Z_(1 − β) is the standard normal deviate for the desired power, f² is Cohen’s effect size for multiple regression (f² = R² / (1 − R²)) and k is the number of factor variables. Assuming a conservative small to medium effect size (f²=0.10), up to 16 factors, a significance level of α=0.05 and a desired power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was 323 participants. To account for potential non-response or incomplete data, this number was increased by 10%; the final target sample size was set at 350 older adults. This ensured adequate statistical power to detect meaningful associations between eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL while maintaining methodological rigour.26 27

Study population, sampling and recruitment process

The study participants were community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years or older residing in the Asir region of Saudi Arabia, including both urban and rural settings. Exclusion criteria were applied to maintain data validity and participant suitability. Individuals diagnosed with cognitive impairments (eg, dementia) or severe psychiatric conditions (eg, major depressive disorder) that could hinder their ability to comprehend or respond accurately to the questionnaire were excluded. Additionally, non-residents or temporary visitors to the Asir region who were unwilling or unable to provide informed consent were excluded. A non-probability convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants. Recruitment took place at four selected PHCCs in Abha, where two trained researchers specialised in public health and gerontological nursing approached older adults attending routine health services. Invitations were extended to approximately 360 older adults. All potential participants underwent initial screening using the Saint Louis University Mental Status Examination (SLUMS) and the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) to assess cognitive function and depressive symptoms (online supplemental file 1). Following screening, three participants were excluded due to depressive symptoms (BDI-II score >20)28 and four were excluded due to cognitive impairment (SLUMS scores indicating possible dementia).29 An additional three participants declined to participate. To ensure voluntary participation, all remaining individuals provided written informed consent. The final sample comprised 350 older adults, yielding a response rate of 97.5% and a dropout rate of 2.5% (figure 2).

Pilot study and survey development

A pilot study involving 40 older adults from the target population was conducted prior to full-scale data collection to assess the feasibility, clarity and cultural appropriateness of the research instruments (Eating Behaviours Scale–Short Form (EBS-SF), Satisfaction with Food-Related Life scale (SWFL) and Geriatric Oral Health Assessment Index Taiwanese Version (GOHAI-T)).30 Participants were recruited from the same PHCCs in Abha to ensure demographic comparability with the main study sample. The pilot study evaluated questionnaire comprehension, completion time and administrative feasibility. No modifications were made to the instruments following the pilot study, as participants demonstrated a clear understanding of all items, and the instruments were considered culturally appropriate and feasible for use in the main study. Pilot participants were excluded from the final analysis. The final survey was developed by compiling the validated instruments and socio-demographic and health-related questions into a single structured questionnaire (online supplemental file 2). The translation process followed standard forward–backward translation procedures. Initially, two independent bilingual researchers translated the original English questionnaire into Arabic. The two translated versions were compared and reconciled into a single Arabic version through discussion among the research team. Subsequently, a separate bilingual translator, blinded to the original English version, independently back-translated the Arabic questionnaire into English to ensure conceptual equivalence and accuracy. Any discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were reviewed and resolved by the research team. The final Arabic version was then reviewed by a panel of five experts in geriatric nursing, nutrition and public health to assess cultural appropriateness, clarity and content validity prior to administration in the main study.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by two trained researchers, a public health specialist and a gerontological nursing specialist, both of whom had PhDs and were employed at King Khalid University. Prior to data collection, the researchers underwent a 2-day training session conducted by the principal investigator. The training covered the study protocol, proper administration of all instruments (EBS-SF, SWFL, GOHAI-T, SLUMS and BDI-II), techniques for obtaining informed consent, ethical considerations and strategies for maintaining participant confidentiality. Role-playing scenarios were used to practice interview techniques and ensure consistency in data collection procedures. The two trained researchers first conducted initial screening instruments using the SLUMS and the BDI-II to exclude older adults with notable cognitive problems or depression. Eligible participants then engaged in private, one-on-one interviews at the selected PHCCs. All interviews were conducted in Arabic using paper-based questionnaires and lasted 15–20 min. Before each interview, researchers explained the study’s purpose and obtained written informed consent from each participant. Participants were reassured that their involvement was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. The data were carefully reviewed for completeness and accuracy, with strict confidentiality measures in place. No missing data were found for these variables, as all items were completed during face-to-face interviews. No incentives were provided to emphasise the voluntary nature of participation.

Data collection measures

Control variables: socio-demographic, lifestyle, clinical and oral health-related characteristics data sheet

This sheet was developed by the researchers, which includes participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviours, clinical conditions and oral health-related factors. Socio-demographic variables included age, gender, marital status, educational level, monthly household income, place of residence and living arrangements. Lifestyle-related factors included smoking status and dietary pattern. Clinical characteristics included chronic illness and denture use. Oral health-related factors covered dental care utilisation, oral hygiene practices and sleeping with dentures (online supplemental file 2).

Key predictor 1: Eating behaviors

Eating behaviours were assessed using the EBS-SF. This short form is a validated psychometric tool, initially developed by The short-form version (EBS-SF) consists of 29 items that measure key dimensions, including irregular meal timing, emotional eating, snack consumption, food preferences and eating motivations. This short form is a validated psychometric tool, initially developed by Sakata et al. (2003), to assess dietary patterns and eating behaviours.31 Responses are recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree to 4=strongly agree), with higher total scores (range: 29–116) indicating poorer eating behaviours. The categories are eating rhythm abnormalities (questions 1–5), the feeling of satiety (questions 6–10), eating style (questions 11–12), cognition of constitution (questions 13–15), meal contents (questions 16–20), substitute eating and drinking (questions 21–26) and motivation for eating (questions 27–29), with total scores ranging from 29 to 116. Eating behaviour scale scores were categorised based on standard cut-off points: good (29–57), fair (58–86) and poor (87–116), where higher scores indicate poorer eating behaviours. The scale has demonstrated strong reliability in Arabic studies, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89, confirming its internal consistency.32 Additionally, the Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.946 in the current study indicates excellent reliability.

Key predictor 2: Food satisfaction

Food satisfaction was assessed using the SWFL. This scale is a validated psychometric instrument developed by Grunert et al to assess individuals’ cognitive and affective evaluation of their overall food-related experiences.33 This concise 5-item scale measures global satisfaction with aspects of food in life, including meal enjoyment, food availability and dietary contentment. Participants respond using a 6-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree to 6=strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 5 to 30. Food satisfaction scores were categorised using standard cut-offs: low (5–14), moderate (15–22) and high (23–30). The SWFL has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across diverse populations. The scale showed a high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.80 to 0.90.34 After translating into Arabic, the scale retained high validity, with factor loadings ranging from 0.58 to 0.89, increasing to 0.70 to 0.94 following varimax rotation and accounted for 78.35% of the variance. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was excellent (0.950), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p≤0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for factor analysis. All items were retained in the Arabic version, and Cronbach’s alpha of 0.860 in the current study indicates acceptable reliability.

Main outcome: Oral health-related QoL

OHRQoL was assessed using the GOHAI-T. This index is a culturally adapted, validated instrument derived from the original GOHAI developed by Atchison and Dolan.35 This 12-item questionnaire assesses OHRQoL in older adults explicitly across three key domains: physical function (eating, speaking, swallowing), psychosocial impact (self-consciousness, social interaction) and pain/discomfort symptoms. Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1=always to 5=never), with total scores ranging from 12 to 60. OHRQoL scores were categorised based on standard cut-off points: poor (12–50), fair (51–56) and good (57–60), where higher scores indicate better OHRQoL. The Arabic version of the GOHAI-T was validated by Atieh and showed a strong reliability with a Cronbach’s α of 0.93.36 Furthermore, the current study’s Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.865 confirms good reliability.

Validity and reliability

The study employed rigorous methods to establish the validity and reliability of the measurement instruments. For content validity, a panel of five experts in geriatric nursing, nutrition and public health evaluated the cultural appropriateness and relevance of the Arabic-translated versions of the EBS-SF, SWFL and GOHAI-T scales. Their feedback led to minor modifications to the wording to better suit the Saudi cultural context while preserving the original constructs. Construct validity was assessed through exploratory factor analysis, which confirmed the expected unidimensional structure of the SWFL scale and the three-domain structure (physical, psychosocial, pain/discomfort) of the GOHAI-T. The KMO measure exceeded 0.95 for all scales, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p<0.001), indicating adequate sampling adequacy for factor analysis. Reliability was evaluated through internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients demonstrated excellent reliability for all scales: EBS-SF (α=0.88), SWFL (α=0.87) and GOHAI-T (α=0.93).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the early stages of the research, including the identification of the research question, study design or selection of outcome measures. The research questions and outcomes were developed by the research team based on existing literature and validated assessment tools, rather than being informed by patient or public priorities, experiences or preferences. Patients were not involved in the study design. They were first involved in the data collection stage, during which eligible participants underwent individual, face-to-face interviews at primary healthcare centres after cognitive and depression screening and after providing informed consent. Participants contributed data through questionnaire responses but were not involved in decisions regarding recruitment strategies, study procedures or assessment of participation burden or time requirements. Patients and the public were not involved in the analysis or interpretation of the data, nor in the dissemination of the study findings. Results will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed publication, and no specific plans were made to involve participants or wider patient communities in deciding what results to share or how they should be communicated.

Data analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics V.27 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; all continuous variables were approximately normally distributed (p>0.05), permitting parametric testing. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means and SDs) were generated to characterise the sample and describe eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL levels. For group comparisons, eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL were analysed as continuous outcomes (mean scores). Associations between socio-demographic, clinical and oral health-related factors and each outcome (eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL) were examined using independent-samples t-tests for binary variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for variables with three or more categories. For significant ANOVA results, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test (equal variances assumed) or Games-Howell test (equal variances not assumed, based on Levene’s test). Prior to calculating Pearson correlations, linearity was assessed visually by inspecting scatterplots for each pair of continuous variables. Pearson correlations were then calculated to examine bivariate relationships among the three key variables. A multiple linear regression model was used to assess whether eating behaviours and food satisfaction were associated with OHRQoL. In this regression, OHRQoL was treated as a continuous dependent variable, with eating behaviours and food satisfaction as the primary independent variables, while controlling for age, gender, education, income, chronic illness, smoking, denture use, dentist follow-up, oral care behaviour and diet pattern. Assumption checks included linearity, independence of residuals (Durbin-Watson=1.89), homoscedasticity, absence of multicollinearity (Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) <5) and normality of residuals (Shapiro-Wilk p=0.082). Regression results were reported with unstandardised coefficients (B), standardised coefficients (β), t values, R² and F statistics. Instrument reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, and construct validity was examined through exploratory factor analysis with Varimax rotation, preceded by KMO and Bartlett’s tests. A significance threshold of p<0.05 (two-tailed) was applied.

Results

Descriptive analyses and bivariate associations among socio-demographic and health-related characteristics, eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL

As shown in table 1, all 350 participants (100.0%) were Arab. Half of the participants (50.0%) were aged 60–69 years. Females comprised 52.3% of the sample, and 63.1% reported sufficient income. Most participants were married (61.1%), resided in rural areas (65.7%), lived with family (76.3%), were non-people who do not smoke (85.4%) and consumed an ordinary diet (59.4%). Half had a chronic illness (50.0%). Regarding oral health, 67.7% did not use dentures, 47.7% visited the dentist only when a problem occurred, 42.0% practised daily oral hygiene with toothpaste and 84.3% did not sleep with dentures. This table also shows that age was not significantly associated with any outcome, though eating behaviours (F=2.99, p=0.050) and food satisfaction (F=3.01, p=0.050) showed borderline significance with no pairwise differences. Males scored higher than females in eating behaviours (70.55±8.03 vs 63.01±6.97; t=4.52, p<0.01). Education was significant for all outcomes: for eating behaviours, basic education (76.06±14.80) and illiterate participants (74.86±15.65) scored higher than those who could read/write (61.03±16.84); for food satisfaction, the read/write group (21.55±4.58) reported higher satisfaction than illiterate (19.02±5.86; mean difference (MD)=2.53, p<0.01) and university-educated participants (18.00±4.93; MD=3.55, p<0.01); for OHRQoL, read/write (38.85±6.71) and secondary-educated participants (38.41±6.84) had better scores than illiterate participants (33.82±10.61). Divorced participants reported higher food satisfaction than widowed participants (20.95±4.78 vs 19.52±5.25; MD=5.71, p<0.01). Sufficient income yielded higher scores across eating behaviours (67.52±17.96 vs 63.75±17.52; t=5.88, p<0.01), food satisfaction (20.80±4.45 vs 18.58±5.56; t=4.01, p<0.01) and OHRQoL (38.43±6.60 vs 34.33±10.48; t=4.44, p<0.01). People who smoke had higher eating behaviour scores (79.54±11.81 vs 64.40±17.79; t=7.21, p<0.01) but lower food satisfaction (18.45±4.74 vs 20.57±4.77; t=3.05, p<0.01). Therapeutic diet participants scored higher in eating behaviours (77.27±12.99) than healthy (63.40±17.74; MD=13.87, p<0.01) or ordinary diet groups (64.13±18.04; MD=13.14, p<0.01), but lower in food satisfaction and OHRQoL. Those without chronic illness scored better across all outcomes (eating behaviours: 61.83±17.32 vs 71.39±17.14; food satisfaction: 21.32±4.51 vs 19.20±4.91; OHRQoL: 39.65±6.66 vs 35.22±8.43; all p<0.01). Regarding denture use, partial upper denture users had higher eating behaviour scores (80.54±14.95) than those with no dentures (64.12±17.52; MD=16.42, p<0.01), while no-denture users reported better food satisfaction and OHRQoL. Annual dental visitors (74.05±14.95) and 6-month visitors (72.27±18.08) scored higher in eating behaviours than problem-only visitors (61.23±16.08). Daily toothpaste users had the lowest eating behaviour scores (59.71±16.97) but the highest OHRQoL (40.39±6.14) compared with other oral hygiene groups. Finally, not wearing dentures during sleep was associated with better outcomes than wearing them (eating behaviours: 64.30±17.56 vs 79.00±13.99; t=5.73, p<0.01).

Discussion

As the world’s population ages, understanding the complex relationship between eating behaviours and cuisine is essential. The swift demographic shift in Saudi Arabia poses particular difficulties because older people frequently experience nutritional deficits, chronic illnesses and deteriorating oral health.37 Even though the amount of literature on these subjects is expanding, there remains a significant gap in research that focuses on how these factors interact in the context of Saudi Arabia’s socioeconomic and cultural environment.38 The present study examined eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL among community-dwelling older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia. Overall, most participants demonstrated fair-to-good eating behaviours and moderate-to-high food satisfaction, while the majority reported fair OHRQoL. These findings suggest that although many older adults maintain relatively satisfactory dietary experiences, oral health challenges remain common in later life. Unlike previous studies that have largely investigated these factors in isolation, this study provides a novel, integrated perspective by simultaneously exploring behavioural, psychological and oral health dimensions within a culturally specific context. These findings were consistent with the Wilson and Cleary Model of Health-Related QoL, which posits associations among individual and clinical characteristics, functional health (eating behaviours), psychological perceptions (food satisfaction) and QoL outcomes (OHRQoL). Our results provide empirical support for this conceptual pathway, as food satisfaction and eating behaviours remained significant correlates of OHRQoL even after adjusting for socio-demographic and clinical variables.

The present study demonstrated that several socio-demographic, lifestyle and oral health-related factors were significantly associated with eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL among older adults. Participants with chronic illnesses, smoking behaviours, poor oral hygiene practices, denture use, lower educational attainment and inadequate income showed fewer favourable outcomes across the factors under investigation. These findings may be explained by the fact that socioeconomic disadvantage and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours can limit access to healthy food, dental services and preventive oral care, thereby negatively affecting dietary intake and oral functioning.11 According to earlier research studies, smoking, poor dietary habits, chronic illness burden and poor oral hygiene are all significant factors that contribute to poor oral health and a lower QoL in older persons.9 10 These results emphasise the significance of integrated health-promotion strategies that include nutritional counselling, smoking cessation, oral hygiene education and routine dental check-ups to enhance oral health and general well-being in older populations.10 11

The results of the current study provide important new insights about older adults’ OHRQoL, food satisfaction and eating behaviours. Many subjects exhibited varying levels of eating quality, and many had poor eating behaviours. This aligns with existing literature that highlights the prevalence of malnutrition among older adults, often influenced by physiological changes, chronic health conditions and social factors.39 40 Poor eating behaviours are associated with poorer health outcomes in this population. Nearly half of the individuals expressed high satisfaction with their food experiences, indicating that satisfaction with food is another common motif in the data. Increased food pleasure is critical since it has been linked to improved dietary compliance and general well-being, supporting the psychological aspects of nutrition.41

The study emphasises that a substantial proportion of older adults rated their OHRQoL as fair to poor. This research highlights the complex relationship between oral health and eating behaviours, as untreated dental issues may be associated with difficulties in eating and food enjoyment, and with less favourable dietary choices.5 Previous studies have shown that oral health issues, such as tooth loss and periodontal disease, adversely affect eating behaviours in older adults, leading to reduced consumption of essential nutrients.5 42 Maintaining healthy teeth and gums can improve overall food satisfaction and QoL; thus, addressing oral health is necessary. Therefore, to promote healthier ageing and ensure that older adults have satisfying lives, integrated treatments that address both oral health and nutrition are crucial.43

The current study’s correlation matrix shows significant correlation between older adults’ eating behaviours, oral health and food satisfaction. Notably, there is a strong positive correlation between OHRQoL and food satisfaction, suggesting that greater satisfaction with eating experiences is associated with more positive perceptions of oral health. This finding is supported by the literature, which indicates that a positive food experience can enhance overall satisfaction, leading to better health outcomes.44 The interplay among these factors underscores the importance of ensuring that eating behaviours not only meet nutritional requirements but also foster psychological well-being through enjoyable eating experiences. The capacity to eat a wide range of foods is directly impacted by oral health, so it is important to recognise that dietary intake pleasure is a key factor in oral health perceptions.5

On the other hand, the negative correlation between eating behaviours and food satisfaction indicates that poor eating behaviours were associated with lower food satisfaction. This relationship is vital because older persons frequently exhibit poor eating behaviours, such as limited dietary diversity and nutrient deficiency, which exacerbate feelings of dissatisfaction and may have an influence on oral health.45 To improve the quality of food intake and related satisfaction, these correlational data highlight the need for interventions that address eating behaviours and food satisfaction, including nutritional education and dietary changes. Ultimately, enhancing eating behaviours is not only vital for nutritional sufficiency but also instrumental in improving the perceived QoL related to oral health among older adults, highlighting the complex nature of health in ageing populations.46

The findings provide a thorough examination of the relationships between clinical data, eating behaviours, food satisfaction, socio-demographic characteristics and OHRQoL in older adults. Notably, age is a significant factor influencing food satisfaction, with younger age groups exhibiting higher satisfaction. This aligns with existing literature indicating that older adults may face limitations in dietary choices due to health conditions and tooth loss, which can negatively affect their eating experiences and overall health perceptions.46 47 Additionally, satisfaction with chewing and oral health is strongly correlated with educational attainment; better eating behaviours and knowledge of oral hygiene are associated with higher educational attainment. This finding underscores the need for targeted educational interventions for individuals with lower levels of education. It supports the view that education is essential for promoting awareness of the value of oral hygiene and healthy eating behaviours.48

The investigation also reveals significant relationships between income, eating behaviours, food satisfaction and oral health outcomes. Economic stability may facilitate access to healthy food options and routine dental care, as participants with adequate income reported better eating behaviours and higher levels of food satisfaction.49 The use of dentures and regular dental check-ups is also strongly associated with improved OHRQoL. Regular dental visits and good oral hygiene practices were associated with better overall perceptions of oral health and satisfaction, suggesting that maintaining oral health is crucial for enhancing QoL in older adults.50 51

The multiple linear regression analysis identifies key factors associated with OHRQoL among older adults living in the community. A key finding is that food satisfaction is significantly and positively correlated with perceived OHRQoL, underscoring the importance of dietary satisfaction for overall well-being. This aligns with existing research indicating that enhanced food satisfaction contributes to a better OHRQoL in older populations.52 Additionally, healthier eating behaviours were associated with improved OHRQoL, supporting earlier studies that connect nutrition to oral health outcomes.5 In contrast, chronic illnesses negatively affected OHRQoL, illustrating the intricate relationship between physical health conditions and oral health status in ageing individuals.42 The absence of significant associations with demographic factors such as age, gender, education and income indicates that, while these might influence health-related behaviours, they do not independently affect oral health outcomes in this group. This finding highlights the necessity for intervention strategies focused on lifestyle changes and chronic illness management to enhance OHRQoL in older adults.9 An integrated approach is crucial for fostering healthy ageing, especially in culturally diverse populations like those in Saudi Arabia.8

This study had several strengths. It employed a robust design and conducted a comprehensive assessment of eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL among older adults. Validated measurement instruments were employed, enhancing the reliability and internal validity of the findings. The use of a conceptual framework based on the Wilson and Cleary model strengthened the theoretical grounding of the analyses. The inclusion of participants from both urban and rural settings within the Abha district enhanced the sample’s diversity. Additionally, the study offers important perspectives on how nutritional status is closely associated with factors related to dietary acceptability, including taste, cultural characteristics and personal choices, elements that are often overlooked in clinical nutritional management. However, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between the study variables, and reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. The sample size, while adequate for the primary analyses, was relatively small. Future multicentred studies with larger, more diverse samples are recommended to validate these findings. Although the response rate was high, this was achieved within a convenience sample from clinical settings and does not eliminate selection bias, as the sample may not represent homebound or frail older adults who do not attend PHCCs. The focus on a single geographical area (Abha district) may limit the generalisability of the findings to other regions of Saudi Arabia or to different cultural contexts. While the sample included participants from both urban and rural settings in Abha, it is not necessarily representative of the entire older adult population in Saudi Arabia, as the Asir region has distinct cultural and dietary practices that may differ from those of other regions. Convenience sampling from PHCCs may have introduced selection bias, as participants were recruited from those attending routine health services rather than from the broader community, potentially over-representing individuals with better health awareness or access to care and under-representing homebound or more frail older adults. Finally, the sample was predominantly Arab, reflecting the region’s demographic composition; therefore, the findings may not be applicable to other ethnic groups.

Conclusion

This study provided a valuable theoretical framework for understanding the complex interactions among eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL in older adults. The results showed that OHRQoL is associated not only with oral diseases and clinical conditions but also with the interplay of behavioural, psychosocial and health-related factors. Despite a large proportion of older adults reporting fair-to-good eating behaviours and moderate-to-high food satisfaction, overall OHRQoL remained fair. Notably, food satisfaction was positively associated with OHRQoL, highlighting the psychological and emotional aspects of dietary experiences. Chronic illnesses, on the other hand, were negatively associated with OHRQoL, emphasising the importance of integrated therapies that target both dietary and oral health concerns. The findings suggest that culturally adapted, community-based training programmes focused on healthy eating behaviours and dental hygiene practices may contribute to greater life satisfaction and healthy ageing among older adults.

Implications in clinical practice

The implications of this study are significant for public health and clinical practice. By recognising the interrelationships among eating behaviours, food satisfaction and OHRQoL, health professionals can develop more effective strategies to address the needs of older adults. The study’s findings provide valuable insights for policymakers in designing community health programmes to address nutritional and oral health challenges among older adults. Additionally, the study underscores the need for ongoing research to examine the longitudinal effects of nutritional and oral health interventions on QoL among older adults across diverse cultural contexts.
