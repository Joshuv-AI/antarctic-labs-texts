Newborns with sickle cell trait have distinct peripheral blood molecular signatures across the first week of life
Authors: Abhinav K. Checkervarty, Casey P. Shannon, Travis M. Blimkie, Bhavjinder K. Dhillon, Reza Falsafi, Asimenia Angelidou, Joann Diray-Arce, Tue B. Bennike, Natasha M. Archer, Olubukola T. Idoko, Kerry McEnaney, Al Ozonoff, Oludare A. Odumade, Rym Ben-Othman, Kinga K. Smolen, Hanno Steen, EPIC-HIPC Consortium, Beate Kampmann, Tobias R. Kollmann, Ofer Levy, Amy H. Lee, Robert E. W. Hancock, Scott J. Tebbutt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sickle cell trait (SCT) is a genetic condition characterized by a heterozygous A-T substitution (Glu to Val) in 1 copy of the β-globin (HBB) gene on chromosome 11. Although often asymptomatic, individuals with SCT can experience complications under stress, such as hypoxia, dehydration, and extreme exertion, leading to vaso-occlusion, organ damage, and impaired immune defenses. Understanding the impact of SCT on the immune system may aid in preventing clinical complications. We genotyped 720 newborns from The Gambia, West Africa, an area in which people are prone to have sickle cell disorders, using RNA-sequencing reads mapped to the HBB gene to identify those with SCT and investigate immune development using single-omics and multiomics approaches. We identified 118 newborns (~17%) with SCT and 4 (0.6%) with sickle cell disease (SCD). Newborns with SCT demonstrated distinct molecular signatures across day of life (DOL) 0, 1, 3, and 7, compared with those without, including reduction of DOL 3 plasma proteins (eg, cysteine-rich secretory protein-3, inter-α-trypsin inhibitor 1, tetranectin, and cadherin-5). Additionally, we observed enrichment of pathways previously associated with SCD, such as the Jison sickle cell pathway, along with those linked to hematologic (chronic leukemia), hepatic (liver cancer), and renal (aging kidney) functions and disorders. These SCT-based associations were validated in an independent cohort of 45 neonates. Despite the absence of overt early clinical phenotypes, SCT-associated changes manifest in the first week of life at the molecular level. Monitoring and characterizing these associations may enable the identification of future disease risks and the development of preventive strategies. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as #NCT03246230.

Introduction

Sickle cell trait (SCT) is a genetic condition that affects ~300 million individuals worldwide.1 SCT results from inheriting 1 mutated copy (heterozygous) of the β-globin (HBB) gene from 1 biological parent. In contrast, inheriting 2 mutated HBB alleles (homozygous), 1 from each parent, leads to sickle cell disease (SCD). The HBB gene encodes the β-globin subunit of hemoglobin, and a point mutation (c.20A>T) can cause polymerization within red blood cells (RBCs), causing them to adopt a sickle shape.2,3 The complications of sickle-cell disorders arise from hemolysis of sickled RBCs with associated oxidative stress, as well as tissue and organ injury due to vaso-occlusion.4–6 Although SCT is typically considered a low-risk carrier state, it is linked to multiorgan complications, such as renal medullary carcinoma, chronic kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, acute chest syndrome, and, in rare cases, sudden death.1,3–10 Environmental stressors, including prolonged hypoxia, extreme physical exertion, and severe dehydration, can trigger sickling in individuals with SCT, potentially influencing vascular and immune pathways. Some studies have reported associations between SCT and infections, including pulmonary and urinary tract infections, COVID-19, and kidney complications.7,11–20 However, these findings may be confounded by socioeconomic and environmental factors, and causality remains uncertain.

The extent to which SCT contributes to immune-related outcomes remains unclear and, in early life, clinical complications are rare, likely reflecting the protective effect of fetal hemoglobin (HbF). The molecular profiles associated with SCT, particularly in neonates, have yet to be extensively studied using comprehensive techniques such as multiomics integration. Studies of SCT during early life have been limited, in part, due to 2 factors. First, the existence of a more severe form of sickle cell disorder, SCD, which often results in more severe clinical outcomes and has therefore attracted extensive research to develop effective therapeutic strategies.21 Second, the clinical complications associated with sickle cell disorders typically manifest several months after birth (beyond 3–6 months), a delay primarily attributed to the gradual decline in HbF and the corresponding rise in sickle hemoglobin (HbS, α and βS chains).22,23 Therefore, further research to explore molecular alterations associated with SCT in early life can provide crucial biological insights into the mechanisms that precede or modulate SCT-associated health and immune outcomes.

In this study, we used multivariate approaches, including single-omics and multiomics classification models, to uncover mechanisms associated with SCT in neonates. Rather than applying multiomics models for diagnostic classification, which is already readily achieved through extensive screening methods for sickle-cell disorders, we used these approaches as discovery tools to uncover molecular features associated with the outcome of interest (SCT vs wild type). This strategy draws upon the established strengths of integrated multiomics in uncovering disease-relevant pathways.24

We leveraged data from a National Institutes of Health–sponsored neonatal systems vaccinology study to investigate the impact of SCT on neonates born in The Gambia, West Africa, an area in which people are prone to have sickle cell disorders.25 The study posits 2 main hypotheses: (1) RAN-sequencing (RNA-seq) reads mapped to HBB, often discarded in blood-based transcriptomics studies, can be used to identify neonates with SCT26; and (2) neonates with SCT exhibit distinct patterns of early-life molecular ontogeny.

Methods

Experimental design

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for research participants have been described previously (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03246230, EPIC-002).27 Approval was obtained from local ethics committees and by the institutional review board at Boston Children’s Hospital. In partnership with the sites, the study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board (or ethics committee) of Boston Children’s Hospital (IRB-P00024239), and Medical Research Council Unit, The Gambia.

Figure 1 is a diagrammatic representation of the experimental design. Two peripheral blood samples were collected from each of the 720 neonates born in The Gambia, West Africa, at birth (day of life [DOL] 0) and at a follow-up visit at either DOL 1, DOL 3, or DOL 7. The days were selected in the original study to capture distinct phases of neonatal immune responses reported in vaccinology studies, including immediate innate (≤24 hours), evolving innate/cytokine (DOL 2–4), and early adaptive changes (DOL 7).28 The samples were used to derive transcriptomics, proteomics, cytokines and chemokines, and metabolomics data sets (supplemental Methods).

At DOL 0, neonates were additionally randomized into 1 of 4 vaccine groups, receiving either the hepatitis B vaccine (HBV), Bacille Calmette-Guérin vaccine (BCG), the combination (HBV + BCG), or delayed vaccination (Delayed). Remaining protocol-required vaccines (either HBV, BCG, or oral polio vaccine) were administered during follow-up. Although anti–hepatitis B titers were measured at DOL 30 and DOL 128 after vaccination; these are not discussed here, because current evidence does not support meaningful differences in vaccine responses between carriers of SCT and noncarriers. Here, the focus was on molecular differences observed between neonates with SCT and wild type during the first week of life.

Validation cohort

An independent cohort of 45 neonates, also born in The Gambia, West Africa, was used to validate our primary findings. Two blood samples were available for each neonate. First at DOL 0, and a second on either DOL 1, DOL 3, or DOL 7. Using these samples, transcriptomics and metabolomics data sets were generated (supplemental Methods). The RNA-seq reads mapped to HBB were genotyped to identify neonates with SCT. Hereafter, the term “validation cohort” is used for this second cohort (EPIC-003), and the main cohort (EPIC-002) is referred to as the “discovery cohort.”

Genotyping analysis

Neonates were genotyped using RNA-seq reads mapped to HBB with the Genome Analysis Tool Kit29 to identify neonates with SCT. Although SCT status was not confirmed using standardized protein-based methods (eg, hemoglobin electrophoresis), the genotyping was internally validated for consistency. Specifically, we genotyped birth (DOL 0) and post–DOL 0 (DOL 1, 3, or 7) samples for each neonate. Globin reads were available for 680 neonates, with both the DOL 0 and post–DOL 0 sequences available for 655, and a single sequence for 25 neonates. All files were analyzed without filtering by read depth or sequence size. The results were inspected for the SCT variation: thymine (T) to adenine (A) on chromosome 11.1 Neonates without this mutation were classified as having wild type, those with this mutation were classified as having SCT, and those identified as homozygous for this mutation were classified as neonates with SCD. No further subclassification within SCT was performed. We note that maternal inflammatory status may contribute to variability in neonatal hematopoiesis30; however, maternal information and sampling were not included in this study design.

Differential expression analysis

Omics-based differences between the SCT and wild-type groups were investigated with limma library in R (version 4.1.2),31 which uses moderated t tests and adjusts the nominal P value for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.32 The comparisons were performed with and without inclusion of SCD (n = 4). Both analyses showed comparable fold changes (FCs) and P values. Results reflect the analysis with SCD merged into the SCT group, referred to as SCT+, and results excluding neonates with SCD are shown in the supplemental Data. The omics profiles of neonates in the SCT+ and wild-type groups were compared separately for DOL 0, DOL 1, DOL 3, and DOL 7. We used the transcriptomics or proteomics results from single-omics and multiomics investigations to perform ranked and unranked pathway analyses using the fgsea and clusterProfiler libraries in R.

Omics-based classification models

We used supervised multivariable approaches at both single-omics and multiomics levels to construct classification models for SCT status: wild-type vs SCT. These models incorporate internal variable selection to identify omics features associated with the SCT status. Subsequently, these features were investigated using pathway analysis.

We used sparse partial least squares discriminant analysis (SPLSDA) from the mixOmics library in R to develop models using each omics data set.33,34 SPLSDA combines variables of a high-dimensional data set (X) to form components, maximizing the covariance between these components and the class labels (Y). X variables are weighted by their covariance with Y, and the feature selection is performed using the L1 regularization. This can identify the most important molecular drivers differentiating SCT from wild-type.

Correlation between different data types is not considered in single-omics, potentially missing crucial biological interactions relevant to the outcome of interest (SCT status). Therefore, we developed multiomics models using data integration analysis for biomarker discovery using latent components (DIABLO; mixOmics),24,33 which selects sets of correlated variables between multiple omics data sets in the model.35 DIABLO uses a correlation constraint, emphasizing variables correlated between different omics data, improving the model’s ability to remove excessive noise and uncover better associations with the outcome, as shown previously.24 This constraint can vary from 0 (no correlation) to 1 (high correlation). A model with a constraint of 1 puts a higher weight on correlated omics features between different omics data sets. All models were run at 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 constraint.

Models were generated separately for DOL 0, DOL 1, DOL 3, and DOL 7, with varying numbers of omics features (from 5–6000) for variable selection. The average prediction performances (average area under the receiver operating curve, AUC) were then assessed using cross-validation.

The top-performing models were further used for an off-the-shelf classification of SCT status in the validation cohort. A strong AUC (area under the curve) would indicate the reproducibility of the results in new data and provide credibility to the associations identified by the models.

Cross-validation

The classification accuracies of single-omics– and multiomics-based models were assessed using repeated k-fold cross-validation (10 × 10-fold) and the AUC.

Results

Identification of neonates with SCT

Figure 2 provides a flowchart outlining the genotyping process for the identification of neonates with SCT. Of 655 neonates with DOL 0 and post–DOL 0 globin reads available, 651 (~99%) showed consistent genotyping calls, in which both samples were identified as either SCT or wild type. The remaining 4 neonates showed inconsistent calls, with 1 sample identified as SCT and the other as wild type. These neonates were excluded. In total, 554 neonates were identified as having wild type, 118 (~17%) were identified as having SCT, and 4 (~0.6%) were homozygous for SCD (families of neonates with SCD were informed about our findings; Table 1; supplemental Tables 1–5). The observed proportions of neonates with SCT and SCD were consistent with the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.36

Univariate differences in SCT

All omics features were compared between the SCT+ and wild-type groups at DOL 0, 1, 3, and 7 (supplemental Table 6). Plasma-based omics, specifically proteins, metabolites, cytokines, and chemokines, generally showed decreased analyte concentration in the SCT+ group at the individual DOLs (Table 2). After multiple testing corrections, most comparisons did not pass the significance threshold (adjusted P value ≤ .05). The only exception was the proteomics data for DOL 3, demonstrating reduced abundance (FC of <0) of ~94% of plasma proteins, with 4 proteins, cysteine-rich secretory protein-3 (CRIS3), inter-α-trypsin inhibitor 1 (ITIH1), tetranectin (TETN/CLEC3B), and cadherin-5 (CDH5/CADH5), passing the significance threshold (Figure 3). Results for a relaxed threshold of adjusted P value ≤ .2 are shown in supplemental Figure 1. To assess whether the substantial decrease in protein abundance on DOL 3 was influenced by sample size imbalance between the SCT+ (n = 34) and wild-type (n = 161) groups, we performed multiple comparisons using randomly selected subsets of 34 neonates with wild type. The lower protein concentrations observed in neonates with SCT+ remained consistently reproducible across these balanced comparisons (supplemental Figure 2). Of note, omics data were available for all neonates with SCD at DOL 0, among whom only 1 neonate contributed data at DOL 3, consistent with the study design in which all neonates were sampled at DOL 0 but only one-third at follow-up (DOL 1, 3, and 7). Excluding SCD showed similar differentially abundant proteins as the SCT+ vs wild-type analysis (supplemental Figures 3 and 4), whereas no differences were observed between neonates with wild-type and those with SCD at DOL 0 (supplemental Figure 5).

We did not compare neonates in the SCT+ and wild-type groups based on vaccine groups (HBV, BCG, HBV + BCG, and Delayed) at postvaccine time points (DOL 1, DOL 3, and DOL 7) due to the modest sample size of the SCT group after this double stratification (supplemental Table 7).

Sex-associated differences in the SCT+ group compared with those in the wild-type group were examined with 2 proteomics-based analyses. First, a sex-stratified analysis, in which comparison was performed separately for male and female neonates. Second, the influence of biological sex was adjusted by adding it as a covariate to the formulation of the moderated t tests.

In the sex-stratified analysis, the DOL 3 proteomics data for only the male neonates showed differential abundance (adjusted P value ≤ .05) of 2 proteins (CADH5 and IL1AP) in the SCT+ group. With a relaxed threshold of adjusted P value ≤ .2, 26 proteins were differentially abundant (supplemental Figure 6). The protein concentration was lower (logFC ranging from 0 to −3) for male and female neonates in the SCT+ group.

The analysis with sex as a covariate demonstrated differential abundance (logFC < 0; adjusted P ≤ .05) of 4 proteins in the SCT+ group on DOL 3, namely ITIH1, CRIS3, CADH5, and TETN. Under a threshold of adjusted P ≤ .2, 59 proteins demonstrated differential abundance in the SCT+ group (supplemental Figure 7).

Early-life changes in SCT

Classification models were developed to identify neonates with SCT and identify features associated with the trait across the various omics data sets. Supervised single-omics models (SPLSDA) were generated using transcriptomics, proteomics, cytokines and chemokines, and metabolomics data for DOL 0, 1, 3, and 7 (Figure 4A). We selected top-performing models from each omics using the principle of parsimony,37 cross-validated AUC, and 95% confidence interval (CI). All models achieved cross-validated AUC estimates with lower bounds exceeding 0.5, demonstrating performance greater than chance. Post–DOL 0 models consistently outperformed DOL 0 models, and DOL 3–based models were selected from each omics due to their consistently high AUC. The best-performing metabolomics model included 200 metabolites with an average AUC of 0.68 (95% CI, 0.64–0.74). For proteomics, a model based on 5 proteins demonstrated an average AUC of 0.68 (95% CI, 0.66–0.73). The transcriptomics model achieved an AUC of 0.67 (95% CI, 0.61–0.71), and the cytokine and chemokine model reached an AUC of 0.65 (95% CI, 0.61–0.70).

Consistent with the single-omics, multiomics post–DOL 0 models outperformed the DOL 0 models, with DOL 3 models showing the highest average performance (Figure 4B). The DOL 3 model with a full correlation constraint of 1, 70 transcripts, 70 proteins, 70 metabolites, and 15 cytokines and chemokines demonstrated an average AUC of 0.69 (95% CI, 0.66–0.72). The best-performing multiomics and single-omics models mostly selected different omics features (supplemental Figure 8).

Validation in an independent cohort

From the validation cohort, 45 neonates were genotyped to identify those with SCT. RNA-seq reads mapped to the HBB gene were available for 43 neonates, and 7 neonates (~16.2%) were identified as having SCT. The best-performing single-omics and multiomics classification models, trained on the discovery cohort, were applied to classify neonates with SCT in this validation cohort (Figure 5). The transcriptomics data were primarily used because it was less sparse and measured on the same sequencing platform as the discovery cohort, making them comparable. The best-performing single-omics model demonstrated AUCs of >0.70, whereas the multiomics model demonstrated AUCs of >0.65. Across DOL 0, DOL 1, and post–DOL 0 samples, the classification performance was consistently greater than chance, with average AUC ranging from 0.65 to 0.84. Both single-omics and multiomics models were deemed validated.

Biological mechanisms in SCT

We carried out pathway enrichment analysis to identify biological mechanisms associated with SCT. Typically, starting from infancy (age of >3–6 months) or older ages, sickle cell disorders, including SCT, are linked to renal and hepatic manifestations (eg, elevated transaminases in those with SCT), blood-based disorders, and distinct expression of certain genes and transcription factors.3,7,10,20,38 Based on this background information, pathways with potential association with SCT were selected and grouped into categories of interest: blood-associated; blood disorder; erythrocytes; FOXO-associated; kidney; liver; oxygen-associated, platelet; KLF, RUNX, and GATA transcription factors; and sickle cell.39–45

Ranked pathway analysis was performed with transcripts ordered based on the FC in the SCT+ group from the differential expression analysis. Across the first week of life, a total of 2045 pathways were enriched (adjusted P ≤ .05) in the SCT+ group (supplemental Table 8), narrowed down to 149 pathways associated with the categories of interest (Figure 6A). The direction of enrichment (positive or negative enrichment score) was consistent from DOL 0 to DOL 7 (Figure 6B). The highest number of pathways, 25 of 149, belonged to the liver category, and 8 of 25 were enriched at DOL 7.

To gain further insight, unranked pathway analysis was conducted using the transcripts from the classification models. Based on the 200 transcripts from the single-omics models, 78 pathways were enriched, including 6 pathways in categories of interest (Figure 6C). The multiomics-based classification models had 2 latent components, each selecting 70 transcripts. As pathway analysis generally demonstrates better enrichment when more omics features are used as input, both components were used to increase the input size to 140 transcripts, showing an overrepresentation of 264 pathways, with 33 in the categories of interest (Figure 6D; supplemental Figure 9). This included the JISON_SICKLE_CELL_DISEASE pathway, covering 12 of 137 queried genes.46

The top-performing single-omics model selected 5 proteins that were most distinct between wild type and SCT: CRIS3, ITIH1, TETN (CLEC3B), CADH5 (CDH5), and α−1-microglobulin/bikunin precursor (AMBP). All the proteins, except for AMBP, were also differentially abundant (adjusted P value < .05) in the SCT+ group compared with the wild type group at DOL 3. The proteins with reduced abundance (logFC < 0), including ADAM-metallopeptidase with thrombospondin-type-1-motif-13 and HBB, have known associations with SCD.47

Discussion

SCT can be asymptomatic or manifest with complications, such as compromised immune function, warranting a deeper understanding to gain insights into formulating preventative strategies and mitigating adverse outcomes.2,3,7,8,15 Therefore, we repurposed sequencing and omics data from neonates born in an area in which people are prone to have sickle cell disorders.25 Our findings suggest that despite the lack of overt clinical manifestations, molecular profiles of SCT neonates demonstrate similarities to SCD and associations with features and pathways involved in hematological, renal, and hepatic disorders.

Genotyping identified 118 neonates (~17%) with SCT and 4 with SCD. This analysis demonstrated highly consistent genotyping calls, and the proportion of neonates with SCT was close to that found in the validation cohort (~16.2%), providing additional credibility to our genotyping approach.

In neonates, HbF is typically more prevalent than HbA or HbS, and has a protective effect against sickling-related complications. Symptoms of sickle cell disorders are generally absent for 3 to 6 months after birth.22,23,48 Limited studies have explored the molecular differences in children (aged <1 year) with SCT for preventive interventions to avoid severe outcomes later in life. Our study examined neonatal blood samples (from neonates aged <28 days) using single-omics and multiomics approaches to identify molecular signatures associated with SCT. We found noticeable SCT-associated molecular differences within the first week of life, evidenced by the classification models (Figure 4) and univariate analysis demonstrating a trend of downregulation in SCT (Figure 3). At DOL 3, we observed a significant drop in protein levels in the SCT group compared with the wild-type group, particularly for liver-associated proteins such as CRIS3, ITIH1, TETN, CADH5, and AMBP. Because the liver is central to protein production, this reduction may reflect an overall decrease in the production of other circulating proteins. Supporting the protein changes, an SCT study also reported an association of SCT with proteins linked to inflammation, hemolysis, and kidney injury.49

Given the low level of HbS and high HbF in neonates, these molecular changes are unlikely to result from sickling alone. One possible explanation is that SCT may induce subclinical changes in erythroid programming or immune function that remain below the threshold of clinical manifestation. Supporting this, an adult study comparing individuals with SCD and SCT, and healthy individuals (n = 10 per group) reported elevated oxidative stress markers and mildly impaired glutathione metabolism in the SCT group without any notable hemolysis or anemia, suggesting only subclinical changes.50

Pathway analysis demonstrated enrichment of pathways associated with the liver and kidneys, the organs most commonly affected by sickling of RBCs.19,38 Sickle cell–associated liver abnormalities, also called sickle cell hepatopathy,51 are often mild in SCT compared with in SCD. However, patients with SCT can also experience liver cirrhosis and acute liver failure.10,52 Renal disorders are usually the most common outcome in patients (aged>6 months) with SCT.7,19,49 A decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate and albuminuria has been observed more frequently in carriers of SCT than in noncarriers.18,49,53 We observed pathways associated with oxidative stress, a hallmark of sickle cell disorders driven by premature hemolysis of RBCs and iron dys-regulation, which can lead to reactive oxygen species production, inflammation, and impaired oxygen delivery.54,55 We also found pathways associated with genes or transcription factors that are either therapeutic targets for SCD or linked to blood functions and disorders. FOXO-based factors involved in heme malignancies have been explored as therapeutic targets for SCD. Higher levels of FOXO3 increase the production of HbF, which can mitigate the effects of adult HbS.39,40,56 The transcription factor KLF1, another therapeutic target for SCD, is involved in erythropoiesis and regulation of globin switching.41,57–59 Finally, the transcription factor RUNX is related to various hematological malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia.42 These results suggest differences in hematological molecular profiles of neonates with SCT compared with those without this trait.

Most studies on clinical complications associated with SCT are conducted on participants aged >28 days.7 Although SCT is not known to affect hematopoiesis in neonates at a clinical level, our study suggests that certain molecular mechanisms linked with later complications are already initiated in those with SCT during the first week of life. These early signals may be due to shifts in immune functions, erythroid programming, or redox balance, without manifesting into clinical phenotypes. Similar subclinical molecular changes are described in adult studies on thalassemia or glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, with observed perturbed erythroid stress markers and altered serum microRNA profiles, suggesting redox imbalance without overt clinical complications.60,61 This is also reflected in our pathway analysis of the transcripts selected by multiomics models (Figure 6), particularly with the overrepresentation of the JISON_SICKLE_CELL pathway, which is reported in study of adults (aged >30 years) with SCD. This adult study reports differential expression of genes involved in heme metabolism, antioxidant and stress response, inflammation, and angiogenesis.46 This suggests that molecular signatures in neonates with SCT may exhibit similarities to those with more severe SCD. Multiomics models showed enrichment of more pathways associated with categories of interest than single-omics models, including those associated with SCD, providing biological insights relevant to SCT. This enhanced enrichment result could be attributed to the ability of multiomics models to incorporate correlation structures between different molecular data sets.24,62

Our study has important limitations including (1) the validation cohort included a small number of neonates with SCT (n = 7) and fewer data types, limiting cross-comparison with the discovery cohort; (2) SCT status could not be clinically validated, although genotyping were highly consistent and prevalence aligned with Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium; (3) hematological parameters, including blood counts, were not measured, preventing adjustment for cellular composition in our models (future studies will be required to understand the relationship between cell type–specific contributions and SCT); and (4) restricted longitudinal follow-up of the neonates, limited ascertaining progression of SCT-associated molecular differences, and whether these discrepancies correlate with clinical end points. These findings are associative and hypothesis-generating, underscoring the need for more studies to determine whether these early signatures are relevant to clinical outcomes later in life.

Our study also has multiple strengths including (1) a unique approach that used sequencing data that are typically discarded in transcriptomics analyses (eg, globin reads), applied here in a population with a high prevalence of the sickle cell mutation; (2) a focus on newborns, which provided fresh insights into early-life molecular ontogeny in this underresearched vulnerable population; (3) rigorous data management including quality control and quality assurance in a centralized data base; (4) leveraging a robust multiomic integration approach to investigate the molecular alterations induced by SCT, providing a novel perspective on immune ontogeny; and (5) validation of key findings in an independent cohort.

In conclusion, this study revealed that SCT-related molecular differences appear in neonatal blood as early as the first week of life. These differences are linked to SCD and hematological, renal, and hepatic functions and disorders. Further validation of these findings in larger cohorts may enable screening and monitoring in early life to predict and prevent future adverse clinical outcomes. These results open new opportunities for molecular studies of SCT to inform novel approaches to diagnose, prognose, prevent, and treat its clinical manifestations.
