Cervical mucus can be used for metabolite screening in cervical cancer
Authors: Rie Kawasaki, Iwao Kukimoto, Tetsuya Tsukamoto, Eiji Nishio, Aya Iwata, Takuma Fujii
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximately 660,000 women are diagnosed with cervical cancer annually. Current screening options such as cytology or human papillomavirus testing have limitations, creating a need to identify more effective ancillary biomarkers for triage. Here, we evaluated whether metabolomic analysis of cervical mucus metabolism could be used to identify biomarkers of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and cervical cancer. The case–control group consisted of 181 CIN, 69 squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) patients, and 48 healthy controls in the primary cohort. We undertook metabolomic analyses using ultra‐HPLC–tandem mass spectrometry. Univariate and multivariate analyses were carried out to profile metabolite characteristics, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified biomarker candidates. Five metabolites conferred the highest discriminatory power for SCC: oxidized glutathione (GSSG) (area under the ROC curve, 0.924; 95% confidence interval, 0.877–0.971), malic acid (0.914, 0.859–0.968), kynurenine (0.884, 0.823–0.945), GSSG/glutathione (GSH) (0.936, 0.892–0.979), and kynurenine/tryptophan (0.909, 0.856–0.961). Malic acid was the best marker for detection of CIN2 or worse (0.858, 0.793–0.922) and was a clinically useful metabolite. We confirmed the reproducibility of the results by validation cohort. Additionally, metabolomic analyses revealed eight pathways strongly associated with cervical neoplasia. Of these, only the tricarboxylic acid cycle was strongly associated with all CINs and cancer, indicating active energy production. Aberrant arginine metabolism by decreasing arginine and increasing citrulline might reduce tumor immunity. Changes in cysteine‐methionine and GSH pathways might drive the initiation and progression of cervical cancer. These results suggest that metabolic analysis can identify ancillary biomarkers and could improve our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying cervical neoplasia.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, approximately 660,000 women receive a cervical cancer diagnosis annually, 1 and the number of patients diagnosed with high‐grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), a precursor to cervical cancer, is approximately 7–20 times higher than cervical cancer in developed countries. 2 , 3 Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop a screening system that can effectively detect aberrant lesions in patients and match them to the appropriate treatment. While cytology‐based cervical cancer screening has contributed to a reduction in cervical cancer incidence, its low sensitivity precludes the detection of high grade CIN. Recent additions to screening systems, such as human papillomavirus (HPV) testing, show higher sensitivity compared to cytology. However, as HPV infection does not invariably lead to cervical neoplasia, it is a relatively nonspecific biomarker. Therefore, there is a critical need for the establishment of more specific biomarkers for triage. Recent advancements in metabolomics have shown that metabolic abnormalities, involving the accumulation or depletion of metabolites, play a crucial role in cancer initiation and progression. 4 , 5 Detection of aberrant metabolite levels can help to grade pathophysiology and identify pathophysiology‐specific biomarkers. Indeed, metabolomics is a powerful analytical approach for monitoring cancer development and progression, and for elucidating pathophysiological mechanisms. 6 , 7

Recent metabolomic analyses of various cancers, including lung, liver, colorectal, bladder, breast, and endometrial malignancies, have described biomarkers derived from samples including tissues, blood, urine, and saliva. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 For patients with cervical cancer, predictive, diagnostic, and prognostic biomarkers have been reported in urine, blood, cervical canal lavage fluid, and tissues. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Despite the clear value of exploring metabolomics‐based biomarkers in cervical cancer, there is a paucity of cervical mucus‐derived ancillary biomarkers that would help to differentiate between various premalignant lesions and invasive cancer. The composition of cervical mucus, which is directly secreted or exuded from the affected cervix, is reflective of the tissue microenvironment, making mucus an ideal sample for the detection of biomarkers associated with local metabolic changes.

The objective of our study was to identity potential biomarkers by analyzing the metabolites in cervical mucus, with a view to nominating highly sensitive and specific candidate biomarkers for CINs and invasive cancer. During our work, the process of profiling metabolic changes in CINs and SCC revealed specific metabolic pathways that may be associated with the development and progression of cervical neoplasia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient characteristics and sample collection

For the series of metabolomics analysis, cervical mucus was collected from 399 patients who attended the outpatient clinic at Fujita Health University Hospital for gynecological examinations from July 2017 to May 2022 (Table S1). The cervical mucus was collected with a 1 cm diameter cotton ball for metabolome analysis and stored at −80°C. A cervical brush was inserted into the cervical canal to collect ectocervical and endocervical cells for HPV genotyping. In the primary cohort, samples were classified as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN1, n = 19; CIN2, n = 80; or CIN3, n = 82) or squamous cell carcinoma (SCC; n = 69). Our patient selection criteria were predicated on the presence of HPV in cases of CIN and SCC, while excluding cases of adenocarcinoma. It is ethically difficult to obtain a biopsy from healthy volunteers. Therefore as our control, we recruited 48 healthy women who were under consultation for infertility, defining “normal” based on an HPV‐negative status following analysis of exfoliated cervical cells.

The vaginal microbiota in women of reproductive age changes during the menstrual cycle. To exclude the influence of these changes, we did not collect samples during menstruation for this analysis. 23 , 24

The HPV genotyping assay was undertaken using PCR with PGMY primers followed by reverse line blot hybridization. 25 The median age and body mass index (BMI) of each group are shown in Table 1. In the validation cohort, samples classified as normal (n = 18), CIN1 (n = 10), CIN2 (n = 22), CIN3 (N = 22), and SCC (n = 29) were collected separately.

Metabolite extraction from cervical mucus

To extract metabolites, 500 μL prechilled methanol with 10 μM internal standard solution (2‐morpholinoethanesulfonic acid) was added to the cotton balls containing cervical mucus. After incubation at 4°C for 30 min, 200 μL supernatant was collected. Prechilled chloroform : water solution (325 μL, 2:1.25, v/v) was added to the collected supernatant, mixed vigorously, and centrifuged at 13,500 g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 10 kDa molecular filter (Merck) and dried under vacuum.

Metabolomics data acquisition

Extracts for cervical metabolomics were diluted with 50 μL water and analyzed using an LCMS‐8060 system (Shimadzu Corporation), which is comprised of a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. A Supelco Discovery HS F5‐3 (3 μm, 150 mm × 2.0 mm; Merck) was used as the analytical column for chromatographic separation. The liquid chromatography (LC) parameters used were as follows: mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The mobile phase flow rate was 250 μL min−1 in gradient mode and the sample injection volume was 1 μL. The columns were maintained at 40°C. The gradient program was: 0% B at 0–2 min; 0%–25% B at 2–5 min; 25%–35% B at 5–11 min; 35%–95% B at 11–15 min; and 95% B at 15–20 min. Labsolutions LC‐MS software version 5.114 (Shimadzu Co.) was used for instrument control. The compounds were identified using LC/MS/MS Method Package for Primary Metabolites version 2 and LabSolutions Insight software version 3.8 SP4 (Shimadzu Co.). The peak area of each compound was calculated and normalized to the peak area of the internal standard.

Metabolomics data analysis

Univariate and multivariate analyses were carried out using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 and 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) online analysis software. In the normalization process, the metabolite intensity values were autoscaled (mean‐centered and divided by the SD of each variable). Missing values were replaced by 20% of the minimum positive value for each metabolite. Fold change analysis, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, volcano plot analysis, and partial least square discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) were undertaken with normalized metabolite data. In the volcano plot analysis, the significance of metabolic changes was determined using the following criteria: −log10 (p value) >1.3 (i.e., p < 0.05) and absolute log2 (fold change [FC]) >1 (i.e., FC > 2). The importance of each metabolite in the PLS‐DA model was measured using the variable importance in projection (VIP) score, which quantifies the contribution of each metabolite to the separation of the group to be analyzed. A VIP score of 1.0 was used as the threshold for variable selection; a higher VIP score indicates that the metabolite has more weight in distinguishing the two groups. False discovery rate (FDR) was calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. 26 , 27

Pathway analysis

Pathway analysis was carried out using the Metaboanalyst 5.0 Pathway Analysis Module. To identify relevant pathways involved in each target group, the names of metabolites with significantly different abundances in the target groups were converted to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) IDs and imported into Metaboanalyst 5.0. This allowed us to combine pathway enrichment analysis with topology analysis. 28

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS software version 29 (IBM Corp.) with p < 0.05 as the significance threshold. The Jonckheere–Terpstra trend test was applied to identify significant trends between disease category groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the overall differences between the groups. The comparison between the two groups was made using a Mann–Whitney U‐test with Bonferroni correction. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) method was used to evaluate the disease diagnostic potential of significant metabolites. A ROC curve is calculated from the sensitivity and specificity between disease groups. The optimal cut‐off value was determined based on the maximum Youden Index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). To obtain ROC curves for the combination, predicted probabilities were calculated from the logistic regression of multiple metabolites. The Akaike information criterion (AIC), which is the more optimal combination for lower values, is defined as: AIC = −2(log likelihood) + 2K, where K is the number of model parameters. 29

RESULTS

Ability of cervical mucus metabolites to discriminate between CIN and cancer

Eighty‐four of 97 primary metabolites in the LC/MS/MS Method Package for Primary Metabolites version 2 were detected in cervical mucus samples from the primary cohort. We used PLS‐DA to assess the differentiation between the two groups and identify the main metabolites contributing to their discrimination. Malic acid was the only commonly detected metabolite in the top 15 normal versus all disease groups and CIN3 versus SCC, and had high disease‐discriminatory power (VIP score, 1.8–2.4). Nicotinamide was included in normal versus SCC and CIN3 versus SCC, where it showed high discriminatory power for cervical neoplasia.

Volcano plot analysis was undertaken on the same dataset to identify metabolites with significant changes. Malic acid, xanthine, and citric acid were significantly increased in all disease groups compared to normal controls, while succinic acid was only significantly increased in CINs relative to normal (Figures 1C,F and S1C). There was a significant increase in 19 metabolites and a decrease in 10 metabolites in SCC compared to normal, and a significant increase in 13 metabolites in SCC compared to CIN3. Consistently, oxidized glutathione (GSSG), kynurenine, and nicotinamide levels were increased in the disease groups; this was particularly the case in SCC. In addition, there were significant changes in metabolite levels in CIN1 versus CIN2 and in CIN2 versus CIN3, which are adjacent histological classifications (Figure S1).

Performance of potential metabolic biomarkers

To examine the performance of the biomarker candidates for each CIN grade and cervical cancer, we ran ROC curve analyses for metabolites with high VIP scores in PLS‐DA and for significantly increased metabolites identified by the volcano plot analysis. Among these, GSSG, malic acid, nicotinamide, and kynurenine showed high diagnostic efficacy for cancer (Figure 2A). Of note, GSSG had a high discriminatory ability for CIN3 versus SCC (area under the ROC curve [AUC], 0.904; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.858–0.950) and normal versus SCC (0.924, 0.877–0.971). Given the excellent cancer discriminatory abilities of GSSG and kynurenine, the GSSG/glutathione (GSH) ratio and the kynurenine (Kyn)/tryptophan (Trp) ratio were also investigated; each demonstrated a high diagnostic capability and qualified as a potential disease biomarker (Figure 2B). The AUC of malic acid was 0.857 (0.793–0.922) in the normal versus CIN3 or worse comparison, indicating that this metabolite is a suitable disease biomarker (Figure S2). All candidate metabolites and metabolite ratios, except for lactic acid, succinic acid, and fumaric acid, showed significant increases or decreases with disease progression (Jonckheere–Terpstra test, p < 0.05; Figure 3). The ROC analysis of multiple metabolites was undertaken to evaluate the optimal combinations based on AIC values; this improved diagnostic power compared to single metabolites (Figure S3).

Pathway analysis of differential metabolites for disease progression

Metabolome pathway analysis was carried out for metabolites that were significantly increased or decreased in each group (Table S2) identified eight relevant pathways (FDR p < 0.05, pathway impact value >0.2; Figure 4A,B). Three of these pathways showed persistent metabolic changes during disease, or were characteristic of CIN3 versus SCC. First, metabolites of the TCA cycle continuously changed from low‐grade CIN to SCC, as characterized by higher pathway impact values (0.20–0.25). The levels of malic acid, citric acid, and fumaric acid, which are included in the TCA cycle, increased significantly throughout the progression from CINs to SCC. Second, the arginine biosynthesis pathway was strongly associated with CIN2, CIN3, and SCC. The upregulation of this pathway was particularly pronounced in SCC, where levels of citrulline, arginosuccinic acid, fumaric acid, and adenylsuccinic acid were increased compared to normal. Finally, the cysteine and methionine metabolism pathway was strongly associated only with SCC; significant increases and decreases in metabolites belonging to this pathway were observed (Figure 4C).

DISCUSSION

Through our metabolomic analysis of primary metabolites from cervical mucus, we have identified potential diagnostic markers for cervical neoplasia, and specific changes in metabolic pathways that accompany disease progression. This could facilitate the development of new molecular markers for use in triaging during cervical cancer screening. The development of an effective postscreening triage test will reduce the need for colposcopy, which is a burdensome diagnostic procedure for patients. Metabolites in cervical mucus discriminated normal from cervical neoplasia with high sensitivity and specificity. Five metabolites and/or their ratios (GSSG, malic acid, kynurenine, and Kyn/Trp and GSSG/GSH ratios) were able to discriminate SCC from other disease groups. Ou et al. have reported that the level of GSSG is higher in cancer compared to normal, but we have specifically shown that it is distinguishable particularly in CIN3 and cancer. 30 Malic acid is most discriminating in CIN3 (as well as CIN2) or worse and is an excellent biomarker candidate for disease detection due to its high diagnostic accuracy. It is reported that malic acid levels are increased in prostate cancer and oral SCC. 31 , 32 We also showed that high levels of kynurenine and high Kyn/Trp ratios have excellent discriminatory power for SCC. The previous reports of increased kynurenine and high Kyn/Trp ratios in several cancer types are reported. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39

We recognize the importance of linking the changes in metabolite levels and associated pathways to the biology of cancer. The levels of five TCA cycle metabolites (including malate) were upregulated; as well as indicating an increase in ATP production, these metabolites could serve as promising disease biomarkers. 40 , 41 Arginine metabolism dysfunction can affect antitumor immune activity. 42 , 43 We observed that, of the metabolites in the arginine biosynthesis pathway, citrulline increased while arginine and aspartate decreased. Arginine is involved in T cell activation, 42 , 44 and a reduction in its levels may thus attenuate tumor immunity, which in turn would favor tumor formation. Conversely, citrulline (which peaked at the CIN2 disease stage) helps maintain T cell proliferation and prevents CD3ζ chain loss under low‐arginine conditions. 45 The cysteine–methionine pathway is also crucial in tumor formation and cancer survival. 46 , 47 , 48 This pathway intersects with the glutathione metabolism pathway, playing a significant role in maintaining redox homeostasis. 49 We observed that levels of metabolites involved in the cysteine–methionine pathway, such as cystine, cysteine, serine, glycine, and glutamate, were decreased in CIN and cancer. Conversely GSSG, a key metabolite in the glutathione pathway, was elevated only in cancer. This suggests that altered metabolic flux in this pathway may contribute to the initiation and/or progression of cervical cancer.

We excluded HPV‐negative cases of CIN, SCC, and adenocarcinoma from the study cohort. One reason for this was to avoid sampling errors, as the small number of HPV‐negative CIN cases might have skewed the statistical analysis. Furthermore, the etiology of HPV‐negative cancers or adenocarcinoma may involve carcinogenic processes that are distinct from those of HPV‐positive cancer. Future research efforts with larger sample sizes are required to identify metabolite biomarkers in HPV‐negative cases. While there might be variations due to tumor size within the same stage, we did not analyze the extent or size of the tumors in the current study. However, comparing stages Ib1 and Ib2 or worse (Figure S4), we found that nicotinic acid and succinic acid levels decreased (FC > 2.0). No significant differences were found for other metabolites between these two groups. While succinic acid and nicotinic acid may be involved in the initiation of carcinogenesis, they may not affect cancer progression. Again, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings.

Our study has some limitations. First, our analysis focused on primary metabolites, meaning that unmeasured metabolites such as lipids have been overlooked. Second, metabolic profiles are influenced by factors such as diet, smoking, BMI, and age. However, individual data for factors other than BMI and age were not collected, thus limiting our consideration of these influences. Although there was no significant intergroup difference in BMI, there was a significant difference in age among disease groups. The risk of cancer tends to increase with age, limiting the ability to distinguish whether metabolic differences are due to aging or cancer. Third, we have not shown in vitro experiments to test the hypotheses generated from our analysis. In previous studies using cervical cancer‐derived cultured cells, the levels of nicotinamide, GSSG, AMP, and xanthine (which we identified) were significantly increased. 50 However, levels of kynurenine and malic acid, which showed excellent diagnostic ability for SCC in our study, were unchanged in the previous study. Some of these discrepancies might be explained by differences between cultured cells and clinical samples.

A fourth limitation is that our study cohort comprises patients identified with abnormalities by cytology during cancer screening. Therefore, we cannot compare the accuracy of cytology with our data. Future studies should involve multicenter collaborations to verify the reproducibility of our findings across different facilities. Additionally, epidemiological studies targeting the general population are necessary to compare the accuracy of traditional cytology or HPV tests with our method.

Finally, the metabolomic analysis reported here requires expensive analytical instruments, and ensuring sample quality is crucial. We used peak area value analysis using mass spectrometry for metabolites and did not directly measure metabolite concentrations. Although it cannot be immediately used in practical clinical settings as an auxiliary diagnostic tool, we have found that the metabolism of metabolites in the cervix dynamically increases or decreases during the initiation and progression of cervical tumors. If the measurement of these metabolites becomes easier in the future, it could be used to further elucidate the mechanisms of cancer development and may be exploited in novel clinical applications.

Despite these limitations, our study also has several strong points. The most significant novelty of our study lies in the comprehensive collection and analysis of mucus samples from patients progressing from precancerous lesions to invasive cancer. While previous studies have analyzed plasma, 16 , 17 , 18 , 51 this fluid contains a different spectrum of metabolites. Because our mucus samples are taken from the site‐of‐origin of cervical cancer development, they provide a more physiologically relevant source of metabolites that can be used to understand the initiation and progression of cancer. By measuring metabolite levels, we discovered that the metabolite flux varies progressively with increasing disease severity. This was further validated by our cohort study, supporting our hypothesis that some of these metabolites could be potential biomarkers. Indeed, even though distinguishing between CIN3 and cancer using histological examination is often challenging, our study successfully differentiated CIN3 and SCC with high accuracy using metabolites. Finally, cervical mucus is an excellent test sample that can be easily collected from patients in a minimally invasive manner and can be easily incorporated into clinical diagnostic workflows due to its abundance and significant content of disease‐related metabolites.

In conclusion, profiling of cervical mucus in precancerous lesions and SCC revealed distinct alterations in metabolite levels that were specific to each disease category; the metabolite changes were strongly associated with multiple canonical metabolic pathways. The analysis of cervical mucus, reflecting local metabolism and carried out with minimal invasiveness, has great potential as a low psychological burden ancillary screening method for women.
