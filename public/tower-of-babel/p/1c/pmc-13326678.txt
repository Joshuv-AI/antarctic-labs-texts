Aqueous extract of Duyun Maojian tea ameliorates the ileal microbiota and associated hepatic metabolome in HFD-induced obese mice
Authors: Xiaolu Zhou, Yonglong Zhang, Qian Wang, Nguyen Huy Hoang, Caibi Zhou, Shanggao Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Duyun Maojian tea (DYMJ), a renowned Chinese green tea, exhibits potential anti-obesity properties, though its mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated DYMJ's regulatory effects using a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mouse model, with Xuezhikang (XZK) as a positive control (HP). Hepatic/serum biochemical parameters, histopathology, liver metabolomics and ileal microbiota were analyzed. DYMJ significantly reduced body weight, hepatic malondialdehyde, aminotransferase activity and steatosis while enhancing superoxide dismutase activity. Gut microbiota analysis revealed that HFD-induced Firmicutes phylum related to energy dysregulation and insulin resistance was modulated by DYMJ. Notably, Anaerotruncus genus abundance was positively correlated with pyridoxal 5′-phosphate level. In contrast, XZK increased the abundance of Proteobacteria, potentially exacerbating insulin resistance despite improving energy metabolism. DYMJ treatment restored microbial balance closer to normal-diet (ND) conditions, particularly by elevating nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide and enriching Bacteroidetes phylum species associated with metabolic health. These findings suggested that DYMJ mitigated obesity through dual mechanisms: alleviating oxidative stress and hepatic lipid accumulation, while reshaping gut microbiota toward a metabolic health-promoting composition. This study supports DYMJ as a safe dietary supplement for body weight management, and highlights the gut–liver axis as a pivotal target for addressing metabolic disorders.

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a global pandemic, posing a severe threat to public health and imposing a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide. Its pathogenesis extends beyond simple energy imbalance, involving a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors. A hallmark of obesity is the disruption of lipid metabolism, manifested as dyslipidemia, which is usually characterized by elevated levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).1 This metabolic dysregulation is a primary driver for a series of associated complications, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).2 For decades, the management of obesity and its metabolic sequelae has relied on lifestyle interventions and pharmaceutical agents. While drugs (e.g., statins and fibrates) are effective, their long-term use is often affected by adverse effects, including muscle toxicity and abnormal liver function, which limits patient compliance and necessitates the exploration of safer alternatives. In this context, food and medicine homologous (FMH) resources have attracted increasing attention as safer alternatives for managing hyperlipidemia and related metabolic disorders.3

A critical frontier in understanding metabolic health is the gut–liver axis, a bidirectional communication network where the gut microbiota and its metabolites play a significant role.4 The gut microbiota, a complex ecosystem of trillions of microorganisms, is essential for host digestion, energy harvest, immune function, and the production of numerous metabolites.5 High-fat diets (HFD) are known to induce gut microbial dysbiosis—a state of ecological imbalance typically characterized by an increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, a reduction in microbial diversity, and a bloom of pro-inflammatory pathobionts.6 This dysbiosis impairs the integrity of the intestinal barrier, which increases gut permeability and allows the translocation of bacterial endotoxins, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), into the portal circulation. Upon reaching the liver, these microbial products trigger chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and subsequent metabolic dysfunction, thereby accelerating development of obesity and NAFLD.7

Tea [Camellia sinensis (L.) Kuntze] is one of the most widely consumed beverages globally, valued for its flavor and health benefits.8 The phytochemical complexity of C. sinensis derivatives is categorized into six principal types (green, black, white, oolong, dark, and yellow teas) through differential processing techniques.9 Green tea, a non-fermented tea, is particularly rich in polyphenols, with catechins such as epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), epigallocatechin (EGC), and epicatechin gallate (ECG) being the major constituents responsible for its potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-obesity, and hypolipidemic effects.10 Notably, green tea has been demonstrated to effectively alleviate the adverse health risks of HFD by reducing blood lipid levels and relieving intestinal microbiota disorder. Previous study reported that green tea polyphenols have been proven to ameliorate lipid metabolic dysfunction by regulating intestinal bacteria.11 Anji white tea improved HFD-induced obesity by alleviating liver oxidative injury, lipid metabolism, and gut microbiota.12 Duyun Maojian (DYMJ) tea is a renowned Chinese green tea, recognized as a National Geographical Indication Product (NGIP) from Guizhou Province. It was awarded the Excellence Award at the Panama–Pacific International Exposition in 1915, recognized as one of China's Top Ten Famous Teas in 1982, and inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2022. Grown in a unique high-altitude (800–1200 m), low-temperature, and cloudy-fog environment (annual fog days > 180), DYMJ is celebrated for its distinctive quality and is presumed to possess a rich profile of bioactive constituents.13

It is well established that tea polyphenols, especially catechins (e.g., EGCG), are primarily metabolized by the colonic microbiota, as most of these compounds escape absorption in the small intestine and undergo extensive fermentation in the colon.14 The ileum, as the final segment of the small intestine, is a critical site for nutrient absorption and host–microbiota interaction. Its microbial community is uniquely positioned to influence bile acid metabolism, immune signaling, and the production of key metabolites that directly impact hepatic physiology.15 Although the colon harbors a much higher density and diversity of polyphenol-metabolizing bacteria, the primary focus of this study is not to track the microbial metabolism of tea polyphenols per se, but to investigate the gut–liver crosstalk mediated by ileal microbes under HFD conditions and its amelioration by DYMJ. The ileal microbiota is anatomically and physiologically more directly connected to the liver via the portal circulation; its luminal and mucosa-associated microbes interact intimately with host absorptive and immune systems, and their metabolic products can rapidly reach the liver, thereby exerting a more immediate impact on hepatic physiology.16 In contrast, the colonic microbiota primarily influences host physiology through slower, fermentation-dependent mechanisms, and fecal excretion. Recent studies have demonstrated that small intestinal dysbiosis plays a key role in the development of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis and that analysis of small intestinal contents provides valuable insights into the gut–liver axis.17,18 Therefore, ileal content was chosen to better represent the microbiota that directly interacts with the host's absorptive and immune systems in the proximal gut–liver axis.

This study was therefore aimed to comprehensively investigate the preventive effects of DYMJ aqueous extract on obesity and associated metabolic disorders in an HFD-induced mice model (Fig. 1). We hypothesized that DYMJ would alleviate weight gain, dyslipidemia, and hepatic steatosis not only through direct metabolic actions but also via the modulation of the ileal microbiota and related hepatic metabolome. To test this hypothesis, we first characterized the major chemical constituents and metabolic profile of DYMJ. The intervention's effects were then evaluated through body weight, serum lipids, liver injury markers, oxidative stress, and hepatic histopathology. Most importantly, a multi-omics approach, integrating UPLC-MS/MS-based widely targeted metabolomics of liver tissues with 16S rRNA sequencing of ileal contents, was employed to reveal the coordinated changes in the host metabolism and gut microbial ecosystem. Furthermore, a correlation network analysis was conducted to explore the complex interactions between specific bacterial taxa and pivotal hepatic metabolites. Our findings provide a mechanistic foundation for the application of DYMJ as a functional food or a natural therapeutic agent against obesity and its related metabolic syndromes through the gut-liver axis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Duyun Maojian tea (DYMJ) aqueous extract

DYMJ aqueous extract was prepared following the method previously described.19 Specifically, DYMJ was ground and subjected to aqueous extraction with boiling water (100 °C) at a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1 : 10 (g mL−1) for 30 min with gentle stirring, followed by a second extraction of the residual dregs at a ratio of 1 : 8 (g mL−1) for 20 min. The two extracts were combined and filtered through three layers of gauze to remove insoluble residues. The obtained filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator (ChemTron STRIKE 285, Germany) under reduced pressure and subsequently lyophilized using a freeze dryer (Christ Beta 2–8 LD plus, Germany). The final freeze-dried powder was stored at −80 °C until further animal experiments.

Detection of major chemical constituents and metabolic profile of DYMJ

Total free amino acids were quantified by the ninhydrin chromogenic method following a previously described procedures,20 with absorbance measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader. Soluble sugars were determined by the anthrone–sulfuric acid assay using d-glucose as the standard, and absorbance was recorded at 628 nm with the same microplate reader.21 The content of water extracts was measured following the Chinese National Standards GB/T 8305-2013. Total polyphenol content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method according to the Chinese national standard GB/T 8313-2018. Briefly, 0.2 g of tea powder was extracted with 5 mL of 70% methanol at 70 °C for 10 min, centrifuged at 3500×g for 10 min, and re-extracted twice. The combined supernatants were diluted to 25 mL with 70% methanol, and then reacted with 5 mL 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 4 mL 7.5% Na2CO3 for 60 min, with absorbance at 765 nm quantified against gallic acid.

The contents of catechins and caffeine (CAF) were quantified using an Ultimate 3000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA), as previously described.13 Chromatographic separation was achieved by a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 HPLC column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 µm, Agilent, CA, USA) using a gradient elution program (0–5 min, 95% A; 5–15 min, 95–90% A; 15–20 min, 90–80% A; 20–25 min, 80–90% A; 25–30 min, 90–95% A). The analytical conditions were set as follows: mobile phases, 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B); column temperature 35 °C; flow rate 0.8 mL min−1; injection 10 µL. Catechins and CAF were quantified at 278 nm using external standard calibration curves, with triplicate analysis for each sample. EGCG, ECG, EGC, gallocatechin gallate (GCG), catechin (C), catechin gallate (CG), epicatechin (EC), gallocatechin (GC), and CAF standards (purity ≥ 98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA).

The metabolic profile of DYMJ was subjected to an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (UPLC, ExionLC™ AD) coupled with Tandem mass spectrometry system (MS/MS, QTRAP® 6500+), as previously described.13 The analytical conditions were set as follows: The UPLC column was ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters, MA, USA); the column temperature, flow rate, and injection volume were set at 40 °C, 0.4 mL min−1, 2 µL, respectively. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.04% acetic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.04% acetic acid (B), using the following gradient program: 95% A : 5% B (v/v) at 0 min, changing to 5% A : 95% B (v/v) at 11.0 min, held until 12.0 min, returned to 95% A : 5% B (v/v) at 12.1 min, and maintained until 14.0 min. LIT and triple quadrupole (QQQ) scans were acquired on a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer (QTRAP), QTRAP® 6500+ LC-MS/MS System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion–Spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: The source temperature was 500 °C; The ion spray voltage (IS) was 5500 V (positive), −4500 V (negative); The ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) were set at 55, 60, and 25.0 psi, respectively; The collision gas (CAD) was high. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 µmol L−1 polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period.

Animal experiments

Healthy male specific pathogen-free (SPF) Kunming mice with body weight 35 ± 2 g were obtained from Tianqin Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Qiannan, Guizhou, China). The mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions with a room temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 55 ± 5%, and were provided with unrestricted access to food and water. After 7 days of acclimatization, the experimental period was conducted over 28 days. A total of 50 mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 10): a normal diet group (ND), a high-fat diet group (HFD) for establishing an obesity model, a positive control group (HP) receiving HFD and Xuezhikang (XZK), a low-dose DYMJ group (HL) receiving HFD and low-dose DYMJ, and a high-dose DYMJ group (HH) receiving HFD and high-dose DYMJ. The ND group was fed a standard diet (corn, soybean meal, wheat bran, fish meal, vitamins, and minerals) and distilled water, while the other four groups received HFD consisting of 1.5% cholesterol, 10.0% lard, 5.0% egg yolk powder, 0.5% bile salt, and 83.0% standard diet. For liquid supplementation, mice in each group received a different gavage solution: distilled water for the HFD group, XZK (91 mg kg−1) for the HP group, and DYMJ at 210.0 mg kg−1 for the HL group or 840.0 mg kg−1 for the HH group. Based on a human-to-mouse dose conversion factor of approximately 9.1, the low (1-fold) and high (4-fold) doses for the DYMJ groups were derived from the recommended intake of tea for humans (6 g dry tea/60 kg adult/day, equivalent to 100 mg kg−1), calculated as 100 mg kg−1 × dose multiple × tea extraction yield. For XZK, the dose of 91 mg kg−1 was calculated from the clinical dose of 600 mg day−1 for a 60 kg adult (10 mg kg−1) converted to mouse dose using the same factor (10 mg kg−1 × 9.1 = 91 mg kg−1). The liver coefficient (%) was calculated as the ratio of liver weight to body weight. All animal experiments were approval by the Animal Experimental Ethical Inspection Committee of Qiannan Normal University for Nationalities (Approval No. 2024105), and all experimental procedures were performed in strict compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health).

Biochemical analysis of serum lipid indexes

Following the feeding experiment, the mice were fasted for 12 h with access to water and subsequently euthanized by blood collection from eyeball enucleation. After centrifugation at 3000×g and 4 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was collected and all biochemical assays were performed promptly. Serum total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were detected with the kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Determination of hepatic biochemical indexes

After rinsing with normal saline, 0.2 g of mouse liver tissue was homogenized in normal saline using an ice bath to prepare a 10% homogenate. Following centrifugation at 3000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Levels of TC, TG, malondialdehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) in the liver homogenate were measured using commercial kits from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Hepatic histological analysis

Fresh tissues from mouse liver were fixed in 10% formalin solution for over 24 h. Sections were dewaxed and rehydrated at 60 °C, followed by incubation in xylene for 10 min. The sections were then dehydrated with a graded alcohol series (75–100%) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for 5–10 min each. After subsequent dehydration with gradient alcohol (95–100%), the slices were sealed with the neutral gum and the images were obtained under the biological microscope (Motic Ltd Co., Xiamen, Fujian, China).

Metabolomics in liver

Fifty milligrams of liver were homogenized with cold steel balls in 1000 µL of ice-cold methanol/water (70%, v/v) at 30 Hz for 3 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 12 000×g and 4 °C for 10 min to obtain the supernatant. The sample extracts were analyzed using an UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, Shim-pack UFLC SHIMADZU CBM A system; MS, QTRAP® 6500+ System). The UPLC-MS/MS conditions were identical to those described for the metabolic profiling of DYMJ. Raw MS data were preprocessed with Analyst 1.6.3 to extract ion features, including mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time (RT), and peak intensity. Subsequent peak alignment, RT correction, and peak area extraction were performed using MultiQuant software. Metabolites were qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed by MS against an in-house MetWare database (MWDB, Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China).

Analysis of microbiota composition in ileal contents

Given the anatomical relevance of the ileum in gut–liver signaling, we analyzed the microbiota composition of ileal contents rather than colonic or faecal samples. Total genomic DNA was extracted from 100 mg of ileal content using the Genewiz MagBead Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Genewiz, Suzhou, China). DNA purity and concentration were assessed via Qubit® 4.0 and Nanodrop™ One. The V3–V4 regions of 16S rRNA were amplified with the upstream primer 341F (CCTACGGGRRBGCASCAGKVRVAAT) and the downstream primer 805R (GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAATCC). Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Kit (New England Biolabs) and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp, paired-end). Raw data have been deposited in National Genomics Data Center (GSA: CRA044417). Reads were processed with QIIME2 (2023.9). DADA2 was used for denoising, merging, and chimera removal to generate ASVs. Taxonomy was assigned against the Silva 138 database. Alpha (Chao1, Shannon) and beta diversity (Bray–Curtis) were calculated, with PCoA for visualization. LEfSe analysis (LDA > 2.0) was performed on the Galaxy platform. Statistical analysis was conducted using R (v4.0.3) using packages ‘vegan’ (v2.6–4), ‘ggplot2’ (v3.4.0), ‘pheatmap’ (v1.0.12), and ‘corrplot’ (v0.92). Pearson correlation was used to evaluate relationships between bacterial taxa and hepatic metabolites.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by SAS software (Ver. 9.2), and results were displayed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were performed in at least three biological replicates. Group differences were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by multiple comparisons using the least significant difference (LSD) method. Statistical significance between experimental groups was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Analysis of major chemical constituents and metabolic profile of DYMJ

The major chemical constituents of DYMJ were first characterized by routine analytical methods. The contents of free amino acids, soluble sugars, total tea polyphenols, and water extract were determined to be 3.14%, 2.45%, 28.56%, and 43.79%, respectively (Fig. 2A).

Overview of the main chemical constituents and metabolite profile of DYMJ. (A) Contents of free amino acids (FAA), soluble sugars (SS), total tea polyphenols (TP), and water extract (WE) determined by routine analytical methods. (B) Classification and proportion of metabolites identified by UPLC-MS/MS. (C) Individual contents of seven catechins (EGCG, EGC, ECG, GCG, EC, C, GC) and caffeine (CAF) quantified by HPLC analysis.

Subsequently, a widely targeted metabolomic approach based on UPLC-MS/MS was employed to comprehensively profile the chemical composition of DYMJ. As is known, green tea is abundant in bioactive compounds, particularly polyphenols, which are responsible for its significant medicinal values. These compounds exhibit potent anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, hypolipidemic properties.11 In the present study, a total of 3018 metabolites were identified after peak extraction and alignment. By matching the secondary mass spectra (containing all fragment ions) and retention times against an in-house database with a minimum match degree of 70%, 1404 metabolites were classified into 14 major categories based on their chemical structures (Table S1). These included 317 flavonoids, 187 lipids, 184 phenolic acids, 161 terpenoids, 100 amino acids and derivatives, 78 alkaloids, 72 saccharides, 59 tannins, 44 nucleotides and derivatives, 40 lignans and coumarins, 36 organic acids, 6 quinones, 6 steroids, and 114 other compounds. In terms of proportion, flavonoids (22.6%), lipids (13.3%), phenolic acids (13.1%), and terpenoids (11.5%) accounted for the largest fractions of the identified metabolites (Fig. 2C).

As catechins are recognized as the predominant bioactive components in green tea, we further quantified their individual contents using HPLC analysis. Among seven catechins and CAF, EGCG possessed the highest content of 98.01 mg g−1, followed by EGC (79.65 mg g−1), CAF (79.38 mg g−1), ECG (31.39 mg g−1), GCG (11.67 mg g−1), EC (9.97 mg g−1), C (4.82 mg g−1), and GC (1.06 mg g−1) (Fig. 2B). The detailed chemical composition and metabolic profile of DYMJ provide a solid basis for understanding its potential biological activities.

DYMJ reduces body and liver weights in HFD-induced obese mice

In this study, a mouse model of hyperlipidemia was induced by administration of HFD, with concomitant graded-dose DYMJ treatment to assess its preventive effects against obesity. After 28 days, the body weight of the HFD group was 18.0% higher than that of the ND group, with a 3.2-fold elevation in weight gain (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this, the HFD group showed a 27.9% increase in absolute liver weight and a 0.39% higher liver-to-body weight ratio. Administration of the lipid-lowering drug XZK (HP) was effective in reversing the HFD-induced increases in body weight gain, liver weight, and liver coefficient; however, the final body weight remained significantly higher (by 4.2%) than that of the ND group. Although the weight-reducing effect of low-dose DYMJ (HL) was inferior to HP, the high-dose DYMJ (HH) restored all weight-related indexes to the level observed in the ND group.

The effects of DYMJ on biochemical indexes and histopathology in HFD-induced obese mice

Regarding serum indicators, the HFD group displayed a significantly altered serum lipid profile relative to the ND group, with TC, TG, and LDL-C levels increased by 29.7%, 10.0%, and 23.1%, respectively, and HDL-C levels reduced by 24.2% (Fig. 3B). In contrast, interventions with both HP and DYMJ markedly attenuated these changes, significantly lowering TC, TG, and LDL-C and raising HDL-C compared to the HFD group. Notably, the high-dose DYMJ (HH) produced an effect comparable to the lipid-lowering drug XZK (HP), with its impact on TG reduction being 19.6% greater than that of XZK.

Hepatic injury associated with HFD-induced obesity was indicated by a significant release of transaminases. Specifically, the HFD group exhibited a 60.8% higher ALT level than the ND group (Fig. 3C). In contrast, both the HP and DYMJ groups showed significantly lower ALT levels. Notably, HH demonstrated a stronger hepatoprotective effect than XZK, as indicated by a 14.5% greater restoration of ALT activity. Meanwhile, the HFD-induced 3.0-fold increase in AST was significantly attenuated by DYMJ or XZK. More effectively, treatment with HP or HH restored AST levels to those of the ND group. Oxidative stress, indicated by a significant 19.2% increase in MDA in the HFD group, was significantly alleviated by DYMJ and XZK treatments. Conversely, SOD activity was only significantly restored by XZK, whereas the increases seen with HP (9.5%) and HH (4.1%) interventions, despite showing a partial restorative trend, did not reach statistical significance. Histopathological examination of liver sections demonstrated that ND group exhibited a well-preserved liver architecture, characterized by uniform hepatocyte size and consistency within the cytoplasm (Fig. 3D). In contrast, HFD group displayed a marked loss of normal hepatocytes, disorganization of hepatic lobule structure, severe steatosis accompanied by ballooning degeneration, and accumulation of lipid droplets within the cells. Whereas, the interventions with XZK and DYMJ effectively ameliorated hepatocyte steatosis and reduced lipid droplet deposition.

Liver metabolome

A widely-targeted metabolomic analysis of mouse liver tissue was conducted to assess the influence of DYMJ intervention on hepatic metabolism. Totally, 602 metabolites were identified in the metabolome profiling of liver homogenate. The orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) score plots showed obvious separations between HFD and the other groups (Fig. 4A).

Liver metabolomics analysis reveals DYMJ-induced metabolic alterations in HFD-fed mice. (A) OPLS-DA score plots between HFD and the other groups. (B) Heatmap of representative differentially accumulated metabolites across all groups.

Comparative analysis revealed a marked upregulation of metabolites in HFD relative to ND. These differentially expressed metabolites were primarily enriched in key metabolic pathways, including the phosphotransferase system (PTS), glucagon signaling pathway, insulin resistance, carbohydrate digestion and absorption (CDA), as well as the metabolism of fructose and mannose, galactose, starch and sucrose, pyruvate, and propanoate (Table 1). Notably, the upregulated metabolites fell into three distinct biochemical categories: monosaccharides and derivatives (e.g., d-fructose 6-phosphate, d-galactose, d-glucose, d-glucose 6-phosphate, d-mannose, and d-mannose 6-phosphate); disaccharides and polyols (e.g., d-sucrose, lactose, d-mannitol, d-sorbitol, and dulcitol); and energy metabolism intermediates (e.g., dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), l-lactic acid (L-LA), d-lactic acid (D-LA), and succinic acid) (Fig. 4B and Table S2). This metabolic profile suggested enhanced gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance under HFD conditions, which are mechanistically linked to hepatic lipid accumulation.

Whereas, the consumption of lipid-lowering drug XZK (HP) and DYMJ reversed the metabolic alterations induced by HFD. More specifically, HP group exhibited reduced levels of 14,15-EET [(±)14(15)-epoxy-5Z,8Z,11Z-eicosatrienoic acid], chenodeoxycholic acid, deoxycholic acid, and glycocholic acid within the pathways of secondary BA biosynthesis, bile secretion, arachidonic acid metabolism, and serotonergic synapse, relative to HFD group. Conversely, XZK treatment specifically promoted the enrichment of saccharides involved in galactose metabolism and CDA pathway. Notably, administration of DYMJ at both doses decreased the levels of saccharides, L-LA, and DHAP across key pathways involving PTS, CDA, insulin resistance, and carbohydrate metabolism (fructose and mannose, galactose, starch and sucrose). Additionally, the low-dose DYMJ also impaired these metabolites in ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters pathway and inositol phosphate metabolism, whereas the high-dose DYMJ uniquely suppressed the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and taste transduction pathways associated with D-sorbitol and dulcitol.

Moreover, relative to ND, HP exhibited higher concentrations of saccharides, dulcitol, l-LA, DHAP, N6-acetyl-l-lysine, succinic acid, and urocanic acid, which were enriched in the pathways of PTS, CDA, and lysine degradation, and the metabolisms of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar, histidine, fructose and mannose, starch and sucrose, and galactose. While, the low-dose DYMJ (HL) demonstrated markedly elevated levels of phosphorylated glucose and mannose that were mainly associated with the metabolisms of starch and sucrose, PTS, and inositol phosphate, in comparison to ND. Notably, the high-dose DYMJ (HH) displayed a metabolic profile most comparable to ND, differing only in histidine and linoleic acid metabolisms, though no metabolites with significant differences were identified.

Microbiota composition in ileal contents

Given that gut microbiota dysbiosis can impair intestinal barrier function, which promote intestinal permeability and systemic inflammation, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing of the V3–V4 regions on the Genewiz platform to characterize the gut microbiota composition across all five groups. Ileal microbial profiling revealed distinct microbiota architectures among experimental groups, with operational taxonomic units (OTUs) clustered at 97% sequence similarity threshold. Sufficient sequencing depth was confirmed through stabilized rarefaction curves, ensuring comprehensive species representation (Fig. 5A). Core microbiome analysis identified 190 conserved OTUs across all groups. Diet-specific modulation was evident: the ND group exhibited the highest α-diversity with the most unique OTUs; HFD depleted 60 ND-associated OTUs and introduced 15 novel taxa; HP induced 15 unique OTUs, indicating protein-driven selection; whereas the HL and HH groups acquired only 0 and 1 novel OTU, respectively. Notably, dietary interventions differentially restored HFD-depleted microbiota: HP recovered 34 OTUs of the 60 lacking OTUs, and inhibited 2 OTUs induced by HFD; HL and HH recovered 23 and 20 OTUs, and inhibited 3 and 10, respectively.

Bacterial richness (Chao1 index) was highest in ND and comparable to that in HP and HL (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, the Shannon index, which also considers species evenness, revealed that HFD treatment reduced overall diversity (Fig. 5C). Despite this reduction induced by HFD, the Shannon index indicated a greater number of bacterial species in HP than in ND. Beta diversity (variation in species composition between habitats) indicates biodiversity differences between samples. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) showed that replicates within each group clustered closely, while inter-group differences were more pronounced than intra-group differences (Fig. 5D). Notably, the ND group was the most distanced from the HP group but exhibited similarity to the DYMJ-treated groups, particularly HH. In contrast, there was a considerable distance between the HFD and DYMJ groups. Furthermore, the sample clustering analysis using UPGMA Tree demonstrated that the replicates of the ND group clustered tightly together, distinctly separate from the other groups (Fig. 5E). The HFD group showed overlap with both the HP and HL groups, whereas the HH group displayed relative independence.

The community structure at the phylum level was illustrated in a histogram (Fig. 5F). Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the predominant phyla in the ileal microbiota across all groups. In comparison to ND, the proportion of Firmicutes in HFD was significantly increased to 82.2%, while that of Bacteroidetes decreased to 6.9%. This shift reflected a state of dysbiosis associated with HFD, marked by an expansion of harmful bacteria and a contraction of beneficial taxa. Furthermore, the abundance of Proteobacteria was notably elevated in HFD, HP and HL groups. Relative to HFD, the relative abundance of Firmicutes was reduced in HP and DYMJ, whereas that of Bacteroidetes was increased. It was notable that the Firmicutes content in the HH group (48.6%) was even lower compared to the ND group.

The LEfSe analysis identified distinct microbial biomarkers for each group (Fig. 5G and Table S3). Specifically, the order Mollicutes_RF39 was significantly enriched in the ND group. In contrast, the HFD, HP, HL, and HH groups were characterized by a marked increase in Firmicutes phylum, Proteobacteria phylum, Lachnospiraceae genus, and Bacteroidetes phylum, respectively.

Integrated analysis of ileal microbiota and hepatic metabolome

Considering that the gut microbiota influences the liver primarily through its metabolites and by altering intestinal barrier function, we investigated the complex interactions between the gut microbiome and liver metabolism. As shown in Fig. 6A, pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) was positively correlated with the obesity indexes, while nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NAAD) was negatively correlated. Another correlation analysis was performed between biochemical indexes and critical microbiota from the LEfSe results (Fig. 6B).

Correlation analysis. (A) Correlation between obesity-related indexes and hepatic metabolites. (B) Correlation between ileal microbiota (from LEfSe results in Fig. 5G and Table S3) and chemical indexes.

Bacterial taxa in the HFD group were strongly positively correlated with obesity indices and certain hepatic metabolites. Specifically, Anaerotruncus genus was highly associated with obesity indexes and PLP, whereas Firmicutes phylum, Lachnospiraceae family, Clostridiales order, and Clostridia class showed significant correlations with d-glucopyranose, d-LA, and l-LA. Interestingly, HP exhibited a similar heatmap pattern with HFD, characterized by significant associations between a cluster of phylogenetically related taxa (Proteobacteria phylum, Deltaproteobacteria class, Desulfovibrionales order, and Desulfovibrionaceae family) and succinic acid and methylmalonic acid.

The specific gut microbiota Lachnospiraceae_UCG_001 genus in HL had little correlation with the biochemical parameters. Meanwhile, the HH group shared a similar correlation profile with the ND group, where multiple taxa including Ruminococcus_1 genus, Ruminococcaceae_UCG_014 genus, Mollicutes_RF39 order, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group genus, and Prevotellaceae family were highly related with l-ascorbate, 2-aminoadipic acid, glycocholic acid, oxoODEs, 3-N-methyl-l-histidine, l-carnosine, histamine, and 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid (5HIAA). However, the relative abundance of these bacteria was relatively lower in other groups (Table S3). In the HH group, Bacteroidaceae family, Bacteroides genus, Tannerellaceae family, Parabacteroides genus, Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 genus, Muribaculaceae family, Bacteroidia class, Bacteroidales order, and Bacteroidetes phylum presented a negative correlation with Asp-Phe methyl ester (aspartame), d-glucopyranose, and l/d-LA. Furthermore, NAAD was highly correlated with Bacteroides genus, Bacteroidaceae family, Muribaculaceae family, Bacteroidales order, Bacteroidia class and Bacteroidetes phylum.

In summary, the correlation network revealed three major gut–liver axis patterns modulated by DYMJ. First, HFD-induced obesity was associated with positive correlations between Anaerotruncus and pro-inflammatory PLP, as well as between Firmicutes-related taxa and LA. Second, high-dose DYMJ (HH) shifted these correlations toward a beneficial profile, characterized by strong positive associations between Bacteroidetes-affiliated taxa (e.g., Bacteroides, Muribaculaceae) and NAAD. Third, in contrast to DYMJ, the positive control XZK (HP) did not suppress Proteobacteria-associated correlations (e.g., with succinic acid and methylmalonic acid), indicating divergent mechanisms.

Discussion

Green tea, a type of non-fermented tea, is known to its health benefits in the alleviation of obesity-related metabolic syndromes by improving lipid metabolism, reducing hepatic inflammation, and regulating gut microbiota.11,22 Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which DYMJ provides disease protection have not been fully revealed. It remains unclear whether its hepatoprotective action involves the concurrent maintenance of gut microbiota homeostasis (as exemplified by intestinal barrier function) and a reshaped hepatic immune microenvironment. Our research indicated that the preventive effect of DYMJ against obesity was not only associated with hepatic metabolic pathways but was also attributable to its impact on the gut microbiota and intestinal barrier.

Regular indexes

In this study, the obesity model was successfully established, as evidenced by significant increases in both body weight and liver weight in the HFD group. Following intervention, only the HH group restored these parameters to the level of the ND group, whereas the HP group (XZK) showed partial but incomplete recovery. In terms of blood lipid profiles, DYMJ and XZK alleviated HFD-induced dyslipidemia, with high-dose DYMJ exhibiting effects comparable to XZK and a more pronounced reduction in TG. Besides, liver damage induced by HFD was alleviated in both HH and HP groups, with AST and MDA levels restored to those in ND group; high-dose DYMJ also showed a greater effect on ALT reduction compared to XZK. Collectively, these results indicate that DYMJ has efficacy comparable to, and in some parameters better than, XZK in reducing body weight and lipid levels. To further explore the mechanistic basis for these differences, we performed integrated metabolomic and microbiota analyses.

XZK was selected as a positive control because it is a widely used, clinically approved lipid-lowering agent in China,23 providing a relevant benchmark for the efficacy of DYMJ as a dietary supplement. We acknowledge that XZK and DYMJ have distinct mechanisms of action; XZK primarily inhibits HMG-CoA reductase via statin-like compounds, whereas DYMJ's effects are attributed to catechins and other polyphenols acting through gut microbiota modulation and antioxidant pathways. Therefore, the observed differences in TG and ALT recovery do not imply mechanistic superiority but rather highlight the potential of DYMJ as a complementary dietary strategy. Dose equivalence between the two interventions cannot be established due to their different pharmacological and nutritional natures. Future studies should include an EGCG control group and use dose-finding designs to enable more direct comparisons.

Metabolic pathways

In HFD, DHAP was elevated in the metabolic pathways of pyruvate and propanoate. DHAP is an intermediate product linking glycerol and gluconeogenesis.24 The glucagon signaling pathway was also up-regulated, which promoted glycogen decomposition and gluconeogenesis, thereby increasing blood glucose to induce hyperglycemia.25 CDA pathway was enhanced. PTS is a class of transport systems involved in carbohydrate phosphorylation and regulation of microbial gene expression. It was enriched in the obese human gut microbiome to process carbohydrates.26 The obese microbiome exhibits an increased capacity to harvest energy from the die.27 Carbohydrate levels were elevated in the galactose, starch, and sucrose metabolism pathways, with glucose, mannose, and fructose promoting insulin synthesis and secretion.28 However, insulin resistance had happened in the obese mice. In brief, HFD increased DHAP and glucagon levels, enhanced gluconeogenesis as well as carbohydrate absorption and phosphorylation, and stimulated bacteria involved in sugar utilization. Meanwhile, the rise in blood glucose promoted insulin secretion but also led to insulin resistance. The accumulation of carbohydrates and their intermediates in HFD-fed mice, despite a low-carbohydrate HFD, reflects insulin resistance-driven enhancement of gluconeogenesis rather than a block in glycolysis.29 This is supported by upregulation of the glucagon signaling pathway and elevated gluconeogenic intermediates (DHAP, glucose-6-phosphate, lactate), without accumulation of downstream glycolytic products.30 Importantly, these intermediates directly fuel hepatic lipid accumulation: DHAP provides the glycerol-3-phosphate backbone for TG synthesis, glucose-6-phosphat activates the lipogenic transcription factor ChREBP, and lactate promotes de novo lipogenesis while suppressing fatty acid oxidation.31 Thus, the observed increase in carbohydrate metabolites is causally related to hepatic steatosis.

In HP, XZK down-regulated secondary BA biosynthesis, bile secretion, arachidonic acid metabolism, and serotonergic synapse, but promoted galactose metabolism and CDA (HFD vs. HP). The secondary BAs are derived from the primary BAs through the process of deconjugation and dihydroxylation. HFD increase secondary BAs, which is a risk factor for colonic inflammation. These effects may be due to the increased production of short chain fatty acids (SCFAs).32 Functional components in XZK, such as isoflavones, repressed BA reabsorption and augmented BA biliary secretion, thereby lowering the hepatic BAs and increasing the fecal BAs.33 DYMJ and XZK differentially modulated BA profiles: DYMJ primarily reduced conjugated BAs, whereas XZK lowered unconjugated species and overall bile secretion (Table S4), suggesting distinct mechanisms of action on BA metabolism.

Furthermore, XZK modulated arachidonic acid metabolism. Arachidonic acid is easy to be oxidized and produces a plethora of metabolites related to inflammation.34 In serotonergic synapse pathway, XZK decreased the postsynaptic axonal terminals and increased the presynaptic axonal terminals. The attenuation of food intake could be induced by an increase in serotonergic efficacy.35 Moreover, HP up-regulated amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism which was induced by oxidation,36 promoted lysine degradation pathway which were involved in the modulation of microbiota composition related with obesity (e.g., Prevotella and Ruminococcus), and accelerated histidine metabolism. Urocanic acid in histidine metabolism was 2.8-fold higher in HP, even more than that in HFD (2.2-fold higher). The increased level of urocanic acid, the deamination product of histidine, was closely related to significantly associated with changes in body weight, BMI and waist/hip ratio.37 Taken together, XZK might achieve certain effects on reducing weight, and thus BA secretion is weakened, and secondary BA and arachidonic acid related to inflammation were reduced; The histidine associated with obesity and lysine associated with beneficial bacteria in obese people were changed; But the absorption of carbohydrates and metabolism of amino sugars and nucleotide sugars were increased.

The low-dose DYMJ treatment down-regulated many pathways. Especially, it decreased glucose and DHAP in the inositol phosphate metabolism. Inositol phosphates might enhance the browning of white adipocytes and directly improve insulin sensitivity through adipocytes.38 In addition, the low-dose DYMJ down-regulated ABC transporters which are involved in cholesterol metabolism.39 ABC transporters were just found to have a relationship with obesity a few years ago,26 and herein DYMJ might inhibit the transport of saccharide into cell membrane. The transporters of d-sucrose, d-mannose and d-mannitol were decreased, while the transporters of deoxyguanosine were increased. Briefly, glucose, DHAP and the transportation of saccharides into cell membrane were reduced in HL. The high-dose DYMJ treatment down-regulated more pathways and almost recovered them to ND level. Gluconeogenesis was inappropriately activated in obese mice,40 but it was reversed in HH. There is a direct link with HFD increasing fat taste thresholds.41 Among taste bud cells, HFD might mediate signaling cascade in Type II receptor cells, and high-dose DYMJ alleviated this effect.

Relationship between microbiota and metabolome

A total of 75 OTUs were altered by the HFD, and 20 OTUs that were detected only in the ND group were disappeared in all HFD-fed groups, irrespective of DYMJ or XZK intervention (Fig. 5A). Disruption of the gut microbiota (dysbiosis) by HFD contributes to the development of metabolic disorders. Anaerotruncus genus belongs to Oscillospiraceae family, Eubacteriales (a synonym of Clostridiales) order, Clostridia class and Firmicutes phylum. Anaerotruncus is an inflammatory-related bacteria.42 It herein was positively correlated with PLP, which was associated with inflammation and polyunsaturated fatty acids.43 Moreover, Anaerotruncus and PLP were both correlated with obesity indexes. Another specific bacterium in HFD, Lachnospiraceae family, belongs to Eubacteriales order, Clostridia class and Firmicutes phylum. It produces SCFAs, and herein was highly associated with LA. It inhibits intestinal inflammation, enhances energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity, maintains the intestinal barrier, and modulates gut motility.44 Thus, although Firmicutes phylum was induced by HFD, Anaerotruncus genus and Lachnospiraceae family had different effects: Anaerotruncus potentially promoted PLP, inflammation, and obesity, whereas Lachnospiraceae contributed to anti-inflammation, energy metabolism, and insulin sensitivity.

Compared to ND and HFD, HP induced more pronounced disruptions in metabolic pathways and enriched the abundance of obesity-associated bacteria. The specific Desulfovibrionaceae family identified in the HP group was classified within Desulfovibrionales order, Deltaproteobacteria class and Proteobacteria phylum. In the HFD, HP, and HL groups, Proteobacteria phylum ranked third in abundance among all phyla, following only Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (Fig. 5F). This phylum is typically overrepresented in obese individuals.45 Disturbances in glucose homeostasis were associated with alterations in the intestinal microbiota, specifically an expansion of Proteobacteria. In addition, pH, bile flow, and hormonal fluctuations (especially of glucagon-like peptides) can influence the abundance of Proteobacteria.46 In HP, Proteobacteria abundance was positively correlated with metabolites linked to impaired glucose regulation (succinate and methylmalonic acid). Consistent with previous reports, succinate did not improve glucose control despite inducing mitochondrial biogenesis,47 and elevated methylmalonic acid predicted insulin resistance in obesity.48 Thus, XZK failed to suppress Proteobacteria or alleviate glucose dysregulation.

Despite some overlap in the hierarchical clustering (Fig. 6B), HH and ND samples preserved unique profiles. In ND, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group genus belongs to Prevotellaceae family, Bacteroidales order, Bacteroidia class, and Bacteroidetes phylum. Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group was negatively correlated with serum concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α, exhibiting anti-inflammatory properties.49Mollicutes_RF39 order, belongs to Mollicutes class and Tenericutes phylum, was associated with allergic response and immune cell activity in the mesenteric lymph nodes of mice.50Ruminococcus genus also belongs to Oscillospiraceae (a synonym of Ruminococcaceae) family and Firmicutes phylum. Both Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group and Oscillospiraceae family produce SCFAs and alleviate chronic inflammation and insulin resistance. Previous study suggested that Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, Mollicutes_RF39, Ruminococcus_1, and Ruminococcaceae_UCG_014 were all found to be beneficial for mitigating diabetes.51 In ND, these bacteria were highly correlated with the differential metabolites affected by HFD. For example, it has been demonstrated that 2-aminoadipic acid in lysine metabolism could modulate insulin secretion and predict type 2 diabetes.52 Absorption of ascorbate could be impaired by lipopolysaccharide which was promoted by gut barrier dysfunction induced by HFD.53 The serum GCA-to-total BA ratio is independently associated with NAFLD.54 9-OxoODE and 13-oxoODE, which are oxidation products of linoleic acid, are mechanistically linked to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH).55 Histamine and 3-N-methyl-l-histidine, both of which were involved in histidine metabolism, were decreased in piglets with a low-protein diet.56 In addition, the increase in neuronal histamine may mediate potent anti-obesity and antidiabetic effects.57 As a carbonyl scavenger, carnosine could ameliorate dyslipidaemia in Zucker obese rats.58 In obese individuals, 5HIAA, a known gut microbial metabolite of the Alistipes genus, is positively associated with level of C-reactive protein, which is produced by the liver in response to inflammation.59 Meantime, 5HIAA is a known gut microbial metabolite related to Alistipes genus (Bacteroidetes phylum).59 In short, these beneficial metabolites were implicated in anti-inflammatory, insulin-secretory, anti-dyslipidemic, and anti-obesity activities. The balance of these pathways appeared to be regulated by critical microbiota in ND.

Meanwhile, the beneficial bacteria that were more abundant in the HH group than in the ND group were exclusively from Bacteroidetes phylum. The Bacteroides genus, belonging to Bacteroidaceae family, Bacteroidales order, Bacteroidia class, and Bacteroidetes phylum, is characterized by its ability to hydrolyze polysaccharides.60Parabacteroides (Tannerellaceae family, Bacteroidales order) is an acetate/succinate producer that reduces blood neutrophil counts, which contributes to its anti-inflammatory activity.61 Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, a member of Prevotellaceae family, is involved in proteolytic metabolism and may contribute to the indirect enhancement of fiber degradation.62 Herein, it was negatively correlated with aspartame. Muribaculaceae family are recognized as butyrate-producing bacteria within the Bacteroidales order, with potential health benefits including the amelioration of lipid metabolic disorders and obesity alleviation.63 In our study, it was inversely proportional to d-glucopyranose following administration of high-dose DYMJ (HH). Interestingly, a recent study in mice demonstrated that Muribaculaceae emerged as key utilizers of monosaccharides.64 More importantly, Bacteroides genus and Muribaculaceae family were positively associated with NAAD, a key intermediate in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) salvage pathway. NAAD levels exhibited a positive correlation with NAD synthesis and a negative association with age.65 Given the critical role of NAD in maintaining energy balance and circadian rhythms, it has beneficial metabolic effects that alleviates obesity and its related disruptions in diurnal rhythmicity.66 In a word, the increase in gut microbiota abundance in HH was associated with enhanced degradation of saccharides, amino acids, and lipids, as well as an elevated level of NAAD, a metabolite that may contribute to energy homeostasis.

Although tea polyphenols are primarily metabolized in the colon, changes in the ileal microbiota can still reflect the regulatory mechanisms of DYMJ polyphenol metabolism, because the ileum serves as a key sensor and transmitter of gut–liver signals. A critical question arises: What specific constituent in DYMJ is responsible for the enrichment of Bacteroidetes and the associated beneficial metabolites? Our chemical characterization identified EGCG as the most abundant catechin in DYMJ (98.01 mg g−1), followed by EGC (79.65 mg g−1). These values were notably higher than those typically reported for other well-known Chinese green teas, such as Longjing and Biluochun (EGCG: approximately 50–80 mg g−1),67,68 indicating that DYMJ was particularly rich in bioactive catechins. Increasing evidence has directly implicated EGCG in modulating gut microbiota composition toward a metabolic health-promoting profile. Previous study demonstrated that 12-weeks EGCG intervention (50 mg kg−1) in HFD-fed mice not only ameliorated weight gain and liver dysfunction but also significantly decreased the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and restored ileal barrier integrity via stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α).69 This aligned with a recent review by Zhang et al.,14 who emphasized that EGCG modulated gut microbiota composition by promoting beneficial Bacteroides (Bacteroidetes phylum) and upregulating intestinal tight junction proteins, thus exerting systemic health effects through the gut–liver axis. Notably, the enhanced enrichment of Bacteroidetes observed with high-dose DYMJ might be explained by the selective substrate-utilization capacity of these bacteria. In vitro evidence further indicated that EGCG directly stimulated the growth of beneficial Bacteroides species while suppressing pathogenic bacteria.70Bacteroides are proficient polysaccharide hydrolyzers that convert dietary fibers into short-chain fatty acids, which in turn strengthen intestinal barrier integrity, reduce endotoxin translocation, and alleviate hepatic inflammation.71 Moreover, Qu et al. systematically profiled the utilization of 20 medicinal polysaccharides and polyphenolic compounds by 28 human gut Bacteroides and Parabacteroides species, revealing strain-specific substrate specificities.71 Although EGCG is not a polysaccharide, its gallate moiety and polyphenolic structure may be recognized by the same bacterial enzymes that process dietary polyphenols. This provided an ecological advantage for Bacteroidetes in the presence of catechin-rich DYMJ. Consistent with this, our integrated analysis showed that the HH group exhibited strong positive correlations between Bacteroides and NAAD, as well as between the Bacteroidetes phylum and NAAD. NAAD has been positively associated with NAD synthesis and beneficial metabolic effects that alleviate obesity-related disruptions in energy balance and circadian rhythmicity.65,66 Collectively, these findings suggested that the catechin-rich profile of DYMJ, particularly its high EGCG and EGC content, likely contributed directly to the amelioration of HFD-induced obesity by enriching Bacteroidetes-affiliated taxa, elevating NAAD levels, and thereby promoting gut-liver metabolic homeostasis. Thus, although the ileal microbiota does not directly degrade polyphenols, its compositional shifts accurately reflect the downstream regulatory effects of DYMJ polyphenol metabolism on the gut–liver axis. To causally validate this proposed mechanism, future studies should utilize catechins (e.g., EGCG) extracted from DYMJ in conjunction with fecal microbiota transplantation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that DYMJ effectively ameliorated HFD-induced obesity and associated metabolic dysfunctions in mice. High-dose DYMJ not only restored body and liver weights to normal levels but also improved serum lipid profiles, attenuated liver injury, and reduced oxidative stress. Through integrated metabolomic and microbiota analyses, we revealed that DYMJ modulated key hepatic metabolic pathways including carbohydrate metabolism, insulin resistance, and bile acid biosynthesis, along with restored gut microbiota homeostasis by promoting beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bacteroidetes) and suppressing obesity-associated taxa (e.g., Firmicutes). The correlation between specific microbial shifts and metabolic improvements underscored the role of the gut-liver axis in mediating the protective effects of DYMJ. Importantly, high-dose DYMJ exhibited comparable or improved effects in certain metabolic parameters relative to the positive control XZK. These results highlight DYMJ as a promising dietary supplement or functional food for the prevention and management of obesity and related metabolic syndromes.
