Stent-assisted coil embolization for a graft-related aneurysm following high-flow bypass: illustrative case
Authors: Hiroki Ota, Ayumu Yamaoka, Issei Asari, Chie Kamada, Ryota Sato, Katsuya Komatsu, Sangnyon Kim, Yukinori Akiyama, Nobuhiro Mikuni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDHigh-flow bypass (HFB) combined with parent artery occlusion is an established treatment for complex cerebral aneurysms. Graft-related aneurysm (GA) at the anastomotic site is a rare late complication of HFB, and its optimal management remains poorly defined. The authors report a case of an unruptured GA that enlarged more than 10 years after HFB and was treated with stent-assisted coil embolization (SACE).OBSERVATIONSA 50-year-old woman had undergone HFB using a radial artery (RA) graft for a cavernous segment aneurysm of the internal carotid artery 10 years earlier. A small aneurysmal dilation at the anastomosis was detected 2 years postoperatively, but long-term follow-up was interrupted. Ten years after the initial surgery, imaging revealed enlargement of a saccular GA with a bleb at the anastomotic site. SACE was performed through the tortuous RA graft using a distal access catheter for stable support, with deployment of an intracranial stent across the anastomosis to secure coil stability. Complete aneurysm occlusion was achieved without procedural complications. High-flow bypass (HFB) combined with parent artery occlusion has long been used as a definitive treatment for complex cerebral aneurysms, including giant or cavernous segment aneurysms of the internal carotid artery (ICA).1–3 Previous studies on long-term outcomes after HFB have primarily focused on graft patency and ischemic complications.2,3 In contrast, a graft-related aneurysm (GA) at the anastomosis site is a rare late complication, and its optimal management remains poorly defined. To date, most reported cases of GA following HFB have been treated surgically, and endovascular treatment has not been well documented. Here, we report a rare case of an unruptured GA that gradually enlarged more than 10 years after HFB and was successfully treated with stent-assisted coil embolization (SACE).

Illustrative Case

A 40-year-old woman was admitted with diplopia and ptosis. MR angiography (MRA) of the head revealed a cavernous segment aneurysm of the right ICA along with two small aneurysms (Fig. 1A). We planned surgical treatment combined with revascularization. A radial artery (RA) graft was anastomosed end-to-side to the right M2 inferior trunk and subsequently to the external carotid artery (Fig. 1B–D). An HFB was then established, and the right ICA was ligated (Fig. 2A). The aneurysm at the ICA–posterior communicating artery junction and the C2 segment were clipped. The perioperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged without new neurological deficits. At the 2-year follow-up, a small aneurysmal dilation was detected at the anastomotic site (Fig. 2B). The patient was followed regularly for 3 years postoperatively but subsequently discontinued follow-up on her own. Ten years postoperatively, at the age of 50 years, she was referred for evaluation of right-sided mydriasis by a local ophthalmologist. The pupillary findings were unchanged from those observed 10 years earlier, and no new neurological deficits were noted. However, MRA revealed a saccular aneurysm with a bleb at the anastomotic site, representing a GA. Cerebral angiography showed that the aneurysm measured 5.8 mm × 5.5 mm at the dome, 5.3 mm in height, and 5.3 mm in neck width (Fig. 2C–E). Three M2 branches originated from the M1 segment, and an aneurysm arose directly from the anastomotic site between the RA graft and the inferior M2 branch. The patient had developed hypertension 1 year before re-presentation and was treated with antihypertensive medications. She also had multiple small aneurysms involving the right posterior cerebral artery, splenic artery, and left renal artery. There was no laboratory evidence suggestive of a connective tissue disorder, although genetic testing was not performed. Her family history included subarachnoid hemorrhage in her grandmother. Based on the aneurysm’s size, morphology, and the patient’s family history of subarachnoid hemorrhage, therapeutic intervention was considered appropriate. Based on the wide-neck morphology of the GA, SACE was planned.

Endovascular Treatment

Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin (100 mg/day) and prasugrel (3.75 mg/day) was initiated 14 days before the procedure. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. After induction of anesthesia, intraoperative motor evoked potential (MEP) monitoring was established. A 6-Fr Axcelguide 78-cm guiding sheath (GS; Medikit Co. Ltd.) was inserted via the right femoral artery and positioned in the right common carotid artery. Using an Excelsior SL-10 Microcatheter 150 cm (microcatheter [MC]; Stryker) over a Synchro SELECT Soft Guidewire 215 cm (Stryker), we carefully advanced a 6-Fr SOFIASELECT 125-cm distal access catheter (DAC; Terumo Corporation) into the markedly tortuous and curved RA graft (Fig. 2C). The DAC was positioned extracranially, without being advanced close to the GA (Fig. 3A). The MC was navigated to the M1 segment through the DAC. Another MC was inserted through the GS and navigated to the aneurysm (Fig. 3B). The selected framing coil was a Target XL 360 Soft Coil 6 mm × 20 cm (Stryker), and two loops were deployed within the GA. A Neuroform Atlas Stent System 4.0 mm × 21 mm (Stryker) was deployed from the proximal segment of the right M2 inferior trunk, with the stent struts intentionally positioned to protrude from the origin of the right M2 and extend into the RA graft (Fig. 3C). A framing coil was deployed, followed by the placement of a Target 360 Soft Coil 5 mm × 10 cm as a filling coil (Fig. 3E). With these coils, a volumetric embolization rate of 27.6% was achieved. Final angiography confirmed complete aneurysm occlusion without distal embolism (Fig. 3F). No abnormalities were observed in the MEP waveforms during the procedure. The operation time was 3 hours 20 minutes, and the total heparin dose was 5000 units.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient awoke uneventfully from anesthesia, with no new neurological deficits observed. Postoperative MRI demonstrated complete obliteration of the GA, with preserved patency of the right M1 and all three M2 branches (Fig. 4A). The postoperative course was uneventful without any neurological or vascular complications, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 after coordination of routine follow-up care. Follow-up cerebral angiography at 6 months (Fig. 4B and C) and CT angiography at 1 year both confirmed complete occlusion of the aneurysm with no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

This case illustrates two important clinical points. First, GA is a rare late complication of HFB, underscoring the importance of long-term postoperative surveillance and risk factor management. Second, selected GAs can be successfully treated with endovascular techniques.

We conducted a comprehensive review of GAs following HFB using PubMed and major Japanese databases, including Igaku Chuo Zasshi. This search identified only 5 reported cases, including the present case (Table 1).4–7 Among these 5 patients, 4 were women, and the median age at GA diagnosis was 57 years. Notably, 2 patients had undergone HFB at 40 years of age or younger. The median interval from the HFB procedure to aneurysm detection was 1 year 9 months; however, 2 cases were identified more than 10 years postoperatively, and 3 cases demonstrated radiographic enlargement during follow-up. Although comorbidities were not consistently reported, 2 cases had documented hypertension. Collectively, GAs may develop over a prolonged postoperative course, sometimes more than 1 decade after HFB, during which vascular risk factors such as hypertension can emerge.

The mechanisms underlying GA formation following HFB have not been clearly established. Previous reports have discussed the pathogenesis by analogy to cardiovascular bypass surgery and superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass. Two mechanisms have been suggested: technical injury during the bypass procedure and chronic hemodynamic stress. Minor intraoperative trauma may predispose the anastomosis to later aneurysm formation in the context of STA-MCA bypass.5,8–11 However, because HFB is technically demanding and typically performed by experienced surgeons, the contribution of technical injury alone may be limited. Experimental and pathological studies have suggested that chronic hemodynamic stress at the graft–native artery junction may contribute to aneurysm development. Computational flow studies of STA-MCA bypass have demonstrated that localized elevations in wall shear stress (WSS) can occur at graft–native artery junctions.8,10,12 Given the substantially higher flow provided by HFB, this mechanism may plausibly play an even greater role. Histopathological findings support hemodynamic vulnerability at the anastomosis, with RA grafts showing smooth muscle cell loss and intimal hyperplasia, and saphenous vein grafts exhibiting fibrotic or inflammatory changes under arterial pressure.5,6 Systemic hypertension further augments these stresses and may accelerate aneurysm growth.5,8,11,13 In the cardiovascular surgery literature, an anastomotic angle of approximately 30° has been proposed as optimal because unfavorable anastomotic geometry increases WSS and flow turbulence.12 On retrospective review of the present case, the RA graft appeared more vertically oriented relative to the recipient artery than on the immediate postoperative images. The substantially higher flow provided by HFB may influence the graft-recipient geometry over time. The patient had also developed hypertension during the interval, raising the possibility that inadequate blood pressure control contributed to progressive aneurysmal enlargement. From a hemodynamic perspective, a higher number of such cases might be expected. The limited number of reported cases may partly reflect insufficient long-term surveillance after HFB. In the current era, HFB is performed less frequently than in the past because many complex aneurysms can now be treated with endovascular techniques such as flow diversion. As a result, the opportunity to encounter GA formation following HFB may decrease in the future. Nevertheless, long-term surveillance remains important for patients who have previously undergone HFB. In addition, appropriate management of vascular risk factors during the follow-up period is essential.

In our review, most reported GAs following HFB were treated with direct surgery. One reported case underwent endovascular intervention in the acute ruptured phase followed by definitive surgical reconstruction,4 whereas endovascular treatment for an unruptured GA was performed only in the present case. Although direct surgery is definitive, it may be technically demanding because of adhesions and the need for bypass reconstruction in previously operated fields. In contrast, endovascular treatment preserves the existing bypass and may be considered when graft patency is maintained. From a technical standpoint, the present case required navigation through a markedly tortuous RA graft and management of a wide-neck aneurysm. To address these issues, we focused on two technical strategies.

First, we placed a DAC within the RA graft to secure stable support. Graft vessels used for HFB fundamentally differ from native cervical or intracranial arteries in their anatomical environment. Because bypass grafts lack the rigid osseous and dural support of native vessels, stable catheter positioning can be challenging. In RA grafts, forceful advancement of a guiding catheter may increase the risk of vasospasm or vessel injury.14,15 A DAC provides stable support in such anatomy, allowing controlled navigation and reducing tracking stress during device delivery.16,17 In the present case, the RA graft showed marked curvature toward the anastomotic site (Fig. 2C). Accordingly, the DAC was positioned slightly proximal to the curved segment to avoid excessive mechanical stress on the anastomosis. Second, an intracranial stent was deployed across the HFB anastomosis to secure coil stability. The key clinical question is whether direct stent deployment across the HFB anastomosis is feasible and safe. In our review, one case of ruptured aneurysm was treated with SACE directly across the HFB anastomosis; however, definitive treatment could not be completed with SACE alone.4 Nevertheless, the technical feasibility of endovascular manipulation across the HFB anastomosis has been supported by multiple reports describing procedures traversing the anastomotic site. Notably, the earliest such intervention was performed only 7 days after bypass surgery.15 In our case, more than 10 years had elapsed since the bypass procedure, and we therefore considered the anastomosis and graft to be sufficiently mature to tolerate catheter navigation and stent deployment without excessive wall stress. The Neuroform Atlas stent is characterized by relatively low radial force, which may be advantageous in reducing mechanical stress on the anastomosis.18 In this case, marked tortuosity and caliber mismatch between the M2 inferior branch and the RA graft represented an additional technical challenge, which was effectively addressed by selecting an open-cell stent with high conformability.19 Follow-up CT angiography at 1 year demonstrated no morphological changes at the HFB anastomosis, indicating preserved structural integrity.

Observations

This case demonstrates that GAs after HFB may develop over a prolonged postoperative course. This finding highlights the importance of not only long-term radiological surveillance but also continued management of vascular risk factors, particularly hypertension. Similar cases have rarely been reported, and the mechanisms underlying GA formation remain poorly understood. SACE may be a useful treatment option in selected patients, with the additional advantage of preserving bypass flow.

Lessons

GAs following HFB are extremely rare but can occur even many years after surgery, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up and comprehensive medical management, including blood pressure control. Endovascular treatment can serve as a less invasive alternative to direct surgical reconstruction for selected GAs following HFB. Further case accumulation may contribute to a clearer understanding of the indications and limitations of endovascular treatment for this rare condition.
