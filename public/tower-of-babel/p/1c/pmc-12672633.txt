Analysis of the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of multicenter childhood Burkitt leukemia
Authors: Wenting Pei, Yanlong Duan, Ling Jin, Xiaomei Yang, Yunpeng Dai, Mincui Zheng, Leping Zhang, Ying Liu, Xiaojun Yuan, Kailan Chen, Lirong Sun, Jian Jiang, Xiaowen Zhai, Ansheng Liu, Rong Liu, Jian Wang, Wei Liu, Baoxi Zhang, Yufeng Liu, Xige Wang, Lian Jiang, Lihua Yang, Chunju Zhou, Zifen Gao, Yonghong Zhang, Fu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurkitt lymphoma (BL) is a B-cell malignancy with a rapid doubling time, originating in follicular germinal centers. We aimed to explore the characteristics and prognosis of childhood Burkitt leukemia. A total of 124 children with Burkitt leukemia enrolled during the 6-year period of China Net Childhood Lymphoma- mature B-cell lymphoma 2017 regimen (CNCL-B-NHL-2017) were assessed. The median age at onset was 7 years (1–15 years), with a male-to-female ratio of 4.17:1. Of the total, 50.8% children were aged 5–10 years. Children with Burkitt leukemia were more likely to have tumor lysis syndrome, renal insufficiency, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) > 4 times, multiple organ involvement, and central nervous system infiltration at diagnosis, whereas those with large tumor mass were rare. Eleven children had pure Burkitt leukemia (8.9%), with no significant differences in relapse, progression, white blood cell counts at initial diagnosis, LDH levels, CNS infiltration, and rates of tumor lysis syndrome before treatment compared to those of children with tumor masses. (P > 0.05). The median follow-up time for the entire group was 32.85 months (0.4–70.7), with 3-year overall survival and event-free survival rates of 87.1% and 81.5%, respectively. Thirteen (10%) children progressed or relapsed during treatment, of which nine received chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, with only three fatalities. The analysis identified residual disease at midterm evaluation (P = 0.024) and LDH elevation ≥ 2000 U/L (P = 0.014) as independent prognostic factors affecting survival. Introduction

Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is a B-cell malignancy with a rapid doubling time, originating in follicular germinal centers. Notably, most patients present with bone marrow involvement and progression to the leukemic stage (≥ 25% bone marrow tumor cells), termed Burkitt leukemia [1]. The application of short-course, high-intensity, multi-drug regimens and central nervous system prophylaxis has led to long-term survival rates exceeding 85% for children with Burkitt lymphoma [2]. Small-sample studies have shown long-term survival rates exceeding 80% for children with bone marrow involvement or those in the leukemic stage [3, 4]. Few multicenter, large-sample studies exist on the clinical characteristics and survival rates of childhood Burkitt leukemia. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the case data of 124 children with Burkitt leukemia admitted to member hospitals of the China Net Childhood Lymphoma (CNCL) -mature B-cell lymphoma 2017 regimen (CNCL-B-NHL-2017) between April 2017 and December 2022 to summarize their clinical characteristics, treatment, and prognosis.

Methods

Patients

The data of 124 children with Burkitt leukemia admitted to 19 hospitals in CNCL from April 2017 to December 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) confirmed Burkitt leukemia diagnosis, (2) age ≤ 18 years, and (3) absence of congenital or acquired immunodeficiencies. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prior chemotherapy (except glucocorticoids alone for < 1 week) and (2) other underlying diseases with organ insufficiency not caused by the disease. The Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital discussed and approved the study protocol (IEC-C-008-A08-V.05.1), and guardians and children (aged ≥ 8 years old) signed an informed consent form.

Data collection

Age at onset, sex, duration of disease, first symptoms, location, tumor foci size, lactate dehydrogenase level, comorbidities with tumor lysis syndrome, renal function, bone marrow cytology and pathology, treatment response, chemotherapy, and other clinical data were collected from the CNCL database of the children.

Diagnosis and treatment

All patients were diagnosed based on histopathology, bone marrow biopsy, flow cytology, and Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization (FISH). Pathologists from at least two to three tertiary-level hospitals reviewed patients’ pathological data, and their opinions were unanimous. Clinical staging followed the International St. Jude Staging System for pediatric NHL [5]. CNS stratification criteria were based on the China Net Childhood Lymphoma-mature B-cell lymphoma 2017 regimen (CNCL-B-NHL-2017) criteria [6]. CNS infiltration was categorized as follows: CNS1: Normal cerebrospinal fluid(CSF)at the first lumbar puncture, no abnormalities in the brain parenchyma or spinal cord on imaging, and no neurological symptoms. CNS2: At least one of the following: (1) >5 CSF cells and suspected blasts on the first lumbar puncture; (2) bone marrow involvement and bleeding from the first lumbar puncture; and (3) tumor in the head, face, nasopharynx, paraspinal area, or other areas adjacent to the CNS. CNS3: At least one of the following: (1) elevated CSF cell count at the first lumbar puncture, confirmed as blasts by morphology or flow cytometry; (2) definite intracranial and/or spinal cord mass on imaging; and (3) clinical signs of cranial nerve deficits.

The chemotherapy regimen adopted was group C chemotherapy according to the CNCL-B-NHL-2017, which was a modified LMB89 protocol (high-risk group C). The specific sequential chemotherapy regimen was as follows: COP (vincristine, cyclophosphamide, prednisone) → COPADM1 (vincristine, cyclophosphamide, prednisone, daunorubicin, methotrexate) → COPADM2 → CYVE1 (small-dose cytarabine, high-dose cytarabine, etoposide) + methotrexate → CYVE2 → M1 (vincristine, cyclophosphamide, prednisone, epirubicin, methotrexate) → M2 (cytarabine, etoposide) → M3 (vincristine, prednisone, epirubicin, cyclophosphamide) → M4 (cytarabine, etoposide). According to the CNS status at the disease onset, the patients were divided into C1 (CNS1, CNS2) and C2 (CNS3) groups, and the first high-dose methotrexate was 3 g/m2, and 5 g/m2 in groups C1 and C2, respectively, and thereafter 5 g/m2 and 8 g/m2, respectively if no metabolic gene abnormality existed. Supplementary Table 1 shows the cumulative doses of the chemotherapeutic agents, and the regimen consisted of 11 triple sheath injections plus rituximab (375 mg/m2 per dose) for 4–6 doses. Rituximab was administered just prior to chemotherapy administration (day 0), and the first dose of rituximab started after COP or COPADM1.

Children with progression or relapse in the treatment of non-end-stage tumors can receive chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, as desired.

Efficacy assessment

The assessment time point and content, and efficacy judgment during the course of treatment adhered to CNCL-B-NHL-2017 standards. The efficacy criteria were as follows: (1) complete remission: the tumor disappears completely; (2) partial remission: the tumor shrinks by more than 50%; (3) stable: the tumor shrinks by less than 50% or does not increase in size by more than 25%; (4) progression: the tumor increases in size by more than 25% or the emergence of new lesions. Midterm assessment after four sessions included screening for tumor foci, relevant blood tests (lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], uric acid, ferritin), and morphology and microscopic residual disease of bone marrow and cerebrospinal fluid. Time to tumor progression was defined as the time from the start of treatment to confirmation of lesion re-progression in children who achieved remission, partial remission, and stability.

Follow-up

Review was conducted every 3 months within 2 years of stopping chemotherapy and every 6 months thereafter to monitor routine blood, organ, and immune function and tumor imaging. Follow-up was conducted on March 31, 2023. Event-free survival (EFS) time was defined as the time from diagnosis to progression or recurrence of the primary disease, death related to adverse reactions to chemotherapy, or follow-up endpoint. Overall survival (OS) time was defined as the time from diagnosis to death or the follow-up endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically processed and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences 22.0. Count data are expressed as rates (%), and intergroup comparisons were carried out using the chi-square test when expected frequencies were ≥ 5, or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were < 5. Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was used to calculate EFS and OS. In Kaplan–Meier survival analyses, censoring was applied to patients who remained alive at the end of follow-up, were lost to follow-up, or who died from causes not related to the disease under investigation. Survival curves between groups were compared using the log-rank test, with the significance level adjusted for multiple comparisons via the Bonferroni method (corrected α = 0.025). A Cox regression model was used to identify the independent prognostic factors affecting disease survival. P value of < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Overall

This study included 124 patients with Burkitt leukemia, accounting for approximately 21% (124/591) of Burkitt lymphoma cases in the same period. The median age at diagnosis was 7 years (range: 1–15 years), with 29.8% (37/124) < 5 years, 50.8% (63/124) 5–10 years old, and 19.4% (24/124) ≥ 10 years. The male-to-female ratio was 4.17:1. The initial symptoms of pediatric patients were mostly blood abnormalities, abdominal symptoms, fever, and enlarged lymph nodes. In addition, swelling of the jaw and face, eye symptoms, swollen gums, and pulmonary and mediastinal symptoms could be observed in some patients.

General clinical characteristics and laboratory parameters of children with Burkitt leukemia

Burkitt leukemia is an advanced stage of Burkitt lymphoma. Compared with non-leukemia patients, patients with Burkitt leukemia had significantly higher rates of tumor lysis syndrome (P < 0.001), renal insufficiency (P < 0.001), LDH elevation (> 4 times) (P < 0.001), multiple organ involvement (P < 0.001), and central nervous system infiltration (CNS3) (P < 0.001) at diagnosis. Conversely, patients with Burkitt leukemia rarely present with large tumor mass (P = 0.009). All patients were treated according to the CNCL-B-NHL-2017 protocol. Specifically, patients with leukemia were more likely to die from infection after chemotherapy (P < 0.001) and experience chemotherapy delay (P = 0.008) than non-leukemia patients. (Table 1).

Comparison of features between Burkitt leukemia with tumor mass and pure Burkitt leukemia

We classified Burkitt leukemia into two categories: Burkitt leukemia with tumor mass and pure Burkitt leukemia. Burkitt leukemia with tumor mass was characterized by bone marrow involvement and imaging (CT/MRI/PET-CT) positive for tumor masses. Pure Burkitt leukemia was defined as isolated bone marrow involvement without clinical or imaging evidence of tumor (PET-CT negative). Of the 124 children in this study, 11 (8.9%) had pure Burkitt leukemia and were immunophenotypically positive for CD19, CD20, and Ig light chain restriction (Lambda or Kappa) but negative for TDT and CD34. No significant differences were observed between the two groups of pediatric patients in the incidence of recurrence and progression, white blood cell counts at diagnosis, LDH levels, CNS infiltration, and rates of tumor lysis syndrome at diagnosis. (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Hematopathology and FISH results of children with Burkitt leukemia

Except in one case, which was Ig light chain restriction and surface immunoglobulin (SIg) negative, CD20 and Ig light chain restriction (κ or λ) were uniformly expressed, but TDT was negative on all centrally reviewed cases. Conventional cytogenetic studies were attempted on 118 cases and resulted in abnormal findings in 52 (44%). Normal karyotypes were observed in 66 cases (56%). Cytogenetically, 46/52 (88%) abnormal karyotypes exhibited a MYC rearrangement. The various MYC rearrangements detected included a t (2;8) (p11; q24) in 1/46 cases (2%), a t (8;22) (q24; q11) in 2/46 cases (4%) and t (8;14) (q24; q32) in 43/46 cases (94%). Other cytogenetic abnormalities included loss of 3q, loss of 14q, gain of 7q, t (1;9) (q21; q34.1), t (5;22) (q33.3; q13). All 66 cases that had a normal karyotype were positive for MYC rearrangement by FISH on bone marrow biopsy or pathologic tissue.

Treatment outcomes

Overall OS and EFS in patients with Burkitt leukemia

The median follow-up for the entire group was 32.85 (range, 0.4–70.7) months. The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS rates were 88.7%, 87.1%, and 87.1%, respectively. The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year EFS rates were 82.3%, 81.5%, and 81.5%, respectively. The survival rates of patients with Burkitt leukemia were significantly lower than those of patients in the non-leukemia stage (P = 0.00053, P = 0.000023) (Fig. 1).

The 3-year OS and EFS of patients with pure Burkitt leukemia were 100% and 90.9%, respectively. The 3-year OS and EFS of patients with tumor mass were 85.8% and 80.5%, respectively. No statistically significant differences in 3-year OS and EFS existed between the two groups (P = 0.189, P = 0.396) (Fig. 2).

Recurrence and progression in treatment

Among 124 patients, seven experienced recurrence, including two cases of recurrence after treatment. The median time to relapse was 6 months (range, 4–8 months), and the relapse sites were primarily the central nervous system and bone marrow. Of the seven patients, four died, and three survived after receiving CAR-T therapy (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, one patient developed a second tumor (acute myeloid leukemia) 17 months after diagnosis and survived after receiving allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

In this study, six patients progressed during treatment, with a median time of 3.5 months (1–6 months), all of which were extramedullary progressions. Four patients with disease progression received CAR-T therapy, three of whom survived (Supplementary Table 3).

Treatment-related deaths

A total of 12 treatment-related deaths occurred, all of which were caused by severe infections leading to multiple organ failure. Severe infections mostly occurred in the early stage of the treatment, approximately 1 month after chemotherapy. The treatment-related mortality rate of this group was 9.7%.

Prognosis analysis of Burkitt leukemia

Univariate Cox regression analysis (Table 3) was performed to identify factors associated with prognosis, including concomitant tumor mass, family history of tumor, residual disease at midterm assessment, tumor lysis syndrome at diagnosis, renal insufficiency at diagnosis, LDH ≥ 2000 U/L, large tumor mass, multiple organ involvement, CNS3, bone marrow suppression(3/4 degree), chemotherapy delay, and delay after COP. Our results revealed a significant association with EFS for residual disease at midterm assessment (P = 0.01), and LDH ≥ 2000U/L (P = 0.006) (Fig. 3). The multivariable Cox analysis of the above two factors revealed that residual disease at midterm assessment (P = 0.024) and LDH ≥ 2000 U/L (P = 0.014) were independent prognostic factors for Burkitt leukemia (Table 4).

Discussion

In 2001, the World Health Organization (WHO) simplified the classification of malignant lymphatic system tumors, noting that acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) type L3 and the leukemic stage of Burkitt lymphoma differed from precursor B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (pre-B-ALL) in several aspects, such as clinical presentation, immunophenotyping, and prognosis. Therefore, ALL-L3 was named Burkitt leukemia, which was classified into mature B-cell tumors, combining the characteristics of cytomorphology, immunology, cytogenetics, and molecular biology typing. Notably, in some patients with difficult flow cytometry diagnosis, emphasizing the presence of t (8; 14) (q24; q32) and its variants or the MYC gene rearrangements was important [7]. All 124 patients in our group were diagnosed with Burkitt leukemia according to the above criteria, representing approximately 21% (124/591) of Burkitt lymphoma in our collaborative group during the same period [8]. In this study, patients aged 5–10 years constituted the largest proportion (50.8%), which aligns with the age distribution pattern of childhood Burkitt leukemia reported in a previous single-center study in China (median age 6.5 years; 48.2% in the 5–10-year age group) [4], indicating a consistent age-specific epidemiological profile of the disease among Chinese children. Foreign scholars have reported that about 14% of children with Burkitt lymphoma reached the leukemia stage [9]. However, the incidence rate in our study was higher than the data reported in the literature, which may be related to the late consultation of children in China and the low level of medical care in some areas.

The present study emphasized that patients with Burkitt leukemia were more likely to experience tumor lysis syndrome and renal insufficiency, elevated LDH levels (>4 times), multiple organ involvement, and CNS infiltration at the time of diagnosis, indicating that Burkitt leukemia is more severe than the ordinary ALL, generally manifesting as more acute and severe cases, often in an advanced state of Burkitt lymphoma [4].

Rarely, Burkitt lymphoma may involve only the bone marrow, without clinical or imaging evidence of a tumor mass, known as pure Burkitt leukemia. Our findings revealed that pure Burkitt leukemia cells all have typical mature B cell markers (CD20 and κ or λ -positive), and are TDT negative. This was inconsistent with previous studies reporting that a small number of patients with Burkitt’s leukemia may have TDT-positive but SIg-negative precursor B cells [10, 11]. A study reported that cases of precursor B-cell immunophenotype were rare, accounting for about 5% of all Burkitt leukemia cases [12]. However, the present study comprised a small sample size, and one patient with Burkitt leukemia with an SIg and TDT -negative immunophenotype existed among the study participants. Compared with children with Burkitt leukemia and a tumor mass, no significant differences were observed in white blood cell counts, CNS infiltration, elevated LDH levels, and rates of tumor lysis syndrome at initial diagnosis in children with pure Burkitt leukemia. In addition, the incidence of disease relapse or progression on therapy was comparable between the two groups. Therefore, the biological and clinical characteristics of children with pure Burkitt leukemia were similar to those of children presenting with a tumor mass.

In a previous study, Atra et al. [13] reported the outcomes of the UK Children’s Cancer Study Group (UKCCSG) 9002 protocol—a modified LMB84 regimen—for the treatment of advanced mature B-cell lymphoma in children. Among the cohort, 42 patients had progressed to the leukemic phase, with a mean age of 8.3 years. Notably, the overall EFS and OS rates were as high as 83% and 87%, respectively. Subsequently, investigations by Cairo et al. [14] and Patte et al. [15] from the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) using LMB-based protocols (LMB89 and LMB96) demonstrated further improved efficacy in tumor control. Based on these findings, the 124 patients with Burkitt leukemia in this study were treated using the CNCL-B-NHL-2017 protocol, a modified LMB89-based regimen. The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS rates were 88.7%, 87.1%, and 87.1%, respectively, and the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year EFS rates were 82.3%, 81.5%, and 81.5%, respectively, which were also similar to the survival rates reported in previous studies on Burkitt lymphoma in East Asian countries [16]. However, compared with cases of non-leukemic Burkitt lymphoma, OS and EFS were lower, which may be related to the following factors: (1) children in the leukemic stage have large tumor burden, resulting in a high incidence of tumor lysis syndrome, and renal insufficiency; (2) children in the leukemic phase had impaired bone marrow hematopoiesis, and the intensity of chemotherapy was high, leading to severe bone marrow suppression after chemotherapy, especially in the early stages of chemotherapy, which can be complicated by severe infections that interfere with chemotherapy and even lead to death; (3) significant differences in the level of medical care existed across regions, and patients often seek treatment at a late stage or received improper treatment in the early stages. In further analysis, the OS and EFS of children with pure Burkitt leukemia were comparable to those of cases with a tumor mass, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. High-dose, short-course, multi-drug combination intensive chemotherapy regimens are suitable for cases of pure Burkitt leukemia [3, 17], unlike cases of common ALL, which require maintenance chemotherapy.

In addition, numerous studies both domestically and internationally indicated that children with Burkitt lymphoma/leukemia who experience progression or relapse during treatment had a poor prognosis [18–20]. In this study, approximately 10% (13/124) of pediatric patients with Burkitt leukemia experienced disease progression or relapse during treatment, with only one case involving pure Burkitt leukemia, who survived following CAR-T cell therapy. Nine received CAR-T therapy, and three died, which was consistent with the results of a multicenter clinical trial in adult patients that CAR-T therapy may be an effective salvage therapy [21]. The infectious mortality rate in children with Burkitt leukemia reached 9.7%, which was significantly higher than that in the non-leukemic group. This may be related to the high intensity of chemotherapy in the group C regimen, high tumor burden, organ dysfunction at the time of diagnosis, and severe tumor lysis.

Woessmann et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 157 children with relapsed/refractory Burkitt lymphoma who did not respond to first-line treatment for BFM and identified high LDH levels at diagnosis, high-risk group, and the absence of rituximab as high-risk factors for relapse [20]. Malkan et al. summarized the prognosis of adult Burkitt leukemia, finding that the extent of CNS or extramedullary involvement, LDH levels, and white blood cell counts at diagnosis were associated with prognosis [22]. Minard-Colin et al. performed an open-label, randomized, phase 3 trial, which demonstrated that rituximab combined with standard LMB chemotherapy significantly prolonged EFS and OS in children and adolescents with high-risk, high-graded mature B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma [23]. The CNCL-B-NHL-2017 protocol used in this study, which adds rituximab 4–6 times to chemotherapy, showed definite efficacy. We performed COX univariate and multivariate analyses of factors influencing prognosis, which showed that LDH ≥ 2000 U/L and residual disease at mid-term evaluation were independent prognostic factors affecting survival in children with Burkitt leukemia. In the present study, CNS infiltration did not affect the prognosis of the children, which may be related to the intrathecal injection performed 11 times and the increase of high-dose methotrexate to 8 g/m2; similarly, this result had been confirmed by Stanton Goldman [24].

Although this study provided important clinical evidence on the clinical characteristics and prognosis of pediatric Burkitt leukemia through analyzing multi-center large-sample data, several inherent limitations must be acknowledged: 1. Inherent biases due to the retrospective design: Key factors affecting treatment efficacy and prognosis (such as patients’ performance status, specific genetic mutation profiles, etc.) may not have been fully recorded or measured. Although every effort was made to standardize data collection, potential errors or subjective judgments in original records could not be retrospectively verified.2. Heterogeneity challenges in multi-center data: Although the study protocol established unified criteria for major research aspects, differences in historical practices were difficult to completely eliminate. Patient follow-up was conducted until March 31, 2023, which represents the data cutoff date for this analysis. Consequently, some patients had a relatively short duration of follow-up. 3.The small sample size in the pure Burkitt leukemia subgroup (n = 11) may limit the generalizability of the comparative outcomes between patients with pure Burkitt leukemia and those with tumor masses. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate the conclusions of this study. In the future, we advocate for future prospective, randomized controlled trials to confirm our observations and to generate higher-quality real-world evidence. Additionally, we did not focus on patient quality of life or long-term treatment complications; subsequent research should incorporate these important outcomes.

In conclusion, patients with Burkitt leukemia were more likely to experience tumor lysis syndrome, renal insufficiency, elevated LDH (> 4 times), multiple organ involvement, and CNS infiltration at diagnosis, whereas presentation with large tumor masses was uncommon. The incidence of pure Burkitt leukemia was low, and its biological and clinical features were similar to those of children with tumor masses. The CNCL-B-NHL-2017 protocol demonstrated significant efficacy in treating children with Burkitt leukemia. LDH ≥ 2000 U/L and residual disease at mid-term evaluation were identified as independent prognostic factors affecting survival. CAR-T therapy showed promise in improving the prognosis of patients with progressive or relapsed during treatment and needs to be further studied and explored.
