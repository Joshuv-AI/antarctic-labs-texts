A multimodal LLM-agent framework for personalized clinical decision-making in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Liyang Wang, Fa Tian, Chengquan Li, Jitao Wang, Jiahong Dong, Jiabin Cai, Shizhong Yang, Xiaobin Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treatment is challenging due to tumor heterogeneity and patient variability. Current guidelines often overlook individual factors, limiting treatment precision. We developed an integrated framework combining radiomics, deep learning, and large language model (LLM)-based decision agents to generate personalized HCC treatment recommendations. A modified GhostNet incorporating dilated convolutions, channel and spatial attention mechanism (CBAM), and residual channel attention (RCA) modules was trained on MRI to predict pathological markers such as microvascular invasion (MVI), capsule presence, and tumor differentiation. A fusion model integrating radiomics and deep learning enhanced prediction accuracy. Six AI agents processed structured multimodal data and generated individualized treatment strategies, which were evaluated by hepatobiliary surgeons. The fusion model significantly improved prediction accuracy, with MVI and capsule presence reaching 0.8902 and 0.8765, respectively. DeepSeek-R1 achieved the highest clinical relevance score, followed by GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2. This framework demonstrates the feasibility of AI-assisted, patient-specific HCC decision-making, offering a promising direction for precision oncology.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, radiomics, deep learning, LLM-based decision-making, pathological marker prediction

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks as the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, representing a major global health challenge.1,2,3 Despite significant advances in diagnostic and therapeutic modalities, HCC prognosis remains poor, primarily due to late-stage diagnosis, rapid disease progression, and high recurrence rates after curative treatments. The incidence of HCC continues to rise worldwide, driven by increasing prevalence of risk factors such as chronic viral hepatitis infection, alcohol consumption, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).4 This escalating burden underscores the urgent necessity for more effective, individualized strategies to facilitate early detection, accurate staging, and optimized therapeutic decision-making in clinical practice.

Clinical decision-making in HCC is inherently complex and influenced by multiple dynamic and interrelated factors.5 Staging systems such as the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) and China Liver Cancer (CNLC) classifications provide a framework for treatment selection.6,7 However, real-world clinical practice often deviates from these guidelines due to the heterogeneity in tumor biology, liver function, and patient performance status. Even within the same clinical stage, patients may exhibit substantial differences in treatment response and prognosis due to underlying cirrhosis, comorbidities, and variations in hepatic reserve. Therefore, decision-making in HCC requires a nuanced approach that considers a broad spectrum of factors, including clinical parameters, imaging features, and pathological markers.

The diversity of available treatment options—including surgical resection, liver transplantation, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), ablation, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy—adds another layer of complexity.8 While BCLC stage B patients are generally recommended for TACE, selected patients with preserved liver function and favorable tumor burden may benefit more from surgical resection. Similarly, systemic therapy (e.g., lenvatinib or sorafenib) is the standard of care for BCLC stage C patients, but therapeutic response remains highly variable. The increasing adoption of combination therapies, such as TACE with immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted agents, further complicates treatment selection due to the absence of standardized guidelines and consistent clinical evidence. This complexity is exacerbated in resource-limited settings, where access to advanced imaging, molecular profiling, and comprehensive pathology services is limited, and clinical expertise varies widely.9,10,11 Developing an adaptive decision-making framework that integrates clinical, radiological, and pathological information to generate personalized treatment recommendations remains a critical unmet need in HCC management.

In recent years, the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has introduced new possibilities for enhancing clinical decision-making in oncology.12,13,14 Machine learning (ML) has shown promise in various aspects of HCC management, including tumor detection, segmentation, and outcome prediction.15,16 Most existing ML-based studies in HCC have focused on imaging-based models, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for automated tumor detection and radiomics models for prognostic stratification.17,18 Additionally, ML algorithms have been applied to predict treatment response and survival outcomes based on clinical and pathological data. However, these models are often limited in scope, relying on single-modality inputs (e.g., imaging or clinical data) and lacking the ability to synthesize complex, multimodal information in a dynamic clinical context. The emergence of large language models (LLMs) and intelligent agents offers a promising new approach for clinical decision-making in HCC.19,20,21 LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding and generation, enabling them to process complex medical data and generate human-like recommendations. Unlike traditional ML models, LLMs can integrate and interpret multimodal inputs—including clinical history, laboratory results, imaging findings, and pathological markers—within the framework of established clinical guidelines and evolving research evidence. Despite this potential, there are currently no published studies applying LLMs or agent-based systems to clinical decision-making in HCC. Developing an LLM-based agent that synthesizes multimodal patient data to generate evidence-based, individualized treatment recommendations represents a novel and unexplored direction in HCC management.

In this study, we propose an AI-driven framework to enhance clinical decision-making in HCC by integrating preoperative imaging, clinical parameters, and pathological insights. First, we developed a deep learning-based radiomics model to predict key pathological markers—capsule presence, cancer embolus, tumor differentiation, microvascular invasion (MVI), Hepa, GPC3, and CK19—directly from preoperative MRI scans. These markers are critical for guiding treatment decisions but are typically assessed through invasive biopsy or postoperative examination. Noninvasive prediction of these markers preoperatively enables more accurate risk stratification and treatment planning without the need for biopsy. Next, the predicted pathological markers were combined with clinical data (e.g., liver function, tumor markers) and imaging findings (e.g., tumor size, enhancement pattern) to generate structured patient profiles. These profiles were converted into natural language reports using an LLM-based framework. The generated reports were then processed by a series of specialized AI agents (e.g., GPT-4, DeepSeek, BioGPT, Med-PaLM 2) connected to external knowledge sources, including clinical guidelines (e.g., BCLC, CNLC) and recent literature. The agents generated individualized treatment recommendations based on the integrated patient data and clinical evidence. Finally, the clinical utility and consistency of the AI-generated recommendations were evaluated by experienced hepatobiliary surgeons. This approach enables noninvasive, evidence-based, and individualized treatment decision-making in HCC, addressing a critical gap in current clinical practice. Methods

Patient enrollment

This study retrospectively included patients with primary HCC from two independent cohorts (N = 435) to develop and validate the AI-based clinical decision-making framework. A total of 330 patients who underwent surgical resection at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, between January 2020 and December 2023 were used to develop the pathological prediction model, as these patients had detailed postoperative pathological reports available. An independent cohort of 105 patients with a broader range of treatment modalities (including surgical resection, TACE, ablation, and systemic therapy) was collected from Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital during the same period to evaluate the performance and generalizability of the clinical decision framework across different clinical scenarios.

Inclusion criteria for the Fudan cohort were as follows: (1) histopathological confirmation of primary HCC after surgical resection; (2) availability of high-quality preoperative enhanced MRI performed within 1 month before surgery; and (3) comprehensive clinical and laboratory data, including liver function tests, tumor markers (e.g., AFP, CEA), and detailed postoperative pathological reports (including immunohistochemistry for tumor biology analysis). For the Tsinghua cohort, patients were included if they had (1) a confirmed diagnosis of primary HCC based on histopathology or imaging; (2) complete clinical and imaging data/reports within 1 month before treatment; and (3) received at least one treatment modality (e.g., surgical resection, TACE, ablation, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy). Exclusion criteria for both cohorts included (1) incomplete clinical, imaging, or pathological data/reports; (2) previous liver resection or preoperative anti-tumor therapy; (3) other malignancies or severe organ dysfunction; and (4) imaging evidence of extensive vascular invasion, diffuse bilobar lesions, or extrahepatic metastasis at diagnosis.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committees of both participating centers (approval numbers, ZS: B2024-184R; BTC: 23654-4-01). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were anonymized, and the study followed the STROBE and TRIPOD-AI guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility in model development and evaluation.

Multi-modal data acquisition

Imaging protocol

Preoperative enhanced MRI was performed using 1.5T MRI scanners (Siemens Magnetom, GE Discovery) following a standardized liver imaging protocol. The imaging sequences included axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging acquired during the arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases. For DCE imaging, a gadolinium-based contrast agent (Gadovist, Bayer) was administered at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg through an antecubital vein at a rate of 2–3 mL/s, followed by a 20 mL saline flush. Imaging parameters were standardized across centers: repetition time (TR), 4.0–5.5 ms; echo time (TE), 1.5–2.1 ms; slice thickness, 3–5 mm; field of view (FOV), 320–380 mm; and matrix size, 256 × 256. Imaging data were annotated using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0) by two experienced radiologists (each with over 10 years of experience in abdominal imaging) who independently delineated the tumor region across all dynamic contrast-enhanced phases (arterial, portal venous, and delayed) on MRI. Any discrepancies in tumor boundary or enhancement pattern were resolved through consensus to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Clinical parameters

Clinical baseline data were collected from the electronic medical records and structured into a standardized format suitable for AI processing. The parameters included demographic information (e.g., age, sex), liver function tests (e.g., total bilirubin, albumin), tumor markers (e.g., AFP, CEA, CA19-9), and hematological indices (e.g., hemoglobin, platelet count). Additional information such as viral hepatitis status (HBV, HCV), alcohol consumption history, comorbidities, and tumor staging (BCLC and CNLC) was also included. Since the data were intended for input into the decision-making agent, minor variations across centers were accepted to reflect real-world clinical complexity. The collected data were preprocessed and converted into structured text format, which was integrated with imaging and pathological data to generate comprehensive clinical summaries for downstream decision-making by the AI agents.

Pathological markers

Pathological data were obtained from postoperative histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) reports for surgical cases. The seven selected markers—capsule presence, cancer embolus, tumor differentiation, microvascular invasion (MVI), Hepa, GPC3, and CK19—were identified based on extensive clinical evidence, consensus guidelines, and multidisciplinary expert agreement regarding their importance in HCC management. These markers (1) directly influence treatment decisions, (2) reflect tumor biology with prognostic significance, and (3) are routinely included in pathological reports across tertiary centers, ensuring high availability and data consistency. Capsule presence is associated with better prognosis, while cancer embolus and MVI reflect aggressive tumor behavior and higher recurrence risk, influencing the need for adjuvant therapy. Tumor differentiation reflects biological aggressiveness, affecting treatment strategy. Hepa and GPC3 confirm hepatocellular origin, while CK19 positivity indicates stem-cell-like features and poor prognosis. Among the seven markers, five (capsule presence, cancer embolus, Hepa, GPC3, and CK19) were treated as binary variables based on pathology reports, indicating either positive (1) or negative (0) status. In contrast, microvascular invasion (MVI) and tumor differentiation were modeled as categorical variables. MVI was classified into three ordinal categories: “absent,” “mild,” and “severe.” Tumor differentiation was similarly categorized as “well-differentiated,” “moderately differentiated,” and “poorly differentiated.” These classifications reflect standard pathological reporting and were preserved in the modeling process to maintain clinical interpretability. Details of label distributions on the dataset are presented in supplemental material S1.

Pathological marker prediction model

Radiomics group

Radiomics features were extracted from the segmented tumor regions on preoperative MRI using PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1). A total of 993 features were initially extracted at each period, including first-order statistics, shape-based features, texture features (gray-level co-occurrence matrix, gray-level run-length matrix), and wavelet-transformed features. Feature selection was performed using a two-step process: least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to eliminate irrelevant features, followed by recursive feature elimination (RFE) to select the most predictive features. Four machine-learning models—gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), light gradient boosting machine (LGBM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and random forest (RF)—were trained to predict pathological markers.

Radiomics-deep learning joint prediction

To enhance the predictive performance, radiomics features and deep learning-based features were integrated to develop a joint prediction model (see Algorithm 1). Radiomics features were extracted from the segmented MRI tumor regions using the radiomics pipeline described in “radiomics group”, while deep learning features were extracted from the penultimate layer of the modified GhostNet model. The two sets of features were combined using a feature-level fusion strategy, where radiomics features were processed by a fully connected network and concatenated with the deep learning feature vectors. The fused feature representation was passed through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to generate the final classification output. To ensure robust model training, MRI image patches and radiomics data were paired during training using a multimodal data loader. The joint model was trained using the cross-entropy loss function and optimized with the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001). The modified GhostNet model was compared against several state-of-the-art architectures, including original GhostNet, DenseNet121, ResNet, and FBNet, to evaluate performance improvements.

Clinical decision-making AI agents

We developed a clinical decision-making framework based on six state-of-the-art LLMs: GPT-4, DeepSeek, BioGPT, Med-PaLM 2, LLaMA 2, and ChatDoctor. Each model was fine-tuned and integrated with a comprehensive HCC clinical knowledge base to generate individualized treatment recommendations. The knowledge base included major HCC treatment guidelines such as the BCLC,6 CNLC,7 American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD),22 and European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL)23 guidelines, as well as recent peer-reviewed literature on combination therapy (e.g., TACE + immune checkpoint inhibitors), predictive biomarkers, and large-scale outcome studies. Additional sources included expert consensus reports and imaging guidelines such as LI-RADS for MRI and CT interpretation, and the WHO Classification of Digestive System Tumors for pathological evaluation.24,25 Knowledge extraction is performed using domain-adapted named entity recognition (NER) and relation extraction models. Specifically, we apply BioALBERT fine-tuned on hepatology-specific corpora (∼250 guideline and literature documents) to identify key clinical entities such as diseases, procedures, drugs, imaging findings, biomarkers, and prognostic factors. Extracted entities are normalized to standardized biomedical ontologies: diseases and procedures are linked to UMLS concept unique identifiers (CUIs) and CMeSH terms, drugs are mapped to RxNorm codes, genes and proteins are aligned to HGNC symbols and UniProt IDs, and imaging terms are resolved using RadLex. Residual or ambiguous terms are further disambiguated using OBO Foundry ontologies and manually verified for semantic accuracy. Relation extraction is carried out using BioREBEL, a BERT-CRF based sentence-level extractor. All validated ⟨entity1-relation-entity2⟩ assertions are organized into a dual-layer knowledge architecture. The symbolic layer is implemented in Neo4j (version 5.19), enabling path-based reasoning, subgraph querying, and explainable inference over OWL2-compliant schemas with 32 node types and 87 relation types. In parallel, a dense vector index is constructed using PubMedBERT-embedded evidence sentences, stored in a FAISS-FlatIP backend to support fast similarity retrieval in the retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline. These two layers are tightly coupled via unified entity identifiers, allowing flexible switching between symbolic logic paths and semantic retrieval results. Further details regarding the methods can be found in supplemental methods. The AI models were configured to process multimodal inputs—clinical, radiological, and pathological data—and generate treatment recommendations in real time, following a multi-turn dialogue framework to adjust for evolving clinical context. The structured clinical data (e.g., liver function, tumor markers), predicted pathological markers (e.g., capsule presence, MVI), and radiological findings (e.g., tumor size, enhancement pattern) were standardized and converted into natural-language reports using a template-based generation approach. This ensured that the input format was consistent across different models and that the AI agents could interpret the data in a unified manner, facilitating accurate and context-aware clinical decision-making.

Evaluation

The performance of the pathological prediction model was evaluated using accuracy (ACC), recall, precision, F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Accuracy reflects overall correctness, recall measures sensitivity, and precision assesses the proportion of true positives among positive predictions. The F1-score provides a balanced measure of precision and recall, while AUC evaluates the model’s discriminative ability.

A blinded expert review was conducted involving four senior hepatobiliary surgeons, each with over 15 years of clinical experience. Each expert independently assessed 105 patient cases using a standardized evaluation rubric (Table 1), which covered four dimensions: clinical consistency, personalization, feasibility, and alignment with actual treatment decisions. The experts were blinded to the identity of the six AI agents (GPT-4, DeepSeek, Med-PaLM 2, BioGPT, ChatDoctor, LLaMA 2). For each patient, the six AI-generated recommendations were anonymized and randomly ordered using unique alphanumeric codes. The experts were instructed to score each recommendation based solely on content, without any knowledge of the model’s origin. To avoid bias from presentation order or fatigue, the order of recommendations was randomized independently for each patient, and evaluation sessions were spaced across multiple days. The evaluation process spanned approximately 8–10 h per expert, conducted over several sessions to maintain judgment quality. All 105 cases were scored individually by each expert without consultation or mutual influence.

Results

Experimental setup

The dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%) using a stratified sampling approach to maintain a balanced distribution of key pathological markers. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a grid search approach, with the optimal combination selected based on the highest AUC values. The pathological prediction model and radiomics-deep learning fusion model were trained on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB), an Intel Core i9-13900K CPU, and 128 GB of RAM. The development environment included Python 3.8, PyTorch 1.12.1, CUDA 11.3, and cuDNN 8.2. Additional libraries included NumPy 1.21.2, SciPy 1.7.3, Scikit-learn 1.0.2, and timm 0.6.7 for deep learning model construction and training.

The clinical decision-making AI agents were developed using OpenAI GPT-4 API, DeepSeek API, and open-source implementations of BioGPT, Med-PaLM 2, LLaMA 2, and ChatDoctor. The dialogue framework was built using Langchain 0.0.266 for multi-turn interaction and context preservation. External knowledge sources, including clinical guidelines and literature, were integrated using Elasticsearch 8.2 for real-time retrieval and indexing.

Interpretability analysis via Grad-CAM visualization

We applied gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) in the improved GhostNet architecture to improve model transparency and enhance clinical interpretability. To better illustrate the interpretability of model predictions, we selected one representative MRI slice for each pathological marker to visualize its corresponding Grad-CAM heatmap. As shown in Figure 5, the resulting heatmaps visualize the most discriminative regions within MRI slices that contributed to the model’s predictions across all seven pathological markers. These activation maps reveal that the model consistently focuses on anatomically relevant regions within or surrounding the tumor, rather than background or unrelated tissues. For example, many predictions demonstrated high activation near tumor boundaries and enhancement zones—areas commonly associated with key histopathological features such as vascular invasion, capsule formation, or differentiation patterns. This correspondence between model attention and known clinical markers lends biological plausibility to the predictions.

Clinical decision-making agent performance

Comparative evaluation on real-world HCC cases

We tested six AI-based clinical decision-making agents on 105 HCC patients from the Tsinghua cohort. The input included structured multimodal data—clinical indicators, imaging findings, and predicted pathological markers—converted into a standardized text format for agent processing. Figures 6A–6F shows the responses from the six agents for a representative case. Most agents, including DeepSeek, GPT-4, Med-PaLM 2, and ChatDoctor, recommended TACE as the first-line treatment, consistent with BCLC and CNLC guidelines for B-stage HCC. However, the follow-up strategies differed: DeepSeek and GPT-4 proposed adding targeted therapy and immunotherapy if TACE response was poor, while BioGPT emphasized GPC3 positivity, suggesting targeted drugs (lenvatinib or sorafenib) and PD-1 inhibitors. ChatDoctor provided detailed surgical advice, recommending anatomical liver resection following successful TACE. The structured input enabled the agents to integrate multimodal data and generate personalized recommendations beyond standard guidelines. The variation in treatment sequencing and combination strategies reflects differences in model architecture and knowledge base, highlighting the potential of AI agents to support individualized HCC management.

Evidence-grounded reasoning and interface transparency

To improve the interpretability and trustworthiness of the LLM-Agent’s recommendations, we integrated a retrieval-augmented chain-of-thought (RAG-CoT) prompting strategy. The agent first retrieves relevant clinical knowledge from a curated HCC guideline and literature database, then reasons through the retrieved content in a stepwise manner to justify its treatment suggestion. Prompts include an {{evidence_path}} placeholder, which triggers the agent to extract top-matching evidence snippets—each tagged with its source, retrieval score, and citation metadata. These are referenced within the agent’s response to support specific decisions (e.g., “Given MVI positivity, CNLC 2022 recommends adjuvant TACE post-resection”). As shown in supplemental material S5, the front-end interface presents the retrieved knowledge in a dedicated panel, with clickable links to original guidelines. The reasoning trace is displayed below the clinical summary, offering clinicians a transparent explanation of the recommendation. All citations are version-controlled and locked to prevent hallucinated or overwritten references. This framework enhances transparency, reduces hallucination risk, and supports clinician review by directly linking model outputs to verifiable, source-based reasoning.

Prompt robustness and ablation analysis

We conducted a controlled prompt robustness experiment to evaluate the stability of the LLM-Agent under prompt variation. This was motivated by the well-documented sensitivity of LLMs to prompt phrasing and aimed to test whether the system could maintain consistent outputs across semantically equivalent instructions. We constructed 40 prompt variants based on 4 base templates and 10 paraphrasing strategies, including expert rewrites and back-translation. The experiment included three ablation settings: without chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, without explicit evidence citation, and without RAG. In total, 105 patient cases were tested across all prompt-configuration combinations, yielding 16,800 LLM responses. The experimental setup is summarized in supplemental material S6.

We assessed robustness using four metrics: (1) self-consistency rate (SCR), the proportion of prompt variants yielding the same recommendation; (2) Fleiss’ κ, measuring inter-prompt agreement; (3) ΔAUC and CV (%), capturing performance drift; and (4) Jaccard@3, evaluating the overlap of retrieved evidence across prompts. As shown in supplemental material S7, the LLM-agent achieved an SCR of 91.4%, a Fleiss κ of 0.82, and an AUC variation within ±0.012. The retrieved knowledge remained stable across prompts, with a Jaccard@3 score of 0.71 ± 0.05. These results demonstrate that the model produces consistent recommendations and reasoning pathways, even under diverse prompt formulations.

Surgeon evaluation and statistical analysis

Four experienced hepatobiliary surgeons evaluated the AI-generated recommendations for 105 HCC patients from the Chang Gung cohort, comparing them against retrospective treatment records and clinical guidelines. The evaluation criteria included clinical consistency, personalization, feasibility, and alignment with actual outcomes. The statistical results are shown in Figure 7. Among the six agents, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated the highest overall score, with an average rating of 17.79–18.58 out of 20, consistently excelling in personalization, clinical feasibility, and accuracy. GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2 performed well, with average scores ranging from 13.96 to 16.22, showing strong consistency with clinical guidelines and decision-making relevance. BioGPT and ChatDoctor scored moderately, with average scores around 12.00 to 14.84, reflecting their ability to provide reasonable but less tailored recommendations. LLaMA 2 consistently received the lowest scores, with averages between 7.78 and 12.00, highlighting limitations in clinical relevance and consistency despite moderate alignment with guidelines.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a radiomics-deep learning fusion model and an AI-based clinical decision-making framework for HCC management. We retrospectively collected multimodal data, including clinical indicators, imaging representation, and pathological markers, from HCC patients in the Tsinghua cohort and Fudan cohort. A modified GhostNet model incorporating dilated convolutions, CBAM, and RCA achieved superior predictive accuracy for key pathological markers compared to standalone radiomics and deep learning models. Six LLM-based decision-making agents were evaluated using structured text inputs generated from multimodal data. Among them, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated the highest clinical relevance and accuracy, approaching real-world clinical decision-making standards. These findings highlight the potential of AI-based frameworks to provide personalized and evidence-based treatment recommendations for HCC.

The pathological marker prediction model based on the modified GhostNet demonstrated superior performance due to its enhanced network architecture and improved feature representation. The integration of dilated convolutions allowed the model to capture multi-scale spatial features, while the CBAM and RCA modules enhanced attention to critical radiological and pathological patterns, improving sensitivity and specificity.26,27 The radiomics-deep learning fusion further boosted performance by combining high-dimensional radiomics features with deep learning-extracted abstract features, allowing the model to learn both global and local tumor characteristics. The ability to accurately predict key pathological markers such as capsule presence, MVI, and tumor grade before surgery provides significant clinical value. Preoperative knowledge of these markers informs more precise treatment strategies; for example, predicting MVI positivity may shift the therapeutic approach from surgical resection to TACE combined with targeted therapy to reduce recurrence risk. Similarly, identifying capsule integrity and tumor differentiation preoperatively helps determine the extent of resection and the need for adjuvant therapies. Thus, the improved predictive accuracy translates directly into more informed and tailored clinical decision-making.

The AI-based decision-making agents demonstrated the ability to generate clinically relevant and personalized treatment recommendations by processing structured multimodal data. The high performance of DeepSeek-R1 reflects its advanced natural language processing capabilities and the integration of HCC-specific guidelines and literature into its decision-making framework. Its ability to adapt recommendations based on tumor stage, pathological markers, and patient status contributed to higher scores for personalization and feasibility. GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2 also performed well, indicating that LLMs trained on extensive medical data can align closely with clinical guidelines. The surgeon evaluation highlighted the consistency and practicality of AI-generated recommendations, with DeepSeek-R1 outperforming other models in aligning with actual clinical decisions and treatment outcomes. This suggests that AI agents, particularly those combining radiomics and deep learning insights with clinical guidelines, have the potential to support complex HCC management decisions.

The integrated radiomics-deep learning and AI-based decision-making framework proposed in this study holds significant clinical value in HCC management. Traditional HCC treatment decisions rely heavily on imaging findings and clinical staging systems (e.g., BCLC, CNLC), which often fail to account for individual tumor biology and patient-specific factors.28 The incorporation of deep learning-based pathological marker prediction into the clinical decision-making process represents a paradigm shift toward more personalized medicine. By accurately predicting key pathological markers such as MVI, capsule integrity, and tumor differentiation preoperatively, the proposed framework enables more precise risk stratification and treatment planning. For example, predicting MVI positivity before surgery may shift the treatment strategy from resection to TACE combined with targeted therapy to reduce recurrence risk, while recognizing capsule integrity could support a more conservative surgical approach. The ability of AI agents to process multimodal data—including clinical, radiological, and pathological information—and generate real-time, patient-specific recommendations enhances the clinical relevance and adaptability of the framework. The structured text-based input allows for seamless integration into existing clinical workflows, reducing the cognitive load on physicians and enabling more consistent treatment decisions. The superior performance of DeepSeek-R1 in generating adaptive and personalized recommendations highlights the potential for AI to move beyond static clinical guidelines and toward real-time, dynamic decision-making.

Although DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated the highest performance among the six LLMs tested, this observation should not be interpreted merely as a model leaderboard result. All models were evaluated using identical structured inputs and aligned to the same clinical guidelines; thus, performance differences are likely attributable to intrinsic architectural factors such as context retention, instruction-following fidelity, and domain adaptation quality. In this context, DeepSeek-R1’s superior alignment with expert consensus highlights its enhanced ability to reason through multi-factorial treatment scenarios and navigate subtle variations across overlapping guidelines like BCLC and CNLC. More importantly, the primary contribution of this study is the construction of a domain-specific, expert-scored evaluation framework for AI-assisted treatment recommendation in HCC. This framework enables structured benchmarking of model behavior in a clinically meaningful way and can serve as a foundation for future fine-tuning, model validation, or regulatory assessment. Its modular design allows application to other clinical domains, facilitating generalizable LLM-based clinical decision support system development.

Despite its promising results, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size, particularly for the training and validation of the pathological marker prediction model, was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader patient populations. Larger, multicenter datasets with diverse patient demographics and tumor subtypes are needed to validate the robustness and scalability of the proposed framework. Second, the radiomics features were extracted from enhanced MRI alone, which may not fully capture the tumor microenvironment or the heterogeneity of HCC lesions. Incorporating additional imaging modalities, such as CT or PET, could improve the comprehensiveness of the radiomics analysis. Third, the AI-based decision-making agents were trained on existing guidelines and retrospective data, which may limit their ability to adapt to emerging treatment options or evolving clinical evidence. Real-time integration of newly published guidelines and clinical trial results could further enhance the adaptability and relevance of the agents’ recommendations. Although the surgeon evaluation demonstrated high consistency between AI-generated and expert-level recommendations, the study was limited to a single-center cohort. Future studies involving external validation and prospective clinical trials are necessary to confirm the clinical utility and real-world performance of the framework. Another limitation lies in the restricted set of seven pathological markers used for AI prediction and downstream decision-making. While these markers were selected based on their clinical significance and consistent availability across both participating centers, we acknowledge that expanding the marker panel could further enhance the granularity and precision of AI-assisted recommendations. Biomarkers such as Ki-67, PD-L1, or EpCAM hold potential value in stratifying immune response or guiding novel therapies but were not included due to non-standardized IHC protocols and high rates of missingness in the multicenter dataset. To ensure label accuracy, our current model was trained exclusively on postoperative, pathologically confirmed HCC cases. However, this may limit its generalizability in broader diagnostic settings. In future work, we aim to incorporate non-HCC liver lesions and radiologically confirmed but non-biopsied cases to enhance specificity and real-world applicability.

Conclusion

This study developed an integrated radiomics-deep learning framework combined with LLM-based decision-making agents for personalized HCC management. The modified GhostNet model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for key pathological markers, enhancing preoperative risk stratification and treatment planning. The radiomics-deep learning fusion model consistently outperformed standalone approaches, underscoring the value of combining multimodal data. Among the six LLM-based agents, DeepSeek-R1 achieved the highest clinical relevance and consistency with expert decisions, highlighting the potential for AI to support complex, individualized treatment strategies in HCC. These findings demonstrate that integrating radiomics, deep learning, and AI-based decision-making could improve clinical decision-making, enhance treatment precision, and ultimately improve patient outcomes in HCC management.
