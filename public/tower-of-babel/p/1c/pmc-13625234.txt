PLIN1 restoration modulates lipid reprogramming and impedes tumor growth in ovarian cancer
Authors: Qing Liu, Minzhi Sun, Wenyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer (OC) is an aggressive gynecologic malignancy characterized by remarkable metabolic reprogramming. Lipid metabolic remodeling is closely associated with malignant proliferation, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance, but key regulators remain incompletely understood.MethodsIntegrated lipid metabolomics and transcriptome sequencing analyses were performed using paired OC and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Differential lipids (VIP > 1.5 and false discovery rate [FDR]-adjusted P < 0.05) and differentially expressed genes (|log2FC| > 1 and FDR-adjusted P < 0.05) were screened and integrated with lipid metabolism-related gene sets. PLIN1 expression and function were further evaluated in clinical samples, OC cell lines, and xenograft models.ResultsPerilipin 1 (PLIN1) was identified as a candidate lipid metabolism-related hub gene with previously underexplored roles in OC. PLIN1 was downregulated in clinical OC specimens and OC cell lines. PLIN1 expression was negatively associated with carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), and positively associated with acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 3 (ACSL3), acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1). Functionally, PLIN1 overexpression suppressed cell viability, migration, and invasion; promoted intracellular lipid accumulation and lipid droplet formation; increased several lipid storage/lipogenesis-associated enzymes; and decreased CPT1A expression. In vivo, PLIN1 overexpression inhibited xenograft tumor growth, enhanced lipid deposition, reduced Ki67 expression, and was accompanied by decreased HSL, ATGL, and p-CREB expression.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that PLIN1 may suppress OC progression, at least in part, by reshaping lipid storage and lipolysis-related metabolic signaling. Further studies are required to define the direct regulatory mechanisms and validate its clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16472-7.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) ranks among the most lethal gynecological malignancies worldwide, with insidious onset, rapid progression, and a high rate of late diagnosis [1, 2]. Despite advances in cytoreductive surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy, the overall survival of patients remains unsatisfactory because of frequent recurrence, metastasis, and platinum resistance [3, 4]. Increasing evidence indicates that cancer cells undergo extensive metabolic rewiring to sustain rapid proliferation, survival under stress, and metastatic dissemination [5, 6]. Lipid metabolic reprogramming, characterized by altered de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid uptake, lipid storage, and lipolysis, has emerged as a hallmark of OC and is tightly linked to tumor malignancy [7–9].

Lipid metabolism is regulated by a series of rate-limiting enzymes and lipid droplet-associated proteins, including acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 3 (ACSL3), acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1), fatty acid synthase (FASN), stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) [10, 11]. Dysregulation of these molecules may promote membrane biosynthesis, lipid droplet dynamics, energy production, and stress adaptation, thereby supporting tumor progression in context-dependent ways [11–13]. However, the upstream regulatory network governing lipid metabolic reprogramming in ovarian cancer remains largely unexplored. Therefore, multi-omics strategies, especially the combination of lipid metabolomics and transcriptomics, provide a useful approach to identify key regulatory factors linking metabolic phenotypes to gene expression profiles.

Perilipin 1 (PLIN1) is a member of the perilipin family that coats lipid droplets and modulates lipolysis and lipid storage in adipocytes [14]. Emerging studies suggest that PLIN1 is dysregulated in several malignancies and participates in cancer progression, such as glioma, breast cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma [15–17]. Nevertheless, the expression pattern, clinical significance, biological function, and regulatory mechanism of PLIN1 in ovarian cancer have not been systematically investigated.

In the present study, we performed integrated lipid metabolomics and transcriptomics analysis to screen for lipid metabolism-associated hub genes in ovarian cancer. We identified PLIN1 as a critical candidate and validated its expression in clinical samples and cell lines. Functional experiments in vitro and in vivo indicated that PLIN1 restoration suppresses malignant phenotypes and modulates lipid storage- and lipolysis-associated metabolic reprogramming. Our findings highlight PLIN1 as a candidate biomarker and potential therapeutic target for OC, although further mechanistic and clinical validation remains warranted.

Materials and methods

Clinical tissue specimens

From January to June 2025, six pairs of primary ovarian cancer tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor ovarian tissues were obtained from patients who underwent surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. All enrolled patients had not received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy before surgery, and all tissue specimens were pathologically confirmed as OC and corresponding adjacent non-tumor ovarian tissue by two senior pathologists independently. The clinicopathological characteristics of the six patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 58 years, ranging from 48 to 67 years. The cohort included five cases of serous carcinoma and one case of adenocarcinoma not otherwise specified. According to FIGO staging, one patient was stage IC3, one was stage IIIA1, one was stage IIIB, and three were stage IIIC. Five tumors were high grade, and one was poorly differentiated. Verbal informed consent was obtained from each patient before enrollment. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval Number: 20200065). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national medical research regulations. After resection, the collected tissue specimens were rapidly rinsed with pre-cooled sterile normal saline to remove residual blood and debris. Each sample was split into two sections: one section was preserved in liquid nitrogen for subsequent multi-omics studies, while the other was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemical staining.

Lipid metabolomics and transcriptome sequencing

The lipid metabolomics and transcriptome sequencing were conducted by SeqHealth (Wuhan, China). Total lipids were extracted from tissue samples and analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). mRNA transcriptome sequencing was performed with 6 Gb data volume per sample. Differentially expressed lipid metabolites were screened with VIP > 1.5 and FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 [18]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with |log2FC| > 1 and FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 [19]. Adjusted P values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method.

Multi-omics integration analysis

A total of 1034 lipid metabolism-related genes were obtained from the GSEA database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/) [20]. DEGs were intersected with lipid metabolism-related genes (LMRGs) to obtain lipid metabolism-related differentially expressed genes (LMRDEGs). The Venn diagram was generated using the ggplot2 package, and heatmaps were generated to visualize expression patterns. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between differential metabolites and lipid-related DEGs. KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on the 54 LMRDEGs using the clusterProfiler package, and significantly enriched terms (P < 0.05) were visualized using bar plots. Pathways involving both differential lipids and differential genes were selected for pathway and network visualization. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of 22 DEGs involved in pathways co-participating with differential lipids was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). Core subnetworks of the 22 DEGs were computed using the default parameters of the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape.

Bioinformatic analysis

The Cancer Genome Atlas-ovarian cancer (TCGA-OV) dataset was obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) using the R package TCGAbiolinks. Patients were dichotomized into high- and low-PLIN1-expression groups according to the median PLIN1 expression level. Kaplan-Meier curves for overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS) were generated using the survival package, and between-group differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

Cell lines and culture

Human normal ovarian epithelial cell line (IOSE-80) and OC cell lines A2780 (endometrioid adenocarcinoma), SK-OV-3 (serous adenocarcinoma), OVCAR-3 (high-grade serous adenocarcinoma), and HEY (serous adenocarcinoma) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The available cell-line source and authentication information from the supplier is provided as supplementary material. Independent STR re-authentication was not performed during the present revision, which is acknowledged as a limitation. All experiments were performed with early-passage cells within 20 passages after resuscitation, and cells used for experiments were routinely monitored and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). A2780 and HEY cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S). SK-OV-3 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. OVCAR-3 and IOSE-80 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The cell line panel was selected to include commonly used OC models with different biological backgrounds; A2780 and OVCAR-3 cells were selected for functional validation because they exhibited relatively low endogenous PLIN1 expression.

PLIN1 overexpression vector construction and transfection

The PLIN1 overexpression vector and negative control (NC) vector were designed by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 2.0 × 10^5 cells/well and transfected with 2.5 µg vector using 5 µL Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection for 48 h, the overexpression efficiency of PLIN1 was verified by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting. Stable PLIN1-overexpressing and NC cell populations were selected using puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 7–10 days and maintained in puromycin (1 µg/mL); stable expression was confirmed before subsequent assays. For in vivo experiments, stable PLIN1-overexpressing and NC A2780 cell populations were used after verification of PLIN1 overexpression.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of approximately 2,000 cells per well. At 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 days after plating, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Each experiment was performed with at least three biological replicates unless otherwise indicated.

Wound healing assay

For wound healing assays, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 5.0 × 10^5 cells/well and cultured until they reached full confluence. A straight scratch was made across the cell monolayer using a sterile pipette tip. Cells were gently rinsed with PBS to eliminate debris and then cultured in serum-free medium. Images were captured immediately and at 24 and 48 h after scratching. Cell migration was evaluated by calculating the wound closure rate using ImageJ software.

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasive potential was measured using Transwell chambers (Corning, USA) pre-coated with extracellular matrix gel (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells (1.0 × 10^5 cells per chamber) were suspended in serum-free medium and added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 10% FBS. After incubation for 48 h, non-invading cells on the upper surface were removed. Invading cells were fixed, stained, and counted under a microscope (Olympus, Japan) in five random fields per chamber.

Oil Red O staining

Intracellular lipid accumulation was determined by Oil Red O staining. For cellular samples, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15–20 min, rinsed with distilled water, and stained with Oil Red O solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 10–20 min. Unbound dye was differentiated with 60% isopropanol, followed by distilled water washing. Images were acquired using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). For tumor tissue samples, frozen sections were subjected to the same staining procedure, and images were captured under a microscope. Lipid droplet deposition was semi-quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

BODIPY 493/503 lipid droplet staining

Intracellular lipid droplets were visualized using BODIPY 493/503 fluorescent staining. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and incubated with BODIPY 493/503 staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the kit instructions. DAPI was used to counterstain nuclei, and fluorescent images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, Germany) to observe the number and intensity of lipid droplets.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA from cells was extracted using a commercial RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and RNA purity (A260/A280 = 1.8–2.0) and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Implen, Germany). Total RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA with a reverse transcription kit. qPCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (QuantReady K9600, Hangzhou, China) using SYBR Green qPCR kit (Yugong Biotech, China). The amplification conditions were: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences were designed by Generalbiol (Anhui, China) as follows. Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein samples (30 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies against PLIN1 (1:5,000, 27716-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), ACSL3 (1:5,000, 20710-1-AP), ACC1 (1:5,000, 21923-1-AP), FASN (1:5,000, 10624-2-AP), SCD1 (1:5,000, 28678-1-AP), CPT1A (1:5,000, 15184-1-AP), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL, 1:5,000, 17333-1-AP), adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL, 1:5,000, 55190-1-AP), phospho-cAMP-response element-binding protein (p-CREB, 1:5,000, 28792-1-AP), CREB (1:5,000, 12208-1-AP), and β-actin (1:5,000, 3784, CynomAb, Hefei, China) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, GB23303, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and quantified using ImageJ software (Version 1.54f, National Institutes of Health, USA). Representative bands shown in the figures were cropped from larger source images for presentation clarity. Available original Western blot source images, together with lane annotations and molecular weight marker information where present, are provided as supplementary source data. Some targets were detected using parallel gels because of differences in target molecular weight, antibody compatibility, and sample-loading capacity; therefore, the Western blot results were interpreted as supportive evidence and not as standalone proof of a direct regulatory mechanism.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a gradient of ethanol solutions, and subjected to antigen retrieval to improve antibody binding. The sections were then incubated with PLIN1 (1:400, 27716-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), ACSL3 (1:400, 20710-1-AP), ACC1 (1:200, 21923-1-AP), FASN (1:500, 10624-2-AP), SCD1 (1:300, 28678-1-AP), CPT1A (1:200, 15184-1-AP), HSL (1:200, 17333-1-AP), ATGL (1:500, 55190-1-AP), p-CREB (1:500, 28792-1-AP), and CREB (1:500, 12208-1-AP) overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody incubation at room temperature for 1 h, and visualized using DAB (Solarbio, Beijing, China) chromogenic substrate before hematoxylin counterstaining. The IHC results were quantitatively analyzed based on the positive staining area.

In vivo xenograft tumor model

Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China) and acclimated in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment for 1 week. The mice were randomly divided into two groups: the NC group and the OE-PLIN1 group, with 5 mice in each group. A2780 cells were selected for the xenograft model because they exhibited relatively low endogenous PLIN1 expression, robust PLIN1 overexpression after vector transfection, and reproducible subcutaneous tumor formation in preliminary cell-line evaluation. Stable PLIN1-overexpressing and NC control A2780 cells were separately resuspended in sterile PBS to a final concentration of 5 × 10^6 cells/mL, and 100 µL of each cell suspension was subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of the corresponding group of nude mice. Tumor volume was measured every four days and calculated using the formula (length x width^2)/2. The mice were deeply anesthetized and then sacrificed by cervical dislocation when the tumor volume approached 1500 mm³. Tumors were carefully dissected from each mouse, weighed using an electronic balance, and photographed for gross morphological observation. The harvested tumor tissues were then fixed and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Oil Red O staining, and IHC staining. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. 20190683).

H&E staining

Tissue sections were baked at 66 °C for 20–30 min, deparaffinized in three successive xylene washes, rehydrated with a descending ethanol gradient, and rinsed with running water. Subsequently, sections were stained with hematoxylin (Solarbio, Beijing, China), differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol, blued with saturated lithium carbonate solution, stained with alcoholic eosin, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in phenol-xylene and xylene, mounted with neutral resin, and examined under an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, paired Student’s t-test was used for paired clinical tissue samples, and two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used for independent cell and animal experiments when data distribution was considered approximately normal. For omics analyses, adjusted P values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method, and the thresholds for differential lipids and DEGs were defined as described above. Spearman correlation analysis was used for correlations between differential lipids, between LMRDEGs, and between differential lipids and LMRDEGs using the R package psych; correlation heatmaps were plotted using ggplot2. Pearson correlation analysis was used for IHC-based correlations between PLIN1 and selected lipid metabolism-related proteins. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Multi-omics screening identifies lipid metabolism-related hub gene in OC

Based on the OPLS-DA model, a total of 41 differential lipids were screened, including 37 upregulated lipids and 4 downregulated lipids (Fig. 1A). These comprised 3 N-acyl-lysophosphatidylethanolamines (LNAPEs), 1 diglyceride (DG), 1 ether-linked diglyceride (DG-O), 1 phosphatidylcholine (PC), 15 phosphatidylglycerols (PG), 1 phosphatidylinositol (PI), 2 phosphatidylserines (PS), 8 phosphatidylethanolamines (PE), 2 ether-linked phosphatidylethanolamines (PE-O), 3 sphingomyelins (SM), 1 lysophosphatidylglycerol (LPG), 2 bis(monoacylglycero)phosphates (BMP), and 1 free fatty acid (FFA) (Fig. 1B). Volcano plot analysis identified 1200 DEGs, of which 719 were upregulated and 481 downregulated (Fig. 1C). According to a previous study [20], a total of 1034 LMRGs were obtained from the GSEA database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/). Intersection analysis of the 1200 DEGs and 1034 LMRGs yielded 54 lipid metabolism-related differentially expressed genes (LMRDEGs) (Fig. 1D). The expression profiles of these 54 overlapping genes were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 1E). We performed a correlation analysis between 41 differential lipids and 54 LMRDEGs, and visualized the results in a correlation heatmap (color scale: -0.4 to 0.8). The heatmap revealed that most LMRDEGs showed strong positive correlations with the majority of differential lipids, while a subset of genes exhibited significant negative correlations with specific lipid species. Statistically significant correlations (P < 0.05) were widely distributed, indicating a complex regulatory network between lipid metabolism genes and the altered lipid profile in the study (Fig. 1F).

PLIN1 is identified as a hub gene in OC and correlates with lipid metabolism-related proteins

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the LMRDEGs were significantly enriched in pathways including glycerophospholipid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 2A). A network visualization was constructed to display 12 pathways co-involved by differential lipids and LMRDEGs, with orange diamonds representing pathways, yellow circles representing genes, and blue triangles representing lipids. Among these, we selected the pathway map of Regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes for further presentation, where genes IRS4, PLIN1, and lipid C00162 were found to jointly participate in this pathway (Fig. 2B). PPI network analysis was performed to identifying direct interactions among 22 proteins, including Phospholipase A2 Group V (PLA2G5), Patatin-Like Phospholipase Domain-Containing Protein 7 (PNPLA7), Glycerol-3-Phosphate Acyltransferase 3 (GPAT3), Phospholipase A2 Group IB (PLA2G1B), Acetylcholinesterase (ACHE), Phospholipase A2 Group III (PLA2G3), Glycerol-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1), Phospholipase A2 Group IVD (PLA2G4D), Fatty Acid Desaturase 2 (FADS2), Arachidonate 15-Lipoxygenase B (ALOX15B), Cytochrome P450 Family 4 Subfamily F Member 8 (CYP4F8), Cluster of Differentiation 36 (CD36), Monoacylglycerol O-Acyltransferase 2 (MOGAT2), Ectonucleotide Pyrophosphatase/Phosphodiesterase 6 (ENPP6), Acyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Short/Branched Chain (ACADSB), Enoyl-CoA Hydratase/Epimerase/Dehydrogenase 1 (EHHADH), Acyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Long Chain (ACADL), Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma (PPARG), Insulin Receptor Substrate 4 (IRS4), Leptin (LEP), Low Density Lipoprotein Receptor-Related Protein 2 (LRP2), PLIN1 (Fig. 2C). A core subnetwork was extracted to highlight the PLIN1-containing interaction module and its related lipid metabolism-associated proteins (Fig. 2D). Notably, the functional role of PLIN1 in ovarian cancer remains largely uncharacterized; given its logFC value of -4.50 and p-value of 0.01, we propose PLIN1 as a candidate for subsequent functional investigations. Survival analysis further demonstrated that patients with low PLIN1 expression exhibited significantly poorer prognosis (Fig. 2E).

PLIN1 is downregulated in OC and correlates with lipid metabolism-related proteins

IHC staining revealed that the expression levels of PLIN1, ACC1, ACSL3, FASN, and SCD1 were all significantly higher in paracancerous tissues than in matched cancerous tissues, while CPT1A was higher in cancerous tissues (Fig. 3A and G, P < 0.01). Correlation analysis further demonstrated that PLIN1 expression was strongly positively correlated with ACC1 (R = 0.98), ACSL3 (R = 0.98), FASN (R = 0.96), and SCD1 (R = 0.99), while negatively correlated with CPT1A (R = -0.97), indicating a tight coordination between PLIN1 and key lipid metabolic enzymes in the context of cancer progression (Fig. 3H).

PLIN1 is lowly expressed in OC cell lines

The expression levels of PLIN1 were first examined across a panel of ovarian cell lines. Compared with the IOSE-80 cells, PLIN1 was significantly downregulated in various OC cell lines (A2780, SK-OV-3, OVCAR-3, HEY), as evidenced by both qRT-PCR and western blot analyses (Fig. 4A and B, P < 0.01). To investigate the functional role of PLIN1, we constructed stable PLIN1-overexpressing sublines. Successful overexpression was validated in A2780 and OVCAR-3 cells, the two cell lines with relatively lower endogenous PLIN1 levels, using both qRT-PCR (Fig. 4C, D, P < 0.01) and western blot (Fig. 4E, F, P < 0.01). CCK-8 proliferation assays revealed that forced expression of PLIN1 significantly impaired the growth rate of both A2780 and OVCAR-3 cells compared to their respective negative controls, indicating that PLIN1 acts as a negative regulator of OC cell proliferation in vitro (Fig. 4G, H, P < 0.01).

PLIN1 overexpression modulates the expression of key lipid metabolism enzymes in OC cells

Oil Red O and BODIPY 493/503 staining showed that lipid droplets (LDs) in OE-PLIN1 A2780 and OVCAR-3 cells had accumulated more and changed distribution compared to controls (Fig. 6A and B). Western blotting showed increased levels of lipid storage/lipogenesis enzymes ACSL3, ACC1, FASN, and SCD1 in both cell lines, with CPT1A expression correspondingly decreased (Fig. 6C-F, P < 0.01). Together, these results suggest that PLIN1 overexpression promotes lipid sequestration into droplets and suppresses fatty acid oxidation, rather than simply activating a proliferative lipogenesis program.

PLIN1 overexpression inhibits tumor growth and reverses aberrant lipid metabolism in vivo

To further validate the anti-tumor effects of PLIN1 in vivo, we established a xenograft tumor model using nude mice injected with OE-PLIN1 or control A2780 cells. At the endpoint of the experiment, mice in the OE-PLIN1 group exhibited markedly smaller tumor burdens compared with the NC group, as evidenced by the gross appearance of isolated tumors (Fig. 7A). Consistently, quantitative analysis revealed that PLIN1 overexpression significantly reduced tumor weight and suppressed tumor growth over time, as demonstrated by the slower tumor volume growth curve (Fig. 7B and C, P < 0.01). H&E staining showed less aggressive histomorphological features in tumors from the OE-PLIN1 group than in those from the NC group (Fig. 7D). Oil Red O staining revealed increased lipid droplet content in tumors from the OE-PLIN1 group compared with the NC group (Fig. 7E, P < 0.01). Compared with the NC group, tumors in the OE-PLIN1 group exhibited higher PLIN1 expression, confirming successful overexpression in vivo. Concurrently, the proliferation marker Ki67 was decreased in the OE-PLIN1 group, indicating suppressed cell proliferation. Lipid storage/lipogenesis-associated enzymes including ACC1, ACSL3, FASN, and SCD1 were upregulated, while CPT1A was downregulated in the OE-PLIN1 group (Fig. 7F and G, P < 0.01). We further detected the expression levels of HSL, ATGL, p-CREB, and CREB in tumor tissues. As presented in Fig. 8, the positive staining areas of these proteins were decreased in the OE-PLIN1 group relative to the NC group. Western blotting provided supportive evidence showing reduced protein expression of HSL, ATGL, and p-CREB in the OE-PLIN1 group (P < 0.01). Collectively, these in vivo results indicate that PLIN1 overexpression suppresses ovarian cancer xenograft growth, promotes lipid droplet accumulation, and is accompanied by reduced lipolysis-related protein expression and CREB phosphorylation. However, these data do not establish a direct regulatory relationship between PLIN1 and CREB signaling.

Discussion

Dysregulated lipid metabolism is now recognized as a hallmark of cancer [21]. In the present study, we conducted an integrated analysis of lipidomic and transcriptomic profiles to map the landscape of lipid metabolic alterations in OC. We identified 41 differential lipids and 54 LMRDEGs that distinguished tumor from adjacent non-tumor tissues in the discovery cohort. KEGG enrichment analysis further revealed enrichment of these LMRDEGs in pathways including glycerophospholipid metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, and regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes. The six-patient discovery cohort mainly consisted of high-grade and advanced-stage ovarian cancers, with five serous carcinoma cases and one adenocarcinoma not otherwise specified. Therefore, although the cohort provides useful exploratory multi-omics information, these findings should not be interpreted as stage-specific or subtype-specific conclusions. Lipid metabolic remodeling may influence tumor behavior through multiple processes, including membrane remodeling, lipid storage, fatty acid oxidation-related energy metabolism, and lipid-stress responses. In some contexts, excessive free fatty acids or toxic lipid intermediates may induce lipotoxic stress involving endoplasmic reticulum or mitochondrial injury, whereas tumor cells may adapt by regulating lipid storage and mobilization [22, 23]. Nevertheless, the integration of lipidomic alterations, transcriptomic changes, clinical IHC validation, cell-based assays, and xenograft data provides convergent evidence supporting the involvement of PLIN1-associated lipid remodeling in OC.

Among the key candidates identified, PLIN1 emerged as a hub gene with potential prognostic relevance. PLIN1 encodes a canonical lipid droplet-coating protein widely recognized for controlling lipolysis and lipid storage in adipocytes, yet its function in OC remains poorly defined. Our data demonstrated reduced PLIN1 expression in OC tissues and cell lines relative to normal controls, which aligns with observations in glioma and hepatocellular carcinoma where low PLIN1 levels correlate with aggressive tumor phenotypes [15, 17]. We therefore speculate that PLIN1 downregulation may allow OC cells to maintain lipid mobilization and metabolic flexibility; however, this hypothesis requires further mechanistic validation.

IHC staining of paired cancerous and adjacent non-tumor tissues demonstrated that PLIN1, along with ACC1, ACSL3, FASN, and SCD1, was downregulated in cancerous tissues, while the fatty acid oxidation (FAO) regulator CPT1A was upregulated. Correlation analysis revealed strong positive correlations between PLIN1 and the four lipid storage/lipogenesis-associated enzymes and a strong negative correlation with CPT1A, indicating coordinated changes in lipid metabolic pathways. This coordinated pattern should be interpreted cautiously. Functionally, ACC1 and FASN are involved in de novo fatty acid synthesis [10], SCD1 regulates the conversion of saturated fatty acids into monounsaturated fatty acids [24], and ACSL3 participates in fatty acid activation and lipid droplet biogenesis-related processes [25]. CPT1A is a key enzyme involved in FAO, and CPT1A-mediated FAO has been implicated in tumor metabolic adaptation and ovarian cancer progression in previous studies [26–29]. Although ACC1, ACSL3, FASN, and SCD1 are commonly viewed as pro-lipogenic factors that can support tumor growth, their biological effects are highly context dependent. In the present setting, PLIN1 restoration was accompanied by increased LD accumulation and decreased CPT1A, HSL, ATGL, and p-CREB expression, suggesting that fatty acids may be preferentially diverted into neutral lipid storage rather than being mobilized for FAO-driven energy production and signaling. Thus, the observed increase in these enzymes should not be interpreted simply as tumor-promoting lipogenesis, but rather as part of a lipid-storage phenotype associated with restricted lipid mobilization and reduced malignant behavior. This interpretation is consistent with the canonical function of PLIN1 in stabilizing lipid droplets and limiting basal lipolysis [30].

To validate the functional significance of PLIN1, we performed gain-of-function studies in OC cell lines. PLIN1 overexpression suppressed cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, consistent with the tumor-suppressive role of PLIN1 reported in glioma [15]. Oil Red O and BODIPY 493/503 staining revealed enhanced LD accumulation and altered subcellular distribution in PLIN1-overexpressing cells. Together with the observed decrease in CPT1A, these findings suggest that PLIN1 restoration may limit metabolic adaptability by stabilizing lipid droplets and reducing lipid mobilization. Importantly, these findings do not imply that global lipid synthesis universally suppresses tumor growth; rather, they suggest that the balance between lipid storage, lipolysis, and FAO may determine whether lipid metabolic remodeling supports or restrains malignant phenotypes.

The xenograft experiments further supported the tumor-suppressive effect of PLIN1 restoration. Mice implanted with PLIN1-overexpressing A2780 cells developed smaller tumors with reduced tumor weights and delayed tumor growth compared with controls. Oil Red O staining confirmed increased lipid accumulation in PLIN1-overexpressing tumors, and IHC analysis revealed reduced Ki67 staining intensity. In addition, PLIN1 overexpression was accompanied by reduced HSL, ATGL, and p-CREB expression in tumor tissues. ATGL and HSL mediate key steps in lipolysis [31], while CREB signaling has been implicated in metabolic regulation. Therefore, the reduced expression of p-CREB and downstream lipolytic enzymes is consistent with attenuated lipolysis-related signaling. Beyond oncology, lipid dysregulation also contributes to non-malignant diseases, including pregnancy-related disorders such as preeclampsia, highlighting the broader biological relevance of lipid homeostasis [32]. However, because our current evidence is primarily based on expression changes and because body weight curves and major-organ H&E staining were not collected, we have moderated the mechanistic and systemic-safety conclusions. We do not claim a direct PLIN1-CREB regulatory axis, and systemic toxicity or non-specific effects cannot be fully excluded. Rescue experiments, CREB activity assays, repeated Western blot validation using full-length membranes, and more complete animal safety assessments will be required to determine whether PLIN1 directly regulates CREB-mediated lipolysis in OC.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the discovery cohort included only six paired clinical samples. Although the available clinicopathological characteristics, including age, histological type, FIGO stage, and tumor grade, have now been provided, the sample size remains small and the cohort is predominantly composed of high-grade and advanced-stage cases. Therefore, the lipidomic/transcriptomic findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. In addition, because the multi-omics analyses were performed using cell type-agnostic bulk tissue approaches, differences in tumor purity, stromal content, immune infiltration, histological composition, and clinical heterogeneity may influence the multi-omics signals. Second, the survival analysis was based on TCGA-OV only, and independent validation cohorts with matched PLIN1 expression and clinical outcomes are required. Third, although the cell lines were obtained from a commercial cell resource provider and monitored for mycoplasma contamination, independent STR re-authentication was not performed during the present revision. Fourth, although IF staining with cell-type markers would further localize PLIN1 expression within tumor tissues, such experiments were not performed in the current revision and should be included in future studies. Fifth, body weight curves and H&E staining of major organs were not included in the animal experiment; therefore, systemic toxicity or non-specific effects cannot be fully excluded. Finally, the mechanistic inference regarding CREB-mediated lipolysis is based mainly on changes in HSL, ATGL, and p-CREB expression, and repeated Western blot validation using full-length membranes, rescue experiments, and direct CREB activity assays will be needed to establish causality.

Conclusions

In summary, our study suggests that PLIN1 acts as a lipid droplet-associated regulator of lipid metabolic reprogramming in OC. Restoring PLIN1 expression inhibited malignant phenotypes of OC cells in vitro and in vivo and was accompanied by increased lipid droplet accumulation, reduced CPT1A expression, and decreased lipolysis-related protein expression. These results improve our understanding of lipid metabolism dysregulation in OC and nominate PLIN1 as a potential biomarker or therapeutic target, while emphasizing the need for independent cohort validation and mechanistic studies before clinical translation.
