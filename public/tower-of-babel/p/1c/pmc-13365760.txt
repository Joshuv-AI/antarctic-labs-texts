Gender differences in cancer care experiences in Switzerland: a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Anthony Pernoud, Angela Pugliesi Rinaldi, Daniele Frisone, Hugo Bothorel, Angele Gayet-Ageron, Chantal Arditi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess gender-related differences in patient-reported experiences across the cancer care pathway in Switzerland among patients with non-sex-specific cancers.DesignMulticentre cross-sectional survey study.SettingSecondary and tertiary oncology care across 21 public and private hospitals in Switzerland, covering all linguistic regions.ParticipantsAdult patients (≥18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of cancer who received oncological care in participating centres and completed a standardised patient experience questionnaire. Of 4408 respondents included in the analysis, both men and women with non-sex-specific cancers were analysed. Patients with sex-specific cancers were excluded.InterventionsNonePrimary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the overall rating of cancer care (0–10 scale). The secondary outcomes were specific patient experiences in the pretreatment phase (consultations before the diagnosis, diagnosis and decision-making), during treatment (inpatient and outpatient care, specific treatments and nurse consultations) and in the post-treatment phase (follow-up, home care and psychosocial support).ResultsWomen rated their overall care lower than men (8.9 vs 9.1 on a 0–10 scale, p<0.001). After adjustment, women had lower odds of reporting a high rating (9 or 10) of their care (OR=0.74, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.87, p<0.001), of having received a written care plan (0.69, 0.59 to 0.82, p=0.001) and of receiving adequate information before surgery (0.42, 0.27 to 0.67, p=0.006) compared with men. Addressing these disparities through gender-sensitive care is crucial to ensuring equitable, patient-centred oncological care for all.Keywords: Patient Satisfaction, Sexual and Gender Minorities, Adult oncology, Health Equity, Quality in health care, Surveys and Questionnaires

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 2 In Switzerland, cancer is the second cause of death, with nearly 17 000 deaths in 2023 and 60 000 new cases diagnosed annually.3 While advances in diagnosis and treatments have markedly improved survival rates, the focus of oncology has progressively shifted towards enhancing health-related quality of life (HRQoL), as survivors often face a wide range of psychological, emotional, physical and financial challenges. Understanding these dimensions is essential to delivering comprehensive care, and patient feedback is now recognised as a core metric of healthcare quality.4 5

Patient-reported experiences of care measures (PREMs) play a pivotal role in this context, as they capture patients’ perspectives on care delivery and help identify specific areas for improvement. Positive care experiences have been linked to better health outcomes, greater treatment adherence and higher HRQoL.6 Consequently, PREMs have become the gold standard for evaluating healthcare service quality in both clinical care and research.7 8 Because healthcare systems strive to provide equitable and high-quality care to all patients, PREMs also offer a means to assess whether experiences of care differ across sociodemographic groups. However, recent studies in both the UK9 10 and Switzerland11 have examined sociodemographic characteristics associated with the overall experience of care and have consistently reported that women report lower experience of care.

Despite these findings, sex and gender equity in oncology research remains an evolving priority. International and national recommendations have emphasised the need to integrate the consideration of sex and gender in health research, including in oncology.12,15 Few studies have examined how gender influences PREMs across the entire cancer care continuum, from diagnosis to follow-up, in real-world multicentre settings. The objective of this study was to conduct a gender-based analysis of patient experiences in cancer care in Switzerland. Specifically, we aimed to compare the overall rating of care of men and women and evaluate potential differences in specific experiences of care across the entire care pathway, thereby providing new insights into gender-related disparities in the quality of oncological care.

Methods

Study design, settings and participants

The study used data from the third wave of the Swiss Cancer Patient Experiences (SCAPE) study, a multicentre, cross-sectional survey conducted from September 2023 to March 2024 in 21 Swiss hospitals.11 16 17 These hospitals were located across the French (n=9), German (n=11) and Italian-speaking (n=1) regions and included five university hospitals, eleven regional hospitals and five private institutions. Patients were included if they were (1) adults (≥18 years old), (2) had a confirmed primary cancer diagnosis that was not sex-specific (ie, excluding breast, ovary, prostate, testicle or uterus) and (3) had received at least one episode of cancer-related care (inpatient or outpatient) at the recruiting hospital between 1 January and 30 June 2023. Each participating hospital identified eligible patients from institutional registries and generated a random sample of 900 patients using a computer-based random number generator if the number of eligible patients exceeded 900, based on sample size calculations. Depending on hospital procedures, invitations were sent by post or email and included the study information sheet and the questionnaire with the declaration of consent on the first page.

Variables and data sources

Data were collected using the Swiss SCAPE survey, adapted from the Cancer Patient Experience Survey originally developed by the National Health Service (NHS) in the UK. Following international guidelines,18 19 the questionnaire was translated and cross-culturally adapted into French, German and Italian. This process included two independent forward translations, reconciliation by a translation committee including both patient representatives and native speakers of the target language, back-translation into English and final review by the committee to ensure conceptual equivalence across language versions. The questionnaire has 89 items divided into two main sections. Section 1 (64 items) evaluates patients’ experiences throughout the cancer care continuum, from prediagnostic consultations to follow-up care. Following the NHS methodology,16 17 20 responses were dichotomised to identify ‘fully positive experiences’ (‘Yes, absolutely’) versus all other responses (‘Yes, to some extent’ or ‘No’). Responses that were neutral (‘Don’t know’ or ‘Can’t remember’) or not applicable responses were excluded. Section 2 (25 items) captures sociodemographic and clinical characteristics (eg, age, gender, cancer type, comorbidities, education and financial hardship).

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was the overall rating of cancer care, which was measured by the question ‘Overall, how would you rate your care?’. Respondents used a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (worst) to 10 (best). The satisfaction score was analysed both as a continuous variable and as a dichotomised variable, using a high threshold ‘top-box’ approach (satisfaction ≥9) to identify excellence.21 22 An additional sensitivity analysis was undertaken using a threshold ≥8.

The secondary outcomes were specific patient experiences according to the period of cancer care pathway: experiences in the pretreatment phase (consultations before the diagnosis, diagnosis and decision-making), during treatment (inpatient and outpatient care, specific treatments and nurse consultations) and in the post-treatment phase (follow-up, home care and psychosocial support). Only applicable items were analysed. For example, patients not undergoing surgery were excluded from surgical-related items. Full item mapping is available in online supplemental additional file 1.

Predictors and confounders

The primary explanatory variable in this study was self-reported gender, assessed with the following question and response options: ‘You are: female; male; non-binary; other’. No respondents selected ‘non-binary’ or ‘other’, resulting in a binary gender variable (woman vs man). Other sociodemographic and health-related variables included age (18–54; 55–64; 65–74; 75–84; ≥85), language (French, German, Italian, other), nationality (Swiss vs non-Swiss), marital status (single, divorced, married, widowed), anxiety and depression status (yes vs no), low health literacy (yes vs no), education level (no schooling/compulsory schooling, general education school, apprenticeship, master’s degree, university, other), employment status (in education, seeking employment, part-time employment (<80%), full-time professional activity (80%–100%), on disability insurance or on long-term sick leave, stay-at-home spouse, retired, other), financial hardship (yes vs no). Clinical oncology characteristics included cancer type (colon/rectum/colorectal, leukaemia, lymphoma, melanoma, lung/bronchi/trachea, other), time since diagnosis (<1 year, 1–5 years, >5 years), recurrence (yes vs no), metastasis (yes vs no) and use of complementary medicine (yes vs no).

Bias and sample size

Efforts were made to minimise selection bias through random sampling of eligible patients at each centre and by offering multiple participation formats. Analyses were conducted on an available-case basis, with no minimum completeness threshold applied at the participant level, and participants with missing data on gender were excluded from analyses. Given the fixed sample size of 1818 women and 2590 men (1:1.42 ratio) and the expected 67% overall satisfaction rate among women,23 the minimum detectable difference in the proportion of satisfied patients between men and women, with 80% power and a two-sided significance level of 0.05, was 4.0%. This difference was considered sufficient to detect clinically meaningful differences in patient experience between genders.21 24 When considering the overall rating as a continuous variable, the available sample allowed the detection of very small effect sizes (Cohen’s d=0.09). Given the pooled SD of 1.19, this corresponds to a minimum detectable mean difference of 0.11 points between men and women, using 80% power and a two-sided α=0.05. Proportions were compared using the χ2 test for independent samples, and mean scores were compared using an independent t-test. These calculations were performed using G*Power software (V.3.1.9.7).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data. Categorical data were reported as absolute and relative frequencies. Differences in the proportions between men and women were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests according to test assumptions. To identify independent associations between gender and experience, multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression models were fitted for dichotomised outcomes, and a multivariable mixed-effects linear regression model was fitted for the overall rating score. For all models, fixed effects included all aforementioned predictors, and hospitals were included as random effects to control for within-centre correlation in patient rating. To improve interpretability and comparability across outcomes, the same set of covariates was included in all multivariable models. However, some outcomes were based on specific patient subgroups and therefore did not fully meet the conventional rule of 10 events per variable (EPV).25 These outcomes are identified in the figure legends and should be interpreted with caution due to potential overfitting. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. All p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. A complete case analysis was performed, and missing data were not replaced. Adjusted gender OR and its 95% CI were reported. Analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the ‘lme4’ package for mixed models. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05.

Results

Population

Among the 16 963 patients contacted for the SCAPE study, 7844 (46%) responded to the survey. Of these, 3385 (43%) were excluded because they had a sex-specific cancer type or an unknown cancer. This left 4459 patients for inclusion, of whom 51 (1%) were excluded due to missing gender data. The final analysis was therefore based on 4408 patients, comprising 2590 men (58%) and 1818 women (42%) (figure 1).

Univariable analyses

The mean overall rating of care was 9.1±1.1 in men and 8.9±1.3 in women (p<0.001). Using the top-box dichotomisation (rating of 9 or 10), a lower proportion of women reported a positive overall experience with care compared with men (67% vs 74%, p<0.001). Results were consistent in the sensitivity analysis including ratings of 8, 9 and 10 in the top-box (89% vs 93%, p<0.001). Across the entire care pathway (table 2), univariable comparisons between men and women showed that during the pretreatment phase, women reported worse experiences of care than men regarding the number of family doctor consultations, the dimensions of the diagnosis announcement and of treatment decisions. During the treatment phase, compared with men, women had less trust in doctors and nurses, they were less satisfied about respect of privacy and staff availability, also in the ambulatory care phase. Women were less satisfied than men regarding the information received before surgery and radiotherapy, the support regarding side effects of surgery, immunotherapy or radiotherapy, and they were less satisfied regarding the information regarding the result of surgery. In the post-treatment phase, women were less satisfied than men regarding home care and follow-up support, family doctor’s and psychological support. Overall, women were less satisfied than men regarding the collaborations among caregivers, the care plan provision and global evaluation of care (table 2).

Multivariable analyses

Adjusted for covariates and hospitals, women had significantly lower global rating scores than men (β=−0.12, 95% CI −0.20 to −0.04, p=0.003), corresponding to lower odds of a high rating of care (OR=0.74, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.87, p<0.001; figure 2). Women were also less likely to report receiving a written care plan outlining their needs and treatment goals (0.69, 0.59 to 0.82, p=0.001). In contrast, they had higher odds of reporting that no unnecessary tests or assessments were repeated during their cancer care (1.42, 1.14 to 1.77, p=0.017; Figure 2).

During the treatment phase, women had lower odds of reporting that staff were available when needed (0.57, 0.40 to 0.80, p=0.016), that they were able to discuss their concerns during ambulatory consultations (0.68, 0.53 to 0.87, p=0.017) and that they received all necessary information before surgery (0.42, 0.27 to 0.67, p=0.006). Discussion

In this multicentre study, we found that women consistently reported worse experiences of care, especially regarding support, information and care management during the cancer care pathway compared with men. These differences persisted even after adjusting for socioeconomic factors and hospital setting, indicating that sex and gender disparities in cancer care experiences remain substantial and multifaceted. These findings align with prior research from large national patient experience surveys that have shown women tend to evaluate their care less favourably than men do, even after accounting for disease type, age and comorbidities.9,1126 27

Health is influenced by sex, as a biological variable, and gender, as a social, cultural and structural variable.28 29 However, much of the research for several decades has focused on restrictive male populations without any consideration of sex and/or gender aspects.30 31 The differences between men and women are most likely multifactorial, stemming from endogenous and exogenous factors, yet they remain poorly understood. Therefore, international and national guidelines were developed to encourage the importance of improving the integration of sex and gender in health research, including cancer.12,15 Our findings contribute to this growing body of evidence by highlighting areas along the cancer care pathway where disparities may arise.

Specifically, during the pretreatment phase, women had lower odds of reporting being informed that a family member could be present during the diagnosis, that the announcement was tactful, or that they received written information about their cancer. These findings are consistent with prior research showing that women are more often than men less satisfied with the information they receive,32 which may reflect differences in clinicians’ communication focus between men and women,33 or the greater importance women tend to place on empathy, emotional support and clear information in medical communication.34 35

During treatment, women were less satisfied than men by the staff availability when needed, by the support they received during ambulatory consultations, or by the information about all necessary information before surgery, adequate practical advice or support during immunotherapy. These differences may reflect both patient-level expectations and provider- or system-level factors. Women may value informational and emotional support more highly, whereas men often view emotional support as inappropriate and consider clear information to be a form of emotional reassurance. Therefore, time-constrained environments may fail to meet women’s needs.34 36 Furthermore, despite guidelines for managing a pathology, treatment can still differ between men and women.37 This reinforces the idea that implicit bias may shape clinician behaviour and contribute to healthcare disparities.38

During the post-treatment phase, other studies have shown, like in our study, that female cancer survivors experience greater unmet needs regarding fatigue, anxiety and their ability to resume daily, social or professional roles.39 40 This imbalance highlights the persistent inequity in post-treatment support where the psychosocial aspects are often overlooked despite their importance to women’s recovery and quality of life.

The findings of this study revealed that women reported less favourable experiences at multiple stages of the cancer care pathway. These differences may partly reflect distinct expectations and communication preferences between men and women, but they could also indicate that implicit gender biases influence how care and information are delivered. To address these gaps, integrating patient perspectives into care design could help ensure that informational and psychosocial needs are met.41 Additionally, training clinicians in communication and raising awareness of gender-specific expectations may enhance satisfaction and overall care experiences.42 43

Major strengths of this study include its large sample size and the representativeness of both public and private oncological centres across whole Switzerland. These strengths allow for a comprehensive and multicentric evaluation of patient experiences throughout the entire cancer care continuum. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional study, causal inferences cannot be established. Second, all participating centres were in Switzerland. Therefore, the results may reflect the system-level or cultural characteristics of Swiss oncology care, which could limit their generalisability to other settings. However, it is also a strength of this study, as this ensured a standardised data collection and comparable care pathways. As a result, our findings are robust within this context and may be generalisable to countries with comparable healthcare systems, such as Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, Quebec or France.44 Third, our survey did not allow us to describe transgender patients as we did not collect sex assigned at birth. According to the recent 2025 Swiss Health Survey, conducted among 19’117 participants representative of the Swiss population, 75 (0.55%) were identified as transgender and 52 (0.32%) self-identified as non-binary or other.45 Data on transgender and non-binary patients are particularly important, as these groups report lower self-perceived health (good or very good) than cisgender individuals, so we can expect the same gaps among patients with cancer,46 highlighting unmet care needs. Addressing these needs is essential to reduce health inequities in Switzerland. For future surveys, it would be essential to incorporate questions on both ‘sex assigned at birth’ (male, female) and ‘current gender identity’ to identify transgender and non-binary individuals and analyse potential differences. We also acknowledge that the response options (female, male, non-binary, other) may conflate sex and gender constructs, and that future studies should more clearly distinguish between sex assigned at birth and gender identity. Fourth, while the response rate was moderate, it is comparable to that of other patient experience surveys,47,49 and the large sample size across multiple hospitals strengthens the reliability and representativeness of the results. Finally, given the number of statistical tests performed, results reported prior to FDR correction should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, as they may be subject to an increased risk of type I error.

Conclusion

This multicentre cross-sectional study demonstrated that women reported a lower overall experience of care compared with men, across multiple dimensions of the cancer care continuum. Gender-related differences were observed in areas related to information provision, communication quality, staff availability and psychosocial support for the managing of long-term treatment effects. These findings suggest that current oncology care practices may not fully address gender-related differences in patient experiences, which could reflect both distinct patient needs and expectations as well as variations in care delivery. Targeted strategies are needed to reduce gender-related disparities in patient experience. Potential approaches include integrating women’s perspectives into care design, training clinicians to recognise and address gender-specific expectations, and raising awareness of implicit biases in care delivery. Further research is warranted to explore the mechanisms underlying these differences and to evaluate interventions aimed at ensuring equitable, patient-centred oncology care for all genders.
