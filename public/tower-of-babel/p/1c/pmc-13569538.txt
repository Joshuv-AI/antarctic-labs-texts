Oral anticoagulant switching in patients with atrial fibrillation: a long-term cohort study using population-level administrative data
Authors: Adenike R. Adelakun, Ricky D. Turgeon, Mary A. De Vera, Kim McGrail, Abdollah Safari, Peter S. Loewen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatient characteristics and clinical factors could necessitate switching between oral anticoagulants (OACs) for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation (AF), a phenomenon whose causes and consequences are understudied.AimOur objectives were to describe longitudinal patterns of OAC switching in patients with AF and to identify factors associated with switching.MethodA cohort of adult AF patients were identified in Canadian administrative health data from 1996 to 2019. An OAC switch was defined as a change from one OAC to a different OAC with a gap of ≤ 30 days between prescriptions. Switch events were characterized, and factors associated with switches were analyzed using Cox regression.ResultsThe cohort comprised 30,264 patients. One quarter experienced an OAC switch and 22% of those had ≥ 2 switch events. Vitamin K antagonist (VKA) to direct-acting oral anticoagulant (DOAC) was the most frequent switch type (63%). Switching to a previous OAC occurred commonly (19%-26%). A quarter of patients who switched from warfarin to a DOAC switched back to warfarin. Hospitalizations for AF, acute renal failure, vascular disease, gastrointestinal bleeding, and/or stroke ≤ 90 days before a switch had the highest hazards for VKA to DOAC switch. Hospitalizations for AF, stroke, major bleeding, vascular disease, and/or heart failure (HF) ≤ 90 days before a switch had the highest hazards for DOAC to VKA switch. Hospitalizations for AF, major bleeding, acute renal failure, vascular disease, stroke, and/or HF ≤ 90 days before a switch had the highest hazards for switch between DOACs.ConclusionOAC switching is frequently required in AF patients, with many needing to return to a previously used agent. Switches may reflect an intention to maximize stroke prevention effectiveness and safety, but further research is needed to assess the risks and benefits of OAC switching.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-026-02163-7.

Impact statements

OAC switching is common in AF patients and many patients require a switch back to an OAC they had previously used.It is strongly associated with recent hospitalisation, highlighting a high-risk window for targeted transition care.Pharmacists and ambulatory care providers should proactively screen for OAC switches and provide tailored education and adherence support.Patients who require multiple switches or a return to a previously used OAC may benefit from enhanced medication management and follow-up.Implementing comprehensive care transition programmes incorporating medication reconciliation, and patient-centred discharge planning may reduce OAC-related complications and improve outcomes in this population.

Introduction

With 5 oral anticoagulants (OACs) available for stroke prevention in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF); Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs): mainly warfarin, and direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs): dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban, switching between them is expected given their different attributes, differences in patients’ stroke and bleeding risks and preferences, and different timelines for their approval and marketing [1, 2].

VKAs were the standard therapy for stroke prevention in AF before DOACs became available beginning in 2009. DOACs are now recommended as the first-line therapy, leading to a significant increase in their utilization at the expense of VKA [3–6]. This has involved a significant amount of switching from VKA to DOAC [7–9]. With multiple DOACs available, there has been a significant rise in prescription claims for DOACs [10], while VKA remains an important therapeutic option for many patients [11, 12]. Differences in safety and efficacy profiles, dosing frequencies, and patient factors may contribute to decisions to switch between OACs. Switching can occur between DOACs, as well as between DOACs and VKA.

There are substantial gaps in the literature about the phenomenon of OAC switching in AF stemming from analyses that censor at the first switch [9, 13], short follow-up periods, omission of dose information [8, 9], and limited examination of switching back to previously used OACs [9]. Dose information is relevant because, for example, switches from warfarin to dabigatran 150 mg twice daily vs. to dabigatran 110 mg twice daily have different efficacy and safety attributes [14]. A recent study reported that 7% of OAC-naive AF patients who were initiated on a DOAC required switching within one year, however, longer-term patterns, multiple switches, and switchbacks were not examined [15]. Importantly, evidence about clinical factors associated with OAC switches is lacking, frameworks for studying medication adherence do not address switching [16, 17].

OAC utilization outcomes such as switch types, rates, and patterns, are surrogate measures that may reflect patients’ choices, physicians’ prescribing behaviour, and reimbursement decisions [18].

Aim

Our aims were to describe longitudinal patterns of OAC switching in patients with AF and to identify factors associated with switching using long-term real-world data.

Method

Data sources and study population

The study population was AF patients in British Columbia (BC), Canada. Administrative health data from Population Data BC of AF patients covering 1996 to end of December 2019 was used. Datasets included outpatient billing, details of hospitalizations, participant demographics, date and primary cause of death, and data for all prescription medications dispensed outside of the hospital (PharmaNet). Person-level linkage used a study-specific unique identifier created by the data provider and linkage was performed before the data was released to the investigators. Details of the data sources, which are used extensively for patient outcomes research are available at https://www.popdata.bc.ca/data [19].

Study cohort

The cohort consisted of adults with non-valvular AF using the algorithm validated by Navar et al., with a positive predictive value of 95.7% [20, 21]. We included individuals if they had ≥ 3 recorded outpatient visits or hospitalizations related to AF or atrial flutter, with at least one of the three coded as AF-specific (International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9: 427.31 or ICD-10: I48) [20]. To distinguish paroxysmal, persistent, and permanent AF from single or speculative AF episodes, at least two of the visits had to occur within 1 year [20]. To improve specificity, individuals with indications for OAC other than AF (e.g., valvular disease, venous thromboembolism) were excluded. Individuals were also excluded if they did not have continuous Medical Services Plan (MSP) enrolment during the 1-year prior to the “index date”, which was defined as the date on which the first OAC prescription was filled after the first AF diagnostic code, or within 60 days before the first AF code. This cohort was called the “full cohort”. A DOAC-era cohort, whose first OAC prescription date was on or after October 1st 2009 when dabigatran became available in Canada, was also created. OAC users identified prior to 2009 were monitored for switches occurring after 2009 and compared to OAC users identified after the availability of DOAC therapy. This approach allowed us to assess the impact of DOAC availability on medication switching patterns while considering historical data. As such, the full cohort captured switches for prevalent OAC users, and the DOAC era cohort did so for incident OAC users. Supplemental Figure S1 depicts the flow of cohort creation. ICD codes/definitions are in Supplemental Table S1.

Public drug plan coverage in the study jurisdiction stipulated that patients could receive coverage for dabigatran, rivaroxaban, or apixaban only after a trial of warfarin without adequate anticoagulation [22]. Edoxaban was not covered in the study jurisdiction during the study period.

Study design

This was a population-level cohort study. We evaluated patients' baseline characteristics during a one-year period before the index date and followed them until end of 2019, date of death, or discontinuation of public medical insurance enrolment, whichever occurred first. (Supplemental Figure S2). The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist was used in reporting this study [23].

Outcomes and covariates

The primary outcome was OAC switch, defined as receiving a prescription for a different OAC within a 30-day gap between the end of the previous OAC prescription and the beginning of the next one. A permissible gap of ≤ 30 days was selected to distinguish switches from discontinuations. We obtained days’ supply for DOAC prescriptions directly from a central database of all prescriptions filled in British Columbia (PharmaNet). For warfarin (the only VKA in the study), for which the dose is often adjusted based on International Normalized Ratio (INR) test results, we used the Random Effects Warfarin Days’ Supply (REWarDS) method to estimate days’ supply based on average daily dose. REWarDS has been validated using the study prescription database and is the most accurate method to estimate days’ supply with warfarin [24]. We defined the “switch date” as the date of the new prescription meeting the switch definition.

Definitions for demographic variables are in Table S2 [25–27]. To analyze switches which may have been influenced by a hospitalization, ICD-10 codes for hospitalizations due to clinical events where an OAC switch might be expected (thromboembolic or bleeding events, heart failure (HF), renal failure, myocardial infarction, and anemia related to bleeding) were ascertained during 90 days before each switch as done by Hellfritzsch et al. Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of patients were analyzed descriptively, and inferential tests (t-tests and chi-square tests) were used to identify significant differences between males and females.

Switch types were defined by the OAC ‘switched from’ and ‘switched to’ at the individual drug and drug class (VKA, DOAC) levels. The switch types were analyzed descriptively and reported as the proportion of switch events in the full cohort and the DOAC era cohort. Patterns of OAC switches (more than one OAC switch event or type in a patient) were descriptively reported for both cohorts. OAC switch trends were descriptively reported for both cohorts.

The time to switch was calculated for the DOAC era cohort and it was defined as the duration between the start of the first OAC and the new OAC. The number of “switchbacks” (prescriptions for an OAC the patient was previously receiving i.e. a return to any OAC that the patient had previously received during the study period, regardless of the number of intermediate OACs used) was also determined for each patient, and hospitalizations preceding a switch for events that may prompt an OAC switch were evaluated.

Cox proportional hazard regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with time to first switch in the DOAC era cohort, with candidate covariates including index date, age, sex, AF-related hospitalizations, and OAC-relevant hospitalizations within 90 days before the switch. All patients (switchers and non-switchers) were followed up for up to 2 years after the index date. Separate models were created for VKA to DOAC, DOAC to VKA, and DOAC to DOAC switches to reflect the distinct clinical contexts that may drive each type of switch. Specifically, for the VKA to DOAC model, the risk set comprised all patients who initiated VKA at the index date and were followed for up to 2 years, including both those who switched to a DOAC and those who did not switch. Similarly, the DOAC to VKA and DOAC to DOAC models included all patients initiating a DOAC at the index date, with events defined respectively as switching to VKA or switching to a different DOAC.

R Statistical Software (v4.0.5; R Core Team 2021) was used for all analyses, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Ethics approval

Certificate of approval was obtained from the UBC Behavioral Research Ethics Board in 2021 (H17-02420 and H21-03045).

Switch events

In the full cohort, there were 9771 switches, with 7582 patients (25%) having at least one switch event. In the DOAC era cohort, 6204 switches occurred in 4920 patients (28%). Multiple (> 1) switches occurred in 22% and 20% of patients in the full and DOAC era cohorts, respectively. Supplemental Table S3 shows the number of switches per patient.

Switch types

In the full cohort and DOAC era cohort, there were 20 and 18 switch types observed, respectively, with 54% of switches in the DOAC era cohort being VKA to DOAC, 32% between DOACs, and 14% from DOAC to VKA. In the full cohort, 63% of switches were VKA to DOAC, 25% were between DOACs, and 12% were switches from DOAC to VKA (Table 2). Supplemental Table S4 shows switch types by drug identity with dose. Switch patterns

More than one switch was observed in 1677 patients in the full cohort and 1002 in the DOAC era cohort. There were 159 unique switch patterns observed in the full cohort, while in the DOAC era cohort, there were 139 unique switch patterns, although most had low frequencies (Table 3). The most common patterns involved switching from warfarin to a DOAC then to another DOAC, which occurred in 26% of patients in DOAC era cohort who had > 1 switch. Discussion

This retrospective cohort study used population-based administrative health data to evaluate OAC switching in a large cohort of AF patients. A quarter of patients experienced a switch, mostly from VKA to DOAC. Switches between DOACs and switching to a previous OAC were also common. OAC switching was associated with hospitalizations for AF, hospitalizations for stroke risk factors, and hospitalizations for bleeding risk factors, ≤ 90 days before the switch date.

In the last 3 years of our study period, the only switch type whose frequency was increasing was warfarin to apixaban. Fosbol et al. also showed a higher proportion of patients switching from warfarin to apixaban over time compared to dabigatran [29]. Others found similar secular trends in VKA to DOAC switching as we did, with dabigatran representing a shrinking share of switches from VKA [29–31]. On the other hand, a switch from apixaban to another OAC may be needed due to adherence difficulties with twice-daily dosing, high out-of-pocket costs, or declining renal function, and approximately 40% of switches from apixaban in our study were to warfarin.

Switching to low doses of DOACs was common. Low doses are indicated in cases of renal insufficiency, higher risk of bleeding, and dual therapy with an antiplatelet following percutaneous coronary intervention [32, 33]. Describing OAC switching at the dose level has not been explored in previous studies. We did not evaluate the appropriateness of non-standard DOAC dosing, but a systematic review found inappropriately low and high doses for patient’s renal function, age, and weight have significant negative consequences in terms of loss of efficacy or major bleeding [34]. Data from the HERA-FIB registry further confirmed that under-dosed AF patients had an 84% higher risk of a composite endpoint of all-cause mortality, stroke, major bleeding, and MI compared with appropriately dosed patients [35].

The most frequent switch patterns we observed reflected the uptake of the new OACs chronologically as they came to market. Interestingly, one-quarter of patients required a switch back to warfarin, possibly due to cost, limited coverage for DOACs, idiosyncratic adverse effects, or renal dysfunction [22]. This aligns with previous studies, where switchbacks to warfarin occurred in 5% to 14% of cases [30, 36, 37].

One-third of patients who experienced a switch in our study had at least one hospitalization within 90 days before the switch, a factor significantly associated with switching. Hellfritzsch et al. reported about 45% of patients who experienced a switch had at least one hospitalization within 60 days before the switch [28]. Nguyen et al. reported that adherence to OACs decreased after hospitalization, regardless of the reason for hospitalization and that it decreased less in patients who switched from warfarin to DOAC compared with other switches [38]. Collectively these findings should prompt care providers to expect hospitalizations to potentially result in OAC switches, and to ensure patients receive proper educational support for this to prevent lapses in adherence.

Our study is among the few to identify factors associated with DOAC to VKA switches and DOAC to DOAC switches. Similar factors drove OAC class switches, including recent hospitalization for AF, HF, stroke or TIA, and major bleeding. This raises concerns about the appropriateness of these switches. While some switches may be justified due to therapeutic failure (stroke or TIA) or adverse reactions (major bleeding), others may be influenced by non-clinical factors such as local practice patterns, formulary policies, or provider preferences, factors not evaluable in population-level studies such as ours. Furthermore, emerging evidence shows switching between OAC classes can have undesirable effects: A recent trial in which frail older AF patients were randomized to switch from INR-guided VKA treatment to a DOAC vs. continuing VKA showed that switching to DOAC was associated with more bleeding complications than continuing VKA [39]. Given the extent of OAC switching and switchbacks, there is a need to thoroughly evaluate OAC switches while taking into account factors beyond clinical indications.

Hospitalization for AF, acute renal failure, gastrointestinal bleeding, or stroke/TIA ≤ 90 days before switching had the highest hazards of VKA to DOAC switches. These findings align with those of others [40–43]. For DOAC to VKA switches, having a hospitalization for AF, stroke or TIA, major bleeding, vascular disease, or HF ≤ 90 days before switch had the highest hazards. Rollins et al. also found four times increased odds of switching from DOAC to warfarin for patients with a history of HF [44]. The strong association between hospitalizations and switches should prompt inpatient clinicians to reflect on whether switching OACs is clinically justified (vs. a personal prescribing preference), to revert to the preadmission OAC at discharge for continuity of care, or if not, to prioritize notifying outpatient providers, and the patient, about the change and its rationale. Ambulatory care providers, including pharmacists, should screen for OAC switches following hospitalization and provide appropriate patient education and adherence support. Comprehensive care transition programs leveraging pre-discharge (e.g. medication reconciliation, patient education), post discharge (e.g. follow up calls), and bridging interventions (e.g. patient-centered discharge instructions, continuity between inpatient and outpatient settings) are effective for reducing readmission rates in various medical conditions including acute myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and diabetes [45, 46]. Using these practices may be useful for patients with AF who are undergoing an OAC switch during a hospitalization.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted considering the study’s limitations. The study databases lacked several variables that could affect switching, including psychosocial factors, laboratory data, patient preferences, prescriber characteristics, provider preferences, and actual reasons for switching. We also lacked data on contextual factors such as income changes, insurance coverage, adverse effects, drug interactions, and hospital formulary changes. These limitations impede our ability to assess how broader systemic factors affect OAC switching patterns over time. Switches involving edoxaban were not described because of insufficient exposed patients. A potential limitation of the cox model is the risk of immortal time bias, as covariates such as hospitalizations occurring within 90 days before switching were measured post-baseline without accounting for the time-dependent nature. The Cox models treated death and discontinuation as censoring under the assumption of non-informative censoring. Lastly, a maximum follow-up of 2 years for the cox model may limit the observation of late switches or rare exposures. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the strength of associations reported.

Conclusion

OAC switching is common in AF patients, with VKA to DOAC being the most prevalent switch type. Patients often require more than one switch, frequently needing to return to a previously used OAC. Factors associated with OAC switches were mainly hospitalizations for AF, hospitalizations for stroke risk factors, and hospitalizations for bleeding risk factors, possibly reflecting an intention to maximize stroke prevention and safety. The extent and patterns of OAC switching we found could prompt studies on the risks and benefits of OAC switches, as well as more specific educational support for patients who require frequent switching or need to return to a previously used OAC, to enhance adherence and minimize adverse events.
