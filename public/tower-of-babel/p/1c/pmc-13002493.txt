Adjuvant Cilostazol Therapy to Help Restore Atrioventricular Conduction in Lyme Carditis

Published: 2026

Lyme carditis is a manifestation of Lyme disease, which is caused by the tick-borne bacterium Borrelia burgdorferi. Lyme carditis occurs in 1% to 10% of Lyme disease cases and most frequently manifests as atrioventricular (AV) block of varying degrees. This condition commonly manifests during the early disseminated phase with second- or third-degree AV block several days to a few weeks after the onset of Lyme disease. This involves the spread of the infection to multiple organs, including the heart. Lyme carditis manifests most often as a variable AV block. Other manifestations may include Lyme arthritis and neuroborreliosis. In Lyme carditis, conduction abnormalities with higher-degree AV block can fluctuate and are reversible with timely antibiotic treatment. The time to resolution after initiation of antibiotic therapy is typically 3 to 7 days; however, some patients may require up to 2 to 3 weeks to restore AV conduction.1

Early recognition is essential because appropriate therapy can re-establish AV conduction and obviate the need for permanent pacing. A systematic review found that of 45 reported cases, only 1 in 25 of affected patients required permanent pacemaker implantation, highlighting the reversible nature of this condition.2 Although temporary pacing is recommended in patients with hemodynamic instability, there is no established pharmacologic approach to enhance AV conduction. Cilostazol is an oral phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE-3) inhibitor with an excellent safety profile, which increases the intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) level in cardiac myocytes, including the specialized cells of the sinus node and AV node. This results in positive chronotropic, dromotropic, and mildly inotropic effects. Here, we report a case of Lyme carditis with second-degree AV block that promptly recovered after starting cilostazol in addition to standard antibiotic therapy. Although this effect could have been incidental, a case description and mechanistic discussion are warranted.

Case Description

On examination, the patient was alert but appeared fatigued, with mild diaphoresis, without overt signs of heart failure. His physical examination revealed a pulse rate between 38 and 44 beats/min, with a blood pressure of 135/82 mm Hg and a respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min. Cardiac auscultation revealed a slow, regular rhythm without murmurs. His initial electrocardiogram showed a second-degree Mobitz type II AV block with intermittent nonconducted P waves and a prolonged PR interval on the conducted beats (Figure 1). An initial C-reactive protein level was elevated at 47 mg/L.

Further laboratory findings obtained in the emergency department included a cardiac troponin level that was unremarkable. The cardiac electrophysiology service was consulted. Because of his age and lack of prior cardiac disease, the patient was recommended to undergo cardiac magnetic resonance to assess for infiltrative, inflammatory, and structural heart disease. External pacing electrodes were placed for optional backup pacing, and the possibility of requiring pacemaker therapy was discussed. Despite the absence of tick bites and rashes, the patient's symptoms, and a new-onset AV block, serology testing for Lyme disease was recommended. The diagnosis of Lyme disease was confirmed based on the presence of Borrelia antibodies (IgG and IgM antibodies), as shown in Table 1. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed normal left and right ventricular size and function (left ventricular ejection fraction: 69%, right ventricular ejection fraction: 62%) without valvular abnormalities or focal late gadolinium enhancement.

The patient was started on 2 g of intravenous ceftriaxone daily. Because the patient was hemodynamically stable, the electrophysiology service deferred pacemaker therapy. Because of persistent symptomatic bradycardia with heart rates between 30 and 40 beats/min, cilostazol 100 mg twice daily was initiated on hospital day 2. Within 4 hours, the pulse rate normalized to between 70 and 90 beats/min, and by day 4, his electrocardiogram demonstrated a sinus rhythm with stable AV conduction and first-degree AV block (Figure 2). At that time, C-reactive protein levels had decreased to 27 mg/L.

Electrocardiogram on Hospital Day 4 Showing Normal Sinus Rhythm With First-Degree Atrioventricular Block and a Dropped Beat

He was prescribed 7 days of cilostazol and transitioned to oral antibiotics per infectious disease recommendations and was discharged the next day with a 14-day mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry monitor. At the 4-week follow-up visit, he showed normal sinus rhythm without residual conduction abnormalities.

In the next discussion, we will explore why B. burgdorferi affects the AV conduction system, the antibiotic regimen, and the rationale behind using cilostazol as a potential adjuvant.

B. burgdorferi and the conduction system

Infections with B. burgdorferi can cause inflammation of the conduction system that disrupts AV conduction. The activation of macrophages is a key component of the myocardial inflammatory response. It is unknown why this response has an affinity for the conduction system. Still, it is well established that systemic infection induces rapid B-cell proliferation, which disrupts lymph node architecture and impairs normal germinal center formation. This limits the emergence of long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells that typically help clear the bacteria and explain the persistence of the infection. The ensuing immune response includes production of both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, of which IL-10 is of relevance because it furthers bacterial persistence by down-regulating phagocytic and pro-inflammatory pathways. Molecular mimicry and autoimmune mechanisms have also been proposed, whereby immune cross-reactivity between Borrelia antigens and host cardiac tissue could contribute to the inflammation and conduction abnormalities seen in Lyme carditis. These changes reverse when antibiotic therapy is started, but in some extended cases of Lyme carditis, conduction system disease can become irreversible. Our patient's presentation, positive serology results, and cardiac magnetic resonance findings fulfilled some of the criteria for Lyme carditis. The diagnosis of Lyme carditis is usually established by the combination of new conduction abnormalities, most often a fluctuating AV block, epidemiologic risk, compatible clinical features, and laboratory evidence of Lyme disease.3 The Suspicious Index in Lyme Carditis score is a clinical risk stratification tool designed to estimate the likelihood that high-degree AV block is due to Lyme carditis, which would have categorized our patient as having an intermediate risk (Table 2).

Antibiotic therapy for Lyme disease

The guidelines recommend intravenous ceftriaxone for hospitalized patients with high-degree AV block caused by Lyme disease, with pacing reserved for patients with hemodynamic instability. The response time to treatment in Lyme carditis is typically relatively fast, with most patients recovering within 3 to 7 days after initiation of antibiotic therapy, according to the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), American Academy of Neurology (AAN), and American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines.4 The American College of Cardiology (ACC), American Heart Association (AHA), and Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) further note that AV conduction is typically restored after 1 to 2 weeks of antibiotic treatment. In some cases, resolution may take up to a month. The literature also emphasizes that adjunctive therapies, such as temporary pacing, may be required for symptomatic high-grade heart block, but permanent pacemaker implantation is rarely necessary. The duration of antibiotic therapy is generally between 14 and 21 days, as recommended by the ACR, with the choice of an agent (doxycycline, amoxicillin, cefuroxime axetil, or intravenous ceftriaxone) tailored to disease severity and patient factors.4 As evident in the literature, hospital stays can be prolonged due to the delayed recovery of AV conduction. It is therefore that cilostazol could be considered as an adjuvant therapy that has the potential to improve the care of affected patients.

Rationale for using cilostazol

Cilostazol is an oral prodrug with 2 PDE-3 inhibiting metabolites. Cilostazol-mediated inhibition of PDE-3 primarily increases cAMP levels, predominantly located in platelets, cardiac myocytes, vascular and visceral smooth muscle, liver, kidney, and the corpus cavernosum, by inhibiting the adenosine reuptake to increase interstitial and circulating adenosine levels.5 Conceptually, increased adenosine levels could lead to bradycardia and slowed AV conduction; this, however, has not been reported in patients. The active molecules inhibit cellular degradation of cAMP, an activator of protein kinase A, which phosphorylates multiple target proteins that explain the pleiotropic adrenergic effects. Another effect is minor inhibition of cyclic guanosine monophosphate degradation, which may contribute to both safety and efficacy. Pharmacokinetic studies and the accrued clinical evidence established that peak serum levels are reached within 4 hours without the need for dose adjustments in patients with chronic kidney disease, and the elimination half-life is 11 to 13 hours.6 Compared with placebo, headaches and gastrointestinal symptoms are more common, which can be reliably overcome with intermittent dose reductions.7 Cilostazol is approved in the United States since 1999 to improve the walking distance of patients with claudication at doses of 50 mg or 100 mg twice a day. At the time of approval, it was unknown if cilostazol had untoward effects in patients with heart failure, which led the FDA to issue a precautionary warning based on its relationship to milrinone and vesnarinone. However, the wealth of evidence gleaned from randomized clinical trials, an analysis of the cilostazol safety database, and a Cochrane review did not uncover adverse cardiovascular effects.6 Shortly after its approval in the United States, it was discovered that cilostazol reduced the risk for recurrent strokes by about 40%, which was replicated in subsequent randomized trials.6 This led to the approval of cilostazol for secondary stroke prevention in some Asian countries. Recovery of AV conduction of third-degree AV block with occasional AV synchrony has been reported, and cilostazol significantly increased the ventricular escape rate and decreased the level of circulating natriuretic peptides in patients with slow conduction and atrial fibrillation (Table 3).8,9 Cilostazol has also been used in drug-induced or functional bradyarrhythmias.10 The increase in pulse rates is mediated by sinus node activation or, in patients with atrial fibrillation, by accelerated conduction through the AV node.10 Although it is categorized as a vasodilator, randomized clinical trials and the cilostazol safety database did not reveal systemic blood pressure effects. However, concerns have been raised that the elevation of intracellular cAMP levels could exert direct toxic effects on myocardial cells and enhance the electrophysiological mechanisms underlying arrhythmogenesis. Given the association between the use of PDE-3 inhibitors and increased mortality in patients with NYHA functional class III or IV heart failure, cilostazol is not recommended for use in patients with any degree of heart failure.7

As discussed, although reports of cilostazol in drug-induced and functional bradyarrhythmia exist, its use in Lyme carditis is novel. Given its mechanism of action and rapid effect, cilostazol may have very well contributed to our patient's expedited recovery and short hospital stay.

Conclusions

Lyme carditis is a reversible cause of AV block that should be promptly recognized in young patients without structural heart disease. Early recognition and antibiotic treatment are crucial in preventing permanent damage to the conduction system. Cilostazol may be considered as a safe pharmacologic bridge to help recover electrical conduction, shorten hospital stays, and potentially obviate the need for pacing.
