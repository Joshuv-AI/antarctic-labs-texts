Aroma compound production and probiotic functionality of Bacillus paralicheniformis isolates from Berma, a fermented fish product of Tripura, India
Authors: Debashree Borthakur, Bipin Kumar Sharma, Twesigye Duncan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Berma is a traditional fermented food from Tripura, India, valued for its unique odor and potential health benefits. However, its beneficial microbes remain underexplored. This investigation focussed on the isolation and evaluation of Berma-derived strains for combined probiotic functionality and aroma-producing potential, with emphasis on their suitability as starter cultures in food fermentation. Microbial isolates from Berma were assessed for key probiotic traits, including tolerance to bile (2%), salt (8% NaCl), phenol (0.4%) and acid (pH 2.0), with phenol tolerance indicating intestinal resilience. Auto-aggregation and adhesion assays evaluated their colonization potential. Safety was confirmed through haemolysis, DNase activity, and antibiotic susceptibility tests. Functional performance was validated by fermenting milk and rice substrates, followed by GC–MS analysis of flavor compounds. The isolates were identified using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Among 40 isolates, BER/NA/3 (Bacillus paralicheniformis; GenBank PQ471483.1) exhibited superior tolerance to gastrointestinal stress conditions, strong auto-aggregation (27.43%) and epithelial adhesion (72.81%), while being non-hemolytic, DNase-negative, and antibiotic-sensitive. GC–MS analysis detected beneficial flavor compounds and sensory evaluation confirmed enhanced flavor and acceptability. Strain BER/NA/3 demonstrated robust probiotic potential and flavor-enhancing ability, highlighting it as a safe, multifunctional starter culture for traditional and functional fermented food systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55927-6.

Introduction

Probiotics are live microorganisms that confer health benefits by supporting gut health, immune function, and protection against gastrointestinal pathogens and are commonly associated with fermented foods1,2. Commercial probiotic formulations are dominated by Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Streptococcus3,4. Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) microorganisms are increasingly exploited in food, dairy, and beverage industries due to their fermentative versatility, metabolic activity, and health-promoting potential2,5.In food systems, probiotic microorganisms contribute to product safety, shelf life, and sensory quality6–8, while effective probiotic strains must exhibit tolerance to gastrointestinal stress, adhesion capacity, and absence of virulence factors to ensure functional safety9,10.

Fermentation is a long-established biotechnological process integral to traditional diets, particularly in Asia, where it plays a vital role in food preservation and flavor development11. The region of Northeast India, characterized by rich biodiversity and strong ethnocultural traditions, hosts a wide range of indigenous fermented foods that form an integral part of the regional diet12. Among these, Berma, a traditional salt-fermented fish product of Tripura, India is widely consumed for its distinctive aroma and nutritional value. Despite its cultural significance, the microbial diversity of Berma remains poorly characterized, particularly with respect to probiotic functionality and metabolite production.

Berma: a culturally and microbiologically significant fermented product

Berma is prepared by salt fermentation of silver barb fish (Puti) and is valued for its pungent aroma and complex flavor profile13. Flavor development in fermented foods is primarily driven by microbial metabolism, particularly proteases and lipases, which hydrolyze proteins and lipids into volatile compounds such as esters, aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols14–16. These metabolites strongly influence sensory perception and consumer acceptance17,18. In addition to sensory enhancement, certain fermented food–associated microorganisms exhibit probiotic traits such as stress tolerance, antimicrobial activity, and epithelial adhesion19. However, systematic investigations targeting dual-functional strains that combine probiotic efficacy and aroma-generating capacity remain limited, particularly in indigenous fermented foods of Northeast India.

Overall, this study investigates the suitability of Berma-derived strains as multifunctional starter cultures capable of delivering probiotic benefits alongside enhanced sensory attributes.

Probiotic research has largely focused on lactic acid bacteria (LAB) due to their established safety and health benefits20,21, while the probiotic potential of non-LAB microorganisms remains underexplored. Only a few non-LAB species, including Staphylococcus xylosus and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, have demonstrated both probiotic and flavor-enhancing properties22,23. Increasing evidence demonstrates the strain-specific probiotic potential of Bacillus species, including Bacillus subtilis DSM 32315 and DE111®24–27, and Bacillus coagulans MTCC 5856 and Unique IS2 which exhibit gastrointestinal survivability and industrial relevance28,29. In addition, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens EMD17 has been reported to contribute to antimicrobial activity and flavor-active metabolite production in fermented foods30. Despite these advances, indigenous fermented foods of Northeast India remain largely unexplored as sources of multifunctional microbes, underscoring the need for systematic investigation to unlock their probiotic and industrial potential. Most previous studies have addressed either probiotic attributes or flavor generation independently. In contrast, the present study integrates both dimensions to identify dual-functional microbial strains from a culturally significant fermented food. This holistic approach aligns with the increasing demand for natural, health-promoting, and flavor-rich functional foods. We hypothesized that Berma harbors indigenous microbial strains capable of simultaneously exhibiting probiotic functionality and aroma-generating potential, thereby serving as multifunctional starter cultures for fermented food applications.

Broader implications and sustainability

Exploring the probiotic and sensory-enhancing potential of indigenous microbes from traditional foods such as Berma holds significant socio-economic value. The use of locally sourced microbial cultures supports rural entrepreneurship, preserves culinary heritage, and reduces dependence on commercial starters. Multifunctional starter cultures with probiotic and sensory benefits promote community-driven food innovation, public health, and sustainable food systems. This study highlights the probiotic and flavor-producing potential of Berma-associated microbes for functional foods, rural biotechnology, and microbial resource conservation. By integrating traditional knowledge with modern scientific approaches, the work contributes to culturally grounded, health-oriented, and environmentally sustainable food innovation. Although multiple isolates were initially screened, all functional, sensory, and molecular analyses were focused exclusively on strain BER/NA/3, identified as the most promising candidate.

Methods

Collection and initial processing of traditional fermented Berma samples

This investigation focussed on isolating and characterizing the microbial isolates from Berma, an indigenous fermented fish delicacy of Tripura, located in the Northeastern India. To capture microbial diversity across cultural and ecological gradients, Berma samples were systematically collected from five geographically and ethnographically distinct locations across the state, as outlined in Table 1. The sampling locations are described in Fig. 1. The sites were deliberately shortlisted on the basis of regional variations in traditional fermentation practices, which are hypothesized to influence the microbial composition and diversity of Berma. The samples were collected after 4 months of traditional fermentation under ambient conditions, followed by a final maturation period of 7–10 days prior to analysis. At this stage, the fermentation process is generally considered complete and the associated microbial community is relatively stable.

All the samples were gathered under autoclaved circumstances and carried to research facility in aseptic, insulated polypropylene boxes containing ice packs to preserve a temperature of 4 °C31. The cold chain was strictly maintained to preserve microbial viability, and processing of all samples was completed within 4 h after collection. Each Berma sample underwent homogenization in aseptic physiological saline solution (0.85% NaCl), followed by serial dilutions spanning 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁶. Each dilution was plated in triplicate (100 µL per plate) on two different media: Nutrient Agar (NAM) (HiMedia, India) to determine total culturable bacterial populations, and de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) agar (HiMedia, India) to selectively isolate LAB. Following inoculation, the plates were subjected to incubation at 37 °C—for 24 to 48 h to allow optimal colony development. Colonies exhibiting unique structural characteristics were picked and subjected to successive sub-culturing to achieve purified strains. All purified strains were kept in NAM broth (HiMedia, India) supplemented using 20% (v/v) glycerol and cryopreserved at − 80 °C for downstream characterization and functional assays.

Isolation and cultivation of microbial strains

Microbiological evaluation of Berma samples was performed using the serial dilution technique. Homogenized samples were spread-plated onto NAM and MRS agar to facilitate the growth of total culturable bacteria and LAB, respectively. The culture plates were maintained under incubation parameters at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Colonies demonstrating unique structural features—such as shape, color, size, margin, elevation, and surface texture—were manually shortlisted and repeatedly sub-cultured on MRS agar to ensure purity. These purified isolates were then cultivated in MRS broth and monitored for consistent growth. Preliminary colony characteristics were documented and interpreted as per the taxonomic framework provided in Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology32. Isolates demonstrating robust and stable growth upon repeated sub-culturing were prioritized for further functional and safety evaluations. For long-term preservation, cultures were stored on NAM agar slants at 4 °C and also cryogenically preserved in 20% glycerol at − 80 °C for downstream analyses.

Morphological and biochemical profiling of isolates

To assess enzymatic and phenotypic attributes essential for aroma and taste enhancement in fermented foods, all purified microbial strains were initially cultured on NAM and MRS media and then subjected to incubation for 24–48 h at 37 °C. Structurally unique colonies were sub-cultured to obtain axenic cultures. Preliminary identification was conducted through detailed observation of morphology of the colonies, followed by Gram staining and standard biochemical tests including catalase and oxidase assays33. These assays were employed to distinguish among Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, and to infer potential metabolic capabilities relevant to flavor compound biosynthesis. The results from morphological and biochemical profiling informed the selection of promising strains for advanced probiotic and functional validation.

Evaluation of enzymatic activities involved in Flavor formation

The strains were evaluated for enzyme-related activities essential for development of flavor in fermented foods. Protease production was assessed on skim milk agar (HiMedia, India), with the formation of clear zones indicating casein hydrolysis34,35. Lipase activity was determined using tributyrin agar (HiMedia, India), where the appearance of transparent halos around colonies signified lipid hydrolysis36. Carbohydrate fermentation capabilities were examined in phenol red broth appended using sucrose, glucose, lactose, mannitol and arabinose, using Durham tubes to detect gas and acid production. Positive results indicated active sugar metabolism37. These enzymatic traits serve as critical indicators of the isolates’ potential to enhance flavor development in fermented foods38,39.

Safety assessment of isolates

To verify the safety of microbial strains intended for application in food delicacies, thorough in vitro analyses were performed to identify potential virulence factors, including hemolysis, deoxyribonuclease (DNase) activity, and sensitivity to antibiotics.

Hemolytic Assay

The hemolytic potential of each isolate was determined using blood agar plates to evaluate their cytotoxicity. Colonies were streaked on 5% (v/v) sheep blood agar and subjected to incubation at 30 ± 2 °C for 24 h in a B.O.D. incubator. The isolates’ hemolytic patterns were designated α (partial), β (complete), or γ (negative). Strains producing α- or β-haemolysis were considered potentially pathogenic and excluded from further probiotic evaluation40.

DNase Activity

The strains were evaluated for deoxyribonuclease activity on toluidine blue–enhanced DNase agar (HiMedia, India). Following incubation for 48 h at 37 °C, the formation of clear regions encompassing the colonies was indicative of deoxyribonuclease (DNase) activity41. Isolates that did not produce such zones were categorized as DNase-negative and classified as non-virulent.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Assay

The antimicrobial resistance profile of each isolate was evaluated through the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion assay, following the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024)42,43. Bacterial suspensions were uniformly spread onto NAM plates, and antibiotic-impregnated discs representing clinically relevant antibiotics— frequently employed in the management of infections, especially those attributed to Gram-positive pathogens— were precisely deposited on the agar. The antibiotics were tested: Ciprofloxacin (5 µg), Ofloxacin (2 µg), Azithromycin (30 µg), Amikacin (30 µg), Gentamicin (50 µg), Ampicillin (2 µg), Linezolid (30 µg), Imipenem (10 µg), Levofloxacin (5 µg), Moxifloxacin (5 µg), Chloramphenicol (50 µg) and Neomycin (30 µg). The selection of these antibiotics was based on their critical importance in human medicine, as these antibiotics represent key therapeutic classes—fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, macrolides, β-lactams, and oxazolidinones—and are commonly indicated for the treatment of respiratory, urinary, and gastrointestinal tract infections, skin, and bloodstream44. After incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h, the diameters of growth inhibition were recorded and interpreted using CLSI breakpoint criteria45. Only isolates showing sensitivity to critical clinical antibiotics were considered safe for further application.

Selection of strains

Strains demonstrating strong lipolytic, proteolytic and carbohydrate fermentation capabilities, along with negative profile for hemolysis and DNase activity, and susceptibility to clinically important antibiotics, were shortlisted as dual-functional candidates. These selected isolates were further incorporated into model food systems—curd and rice beer—to evaluate their real-time impact on product taste, aroma, and overall sensory quality.

Quantitative assessment of microbial survival and viability

To maintain experimental uniformity and enable precise comparisons of microbial behaviour under various physiological test conditions, standardized numerical analyses were utilized for all viability and survival tests. The readings of optical density (OD) at 600 nm were obtained through the use of a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). Depending on the experimental design and presence of untreated control groups, two distinct formulae were employed.

Microbial survival rates under acid and bile salt stress

In experiments where microbial survival under stress conditions was compared to growth controls under optimal conditions including bile salt and acid tolerance assays, the following equation was applied:

Evaluation of functional probiotic properties

All probiotic functional assays were carried out under strict aseptic conditions. The media utilized for bile salt, acid, NaCl, and phenol tolerance tests were thermally sterilized using autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. All pipettes and glassware were subjected to sterilization before application. Aseptic nutrient broth (NAM) (HiMedia, India) modified to specific stress parameters was employed to prevent contamination by foreign microorganisms.

Acid Tolerance Assay

The ability of the bacterial isolates to withstand acidic conditions was evaluated based on the methodologies46,47, with slight adjustments to enhance consistency and reproducibility. NAM broth was modified to pH 2, 3, and 4 with the help of aseptic 1 N NaOH or 1 N HCl and then sterilized by filtration to maintain pH integrity. A 2% (v/v) inoculum of log-phase microbial culture was mixed with pH-modified media and subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 12–24 h. Untreated controls consisted of NAM broth supplemented to pH 7.0, with uninoculated broth employed as the blank control. Following incubation, OD₆₀₀ readings of all samples were obtained with the help of UV–visible spectrophotometer, and survival (%) was measured through the equation from Sect. Microbial survival rates under acid and bile salt stress. All assays were conducted in three replicates to confirm statistical robustness and reproducibility of the findings.

Bile Salt Tolerance Assay

Bile salt tolerance of the bacterial isolates was evaluated following modified methods46,47, with appropriate modifications to maintain standardization. NAM broth was fortified with 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0% (w/v) bile salts. A 2% (v/v) inoculum of actively growing microbial cells was appended to test tubes containing the bile concentrations, and cultures were subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 12–24 h. Control assays were performed using NAM broth lacking bile, with sterile, uninoculated broth serving as the blank. OD₆₀₀ was measured for each group, and survival percentages were measured with the help of equation as per Sect. Microbial survival rates under acid and bile salt stress. All experiments were carried out in three replicates to confirm reproducibility and accuracy of analysis.

NaCl Tolerance Assay

Halotolerance of the bacterial isolates was evaluated by culturing isolates in nutrient broth supplemented with 2, 4, 6, and 8% (w/v) concentrations of NaCl46, with minimal adjustments. Each salt-fortified medium received 2 mL of a 24-hour-old, actively multiplying culture, and the cultures were subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. To assess the impact of salt-induced stress on microbial survival, the samples were retrieved at 0 and 24 h, serially diluted, and spread on NAM agar for determination of culturable cell counts, recorded in CFU/mL. Viable CFUs were quantified at 0 and 24 h to assess time-dependent growth, and viability (%) was calculated using the equation in Sect. Viability in NaCl and phenol tolerance assays. All assays were done in three replicates to confirm reproducibility and accuracy of analysis.

Phenol Tolerance Assay

The ability of the bacterial isolates to withstand phenol stress was assessed, following methods48, with slight modifications to improve methodological uniformity and analytical accuracy. Overnight cultures were inoculated (100 µL) into nutrient broth supplemented with 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4% (v/v) phenol. Cultures were subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. To investigate the influence of phenol exposure on microbial survival, samples were obtained at 0 and 24 h, serially diluted, and evenly plated onto NAM agar to assess CFU/mL. The percentage viability was then measured using the formula mentioned in Sect. Viability in NaCl and phenol tolerance assays. Each test was carried out in triplicates to confirm consistency and analytical robustness.

Auto-Aggregation Assay

The auto-aggregation ability of the bacterial isolates was assessed following the protocol36. Overnight-grown cell suspensions were pelleted by centrifugation for 10 min at 5000 rpm at ambient temperature. The obtained cell pellets were rinsed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by resuspension in the corresponding buffer solution. For the auto-aggregation assay, initial OD₆₀₀ was measured (A₀), and 4 mL of the microbial culture were vortexed and then incubated at 30 °C for different intervals of time (0 to 5 h). At every hour, OD₆₀₀ was recorded (Atime). Each experiment was run in three replicates to confirm statistical robustness and reproducibility. Adhesion Assay

The ability of the bacterial isolates to adhere was evaluated based on the method49, with slight modifications. Fresh chicken crop epithelial cells used for adhesion assays were obtained from freshly slaughtered poultry from a local commercial source. No live animal experimentation was conducted as part of this study. The collected chicken crop tissues were washed using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), maintained at 4 °C for 30 min, subsequently rinsed with potassium phosphate buffer, and gently scraped to obtain epithelial cells. The isolated mucosal epithelial cells were suspended in PBS and observed using a monocular compound light microscope (CIPPO NILPA, Japan; S-500) to ensure the absence of indigenous microbial contamination. Microbial suspensions (100 µL) were co-incubated with epithelial cells (400 µL) at 37 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged and washed with PBS to remove loosely attached microorganisms. Pelleted cells were crystal violet–stained and visualized using a monocular compound microscope to evaluate microbial attachment, which was quantitatively assessed by CFU counts after epithelial cell lysis.

Temperature tolerance

The heat resistance of the bacterial isolates was assessed according to the methods48,50. Aseptic nutrient broth, inoculated with overnight-grown cultures, was incubated at temperature spectrum (4, 28, 32, 37 and 40 °C) for 24 h. Microbial growth was quantified by calculating OD₆₀₀ using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer. The investigation determined the optimal growth temperature and tolerance range, highlighting the isolates’ metabolic flexibility and appropriateness for varied fermentation and storage environments.

Application of starter culture in fermented foods

Preparation of curd and sensory evaluation

To validate the technological potential of promising isolates, each isolate was tested as a starter culture for curd production. Pasteurized milk (100 mL) was inoculated with microbial isolates inoculated at 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL and subjected to incubation at 37 °C till coagulum formation was apparent, as depicted in Fig. 2. Before inoculation, the milk was subjected to further sterilization via heat treatment at 85 °C for 15 min and then cooled to 37 °C under sterile conditions. Sterility was verified by plating 100 µL of the heat-treated milk onto NAM agar and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, without any microbial contamination recorded. The resulting curd was subjected to organoleptic evaluation by a trained sensory panel, in accordance with established sensory assessment protocols51,52. Sensory parameters evaluated comprised appearance, aroma, mouthfeel, taste, and overall preference, utilizing a 10-point hedonic scale, where 1 denoted dislike strongly and 10 indicated like strongly.

Rice beer fermentation and sensory evaluation

To assess the functional potential of selected bacterial isolates in a cereal-based matrix, simulated rice beer fermentation trials were conducted under laboratory conditions. Aseptically prepared rice (100 g), previously soaked overnight in distilled water, was subjected to sterilization by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min and subsequently cooled to ambient temperature under sterile conditions. Each candidate strain, standardized to 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, was used to inoculate the sterile rice substrate with a 1 mL aliquot. Each isolate was applied independently as the sole fermentative agent to simulate traditional rice beer fermentation. Fermentation was carried out through solid-state fermentation in sterile, air-tight containers at 28 °C for 11 days, following modified protocols53,54. Prior to inoculation, sterility of the rice matrix was confirmed by plating 100 µL aliquots of the cooled, autoclaved rice water onto NAM and subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 48 h without any growth of microorganisms was detected. Upon conclusion of fermentation, the fermented rice mixture was subjected to pressing and filtration to extract the rice beer samples. The rice beer samples were subjected to sensory assessment by a trained, independent organoleptic panel. Sensory attributes assessed included appearance, aroma, texture, flavor, and overall acceptability, as depicted in Fig. 3, using a 10-point hedonic scale where 1 represents extreme dislike and 10 represents extreme like, in accordance with sensory protocols55.

Organoleptic assessment of fermented products

Organoleptic assessment was carried out to assess the sensory acceptance and sensory characteristics of rice beer and curd fermented with shortlisted isolates. Eight trained panel members (4 males and 4 females, age 20–30 years; mean age: 24 years), involving skilled participants who, after going through a Participant Information Sheet, voluntarily provided written voluntary consent. The panel size was selected in accordance with established sensory evaluation guidelines (ISO 8586 and ASTM E1958). The sensory assessment methodology was authorized by the Research Integrity and Ethics Committee of Assam Don Bosco University, Tapesia Gardens, Assam, India (Approval No. ADBU-RIERB/25/2). All participants provided their consent voluntarily, and all human-subject procedures adhered to the ethical frameworks of both the institution and national regulations, in alignment with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its revisions.

Prior to evaluation, each panel member completed a one-hour training session, during which they were trained on sensory descriptors and scoring procedures and calibrated their assessments using reference samples. All evaluations were performed in three replicates under standardized environment-related parameters (25 ± 2 °C, controlled humidity, white lighting) in separate sensory booths to reduce external interference. Samples were offered in a random sequence in aseptic, coded, single-use polystyrene cups. To maintain double-blind conditions and eliminate bias, both the panelists and the individuals administering the samples were unaware of the microbial identities used in the preparation of curd and rice beer. Curd samples (20–30 mL) were served at 10 ± 2 °C, and rice beer samples (20–30 mL) at 8 ± 2 °C to confirm favourable sensory recognition.

In order to prevent sensory carryover, panel members cleansed their palates between evaluations with lukewarm water and unsalted soda crackers. Organoleptic attributes assessed include aroma, appearance, mouthfeel, texture, flavor, and general acceptability. A structured sensory assessment questionnaire (provided in Supplementary Material) developed specifically for this study, guided the assessment using a standard 10-point hedonic scale. The 10-point hedonic scale, a standardized sensory tool, was used to evaluate user acceptability by having panel members rate their liking of specific product characteristics. Scores were recorded using a 10-point hedonic scale, with 1 denoting “dislike extremely” and 10 indicating “like extremely”56. The final scores were expressed in the form of mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on all panel members and replicates. Microbial isolates producing the top-most organoleptic ratings across both rice beer and curd matrices were designated as best strains for starter culture development. Variations among groups were analyzed with the help of one-way ANOVA, and subsequently analyzed using Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, with statistical significance defined at p < 0.05.

Analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile metabolite profiling of fermented curd and rice beer was conducted using GC–MS to identify volatile compounds produced by the selected microbial isolates57. Compounds were identified using TurboMass software (PerkinElmer, USA) (Software Name: TurboMass™ Software with NIST Mass Spectral Search Program; Version: NIST MS Search Program v2.2 (NIST 14 Database); Link: https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/srd/NIST1aVer22Man.pdf) by comparing mass spectra using the NIST 2014 Mass Spectral Library. Volatile compounds achieving a spectral match ≥ 90% were retained for further interpretation. Volatile compounds were categorized based on reported odor descriptors in food chemistry literature58. Although retention indices (RI) were not determined due to the lack of n-alkane standards, stable chromatographic signals observed in all replicates were used for further investigation. Comparative analysis of volatile compounds between unfermented and fermented samples identified ketones, alcohols, organic acids and esters, that are recognized as key contributors to sweet, fruity, umami, and citrus-like aroma profiles. The results highlight the metabolic potential of the shortlisted isolates to generate a diverse array of odor-active compounds, thus elevating the organoleptic characteristics of fermented food matrices.

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

Taxonomic classification of the shortlisted microbes was done through 16S rRNA gene sequencing, following initial morphological and metabolic profiling. The microbes were sent to Biokart India Pvt. Ltd., Bengaluru, for sequencing analysis. Genomic DNA isolation was performed with the Xploregen™ DNA Extraction Kit, applying minimal adjustments to the manufacturer’s instructions to maximize DNA recovery and purity. The 16S rRNA gene was PCR-amplified using universal primers.: Forward (16 F) – GGATGAGCCCGCGGCCTA and Reverse (16R) – CGGTGTGTACAAGGCCCGG. The amplified products were separated on 1.2% agarose gel (HiMedia, India) stained with ethidium bromide and visualized under ultraviolet illumination59. The forward and reverse purified amplicons were sequenced, and the resultant nucleotide data were aligned and matched with reference sequences available in the NCBI database with the help of BLAST for homolog identification. After GenBank deposition, nucleotide sequences were queried using BLAST. Phylogenetic trees were generated in MEGA X (Software Name: Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis software; Version: MEGA X version 10.2.6; Link: https://megasoftware.net) utilizing the Jukes–Cantor model, and tree robustness was confirmed by 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Isolate BER/NA/3 has been submitted to GenBank database.

Statistical analysis

The assays were executed in three replicates, and data are reported in the form of mean ± SD. All statistical computations were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics v30. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess whether the data followed a normal distribution. The differences between groups were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis and one-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s HSD test for post hoc evaluation. The statistical significance was considered at p-value below 0.05.

Results

Isolation and morphological characterization

In the investigation, ten Berma samples were collected for analysis (Fig. 4). Bacterial isolation was performed on NAM and MRS agar, incubated at 28 °C and 37 °C. Each colony was coded with the sample ID, medium type, and a sequential number (e.g., 1, 2, 3) to ensure systematic tracking and documentation.

Berma sample, ethnic fermented fish from Tripura, India.

Forty microbial strains, displaying diverse morphological characteristics on NAM and MRS agar, were evaluated according to the phenotypic traits and metabolic traits, including Gram staining and catalase activity. Out of the forty microbial isolates, fourteen strains (designated BER/NA/1 to BER/NA/14) exhibited rod-shaped morphology, were Gram-positive, and showed a catalase-negative reaction, as summarized in Supplementary Material. Eleven strains (BER/NA/15 to BER/NA/25) were Gram-positive cocci with catalase-positive activity, whereas fifteen strains (BER/NA/26 to BER/NA/40) were rod-shaped, Gram-positive, and catalase-positive. Each isolate showed variable endospore development and could proliferate anaerobically at 37 °C in MRS medium. From this collection, fourteen strains exhibiting classic LAB attributes—specifically Gram-positive staining, rod-like morphology, and catalase negativity—were chosen for further characterization through detailed microscopic examination and biochemical profiling, as per the protocols described in Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology32.

Enzymatic and biochemical profiling

In order to identify the prevalence of flavor-producing microbes, an array of biochemical assays was employed on fourteen bacterial strains55. The metabolic characterization comprised of assessment of proteolytic and lipolytic activities, production of acid and/or gas from different carbohydrates (glucose, lactose, sucrose, arabinose, and mannitol), and standard biochemical tests such as oxidase, catalase, IMViC (indole production, methyl red (MR), Voges–Proskauer (VP), and citrate utilization) assays. The principal aim of these biochemical tests was to facilitate the initial characterization of the strains based on their metabolic and enzymatic profiles. Within the fourteen isolates exhibiting lactic acid bacterial characteristics, five strains (designated BER/NA/1 to BER/NA/5) demonstrated both proteolytic and lipolytic activities and were capable of fermenting multiple carbohydrates, including lactose, glucose, sucrose, mannitol and arabinose, as summarized in Table 2. All five strains tested negative for IMViC, catalase, and oxidase assays, indicating possible metabolic variability across the isolates.

Safety Assessment

Hemolytic Assay

The hemolysis test serves as a critical criterion in evaluating the biosafety of potential probiotic isolates, because hemolysis is often correlated with cytotoxic and pathogenic behaviour. The strains BER/NA/1, BER/NA/3, and BER/NA/4 displayed γ-haemolysis, indicated by the absence of clear zones surrounding the colonies, confirming their non-hemolytic nature (Table 3).

DNase Assay

The DNase assay serves as an important indicator of virulence of microorganisms, as the secretion of deoxyribonuclease enzymes enables the breakdown of host genetic material, aiding in avoidance of host immune responses and elevating pathogenic potential. The isolates lacked clear hydrolysis areas, demonstrating the absence of DNase production (Table 3).

Antibiotic Susceptibility Assay

Antibiotic susceptibility profiling represents a crucial component in assessing the biosafety of potential probiotic strains, as it aids in minimizing the risk of horizontal dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes. The test encompassed antibiotics representing diverse pharmacological classes, including fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, oxazolidinones, macrolides, carbapenems, β-lactams, and chloramphenicol (Table 3). All procedures were performed as per Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024)42.

Strain BER/NA/1 demonstrated susceptibility to levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, amikacin, gentamicin, linezolid, azithromycin, ampicillin, and chloramphenicol, but was resistant to ofloxacin, neomycin, moxifloxacin, and imipenem, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The resistance observed was mostly within fluoroquinolones and carbapenem classes, suggesting a moderately safe profile. Strain BER/NA/2 showed resistance to most fluoroquinolones (ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin), aminoglycosides, while exhibiting sensitivity to levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, ampicillin and linezolid. This limited susceptibility profile reduces its biosafety potential for probiotic applications. Strain BER/NA/3 demonstrated the widest spectrum of antibiotic sensitivity, exhibiting susceptibility to most of the evaluated antimicrobial agents, except for chloramphenicol. Strain BER/NA/4 demonstrated moderate susceptibility, showing sensitivity to ofloxacin, moxifloxacin, amikacin, linezolid, imipenem, chloramphenicol and ampicillin, while being resistant to ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, gentamicin, neomycin, azithromycin. The overall susceptibility pattern positions BER/NA/3 as a favorable strain with a relatively safe antibiotic profile. Strain BER/NA/5 showed sensitive responses to ofloxacin, levofloxacin, neomycin, azithromycin, and chloramphenicol, but exhibited resistance to amikacin, ciprofloxacin, moxifloxacin, gentamicin, linezolid, ampicillin and imipenem. While this strain retained sensitivity to multiple clinically relevant antibiotics, the presence of resistance to key antibiotics warrants careful consideration. Overall, among the five strains analyzed, BER/NA/3 demonstrated the most favorable antibiotic susceptibility pattern, followed by BER/NA/4 and BER/NA/1. Strains BER/NA/2 and BER/NA/5 showed the least favorable safety profile due to its extensive resistance spectrum. These results underscore the critical need for rigorous strain-level antibiotic susceptibility assessment in the screening of probiotic candidates, especially when intended for clinical, food, or immunocompromised applications.

Selection of strains

Forty microbial strains, labelled BER/NA/1 to BER/NA/40, were originally obtained from Berma samples, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Preliminary evaluation based on detailed biochemical characterization—including assessments of protease, lipase, and carbohydrate-utilizing activities—led to the identification of five superior isolates (BER/NA/1 to BER/NA/5) that consistently demonstrated enhanced enzymatic efficiency across these functional categories. These enzymatic properties serve a pivotal function in the degradation of proteins, fats, and sugars during fermentation, thus improving essential organoleptic parameters including taste, aroma and texture. Isolates exhibiting poor growth performance, production of undesirable sensory compounds (e.g., off-odors), or signs of spoilage were systematically eliminated from further experimentation to ensure the selection of stable and functionally competent candidates for advanced characterization. To determine their probiotic potential, the five selected strains were subjected to a series of standardized biosafety evaluations. Among the five tested isolates, three (BER/NA/1, BER/NA/3, and BER/NA/4) were classified as non-virulent, displaying γ-hemolysis (indicative of non-hemolytic behaviour), absence of DNase enzyme production, and broad sensitivity to antibiotics. These safety parameters conform to internationally accepted probiotic assessment criteria and highlight the appropriateness of these isolates for incorporation into functional food systems and potential health-promoting formulations.

Among the evaluated isolates, BER/NA/3 exhibited the highest thermotolerance, with viability increasing from 49.57 ± 1.43% at 28 °C to 78.29 ± 0.97% at 32 °C, peaking at 94.62 ± 1.58% at 37 °C, and remaining high at 40 °C (88.39 ± 0.66%), as illustrated in Fig. 7. BER/NA/1 showed moderate thermotolerance, with survival rising from 38.97 ± 1.69% at 28 °C, to 57.93 ± 1.28% at 32 °C, 68.97 ± 1.09% at 37 °C, followed by a decline at 40 °C (55.37 ± 1.74%) and limited cold tolerance at 4 °C (4.83 ± 1.82%). Similarly, BER/NA/4 exhibited temperature-dependent viability, maintaining 34.76 ± 1.61% at 28 °C, 59.76 ± 1.76% at 32 °C, 77.18 ± 0.48% at 37 °C, and 62.41 ± 1.19% at 40 °C, with 8.74 ± 1.28% viability at 4 °C.

Preparation of substrates and regulated fermentation

In the current investigation, heat-treated milk was utilized to remove native or background microbial populations, ensuring that all biochemical changes observed during fermentation could be definitively attributed to the added microbial strains. The sterilization process, accomplished via thermal treatment methods such as autoclaving or pasteurization, effectively suppressed native microbes and established a controlled experimental environment for evaluating the functional characteristics of the shortlisted strains. Under these sterile and standardized conditions, curd formation was solely facilitated by the Berma-derived strains—BER/NA/1, BER/NA/3, and BER/NA/4. These strains exhibited the enzymatic potential to metabolize lactose, the primary sugar in milk, leading to the generation of organic acids, predominantly lactic acid. The ensuing decrease in milk pH triggered the precipitation of casein proteins, thereby commencing curd development. In addition to their acid-producing capacity, the chosen strains displayed significant protease and lipase activities. These enzymatic functions are crucial for improving the flavor complexity and consistency of the curd by facilitating the hydrolysis of milk proteins and lipids. These biochemical transformations are key to enhancing the sensory and organoleptic properties of fermented dairy products. The isolates were pre-selected for acidification efficiency, enzymatic activity, and tolerance to simulated gastrointestinal conditions. Molecular identification via 16S rRNA sequencing confirmed the taxonomy of the top-performing strains. Uninoculated, treated milk served as negative controls to enable comparative evaluation of fermentation-induced changes.

In this study, boiled rice was sterilized to remove native microbes, ensuring that fermentation was exclusively driven by the inoculated Berma isolates—BER/NA/1, BER/NA/3, and BER/NA/4—under controlled, aseptic conditions. These isolates demonstrated enzymatic capability to break down the complex starches in rice into simpler, fermentable sugars. The released monosaccharides and oligosaccharides acted as substrates for metabolism, driving the production of ethanol and other fermentation-related compounds. Despite the absence of exogenous yeast inoculation, the selected isolates demonstrated the capacity to generate low levels of ethanol, suggesting their role in both saccharification and initial fermentation phases. Moreover, these isolates actively adjusted the pH of the fermenting substrate, creating favorable conditions for the development of desirable sensory and organoleptic traits. The generation of low levels of ethanol, along with aromatic metabolites, underscores their metabolic versatility and potential application in crafting traditional rice-derived fermented beverages. Strain selection was based on preliminary functional assessments, including enzymatic profiling and acid tolerance evaluations. Final taxonomic identification was conducted through molecular techniques at the conclusion of the study to authenticate species-level classification. Parallel negative controls, consisting of unfermented boiled rice maintained under identical conditions without microbial inoculation, were included to enable comparative analyses of fermentation-induced changes.

Sensory assessment and profiling of volatile metabolites

The organoleptic assessment of rice-based and curd beverage samples fermented with shortlisted strains (Fig. 14) revealed significant differences in aroma and texture (p < 0.05). Products fermented with strain BER/NA/3 scored highest for aroma, texture, mouthfeel, and overall acceptability. GC–MS analysis confirmed that the enhanced flavor profile of these samples was primarily due to the metabolic activity of BER/NA/3, whereas uninoculated controls showed negligible volatile compounds.

GC–MS analysis of products fermented with strain BER/NA/3 identified putative bioactive volatile compounds—including aldehydes, esters, ketones, organic acids and alcohols—that play crucial roles in aroma and flavor development, as tabulated in Table 4. Notably, propionic acid contributed to savory, cheese-like notes, while oxalic acid imparted a sharp, lemony-sour profile, enhancing the tanginess of the products58. These compounds were largely absent or detected at markedly lower levels in control samples, consistent with the sensory evaluation outcomes and supporting the role of BER/NA/3 in aroma enhancement, as a functional and flavor-enhancing starter for fermented foods.

Further examination of the volatile profile indicated that aroma development was dominated by a limited number of compounds with high relative abundance. Organic acids and alcohols constituted the major fraction of the volatile composition in BER/NA/3-fermented samples, with benzamide, 2,3-butanediol, methanol and isobutanol accounting for high relative abundance. In contrast, other detected compounds occurred at substantially lower proportions and are therefore considered secondary contributors to aroma complexity. The predominance of these major volatiles explains their pronounced influence on overall sensory perception observed during organoleptic evaluation.

Statistical summary

The Kruskal–Wallis test indicated notable variations in functional and biochemical, and organoleptic traits among strains from Berma (p < 0.05), with notable variation in probiotic potential (H-statistic = 12.78, p = 3.00 × 10⁻⁴), enzymatic activities related to flavor compound biosynthesis (H-statistic = 10.99, p = 4.1 × 10⁻³), and the correlation between volatile flavor metabolites and sensory attributes for strain BER/NA/3 (H-statistic = 34.00, p = 5.51 × 10⁻⁹). Sensory evaluation of curd and rice beer confirmed highly significant differences (p < 0.001), with BER/NA/3 achieving the highest overall acceptability scores (curd: 6.67; rice beer: 8.60). Post hoc Tukey’s HSD test validated BER/NA/3’s superiority across sensory and biochemical parameters, and key volatiles—propionic acid, 3-hexanol, and oxalic acid—were putatively identified as major contributors to enhanced flavor, supporting its potential as a probiotic and flavor-enhancing starter culture for fermented foods.

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

The strain BER/NA/3, recovered from odor-positive fermented samples, was classified as Bacillus paralicheniformis strain FPIB1 through 16 S rRNA gene sequencing (GenBank accession no.: PQ471483.1). Phylogenetic reconstruction conducted with MEGA X software, employing the Weighbor clustering method in combination with the Jukes–Cantor model for nucleotide substitutions, demonstrated that the isolate formed a well-supported clade with B. paralicheniformis strain OZ-69, as evidenced by high bootstrap support values (> 90%). These results confirm the precise phylogenetic placement of the isolate within the B. paralicheniformis lineage and highlight its potential utility as a flavor-enhancing microbial isolate for application in fermented food matrices. Discussion

The present investigation highlights the probiotic attributes and aroma-producing potential of microbial strains derived from fermented foods of Northeast India. Among the isolates evaluated, strain BER/NA/3 (B. paralicheniformis) demonstrated superior functional performance, including high tolerance to bile salts and acidic conditions, strong adhesion capacity, and the production of key volatile compounds associated with desirable aroma profiles, supporting its potential application in functional food systems. In this study, 40 microbial isolates recovered from Berma were subjected to a stepwise screening strategy. Fourteen isolates were initially shortlisted based on Gram-positive morphology and biochemical characteristics, followed by selection of five strains with strong enzymatic and carbohydrate-fermenting abilities. Subsequent biosafety assessment further narrowed down the selection to three non-virulent strains. Among these, BER/NA/3 consistently outperformed the others across probiotic stress tolerance, adhesion properties, sensory enhancement, and aroma compound production. Accordingly, BER/NA/3 was selected for comprehensive functional and molecular characterization.

Safety assessment

Hemolytic Assay

The lack of erythrocyte lysis suggests that these isolates possess lack of hemolytic effect on erythrocytes, implying a minimal risk of pathogenicity. The γ-hemolytic phenotype is consistent with internationally recognized probiotic safety standards and substantiates the inclusion of these isolates, in particular, BER/NA/3—as suitable strains for incorporation into food products. Furthermore, the non-hemolytic characteristic complements other safety parameters, including DNase negativity and antibiotic susceptibility, reinforcing their non-virulent profile.

DNase Assay

The absence of DNase activity indicates that these strains do not possess nucleic acid–degrading properties usually associated with virulence. These finding supports their non-pathogenic nature and strengthens their qualification as safe probiotic candidates for incorporation into food matrices. The lack of this enzyme additionally corroborates their biosafety profile, an essential requirement for probiotic strain selection and regulatory compliance.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Assay

The antibiotic susceptibility profile of strain BER/NA/3 provides important insight into its safety and potential applicability as a probiotic candidate. While resistance to certain antibiotics was observed phenotypically, such resistance does not necessarily indicate the presence of transferable antimicrobial resistance determinants. However, the present study did not investigate the genetic basis of these resistance traits, and therefore cannot distinguish intrinsic from acquired resistance. As per the current FAO, WHO and EFSA recommendations, further whole-genome analysis will be required to confirm the absence of transferable antibiotic resistance determinants.

Probiotic characterization

Acid and bile tolerance were assessed using OD-based measurements as a preliminary screening approach; however, optical density reflects overall biomass and may overestimate viable cell numbers. CFU-based enumeration would provide a more conservative assessment of survival and should be considered in future confirmatory studies. The viability values exceeding 100% reflect enhanced growth relative to the untreated control rather than absolute survival. Such values arise from normalization against control growth and indicate growth stimulation under sub-inhibitory stress conditions, rather than an increase beyond baseline viability. As viability was calculated based on optical density relative to the control, values above 100% indicate increased biomass accumulation and should not be interpreted as absolute cell survival.

Temperature Tolerance

Thermal resilience is a critical functional criterion for probiotic selection, as it governs survival during food processing, storage, distribution, and gastrointestinal transit. Strains capable of tolerating temperature fluctuations exhibit improved technological reliability and shelf stability. Among the tested isolates, BER/NA/3 demonstrated superior intrinsic thermotolerance, maintaining high viability at elevated temperatures, thereby indicating strong physiological robustness under thermal stress. Although BER/NA/1 exhibited lower thermotolerance than BER/NA/3, its survival profile supports its applicability in food systems stored at ambient or moderately elevated temperatures. BER/NA/4 showed acceptable performance under moderate heat stress; however, its reduced cold tolerance may restrict its use in formulations requiring prolonged refrigerated storage. Collectively, BER/NA/3 emerged as the most thermotolerant isolate, retaining over 85% viability at 40 °C without thermal preconditioning. This performance compares favorably with established probiotics such as Lactobacillus acidophilus NCFM and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG, which typically require adaptive treatments to maintain comparable viability under similar thermal stress conditions60,61. The inherent thermotolerance of BER/NA/3 provides a clear technological advantage, supporting its potential application in dynamic probiotic formulations, including fermented beverages, shelf-stable foods, and encapsulated nutraceuticals.

Acid Tolerance

Acid resistance is a key physiological criterion for probiotic selection, as it determines a strain’s ability to survive the highly acidic gastric environment and reach the intestine in a viable state. In this study, all isolates showed improved survival with increasing pH, indicating variable adaptive responses to acid stress. Among them, BER/NA/3 consistently exhibited the highest viability across acidic conditions, reflecting strong physiological resilience and a high likelihood of successful gastric transit and intestinal functionality. Although BER/NA/1 showed lower survival than BER/NA/3, its tolerance to low pH supports its classification as a moderately acid-tolerant probiotic candidate. In contrast, BER/NA/4 displayed reduced survival under extreme acidity, though its performance at higher pH levels suggests potential suitability for applications involving limited gastric exposure or buffered delivery systems. Collectively, BER/NA/3 emerged as the most acid-resilient isolate. Its performance is comparable to established probiotic strains reported in the literature, including Lactobacillus fermentum MTCC 8711 retained 50–55% viability at pH 2.0 for 3 h62, Exiguobacterium acetylicum strains from Hentak exhibited ~ 79% survival under similar conditions63, the commercial probiotic Limosilactobacillus reuteri DSM 17938 maintained 40–60% viability at pH 2.064. These comparisons underscore the strong acid tolerance of BER/NA/3 and support its suitability for inclusion in functional foods and nutraceutical formulations targeting gastrointestinal delivery.

Salt Tolerance

NaCl tolerance is a key functional criterion in probiotic selection, as it reflects a strain’s ability to withstand osmotic stress during food processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit. Strains that retain viability under elevated salinity are particularly suited for fermented and salt-preserved foods. In this study, strain BER/NA/3 exhibited superior salt tolerance across all tested NaCl concentrations, indicating a stable adaptive response to osmotic stress and highlighting its robustness in saline environments. These findings are consistent with previous reports on salt-tolerant lactic acid bacteria. For instance, Tetragenococcus halophilus tolerates up to 10% NaCl, with optimal performance at approximately 6%65, while Lactobacillus sakei retains 80% viability at 6% NaCl66. In comparison, BER/NA/3 maintained nearly 63% of its maximum viability at 6% NaCl and remained viable at 8%, underscoring its applicability in salt-enriched food fermentation systems. Overall, BER/NA/3 emerges as a promising candidate for functional food and probiotic applications requiring stability under osmotic stress.

Phenol Tolerance

Phenol tolerance is a critical functional trait for probiotic selection, as it reflects a strain’s ability to withstand toxic metabolites generated in the gastrointestinal tract from microbial degradation of aromatic amino acids. In this study, BER/NA/3 exhibited outstanding phenol tolerance, maintaining high viability under increasing phenolic stress, suggesting effective intrinsic detoxification or adaptive stress-response mechanisms. This strong resilience positions BER/NA/3 as a suitable candidate for probiotic applications where sustained exposure to intestinal metabolic by-products is anticipated. In contrast, BER/NA/1 showed lower phenol tolerance; however, its consistent survival profile indicates potential utility in multi-strain probiotic formulations, where functional complementation may enhance overall robustness. Strain-specific differences in phenol resistance highlight inherent variability in stress adaptation among isolates. Notably, BER/NA/3 performed comparably or better than established probiotics such as Lactobacillus salivarius UCC118 and Pediococcus pentosaceus, which typically retain 60–63% viability at 0.4% phenol67,68. By comparison, BER/NA/3 maintained nearly 95% viability at the same concentration without prior adaptation, underscoring its intrinsic resistance to phenol toxicity. Overall, BER/NA/3 emerged as the most phenol-tolerant isolate, followed by BER/NA/4 with moderate resilience and BER/NA/1 with lower tolerance. Phenol tolerance values exceeding 100% represent enhanced growth relative to the control rather than mere survival, indicating that exposure to sub-inhibitory phenol concentrations may promote cellular activity in certain strains. This observation is consistent with adaptive physiological responses to mild stress and/or limited metabolic utilization of phenolic compounds, and should not be interpreted as absolute survival beyond baseline levels.

Bile Tolerance

Bile salt tolerance is a critical determinant of probiotic efficacy, as it reflects a strain’s ability to survive and function during intestinal transit. Bile salts disrupt microbial cell membranes, making resistance to bile-induced stress essential for intestinal persistence. In this study, the progressive decline in viability with increasing bile concentration suggests the presence of adaptive responses such as bile salt hydrolase (BSH) activity, membrane stabilization, or active transport mechanisms69. Among the tested isolates, BER/NA/3 exhibited the highest bile tolerance, indicating strong intrinsic resistance to bile stress. BER/NA/1 showed moderate tolerance, retaining appreciable viability under increasing bile concentrations, whereas BER/NA/4 displayed limited resistance at higher bile levels, suggesting reduced probiotic potential in bile-rich environments. The performance of BER/NA/3 is comparable to established probiotic strains, including Lactobacillus acidophilus ATCC 4356 and Enterococcus durans F3, which retain approximately 63–70% viability at 2.0% bile70,71. Although BER/NA/3 showed lower absolute survival, its unconditioned tolerance highlights inherent resilience, supporting its suitability for functional food and probiotic formulations targeting gastrointestinal health.

Auto-Aggregation

Auto-aggregation is an important probiotic trait, reflecting a microorganism’s ability to self-associate and form stable cell clusters. This property is closely linked to epithelial adhesion, biofilm formation, and competitive exclusion of intestinal pathogens, thereby supporting sustained gut colonization and prolonged probiotic efficacy. In this study, BER/NA/3 exhibited a strong, time-dependent increase in auto-aggregation, indicating effective self-adherence and intercellular cohesion, supporting stable mucosal attachment and sustained gut colonization. BER/NA/4 showed a steady but comparatively lower increase in aggregation over time, suggesting moderate adhesive potential, while BER/NA/1 displayed the lowest yet progressive aggregation response. The observed time-dependent enhancement in aggregation across all strains may be associated with quorum-sensing regulation, cell-surface hydrophobic interactions, or stress-induced adaptive mechanisms. Variations in aggregation performance are likely attributable to differences in surface properties, including the expression of adhesins, exopolysaccharides, or aggregation-promoting proteins. Overall, BER/NA/3 emerged as the most promising candidate in terms of auto-aggregation capacity. Its performance aligns with that of well-characterized probiotic strains such as Lactobacillus plantarum and Enterococcus faecium, which typically exhibit aggregation levels ranging from 20% to 30% under comparable experimental conditions72.

Adhesion to epithelial cells

Adhesion to host epithelial surfaces is a key determinant of probiotic efficacy, as it supports intestinal colonization, prolonged persistence, and competitive exclusion of pathogens. In this study, adhesion potential was evaluated using chicken crop epithelial cells, a widely accepted in vitro model. Among the tested isolates, BER/NA/3 demonstrated the highest adhesion capacity, indicating a strong affinity for epithelial surfaces and suggesting its ability to establish stable mucosal colonization. Strain-specific differences in adhesion are likely attributable to variations in surface physicochemical properties, including cell surface hydrophobicity, exopolysaccharide production, and the expression of adhesion-associated structures such as mucus-binding proteins or pili. The superior adhesion of BER/NA/3 positions it as the most promising candidate for probiotic applications requiring effective mucosal interaction and prolonged gastrointestinal residence. Notably, the adhesion efficiency of BER/NA/3 (72.81%) is comparable to that of well-characterized probiotic strains reported in the literature, including Lactobacillus plantarum WCFS1 (60–70%) and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (~ 65%) under comparable in vitro conditions73,74. These findings underscore the intrinsic potential of BER/NA/3 for inclusion in functional foods and therapeutic probiotic formulations aimed at enhancing mucosal health and host–microbe interactions.

The adhesion property was evaluated using chicken crop epithelial cells as an initial in vitro screening model to assess the epithelial interaction potential of the isolates. This model provides a biologically relevant epithelial surface containing mucus-associated binding sites that facilitate interactions with bacterial adhesins and surface polymers, enabling rapid comparative assessment of adhesion capacity. However, it is acknowledged that chicken crop epithelial cells do not fully replicate the structural and molecular complexity of the human intestinal epithelium. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as preliminary indicators of adhesion potential, and future studies will employ human intestinal cell lines such as Caco-2 or HT-29 for confirmatory validation.

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

Although strain BER/NA/3 was identified as Bacillus paralicheniformis based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing, it is acknowledged that 16S rRNA analysis has limited resolution within the Bacillus licheniformis species complex. Future studies employing whole-genome sequencing, average nucleotide identity (ANI), or multilocus sequence analysis will be required to confirm species-level assignment.

Comparison with reference probiotic strains

Isolate BER/NA/3 (Bacillus paralicheniformis) demonstrated robust tolerance to gastrointestinal stressors, with survival percentages of 56.44 ± 1.54% in highly acidic conditions (pH 2.0) and 42.17 ± 0.48% in media containing 0.5% bile salts following 24 h of incubation. These levels of acid and bile resistance are comparable to those reported for established probiotic strains such as Lactobacillus plantarum and Bacillus subtilis, supporting the suitability of BER/NA/3 for gastrointestinal transit and probiotic applications48,75,76. In addition, BER/NA/3 demonstrated an auto-aggregation capacity of 27.43 ± 1.75% after 5 h and exhibited a strong adhesion rate of 72.81 ± 1.49% to chicken epithelial cells—functional attributes recognized as crucial for effective mucosal colonization and persistent probiotic action. These values position BER/NA/3 within the performance range of benchmark strains such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG and Bacillus subtilis CU1, which are highlighted in literature77 for their high adhesion and aggregation abilities. Regarding thermal adaptability, BER/NA/3 maintained 88.39 ± 0.66% viability at 40 °C, signifying mesophilic traits. This thermotolerant profile is consistent with the physiological adaptability noted in closely related Bacillus species and supports its technological robustness for varied processing and storage environments. Comparisons with commercial probiotic formulations reveal that Bacillus strains including Bacillus coagulans and Bacillus subtilis are widely verified for gastrointestinal resilience, biosafety, and functional health-enhancing effects. In this investigation, BER/NA/3—along with other robust isolates—achieved survival rates above 50% under simulated gastrointestinal conditions (pH 2.0, 0.5–2.0% bile salts), auto-aggregation levels exceeding 25%, and adhesion efficiencies over 70%. These parameters are directly comparable to, and in several instances exceed, those reported for commercial and reference strains, underscoring their competitive potential as next-generation probiotic candidates78,79. Notably, beyond standard probiotic attributes, strain BER/NA/3 also demonstrated the ability to elevate the sensory attributes of fermented foods, leading to the production of desirable volatile aroma compounds that contributed to improved flavor and acceptability in model dairy and rice-based fermentations. This dual-functionality—integrating gastrointestinal resilience with sensory enhancement—distinguishes BER/NA/3 and comparable strains as potential isolates for use in therapeutic probiotic formulations and novel functional food products.

Evaluation of biosafety of Bacillus Strains

Even though isolate BER/NA/3 could only be taxonomically classified to the species level as Bacillus paralicheniformis, its safety attributes were convincingly corroborated through extensive phenotypic evaluations. These analyses confirmed the absence of hemolytic properties and DNase activity, as well as demonstrated sensitivity to a broad spectrum of clinically relevant antibiotics. Collectively, these features are in line with previous reports of non-pathogenic Bacillus species isolated from traditional fermented foods—especially B. licheniformis, B. subtilis, and B. amyloliquefaciens—which are well-established for their probiotic functionality and enzymatic contributions to flavor development80. For example, Bacillus subtilis natto has a well-documented history of application in soybean-derived fermentation delicacies in Japan. It exhibits strong proteolytic activity and has been demonstrated to colonize the gastrointestinal tract safely without eliciting negative effects81. Similarly, B. licheniformis strains isolated from fermented foods such as Korean doenjang and Chinese sufu have been found to exhibit comparable safety profiles—namely, the lack of hemolytic and DNase activities—thereby supporting their classification as food-grade, beneficial microorganisms following rigorous evaluation82. Strain BER/NA/3 exhibits non-hemolytic, DNase-negative traits and broad antibiotic susceptibility, distinguishing it as a safe, non-pathogenic candidate. To fully confirm its suitability for food applications, further molecular analyses—including whole-genome sequencing and screening for toxin genes—are necessary to rule out latent virulence factors.

Volatile compound production and functional value

Apart from probiotic characteristics, isolate BER/NA/3 (Bacillus paralicheniformis) generated various odor-active volatile organic compounds (VOCs), such as 3-hexanol, oxalic acid, and propionic acid. These compounds contribute to favorable sensory characteristics, including sour, citrus-like, and cheese-associated notes. The manifestation of both probiotic and organoleptic attributes by strain BER/NA/3 (Bacillus paralicheniformis) indicates its potential to improve the nutritional functionality and the sensory appeal of fermented food products. Volatile compounds were putatively identified based on mass spectral matching with the NIST library.

Approaches for commercial application of probiotic strains

Although isolate BER/NA/3 demonstrates robust probiotic and flavor-enhancing capacities, its commercial adoption necessitates a systematic appraisal of several scale-up variables. Principal challenges include preserving cellular viability and bioactivity during large-scale cultivation, safeguarding formulation integrity throughout storage, and adhering to internationally recognized nutritional safety and microbial quality standards (e.g., EFSA, FSSAI, FDA). Cost-effectiveness—particularly with respect to culture-medium optimization and volumetric productivity—also remains pivotal. Employing encapsulation technologies, freeze-drying, and precise fine-tuning of bioprocess parameters may bolster storage stability and delivery efficacy. Finally, pilot-scale verification and organoleptic trials within commercially pertinent food matrices are essential to translate laboratory insights into market-ready applications.

Conclusion

This investigation presents the initial verified identification of Bacillus paralicheniformis isolates from Berma exhibiting dual functionality—combining probiotic potential with flavor-enhancing capabilities. The distinctive metabolic attributes of these isolates highlight their prospective contribution to enhancing both the nutrient content and sensory quality of traditional fermented foods. This finding lays a foundational basis for the development of innovative starter cultures specifically designed to sustain and augment indigenous fermentation practices. These strains emerge as promising candidates for enhancing the industrial applicability and functional health potential of indigenous fermented foods.

Study limitations and future work

Limitations

Although this study provides a strong preliminary assessment, several limitations highlight opportunities for future research. The safety evaluation relied entirely on in vitro assays; thus, whole genome sequencing and safety evaluation using suitable models are needed to confirm molecular safety. Probiotic potential was not tested in vivo, limiting insights into colonization, immune modulation, and long-term effects. Although antibiotic susceptibility was profiled, direct antagonistic activity against enteric pathogens was not assessed. GC–MS analysis targeted a limited range of volatiles; untargeted metabolomics could uncover additional bioactives. The small sensory panel (n = 8) may constrain the generalizability of consumer acceptability data. The absence of a commercial or defined traditional starter culture as a benchmark limits direct comparison of functional performance, sensory attributes, and volatile compound profiles with established starter systems.

Future directions

Our research will undertake a comprehensive evaluation of the probiotic potential and application versatility of the identified isolates. Whole-genome sequencing of strain FPIB1 will be conducted to elucidate the availability of probiotic-related genes and to assess the genome for antibiotic resistance determinants and potential virulence factors. Animal-based safety and gut colonization assessments will be performed using rodent or human gastrointestinal models to validate the strains’ functional stability and safety profile. Sensory trials will be expanded using larger, blinded panels to confirm consumer acceptance across diverse demographic groups. Additionally, the performance of these strains will be evaluated in a variety of alternative fermentation substrates, including plant-based matrices and functional beverages, to explore their suitability for broader industrial applications. Future work should also incorporate well-characterized commercial probiotic or traditional starter cultures to enable direct comparative evaluation and to further validate the industrial applicability of strain BER/NA/3 under controlled fermentation conditions.
