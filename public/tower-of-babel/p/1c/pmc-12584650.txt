Western equine encephalitis virus: A comprehensive review of epidemics, transmission, hosts, and strategies for mitigation
Authors: Lan Wang, Ruoqi Zheng, Zichen Li, Leiliang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent increases in cases of western equine encephalitis (WEE) in South America have raised significant concerns about the virus’s potential to cause an endemic disease due to its adaptation to mosquito vectors. Currently, there are no effective vaccines or treatments for WEEV, despite ongoing research into various biochemical products in animal models. The virus presents different pathological effects depending on the host. In humans, WEEV infection leads to central nervous system damage, resulting in encephalitis and severe neurological sequelae, which underscores the need for further research into its pathogenic mechanisms. To enhance understanding of WEEV and address potential threats, this review focuses on the virus’s natural transmission cycle, the development of biochemical products for combating WEEV infection, and new diagnosis methods. It also highlights research on mechanisms in animal models, offering potential strategies for defending against WEEV.KEYWORDS: WEEV, mosquito vector, encephalitis, vaccine, diagnosis

Introduction

The western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV) is a positive single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the Alphavirus genus within the Togaviridae family. It is a recombinant of the New World Alphavirus eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV) and the Old World Alphavirus Sindbis virus [1]. WEEV causes WEE, which results in symptoms like fever, chills, headache, myalgia, and particular neurological sequelae, though it is generally milder compared to eastern equine encephalitis (EEE). EEEV is estimated to have a mortality rate of 30% (https://www.cdc.gov/eastern-equine-encephalitis/php/surveillance-and-control-guidelines/index.html), exceedingly higher than the one of WEEV with 3% to 15% (www.cdc.gov/wee/symptoms-diagnosis-treatment/index.html). As an arthropod-borne virus, WEEV cycles naturally between mosquito vectors and vertebrate hosts, including amplifying hosts like birds and dead-end hosts such as humans, horses, and other mammals [1–3]. The primary vector is Culex tarsalis, which can transmit the virus from birds to mammals, leading to central nervous system diseases and even death [4]. Although mosquito bites are a common route of infection, WEEV was contracted via aerosol transmission in a laboratory setting by five individuals, resulting in two deaths [5]. This highlights the severity of the WEEV in humans and raises concerns about its potential use as a biological weapon.

While the mpox virus has been a major public health concern, the outbreak of WEE in Southern America, including Argentina and Uruguay, has also drawn global attention. The WEE virus was first isolated in 1930 but then became less prominent after several human epidemics [3,6,7]. WEEV strains have evolved into two groups: Group A, with high virulence like the Fleming strain, and Group B, with lower virulence such as the Imperial 181 strain. Recent studies suggest that changes in virulence are linked to alterations in receptor affinity [8–10]. Despite the use of equine vaccines to control WEEV in horses, there are currently no effective vaccines or treatments available for humans, making WEEV a concerning issue in concentrated horse-breeding areas. The virus’s long period of relative obscurity may hinder vaccine development due to a lack of clinical trial subjects and the misconception that WEEV has disappeared. However, ongoing research into the virus’s pathology, infectious factors, and prevention methods for both vectors and vertebrate hosts provides a foundation for developing strategies to protect humans and animals involved in its natural transmission cycle.

Epidemic in history

WEEV has been reported across North America, including the southwestern United States and Canada, as well as in southern South America, notably Argentina and Uruguay [1]. Since its discovery in 1930, thousands of horses have been infected, with the first human fatality recorded in 1938. A significant outbreak occurred in 1941, resulting in over 3,000 human cases in North America, with a case fatality rate ranging from 8% to 15%. Another epidemic in 1952, particularly affecting California, resulted in 9 fatalities. Human cases of WEE increased annually over the next 30 years, peaking until 1988, when only one case was reported in the U.S. (www.cdc.gov/wee/data-maps/index.html).

In 1970, WEEV antibodies were detected in the serum of children in Uruguay. This was followed by equine epizootics in Argentina and Uruguay between 1972 and 1973, during which WEEV was isolated from infected horses. From the 1990s onward, both human and equine WEEV cases declined, suggesting a reduction in the epidemic’s intensity [3,7]. However, a resurgence occurred when a rural adult in Argentina’s Santa Fe province, where WEEV-positive horses had been identified, was diagnosed with WEE on 20 December 2023. This marked the first human case in Argentina since the outbreaks of 1982–1983, which were linked to equine epizootics, and a 1996 case that was not associated with equine outbreaks (www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2023-DON499).

As of 25 January 2024, Argentina has reported 39 human cases of WEE distributed across the central and eastern regions of the country: Buenos Aires (23 cases), Cordoba (2 cases), Santa Fe (10 cases), Entre Rios (3 cases), and Santiago del Estero (1 case). These cases include five fatalities-three in Buenos Aires, one in Entre Rios, and one in Santa Fe (www.infobae.com/salud/2024/01/30/ya-son-7-los-muertos-por-encefalitis-equina-en-argentina-y-se-detectaron-al-menos-41-casos-en-humanos). Since the alert was issued on 25 November 2023, there have been 1,323 confirmed outbreaks of WEEV in equines across 16 provinces of Argentina, although the number of new equine infections has been declining in 2024. Additionally, Uruguay, Argentina’s neighboring country, reported its first human case of WEEV since 2009 (www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2024-DON505). This case involved a 42-year-old from a rural area in San Jose, confirmed on 30 January 2024.

Notably, Rio Grande do Sul, a state on Brazil’s border with Argentina and Uruguay, has experienced active WEEV circulation. This outbreak, which had resulted in 217 human cases and 12 fatalities by April 2024, was attributed to limited enzootic transmission between vectors and birds or insufficient WEEV surveillance, particularly in rural and remote areas [11]. Significantly, a new study explored a model predicting potential regions for an increase in WEEV by 2050 and 2100 in South America [12]. The study revealed that thermal seasonality and annual rainfall, which are important factors related to climate change, could affect mosquitoes and birds and further influence the expansion of WEEV. It is crucial for people to remain vigilant, regularly vaccinate equines, and monitor WEEV genetic recombination with other viruses, as this could lead to new mutations and potentially sudden outbreaks among equines and humans.

Virology

Alphaviruses share highly similar biology, and WEEV is no exception. Originating approximately 1300 to 1900 years ago, WEEV is a recombinant virus formed from the New World EEEV and the Old World Sindbis-like Virus [1,9]. WEEV is an enveloped virus with a nucleocapsid structure, featuring a central positive single-stranded RNA of about 11.5–11.7 kb in length. This RNA genome encodes both structural proteins – capsid, E1, E2, 6K, and E3—and nonstructural proteins (nsP1-4) essential for RNA transcription, replication, and translation [13]. The glycoproteins E1 and E2 are related to the Sindbis-like virus, while the capsid and nonstructural proteins are more closely associated with EEEV [1]. Both EEEV and WEEV exhibit neurotropism due to their ability to replicate in neurons, suggesting that WEEV’s neuropathology is influenced by its relationship with EEEV. Differences in nucleotide sequences may account for why WEEV typically causes milder neurological symptoms compared to EEEV. The invasion and release processes of alphaviruses are similar, yet research on WEEV biology remains limited. Recent studies have shown that avian MXRA8 interacts with the WEEV E1 glycoprotein, facilitating viral entry in avian hosts rather than in mammals (Figure 1) [14]. Further cellular studies are needed to develop targeted treatments for affected animals and patients.

Overview of the WEEV natural transmission cycle. Mosquito vectors, such as Culex tarsalis and Aedes melanimon, transmit WEEV to amplifying hosts. Culex tarsalis primarily infects avian species (e.g. house finches and house sparrows), while Aedes melanimon targets blacktail jackrabbits. WEEV can infect mammals through mosquito bites or aerosol exposure. Dead-end hosts, mainly mammals, develop western equine encephalitis and associated neurological sequelae due to neural damage and neuroinflammation. Neuronal receptors for WEEV in mammals include PCDH10, VLDLR, and ApoER2; however, MXRA8 is the receptor in avians, and mammalian MXRA8 does not facilitate WEEV infection. The virus-induced activation of IRF-3 helps neurons protect themselves, though WEEV capsid proteins can inhibit this activation. Type I IFN effectively suppresses WEEV infection in differentiated neurons. Dopaminergic neurons are damaged when astrocyte innate immunity triggers the secretion of C3 protein, causing α-synuclein protein aggregation, which leads to neurodegeneration and Parkinsonism. Further research is needed to explore the pathophysiological molecular mechanisms of WEEV interactions with neurons and neuroglia in the CNS.

Phylogenetic studies have classified WEEV strains into two main lineages, Group A and Group B, with Group B further divided into sublineages B1, B2, and B3 [8,9]. A recent investigation identified a novel WEEV lineage, designated as Lineage C, from three fatal equine cases in the Rio Grande do Sul state. This new lineage is closely related to the 1958 CBA87 strain and may be linked to recent WEEV outbreaks in Argentina and Uruguay [11]. Research into WEEV virulence across different strains has yielded inconsistent results, making it challenging to correlate viral virulence with human cases. Although historical strains like McMillan were highly virulent, newer strains such as Imperial 181 have shown reduced virulence. Despite this, studies on WEEV Group B strains in mouse models have not consistently indicated a decline in virulence. The emergence of high-virulence strains does not necessarily correlate with increased incidence of human cases [15–18]. This suggests that virulence might not be the primary factor influencing WEEV transmission. The high conservation of WEEV sequences could contribute to the lack of human cases over the past 20 years, despite the variability in strain virulence. Recent studies have highlighted changes in the interaction between WEEV and its receptors, including protocadherin 10 (PCDH10), very low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR), and apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (ApoER2) (Figure 1) [19–22]. These receptors bind with the WEEV E2-E1 spike protein and vary among evolutionary strain lineages. Group A strains can interact with all three receptors, while Group B strains primarily bind with PCDH10 EC1, with exceptions noted for strains Imperial 181 and R02PV003422B from Group B3. The presence of these receptors in neurons supports the neurotropism of WEEV, which can lead to neural sequelae and mortality. For example, Imperial 181 binds to avian PCDH10 rather than mammalian receptors, which may explain its lower virulence in mammals [10]. Two recent studies have demonstrated that one to three substitutions in the E2 glycoprotein of the WEEV can significantly alter its binding ability to different receptors, which may explain the sharp decline and resurgence of WEEV epidemics [23,24]. WEEV evolution appears to be more about forming reservoirs than increasing pathogenicity. The recent outbreaks in Argentina and Uruguay underscore the importance of monitoring strain variations and highlight the need for continued vigilance and vaccination efforts.

Human and animal transmission

WEEV transmission involves three key processes: the source of infection, the route of transmission, and susceptible animals (Figure 1). The transmission cycle primarily involves the vector Culex tarsalis and avian hosts with viremic blood, such as house finches and house sparrows. Additionally, there is a secondary cycle involving Aedes melanimon and the amplifying host blacktail jackrabbit, which also originates from the primary cycle. Culex tarsalis can infect a broad range of birds, serving as secondary amplifying hosts in areas where mosquitoes are prevalent. Equines, humans, and some wild animals are considered dead-end hosts, as they rarely contribute to the spread of the virus; hence, transmission to distant areas may rely on the migration of infected birds [5,25]. Intriguingly, equines with high-titer viremias can serve as amplifying hosts for the Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), leading to severe periodic or scattered epidemics and resulting in spillover transmission to humans [26]. This phenomenon may help explain the outbreaks of WEEV and other alphaviruses to some extent. The virus can cause a range of symptoms in different hosts, which may suggest a transformation in WEEV’s invasion mechanisms amid natural evolution. Research into WEEV’s hosts can enhance our understanding of virus-host interactions and aid in the development of more effective drugs and vaccines to combat WEEV at all stages of transmission.

Vector

As the sole vector involved in WEEV transmission among animals and humans, mosquitoes play a crucial role in the virus’s natural transmission cycle. Studies have revealed an adverse relationship between mosquitoes and WEEV, leading to pathological lesions in the abdominal midgut of Culex tarsalis, including epithelial cell detachment, necrosis, vacuolation, microvilli separation, and epithelium degradation [27]. Besides the midgut, WEEV can affect various Culex tarsalis tissues such as salivary glands, chorionated eggs, neural ganglia, and trachea [28]. Damage to the salivary glands, evidenced by vacuolation, may explain the gradual decline in transmission rates and suggests that these glands could serve as viral reservoirs, potentially facilitating covert propagation of WEEV [29]. Infected female mosquitoes exhibit a higher spawning rate compared to uninfected controls, likely due to changes in yolk texture from granular to smooth [29,30]. The virus also impacts feeding and reproductive behaviors by affecting the mosquito’s nervous system, including neuroendocrine cells [28]. Despite causing tissue pathology and reducing life expectancy and fitness, mosquitoes have developed four barriers to block viral dissemination: the midgut infection barrier, midgut escape barrier, salivary gland infection barrier, and salivary gland escape barrier. The infection barriers primarily protect against infection of mesenteron or salivary gland cells, while the escape barriers prevent secondary dissemination [31,32]. Additionally, Culex pipiens quinquefasciatus, Culex pipiens pallens, Culex tritaeniorhynchus, Aedes albifasciatus, and Aedes aegypti are identified as potential WEEV vectors, with Culex pipiens pallens showing similar pathology and dissemination as Culex tarsalis [33–35]. Unlike Culex tarsalis, the VLDLR orthologues in Aedes aegypti can act as receptors for the WEEV strain McMillan and Fleming, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms between the virus and mosquito cells [10]. Despite advances in understanding macroscopic pathology and defense barriers, specific interaction mechanisms between WEEV and mosquitoes remain incomplete, and vertical transmission through mosquito eggs is still unknown. Moreover, emerging vector species may contribute to outbreaks in certain regions.

Amplifying host

Primary amplifying hosts, notably house finches and house sparrows, play a crucial role in the persistence, distribution, and outbreaks of WEEV. While song sparrows have been considered potential amplifying hosts based on laboratory subcutaneous inoculation studies, they are rarely infected naturally through oral transmission or mosquito bites [36]. The decline in WEEV cases, despite no changes in vector or host competence, may be partially explained by lower WEEV antibody levels in California birds from 1998 to 2008 [37,38]. House finches infected with WEEV produce sufficient antibodies to prevent reinfection. However, RT-PCR has detected residual virus in their spleen and lungs, indicating a chronic infection [39]. Chronic infection likely enhances a bird’s role as an amplifying host by maintaining high antibody titers that contribute to the host’s survival [40]. Interestingly, the infectious dose of WEEV does not necessarily correlate with the level of viremia or the frequency of chronic infection in house finches [41]. Immunosuppression can lead to acute rather than chronic WEEV infection [40].

Several factors influencing WEEV infection rates in birds, as indicated by seroprevalence studies, have been explored. Adults are generally more attractive to mosquitoes than nestlings, suggesting that adults may serve as primary amplifying hosts. Current infection status does not appear to attract vectors significantly [42]. Forecasting models have demonstrated that increased abundance of female Culex tarsalis heightens WEEV transmission risks, especially when both pre-sampling temperatures and Culex tarsalis abundance are extreme [43]. Hatching-year birds play an important role in amplifying the virus due to their seasonal presence, and resident species generally exhibit higher seroprevalence compared to migratory species [44].

Understanding these factors, particularly chronic infection, deepens our comprehension of WEEV transmission dynamics and informs strategies to disrupt the mosquito-bird cycle. Research into WEEV pathology in birds is expected to reveal potential drug targets for controlling virus transmission. Overall, a nuanced understanding of chronic infection and the various factors influencing WEEV transmission will aid in developing effective methods to curb the spread of the virus between mosquitoes and birds.

Dead-ended host

Without the ability to generate abundant viremia, humans and other animals, particularly equines, are at the end of the WEEV natural cycle. Unlike EEE, which is highly virulent, most human cases of WEE are asymptomatic, with mortality rates ranging from 3% to 15%. Typically, a few patients experience mild symptoms such as fever, chills, headache, and myalgia after a 2 to 7-day incubation period, which may be followed by more severe encephalitic symptoms. Infants, due to their higher susceptibility, often suffer severe neurological outcomes and sequelae, including convulsions, quadriplegia, spasticity, or rigidity. In contrast, adults with WEEV infection may experience intellectual deterioration and personality changes, while the elderly are at an increased risk of developing Parkinsonism as a result of WEEV infection [45].

The invasion of neurons by WEEV leads to its replication and subsequent pathological lesions characterized by neuronal destruction, microglial nests, and amorphous spongy lesions. These lesions are often concentrated in the striatum, thalamic nuclei, pontine nuclei, and the Purkinje and molecular cell layers of the cerebellum [46]. Perivascular infiltrates and multifocal necrosis are primarily observed in the deep gray matter, particularly the corpus striatum, with fewer lesions detected in the brain stem, spinal cord, and cerebral cortex. Additionally, lesions can vary in the basal ganglia, thalami, brain stem, and subcortical white matter, as evidenced by previous studies [47,48]. Hypoventilation, a complication of encephalitis, often starts with respiratory distress during the day and progresses to heightened sensitivity to chemical stimuli at night. This may result from the destruction of the brain stem, where the respiratory center is located, or from damage to the vagus nerve, which is crucial for regulating respiration [49].

The innate immune response plays a significant role in limiting neuronal damage through WEEV replication-dependent IRF-3 activation, which mediates neuronal cytoprotection. However, the WEEV capsid protein can inhibit IRF-3 activation by suppressing downstream signaling molecules of the pattern recognition receptor (PRR) pathway related to IRF-3 activation (Figure 1) [50].

Viral invasion, interferon (IFN) attack, and neuronal maturation all contribute to neuronal destruction. Neuronal maturation can reduce cytopathology induced by the virus, independent of IFN secretion. The amount of type I IFN required to activate self-defense mechanisms and suppress the virus may explain why severe neurological symptoms and sequelae are more common in infants, whose neurons are more immature compared to adults [51]. The innate immune system is crucial for self-protection, although lymphocyte infiltration suggests some role of the adoptive immune response, despite its general refractoriness to WEEV [52].

In summary, the interplay between viral factors, immune responses, and neuronal maturation underscores the complexity of WEEV pathogenesis and its impact on different age groups. Continued research into these mechanisms will be vital for developing targeted interventions and improving outcomes for affected individuals.

Animal models

Explorations into the pathological mechanisms and antiviral methods for WEEV necessitate diverse animal models that simulate human infection, including mice, hamsters, and cynomolgus macaques. Research involving mice models has utilized various viral infection methods such as intranasal, aerosol, subcutaneous, and intraperitoneal routes [53–56]. For example, outbred CD-1 mice infected intranasally with a recombinant WEEV McMillan strain expressing firefly luciferase (fLUC) exhibited neurological symptoms like depression, motor deficits, ataxia, visual impairment, and lateral recumbency with tachypnea. These symptoms resulted from widespread lesions in the brain and cranial nerves, beginning with neuroinvasion in olfactory bulb neurons and extending to the trigeminal nerve, which increases brainstem infection [56]. In contrast, when WEEV was introduced into CD-1 mice via footpad injection to mimic mosquito infection, the virus primarily accessed the brain through circumventricular organs where the blood-brain barrier (BBB) is absent, followed by centripetal spread along the neuronal axis, without affecting the olfactory bulb neurons. This suggests a potential mechanism for viremia bypassing the BBB [55]. Hematogenous dissemination of alphaviruses like VEEV, EEEV, and WEEV may occur across the intact BBB through caveolae-mediated transcytosis in brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs), possibly regulated by IFN which inhibits viral replication, and RhoA GTPase which stabilizes caveolae structure in BMECs. Additionally, aerosol exposure to WEEV in Balb/c mice can cause severe acute neurological symptoms and lesions in the bilateral olfactory bulb and piriform cortex, with increased particle deposition in the lungs [54]. Notably, subcutaneous infection of infant B-629 mice with WEEV leads to necrotizing degeneration in muscles, cartilages, and bone marrows, causing death within 48 hours, whereas young adults develop necrotizing meningoencephalitis with pathological changes in the heart, lung, liver, kidney, and brown fat. This indicates that the infection route significantly impacts the manifestation of symptoms and disease progression [53].

Comparative studies between intracranial and subcutaneous infections demonstrate that intracranial infection results in complete encephalitis, while subcutaneous infection results in milder viremia with less than 50% likelihood of encephalitis. This highlights that selecting the appropriate infection route can optimize the utility of animal models for studying human symptoms [57]. Hamster models, infected via methods such as intracerebral, intraperitoneal, intradermal, and respiratory routes, show distinct peak titers and timings in the brain and olfactory lobe, with common pathological symptoms including glial proliferation, perivascular cuffing, hemorrhages, spongy degeneration, and neuronal necrosis. Notably, hamsters infected intracerebrally with the attenuated clone 15 strain did not develop the severe sequelae seen with other infection methods, suggesting that intracerebral infection may mitigate significant neuronal necrosis or that earlier activation of astrocytes may help control excessive neuroinflammation and other immune responses [58].

Parkinsonism sequelae can be effectively duplicated in CD-1 and C57BL/6 mice infected intranasally with WEEV, allowing for the exploration of the pathological mechanisms underlying WEEV-induced Parkinsonism. Persistent gliosis, driven by neuroinflammation factors produced by the innate immune response of astrocytes, is a key feature of this condition. This gliosis is associated with the loss of dopamine neurons, particularly in the substantia nigra pars compacta, and the aggregation of α-synuclein protein. The latter process is regulated by C3 protein secreted by neurotropic astrocytes in the cortex, hippocampus, and midbrain, all contributing to the development of Parkinsonism (Figure 1) [59,60].

In non-human primate (NHP) models, such as cynomolgus macaques, the route of infection significantly affects the severity of the disease. Subcutaneous infection results in milder outcomes in terms of survival, hematology, and pathological changes in the brain compared to infection via the aerosol route, even though both methods do not lead to viremia. This difference underscores the importance of infection route in studying disease progression and pathology [61,62]. Given that the aerosol route can lead to more severe consequences for humans, there is a pressing need for an appropriate animal model to test and evaluate the efficacy of vaccines. In studies involving cynomolgus macaques infected with WEEV via the aerosol route, clinical signs of encephalitis were observed. Immunohistochemical evidence confirmed WEEV invasion, which induced a marked infiltration of lymphocytes in the central nervous system (CNS). Additionally, increasing serum glucose levels correlated with the severity of clinical signs. Unfortunately, three cynomolgus macaques died during this experiment for unknown reasons. Notably, it is presumed that WEEV directly invades the brain by violating the olfactory nerves, as the virus was not detectable in the blood or other organs [61].

Hamster models, particularly those infected intraperitoneally with WEEV and pre-treated with IFN or an IFN inducer, demonstrate prolonged survival. This suggests that such models could be valuable for assessing antiviral agents and therapies [63]. The modulation of host factors – including age, gender, and genetic background – in mice can help simulate the infectious environment of NHPs and even humans, providing insights into antiviral drug efficacy [64].

Animal models are crucial for understanding the entry, circulation, and pathogenesis of WEEV, offering a broad platform for research and development of effective treatments. By leveraging these models, researchers can gain valuable insights into the mechanisms of disease and explore potential therapeutic strategies. In this review, we collect relevant animal models to provide detailed information about research involving WEEV. Each model has its own strengths and weaknesses, influenced by the different animals used in studies. Non-human primates are closer to humans than mice; however, they have slower reproduction rates, higher costs, and are generally more aggressive. Additionally, the current level of genetic purification in non-human primates is significantly lower compared to that of laboratory mice. While mice may not be as closely related to humans as non-human primates, they reproduce quickly, are more cost-effective, and have a gentler disposition. They also offer a high degree of genetic purification and are highly operable in laboratory settings. Despite the use of different animal models, the survival rate after vaccination or treatment can approach 100%. Thus, factors such as safety may be more crucial when determining the efficacy of treatments for humans.

Diagnosis

Accurate diagnosis of WEEV infection relies on laboratory methods such as PCR, viral cell culture, and serological tests, given the lack of specific clinical symptoms distinguishing it from other encephalitides like VEE or EEE. Vector surveillance is also crucial for epidemiological investigations. A cost-effective method for monitoring WEEV infection in vectors is reverse transcription loop-mediated isothermal amplification (RT-LAMP), though it is less sensitive compared to reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) [65]. RT-qPCR offers high sensitivity, and a duplex TaqMan RT-PCR targeting WEEV nsP3 and EEEV E3 can detect as few as one copy per reaction, making it suitable for both human and vector surveillance [66].

Antibody-based methods have advanced for detecting WEEV glycoproteins with high sensitivity and efficiency. For instance, the single-chain fragment variable (scFv) antibody RS10B5huFc, which includes a human heavy chain IgG1 constant region and a 6 His tag with an enterokinase recognition site, binds effectively to WEEV antigens in ELISA assays. Additionally, the 11D2-biotin mimic ScFv, designed with VL – VH orientation and a (gly4ser)3 linker, exhibit ultrasensitive detection of WEEV antigens in ELISA tests [67,68].

Recent advancements include seven new single-domain antibodies (sdAbs) that offer advantages such as heat resistance, refolding capability after degeneration, and good water solubility. These sdAbs effectively bind to WEEV E2/E3E2 and could significantly enhance the diagnosis and treatment of WEE [69]. Moreover, distinctive probes for RNA virus detection, which are less complex and time-consuming, are being developed to improve WEEV detection methods.

Prevention and treatment

To date, there are no specific vaccines or treatments available to prevent or manage WEEV infections in humans. Given that WEEV poses a potential threat as a lethal biological weapon, and considering that only three out of five laboratory workers accidentally infected through aerosol exposure survived [5], the development of vaccines and therapeutics is urgently needed to prevent a potential global pandemic. Attenuated viruses and chimeric alphaviruses have shown promise as vaccine candidates due to their low transmissibility and infectivity in mosquito and avian hosts. These modified viruses are unlikely to participate in the natural transmission cycle, making them suitable for vaccine development. Studies have demonstrated their efficacy in preventing WEEV infection in mouse models [70–73]. For instance, vaccines utilizing human adenovirus serotype 5 (HAd5) as a delivery vector, which lacks replication capability, have been developed. These vaccines incorporate the WEEV E3-E2-6K-E1 structural protein gene, producing Ad5-WEEV expressing E1 and E2 proteins and Ad5-E1 expressing only E1 protein. These formulations have induced neutralizing antibodies that protect mice from lethal challenges by various WEEV strains, sometimes even with a single dose [74–76].

Additionally, the modified vaccinia Ankara Bavarian Nordic (MVA-BN®) vaccines, whether multivalent or monovalent, have proven effective. These vaccines provide substantial protection against WEEV when tested via subcutaneous, intramuscular, or aerosol routes. Moreover, a Phase 1 clinical trial has suggested that a trivalent vaccine targeting VEEV, EEEV, and WEEV could be a promising candidate for preventing equine encephalitis [77–79].

Another approach involves DNA and virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines, which have demonstrated promising efficacy. The C-E3-E2-6K-E1 gene, incorporated into DNA vaccines and VLP vaccines through appropriate vectors, has shown potential in eliciting robust immune responses [80,81]. Notably, a DNA vaccine expressing the E1 protein alone has provided complete protection against WEEV infection, though it may have limitations due to heterologous responses [82]. Additionally, DNA vaccines delivered via intramuscular (IM) electroporation (EP) also exhibit strong efficacy in eliciting protection against WEEV infection [83].

Aerosol-challenged NHPs achieved full protection against EEV through pre-immunization with a trivalent VLP vaccine [80]. The effectiveness of virus-like replicon particle (VRP) vaccines for VEEV, EEEV, and WEEV has also been evaluated in cynomolgus macaques, with encouraging results [84]. Recombinant E2 proteins and lipid-antigen-nucleic acid complexes (LANACs), which consist of cationic lipid-nucleic acid complexes (CLNCs) and the ectodomain of WEEV E1 protein (E1ecto), are potential vaccine candidates due to their efficacy, immunogenicity, and biosafety [85–87]. Moreover, CLNCs have demonstrated the ability to induce innate immune responses, enhancing the production of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α in the brain. This response helps protect mice from lethal challenges, whether pre-treated or post-treated following WEEV infection [88]. Additionally, IFN-α expression mediated by Ad5 has been shown to protect mice from lethal damage caused by WEEV, both when administered before or after infection [89].

These advancements in vaccine development highlight the significant progress being made in combating WEEV. Notably, the TSI-GSD 210 WEE vaccine shows promise as a feasible candidate for emergency use [90]. Continued research and clinical trials are essential to optimize these vaccine candidates and address the challenges associated with WEEV infections. With sustained efforts, it is hoped that effective vaccines and treatments will become available to prevent and manage WEEV outbreaks, mitigating the risk of a global pandemic.

The baculovirus expression system has been developed to produce the glycoprotein structure of WEEV for vaccine production, utilizing optimized procedures. Recombinant baculoviruses in insect cells generate WEEV VLPs, which have demonstrated the ability to induce immune responses and produce neutralizing antibodies in mouse models. These VLPs show promise as potential vaccine candidates for combating WEEV [91,92]. As the global threat of viral epidemics intensifies, further research into virus evolution is anticipated to identify appropriate targeting sites. This would enable the development of vaccines capable of eliciting more rapid and long-lasting immune responses.

In addition to artificial active immunity, which involves the stimulation of the immune system to produce its own protective responses, artificial passive immunity is also critical for immediate prevention and treatment. The use of specific antibodies provides significant protection against WEEV. Various methods for screening WEEV antibodies have proven effective in offering protection in mouse models and hold promise for advancing detection techniques for WEEV infection (Table 2) [69,93–96].

Despite limited research on anti-WEEV drugs, there has been notable progress in developing potential treatments (Table 3). For instance, a thieno[3,2-b]pyrrole compound, which inhibits live alphavirus in neural cells, has shown improved metabolic stability through bioisosteric replacement of its core structure with indole. The modification of chirality has enhanced target enantioselectivity, leading to the creation of an indole analog, CCG-203926. This compound has demonstrated the ability to alleviate symptoms and improve survival rates in mouse models [97,98]. Furthermore, indole-2-carboxamides, another class of alphavirus inhibitors, have shown increased efficacy in combating WEEV. The development and optimization of conformationally restricted analogues of indole-based drugs offer a promising avenue for creating more potent antiviral medications [99,100]. Additionally, broad-spectrum antiviral drugs such as antimycin A and T-705, which target a range of RNA viruses, have been effective in inhibiting WEEV and improving outcomes in mouse models [101,102]. Extracts from Achyrocline satureioides (Asteraceae), known for their antiviral properties, have shown some effectiveness against WEEV. However, their efficiency remains relatively low, and further research is needed to fully explore their antiviral mechanisms and improve extraction methods [103]. Molecular docking analyses have identified potential plant-derived natural products and small molecules that interact with WEEV, targeting the nsP2 proteases [104,105]. These findings suggest that natural products could be a valuable resource in the search for effective antiviral treatments.

Despite these advancements, most vaccines and drugs are currently stalled at the animal testing stage. This stagnation may be attributed to a prolonged period of relative inactivity regarding WEEV, which may have led to decreased global vigilance. Immediate clinical trials are essential to assess the effectiveness of various vaccines and drugs. These trials should keep pace with ongoing research and development to address emerging threats posed by WEEV.

In summary, while significant strides have been made in the development of vaccines and antiviral drugs for WEEV, continued research and clinical testing are crucial. Advances in vaccine technology, antiviral drug design, and natural product research hold promise for improving prevention and treatment strategies. The urgency of combating WEEV requires a concerted effort to translate these findings into effective clinical interventions, ensuring timely and robust responses to this persistent and potentially deadly virus.

Conclusion

A comprehensive understanding of the epidemic, including the hosts involved in the natural transmission cycle and the biochemical characteristics of WEEV, is crucial for developing effective response strategies against WEEV infections in both equines and humans. One of the most pressing issues is that most vaccines, drugs, and antibodies currently available for WEEV are still in the animal testing phase. These products need to undergo clinical trials to confirm their efficacy and address any existing limitations. Identifying the specific strain responsible for an outbreak is equally important, as it helps determine whether WEEV has evolved significantly or if a previously known, highly virulent strain has reemerged. WEEV, as a recombinant virus of EEEV and Sindbis virus, may benefit from antiviral products developed for these related viruses. There is a possibility that these products could offer shared effectiveness in controlling WEEV. Effective prevention should focus on both internal immune system activation and external protective measures. This includes wearing protective clothing to cover all exposed skin, using effective insect repellents, and reducing outdoor activities during peak mosquito breeding seasons. To address WEEV comprehensively, it is recommended to approach it as part of a broader strategy involving the development of multivalent antiviral products. Multivalent products could offer broader protection by targeting multiple related viruses. International cooperation and communication are essential to advance clinical research on WEEV, enhance our understanding of its pathogenesis, and build cumulative clinical experience. Increasing awareness of WEEV prevention measures is also vital. This includes periodically vaccinating equines, especially in areas with significant horse commerce, and implementing effective outbreak control measures. Vaccination of equines should be prioritized in regions where horse commerce is prominent, as these areas are at higher risk of WEEV transmission. Effective measures to prevent outbreaks include developing and deploying multiple strategies tailored to different aspects of WEEV transmission and impact. These strategies should involve both individual protective actions and community-wide initiatives to reduce the risk of WEEV spreading. The goal is to prevent WEEV from causing widespread outbreaks with global consequences, similar to the impact of the mpox virus. By promoting research, improving prevention strategies, and fostering international collaboration, we can better manage and control WEEV infections and mitigate their potential global impact. In summary, a multifaceted approach is required to tackle WEEV effectively. This includes advancing vaccine and drug development, enhancing surveillance to identify and track strains, implementing robust preventive measures, and fostering international research collaboration. By taking these steps, we can work toward minimizing the impact of WEEV and preventing it from becoming a major global health threat.
