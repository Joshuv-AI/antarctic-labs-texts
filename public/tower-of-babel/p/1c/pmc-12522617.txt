Dexmedetomidine pretreatment improves postsurgical delay in neurocognitive recovery in aged mice by inhibiting hippocampal microglial activation via activation of cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathways
Authors: Qidi Zhang, Shiyu Hao, Guicheng Wang, Chengxiao Liu, Gongming Wang, Jie Zhang, Jinyang Zhao, Xiaowei Li, Jingjing Li, Jiangnan Wu, Xu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative use of dexmedetomidine (DEX) reduces the incidence of Delayed Neurocognitive Recovery (DNR) in elderly patients, though mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated whether DEX improves DNR by inhibiting microglial activation and explored the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway’s role.MethodsAn exploratory laparotomy model was established in aged C57BL/6J mice, with preoperative treatment using DEX or DEX combined with α-bungarotoxin. Cognitive function was assessed through the novel object recognition (NOR) and Morris water maze (MWM) tests, while immunofluorescence was used to observe microglial morphology, and qPCR and ELISA were employed to detect inflammatory factor expression.ResultsDEX pretreatment significantly reduced the escape latency of aged mice on postoperative days 3–5 (50.50 ± 3.73 vs. 55.01 ± 4.01, P = 0.04; 36.36 ± 4.31 vs. 43.42 ± 5.64, P = 0.01; 27.00 ± 3.94 vs. 34.50 ± 5.54, P = 0.006), increased number of times crossing the previous platform location (2.50 ± 1.31 vs. 1.08 ± 0.90, P = 0.02), and percentage of dwell time in the target quadrant (37.87 ± 9.66 vs. 25.00 ± 4.48, P < 0.001). DEX pretreatment also reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampus (TNF-α, 604.10 ± 165.40 vs. 915.30 ± 97.64, P = 0.002; IL-6, 145.30 ± 12.11 vs. 176.50 ± 16.15, P = 0.002; IL-1β, 59.68 ± 4.29 vs. 68.73 ± 3.11, P = 0.001) of aged mice postoperatively, as well as in the spleen and serum. Additionally, DEX pretreatment reduces microglia count (96.60 ± 11.84 vs. 136.20 ± 32.62, P = 0.05), fluorescence (0.96 ± 0.06 vs. 1.10 ± 0.02, P < 0.001), morphology (7.20 ± 2.17 vs.12.80 ± 2.28, P = 0.002) in aged mice post-surgery. Pre-administration of α-bungarotoxin before DEX pretreatment partially reversed these effects.ConclusionsDEX pretreatment ameliorated DNR in aged mice is related to inhibition of microglial activation, which was at least partially attributed to activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway.Keywords: Delayed neurocognitive recovery, Dexmedetomidine, Microglia, Cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, Α-bungarotoxin

Introduction

Delayed neurocognitive recovery (DNR) is a common central nervous system (CNS) complication that occurs within 30 days after surgery and is characterized by functional decline in several cognitive domains, such as learning ability, spatial orientation, attention, and working memory. DNR might further lead to irreversible cognitive disorders or even dementia without timely intervention [1]. Clinical studies have shown that the incidence of DNR within 30 days after noncardiac surgery is up to 47% [2]. As the population ages, the number of people over 65 requiring surgery is increasing, and these individuals face a correspondingly higher risk of cognitive decline [3]. As a specific subtype of perioperative neurocognitive disorder (PND), DNR is strongly associated with long-term prognosis and mortality of patients, making it an important target for intervention and treatment [1]. However, its underlying mechanisms remain unclear .

There is growing evidence that neuroinflammation plays an important role in DNR, with postoperative cognitive decline accompanied by the upregulation of inflammatory factor (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, etc.) expression in both the peripheral circulation and hippocampus [4, 5]. Notably, inflammatory factor inhibitors can be applied to treat hippocampus-dependent cognitive dysfunction mediated by chronic neuroinflammation [6]. Thus, perioperative inflammatory modulation may be a potential preventive and therapeutic target for DNR.

Microglia are a class of macrophages widely distributed in the CNS, and their activation has been observed in various neurodegenerative diseases [7]. Microglia serve as immune sentinels in the CNS. Under normal physiological conditions, they exhibit a highly branched morphology to monitor environmental changes and maintain brain homeostasis [8]. When stimulated, microglia rapidly transform from a branched morphology to an amoeboid form, characterized by an enlarged cell body, shortened processes, increased phagocytic activity, and the production of cytokines. This process is known as microglial activation [9]. There is growing evidence that microglia are highly heterogeneous and plastic [10]. Under different physiological or pathological conditions, microglia exhibit different behavioral patterns based on the signals they receive. They respond to changes in the external environment by altering their morphology and function, including changes in activation state, movement, proliferation, and secretion of various substances [11, 12]. It has been shown that surgery involving anesthesia induces microglial activation, which plays an important role in neuroinflammation [13].The degree of microglial activation is often used as a key indicator to assess neuroinflammation and neuronal damage [14]. Persistent or excessive release of inflammatory mediators can exacerbate brain injury, leading to progressive neuronal dysfunction and ultimately cognitive impairment [11]. Inhibiting microglial activation or depleting these cells can alleviate neuroinflammation and improve cognitive decline [13, 15]. Therefore, microglial activation may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of DNR.

Cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathways are neural circuits that respond to and modulate the inflammatory response, with the vagus nerve and its neurotransmitter, acetylcholine, playing a central role in this regulatory process [16]. Inflammatory signals transmitted to the vagal center result in enhanced vagal efferent impulses and increased release of acetylcholine from terminal fibers [17]. Acetylcholine binds to specific receptors on mononuclear macrophages, which are widely distributed in the tissues of various organs, and inhibits the excessive release of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, thus antagonizing the systemic inflammatory response [18, 19]. It has been shown that α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) is present on the surface of macrophages, endothelial cells, epithelial cells and other cells and mediates the anti-inflammatory effects of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway [18]. Further studies have shown that electrical stimulation of the vagus nerve or pretreatment with a α7nAChR agonist attenuates neuroinflammation associated with reduced nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activity [17, 20, 21]. In contrast, severance of the vagus nerve or the use of methyllycaconitine (a selective α7nAChR antagonist) enhances inflammatory responses [22, 23]. Moreover, vagus nerve stimulation can modulate microglial activity via the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, primarily through the activation of the α7nAChR on microglia. This pathway can reduce neuroinflammation by inhibiting the activation of inflammasomes, such as the NLRP3 inflammasome, in microglia [24]. Given that the onset of DNR is closely associated with neuroinflammation [25], we propose that the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway may play a critical role in mitigating the inflammatory effects underlying DNR.

Dexmedetomidine (DEX) is a selective and potent α2-adrenergic receptor agonist that is widely used to sedate critically ill patients by activating adrenergic receptors in the brain and spinal cord, inhibiting the release of norepinephrine, and decreasing the efferent activity of central sympathetic nerves [26], thus reducing the stress response to surgery and exerting the effects of sedation, analgesia, and maintenance of hemodynamic stability [27]. In addition to its sedative effect, DEX has excellent anti-inflammatory properties [28]. It has been shown to attenuate the inflammatory response to sepsis-associated acute lung injury and protect against ischemia/reperfusion injury in the heart, kidneys, brain, or intestines in animal models [29–34]. DEX has been shown to inhibit the expression of various inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β [35]. Moreover, other studies have shown that the anti-inflammatory effect of DEX is mediated by the activation of central rather than peripheral adrenergic receptors; specifically, the mechanism of action is likely to involve the activation of central medullary receptors, which reduce central sympathetic nerve activity, thereby enhancing parasympathetic excitability and producing a vagal-like effect, which in turn downregulates the expression of inflammatory cytokines [35, 36]. Although some studies have already explained the anti-inflammatory mechanism of DEX [37], we would like to take a step further to investigate the specific mechanism of DEX to improve cognitive function by establishing an exploratory laparotomy surgical model in aged mice.

To date, the specific mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory and organ-protective effects of DEX have not been fully elucidated, and its role in DNR has been studied even less. We aim to hypothesized that the anti-inflammatory and cognitive protective effects of DEX in a mouse model of exploratory laparotomy are mediated through its activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. This activation inhibits microglial activation, regulates systemic inflammatory cytokine levels, and ultimately protects postoperative cognitive function. Therefore, this anti-inflammatory mechanism based on DEX could lead to effective interventions during the perioperative period for elderly patients at risk of DNR and may be the key to preventing and treating DNR.

Materials and methods

Animals

The experimental design strictly complied with the Shandong First Medical University regulations on the use and protection of animals, met the ethical requirements for the use of experimental animals, and was approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of Shandong First Medical University and Provincial Hospital of Shandong First Medical University. Male C57BL/6J mice aged 18 months and weighing 30–40 g were purchased from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.). The mice were housed in separate cages with a 12/12 h circadian rhythm, the temperature was maintained at 22 ± 1 °C, the relative humidity was 40–60%, and the mice were allowed to ingest food and water freely.

Subgroup design and mode of administration

The study was divided into two parts. In Part 1, the mice were randomly divided into the following groups: (1) naive, (2) surgery, (3) surgery + DEX, and (4) surgery + vehicle. Mice in the naive group did not undergo any treatment; mice in the surgery group underwent exploratory laparotomy; mice in the surgery + DEX group were pretreated with an intraperitoneal injection of 50 µg/kg DEX 30 min before surgery (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) [38]; and mice in the surgery + vehicle group were injected intraperitoneally with an equal volume of saline 30 min before surgery. In Part 2, the mice were randomly divided into the following groups: (1) surgery, (2) surgery + DEX, (3) surgery + DEX + α-bungarotoxin (α-BGT), and (4) surgery + α-BGT. Thirty minutes before the DEX pretreatment, the surgery + DEX + α-BGT group was administered an intraperitoneal injection of 1 mg/kg of the cholinergic pathway α7nAChR receptor-specific antagonist α-bungarotoxin (Glpbio, USA) [39]; mice in the surgery + α-BGT group were injected with 1 mg/kg α-BGT via intraperitoneal injection 60 min before surgery.

Surgical procedure

As in our previous studies, exploratory laparotomy was used to establish a model of DNR in elderly mice [40]. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (0.2 ml/10 g). The abdominal area was shaved and disinfected with 70% ethanol and iodophor. With the experimenter wearing sterile latex gloves, a 2 cm vertical incision was made approximately 0.5 cm below the right lower rib cage, penetrating the peritoneal cavity. A sterile cotton swab was used to probe the intra-abdominal organs, after which approximately 10 cm of bowel was exposed and rubbed firmly between the thumb and forefinger for 30 s. Finally, the intestines were placed back into the abdominal cavity, and the peritoneum, abdominal muscle and skin were sutured closed layer by layer. To prevent infection, the wound was coated with polysporin (Pfizer, USA), and a subcutaneous injection of 3 mg/kg bupivacaine (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) was used for postoperative analgesia. The surgery lasted approximately 60 min. A heated blanket was used during the operation and recovery periods. In addition, to ensure the safety of the mice during surgery, mask oxygen was given and blood oxygen saturation and respiratory status were continuously monitored using a mouse pulse oximeter (Starr Life Sciences Corp, USA) to prevent potential hypoxemia risk.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

NOR test is a behavioral experiment used to assess recognition memory in animals, typically rodents. It is based on the natural tendency of animals to explore novel objects more than familiar ones [41]. This experiment was divided into three phases: habituation, training, and testing. In the habituation phase, the mice were placed in the open box to familiarize themselves with the environment for 5 min. In the training phase, on the second day of the NOR test, two identical cylinders (familiar objects) were placed in two symmetrical corners of the box, with the distance of the objects from the side and the back walls of the box being 5 cm. The mice were placed in the open box with their backs facing the two objects, with an equal distance from the tip of the nose to the two objects. The total exploration time in a 10 min period was recorded as the duration when the mouse’s nose or mouth touched or was within 1.5 cm of the objects; additionally, placement of the front paws on the objects and smelling the objects were considered to indicate exploration of the objects, but cases when the mouse climbed onto the objects and did not move were not counted as exploring the new objects. The test period was on the third day (i.e., 24 h after the training period). During the test period, one of the cylinders was replaced by another cube of a different color (novel object), and the exploration times of the new object and the old object were recorded over a 5 min period. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated to assess the memory discrimination ability of the mice using the formula DI = N / (N + F) × 100%, where N is the exploration time of the new object and F is the exploration time of the familiar object. To avoid odor and lateral dominance effects, the objects were scrubbed with 75% alcohol and randomly placed in different corners of the box.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

The MWM is a widely used behavioral experiment designed to evaluate spatial learning and memory in animals, typically rodents [42]. The MWM experiment consists of three main components: a circular pool, a cylindrical platform, and a recording system, which includes two parts: the place navigation test and the spatial probe test. The pool is 40 cm high with a diameter of 120 cm. Before the experiment begins, the pool is filled with water to a depth of 30 cm, and the water temperature is maintained at 22 ± 1℃. The pool is evenly divided into four quadrants (NE, SE, SW, NW) according to the four cardinal directions, with black visual cues posted at the midpoints of the pool wall arcs in each quadrant, serving as the entry points for the mice. Our visual cues consist of geometric shapes, including squares, triangles, circles, and pentagons. A platform with a diameter of 6 cm is placed in the NE quadrant, submerged 1 cm below the water surface. A camera is positioned approximately 2 m above the center of the pool to automatically capture images of the animals’ swimming trajectories, which are then analyzed and processed by an animal behavior analysis system (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd) and its accompanying VisuTrack software. The first five days of the experiment are dedicated to the place navigation test, where researchers gently place the mice facing the pool wall into the water and allow them to swim for 60 s to find the submerged platform. If they fail to find the platform within 60 s, the mice are guided to it and allowed to rest on the platform for 20 s. Each mouse undergoes four training sessions per day, one in each quadrant. After completing the exploration of each quadrant, the mice are thoroughly dried and placed on a heating pad for warmth and rest, with 20 min interval between each training session. The time it takes for the mice to find the platform from the moment they enter the water (escape latency) is recorded, and if they do not find the platform within 60 s, their latency is recorded as 60 s. On the sixth day, the platform is removed from beneath the water surface for the spatial probe test, during which the mice are allowed to swim for 60 s, and the time they spend in the quadrant where the platform was originally located, as well as the number of times they cross the position where the platform was, are recorded.

Immunofluorescence

After anesthetizing the mouse via intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (0.2 ml/10 g), its limbs were secured to the experimental platform. An incision was made below the xiphoid process to open the abdomen, allowing access to the diaphragm, which was pierced to free the heart from the thoracic cavity. A perfusion needle was then inserted into the left ventricle from the apex and secured, followed by the cutting of the right atrial appendage. Warm physiological saline was perfused uniformly through the heart to clear the blood, after which 4% paraformaldehyde was perfused to euthanize the animal. The brain tissue was subsequently extracted, placed in fixative, and stored at 4 °C until sectioning. The tissue was transferred to a 30% sucrose solution for dehydration at 4 °C overnight. OCT-embedded tissue was sliced into coronal Sect. 10 μm thick. The sections, including spare samples, were stored at -80 °C. After rewarming, the sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in Tris-EDTA buffer using a pressure cooker, following a PBS wash. The sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum for 30 min, after which primary antibody (rabbit anti-Iba1, 1:200; Abcam, UK) was added and the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the sections were washed with PBS, and secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:1000; Abcam, UK) was applied. The sections were incubated for 50 min at room temperature in the dark and subsequently stained with DAPI.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica) was employed for imaging. ImageJ software was utilized to analyze the number of Iba1-positive cells and the fluorescence intensity of Iba1. Furthermore, we quantified the morphological changes of microglial cell body enlargement and process retraction by calculating the ratio of the microglial cell body area (cell body) to the area covered by the outermost extensions of the processes (cell size). Three standard hippocampal sections per mouse were randomly selected when quantifying Iba1 + cell number, measuring relative fluorescence intensity, and calculating cell body/cell size (%). The averages of these sections were used for statistical analysis. Data were statistically analyzed from 5 mice per group.

Quantitative PCR

Mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (0.2 ml/10 g). After performing cardiac perfusion on the mouse with saline, the brain tissue was extracted. Hippocampus was isolated under a microscope using a glass splitter needle. Total RNA was extracted via the TRIzol method. The concentration was determined by an ultramicro spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000). The reaction mixture was prepared with Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Reagent Premix (Accurate Biotechnology, China) under reaction conditions of 37 ℃, 15 min, 85 ℃, 5 s, and 4 ℃. Real‒time PCR was performed using a SYBR Green® Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, China). Amplification was performed on a Roche 480 instrument with the following cycle parameters: 95 ℃ for 30 s, then 45 cycles at 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. The primer sequences used were as follows:

β-actin forward: 5’-GTGACGTTGACATCCGTAAAGA-3′;

β-actin reverse: 5’-GCCGGACTCATCGTACTCC-3′;

TNF-α forward: 5’-ACTCCAGGCGGGTGCCTATGT-3′;

TNF-α reverse: 5’-GTGAGGGTCTGGGCCATAGAA-3′;

IL-6 forward: 5’-CCACTTCACAAGTCGGAGGCTTA-3′;

IL-6 reverse: 5’-TGCAAGTGCATCATCGTTGTTC-3′;

IL-1β forward: 5’-TCCAGGATGAGGACATGAGCAC-3′;

IL-1β reverse: 5’- GAACGTCACACACCAGCAGGTTA-3′.

ELISA

Peripheral blood was collected from the strongest pulsation point of the mouse’s heart apex, followed by cardiac perfusion. The spleen and hippocampus were then extracted, weighed, cut into small pieces, and added to PBS (1 g tissue: 10 ml PBS). The homogenate was made by grinding the sample with a grinding rod and then placing it at -20 °C overnight, it was then frozen and thawed twice. Then, 5000xg centrifugation was performed at 4 °C for 5 min, after which the resulting supernatant was taken as the desired tissue lysate. Whole-blood specimens were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C overnight, after which the supernatant was collected. The corresponding steps of the Mouse TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 ELISA kits (Research Cloud Biology, China) were followed to detect the concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 in the samples.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were obtained from three or more independent experiments. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among three groups, and the Tukey’s test was used for post hoc comparisons. For differences in learning curves between groups based on escape latency in the water maze test, repeated-measures ANOVA was used. The Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used if the assumption of sphericity was not met. Behavioral experiments were conducted with 12 animals per group, while ELISA, PCR, and immunofluorescence analyses were performed with 5 animals per group. Cell number or fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (W. Rasband, NIH, USA). SPSS 25.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) were used for statistical analysis and graphing. P < 0.05 was considered the threshold for a statistically significant difference.

Results

DEX pretreatment improved cognitive dysfunction after exploratory laparotomy in aged mice

To assess the effect of DEX pretreatment on improving postoperative cognition in aged mice, we randomly divided 18-month-old aged mice into the naive group, surgery group, surgery + DEX group and surgery + vehicle group. DEX was administered at a dose of 50 µg/kg via intraperitoneal injection 30 min before surgery.The specific experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 1A. The preoperative cognitive status of the mice in each group was tested by the NOR test, and there was no difference among the 4 groups (Fig. 1B). The MWM test started on day 7 after surgery. The results show that the comparison of escape latency between the surgery group and the naive group at different time points is statistically significant (F = 8.518, P = 0.008), and there is an interaction effect between time and group (F = 11.745, P < 0.001). There was also no significant difference in the escape latency of mice in the 4 groups during the first two days of the training period, but the escape latency of mice in the surgery group on the 3rd-5th days of the training period was significantly greater than that of mice in the naive group (surgery vs. naive, 54.69 ± 4.89 vs. 49.57 ± 3.20, P = 0.03; 41.78 ± 5.61 vs. 35.43 ± 4.34, P = 0.03; 33.74 ± 2.98 vs. 26.13 ± 5.81, P = 0.005) (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, both the number of times crossing the previous platform location (surgery vs. naive, 1.25 ± 0.87 vs. 2.00 ± 1.48, P = 0.004) and the percentage of dwell time in the target quadrant (surgery vs. naive, 26.42 ± 7.66 vs.40.84 ± 6.84, P < 0.001) were significantly lower in the surgery group than in the naive group (Fig. 1E, F). The results show that the comparison of escape latency between the surgery + DEX group and the surgery + vehicle group at different time points is statistically significant (F = 7.351, P = 0.01), and there is an interaction effect between time and group (F = 14.844, P < 0.001). Escape latency on days 3–5 was significantly shorter in the surgery + DEX group than in the surgery + vehicle group (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, 50.50 ± 3.73 vs. 55.01 ± 4.01, P = 0.04; 36.36 ± 4.31 vs. 43.42 ± 5.64, P = 0.01; 27.00 ± 3.94 vs. 34.50 ± 5.54, P = 0.006) (Fig. 1C); the number of times crossing the previous platform location (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, 2.50 ± 1.31 vs. 1.08 ± 0.90, P = 0.02) and the percentage of dwell time in the target quadrant (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, 37.87 ± 9.66 vs. 25.00 ± 4.48, P < 0.001) were significantly greater in the surgery + DEX group than in the surgery + vehicle group (Fig. 1E, F). In addition, there was no significant difference in the mean swimming speed among the above four groups of mice (Fig. 1G).

DEX pretreatment attenuates the expression of proinflammatory factors after exploratory laparotomy in aged mice

To evaluate the effect of DEX pretreatment on postoperative inflammation in aged mice, the expression of inflammatory factors in hippocampus, serum and the spleen was detected after surgery. The levels of proinflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β in the hippocampus of aged mice were detected using qPCR. The mRNA expression levels of the inflammatory factors were greater in the surgery group than in the naive group (surgery vs. naive, TNF-α, 3.16 ± 0.88 vs. 1.03 ± 0.03, P = 0.004; IL-6, 8.38 ± 2.71 vs. 1.09 ± 0.16, P = 0.001; IL-1β, 6.18 ± 2.31 vs. 1.03 ± 0.03, P = 0.001). However, compared with those in the vehicle group, the mice in the surgery + DEX group showed lower levels of each of the inflammatory factors and had lower mRNA expression levels (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, TNF-α, 1.22 ± 0.46 vs. 3.04 ± 1.29, P = 0.01; IL-6, 1.13 ± 0.62 vs. 5.80 ± 3.96, P = 0.04; IL-1β, 0.91 ± 0.24 vs. 4.22 ± 2.58, P = 0.04) (Fig. 2A). The expression of proinflammatory factors, such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β, in hippocampus, serum and spleen from aged mice was also detected by ELISA; for example, the surgery group presented elevated TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β protein expression compared to that in the naive group. Similarly, the mice in the surgery + vehicle group presented elevated TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β protein expression compared to that in the surgery + DEX group (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, hippocampus: TNF-α, 604.10 ± 165.40 vs. 915.30 ± 97.64, P = 0.002; IL-6, 145.30 ± 12.11 vs. 176.50 ± 16.15, P = 0.002; IL-1β, 59.68 ± 4.29 vs. 68.73 ± 3.11, P = 0.001; serum: TNF-α, 708.00 ± 177.20 vs. 1012.00 ± 121.10, P = 0.004; IL-6, 72.03 ± 9.51 vs. 93.14 ± 11.88, P = 0.04; IL-1β, 69.67 ± 4.14 vs. 84.05 ± 2.86, P < 0.001; spleen: TNF-α, 502.30 ± 74.98 vs. 629.10 ± 63.47, P = 0.05; IL-6, 163.40 ± 19.60 vs. 199.90 ± 14.10, P = 0.003; IL-1β, 69.62 ± 12.52 vs. 90.92 ± 7.28, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2B, C, D).

DEX pretreatment ameliorates the activation of hippocampal microglia after exploratory laparotomy in aged mice

Activation of hippocampal microglia plays an important role in the initiation of the hippocampal inflammatory response and DNR. Thus, to evaluate the effect of DEX pretreatment on inhibiting microglia activation after exploratory laparotomy in aged mice, 50 µg/kg DEX was injected intraperitoneally 30 min before surgery, and the activation status of microglia was analyzed via immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3A); additionally, the activation pattern was analyzed based on the enlargement of the cell body and shortening of the branches. The surgery group of mice showed a significant increase in the number of Iba1 + cells compared with that in the naive group (surgery vs. naive, 139.0 ± 23.75 vs. 78.60 ± 10.26, P = 0.002) (Fig. 3B) and an increase in fluorescence intensity (surgery vs. naive, 1.15 ± 0.06 vs. 1.00 ± 0.01, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C); in contrast, the surgery + DEX group showed a decrease in Iba1-positive cell expression (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, 96.60 ± 11.84 vs. 136.20 ± 32.62, P = 0.05), and the fluorescence intensity was decreased (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, 0.96 ± 0.06 vs. 1.10 ± 0.02, P < 0.001). The microglial morphology of the mice in the surgery group transitioned from a highly branched lace morphology to one with shortened processes and enlarged cell bodies (morphological features of microglial activation), which was indicated by the ratio of the microglial body area to the convex envelope area (the area surrounded by the outermost points) was increase; this pattern was reversed in the surgery + DEX group compared to the surgery + vehicle group (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + vehicle, 7.20 ± 2.17 vs.12.80 ± 2.28, P = 0.002) (Fig. 3D).

The cognitive protective effect of DEX pretreatment on aged mice after exploratory laparotomy is reversed by α-BGT

The above findings showed that preoperative administration of DEX improved DNR and attenuated microglial activation and inflammatory factor expression. To test the hypothesis that DEX pretreatment acts through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, we divided aged mice into surgery, surgery + DEX, surgery + DEX + α-BGT and surgery + α-BGT groups. The α7nAChR receptor-selective antagonist α-bungarotoxin (α-BGT) was administered at a dose of 1 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection 1 h before surgery. Our results showed that there were no significant abnormalities in the cognition of the mice as detected by the NOR test before surgery (Fig. 4B). The results show that the comparison of escape latency between the surgery + DEX group and the surgery + DEX + α-BGT group at different time points is statistically significant (F = 10.751, P = 0.003), and there is an interaction effect between time and group (F = 6.200, P = 0.006). According to the postoperative water maze test, the escape latency on days 3–5 was significantly shorter in the surgery + DEX group than in the surgery + DEX + α-BGT group (surgery + DEX vs. surgery + DEX + α-BGT, 43.45 ± 7.14 vs. 50.89 ± 4.70, P = 0.03; 33.81 ± 4.06 vs. 40.20 ± 5.43, P = 0.02; 24.76 ± 5.99 vs. 32.70 ± 4.39, P = 0.007) (Fig. 4D). We found a decrease in the expression of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β in the hippocampus, serum and spleen of aged mice pretreated with DEX before surgery. DEX pretreatment attenuates the activation of hippocampal microglia after exploratory laparotomy in aged mice, which is reversed by α-BGT

This study demonstrated that DEX pretreatment inhibits the activation of hippocampal microglia through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. Discussion

Many studies have demonstrated that DEX possesses anti-inflammatory and organ-protective effects [43, 44]. However, the mechanisms underlying DNR are complex, and the preventive and therapeutic mechanisms of DEX in relation to DNR are not yet fully understood. We found that (1) after exploratory laparotomy, aged mice exhibited cognitive dysfunction and elevated expression of inflammatory factors in the hippocampus, serum and spleen; (2) the expression of these inflammatory factors could be effectively attenuated by pretreatment with DEX before surgery, which could in turn attenuate the symptoms of DNR; (3) further analysis of hippocampus showed that the above effects of DEX were associated with the inhibition of hippocampal microglial activation; and (4) the anti-inflammatory and microglial activation inhibitory effects of DEX could be partially reversed by administration of α-BGT, a selective antagonist of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway α7nAChR receptor, which attenuates the effects of DEX on ameliorating the effects of DNR. These results suggest that DEX preconditioning may improve the postoperative DNR by controlling systemic inflammatory responses and inhibiting microglial activation through the activation of cholinergic pathways.

This study focused on neurocognitive deficits that develop early after surgery involving anesthesia, excluding preoperative cognitive deficits; More precisely, this article discusses the specific subtype of PND, namely DNR, which refers to cognitive impairment occurring within 30 days post-surgery [1]. The study of DNR is highly important for determining the appropriate interventions and treatments in the clinic. To prevent pre-operative learning and memory from confounding post-operative assessment while simultaneously optimizing screening efficiency and analytical precision, we employed two behavioral paradigms: the NOR test and the MWM test. Pre-operatively, the brief and easily implemented NOR allowed rapid baseline screening of all mice. animals with pre-existing recognition-memory deficits were excluded, ensuring homogeneous cognitive starting levels across groups [45]. Because post-operative re-testing with NOR requires both novel objects and an interval of 2 to 3 weeks to eliminate familiarity effects, we switched to the highly sensitive MWM, which provides a comprehensive evaluation of spatial learning and memory [42]. MWM testing began on post-operative day 7, coinciding with the peak of cognitive impairment in our model while allowing sufficient recovery from anesthesia and incisional pain. Both NOR and MWM engage hippocampal function and effectively assess hippocampus-dependent cognitive processes. The exploratory laparotomy surgical model is widely used to study DNR in mice [46]. We chose the abdominal surgical model because abdominal surgery does not affect the locomotion of the mice and excludes behavioral disturbances due to activity restriction. The exploratory laparotomy is a highly standardized surgical procedure, and we ensured a consistent level of trauma by strictly adhering to the surgical protocol, controlling the duration of the procedure, and closely monitoring the post-operative recovery process. This meticulous approach helps to minimize uncertainty and reduce experimental errors. Cognitive dysfunction often occurs in clinically elderly patients undergoing abdominal surgery [47], and the use of this model is closer to the clinical situation.

DEX has unique sedative and anti-anxiety properties. Compared to other sedative drugs, DEX does not significantly suppress respiratory function and offers protective effects on the heart, kidneys, brain, and other organs [48, 49]. Due to its many advantages, DEX has been widely used in the perioperative period, in recent years. Many clinical studies have shown that DEX can effectively reduce the incidence of perioperative neurocognitive deficits [50, 51]. Most of the previous studies on DEX have been limited to its anti-inflammatory and organ-protective effects [52]. DEX significantly reduces manifestations of acute inflammation in various animal models, decreases the expression of inflammatory cytokines in serum and tissues, and exerts an overall anti-inflammatory effect [53]. Although there have been studies that have addressed the specific mechanisms of DEX’s role in surgical models [37], we focused on whether preoperative prophylactic administration of DEX could play a role in protecting cognitive functions. Following a comprehensive review of the literature to establish the safe dosage range, timing, and route of administration for DEX [38, 54], we conducted preliminary experiments to identify and repeatedly confirm an optimal dose that balances efficacy with safety. The regimen ultimately adopted—an intraperitoneal injection of 50 µg/kg DEX administered 30 min before surgery—robustly attenuates neuroinflammation and confers cognitive protection while minimizing potential adverse effects, thereby providing a reliable dosing protocol for subsequent mechanistic studies. In our study, pretreatment with DEX significantly improved the performance of aged mice in behavioral tests after surgery, and its anti-inflammatory effect was shown in both the central and peripheral regions by detecting the expression of inflammatory factors. We further found that DEX pretreatment could reduce the activation of hippocampal microglia, as shown by immunofluorescence staining. The use of DEX in current clinical practice mainly involves continuous infusion of DEX during surgery, and it is worthwhile to investigate whether other dosing strategies may provide better results. In our study, we demonstrated that preoperative DEX administration still has anti-inflammatory and cognitive improvement effects by pre-treatment of aged mice with DEX. Our findings provide a preoperative prophylactic dosing strategy.

Extensive research has indicated that anesthesia and surgery can lead to an increase in the levels of inflammatory mediators in the body, which may adversely affect the function of neurons and synapses, ultimately resulting in cognitive impairment [55–57]. Specifically, these inflammatory factors, after entering the CNS via pathways such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB), blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCB), and circumventricular organs (CVOs), may impact the proliferation and differentiation of neurons as well as synaptic function connections, thereby leading to a range of neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as perceptual disorders, consciousness disturbances, and memory impairments [58–60]. To investigate this phenomenon, we chose to measure the expression of inflammatory cytokines six hours after laparotomy in elderly mice, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. The selection of this time point is based on our preliminary experimental findings that the expression of inflammatory cytokines is most pronounced six hours after surgery in elderly mice. Our experimental results show that six hours after laparotomy in elderly mice, the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in serum and spleen significantly increased. Additionally, a similar increase in the expression of inflammatory factors was observed in the hippocampus. These results suggest that the inflammatory response caused by surgery and anesthesia involves not only the peripheral immune system but also the CNS. Our experimental findings confirm the impact of inflammatory factors on the CNS during the process of anesthesia and surgery, providing an important basis for further research on the effects of anesthesia and surgery on cognitive function.

Studies have shown that microglia play a key role in the development of hippocampal neuroinflammation following sterile peripheral injury [13]. After peripheral inflammation and blood-brain barrier disruption caused by anesthesia and surgery, microglia are more likely to exhibit an “activated” state [27, 29], characterized by an enlarged cell body, shortened processes, and increased phagocytosis and cytokine production. However, the state of microglia is far more complex than this. They can exhibit a wide range of phenotypes and functions depending on the needs of the CNS and external stimuli [61, 62]. Acute activation of microglia typically promotes tissue repair by clearing invading pathogens and cellular debris, whereas prolonged microglial activation exacerbates chronic neuroinflammation, aggravating damage and promoting disease progression [62]. Therefore, microglia are particularly important in regulating hippocampal neuroinflammation. Our study found that microglia in the hippocampus of aged mice were activated following exploratory laparotomy, with the activated microglia showing enlarged cytoplasmic areas and shortened processes. However, pretreatment with DEX significantly inhibited this microglial activation. These results suggest that DEX pretreatment may improve DNR by suppressing microglial activation.

The cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway is a neuroimmune regulatory mechanism known for its rapid onset and direct action. Activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway is dependent on the integrity of the vagus nerve, acetylcholine and α7nAChR. When the body is stimulated by trauma, the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway is activated, which protects the body from the associated secondary injuries caused by this inflammatory response [22]. It has been shown that electrical stimulation of the vagus nerve does not reduce TNF-α levels in the serum of α7nAChR knockout mice, suggesting that α7nAChR is an important component of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway in vivo [18]. Research has shown that α7nAChR is expressed in a variety of cell types, and beyond its role in microglia, it is also found in astrocytes, neurons, and immune cells such as macrophages, T cells, and B cells [63]. The function of α7nAChR in these cells may indirectly influence the inflammatory responses of microglia and even impact the broader neuroimmune environment. For instance, the activation of α7nAChR in neurons and astrocytes may affect the immune responses of microglia and further regulate neuroinflammation through neuron-glia interactions [64]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that the activation of α7nAChR in macrophages can suppress the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, while enhancing anti-inflammatory responses. The widespread expression of α7nAChR in immune cells may also play a significant role in modulating neuroimmune reactions [65]. DEX inhibits sympathetic nerve by activating α2 adrenergic receptors in the medulla, which in turn stimulates the vagus nerve [66]. Its imidazolium ring structure allows it to activate imidazoline receptors as well [67], indirectly enhancing the anti-inflammatory effect of the vagus nerve through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway [37, 68]. We focused on whether DEX can exert its anti-inflammatory effects through the activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway by α7nAChR. The dose of α-BGT was set at 1 mg/kg, administered intraperitoneally, based on a systematic review of the literature and the product insert [39]. To align with our experimental design, α-BGT was given 30 min before dexmedetomidine administration. Subsequent analyses confirmed that this regimen selectively antagonizes the target receptor without inducing nonspecific toxicity, demonstrating both efficacy and safety under our experimental conditions. We found that the above effects of DEX were reversed when the α7nAChR antagonist α-BGT was administered to aged mice prior to pretreatment with DEX. These findings suggested that DEX pretreatment acts precisely through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. Our findings reaffirmed the involvement of DEX in the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway of the preventive and therapeutic effects of DNR through a vagomimetic action [37]. And on this basis, we found the correlation between the activation of cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway and the inhibition of microglia activation by establishing a surgical model of aged mice, this preclinical study lays the basis for the use of DEX as a drug candidate to prevent the development of DNR.

Our study has several limitations. First, in our current experiments, we verified the anti-inflammatory and cognitive-enhancing effects of DEX by using a surgical model of aged mice pretreated with DEX. We also observed its inhibitory effect on microglial activation. However, direct evidence for this mechanism is lacking. Future studies will further explore the mechanisms related to microglia. Additionally, we did not measure acetylcholinesterase (AChE) or acetylcholine (ACh) levels, which could provide further insights into the role of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway in the observed effects of DEX. Future studies should incorporate these measurements to better elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Second, our choice of inhibitor may have been somewhat limited, as we did not use the more typical α7nAChR-specific antagonist, methyllycaconitine. In the future, comparing the effects of both inhibitors on the α7nAChR could provide further insights.Third, we focused only on the role of DEX in improving cognitive dysfunction during the early postoperative period and did not assess cognitive function one month or longer after surgery. We believe that early intervention in patients at risk for cognitive impairment is more effective in reducing perioperative neurocognitive deficits.

In conclusion, the present results suggest that DEX pretreatment reduces the expression of inflammatory factors after exploratory laparotomy in aged mice and is involved in inhibits the activation of hippocampal microglia, thereby ameliorating delayed recovery of neurocognition in the postoperative period. Additionally, the mechanism of action involves activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. Therefore, DEX could be chosen perioperatively as an intervention and therapeutic strategy for DNR.
