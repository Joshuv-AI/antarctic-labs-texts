Sexual Health Problems and Service Needs of Menopausal Women: A Mixed Method Study in Osun State Nigeria
Authors: Ademidun Oluwatoyin Adedeji, Aanuoluwapo Omobolanle Olajubu, Mercy Kikelomo Aladegboye, Adekemi Eunice Olowokere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to describe the sexual health problems of menopausal women and the sexual health services required to promote their well-being and provide recommendations that could help improve sexual health during menopause.MethodsA concurrent mixed-method design was used. Data were collected from menopausal women aged 40–65 years using a structured questionnaire (n = 420) and an in-depth interview guide (n = 20). Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, whereas qualitative data were subjected to content and thematic analysis.ResultsThe mean ages at menopause among the quantitative and qualitative participants were 47 ± 4.22 and 45 ± 3.38 years, respectively. Quantitative and qualitative findings indicated that participants most commonly experienced vaginal dryness, reduced sexual desire, urinary tract infections, sexually transmitted infections, and pain during intercourse. Across both data strands, women also expressed similar needs, which included access to accurate and helpful information to navigate the menopausal period, improved sexual satisfaction, and the availability of contraceptive services to support safe sexual practices during this period.ConclusionsThe study provides insight into the sexual problems and needs of menopausal women, which may guide healthcare providers and policymakers in developing appropriate strategies aimed at enhancing the sexual health of this demographic.Keywords: Health services needs and demand, Menopause, Nigeria, Postmenopause, Sexual health

INTRODUCTION

Menopause is a normal physiological change in a woman’s midlife and a natural part of the aging process [1]. However, this transition is often accompanied by a range of physical, emotional, and health challenges. As women approach and go through menopause, hormonal changes can significantly impact their overall well-being, particularly their sexual health and intimate relationships [2].

Sexual dysfunction is a common issue among menopausal women, with frequently reported symptoms such as vaginal dryness, low sexual desire, arousal problems, difficulties achieving orgasm, and pain during sexual activity (dyspareunia) [2,3,4]. Globally, the prevalence of female sexual dysfunction is high [3,4], and in Nigeria, this burden is compounded by unique contextual challenges such as socio-cultural norms that discourage open discussions about sexuality in older age, limited health literacy, and poor access to specialized healthcare services for menopausal women.

Despite the prevalence of sexual health problems among menopausal women, these issues are frequently underreported or ignored due to stigma and societal silence around women’s sexuality in the post-reproductive years. Many affected women feel uncomfortable, embarrassed, or ashamed to seek help, further limiting their access to appropriate care [5,6]. In addition, existing reproductive health services in Nigeria tend to prioritize younger women of reproductive age, leaving a significant gap in care for women in midlife and beyond.

Most studies have used quantitative methods, which often overlook the in-depth personal experiences and feelings of women undergoing menopausal transition. There is a scarcity of data that examined the sexual health dimension of menopause or identified the specific sexual health needs of Nigerian women using a mixed-method approach. This gap may limit the implementation of culturally-responsive intervention and support strategies tailored to the sexual health needs of menopausal women. Addressing the sexual health problems and service needs of menopausal women in Nigeria is crucial, not only for improving their sexual well-being but also for promoting gender-sensitive and appropriate healthcare services for this group of vulnerable population.

By investing in sexual health services for menopausal women, Nigeria can make meaningful progress toward achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3, which emphasizes ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all at all ages [7]. This study, therefore, aims to assess the sexual health problems and service needs of post-reproductive women in Nigeria, with the ultimate goal of informing programs and policies that improve their sexual health and overall well-being.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This is a community-based study which utilized a concurrent mixed-method design to assess women’s sexual health problems and their service needs. Both qualitative and quantitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and then integrated during interpretation to allow for comparison, corroboration, and expansion of findings [8]. This method is used to confirm, cross-validate or corroborate findings. It is often used to overcome a weakness in one method with the strengths of another, and it provides a more comprehensive understanding of the complex phenomenon of menopausal sexual health.

Setting

The study was conducted in Ilesa West Local Government Area (IWLGA) of Osun State, Nigeria. Ilesa West Local Government is one of the 30 local governments in Osun State, with its headquarters located in Ereja Square, Ilesa town. Ilesa West covers a total area of 63 km2 and has a density of 1,643 km2, with a female population of 53,824 out of a total population of 103,555 [9]. The local government consists of multi-ethnic nationalities, but is predominantly dominated by the Yorubas and other sub-ethnic groups (Igbos and Hausas). The inhabitants are mostly traders, civil servants, students, etc.

Target population

These are menopausal women aged 40–65 years in IWLGA of Osun State.

Sampling and sampling technique

A multistage sampling technique was used to select the menopausal women (40–65 years) who participated in the study. Eight communities, namely Isokun, Araromi, Omofe, Oke-Ese, Orinkiran, Oke-Iyin, Adeti, and Ilaje, were selected from the 23 communities in the Local Government Area. The women were selected at the household level. Households within these communities were selected using systematic sampling, and eligible women who consented were recruited. A total of 420 menopausal women participated in the quantitative study, while 20 menopausal women were recruited for the in-depth interview (IDI). The sample size for the qualitative study was determined by data saturation, which was reached when no new information emerged during successive interviews.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: women aged 40–65 years who had stopped menstruating for at least one year, women who were residents of the selected communities and those who provided informed consent. Women with debilitating illnesses (e.g., cancer, renal disease, poorly controlled diabetes with complications, or mental illness) were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data were collected with the use of a structured questionnaire and one-on-one IDI.

Quantitative instrument

The questionnaire has four sections. Section A elicits information on socio-demographic characteristics and sexual & reproductive history; Section B contains 12-item questions that identify the common sexual health problems experienced by menopausal women. The items have options of either “Yes” or “No.” Section C contains 17-item questions on sexual health service needs, which are measured on a 3-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all,” “to some extent,” and “to a large extent.”

Qualitative instrument

An IDI guide was used to collect data on sexual health problems and service needs of respondents. The instrument was translated into Yoruba and back-translated into English to ensure accuracy and also to make it easy for the respondents to read and understand.

Validity and reliability of quantitative instrument

The instrument was prepared by the researchers with a theoretical base from the literature. The validity of the instruments was determined by face validity criteria. The face validity was ensured by giving the instrument to experts in sexual and reproductive health for review in order to assess its relevance to the subject matter, scope, and coverage. The reliability of the questionnaire was established by the test-retest method and internal consistency. The correlation coefficient for sexual health problems (past and present) was 0.78 and 0.81, respectively, while that of the service needs was 0.90, indicating high reliability.

Rigor of qualitative instrument

The rigor of the data collection was ensured through the criteria of credibility, transferability, confirmability, and dependability [10]. Participants were selected with maximum variation, sufficient time was assigned for data collection, and interviews were conducted at different times and locations. Credibility was achieved through prolonged engagement with the data, an external check on the research process, data coding simultaneously with data collection, and triangulation with quantitative findings. Dependability and confirmability were ensured by documenting the research process clearly, using the same IDI guide for all interviews, audio-taping and transcribing all interviews verbatim and by comparing the codes, categories, and themes. Transferability was enhanced through description of the participants, data collection procedure, analysis, and quotes from the participants.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Health Research Ethical Committee of Institute of Public Health, Obafemi Awolowo University with the HREC number IPHOAU/12/1404. A letter of permission to collect data was also obtained from the appropriate authorities. Individual informed verbal and written consents were obtained after a brief explanation of the purpose and benefits of the study to each participant before the data collection. Privacy and confidentiality were strictly protected by ensuring that no personal identifiers were included in the questionnaire in order to get their full cooperation. Voluntary participation was ensured, and the right to take part or terminate at any time was emphasized.

Data analysis

The data was prepared and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Co.). Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequency table, mean, and charts). Qualitative data were analyzed using content and thematic analyses.

RESULTS

Socio-demographic, sexual & reproductive history of respondents

The mean age of the respondents in the quantitative study was 54.74 ± 5.49 years and about 51.2% are within the age range of 50–59 years. The highest proportion of the respondents had partners (82.4%), and majority (91.4%) were Yorubas. The mean duration of menopause among the respondents was 9 ± 5.14 years. In addition, 65.2% of the respondents had information on menopause, and more than one-third (39.3%) of them received their information from health care personnel. Preponderance of the respondents (97.9%) had their menopause occur naturally, and 64.8% of the respondents were sexually active at the time of study (Table 1). Findings of the IDI corroborate the socio-demographic characteristics of respondents in the quantitative study. The mean age of the participants for the IDI was 53.40 ± 4.13 years, and the mean age at menopause was 45 ± 3.38 (Table 2).

Sexual health problems experienced by menopausal women

Results show that vaginal dryness, urinary tract infection, and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in this order were the most commonly experienced past sexual health problems among the respondents. Also, vaginal dryness and loss of interest in sex were the leading problems that respondents were currently experiencing, followed by weak or absent orgasm. Findings showed that vaginal dryness has been a consistent problem for women (Fig. 1). Overall, 31.2% of the respondents experienced sexual problems in the past, while 30.0% are currently experiencing sexual problems. Qualitative findings revealed two major themes related to women’s experiences of sexual health problems: “body speaking in painful whispers” and “caged desires in the silence of widowhood” (Table 3). The first theme reflects women’s experiences of physical distress and discomfort during sexual activity in menopause. Participants recounted symptoms such as vaginal discharge, bleeding, and pain during intercourse that not only disrupted their sexual lives but also strained relationships with their partners. For many, the body became a silent messenger of unspoken struggles, expressing through pain that could not be easily verbalized. These experiences highlight the intersection between physiological changes, emotional distress, and the social silence surrounding sexual health in midlife. The theme highlights how the menopausal body, through its painful whispers, calls for attention, understanding, and compassionate healthcare support. Qualitative analysis generated one major theme from the participants’ description of their sexual health service needs: “Lighting the path to sexual well-being during menopause” (Table 4). The theme captures women’s expressed desire for enlightenment, guidance, and empowerment regarding their sexual health during menopause. Participants revealed that this stage of life is often characterized by misconceptions and inadequate access to accurate sexual health information. Many women acknowledged experiencing sexual challenges but lacked awareness of available services or trusted sources of help. Consequently, they often resorted to informal advice from friends or endured the problems in silence. Some excerpts from participants are stated below;

“Health workers should create awareness about sexual health in menopause and how women can overcome their problems. If I’m aware of the services, then I will visit the hospital” (P13, 52-year-old woman).

“My most important need is information on how to have good sexual health in relation to enjoying sex during menopause” (P5, 54-year-old woman).

“I need sexual information and treatment for my sexual problem. The time I was experiencing the problem, if I knew any available service, I would have preferred to visit the hospital instead of seeking advice from my friends” (P6, 55-year-old woman).

“I need a contraceptive like female condom, to prevent the occurrence of infection from sperm depositing in my womb” (P15, 54-year-old woman).

Both quantitative and qualitative datasets converge to show that knowledge gaps and a lack of accessible services formed the basis of their needs (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to describe the sexual health problems of menopausal women and their service needs. The results indicated various physical and emotional sexual problems, with vaginal dryness, painful intercourse, decreased libido, and weak orgasms being the most frequently mentioned concerns. These results align with earlier researches by Heidari et al. [11] and Monteleone et al. [12] in sub-Saharan Africa and various global contexts, which also recognize vaginal dryness and discomfort during sexual intercourse as key indicators of menopausal sexual dysfunction. Cucinella et al. [13] pointed out that menopausal transitions often encompass intricate biopsychosocial changes that influence women’s sexual function, thereby reinforcing the patterns identified in this study. The quantitative results showed that approximately a third of the participants indicated past and current health issues, demonstrating relatively low rates of vaginal dryness, loss of arousal, and dyspareunia. However, the qualitative narratives deepen understanding by showing how symptoms disrupt intimacy, reduce sexual desire, and create tension in relationships. Both results converge to show that vaginal dryness and painful intercourse are the core drivers of menopausal sexual problems. The physical symptoms serve as both physiological barriers and emotional triggers, explaining why a significant proportion of women report ongoing sexual challenges.

The prevalence of sexual problems among the respondents is lower than the findings of a previous study, where about two-thirds of their respondents experienced problems in their sexual relationships [14,15]. The low experience of sexual health problems in this study supports the opinion of Mernone et al. [16] that not all women experience sexual decline during menopausal transition. This may be because the majority of respondents had their menopause occur naturally, which is typically associated with a milder decline in sexual function as reported in a previous study [17] that sexual changes are generally more gradual and less severe in women with naturally occurring menopause than in women with surgically induced menopause.

During menopause, changes occur in the intensity and duration of stimulation needed for arousal and orgasm, which makes inadequate lubrication, vaginal dryness, and pain very common, as was found in this study. These physiological manifestations are well-documented in the literature, where estrogen decline and genitourinary syndrome of menopause have been identified as central mechanisms underlying vaginal dryness and dyspareunia [18]. The most reported problems among the women who participated in the IDI were vaginal dryness, loss of sexual arousal, and dyspareunia. The proportion of women experiencing sexual problems in this study is low compared to the report from the study that reported a higher prevalence of vaginal dryness and pain during intercourse [19], which may be attributed to majority of the respondents having naturally occurring menopause as discussed above.

It is essential to recognize that the comparatively low occurrence of vaginal dryness and dyspareunia observed in the quantitative phase may indicate the effects of social desirability bias and underreporting in this delicate area of women’s health. Sexual issues are frequently viewed as personal topics for midlife and older women, especially in the Yoruba cultural setting, where modesty, silence, and emotional control are anticipated [20]. This agrees with the results from Agunbiade and Gilbert [21], who also observed that socio-cultural norms and the lack of discussion regarding sexuality greatly affect how older Nigerian women reveal or cope with sexual issues.

As a result, some participants might have felt uneasy sharing personal symptoms in a structured questionnaire for fear of judgment, embarrassment, or perceived impropriety. This reluctance to self-report may also be reinforced by cultural expectations that women tolerate or normalize menopausal sexual challenges as part of aging. In contrast, the qualitative interviews conducted in a confidential and empathetic setting created a safe environment that enabled participants to articulate their experiences more openly, which explains the richer narratives of persistent vaginal dryness, pain during intercourse, and diminished sexual satisfaction. These discrepancies highlight the methodological value of the mixed-methods approach used in this study, demonstrating how qualitative data illuminate concealed experiences that structured quantitative tools may fail to capture, especially in contexts where stigma and social desirability pressures shape disclosure patterns.

However, among those that indicated that they had experienced sexual health problems in the past, the most frequently reported problems experienced were STIs, urinary tract infections, and loss of interest in sexual activities, while at present, the most frequent problems experienced are loss of interest in sexual activities, loss of sexual arousal, pain with sexual intercourse, and loss of response. This correlates with a previous report that the most common sexual problems in midlife women are decreased sexual desire or loss of libido [22,23]. The findings from the IDI confirmed this as the women reported that they still engage in sexual intercourse, but they experience pain, burning sensations, and dryness while engaging in sexual intercourse, which had been earlier reported among menopausal women [24]. These sexual problems are often associated with a decrease in sexual frequency and pleasure derived from sexual activity, which in turn creates tension and distress for the women [25].

The narratives from the IDI highlight a complex interaction between personal experiences, coping strategies and access to healthcare services among respondents experiencing sexual problems. The finding that some women stopped engaging in sexual intercourse because of the problems they experienced aligns with previous studies showing that sexual dysfunction during menopause can lead to avoidance of sexual intimacy, which can impact marital satisfaction and sexual quality of life [2,13]. The deep inner struggle of widowed women who feel sexual desire but are constrained by cultural, familial, or moral expectations is worthy of note. In many cultural contexts, widowhood is often accompanied by implicit expectations of sexual abstinence, leading to feelings of loneliness, frustration, and internalized guilt [26]. The sexual silence imposed by bereavement and cultural disapproval is further reinforced by restrictive widowhood norms that confine women’s autonomy [27]. This highlights the importance of empathetic and culturally informed sexual health counselling and support for widowed women navigating this hidden aspect of menopausal and postmenopausal life.

Majority of the respondents took no action to solve their problems, which reflects a lack of awareness and a potential socio-cultural barrier which may limit their health-seeking behavior. This pattern has also been reported in literature where sexual health is often considered a private matter and open discussions are stigmatized, especially among older women [28]. The tendency to rely on friends rather than professional healthcare services mirrors previous findings on informal health-seeking behaviors, where social networks often serve as the initial source of advice, of which their guidance may vary in accuracy and effectiveness [29]. The limited proportion of women visiting healthcare facilities and claims of ignorance about available sexual health services underscore the need for targeted health education and service delivery that specifically address sexual well-being in midlife and older women. Studies have shown that when women are informed about available services, they are more likely to seek medical help [30]. Interestingly, the proportion of women currently experiencing sexual problems was lower than those who had experienced them in the past. This reduction could be linked to adaptive coping mechanisms developed over time. Furthermore, experience with sexual dysfunction might prompt proactive information-seeking and behavioral adjustments, thereby reducing the frequency or severity of symptoms.

These findings should also be interpreted within Nigeria’s cultural and health-system contexts. Studies conducted in East and Southern Africa frequently reported higher rates of vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and decreased libido, possibly reflecting environments in which open disclosure is more culturally acceptable or where women feel more empowered to articulate their symptoms [31,32]. Globally, evidence from Western and Middle Eastern countries similarly documents high levels of sexual discomfort driven by estrogen decline, but the extent of help-seeking varies widely depending on healthcare accessibility and social openness [33,34]. The similarities between this study and global findings, particularly regarding decreased desire and pain, suggest that physiological changes are universal, while cultural norms strongly shape how symptoms are reported and discussed.

The sexual health and overall well-being of menopausal women may be compromised when their sexual health service needs are overlooked. In this study, a substantial proportion of women expressed moderate to high levels of sexual health service needs, suggesting that menopause does not diminish the importance of sexual health but rather transforms it into an area requiring focused support.

Although literature on the sexual health service needs of menopausal women in Nigeria and Sub-Saharan Africa is relatively scarce, available evidence points in the same direction. A study conducted in Southwest Nigeria reported that many postmenopausal women expressed unmet needs related to sexual counseling, intimacy, and information on safe sexual practices, yet cultural taboos often restricted them from seeking care [35]. Similarly, a qualitative study showed that women frequently desired more communication with healthcare providers about sexual difficulties during menopause, but these discussions were often absent in routine care [36].

Globally, studies have underscored the persistence of sexual health concerns among menopausal women. Alotaibi et al. [37] reported that women in midlife often seek professional advice on intimacy, sexual enjoyment, and relationship satisfaction, but their needs remain under-recognized in health systems. Hutchings et al. [38] also found that menopausal symptoms such as vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and reduced libido were strongly associated with declines in quality of life, and that appropriate counseling and interventions could mitigate these effects. Importantly, these findings align with WHO’s call for a life-course approach to women’s health, which emphasizes integrating menopause care, including sexual health, into primary healthcare services [39].

This study revealed that women’s expressed needs encompassed a broad range of issues, including information, communication, and education services about sexual health and well-being, counseling on contraception, guidance on sexual engagement and intimacy, and support for enhancing sexual enjoyment. This resonates with international literature that frames sexual health not only as the absence of dysfunction but also as a pursuit of intimacy, pleasure, and relational satisfaction [40]. Taken together, these findings emphasize that menopausal women require comprehensive, person-centered services that address both medical and psychosocial dimensions of sexual health.

Failure to address these needs risks perpetuating silence, stigma, and diminished well-being among aging women. Healthcare providers should therefore normalize conversations about sexuality during menopause, create safe spaces for disclosure, and incorporate sexual health counseling into routine midlife care. Such approaches would not only improve the well-being of menopausal women but also align with broader public health goals of promoting healthy aging for women in Africa and beyond.

The IDI findings revealed that women expressed a strong need for sexual health promotion services, including awareness creation, advice on sexuality, sexual education, treatment for sexual health problems, and access to contraceptive services. These needs are consistent with previous studies, which have highlighted that menopausal and midlife women continue to require sexual health support, particularly in areas of counseling, education, and preventive care [41].

A notable finding from this study is the demand for contraceptive services among menopausal women. While fertility is significantly reduced at this stage, the request may reflect two main concerns: ongoing sexual activity and the risk of STIs. In similar African contexts, women have reported fear of infection due to partners’ extramarital affairs, with promiscuity of husbands being a recurring theme [42]. This highlights the intersection of women’s sexual health with gender dynamics, marital relationships, and cultural silence surrounding male infidelity.

Thus, the findings reinforce the need for a comprehensive sexual health approach that goes beyond reproductive concerns to include preventive services, safe sex counseling, and management of sexual health problems among aging women. Addressing these needs would not only improve quality of life but also align with the life-course model of sexual and reproductive health by the World Health Organization, which recognizes that sexual well-being is essential at all stages of life [43].

Implication for nursing

Maintaining the sexual health of women during menopause should be one of the concerns of health professionals specialized in women’s health promotion. This is important as women navigate the physical and emotional challenges associated with menopause. Studies have shown the benefits of sexual satisfaction on emotional well-being and quality of life [44], a higher sense of purpose in life, interpersonal relations and marital happiness [45]. Nurses can play the role of advocate between the policy makers and menopausal women for making sexual health services available in the three tiers of health facilities, especially in the first level of care. It is essential for healthcare workers to acknowledge the sexual health needs of menopausal women and to develop targeted programmes that respond to these needs.

This study is also significant for policymakers to make decisions that can influence the availability and delivery of quality sexual health services for menopausal women. This will serve as a basis for structuring sexual and reproductive health programmes to include, sexual health promotional activities which can address the sexual health problems and service needs of women in the post-reproductive age.

Strengths and limitations

This study provided in-depth information about sexual health problems and service needs of menopausal women using a mixed methods approach. Some participants initially expressed reluctance to share sexual health information due to the sensitivity of the topic and the prevailing culture of silence in the study setting. However, the researcher emphasized confidentiality and clarified that the information would be used solely for research purposes. Data collection was conducted in private settings to encourage openness and ensure accuracy. However, the findings may not be generalizable to all menopausal women in Osun State. This is constrained by the research’s emphasis on a specific geographic region, IWLGA, which is mainly populated by Yoruba women. The cultural beliefs, communication styles, and social norms of this group may differ from those in other ethnic or regional contexts in Nigeria. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported retrospective data introduces the possibility of recall bias, particularly when participants reflect on past sexual problems, which may lead to underestimation or reinterpretation over time. Also, the absence of content validity index assessment is a limitation, which could have enhanced the rigor of the instrument.

Conclusion

A notable proportion of the women experienced sexual health problems, and they also have sexual health service needs. Menopausal women have the right to be sexually healthy as they desire. Efforts should be made by health care providers to improve menopausal women’s access to sexual health information and services to have a fulfilling sexual life. Therefore, all relevant stakeholders, most especially community health nurses and the government should strategize to make adequate health service delivery available at the first level of care to address the sexual health problems of menopausal women.
