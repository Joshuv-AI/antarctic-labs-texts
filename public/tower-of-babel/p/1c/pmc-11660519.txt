3D surface defect map for assessing buccolingual profile of single tooth gaps following alveolar ridge preservation
Authors: Leonardo Mancini, Shayan Barootchi, Miha Pirc, Enrico Marchetti, Ronald E Jung, Lorenzo Tavelli, Daniel S Thoma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimA new, non‐invasive approach suggests using single intraoral optical scanning to analyze the ridge profile of single‐tooth gaps following alveolar ridge preservation in the absence of a baseline scan. This method involves creating a three‐dimensional (3D) surface map to identify and assess contour changes and ridge profiles based on the adjacent teeth.Materials and MethodsThe present study was designed as a cross‐sectional pilot analysis on a convenience sample of patients undergoing alveolar ridge preservation. Intraoral optical scans were taken on 23 patients, capturing data from 30 edentulous sites. The digital models were then imported into an image analysis software for a 3D surface defect map analysis performed by one examiner. This analysis characterized the buccolingual profile of the single tooth gap relative to the adjacent teeth. 10 linear divergence points, spaced 0.5 mm apart in a corona‐apical direction, were identified at the midfacial aspect of the sites. Based on these points the sites were plotted and grouped in three different buccolingual profiles (linear, concave, and convex). Clinical parameters including Keratinized mucosa Width (KMW), and soft tissue phenotype with Colorvue biotype probes were also recorded.ResultsThree different buccolingual patterns (linear, convex, and concave) were identified. Seven sites exhibited a linear profile, 10 sites displayed a concave shape, and 13 showed a convex profile. The linear profile had surface discrepancies similar to the neighboring teeth. In contrast, the convex profile revealed mid‐buccal discrepancy localized only at the crestal aspect, while the concave had an extended divergence ranging from 1 to 5 mm below the soft tissue margin. Univariate and multiple logistic regression analyses did not reveal any statistically significant variables influencing profilometric analysis; however, when combining phenotype and KMW, thick phenotypes demonstrated a higher proportion of concavity (OR = 4.83) compared to thin ones, suggesting a significant trend. With every 1 mm of increase in KMW, the probability of showing a concavity decreased (p = 0.057).ConclusionA 3D surface defect map represents a useful tool for objectively quantifying ridge defects and profiles by assessing profilometric and surface differences compared to adjacent dentition using a single intraoral scan. This method also indicates that KMW may play a critical role in preventing concavity defects. The 3D defect map can guide decision‐making during soft tissue augmentation procedures by emphasizing the specific location of the defect and providing more detailed insights into its localization. These parameters can enable the tailoring of flap management and soft tissue grafting strategies to address the patient's individual needs.Keywords: dental implant, digital data, optical scanning, pilot study, STL file

INTRODUCTION

The process of tooth extraction triggers a series of changes in the oral tissues, affecting both the hard and soft tissue structure. 1 One of the most significant outcomes is the inevitable loss of alveolar bone and soft tissue volume at the extraction site. 2 , 3 If these changes are not addressed, they can create substantial difficulties for subsequent dental implant placement and impact the overall esthetic and functional results of restorative dentistry. 4 , 5

After tooth extraction, socket resorption occurs because of remodeling and resorption in the surrounding alveolar bone. 2 , 6 This typically leads to a decrease in both horizontal and vertical dimensions of the alveolar ridge, resulting in insufficient bone volume and altered soft tissue contours. 7 , 8 The reduction in bone and soft tissue volume can exacerbate aesthetic concerns further, making prosthetic rehabilitation more complex when dealing with edentulous space. A recent systematic review showed that the magnitude of bone resorption is around 3.79 ± 0.23 mm on the horizontal aspect while for the vertical component, a reduction of 1.24 ± 0.11 mm could be expected at 6 months after extraction, whereas when focusing on the soft tissue changes there is an interesting gain of soft tissue thickness that is estimated between the 0.4 and 0.5 mm. 9

Soft tissue remodeling plays a critical role in post‐extraction healing by significantly contributing to stability and appearance within peri‐implant tissues. 10 , 11 However, spontaneous healing (SH) following tooth extraction is often unpredictable which may cause irregularities or inadequate tissue volume leading to challenges for implant placement that could compromise long‐term esthetic integration and treatment. 12 , 13 According to a recent randomized clinical trial, the use of alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) showed significantly higher soft tissue thickness 3.00 mm and fewer invaginations of the soft tissues compared to SH 1.5 mm. 8 However, when focusing on the implant placement time point in both cases further soft tissue augmentation (STA) or guided bone regeneration (GBR) was required to improve and facilitate the prosthetic component. 12

STA techniques have become crucial in contemporary implant dentistry to address the challenges of post‐extraction alveolar ridge resorption and soft tissue remodeling. 14 , 15 , 16 These techniques aim to restore and enhance the volume, contour, and quality of peri‐implant tissues, creating an optimal environment for successful implant placement and prosthetic rehabilitation. 17 , 18 , 19 By addressing volume deficiencies and optimizing soft tissue contours, these procedures facilitate the achievement of natural‐looking aesthetic outcomes.

Several methods evaluate buccal bone thickness, bone gain, and keratinized tissue thickness objectively. 9 , 20 , 21 However, the description of the edentulous site and profilometric analysis of the soft tissue in that region have not yet been discussed in the literature. With the introduction of minimally invasive concepts today, there is increasing interest in using alternative methods for addressing volume discrepancies, implant positions, and soft tissue thickness. 22 , 23

The use of 3D analysis has revolutionized various areas of dentistry, especially in assessing and treating changes in the alveolar bone and soft tissue after tooth extraction. 6 , 14 , 24 , 25 Traditional methods for evaluating post‐extraction volume deficiency usually rely on two‐dimensional radiographic imaging, which may provide limited information and miss the intricacies of three‐dimensional changes. 6 , 21 , 24 In contrast, 3D analysis techniques offer enhanced accuracy, precision, and comprehensive evaluation of alveolar bone and soft tissue morphology.

Cone beam computed tomography imaging is commonly used for 3D analysis in post‐extraction volume deficiency assessment. 26 , 27 , 28 It provides high‐resolution, detailed images of the alveolar ridge, facilitating bone morphology, density, and volume evaluation. By delineating extraction sockets and surrounding bone boundaries precisely, CBCT enables accurate measurement of volume loss and identification of potential areas for bone resorption or remodeling. 26 Precise mapping of the dimensions and contours of the extraction site using 3D analysis empowers clinicians to design customized treatment strategies tailored to each patient's anatomical characteristics. However, increased invasiveness and costs need to be considered when performing this type of analysis. 29 , 30

In addition to evaluating bone volume deficiency, 3D analysis can also assess soft tissue contours and dynamics after tooth extraction. 15 , 20 , 23 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Surface scanning technologies such as intraoral scanners and digital impression systems capture detailed three‐dimensional images of the soft tissue architecture. These images allow for precise tailoring of STA techniques to complement bone grafting procedures and optimize the esthetic integration of dental implants within the surrounding oral environment. 35 Moreover, sequential 3D analysis enables clinicians to longitudinally monitor post‐treatment changes, evaluate treatment outcomes, and refine therapeutic approaches as needed while maintaining a minimally invasive concept. 36 , 37 , 38

This cross‐sectional pilot study employed a three‐dimensional surface mapping technique to identify and evaluate ridge variations and profilometric patterns, which were subsequently correlated with relevant clinical characteristics, such as tissue phenotype and keratinized mucosa width (KMW). This approach may offer insights into the potential profilometric discrepancies that can arise following ARP procedures, utilizing a novel 3D analytical tool and a single intraoral scan without the need for superimposition methods.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

The present study was designed as a cross‐sectional pilot analysis on a convenience sample of patients undergoing alveolar ridge preservation. Subjects with at least one single tooth gap undergoing ARP were identified and invited to participate. The rationale for inviting patients with single tooth gaps was to minimize the influence of soft tissue shrinkage due to multiple teeth extractions. The study aimed to describe and analyze the buccolingual profile of the ridge in single tooth gaps using a novel digital method in relation to the neighboring teeth.

After obtaining an informed consent, patients were recruited, and intraoral optical scans were performed 4 months after ARP. The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in Fortaleza in 2013, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of L'Aquila (N. 20/2021).

The inclusion criteria were: (i) systemically and periodontally healthy subjects, (ii) having at least one single tooth gap and undergoing ARP (iii) buccal bone wall≥50% (iv) tooth‐bound extraction sites (v) patient receiving standardized ARP procedure with deproteinized bovine bone mineral with 10% collagen (Geistlich Bio‐Oss Collagen; Geistlich Pharma AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland) and collagen membrane (Geistlich Bio‐Gide, Geistlich Pharma AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland).

Exclusion criteria: (i) extended edentulous sites, (ii) absence of a mesial or distal natural tooth; (iii) ARP in conjunction with STA (iv) smokers.

Intraoral scanning and STL file acquisition

The sites of interest (Figure 1A), together with their whole arch, were scanned with an intraoral optical scanner (Trios, 3Shape, Denmark). The generated digital models were exported and saved as STL files. Then, the digital files were imported into an image analysis software (GOM Inspect, GOM, Germany) (Figure 1B) where an examiner with extensive expertise in 3D volumetric assessment (L.M.) performed the 3D surface defect map analysis.

3D surface defect map

A plane was designed to pass through the midfacial aspect of the edentulous ridge. Then, two planes were created for the midfacial aspect of the two adjacent teeth, parallel to the previously designed plan (Figure 1C). A horizontal cutting plane, perpendicular to the vertical planes identified on the midfacial aspect of the gingival margin of adjacent teeth, was traced to define the direction of a cutting section (Figure 1D, E). A region of interest based on the vertical planes of the adjacent teeth, encompassing the mesial and distal papillae of the edentulous site was drawn. Subsequently, an automated function within image analysis software was used to generate a surface defect map (Figure 1F). The 3D surface defect map allowed visualization of all points within these planes and quantified their divergence inside or outside from a horizontal cutting plan perpendicular to those vertical lines (more lingual/palatal or more buccal) at this region (Figure 1G, H).

To report the outcomes of interest from the 3D surface defect map, 10 linear divergence points (LDPs) on the vertical plane designed at the midfacial aspect of the edentulous ridge were identified (Figure 2). These points started from the soft tissue margin and extended 0.5 mm apart in a corona‐apical direction. Positive values of LDPs indicated that the soft tissues were positioned more buccally (“outside”) compared to the parallel point of the adjacent dentition profiles, while negative LDPs denoted that soft tissues were located more lingually/palatally (“inside”) than the adjacent teeth profile. We recorded 10 LDPs for each edentulous site and plotted them to explore possible patterns describing the bucco‐lingual position of soft tissues relative to adjacent dentition profiles. Then, we grouped sites based on their pattern of 3D surface defect map and LDP outcomes. The method was previously described. 33

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported in absolute and relative frequencies and continuous variables in means, standard deviation (SD), medians, and interquartile range. Regarding the inferential analysis: The variable profile (measured in ordinal scale) was associated with independent factors and covariates using simple ordinal logistic regression. Raw odds ratio and 95% confidence intervals were obtained from the Wald's Chi2 statistic. Then, a multiple model was estimated to adjust by potential confounding factors. A test of parallel lines was conducted in order to assure the hypothesis of proportional odds, the significance level used in the analysis has been 5% (α = 0.05). The analyses were conducted by an author with extensive expertise in statistics (SB) using the R software package (Version 1.3.959, Rstudio, Boston, MA, USA).

A total of 27 patients with a mean age of 56.03 ± 12.05 were included, among which 16 were male and 11 were female (supplementary Table 1). A total of 30 edentulous sites were screened and analyzed, with 13 in the mandible and 17 in the maxilla as detailed in Table 1. The phenotypes observed were thin in 9 patients, moderate in 9, and thick in 12. The field of dentistry has been significantly influenced by technological advancements, and one notable innovation in the past 10 years is the emergence of intraoral optical scanning. These scanners were initially created to replace traditional impressions by capturing digital images of teeth, implants, and surrounding soft tissue, which are then saved as STL files. 27 , 40 Ongoing developments in both the technology and its related software have allowed clinicians to use these scanners for evaluating volumetric changes in different clinical situations. 31 , 32 , 34 , 40 Nevertheless, this process requires multiple scans of the same area to overlay the STL files and analyze changes using imaging software. The authors in the current study detailed how they utilized intraoral optical scanning to analyze the soft tissue profile of edentulous ridges and examine surface defects through 3D surface defect map analysis at a single time point. The proposed approach offers several advantages, including noninvasiveness of optical scanning, rapid data acquisition, and high patient acceptance and comfort levels. Furthermore, the authors demonstrated the ability to objectively visualize and define profile deficiencies.

It is reasonable to consider that this factor could also influence the healing process and surgical outcomes of grafting procedures. In periodontal and peri‐implant plastic surgery the recipient site anatomy is one of the factors that affect the stability of the graft and flap design. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 It can be postulated that the ridge profile may play a critical role in STA during the grafting procedure and flap design phase. It is hypothesized that grafts and flaps placed closer to the tongue/roof of the mouth in relation to the horizontal plane of the ridge may receive a more substantial blood supply during healing compared to those positioned nearer to the cheek side; however, further research is needed to validate this assertion. The 3D surface defect maps revealed three distinct patterns in this group of patients. It is important to note that 3D scanners only capture the profile of the alveolar ridge STL without distinguishing between bone and soft tissue. Despite being a limitation, the selection of patients who underwent previous ARP helped minimize potential confounding factors in this study. In line with previous studies on soft tissue remodeling after alveolar ridge preservation. 45

As supported by existing literature, only when combining STL files with CBCT data it is able to clearly delineate different tissues and their involvement in the defects. 46 The 3D surface defect map can be utilized to assess defect extent, anatomy, and grafting site. For instance, deficiencies limited to the first millimeter of the crest should be managed differently from an extensive defect. It is also essential to emphasize that this method enables clinicians not just to locate but also determine a depth for each defect which may impact treatment planning and guide surgeons toward either a substitute or connective tissue graft. 47 As denoted in the results a single tooth gap after ridge preservation might require a graft (concave profile) localized between the 1 and the 5 mm from the crest or in case of a linear/convex profile the edentulous ridge may not need any type of correction.

A 3D map shows surface defects related to flap preparation/elevation. Color codes make it easier to identify changes in tissue, indicating bone irregularities, papilla invagination, or exostoses near neighboring teeth. 40 These observations highlight the challenges of flap elevation and the increased risk of soft tissue damage or compression. 48 , 49 It could be suggested that a convex profile necessitates a more extensive flap design and releasing incisions, as opposed to a concave profile where the surface lies between the adjacent teeth, reducing tension during flap elevation but future studies are needed to validate this assumption. 48 , 49

Preoperative 3D surface defect mapping is a valuable tool in clinical research for studying the impact of various biomaterials and flap approaches around edentulous sites, teeth, and implants. Factors such as the alveolar ridge profile at the site of interest and the buccolingual position of soft tissues should be considered in stratified randomization and statistical analysis from a methodological standpoint. The tool provides quick visualization of divergence profiles at the area of interest compared to adjacent regions, with data reported using different outcome measures such as linear points and volumetric data.

The potential impact of using this tool in the scientific field is demonstrated in this study, indicating that KMW may contribute to preventing concave defects and minimizing tissue remodeling. While there are numerous studies discussing how different phenotypes could result in varying healing and remodeling patterns, limited data exists on the extent of KMW. Over the past 20 years, the role of KM around teeth and implants has been a widely debated topic, highlighting its crucial role for long‐term follow‐up care for both teeth (root coverage procedures) and implants (as a protective factor against peri‐implantitis development). 18 , 50 , 51 Data on the role of KMW in ridge preservation are scarce, but it is well‐known that thickness plays a significant role during the remodeling phase. 9 This particular study revealed that with each millimeter increase, there was a 57% reduction in the probability of developing a concave profile. Thus, it can be inferred that sites with extensive keratinized mucosa exhibit less remodeling after alveolar ridge preservation, potentially reducing the phenotype's impact during this phase.

When analyzing the findings of this research, it is crucial to take into account certain limitations such as the lack of data on bone position and bone thickness, the small sample size, and the inclusion of both single and multirooted teeth in the assessment. Nevertheless, the 3D surface defect map appears to be a promising tool for integration into everyday clinical practice and for use in future randomized clinical trials.

CONCLUSION

A 3D surface defect map generated from a single intraoral scan can be a valuable tool for objectively quantifying and evaluating profilometric and surface differences compared to adjacent teeth. By providing detailed insights into the precise location and depth of the defects, the 3D defect map could guide clinicians in decision‐making during STA procedures. These data‐driven inputs may enable the implementation of tailored flap management and soft tissue grafting strategies, addressing the specific needs of each patient more effectively. However, given the limitations of this study, further clinical investigations leveraging this tool and correlating it with the type of implant placement and STA procedures are needed.
