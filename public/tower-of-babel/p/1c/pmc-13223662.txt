Exploring utilisation of knee and hip osteoarthritis healthcare services and its predictors: a scoping review
Authors: Sara Neumann, Franziska Weber, Christian Grüneberg, Liane Schenk, Christian Thiel, Paul Gellert, Dirk Peschke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDespite growing evidence and specific guidelines, people with knee and hip osteoarthritis often do not receive appropriate care. This review aims to identify healthcare utilisation and its predictors to optimise existing services and identify unmet needs across different healthcare systems using Andersen’s Healthcare Utilisation Model as a reference.DesignWe conducted a scoping review and included studies published between 2010 and 2023 that assessed the healthcare utilisation in people with osteoarthritis. We examined general practitioner and orthopaedist consultations, physiotherapy, medication, hospitalisation and emergency department visits.Data sourcesPubMed, Livivo, Cochrane Library, CINAHL Complete and Web of Science were searched from January 2010 until November 2023. An updated literature search was conducted in December 2025.Data extraction and synthesisWe analysed the included studies by means of thematic analysis and descriptive representation of quantitative data.ResultsThe literature search identified 4228 articles, of which 2380 articles were included in the title/abstract screening after excluding duplicates. After the full-text screening of 97 articles, we included 39 (n=4 233 566) publications for data extraction and data synthesis. Most studies were conducted in the USA, Australia, Germany and the UK—few from Asia, Middle and South America and other European countries. Utilised healthcare services are general practitioner consultations (mean use: 43% of participants, n=6), opioid (36%, n=8) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use (42%, n=7), emergency department visits (27%, n=3), orthopaedic surgeon consultations (26%, n=4), total joint replacement surgery (26%, n=3), physiotherapy (14%, n=8), hospitalisation (11%, n=7) and psychotherapy (6%, n=2). Among predisposing characteristics, older age, female gender, ethnicity, high socioeconomic status, social support and fear of certain treatment options were related to higher healthcare utilisation. In those, gender (n=8 studies) and age (n=6 studies) were primarily discussed. Regarding enabling and need factors, increased healthcare utilisation is associated with urban residence and being insured as well as having pain and comorbidities.ConclusionsResults vary between countries. Against the background of existing guidelines, there is a need for promoting the utilisation of non-surgical and non-pharmacological treatments, such as physiotherapy, which have proven to be effective. Special attention should be given to predictors when promoting appropriate healthcare utilisation. Addressing the identified predictors associated with healthcare utilisation may lead to more appropriate osteoarthritis care. Further research is needed to address healthcare stakeholders’ (physiotherapists, insurers, patients and practitioners) needs and roles in the process.Trial registration numberProtocol registration on PROSPERO (CRD42023475803).Keywords: Health Services Accessibility; Health Services; Delivery of Health Care, Integrated

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a musculoskeletal disease that affects approximately 595 million people worldwide.1 It is most common in the knee and hip joints, resulting in pain, functional limitations, joint stiffness and decreased quality of life in the people affected.1 Due to an ageing population, reduced physical activity and associated obesity rates in developed countries, the prevalence of knee and hip OA will continue to increase over the coming decades.2 3 The widespread prevalence of OA places a significant burden on global healthcare systems and increases the workload in medical and rehabilitation fields.4 Therefore, there is a need to provide appropriate care for people with OA. Effective therapeutic approaches and treatment strategies are required.

Clinical practice guidelines highly recommend exercise programmes, weight management if required, education and mind-body exercises such as yoga as the first-line treatment of people with hip or knee OA.5 6 Additionally, topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and the use of intra-articular corticosteroids and intra-articular hyaluronic acid are recommended for treating people with knee and hip OA.6 Thus, conservative treatments have to be used before recommending patients a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) or a total hip arthroplasty (THA).6 However, according to a recent review of clinical guidelines and their lack of implementation in practice, appropriate conservative care before surgery is underused.7,9 The management of OA symptoms is often characterised by an inadequate use of healthcare services or low-value care (ie, services that provide little or no clinical benefit and/or lead to unnecessary costs).10,13

The Healthcare Utilisation Model by Ronald M Andersen is a framework used to analyse healthcare utilisation patterns and predictors.14 Andersen argues that healthcare utilisation is influenced by predisposing characteristics, enabling factors and need factors. Predisposing characteristics include demographic characteristics (eg, age, gender) and social characteristics (eg, marital status, education, ethnicity, residence) as well as health beliefs. Enabling factors allow people to use healthcare services. These include access to healthcare services, income, waiting time and travel time. Need factors consist of self-perceived health status and professionally assessed health status. Using Andersen’s Model allows us to combine different types of predictors into a common and internationally acknowledged framework. This makes the findings more comparable and transferable to various healthcare contexts.

Concerning the appropriateness of healthcare services provided, non-pharmacological treatments are generally less prescribed, and 22%–68% of surgical treatments are reported to be inappropriate.15 There seems to be a gap in physiotherapy utilisation for men, people with a lower socioeconomic status (SES), low symptom duration, people with health insurance, people receiving state support and migrants.10

Furthermore, prevention and treatment strategies often do not meet the specific individual needs and preferences of affected people and should be tailored towards individual needs.8 For targeted improvement efforts, a deeper understanding of healthcare utilisation of people with OA and insights into utilisation patterns and their predictors are essential and enable allocating limited resources to the most vulnerable patients and optimising health outcomes.16

In a study conducted by Belau et al, 889 people with a musculoskeletal disease were asked about their healthcare utilisation behaviour.17 A higher healthcare utilisation was associated with individual disease factors, for example, joint pain. Higher physiotherapy use was influenced by structural factors, such as residence, access and significant health limitations, as well as a higher use of orthopaedic surgeons (OS).17 Paskins et al conducted a narrative review to assess the attitudes of people with OA in relation to their consultations with general practitioners (GP). The authors found that utilisation barriers included a combination of health beliefs and negative attitudes towards their GP. Predictors to see a GP included the severity of joint pain, limitations in daily activities and the presence of a social support network.18

Despite the increasing focus on OA management, there is still no sufficiently comprehensive synthesis of how people access healthcare services or of the possible healthcare utilisation predictors across different healthcare systems. However, this is crucial to identify cross-system challenges that prevent appropriate care for people with OA. Existing reviews focus mostly on certain healthcare services,18 while others present data from a specific region.19 20 Other studies do not specifically present healthcare utilisation of people with OA17 or only investigate the economic burden.21 22 Current research only provides a partial understanding of how structural, social and individual predictors influence OA care on a global scale.

In this review, we aim to answer two central research questions: first, which specific healthcare services do people with hip and knee OA actually use? Second, what are the structural, social and individual predictors of healthcare utilisation among people with OA?

Methods

We registered the protocol of this review a priori on PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42023475803; see online supplemental file 5). In deviation from the protocol, we used the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) tool to assess the risk of bias (ROB), as the checklists are well suited for assessing the quality of multiple study types alike. Another deviation from the protocol is that a scoping review was conducted instead of a systematic review. Initially planned as a systematic review, this study was reframed as a scoping review due to the heterogeneity of studies, healthcare services examined and outcomes reported. We mapped out existing evidence on healthcare utilisation in OA and describe its predictors. A scoping review was therefore considered more appropriate than a systematic review, as it allowed a broader exploration of the literature.23 Therefore, we structured the review according to key scoping review principles, including systematic literature searching, study selection based on predefined eligibility criteria, double screening processes, structured data charting and data syntheses (as found in Arksey and O’Malley23). As the assessment of intervention effectiveness was not the research focus, and randomised controlled trials (RCTs) were excluded for reflecting trial rather than real-world care, a scoping approach allowed us to identify knowledge gaps and provide a structured overview of OA healthcare utilisation. Reporting is based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-ScR (2020) statement.24 Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Data sources and study selection

We conducted a systematic literature search in the beginning and middle of November 2023. Prior to the systematic literature review, we conducted preliminary searches at the end of October 2023. For the systematic literature search, we used the databases PubMed (MEDLINE), Livivo, Web of Science, CINAHL Complete and Cochrane Library. Additional references were hand-searched through the reference lists of possibly eligible studies. We used the systematic review tool Covidence for title, abstract and full-text screening. After removing duplicates automatically and manually, two reviewers (SN and FW) independently screened the references by title and abstract. We solved conflicts via discussion. When conflicts could not be resolved by discussion, they were forwarded to another author (DP). Afterwards, we conducted full-text screening (SN and FW). In December 2025, an additional literature search was conducted to update existing evidence on healthcare utilisation and its predictors. PubMed (MEDLINE), Livivo and the Cochrane Library were searched using the identical search terms and approach from the original literature search.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The Andersen Model guided our analytical framework. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported predictors that could be classified into the domains of the Andersen Model (predisposing, enabling and need factors), irrespective of whether the model was explicitly applied in the original study. Studies had to report on at least one healthcare service either recommended by OA guidelines (physiotherapy, TJR surgery, medication) or commonly used for OA symptoms (consultations with GP and OS, hospitalisations and psychotherapy).

Studies including people with musculoskeletal diseases were also included if they differentiated between people with OA and people with other musculoskeletal diseases in their results. This broadened the scope of the literature search. The broad timeframe (2010–2023) ensures inclusion of recent research reflecting current scientific knowledge. While guidelines for OA treatment have remained relatively stable, structural aspects of healthcare systems—such as the organisation, funding and accessibility of services—may have changed substantially over the past decade.

This review excludes studies that apply Andersen’s model to diseases other than OA or musculoskeletal diseases. During title and abstract screening, we excluded studies focusing exclusively on single cities, as their findings often reflect highly localised conditions, limiting comparability across healthcare systems. However, studies from specific regions were included if they analysed broader healthcare infrastructure or explicitly discussed their findings and implications beyond the local setting. RCTs were excluded because they primarily take place in controlled settings and do not reflect the care situation reliably. The inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in table 1.

Literature search

The systematic search in the mentioned databases used the following search terms (see online supplemental file 1): Healthcare utilisation, Andersen’s Healthcare Utilisation Model and predictors of utilisation related to OA or musculoskeletal disorders. The indication was operationalised as “osteoarthritis”, “osteoarthritis, knee”, “osteoarthritis hip” and “musculoskeletal diseases”. Healthcare utilisation was operationalised as “healthcare utilisation”, “healthcare use”, “health service use” and “health service utilization”. Andersen’s Healthcare Utilisation Model was additionally operationalised as “Andersen’s Model”. To include studies identifying predictors of healthcare utilisation that did not explicitly mention Andersen’s Model, we chose search words covering predictors but without the model’s terminology. The MeSH terms used can be accessed in the online supplemental files 1 and 3. Two librarians and search specialists, respectively, reviewed and refined the strategy. Then, we implemented the search strategy across multiple databases and adjusted it to each platform if required.

Data extraction and data synthesis

One reviewer (SN) extracted the data, while a second reviewer (DP) reviewed the resulting data extraction table. We recorded healthcare utilisation in tabular form and used thematic analysis to identify predictors of healthcare utilisation. The following data were extracted: study description, analysed type of healthcare utilisation of the study and analysed type of predictors of healthcare utilisation. Study descriptions contained population, origin of study, method, database, main outcomes or measurements and objectives. Type of healthcare utilisation was recorded in percentage (%); predictors were recorded descriptively (see online supplemental file 1). We then analysed data with a qualitative data synthesis method. For this, we conducted thematic analysis following Thomas and Harden.25 We carried out the thematic analysis in three stages: in the first step, we coded the results, discussion and conclusion sections of included studies line by line. In the second step, we developed descriptive themes that are still close to the source. We categorised similarities and differences between studies in a hierarchical structure, resulting in a tree structure.25 In the third and final step, we generated analytical themes that go beyond the descriptiveness of the second step and allow for a broader interpretation.25 Quantitative data were recorded descriptively in the data extraction table.

Standardisation of healthcare utilisation

Healthcare utilisation rates were extracted and recorded in tabular form. Where studies reported on healthcare utilisation for individual years, these values were extracted and presented separately. If annual values could not be separated, the data were described narratively (see online supplemental file 1). In studies reporting data from multiple countries, values were extracted separately for each country. In addition, countries were grouped by continent in a separate table. We compared data narratively as well, as cross-country differences can be found in social security arrangements and public healthcare systems, thus leading to different access to and availability of healthcare services.

Critical appraisal

We conducted the critical appraisal with the help of CASP checklists. CASP checklists are suited for various study designs and are common in healthcare research.26 The checklists enable the assessment of trustworthiness and the quality of studies. Although the checklists do not provide a numerical score, they do allow critical appraisal of studies by assessing validity, results and relevance to real-world problems.26 Online supplemental files 1 and 2 present the results of these studies’ critical appraisal.

Results

From 3859 studies imported from EndNote to Covidence, 93 full-text studies were screened, and 36 studies were included (see figure 1) in the first search period (October 2023). The updated literature search found an additional 317 studies: 310 studies were excluded in title and abstract screening, and four studies were excluded in full-text screening due to insufficient presentation of healthcare utilisation (n=2), wrong population (n=1) and wrong outcome (n=1). Critical appraisal

We performed the critical appraisal using the CASP checklists. All included studies address a clear and focused research question. They have credible results backed up by a clear methodology and a well-documented recruitment of participants. The included studies provide a detailed overview of OA healthcare utilisation and its implications for physiotherapy practice. However, some potentially critical points should be considered: Concerning the assessment of ROB in some studies, it is not clear how ROB is minimised.19 42 45 47 48 Some included studies could be clearer on how their outcomes were measured.10 19 42 45 47 Other concerns included lack of consideration of potential confounders,20 31 34 35 37 45 48 54 incomplete follow-up of subjects,48 consideration of relationship between researcher and participants28 and consideration of the quality of included studies.18

Predictors of healthcare utilisation

We assessed the distribution of codes from thematic analysis, assigning most codes to predisposing characteristics, followed by enabling factors, with the fewest codes to need factors (see online supplemental file 1).

Predisposing characteristics

For predisposing characteristics, we identified the following codes: age, gender, SES, health beliefs, social support and ethnicity.

Age

Six studies found that age was a predictor for healthcare utilisation. Costa et al found that healthcare utilisation was generally higher in younger people than older people (adjusted OR (AOR) 0.96).19 Similarly, older people were less likely to be referred to a specialist for OA pain or to a physiotherapist.10 19 In contrast, Kamsan et al concluded that healthcare utilisation was higher in older persons with OA. Their multivariable models, however, did not find age to be a significant predictor of outpatient care, inpatient care or pharmacotherapy use.42

For TJR surgeries, one study noted that older people were more likely to undergo the procedure, as many surgeons do not recommend it to younger people.28

Regarding pharmacotherapy, age was a predictor of opioid use (AOR 1.50). In the age group of 65–74 years, comparably more people than in other age groups used opioids for OA pain.57 NSAID use decreased with older age (AOR 0.98).30

Gender

Eight studies identified gender as a predictor for healthcare utilisation.1018 29 41,43 59 61 Women were consistently found to use more resources for OA symptoms, including physiotherapy, medication, GP consultations and treatment beyond the scope of usual care (eg, complementary medicine).29 41 42 48 57 One study reported higher odds of receiving OA-related treatments among women compared with men. For example, the odds of receiving physiotherapy were 28% higher (AOR 1.29), corticosteroid injections 31% higher (AOR 1.31) and opioid analgesics 30% higher (AOR 1.30). In contrast, two studies reported that more men got TJR surgery than women,38 52 and women were 87% less likely to be hospitalised due to OA than men.35

Socioeconomic status

Four studies identified SES as a predictor of healthcare utilisation. People with a high SES were more likely to take medication and assistance in general to manage OA symptoms.42 Additionally, people with a higher SES used more physiotherapy (47.6%) compared with people with a middle (36.4%) or low SES (28.1%).10 People with a bachelor’s degree or postgraduate work were more than twice as likely to use physiotherapy services (AOR 2.44).40 41 Boddu et al found that people with low income had trouble accessing appropriate care and were likely to skip medication.59

Health beliefs

One study assessed patients’ health beliefs as a predictor for reduced healthcare utilisation, while two studies found health beliefs to be significant for healthcare use.18 28 50 They identified anger, frustration and fear as main themes. Fear seems to be a central emotion regarding TJR: patients fear a lengthy recovery, anaesthesia and the development of other health problems when receiving TJR surgery.48 Some studies indirectly reflected provider attitudes through patient reports, for example, when patients described being told that OA is a natural but untreatable part of normal ageing.18 However, only a few studies examined provider beliefs directly.

Social support

Five studies identified having a social support network consisting of friends and family as a predictor for appropriate healthcare utilisation.18 28 42 52 53 Medication users reported the lowest level of social support, finding that less support meant more use of stronger medication, such as opioids.52 53 Kamsan et al found that being married was strongly associated with the utilisation of outpatient care (AOR 11.4).42 Conversely, Paskins et al stated in their narrative review that people who lived alone consult their GP more often than married people.18

Ethnicity

Four studies analysed the association of ethnicity with healthcare utilisation. Silverman et al reported in an unadjusted analysis that people of colour (POC) used fewer healthcare resources in comparison to White people.49 These healthcare services included physiotherapy (33% vs 37%), opioid use (79% vs 82%), hospitalisation (25% vs 28%) and TJR surgery (17% vs 43%).38 40 49 Even though POC used fewer healthcare services, the all-cause expenditure was higher in POC than in White patients.49 This was particularly evident in cases of inpatient admission. Wooley et al reported that concerning THA, Hispanic patients were nearly 10 years younger than Black, White or Asian people when getting THA.61

Enabling factors

As enabling factors, the residence and insurance of people with OA have been identified as important predictors for healthcare utilisation.

Residence

Five studies reported on residence as a predictor for healthcare utilisation.18 19 32 39 60 People with OA in rural areas tended to get more TJR surgery than people with OA living in urban areas.19 39 At the same time, people living in urban areas used more outpatient healthcare services than their rural living counterparts (average of 5.24 visits vs 4.62 visits to healthcare professionals).32 An urban residence has been identified as a strong predictor for GP consultations and rheumatologist visits.18 60

Insurance

Five studies identified having insurance as a predictor for healthcare utilisation.19 27 34 49 57 Concerning insurance, multiple studies reported that there is a difference between having insurance and having to pay for using healthcare services. Insured people used more healthcare services and caused higher OA-related costs than uninsured people (AOR 0.65).19 27 49 Furthermore, it was evident that American Medicare beneficiaries used more opioids compared with people outside of Medicare support.57 Dros et al added that having statutory insurance often came with the need to pay deductibles, as some services were not included in statutory insurance.34

Need factors

Pain severity was the most frequently discussed need factor and predictor for healthcare utilisation. Furthermore, we identified comorbidities, especially depression, as need factors, as they could influence self-perception of one’s own health and functional status.

Pain severity

Pain was consistently identified as an important predictor for healthcare utilisation.10 27 30 31 45 46 56 All of the mentioned studies reported that the more severe the pain, the more healthcare services like GP and OS consultations, medication, hospitalisations or physiotherapy are used by people with OA.10 27 30 31 45 46 56 Pain severity was also associated with high rates of TJR surgery.27 45 For instance, Power et al found that higher pain was associated with a higher use of opioids before a TJR surgery (AOR 1.25).46

Comorbidities

We identified that the presence and number of comorbidities were associated with higher healthcare utilisation (41, 43, 49, 54). Especially obesity and depression were mentioned as predictors for high healthcare utilisation.46 57 Depression was a predictor for TJR surgery and GP consultations and was common in opioid users (AOR 1.60).44 46 52 Wright et al found that obese people had 83% higher odds of using opioids prior to TJR surgery (AOR 1.83).57

Discussion

Following the PRISMA-ScR recommendations, this review examined healthcare utilisation and explored predictors influencing utilisation. We addressed two central questions: which healthcare services are most frequently used by people with OA, and which predictors influence their utilisation.

Despite the differences in the healthcare systems, insurance structures and cultural contexts, the challenges faced are similar across the analysed countries. Medication is the most common treatment, while physical therapy is often underused or not assessed in included studies—despite its key role in conservative exercise treatment of hip and knee OA. As a first point of contact, GP may serve as important gatekeepers for appropriate healthcare utilisation. While the Andersen Model was used to categorise the predictors of healthcare utilisation, it also provides insight into patterns of healthcare utilisation among people with OA. The results of this review show that predisposing characteristics were the most frequently reported predictors of healthcare utilisation. Nevertheless, individual enabling characteristics, such as place of residence and need factors, also emerged as important determinants. This indicates that while non-modifiable predisposing characteristics (age, gender, biological and genetic predispositions) seem to influence healthcare utilisation, contextual predictors were also evident in this review and highlight the relevance of examining healthcare utilisation with a broader conceptual framework. Across categories, significant relationships were observed between predictors and multiple healthcare utilisation outcomes.

Utilisation of healthcare services

Medication was consistently among the most frequently reported and analysed healthcare services with high utilisation levels across studies: NSAIDs and opioids showed utilisation rates of up to 85% and 71%, respectively.

We found that the more pain a person with OA suffers, the more healthcare services are used. Other studies recognised this clear trend as well.17 62 63 Even though highly recommended in guidelines, physiotherapy was not used as much as it should be, aligning with current research.7 64 The widespread use of NSAIDs is consistent with treatment guidelines.5 6 The need for psychotherapy presents an increasingly recognised element in the management of OA, found in numerous studies.65 66 The increasing relevance is linked to depression being a common comorbidity for OA patients.67 There is a need for multidisciplinary treatment options, including physiotherapy and psychotherapy.65 67 While the relevance of psychotherapy in OA care has been recognised, it has not been systematically examined, emphasising the need for further health services research.

Concerning predictors of healthcare utilisation, predisposing factors were the most frequently investigated category of predictors, while overall pain emerged as a significant predictor of increased healthcare utilisation.

Discussing predisposing characteristics

Among predisposing characteristics, advanced age, female gender and high social support seem to predict increased healthcare utilisation for OA. Ethnicity is a factor contributing to unequal access to healthcare. Our review found that older people tend to use stronger medication (eg, opioids) and are more likely to receive TJR surgery compared with younger people. This pattern is consistent with prior research. Atella et al highlighted the association between advanced age, increased healthcare utilisation and rising healthcare costs.68 Current guidelines should more explicitly consider the specific challenges of older age, such as polypharmacy and increased risk of side effects. None of the current guidelines address the appropriate care of older people with OA specifically. The Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) guidelines only mention age-related diseases and frailty but do not address care for older people. The limited use of medications due to side effects needs to be acknowledged and other possible treatment options highlighted.6 Additionally, there needs to be an expansion of non-pharmacological interventions and exercise therapy to address the current lack of care in this area for older people with OA.

Regarding gender as a predictor for healthcare utilisation, several of the included studies found that women used more health services than men, excluding TJR surgery. This aligns with the literature reporting higher healthcare utilisation in women in the management of OA.69,71 The place of residence of people with OA is influential for healthcare utilisation as well. Studies found that rural habitants use less healthcare services in general, such as physiotherapy or other outpatient services, compared with urban citizens.72,74

Predictors of healthcare utilisation

Concerning need factors, a well-documented predictor for healthcare utilisation is pain. Our results are consistent with previous findings indicating that pain is a particularly strong predictor of increased healthcare utilisation. Studies highlight the role of pain as a predictor of increased healthcare utilisation, such as physiotherapy for musculoskeletal pain.16 75 Other researchers present the correlation that mild and severe OA pain has with GP consultations and other outpatient services.17 62 63 The low utilisation of physiotherapy suggests a need for patient, GP and OS education emphasising the importance of exercise therapy and physical activity in reducing symptoms such as pain. This includes simplifying the medical prescription and referral process as well as eliminating treatment costs. While high healthcare utilisation is not inherently negative, the focus must be on effective and appropriate use of healthcare services and preventing low-value care. Early identification of people with severe OA pain and a better understanding of predictors hold significant implications for pain management. By improving early detection, healthcare providers can offer targeted interventions, potentially reducing pain and lowering associated healthcare costs. Enhancing physical function and educating patients about the efficacy and accessibility of physiotherapy as a key treatment option for managing OA are also essential steps to increase its utilisation.76

Women tend to have more severe disease, and functional status may explain why women use more health services than men.77 Sikka et al describe women having a lower SES, poorer self-reported health status, and thus having higher healthcare utilisation rates. Biological factors contribute to a more severe disease progression in women, leading to greater levels of pain and a higher likelihood of seeking medical assistance. Women tend to experience more pain due to hormonal influences; their bone density differs from men, and they cope with pain differently from men do69—highlighting the importance of gender-sensitive medical interventions in health politics and a gender-sensitive healthcare policy.77

Urban residents use more health services than people living in rural areas, which may be attributed to easier access to healthcare facilities and providers due to a higher density and shorter distances in urban areas.78 79 Incentives for medical and rehabilitative professionals to practise in rural regions may help to balance out the unequal ratio of doctors and other medical-rehabilitative facilities in rural regions.79 A typical approach is the German Landarztgesetz (rural doctor quota), where medical study places are awarded to those who commit to working in regions with a shortage of doctors for 10 years80 81 or the use of digital innovations and applications.

The relevance of the professionalisation of medical staff

Physiotherapists play a role in educating their patients about OA and developing exercise routines. Full academisation of the physiotherapy profession supports meeting the demands for appropriate care—not just for OA. Professionalisation ensures high-quality care and specialised professionals who know how to treat people with complex diseases and symptoms.82 The role of education is also highlighted in the OARSI treatment guidelines for OA.6 Patient education on the benefits of physical activity instead of relying solely on medication intake is a major task for physiotherapists. Specialised and sensible professionals in exercise therapy are highly sought in the care of complex diseases. This is particularly significant, as medication has only a limited effect on pain relief in contrast to therapeutic interventions.76 83

Strengths and limitations

This review highlights central gaps in care, including the lack of systematic research of psychotherapy relevance in OA care, insufficient age and gender-specific guidelines and disparities in access between urban and rural areas. In discussing the findings of predictors and patterns of healthcare utilisation, it is necessary to acknowledge the heterogeneity among the included studies. The study populations were drawn from different sources in different countries around the world, with structural disparities in health and social systems, as well as social conflicts. Even for the more distinct predictors, this complicates discussion.

In presenting healthcare utilisation, the process of generalising and synthesising studies proved challenging. This difficulty stemmed from the heterogeneity across studies, characterised by variations in study population size and different periods analysed as well as data used from different databases. Some studies reported on healthcare utilisation rates from claims data, and some data were self-assessed (e.g., Ackerman et al; Desilet et al; Hoogeboom et al; Kamsan et al; Liu et al; Nalamachu et al; Power et al; Sibbritt et al and Vina et al 2732 40 42 44,46 48 53), possibly leading to recall bias. As a result, attempts to delineate a cohesive timeline representing the prevalence of healthcare utilisation among people with OA were complicated. Estimates of healthcare utilisation should be interpreted with caution, as direct comparisons across multiple countries are limited by different healthcare systems, health insurance arrangements and access to and availability of healthcare services. This may have influenced reported utilisation estimates across studies. A limitation of this study lies within its data sources: These differ in how OA and healthcare utilisation are captured. Administrative datasets, while offering large samples, often rely on diagnostic codes and typically do not capture the use of self-paid services. Additionally, the accuracy with which OA is captured within these codes varies.

Nevertheless, this study gives a detailed perspective on healthcare utilisation of people with OA and presents predictors that may contribute to appropriate OA prevention, treatment and care. The advantage of this study in capturing a wide range of healthcare utilisation and predictors globally can highlight the extent to which healthcare utilisation is system-shaped and influenced by cultural contexts. Another strength of this review lies in its analysis of the utilisation of a wide range of healthcare services in OA, as opposed to a focus on a single service in isolation. In further research, an assessment of the identified predictors with tools such as the CHecklist for critical Appraisal and data extraction for systematic Reviews of prediction Modelling Studies, the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsesment Tool or Transparent Reporting of multivariate prediction models for individual prognosis or diagnosis can further strengthen research findings. Additionally, further research on addressing provider health beliefs, which are likely to shape referral behaviour and healthcare access, is also recommended. A key strength of this study is the double screening and analysis process. In certain cases, a third reviewer was consulted to determine the inclusion or exclusion of studies. The systematic literature search was comprehensive and further refined through strict inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Conclusion

This scoping review is the first to discuss healthcare utilisation and predicting factors among people with OA using Andersen’s Healthcare Utilisation Model in an international context. The majority of included studies focused on predisposing characteristics, while enabling factors received less attention in the analysis. The identified disparities in care indicate that comprehensive measures are needed to promote appropriate care for people with OA. This includes stronger recommendations and targeted measures to promote conservative treatments, such as exercise therapy and appropriate pain management, especially for older adults, people with low SES and in rural areas. Increased and well-coordinated interprofessional communication between medical, rehabilitation and social professions may support appropriate care. The institutional strengthening of the physiotherapy profession can facilitate access to appropriate physiotherapy care. Special conditions of vulnerable people must be explicitly recognised in current treatment guidelines, and healthcare systems must invest in interprofessional collaboration.
