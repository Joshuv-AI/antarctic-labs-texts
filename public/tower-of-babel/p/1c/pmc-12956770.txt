The efficacy of tamsulosin 0.4 mg when tamsulosin 0.2 mg is insufficient for benign prostatic hyperplasia in Korean patients
Authors: Younsoo Chung, Seong Jin Jeong, Sang Hun Song, Sung Kyu Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study evaluated the clinical impact of escalating tamsulosin dosage from 0.2 to 0.4 mg in men with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) who exhibited inadequate responses to the lower dose.Materials and MethodsA retrospective review was performed on 57 patients treated at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital in 2022. Uroflowmetry parameters, International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), and postvoid residual volume (PVR) were compared before and after dose escalation. Subgroup analysis was conducted by age, prostate volume, and concomitant 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor use.ResultsThe mean maximum urinary flow rate value improved significantly from 14.4 to 17.7 mL/s (p<0.001), with consistent benefits across subgroups. No significant changes were observed in IPSS or PVR overall, although PVR decreases were significant in prostates >50 mL.ConclusionsEscalating tamsulosin from 0.2 to 0.4 mg can meaningfully improve urinary flow in certain BPH patients, particularly those with larger prostates or concomitant 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor therapy.Keywords: Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Lower urinary tract symptoms, Tamsulosin

INTRODUCTION

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is one of the most prevalent urological conditions in aging men. It is characterized by the progressive enlargement of the prostate gland and lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) that negatively affect the quality of life [1,2]. Pharmacologic management with alpha-1 adrenergic antagonists remains the mainstay of initial therapy for men with moderate-to-severe symptoms because of their proven ability to improve urinary flow rates and relieve symptoms [3]. Among these medications, tamsulosin is widely used due to its favorable selectivity for the α1A receptor subtype, which is predominant in the prostate and bladder neck, thereby reducing the risk of systemic side effects [4].

The recommended initial dosage of tamsulosin is 0.2 mg per day in many Asian countries, including Korea and Japan, which is lower than the 0.4 mg starting dose typically employed in Western populations [5,6]. However, clinical experience suggests that a substantial proportion of patients do not demonstrate sufficient improvement in LUTS or maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) when treated with a single 0.2 mg dose [7]. For these patients, escalating the tamsulosin dose to 0.4 mg is a reasonable therapeutic option. Numerous randomized controlled trials have confirmed the efficacy and safety of 0.4 mg tamsulosin dose escalation and demonstrated significant improvements in International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) and Qmax values [8,9]. However, most previous research has compared fixed doses in separate patient groups rather than evaluating the same patients before and after dose increases. This study was conducted to assess the therapeutic impact of escalating the tamsulosin dose from 0.2 to 0.4 mg in patients with BPH.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study included male patients diagnosed with BPH at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2022. Patients who had been treated with 0.2 mg tamsulosin and subsequently switched to 0.4 mg due to inadequate symptom control were selected. The uroflowmetry results before and after dose escalation were compared, including Qmax values, postvoid residual volume (PVR), and IPSSs. Patient baseline data were collected, including patient age, body mass index, history of diabetes mellitus or hypertension, glomerular filtration rate, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, prostate volume, transitional zone volume, tamsulosin treatment duration, and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor usage.

Statistical analyses were conducted using paired comparisons between the two dosages. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for variables that were non-normally distributed, with a statistical significance threshold set at p<0.05. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on age (<65 years vs. ≥65 years), prostate volume (≤30 mL, >30–50 mL, >50 mL), and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor use. All p-values are two-sided, and p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp.) and R statistical software, version 4.5.1 (http://cran.r-project.org/).

We performed this retrospective study after Institutional Review Board approval of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (approval number: B-2503-962-103) and in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The written informed consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective design.

RESULTS

A total of 57 patients were included in the analysis. The mean age was 67.0 years, and the mean prostate volume was 42.4 mL. Forty-two patients (73.7%) were taking 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors. DISCUSSION

The present study evaluated the clinical impact of escalating tamsulosin doses from 0.2 to 0.4 mg in men with BPH who had shown insufficient responses to the lower dose. Significant improvements in Qmax values were seen after treatment with 0.4 mg. These findings suggest that dose escalation is an effective and well-tolerated option for patients who fail to achieve adequate symptom control with the standard 0.2 mg regimen.

Our results are consistent with previous randomized controlled trials demonstrating the superior efficacy of 0.4 mg tamsulosin in improving LUTS and uroflow parameters [8,9]. Our study allowed us to directly evaluate the benefit of increasing the dose in the same patient population, thereby reducing inter-individual variability. The observed increase in Qmax values of approximately 3 mL/s is clinically meaningful.

In Asian countries, a 0.2 mg starting dose of tamsulosin is the standard, based on studies showing good efficacy with lower drug exposure [6,10]. However, some patients continue to have bothersome LUTS after therapy. For these patients, the therapeutic options typically include dose escalation, switching to a different class of medication such as a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor, or combination therapy [11,12,13].

In our study, tamsulosin escalation from 0.2 to 0.4 mg significantly improved Qmax values, whereas no statistically significant changes were observed in symptom scores or postvoid residual urine volume. This finding differs somewhat from previous prospective studies, such as that by Kim et al. [10], in which patients who had inadequate responses to 0.2 mg demonstrated improvements not only in Qmax values but also in IPSSs and PVR after switching to 0.4 mg. Likewise, a large cross-sectional survey in Korea reported that a substantial proportion of patients on 0.2 mg tamsulosin remained dissatisfied due to persistent LUTS, supporting the need for additional interventions beyond low-dose monotherapy [14]. In contrast, a study of Kim et al. [10] generally showed no clear superiority of 0.4 mg over 0.2 mg as an initial therapy in Asian men, suggesting that the benefit of higher doses may be limited to certain patients. Thus, our findings suggest that while dose escalation may enhance objective voiding parameters such as the Qmax, it may not always translate into parallel improvements in subjective symptom scores, emphasizing the need to tailor treatment decisions to individual patient profiles.

Although our study demonstrated a statistically and clinically meaningful improvement in Qmax values after dose escalation, the lack of significant changes in IPSSs suggests that objective improvements in urinary flow may not always correspond to patients’ subjective perceptions of symptom relief. This discrepancy between objective urodynamic measures and patient-reported symptom scores was also noted in previous studies, suggesting that symptom perception is influenced by factors beyond urinary flow alone [15]. Lower urinary tract sensations, which form an essential component of LUTS diagnosis and treatment evaluation, are inherently subjective and may not always correlate with improvements in voiding parameters. Consequently, even when obstruction is relieved and urinary flow improves, patients may not perceive parallel symptom relief if sensory adaptation or bladder compliance remains unchanged.

Several limitations to this study should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the observed improvements could be influenced by placebo effects or the natural course of the disease. Third, the follow-up duration of 8–12 weeks was relatively short; thus, long-term data on the durability of the response and safety are needed. Finally, we did not evaluate other objective measures, such as prostate volume change or serum PSA levels, which could provide additional insight.

Despite these limitations, our study highlights the value of a practical and widely used treatment adjustment strategy in real-world practice. Unlike most previous studies that compared separate fixed-dose groups, our within-subject design allows direct evaluation of dose escalation in the same individuals, thereby reducing variability and more accurately reflecting real-world clinical decision-making. This study’s retrospective design and relatively small sample size, which may reduce the statistical power of the analysis. However, the improvement in Qmax was consistently observed across multiple subgroups including different age categories, prostate volume strata, and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor users, supporting the robustness of the observed treatment effect. For men who do not experience adequate relief from LUTS after taking 0.2 mg tamsulosin, dose escalation to 0.4 mg can be a safe and effective option, especially in high volume BPH >50 mL. Previous evidence indicates that men with larger prostate volumes often exhibit more pronounced bladder outlet obstruction and reduced response to low-dose α1-blocker monotherapy, as highlighted by studies linking increased intravesical prostatic protrusion and prostate size to decreased Qmax and increased residual urine [16]. Furthermore, the addition of a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor may enhance treatment response by reducing prostatic volume and smooth muscle tone, thereby improving the functional effect of α1-adrenergic blockade in this subgroup [17]. Future randomized trials with larger populations and longer follow-up periods are warranted to confirm these findings and determine the optimal timing and patient selection criteria for dose escalation.

CONCLUSIONS

In patients with BPH with insufficient responses to 0.2 mg tamsulosin, dose escalation to 0.4 mg resulted in a significant improvement in Qmax values, while changes in IPSSs and PVR were not statistically significant. The benefit of increased Qmax values was observed consistently across age groups and prostate volume categories, particularly in patients also receiving 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors. These findings suggest that dose escalation can be a useful strategy for improving objective voiding function in certain patients.
