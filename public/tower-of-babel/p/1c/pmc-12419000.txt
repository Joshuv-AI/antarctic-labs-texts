Tweaking the electrocatalytic efficiency of sonochemically synthesized Bi2S3 nanorods through decoration with f-MWCNTs: A selective on-site detection of METOL in environmental samples
Authors: Harikrishnan Sathya, Amal A Abdel Hafez, Mani Govindasamy, Pin-Yi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Bismuth sulfide, Health care analysis, Good health and well-being, Sonochemical synthesis, Electrochemical sensor, Clean Water

Introduction

The ultrasonic synthsis of nanocomposites has emerged as a green and efficient approach. In recent years, significant attention has been directed towards the ultrasound-assisted synthesis of various nanoparticles and nano-composite materials, particularly those involving transition metal oxides, metal sulfides, and carbon nanomaterials due to their ability to enhance dispersion and reduce particle aggregation [1,2]. The sonochemical method uses ultrasound to trigger cavitation in solution. This kind of cavitation is responsible for the generation of intensely reactive molecules such as H•, H2O2, and OH• [3]. As ultrasound moves through the liquid medium, it creates high and low-pressure regions. This results in tiny bubbles forming, expanding, and eventually bursting. During the breakdown of these bubbles, extreme conditions are generated, with transient temperatures reaching up to 5 × 103 K along with pressures exceeding 101. 325 MPa, leading to intense energy environments [4]. Thus, energy accumulates in femtoliter-scale volumes while being dispersed throughout countless locations [5]. Since this burst occurs within a nanosecond, a rapid cooling speed of ≥1011 K/s also exists. Such intense conditions stimulate significant chemical interactions and material transformations [[6], [7], [8]]. The progression of a chemical reaction is governed by the characteristics of its energy sources. Compared to established energy sources such as heat, ionizing radiation or light, the mechanochemical effects of ultrasound vary in energy per molecule, duration and pressure [3]. This mechanism enhances interfacial interactions, reduces particle agglomeration, and improves electron transport properties [9].

According to the 2021 UNESCO World Water Development Report, the global demand for freshwater has surged sixfold over the last century [10]. Beyond the pollutants discharged from agricultural, chemical, and farming sectors, effluents from the photographic industry significantly contribute to the pollution of natural water bodies [11]. As per U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) report, photo processors usually release 3785 L of wastewater in a single day [12]. Particularly, MET is a key component in photography developing solutions and hair color formulas for its steady-tone developing properties. It is also categorized as a harmful ecotoxic organic compound [13]. Due to its significant amino and hydroxyl functional groups in its chemical structure, MET is widely employed in various applications, including portrait, documentary, fine art, and modern imaging techniques. However, prolonged exposure to atmospheric conditions leads to the deterioration of these chemical structures, resulting in significant environmental pollution [14]. The release of these toxic organic compounds from domestic sewage and wastewater generated by the photography industry poses a significant threat to both households and groundwater. Even at 0.25 mg/L concentrations of this compound in freshwater have been shown to exert lethal effects on aquatic flora and fauna [15]. Exposure to MET can lead to various adverse health effects, including irritation of the eyes, nausea, skin irritation, and potential harm to internal organs [16]. In practical, the majority of developing agents are derived from aromatic amines, which are mutagenic, toxic and carcinogenic substances. In response to growing environmental concerns, Japan and the United States enacted legislation under the Environmental Protection Act of 1995 to regulate and mitigate the release of these pollutants [17].

Various analytical methods have been utilized for the determination of MET, including standard enzyme-linked immunoassay, ultraviolet spectroscopy, high-performance liquid chromatography, liquid-phase microextraction, ratiometric fluorescence, photolysis, electrophoresis, the Fenton reagent, and chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry [18]. However, these methods are often limited by their reliance on expensive equipment, intricate procedures, and significant time investment, making them challenging to implement and manage effectively. Therefore, it is crucial to establish a fast, selective, sensitive, and economically viable method for the detection of MET. The electrochemical techniques are widely regarded for their rapid, cost-effectiveness, high sensitivity, simplicity, and effective analytical approach [19,20]. Recently, Parthasarathi Manimaran et al., successfully synthesized La2NiO4@GO/GCE for the electrochemical sensing of MET, demonstrating good analytical performance. Their study reported a higher sensitivity of 10.84 μA μM−1 cm−2, a linear detection range spanning from 0.1 to 110 µM, and a remarkably low limit of detection (LOD) of 6.4 nM [21]. Therefore, the incorporation of advanced materials as electrocatalysts significantly enhances the precision and performance of electrochemical sensors and enables highly accurate detection and measurements. The physiological and structural properties of these materials significantly influence their performance in electrochemical sensing applications [22].

Recent findings highlight metal sulfides' enhanced conductivity over oxides, with improved charge dynamics and adaptable structures for electrochemical systems [23,24]. Metal sulfides such as ZnS [25], Ni2S3 [26], SnS [27,28], MoS2 [29], SnS2 [30], Co1-xS [31], VS4 [32], Bi2S3 [33] and Fe7S8 [34] have garnered significant attention due to their cost efficiency, superior electrical conductivity, and exceptional capacity. Their properties render them strong candidates for broad electrochemical use [35]. Among A2VB3VI compounds, Bi2S3 is recognized for its outstanding multifunctional properties. Its appeal lies in its outstanding electron transport properties and a direct bandgap spanning 1.3 to 1.7 eV [[36], [37], [38]]. The 6p orbitals of Bi and 3p orbitals of sulfur dominate charge transfer during electrochemical processes. Furthermore, the bonding via Bi 6p and S 3p hybridization creates a conduction band that enhances electrochemical performance. Additionally, the nanostructured morphology of Bi2S3 provides an increased number of active sites, which significantly enhances its performance in electrochemical reactions [39]. However, the presence of Bi2S3 particles significantly diminishes the cyclic stability of the system when it comes to practical applications [40,41]. To address these constraints and increase their performance, researchers were focused on developing cost-effective carbon-based materials with high electron transport and catalytic abilities [42].

Out of the various carbon-based materials, MWCNT are widely used in electronic composites due to their outstanding conductivity, thermal stability, and efficient electron transport. The Bi2S3 nanostructures can efficiently bond to the surface of functionalized multi-walled carbon nanotubes (f-MWCNT), creating a strong mechanical interlocking [43,44]. The integration with f-MWCNT induces spin–orbital interactions in bismuth ions, creating additional energy states that enhance sensitivity. Additionally, π–π stacking between the CNTs' aromatic structure and Bi2S3 nanostructures promotes strong interfacial coupling [45]. Similarly, the occurrence of functional groups including hydroxyl (−OH) or carboxyl (−COOH) on the surface of f-MWCNT facilitates the formation of covalent bonds with Bi2S3 [46]. Moreover, spin–orbit coupling reduces the effective mass of charge carriers, boosting their mobility. This, in turn, improves the composite’s conductivity, stability, and electron transfer, thereby enhancing its electrochemical performance [47].

Herein, the current research work highlights a cost-effective, facile and high-performance electrochemical sensor was developed by modifying a GCE with a Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite synthesized through ultrasonication for the detection of MET. By harnessing cavitation effects via ultrasound, the sonochemical method yields a well-integrated nanostructure that enhances electrochemical performance through increased surface area and reactive sites. The integration of Bi2S3 nanorods and f-MWCNT results in an extensive linear range (0.01–2100 µM), excellent sensitivity (10.514 μA μM−1 cm−2), and a better lower detection limit (6.52 nM). This sensor is highly capable of detecting MET in various kinds of water samples. This work not only advances the field of electrochemical sensing but also establishes a robust framework for the onsite detection of MET by merging technical innovation with practical application, thus providing an advancement in analytical techniques that contribute to environmental preservation and public health.

Experimental section

Chemicals

Bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, 98 %), Thiourea (98 %), Ethylene glycol (99.9 %), Multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT), 4-(methylamino)phenol sulfate (99 %), Lead(II)Nitrate (99 %), Mercury(II)Chloride (99.5 %), Sodium Chloride (99.9 %), Uric Acid (99 %), Ascorbic Acid (99 %), p-Nitrotoluene (99 %), Bisphenol-A (97 %), Copper(II)sulfate (99 %), Potassium chloride (99 %), Sodium sulfate (99 %), Sodium thiosulfate (99 %), Catechol (CC, 99 %), Hydroquinone (HQ, 99 %), Sodium dihydrogen phosphate (99 %), Disodium hydrogen phosphate (98.5 %), and potassium ferricyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6], 99 %) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich and used precisely as prescribed. Every chemical and reagent utilized in the study was of analytical reagent (AR) quality and was applied without additional purification. To prepare the reagents and buffers, millipore water (Merck) was used.

Materials and characterizations of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT

The Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite was prepared using an ultrasonic processor (model UP-100, manufactured by Chrom Tech, Taiwan). The device operates at a frequency range 25 kHz and a maximum output power of 100 W/cm2. The transducer is housed within a stainless steel casing, and the probe tip has a diameter of approximately 6 mm. The probe tip is made up of a titanium. During sonication, the probe was positioned vertically at the center of a cylindrical glass beaker, with the tip immersed approximately 20 mm below the liquid surface. The Palmsens4 FRA instrument was employed to carry out the CV, EIS and DPV. The real sample testing was performed with a Zensor R&D wireless potentiostat (connector model: ECWP100C and remote model: ECWP100S). A standard three-electrode configuration was employed for this analysis. The prepared Bi2S3/f-MWCNT was finely coated over the surface of a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) employing the drop-casting approach, acting as the working electrode. A Pt wire utilized as the counter electrode, while a silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) reference electrode immersed in 1.0 M KCl was employed to maintain a stable reference potential. The crystallographic structure was investigated through X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis with a Bruker instrument (Rigaku D/maxB, DMX-2200). Morphological properties were investigated utilizing scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX) utilizing a Hitachi H-7600 system (Japan). The elemental composition of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT substrate was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a PHI 5000 system (ULVAC, Japan). The surface area and surface features were analyzed through Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) testing with a Quantachrome Autosorb IQ series instrument.

Functionalization of MWCNT

Carboxyl-functionalized multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) were synthesized using a previously reported procedure [48]. Initially, a 1 g sample of pristine multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) was dispersed in a 3:1 (v/v) mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and nitric acid (HNO3) and subsequently subjected to ultrasonication for 2 h. Then, the mixture was heated under reflux at 90 °C with continuous vigorous stirring for 12 h to ensure the complete reaction occurred. We filtered the suspension using a porous Teflon membrane paper. The filtered solid was subsequently rinsed thoroughly with millipore water until the filtrate exhibited a neutral pH. The attained solid substance was dried under a vacuum oven at 70 °C for six hours to confirm the complete elimination of residual moisture.

Sonochemical synthesis of Bi2S3 nanorods and Bi2S3/f-MWNCT nanocomposite

The Bi2S3 nanorods and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite were synthesized using an ultrasonic approach (Scheme 1). At first, 0.32 g of bismuth nitrate and 0.16 g of thiourea were measured and dissolved in 25 mL of ethylene glycol as the reaction medium. Thereafter, the reaction solution was immersed directly by a high-intensity ultrasonic probe (Chrom Tech.; 6 mm diameter; Titanium horn, 25 kHz, pulse period – 1 sec, 100 W/cm2) and the irradiation was applied under ambient conditions for 120 min to the solution mixture. The sonication process was performed without cooling, allowing the reaction mixture to reach 343 K at the end of the reaction. The reaction produced a black precipitate at the end. After cooling to ambient temperature, the precipitated material was obtained by centrifugation. The product was then washed repeatedly with acetone and Millipore water to remove residual impurities. Lastly, the obtained solid product was completely dried in a vacuum desiccator for several days to ensure the complete removal of residual moisture before proceeding with further characterization. For the preparation of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite, 25 mg of Bi2S3 and 50 mg of f-MWCNT were evenly dispersed in 50 mL of ethanol. The mixture was then subjected to the same ultrasonication method for 30 min until the solution transitioned to a cloudy appearance (Scheme 1). During this process, high-frequency ultrasonic waves generate intense cavitation in the solvent media, which triggers rapid solvent oscillations and strong hydrodynamic effects. This helps mix and disperse the solid particles effectively. After that, the solution was allowed to dry at 70 °C for 12 h in a hot air oven. The prepared nanocomposite was stored and utilized for further characterization and electrochemical studies.

Schematic diagram for the synthesis of Bi2S3 nanorods and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite.

Synthesis of Bi2S3/f-MWNCT nanocomposite using grinding method

An alternative procedure applied for the preparation of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite was the solid-state grinding method. To begin, 10 mg of Bi2S3 and 20 mg of f-MWCNT were weighed and physically blended using an agate mortar and pestle for approximately 30 min under ambient conditions. The grinding was performed using short intervals between the composite formation. Simultaneously, 10 ml of ethanol was gradually added during the grinding. Finally, collect the dispersed Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite for electrode fabrication.

Fabrication of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT onto the GCE

The bare GCE was polished using alumina slurry helps to achieve a smooth and clean surface. At this time, loosely adhered particles were effectively eliminated from the surface of the GCE. A 6 μL volume of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite was carefully drop-cast onto the GCE surface. After that, the modified GCE was placed at an ambient temperature for 10 min to ensure complete solvent evaporation and stable film formation. The Bi2S3/f-MWCNT-modified GCE was utilized in the electrochemical quantification of MET.

Real samples preparation

Wastewater was collected from the treatment plant on the premises of Ming Chi University of Technology (MCUT). Both pond and river water were sampled from local sources near MCUT. Tap water was obtained from MCUT. The preliminary step involved centrifuging (4000 rpm) the collected water samples to separate any undissolved materials. Following this, the samples were filtered using Whatman filter paper to remove tiny dust particles. An equal amount of Millipore water was further added to the samples, and the samples were used for further investigations.

Results and discussion

XRD pattern of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite

An XRD study was performed to explore the crystalline nature of the synthesized Bi2S3, f-MWCNT, and the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite (Fig. 1a). The recorded XRD pattern of sonochemically synthesized Bi2S3 revealed prominent peaks at around 15.79°, 17.61°, 22.38°, 25.59°, 27.32°, 28.70°, 31.82°, and 33.22° in 2θ values, corresponding to the (020), (120), (220), (130), (021), (211), (221), and (311) crystal planes. As the peak around 10° in the Bi2S3 nanorods, it is often indicative of periodic inter-rod spacing, layered assembly, or lamellar ordering in nanostructured morphologies resulting in low-angle diffraction. This phenomenon has been noted in various nanorod systems where ordered packing or surface-modified stacking introduces additional diffraction at small angles [49,50]. The diffraction pattern obtained matches the standard reference for orthorhombic Bi2S3 (JCPDS card No. 17-0320) [49], ensuring the presence of a crystalline phase. The sharp and strong peaks signify that the ultrasound stabilized the Bi2S3 crystals while promoting the formation of nanorods. During sonication, the formation and collapse of tiny bubbles generated acoustic cavitation and produced short-lived hot and pressurized regions. These transient microenvironments enhanced mass transport, accelerated nucleation and influenced the growth of Bi2S3 nanorods. In addition, the XRD pattern of f-MWCNT featured a broad peak around 26.7°, which aligned with the (002) plane representative of graphitic carbon and peak observed around 42.5° in your f-MWCNT sample can be attributed to the (100) and (101) plane reflection of graphitic carbon, typically present in multi-walled carbon nanotubes, including their functionalized derivatives, and signifies the in-plane arrangement of the hexagonal carbon lattice [51]. Upon the development of the Bi2S3 and f-MWCNT, the XRD pattern unveiled the existence of both Bi2S3 and f-MWCNT peaks, revealing the successful integration of the two materials. A subtle reduction in peak intensity and peak broadening was noted in the composite, pointing to a nanoscale confinement due to the waves of the ultrasonication in the solvent, producing the localized hotspots and microjets resulting in a strong interaction between Bi2S3 and f-MWCNT without affecting the structural order of the semiconductor phase. The orthorhombic crystal structure of Bi2S3, as shown in Fig. 1b, is classified under the Pbnm space group, with unit cell measurements noted as a = 11.149 Å, b = 11.304 Å, and c = 3.981 Å, and the value of α = β = γ = 90°. Furthermore, the Insta Nano software was used to determine the average crystallite size of the Bi2S3 nanorods. This was determined on the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the most intense peak, and the approximate size was calculated at 57.93 nm. The ultrasonically prepared nanosized Bi2S3 rods and f-MWCNT promote rapid electron flow and large surface area [52].

FT-IR spectra of Bi2S3 nanorods, MWCNT, f-MWCNT and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite

The FTIR spectra of Bi2S3, pristine MWCNT, f-MWCNT, and the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite are presented in Fig. 1c to confirm the surface functional groups and interaction among the components. The pristine Bi2S3 spectrum displays well-defined vibrational bands at 560, 611, and 862 cm−1, which are attributed to characteristic bending and stretching modes of Bi–S bonds. These features are in agreement with the orthorhombic crystal structure of Bi2S3, as documented in earlier research [53]. The FTIR spectrum of MWCNT displayed a characteristic absorption band at 1622 cm−1, attributed to the stretching vibrations of C=C double bonds, indicative of the conjugated structure of the nanotube sidewalls. Upon acid functionalization, the broad absorption band near 3632 cm−1 is attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations characteristic of hydroxyl groups, while the peak at 1743 cm−1 indicates the presence of carboxylic (C=O) functionalities, confirming successful acid functionalization of MWCNT. Additionally, peaks observed at 2928 cm−1 and 2861 cm−1 correspond to C–H stretching vibrations, suggesting a methylene group formed at structural defect points created by acid oxidation [54]. The sonication process plays a crucial role in enhancing dispersion and interfacial interaction with Bi2S3. In the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite, characteristic bands of both components are present, confirming the coexistence of Bi–S and acid and hydroxyl groups. Moreover, a noticeable shift and intensity change in the O–H and C=O bands suggest strong interfacial bonding or possible coordination interactions between Bi3+ and oxygen-containing groups on f-MWCNT, affirming successful nanocomposite formation.

BET

Material porousness and surface area parameters can be assessed using Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET). The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and surface area characteristics of sonochemically synthesized Bi2S3 and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT are represented in Fig. 1(d–e). The adsorption isotherm of Bi2S3 and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT was identified as a type IV pattern with an H3 hysteresis loop, this results from the combined effect of Bi2S3 nanorods and the tubular framework of f-MWCNT. Additionally, the ultrasonication process improves pore accessibility and exposes more active sites by preventing particle clumping and helping to create a well-dispersed, interconnected porous network. The Bi2S3 nanorods offers a specific surface area of 7.323 m2/g, which increases to 79.100 m2/g upon forming the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite due to the cavitation-induced uniform dispersion and aggregation prevention. The synergistic combination of f-MWCNT and Bi2S3 resulted in a 10.80-fold rise in surface area compared to Bi2S3, providing the most availability of active sites and enhancing the electrocatalytic performance. Based on the BJH data, the pore diameters of the synthesized Bi2S3 and f-MWCNT are primarily within the 2–50 nm range, confirming their mesoporous nature [55,56]. The existence of mesopores facilitates greater diffusion pathways and active site exposure, which enhances the interaction of MET molecules with the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite and improves the electrochemical detection performance.

XPS analysis of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT

The chemical states and elemental composition of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT were characterized in detail through XPS analysis. The survey spectra prove the existence of Bi, S, C, and O in the material (Fig. 1f). The chemical state of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT was examined by analyzing the deconvoluted Bi 4f, S 2p, O 1s, and C 1s spectra. The XPS signal for Bi 4f shows two spin–orbit-split doublet peaks at binding energies of 163.3 eV (Bi 4f) and 157.9 eV (Bi 4f), corresponding to J values of 5/2 and 7/2 (Fig. 1g). These Bi 4f binding energies are indicative of the Bi3+ oxidation state in the core-level spectra. The S 2p spin–orbit doublet overlaps with the Bi 4f spectral region. To distinguish the overlapping regions, the S 2p core-level spectrum is shown between the Bi 4f spectral range and the S 2p region displays two asymmetric peaks (S 2p1/2 and S 2p3/2) (Fig. 1g). The signal noted at close to 161.9 eV is attributed to S 2p1/2, while the peak at 160.7 eV with regards to the S 2p3/2 spin state. Therefore, it verified the existence of sulfur in the S2− valence state in the composite [23,57]. The deconvolution of the high-resolution O 1s spectrum exhibited two peaks near to 530.2 eV and 531.7 eV (Fig. 1h). The peak at 530.2 eV was attributed to the metal–oxygen lattice, and the peak at 531.7 eV was attributed to hydroxyl groups (Arising from the water molecules in the lattice or presence of moisture) [57,58]. Fig. 1i depicts a high-resolution C 1s spectrum with three well-defined peaks at binding energies of 284.8 eV, 285.81 eV, and 288.29 eV and these peaks are indicative of C=C, C–S, and O–C=O bindings, respectively. It is fascinating to observe that the shoulder peak was fitted at 285.81 eV, suggesting that a C–S linkage developed due to the existence of Bi2S3 and f-MWCNT. This observation further supports the conclusion that ultrasonically prepared Bi2S3 nanorods are chemically anchored to the surface of f-MWCNT [59]. This sonochemical process not only accelerates the nucleation of Bi2S3 nanorods but also ensures their strong covalent anchoring via C–S bonds, as evidenced by XPS, leading to improved structural stability and electrochemical performance.

FE-SEM and HR-TEM analysis of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT

A morphological evaluation of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite was performed using Field Emision-scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). The FE-SEM image confirms the development of Bi2S3 nanorods with a distinct rod-like formation (Fig. 2a). Fig. 2b reveals the structural form of f-MWCNT, showing their densely entangled fibrous network. The efficient incorporation of Bi2S3 nanorods onto the f-MWCNT framework is shown in Fig. 2c. At its core, the whole synthesis process was powered by ultrasonication, where strong acoustic streams and particle collisions helped Bi2S3 form evenly and grow into nanorods. Plus, the shear forces and microjets from the ultrasound ensured the efficient dispersion also the strong immobilization of Bi2S3 onto the f-MWCNT. This structural framework produced by ultrasonication would significantly enhance the electron transfer and electrocatalytic performance through the synergistic interplay between Bi2S3 and f-MWCNT. During the synthesis process, some level of agglomeration among the Bi2S3 nanorods was observed. This is likely due to the high surface energy and reactivity of the freshly formed nanorods, which tend to interact via van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, or even chemical bridging. Such agglomeration is commonly observed in nanostructures with high aspect ratios and small particle sizes, where steric hindrance is insufficient to prevent clustering [60]. The elemental composition of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT has been verified through the brighter spots detected in the group (Fig. 2d) and individual elemental mapping images acquired via FE-SEM. The detected elements are bismuth (Bi) (Fig. S1a), sulfur (S) (Fig. S1b), carbon (C) (Fig. S1c), and oxygen (O) (Fig. S1d), which correspond to the proposed components of the composite material. The percentage composition of elements in the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite was characterized through Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) analysis from FE-SEM (Fig. 2e). The mass percentages of the elements in the composite were computed as follows: Bi (22.7 %), S (26.2 %), C (41.0 %), and O (16.2 %). Furthermore, the fabricated composite was utilized for electrochemical applications.

The TEM analysis offered compelling insights into the nanostructural architecture of the synthesized Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composite, synthesized via an ultrasonication-assisted approach. The low-magnification images show well-dispersed Bi2S3 nanorods intimately interfaced with the functionalized MWCNT (Fig. 2f), forming a cohesive hybrid network. Ultrasonication played a pivotal role in this integration process by generating localized cavitation forces and acoustic effects, which not only enhanced the dispersion of nanostructures but also promoted uniform anchoring of Bi2S3 onto the carbon nanotube surfaces. These high-energy acoustic waves helped prevent agglomeration while facilitating efficient interfacial contact between the two components. Medium-resolution TEM clearly reveals this tight binding, suggesting an electronically favorable interface that contributes to improved conductivity (Fig. 2g). HR-TEM further confirms the crystalline structure of Bi2S3, showing distinct lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.351 nm, indexed to the (130) plane of orthorhombic Bi2S3 (Fig. 2h). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns exhibit well-defined diffraction rings corresponding to the (130), (211), (221), and (311) planes, indicating a polycrystalline nature (Fig. 2i). By employing a sonochemical approach, a well-integrated structure is formed that simultaneously increases the electroactive surface area and improves electron transport properties essential for achieving excellent electrochemical efficiency during MET oxidation.

Electrochemical assessments of sonication-derived Bi2S3/f-MWCNT modified GCE

The sensing competence of an electrochemical sensor is significantly influenced by the specific surface area of the electrode. The combination of good electrical conductivity, large surface area, and high catalytic activity in Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE results in excellent sensing performance against analytes. The EIS approach is applied to explore changes in charge transfer resistance (Rct) and the interfacial dynamics between the modified GCE and the electrolyte. Using the EIS technique, the charge transfer properties of bare GCE, Bi2S3, f-MWCNT, Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(G)/GCE and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(S)/GCE were analyzed (Fig. 3a). The EIS analysis was conducted with an amplitude of 0.005 V and a frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz in a redox solution of 0.05 M [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− and 0.1 M KCl. Both bare and modified GCE exhibit a semicircular region in the high-frequency area of the Nyquist plot, corresponding to the Rct of the electrode. The Rct values obtained from EIS analysis were 326, 179, 96, 27 and 23 Ω for bare GCE, Bi2S3, f-MWCNT, Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(G)/GCE and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(S)/GCE respectively (Fig. 3b). The sonication-prepared Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(S)/GCE showed the lowest Rct (23 Ω), as a result of its improved electron transfer efficiency and catalytic activity over the grinding-based method and other modified electrodes. The enhanced performance of the sonicated nanocomposite can be attributed to the high-energy interfacial reaction between Bi2S3 nanoparticles and f-MWCNT induced by rapid nucleation. Moreover, sonication enhanced the improved ion diffusion pathways owing to the amorphous crystalline phase hybridization during the composite formation.

Applying Rct measurements, the electron transfer rate constant for bare and modified electrodes was determined with the defined equation provided here (Eqn (1)) [61].

The terms are defined as: T (temperature in Kelvin), R (universal gas constant), F (Faraday constant), Rct (charge transfer resistance) and Co (redox probe concentration). Here, n = 1 represents the number of electrons transferred in the redox reaction involving the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− probe. The electron transfer rate constant (Ket) values calculated for bare GCE, Bi2S3, f-MWCNT, Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(G)/GCE and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(S)/GCE are 1.63 × 10−7, 2.97 × 10−7, 5.54 × 10−7, 1. 97 × 10−6 and 2.31 × 10−6, respectively. CV measurements for both unmodified and modified GCE were conducted within a potential range of −0.2 to 0.6 V, applying a scan rate of 0.05 V/s in a solution with 0.005 M [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− and 0.1 M KCl. Relative to the bare GCE, the modified electrodes of the Bi2S3, f-MWCNT, Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(G)/GCE and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(S)/GCE exhibit a greater anodic current (Ipa) response. The current response of the sonochemically prepared Bi2S3/f-MWCNT-modified GCE is 1.53-fold markedly improved over the bare GCE (Fig. 3c and d).

Additionally, the electroactive surface area of the electrodes was determined using the Randles-Sevcik equation (Eqn. (2)) [62].

In this equation, C denotes the concentration of the redox solution (in mol cm−3), A represents the effective surface area, n signifies the number of electrons transferred during the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− redox reaction (where n = 1 for K3[Fe(CN)6] 3−/4−) and D is the diffusion coefficient (7.6 × 10−6 cm2 s−1), and n signifies the number of electrons transferred during the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− redox reaction (where n = 1 for K3[Fe(CN)6] 3−/4−). The electroactive surface area values were determined to be 0.0685, 0.0844, 0.0920, 0.099 and 0.105 cm2 for bare GCE, Bi2S3, f-MWCNT, Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(G)/GCE and Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(S)/GCE, respectively. Driven by strong hydrodynamic forces during sonochemistry, the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT(S)-modified GCE shows pronounced electronic coupling, leading to a higher electrode active surface area and increased Ipa in CV, which confirms its outstanding electrocatalytic capability.

CV analysis was carried out to study the redox characteristics of modified GCE containing composite ratios of 1:1, 2:1, and 1:2, at a potential range of − 0.2 to 0.6 V and a scan rate of 0.05 V/s, in a 0.005 M potassium ferri/ferrocyanide solution with 0.1 M KCl (Fig. S2). A Various composite ratio was synthesized in accordance with the procedure detailed in experimental section 2.4 methodology. Among all examined composite ratios, the 1:2 ratio exhibits the higher Ipa response. Electrochemical characterization of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE was performed through CV various scans at 10 – 100 mV/s in a 0.005 M potassium ferri/ferrocyanide redox medium containing 0.1 M KCl (Fig. 3e). The current responses for both anodic and cathodic processes increase linearly across scan rates of 10 to 100 mV/s, with no significant deviations. The linear relationship dependence of Ipc/Ipa on (ν)1/2 (Fig. 3f), confirms that the electrochemical reaction at Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE is diffusion-controlled.

Effect of pH, different modified electrodes and different composite ratio analysis of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE for the detection of MET

The electrochemical performance of the sensor is significantly influenced by the pH level of the electrolyte solution. A CV study on the influence of pH was conducted for the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT-modified electrode in the presence of 100 μM MET at a scan rate of 50 mV/s (Fig. 4a). A gradual rise in current response is observed as the pH shifts from 3 to 7, attaining a maximum at pH 7 (Fig. 4b), after which it undergoes a significant decline as the pH is further elevated to 11. The reversible nature of the reaction is enhanced in an acidic medium, but an excessive proton concentration can negatively impact the oxidation potential of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT towards MET due to limited electron availability. An increased concentration of hydroxide ions at high pH levels reduces the reversibility of the reaction. The reduction in peak current at elevated pH levels is mainly due to the instability and dissolution of the catalyst. The oxidation of MET was affected due to the inherent protonation and deprotonation processes. The better change in the peak potential under varying pH conditions indicates that the electron transfer is properly facilitated at the modified GCE interface during electrochemical reactions. Based on the assessment, a pH of phosphate buffer (PB) 7.0 was chosen as an optimum electrolyte for further experiments. The linear regression equation describing the relationship between pH and potential (Fig. 4c), given as Epa = − 0.0495 (pH) + 0.4422 (R2 = 0.986), suggests the direct involvement of electrons and protons in the oxidation of MET. The obtained slope value of the linear plot is 50 mV/pH. It is almost closer to the theoretical Nernst value of 58 mV/pH as reported in the previous study [63,64].

Electrochemical mechanism of MET

The proposed mechanism for the electrochemical redox reaction of MET is shown in Fig. 4g. As determined by the molecular structure and electronic characteristics of MET, the compound exhibits a redox cycle wherein the amine group is repeatedly oxidized and reduced. The anode oxidation of MET generates an intermediate product, mainly involving the oxidation of the amine group through electron loss. Following oxidation, it can undergo cathodic reduction, accepting electrons to return to the original reduced form of MET. The redox mechanism of MET is mainly determined by the presence of −OH and amine −NH2 functional groups. These functional groups are involved in proton-coupled electron transfer processes, promoting electron mobility and accelerating redox kinetics. These functional groups improves the electrochemical reactivity of the molecule, leading to greater sensitivity and redox activity of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE and the interplay between these functional groups and the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite facilitates the selectivity and electrochemical response for MET detection.

Electroanalytical performance of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE

Fig. 6a demonstrates the DPV behavior of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE under varying MET concentrations spanning from 0.01–162 and 171–2100 μM at a scan rate of 0.05 V/s in 0.1 M PB. The Ipa scales proportionally with increasing MET concentration, confirming a linear dependence of the electrochemical response on the analyte concentration. The observed linear trend indicates that the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE nanocomposite improves the electrochemical redox efficiency of the electrode ensuring high sensitivity in detecting low MET concentrations. The sharp and distinct peaks observed in the DPV profiles indicate the exceptional sensitivity and efficient electron transfer kinetics enabled by the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE. This performance enhancement is caused by the further in-situ functionalization of MWCNT that occurs during the sonochemical fabrication of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT composites owing to the action of •OH and H• radicals formed via acoustic cavitation, introducing polar oxygenated groups (−OH, −COOH). The in-situ functionalization enables homogeneous nucleation and stable anchoring of Bi2S3 onto the MWCNT matrix, forming a well-integrated hybrid structure with enhanced electrocatalytic and electron transport properties. Further, the enhanced sensitivity and selectivity are attributed to the synergistic effect of π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding between the aromatic ring and −OH of MET and the f-MWCNT surface. Additionally, Bi2S3 contributes through Lewis acid-base interactions and potential coordination with MET’s −NH2 or −OH groups, promoting both charge transfer and diffusion at the electrode interface. The linear calibration plot in Fig. 6b illustrates the direct correlation between oxidation peak current and MET concentration with high precision. The Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE exhibits strong correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.9934 for lower concentrations and R2 = 0.9966 for higher concentrations), indicating precise quantitative analysis. The quantitative linear relationship between MET concentration and its corresponding responsive current is derived within the linear range of 0.01– 162 and 171– 2100 μM. The calculated sensitivity and LOD were found to be 10.514 μA μM−1 cm−2 and 6.52 nM, respectively. Anti-interference study

Investigating interference in MET detection was essential because real samples contained multiple electroactive compounds. The selectivity functioning of the sensor was evaluated by adding potential interferents into a 0.1 M PB with 100 μM MET. As observed in the DPV data (Fig. 7a), it is evident that no considerable oxidation peaks were noted upon the addition of various structural analogs and interfering species, including (1) Pb2+, (2) Hg2+, (3) Cl−, (4) uric acid, (5) ascorbic acid, (6) p-nitrotoluene, (7) bisphenol A, (8) Cu2+, (9) K+, (10) SO42−, (11) Na2+, (12) catechol (CC), and (13) hydroquinone (HQ), 7-fold higher than the MET concentration. It can be noted from Fig. 7b that the Ipa of MET remained unchanged even with the presence of electrochemically active small biological substances like ascorbic acid and uric acid. However, HQ produced a clear peak around 0.18 V, yet it had no interference on the peak current or peak potential of MET. Moreover, catechol exhibited the same potential as MET, resulting in a slightly broader peak without affecting the potential or current intensity of MET. The specificity of this system stems from the molecular interaction between the hydroxyl (−OH) and amine (−NH) groups in MET and the higher active sites of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE nanocomposite, which enhance the selective oxidation of the target species, and this high level of selectivity plays a vital role in ensuring suitability for real-world applications.

Reproducibility, repeatability, and stability of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT electrode

The reliability of the electrode was assessed by testing its reproducibility, repeatability, and stability. To evaluate reproducibility, we tested five individually prepared Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE electrodes under the same experimental conditions using CV in 100 μM of MET. The experimental findings are shown in Fig. S4a, which obtained a 94.97 % in peak current, confirming the high reproducibility of the system. Following ten consecutive measurements of MET using the same Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE, the peak current exhibited an RSD of 2.23 %, demonstrating excellent repeatability (Fig. S4b). As depicted in Fig. S4c, CV measurements were carried out on the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE every week. Even after three weeks, 92.44 % of its original peak current was maintained. Finally, the cyclic stability of the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE was examined in 0.1 M PB through CV measurements Fig. S4d. A well-defined anodic and cathodic response of MET was observed for the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE, with the current response showing only a 9 % deviation after 100 successive cycles. confirming excellent stability.

Spiked real-world sample analysis using wireless potentiostat

Electrochemical sensing via smart potentiostat has created new opportunities for on-site and real-time analysis [77]. The study of Bi2S3/f-MWCNT electrodes is fundamental to ensuring their reliability and efficiency in real-time applications. For this real-sample analysis, we chose four types of water sources, including river water (Fig. 7c), pond water (Fig. 7d), wastewater (Fig. 7e) and tap water (Fig. 7f). Experimental Section 2.7 provides specific details on the collection and purification processes of the real samples used in this application. The presence of MET in water samples was investigated through DPV analysis. A measurable current response was not detected in any of the four collected water samples, confirming the absence of MET. A known concentration of MET was added to water samples, and their significant current responses were analyzed (Supplementary Video S1). The measurement was repeated three times, and the data for the added and recovered sample concentrations along with the associated RSD values are provided in Table S1. The real sample recoveries were determined to be in the 96.4 % to 100.3 % range. The findings demonstrate that an electrode incorporating Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE is an effective electrocatalyst that advances the determination of MET in real-world industrial samples.

Conclusion

In summary, an enhanced electrochemical platform was constructed by loading ultrasonically synthesized Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite onto a GCE for the detection of the environmental toxin, MET. The ultrasonication efficiently generated cavitation-induced hydrodynamic forces and sonic oscillations, significantly aiding in the structural integrity and performance of the composite. Through specified characterization techniques, including FE-SEM, XRD, BET, CV, EIS, and DPV confirmed that the Bi2S3/f-MWCNT/GCE modification improves their conductivity, provides greater active sites, and faster electron transfer kinetics than the unmodified GCE. This simple and cost-effective sensing probe exhibited superior electrochemical performance, with a sensitivity of 10.514 μA μM−1 cm−2 and a LOD of 6.52 nM, linear range spanning 0.01–2100 μM for MET detection. It additionally demonstrated excellent selectivity, repeatability, reproducibility and stability over 20 days. The practical applicability of the fabricated electrochemical probe was examined through MET detection in multiple environmental samples and exhibited satisfactory recovery rates (96.4 %–100.3 %). Real-time observation of MET detection was tested with a portable potentiostat that was connected to a laptop interface. Therefore, addressing both environmental development and ecological harm remediation, the sonochemically Bi2S3/f-MWCNT nanocomposite serves as an effective electrochemical probe for the advancement of these crucial agendas.
