Actionable arrhythmias in low-risk patients with ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI): role for continued telemetry beyond 24–48 h
Authors: Thomas Lowe, Rama Mangipudi, Minyoung Kim, Daniel Baik, Haissam Haddad, Jay Shavadia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•With contemporary STEMI care, the need for prolonged telemetry for surveillance of an actionable arrhythmia is unclear.•Beyond the first 24-48 hours, the incident rate for an actionable arrhythmia is low.•Traditionally established prognostic variables in STEMI would likely guide triage for longer telemetry durations in STEMI.

Introduction

With contemporary reperfusion strategies for patients presenting with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), the downstream risk for actionable arrhythmias has substantially changed. Telemetry is often continued until hospital discharge, yet the contemporary incident rate and role for continued monitoring beyond the initial 24–48 high at-arrhythmia risk period is unclear. This study explores the risk for an “actionable arrhythmia” beyond the first 24–48 h of STEMI presentation.

Methods

From June 2022 to February 2024, we evaluated consecutive “low risk” STEMI presentations for an actionable arrhythmia beyond the first 48 h of presentation, defined as occurrence of any one of the following: >3 second asystole; high grade block; ventricular fibrillation; >15 beats ventricular tachycardia; atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response; and >15 beats supraventricular tachycardia. Telemetry data was collected daily from alarm monitors at the cardiac care unit or cardiology ward, or from patient's charts and electronic medical records when telemetry data was unavailable. Informed written consent was obtained from all patients and the study was approved by the research ethics board University of Saskatchewan (REB ID 3399). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies, continuous variables as medians (25th, 75th percentiles).

Results

Of the overall 386 STEMI presentations, 302 STEMI patients were included for analysis (n = 84 excluded [cardiac arrest/shock = 78; declined/unable to obtain consent = 6]). Baseline patient characteristics are described in Table 1. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention was the most commonly used reperfusion strategy and anterior/anterolateral infarcts were the most common infarct territory. Heart failure was present in 4.3 % of patients and the median in-hospital left ventricular ejection fraction was 50 % (43.4, 56.3). Median time from first medical contact to primary PCI was 2.08 h (1.30, 5.74) for primary PCI treated patients and 5.78 h (4.08, 10.7) among pharmacoinvasive patients, respectively. An actionable arrhythmia occurred in 0.99 % (n = 3/302) patients within 48 h of admission (asystole >3 s, n = 1; high grade atrioventricular block, n = 1; atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular rate, n = 1). Beyond 48-h post reperfusion (day 3), an actionable arrhythmia occurred in 1.99 % (n = 6/302) patients, two of whom had had an actionable arrhythmia within 48 h (asystole >3 s, n = 2; high grade atrioventricular block, n = 5; ventricular fibrillation, n = 1; >15 beats of ventricular tachycardia, n = 2). Patients with an actionable arrhythmia beyond 48 h of admission had more likely presented with an anterior territory infarct, heart failure and had longer ischemic times.

Conclusion

The risk of an actionable arrhythmia both within the first 24–48 h and beyond this “high at-arrhythmia risk period” is very low. Routine telemetry for all STEMI patients particularly beyond 48 h appears of low value and our findings suggest using prognostically recognized variables in STEMI (such as anterior infarction, longer ischemia times, and heart failure at presentation) to guide patient selection needing longer telemetry durations beyond the first 24–48 h.
