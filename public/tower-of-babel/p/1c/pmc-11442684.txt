Investigating the impact of shear and bulk viscosity on the damping of confined acoustic modes in phononic crystal sensors
Authors: Hussein A Elsayed, Ashour M Ahmed, Haifa E Alfassam, Ali Hajjiah, Wail Al Zoubi, Mostafa R Abukhadra, Ahmed Mehaney
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhononic crystal (PnC) sensors are recognized for their capability to control acoustic wave propagation through periodic structures, presenting considerable potential across various applications. Despite advancements, the effects of fluid viscosity on PnC performance remain intricate and inadequately understood. This study theoretically investigates the influence of shear (dynamic) and bulk viscosity on acoustic wave damping in defective one-dimensional phononic crystal (1D PnC) sensors designed for detecting liquid analytes. Acetic acid with varying viscosities is considered to fill a cavity layer intermediated by a multilayer stack of lead and epoxy. The effects of dynamic and bulk viscosity on the resonance characteristics of the defective mode were analyzed. Numerical results reveal that increased dynamic viscosity leads to substantial broadening and decreased intensity of resonance peaks, accompanied by a shift to higher frequencies due to enhanced elastic wave attenuation and damping. At low dynamic viscosity (η = 0.2 ηd), numerous resonance peaks with varying intensities are observed. However, at higher viscosities (η = 2.0 ηd to η = 10.0 ηd), only one prominent peak appears in the spectrum. The intensity of this resonant peak starts at 98% for η = 2 ηd and decreases to 58.8% as the dynamic viscosity increases to η = 10 ηd. Additionally, the combined effect of dynamic and bulk viscosity introduces further damping, causing a strong shift of the resonance peak to higher frequencies, along with an increase in the full width at half maximum (FWHM) and a decrease in the quality factor (QF). These findings emphasize the necessity of incorporating both shear and bulk viscosity in the design of PnC sensors to enhance their sensitivity and accuracy in practical applications. This theoretical framework provides critical insights for optimizing sensor performance and bridging gaps between theoretical predictions and experimental observations, especially in 1D PnCs, offering potential solutions to challenges in real-world PnC sensor applications.Keywords: Phononic crystal, Damping effect, Viscosity, Defect resonance mode, Liquid, Transmission intensitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

In the last three decades, the inclusion of phononic crystal (PnC) structures received profound contributions in many physical, biomedical, and engineering applications. PnC are capable of a significant control on the response of the propagating elastic and acoustic waves1–5. This control could be introduced across the appearance of some stop frequency bands in one, two or three dimensions named phononic band gaps (PnBGs)2,3. In particular, the presence of these stop bands is strongly dependent on the mismatching in the mechanical properties of PnCs’ constructing materials1–3. Notably, PnCs are artificial micro periodic structures in which each unit cell could be designed by stacking of two, or three materials of relatively different acoustic properties6,7. Meanwhile, PnCs became the mainstay for many applications such as, demultiplexers8, energy harvesting5, elastic insulators9, filters10, acoustic signal processing11, detectors12, and logic gates13. Interestingly, PnC structures have been introduced in an exciting way through many chemical and biomedical sensing and characterization processes as well. Notably, the nature of many fluids is strongly sensitive to some external parameters like temperature, concentration, and viscosity which, in turn, could have some significant variations in their densities and/or sound velocities6. In this regard, many researchers have demonstrated the inclusion of chemical and biomedical fluids as a cavity or a defect layer inside PnCs for sensing and monitoring of these fluids14. In particular, the localization of acoustic energy through these fluids at some distinct frequencies named resonant modes is strongly expected6. Then, sharp peaks could be generated inside the PnBG of the designed PnC structure due to the excitation of the cavity eigenmode. Interestingly, these sharp peaks are extremely sensitive to the densities and/or sound velocities of these fluids, which, in turn, makes their detection of a great performance to some extent15. Based on this strategy, many of chemical and biomedical components such as Hydrogen peroxide, Glycine, Vinegar, biodiesel fuels, Fluidic Acids, and heavy metals have been detected using one, two or three dimensions PnC sensors14–24. In this context, T. Y. Fang et. al. proposed a two-dimensional PnC design to detect the concentration of acetone in water14. The suggested design could be of significant interest due to its high sensitivity that reached to 80 Hz for acetone concentration change in the range 1–9.1%.

Moreover, Khaligh et al. demonstrated the emergence of locally resonant modes through a two-dimensional PnC structure comprising a waveguide for Vinegar detection15. Interestingly, the proposed design provided an acceptable performance in the vicinity of a relatively high-quality factor of 40,000. In addition, Khateib et al. have introduced a simple design based on a one-dimensional defective PnC for monitoring of Glycine concentration20. The suggested design investigated an excellent value of sensitivity that could reach over 969 kHz through a small molar ratio of glycine from 0 to 0.383 mol/L, besides its low-cost materials and the ease of fabrication as well. Furthermore, Oseev et al. have experimentally demonstrated a novel chemical sensor based of a 2D PC structure for measuring the octane number of gasoline blends and its physical properties as well22. In addition, one-dimensional PnC structures have been included in many different and novel designs for the detection of heavy metals in pure water due to their ability to monitor very low concentrations in ppm and ppb domains23,24.

However, most of the aforementioned chemical and biosensors designs are lacking for the sufficient compatibility with the manufacturing ones. In fact, the absence of compatibility between these theoretical designs and their experimental counterparts could be due to the negligence of some other influences such as tolerance, viscosity and acoustic losses as well6,25. In this regard, M. Zaremanesh et al. proved that the viscosity possesses a great effect on the transmission properties of a 2D triangular PnC biosensor for the detection of the temperature of the Methyl Nonafluorobutyl Ether6. The investigation found that both the shape and intensity of the transmittance spectrum are strongly affected by the consideration of both longitudinal and shear viscosity. Additionally, the characteristics of the cavity resonant modes are highly sensitive to viscosities, which can lead to changes in the quality factor of the sensor6. Moreover, Lucklum et al. have explored the role of viscosity on the performance of PnC sensors25. In this regard, they designed and fabricated 1D and 2D PnC structures, and concluded that the liquid viscosity is a crucial parameter for acoustic energy dissipation, and must be considered to verify the correlation between the sensor design and its performance. Additionally, Gueddida et al. have both theoretically and experimentally considered the dramatic effect of liquid viscosity, specifically the shear viscosity, on the performance of a tubular PnC sensor26. They fabricated and characterized a test sample of a tubular PnC structure comprising a polymer. Interestingly, the comparison between the experimental measurements and the numerical simulations demonstrated the necessity of considering the damping of the polymer due to its significant effects on the transmittance properties and the sensing performance.

Furthermore, both the shear and bulk viscosities of the considered liquid decreased the quality factor of the designed sensor. Therefore, the viscosity of the liquids presents an important parameter that could lead to some significant effects on the performance of any introduced sensor and must be efficiently studied specially through 1D PnCs designs. Currently, most previous 1D PnC sensors ignored the effect of losses and especially viscosity effect on the performance of PnC sensors and resonant peak transmittance. Therefore, we have considered in this research paper the role of viscosity on a one-dimensional PnC designed sensor. Notably, most of the previous studies have essentially introduced the topic without considering the impact of viscosity on the acoustical characteristics of these structures. Therefore, the novelty of the present study primarily lies in the consideration of the role of viscosity on the transmittance characteristics of the designed 1D PnC structure. In this regard, we believe that our study could be of promising interest towards achieving a good concurrence between the theoretical aspects and the real-world environment, especially through sensing and detection applications. Notably, the viscosity can have a significant effect on the intensity and position of the emerged resonant modes, which in turn may lead to a distinct effect on the performance of the designed sensors. Thus, in this paper, we have considered the role of viscosity on the performance of our 1D PnC sensor. As an example of fluids, our sensor is designed for the detection of vinegar (acetic acid) considering the viscosity properties of this liquid. Here, the choice of the 1D PnC structure is due to the low cost, simplicity in fabrications and absence of such a study in most previous researches of 1D PnCs.

This study presents a novel theoretical investigation into the impact of viscosity on acoustic wave behavior in defective one-dimensional phononic crystal (1D PnC) sensors. Unlike previous research that has largely overlooked these factors, this work demonstrates how increasing viscosity significantly broadens and attenuates resonance peaks, thereby affecting both the quality factor and damping rate. By bridging the gap between theoretical models and practical applications, this research enhances sensitivity and accuracy in complex fluid environments, providing a critical foundation for experimental design. The structure analyzed consists of four unit cells made of lead and epoxy, with a central defect layer filled with acetic acid. Using the transfer matrix method (TMM) and considering the acoustic properties of the PnC materials, our numerical results reveal the emergence of a resonant mode within the PnBG. The study examines the influence of both dynamic and bulk viscosities on the position and intensity of this resonant mode, offering insights into the sensor's performance in terms of quality factor and damping rate, particularly in relation to the viscosities of acetic acid. The results show that viscosity introduces further damping, causing a pronounced shift in the resonance peak, an increase in the full width at half maximum, and a decrease in the quality factor. These findings contribute to optimizing sensor performance, addressing real-world challenges, and advancing the development of more effective PnC-based sensing technologies.

Result and discussion

Transmission spectrum

The transmission spectrum is crucial for understanding and analyzing the behavior of acoustic waves in PnC structures. It offers valuable insights into the interaction between acoustic waves and the materials within the structure. The transmission spectra of both perfect and defective 1D PnCs are depicted in Fig. 2.

Transmission spectra of (a) a perfect PnC, (b) a defective PnC filled with water of dynamic viscosity ηd=1×10-3 Pa·s, and (c) acetic acid of dynamic viscosity ηd=1.211×10-3 Pa·s.

The perfect 1D PnC is composed of four-unit cells. Each cell consists of alternating layers of lead and epoxy with 1.0- and 0.5-mm thicknesses, respectively. This configuration represents a pristine PnC structure without any central liquid analyte layer; denoted as [lead/epoxy]4. The millimeter size of the lattice parameter corresponds to the MHz frequency range, which is commonly used in PnC sensors6. The transmission spectrum is plotted against the normalized frequency ωa/2πcep, where ω is the angular frequency, a is the unit cell size, and cep is the speed of sound in epoxy. The spectrum of the [lead/epoxy]4 configuration highlights a phononic band gap (PnBG) formed by the interference of acoustic waves at the interfaces within the PnC structure31. This band gap ranges from normalized frequencies 0.2–0.55, encompassing a bandwidth of 0.35 as seen in Fig. 2a. The significant contrast in mechanical properties between the lead and epoxy layers plays a crucial role in establishing this wide bandwidth32.

By inserting a defective layer in the center of the PnC structure, it becomes configured as [(lead/epoxy)2/defective layer/(lead/epoxy)2]. The defective layer incorporates a liquid analyte with a thickness of 1 mm. Figure 2b,c depict the transmission spectra of the defective PnC structure filled with two different liquids. Compared with the perfect 1D PnC, the PnBG of the defective 1D PnC structure becomes wider and exhibits a lower initial forbidden frequency. Additionally, the defect cavity introduces a characteristic discontinuity in the bandgap, resulting in a resonance peak mode within the PnBG. This resonance peak arises due to the disrupted periodicity caused by the defective layer. The cavity within the PnC structure confines and localizes acoustic energy at a specific resonant frequency33. At this resonant frequency, acoustic waves constructively interfere, creating standing wave patterns that significantly enhance the interaction between the acoustic waves and liquid.

Figure 2b illustrates the transmission when water (with a dynamic viscosity ηd=1×10-3 Pa s) fills the defective cavity. The resonance peak appears within the PnBG at a frequency of 6.1659 × 105 Hz, exhibiting a high intensity of 95.1%. Conversely, Fig. 2c shows the transmission spectrum when acetic acid (with a dynamic viscosity ηd=1.211×10-3 Pa s) occupies the defective cavity. The resulting resonance peak shifts to a new frequency position at 5.2156 × 105 Hz, with an intensity of 95.0%. These observed changes in peak position and intensity are influenced by the density, sound velocity and lastly viscosity of both liquids. In what follow; we will maintain the type and concentration of the liquid ( so the density and sound speed are fixed) but examine the always ingnored effect in most of previous theoretical sensors; viscosity effect on the transmission properties of this confined peak.

Effect of dynamic viscosity

Viscosity is a critical property of liquids and serves as an independent measurement value. High-viscosity fluids are more difficult to flow compared with low-viscosity fluids. Viscosity can be classified into bulk and shear viscosity. Shear viscosity reflects only translational molecular motion, while bulk viscosity accounts for the relaxation of both vibrational and rotational degrees of molecular freedom34. In a liquid environment, viscosity amplifies loss mechanisms, leading to greater acoustic energy dissipation. Both dynamic and bulk viscosity control acoustic attenuation35,36. Consequently, the position and amplitude of resonance modes are directly linked to the liquid's viscosity37. Therefore, accounting for viscous damping in acoustic systems is essential for developing applications involving mechanical resonators in fluid environments38. The behavior of viscosity is incorporated through the general description of acoustic wave propagation in a compressible Newtonian fluid. Figure 3 demonstrates the impact of varying dynamic viscosity (ηd) of acetic acid on the acoustic behavior of a defective 1D PnC structure. There are notable differences observed when comparing the transmission spectra at different dynamic viscosities.

Transmission spectra at defective 1D PnC filled with acetic acid at (a) dynamic viscosity η=0.2ηd Pa. s, (b) dynamic viscosity η=0.5ηd Pa. s, and (c) different values of dynamic viscosity ηd.

At very low viscosity (η = 0.2 ηd), Fig. 3a shows numerous resonance peaks distributed across the entire frequency spectrum with varying intensities. These peaks result from the constructive interference of acoustic waves creating standing wave patterns within the defective layer. The resonances on the left side of the curves in Fig. 3a exhibit high transmittance, approaching 1. In contrast, more resonances appear on the right side of the curves as the frequency increases, but their intensity decreases. This reduction in intensity is due to increased attenuation and loss with higher frequencies40,41. At low frequencies, acoustic waves experience minimal damping and exhibit strong resonance modes. However, as the frequency increases, damping effects become more pronounced, leading to weaker resonance peaks and greater energy dissipation.

In contrast, at medium viscosity (η = 0.5 ηd), Fig. 3b shows fewer resonance peaks with higher intensities compared with Fig. 3a. This change reflects increased energy dissipation and enhanced damping effects as viscosity rises, resulting in less distinct resonance peaks and more significant energy loss within the PnC structure.

Figure 3c shows how increasing viscosity values from 2.0 ηd to η = 10.0 ηd affect the transmission spectrum. Only one peak appears in the spectrum. The inset in Fig. 3c highlights a rightward shift in peak positions as viscosity increases. Additionally, there is an increase in peak broadening and a reduction in its intensity. Acoustic waves travel by compressing and decompressing the medium they travel through. The velocity of acoustic waves is influenced by the mechanical properties of the liquid, such as density, bulk modulus, and viscosity. Higher viscosity causes more acoustic energy to be converted into heat as the waves travel through the medium, leading to increased energy losses. Energy losses are particularly significant at the interfacial liquid/solid boundary, often referred to as the interface layer boundary. The shear displacement between the solid surface and the liquid results in substantial energy dissipation25. This dissipation arises from the frictional forces generated as liquid molecules move relative to the solid surface, converting mechanical energy into heat. The internal friction opposes the motion of acoustic waves, diminishing the quality of compressional motion. Consequently, greater internal friction slightly reduces the speed of acoustic waves through the medium, leading to attenuation and damping over distance. Shear viscosity significantly impacts the amplitude and intensity of resonant peaks by introducing damping within the liquid6. This damping reduces the amplitude of the resonance peaks. Additionally, the damping losses cause a red shift in the spectra42. The peaks also become more broadened due to higher energy losses within the medium. Moreover, higher viscosity results in fewer resonant modes. A broader resonance peak indicates a lower quality factor (QF) of the resonant mode. The QF measures the ratio of energy stored to energy lost. A high QF in a cavity resonance signifies high accuracy and resolution in detecting changes within the structure. This elevated QF enables precise measurement of the resonant peak frequency, ensuring that successive peaks in the transmission spectrum are distinctly separated. A lower QF signifies greater damping and less effective resonance in energy storage. The quality factor of the peak mode in a cavity is significantly influenced by the dynamic viscosity of the liquid 26. Higher dynamic viscosity increases damping, which reduces the QF by broadening the resonance peaks and diminishing their intensity. The color gradient indicates that the amplitude of the traveling mode decreases with increasing dynamic viscosity, and the resonant peak position shifts towards higher frequencies. The intensity of the resonant mode diminishes due to increased energy losses within the medium. Figure 4b shows that the FWHM of the resonance peak increases with dynamic viscosity, demonstrating the broadening effect of higher viscosity on resonance peaks. The peaks become broader, especially when approaching the edges of the PnBG. These results illustrate a very strong effect of viscosity on the resonance characteristics. At a dynamic viscosity of η=2ηd, the resonant peak position is approximately 7.5482 × 105 Hz with an amplitude of 98%. Effect of both shear and bulk viscosity

In an acoustic system, both dynamic viscosity and bulk viscosity affect acoustic damping. This subsection examines the combined effects of dynamic and bulk viscosity of acetic acid on wave propagation within the PnC. Figure 5 compares the transmission spectra of a defective 1D PnC cavity filled with acetic acid, analyzing the effects of dynamic viscosity alone (Fig. 5a) versus the effects of both dynamic and bulk viscosity (Fig. 5b). The dynamic viscosity is ηd = 1.211 × 10–3 Pa·s, while the bulk viscosity is ηb = 2.48 × 10–3 Pa·s. As indicated in Table 2, the higher bulk viscosity significantly affects the resonant peak's properties. Specifically, the amplitude of the resonant peak decreases to 95.8% when both viscosities are considered, compared with 96.3% with only dynamic viscosity. Bulk viscosity contributes to the damping of acoustic waves by causing energy dissipation during the compression and expansion cycles within the structure. This leads to a reduction in wave amplitude as the waves propagate, resulting in increased overall energy dissipation. Additionally, the resonant peak shifts to a higher frequency range, moving from 5.2228 × 105 Hz to 8.3862 × 105 Hz. This shift is attributed to the bulk viscosity's more pronounced effect on high-frequency acoustics. With both dynamic and bulk viscosities present, the resonant peak broadens, indicating higher energy losses and reduced efficiency of the resonant mode. The quality factor (QF) and damping rate (ζ) can be given by QF= and ζ=0.5QF, respectively 43. The broadening of the peak is associated with a decrease in the quality factor (QF) from 1933.4 to 210.1541 and an increase in the damping rate from 2.586 × 10–4 to 24 × 10–4. Furthermore, the band gap width diminishes when both viscosities are considered, suggesting a narrower range of frequencies over which the PnC effectively blocks acoustic waves. This reduction is attributed to the additional damping introduced by bulk viscosity. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating both dynamic and bulk viscosity into the design and analysis of PnC-based devices, especially for applications requiring precise control over resonant frequencies and amplitudes.

Conclusion

This study theoretically explored the effects of shear and bulk viscosity on acoustic wave behavior in defective one-dimensional phononic crystal (1D PnC) sensors. The primary emphasis is on understanding the relationship between sensor design and performance, rather than on the optimization parameters of the sensor. The results indicated that increasing dynamic viscosity leads to significant broadening and attenuation of resonance peaks within the PnC structure. At lower viscosities, multiple resonance peaks with varying intensities are observed. As viscosity increases, the spectrum simplifies to a single dominant peak whose intensity diminishes from 98 to 58.8% as dynamic viscosity rises from η = 2.0 ηd to η = 10.0 ηd. These changes are primarily due to frictional forces at the liquid/solid interface, which increase viscosity and lead to enhanced wave damping. These results due to amplified loss mechanisms and greater acoustic energy dissipation as waves travel through the medium. Moreover, the inclusion of both dynamic and bulk viscosity introduces additional damping, causing a shift in the resonance peak to higher frequencies and a reduction in the quality factor.

These findings highlight the importance of incorporating both types of viscosity in the design and optimization of PnC sensors. Addressing these factors can significantly improve sensor sensitivity and accuracy, offering a deeper understanding of acoustic wave behavior in complex fluid environments. The insights from this study effectively bridge theoretical models with practical sensor applications, tackling real-world challenges and providing a solid foundation for future developments in 1D and 2D PnC-based sensing technologies.
