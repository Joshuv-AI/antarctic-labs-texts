Gorham-Stout disease presenting as chest wall defect: A case report and review of literature
Authors: Athish Kannan Karur, Dinesh Kumar Sathanantham, Jayakumar Thanathu Krishnan Nair, Adinarayanan Kishore Nochur, Samuel Olaniyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGorham-Stout disease (GSD), also known as vanishing bone syndrome, is an extremely rare, non-malignant condition marked by progressive osteolysis due to abnormal intraosseous lymphatic and vascular proliferation. Extensive chest wall involvement is particularly uncommon and can present without symptoms, complicating diagnosis and management. In this study, a review of similar cases in the literatures is tabulated with comparison of this case to other articles is presented. A 24-year-old male presented with an incidental finding of “hollowness” in his right chest wall following a muscle strain, but remained asymptomatic otherwise. Imaging revealed near-total disappearance of the right 4th to 6th ribs anterolaterally, and partial resorption of the 7th and 8th ribs laterally. MRI confirmed extensive osteolysis with prominent lymphatic and vascular malformations. Histopathologic evaluation showed degenerative bony trabeculae with fibrous tissue and lymphovascular proliferations, confirming GSD. The patient was managed conservatively—vitamin D and calcium supplementation. No progression of disease was detected at subsequent follow-ups. This case underscores the need for high clinical suspicion and comprehensive evaluation in unexplained rib osteolysis. While various medical (bisphosphonates, sirolimus, interferon-α2b) and surgical interventions have been employed, no standard therapy is established. In asymptomatic patients without life-threatening complications, conservative management may be appropriate. The treatment should be personalized based on disease course and complications. The unpredictable prognosis mandates long-term monitoring.

Gorham-Stout disease (GSD)/vanishing bone syndrome is an exceedingly rare, non-malignant sporadic bone disease characterized by abnormal, intraosseous proliferation of lymphatic and vascular (angiomatous) structures, leading to progressive massive osteolysis and osseous resorption.[1, 2, 3, 4, 5] Its exact etiology and pathogenesis remain unknown;[1] however, a few reports have suggested trauma as a trigger.[2] While GSD can affect any part of the skeleton, our patient presented with extensive involvement of the right 4th to 8th ribs, a less common, yet significant, site of disease.[1, 2, 5] The asymptomatic nature of this case contrasts sharply with many reported cases that present with pain, dyspnea, functional impairment, or pathological fractures.[3] This case underscores that GSD can present with extensive bone destruction without overt symptoms, emphasizing the importance of a high index of suspicion in unexplained osteolysis.

Case Report

A 24-year-old male was referred from a primary care center after noticing a “hollowness” in his right chest wall incidentally following a muscle strain during strenuous labor that he started recently. He reported no other associated symptoms such as pain, dyspnea, or constitutional symptoms, with no history of trauma and unremarkable past medical and family history. Physical examination revealed an 8x9 cm palpable defect in the right anterolateral chest wall, predominantly over the 5th and 6th intercostal regions, with circumferential extension involving the 7th and 8th intercostal spaces posterolaterally. Ribs were palpable along the margins of the defect, which was non-tender. There was no soft tissue loss. Routine laboratory parameters were unremarkable, except for mild anemia (hemoglobin 9.8 g/dL).

A chest radiograph revealed near-total disappearance of the right anterior ribs. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax was obtained to ascertain the full extent of osseous destruction. It revealed extensive destruction and resorption of the right 4th-6th ribs anterolaterally and partial resorption of the right 7th and 8th ribs laterally. The appearance was consistent with massive osteolysis. The remaining ribs, sternum, clavicles, vertebral bodies, and scapulae appeared largely intact, without any mass lesion or effusion (Figures 1, 2). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thorax was performed for further evaluation, which confirmed the osteolysis visualized by CT thorax. It also revealed the presence of prominent lymphatic and vascular malformations interspersed with fibrous tissue within the affected areas. On T1-weighted MRI, a homogeneous mass of low signal intensity with that of muscle was noted. On T2-weighted MRI, a heterogeneous mass with high signal intensity was observed. On contrast-enhanced fat-saturated T1-weighted MRI, the mass and bone marrow revealed minimal and relatively homogenous enhancement, consistent with the characteristic soft tissue proliferation associated with GSD, and further delineating the extent of these changes that were subsequently confirmed by histopathological analysis. Two percutaneous bone biopsies were taken from the tip of the right 7th rib, which was approached via a small incision. Histopathology revealed degenerative bony trabeculae with prominent lymphatic and vascular malformations interspersed with fibrous tissue.

Following comprehensive clinical, radiological, and histopathological evaluation, a diagnosis of GSD was established. Given the asymptomatic nature of the condition, the patient opted to defer surgical intervention and radiotherapy, instead choosing conservative management. The patient was managed conservatively with vitamin D and calcium supplementation. Follow-up assessments at 6 months and 1 year demonstrated static disease, with no progression of osteolysis or development of disease-related complications.

Discussion

Gorham’s disease has been described in patients as young as one month and as old seventy-five years.[5] Clinical presentation of GSD is highly variable, ranging from incidental asymptomatic defects to significant dull aching pain, dyspnea, localized swelling, functional impairment of the affected region, or even a pathological fracture.[1, 2, 3, 4] In more severe presentations involving the thoracic skeleton, insidious onset of dyspnea, often complicated by chylothorax, can occur and carries a poorer prognosis.[6] However, significant involvement of the chest wall, as observed in our patient, is notably rare yet well-documented in the medical literature.[1, 2] The clinical manifestation of GSD is highly variable and directly correlates with the anatomical site of involvement. Laboratory evaluation including alkaline phosphatase are usually within normal limits.[5] Among 9 patients across 6 published literatures and this case (Table 1), the age range was 14-48 years, with 6 males and 3 females. Multiple ribs were affected in all cases, with 6/9 cases affecting left and 3/9 cases affecting right side, with sternum involvement only in 3/9 cases. Soft tissue mass was identified in 7/9 cases and chylothorax was present in 6/9 cases (left or bilateral). Conversely, as exemplified by the present case, some individuals remain entirely asymptomatic or without or functional impairment despite experiencing substantial bone destruction.

Diagnosis is typically one of exclusion, based on comprehensive clinical, radiological, and histopathological evaluation. Our patient’s diagnosis followed this established paradigm, with typical radiological findings of massive osteolysis and a confirmatory biopsy showing lympho-vascular malformations interspersed with fibrous tissue. While diagnostic thoracentesis is crucial for cases with pleural effusion, it was not necessary for our asymptomatic patient. In few cases as perfectly echoed by our patient’s presentation, the clinical presentations would be milder than the finding as per imaging.[3]

A critical aspect of GSD is the absence of a universally accepted definitive treatment, necessitating individualized management tailored to the disease’s unpredictable course and site-specific complications, particularly life-threatening effusions like chylothorax.[3, 6, 7] Based on the available literature, the treatment modalities for GSD with rib involvement are diverse: with medical management involving bisphosphonates (pamidronate, alendronate, zoledronate), sirolimus, interferon-α2b, was a common therapy. Other medications included calcitonin, steroid therapy (e.g., dexamethasone, prednisone), propranolol, thalidomide, octreotide and various vitamin/calcium supplements. Radiotherapy was a frequently employed treatment, it is often considered in cases of failed conservative measures or when patients are not surgical candidates, with reports of arresting endothelial cell proliferation or recalcification.[3, 4, 6] Surgical options were local resection with or without graft replacement, or extensive thoracic surgery (e.g., pleurectomy, decortication, rib excision). Treatment for chylothorax, when present, included chest drains, pleurodesis, thoracic duct ligation, pleurectomy, and specialized diets.[3, 6, 7, 8]

The unpredictable outcomes, from spontaneous arrest to severe disability or mortality, underscore the importance of vigilant long-term follow-up and personalized care strategies for this challenging disorder.[2, 3, 4]

In this review comprising 9 cases, 3 had stable disease, 1 had progression of the disease, with death reported in 3 cases and data not available for 2 cases. For patients like ours, particularly a daily laborer, the potential future need for supportive surgical interventions, such as implantable rib prostheses or 3D printed ribs, becomes a crucial point of discussion to address potential long-term functional deficits and provide structural support as he ages. However, due to the extreme rarity of the disease, and the financial constraints in developing countries achieving a consensus on such advanced surgical approaches remains challenging, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up and personalized management plans.

Our case demonstrates that GSD can present with extensive, asymptomatic osteolysis, highlighting the need for vigilance in unexplained bone loss. This case, with its favorable static course on conservative management in the absence of chylothorax, suggests a viable approach for select asymptomatic patients. Ultimately, understanding GSD’s diverse spectrum and prioritizing long-term, personalized care are crucial for managing this rare and challenging condition.
