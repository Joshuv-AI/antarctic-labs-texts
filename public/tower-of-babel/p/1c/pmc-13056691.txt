Infectious Pubic Symphysitis: An Atypical Infection Not to Be Overlooked
Authors: Bakoubassé Aïssata Son, Fulgence Kaboré, Ismaël Ayouba Tinni, Wendyam Nadège Yaméogo, Yannick Laurent Tchenadoyo Bayala, Issa Ouédraogo, Wendlassida Joëlle Stéphanie Zabsonré/Tiendrébéogo, Dieu‐Donné Ouédraogo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe pubic symphysis is a rare site for septic arthritis. We report a case of infectious pubic symphysitis occurring in the postpartum period. A 33‐year‐old woman, gravida 2, para 2, with one living child and one stillbirth at 6 months of gestation was admitted 10 days postpartum with pubalgia and bilateral subacute inflammatory hip pain associated with fever. Initial examination revealed systemic inflammatory response syndrome. Rheumatological assessment showed bilateral clinostasis, tenderness on pubic palpation, and painful hip mobilization with a positive FABER test. Laboratory findings showed elevated inflammatory markers with a C‐reactive protein (CRP) level of 338.15 mg/L. Blood cultures identified Escherichia coli sensitive to amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid. Urinary cytobacteriological examination was sterile. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed edema of the pubic symphysis and significant pelvic muscle infiltration with an intersymphyseal fluid collection extending to the hip joints. Joint fluid aspiration could not be performed due to the low volume. The diagnosis of infectious pubic symphysitis and septic hip arthritis was established. Targeted antibiotic therapy with amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid (4 g/day) was initiated, leading to clinical improvement and a significant decrease in inflammatory markers. Although septic pubic arthritis is rare, it should be considered in cases of pubalgia with fever, particularly in postpartum settings where microbial seeding of the pubic symphysis may occur.Keywords: childbirth, osteomyelitis, postpartum infection, pubic symphysitis, septic arthritis

Introduction

The pubic symphysis, along with the sacroiliac joints, ensures the continuity of the pelvic ring. It comprises the two iliac bones and the sacrum, forming part of the axial skeleton, which has unique features regarding the mechanism and etiology of septic arthritis [1]. Septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis is rare, accounting for approximately 1% of cases [2]. It is a non‐specific septic infection of the pubic symphysis, favored by certain conditions such as urological or pelvic surgery, athletic activity, and the postpartum period [3]. Diagnosis in the peripartum period is often challenging due to its rarity and atypical presentation. Here, we report a case of infectious pubic symphysitis diagnosed postpartum.

Case Presentation

Medical History

A 33‐year‐old woman, gravida 2, para 2, with one living child and one stillbirth at 6 months of gestation was admitted 10 days postpartum with pubalgia and bilateral subacute inflammatory hip pain associated with fever. Her first delivery was performed by cesarean section in 2017, while the second pregnancy ended in a vaginal delivery at 6 months of gestation resulting in stillbirth.

Clinical Examination

On admission, the patient was conscious, well‐oriented, and presented with systemic inflammatory response syndrome, characterized by fever (40.5°C), tachycardia (140 beats/min), and tachypnea (31 breaths/min). Rheumatological examination revealed an inability to stand or walk, bilateral clinostasis, tenderness on pubic palpation without palpable mass, and painful hip mobilization with a positive FABER test. Gynecological examination showed a soft, depressible abdomen, no vaginal discharge, well‐involuted uterus, and no foul‐smelling lochia. Other systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Investigations

Laboratory tests revealed elevated inflammatory markers with leukocytosis (15.940/mm3), predominantly neutrophils (11.360/mm3), and a CRP level of 338.15 mg/L. Blood cultures isolated Escherichia coli sensitive to amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid, while urine cultures were sterile. No vaginal or uterine cultures were performed.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the pelvis showed pubic symphysis edema and significant pelvic muscle infiltration with an intersymphyseal fluid collection extending to the hip joints (Figure 1). Joint fluid aspiration was not feasible due to the low volume. A transthoracic echocardiogram ruled out infective endocarditis, and further workup did not reveal any additional abnormalities.

Diagnosis

A diagnosis of infectious pubic symphysitis and septic arthritis of the hip due to Escherichia coli was established. Some differential diagnoses were considered and excluded, including aseptic osteitis pubis argued against by the presence of high‐grade fever, positive blood cultures, and intersymphyseal fluid collections on magnetic resonance imaging. Postpartum pubic symphysis diastasis, a recognized complication of vaginal delivery defined by a pubic joint widening of more than 10 mm [4], was excluded based on the absence of simple joint widening on MRI and the presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome with positive blood cultures. SAPHO syndrome was excluded based on the absence of characteristic dermatological features such as acne and palmoplantar pustulosis [5].

Treatment

Antibiotic therapy with amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid (4 g/day) was administered intravenously, for 1 week, followed by an oral therapy after discharge with a total planned duration of 6 weeks. After 2 weeks of treatment, leukocyte counts was 11.000/mm3, and CRP levels decreased to 100 mg/L indicating partial biological response. However, inflammatory markers had not yet normalized at this stage. A follow‐up MRI could not be performed. The patient was subsequently lost to follow‐up, likely due to geographical distance, and did not return to further evaluation. The long‐term outcome could not be assessed.

Discussion

Septic pubic arthritis is a rare and often underrecognized condition due to its sometimes challenging diagnosis, involving the symphysis and parasymphyseal regions, typically found in immunocompromised patients [6]. The first descriptions of infectious pubic symphysitis were made by Ellioston in 1827, followed by the French authors Legueu and Roche in 1923. However, it was Beer's 1924 report of pubic symphysitis following radical suprapubic prostatectomy that brought this pathology to light [7]. Initially described as a complication of urological surgery, several other predisposing factors have been identified, including pelvic neoplasms often complicated by tissue necrosis or digestive fistulas, intravenous drug use, and more rarely, mechanical causes (athletes), infections (prostatitis, pyelonephritis), as well as obstetric maneuvers such as childbirth or abortion [8, 9]. The occurrence of this condition during the obstetric period is explained by abnormal micromobility of pelvic joints resulting from estrogenic impregnation of fibrocartilages and connective tissue specific to pregnancy, combined with increased biomechanical stress [10]. In our case, the source of infection could not be definitively identified. Two main mechanisms may explain the occurrence of infection in our patient. First, an ascending genital tract contamination following vaginal delivery, as mechanical trauma to pelvic tissues during childbirth may facilitate bacterial translocation. However, this hypothesis appears less likely in our patient given the absence of vaginal discharge, clinical signs of endometritis (such as foul‐smelling lochia), or abnormal uterine involution on examination. Second, and more probably, hematogenous dissemination of Escherichia coli , as evidenced by positive blood cultures, may have seeded the pubic symphysis, which may be particularly vulnerable during the postpartum period due to increased ligamentous laxity and micromobility of pelvic joints related to hormonal changes during pregnancy. It must be acknowledged that no vaginal or uterine cultures were performed, which represents a limitation of our diagnostic workup. Similar cases reported in the literature have also suggested hematogenous dissemination as a possible mechanism in postpartum pubic symphysitis [11].

The clinical presentation of septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis is highly variable and atypical, typically characterized by the sudden onset of bilateral pelvic or groin pain, usually worsened by standing or walking, contributing to limping, and often evolving in a febrile context [12]. Pubic palpation reveals exquisite tenderness, sometimes associated with a pubic mass [13, 14]. The most commonly implicated pathogens, in order of frequency, include Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus sp., Escherichia coli, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis [14]. Pathogen isolation can be achieved through blood and urine cultures, as well as ultrasound‐guided aspiration of collections. Despite a radioclinical delay of 2 to 4 weeks, conventional radiography remains the initial imaging modality for pubalgia, as it helps exclude other etiologies. The earliest signs are typical of arthritis, including marginal irregularities of the symphysis, widening of the joint space, and bone loss associated with demineralization of the pubic rami [13, 15, 16].

MRI remains the modality of choice for the diagnosis of infectious pubic symphysitis, as with other infections of the axial skeleton. Compared to conventional radiography, which may remain normal for 2 to 4 weeks after symptom onset, MRI allows early detection of inflammatory lesions within 48 h, enabling prompt diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment [12, 17]. MRI offers the advantage of detecting suggestive signs as early as 48 h after symptom onset, revealing the extension of bone involvement to the ilio‐ and ischiopubic branches, and more importantly, confirming the infectious origin through soft tissue inflammation, including the adductor muscles, as well as the presence of deep abscesses and collections within the adductor compartment. Additionally, MRI can delineate the relationships and potential fistulous tracts with nearby pelvic structures, such as the prostatic region and the urogenital system [12]. Antibiotic therapy should be tailored to the identified pathogen, administered for at least 6 weeks, and may be extended to three or 6 months depending on the extent of involvement and the pathogen's susceptibility profile [12]. Surgical intervention is required in nearly half of cases, ranging from abscess drainage, fistula closure, or management of surgical site infections to pubic curettage, symphysectomy, and in severe cases, cystectomy and Bricker cystoplasty [18].

However, our report has some limitations. First, aspiration of the intersymphyseal collection was not performed because of the low volume of fluid, which prevented direct microbiological confirmation of the infection at the symphyseal level. Nevertheless, the diagnosis was strongly supported by the clinical presentation, markedly elevated inflammatory markers, positive blood cultures for Escherichia coli , and compatible MRI findings. Second, the patient was lost to follow‐up after 2 weeks, which prevented assessment of the long‐term clinical and biological outcome and confirmation of complete resolution after the recommended 6‐week antibiotic therapy.

Conclusion

Septic pubic arthritis is a rare condition that is favored by specific circumstances such as childbirth, which facilitates microbial colonization of the pubic symphysis. The diagnosis is suggested by pubalgia associated with fever and is confirmed through imaging supported by laboratory findings.
