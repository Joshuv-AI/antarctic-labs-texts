Massive bowel intussusception requiring right hemicolectomy
Authors: Jeffrey Baum, Paige Varney, Neema Patel, Maria Chimarios, Charles Thompson
Published: 2025

Description

A male in his 40s with a medical history of multiple sclerosis and a virgin abdomen presented to the hospital with a 1-month history of progressively worsening colicky abdominal pain with intermittent obstructive symptoms. He was tender in his right hemi-abdomen and had normal vitals and laboratory values. A CT scan obtained on presentation revealed evidence of a large ileocaecal intussusception due to a 3-cm ileocaecal lipoma (figure 1), requiring an urgent robotic ileocaecal resection. In the operating room, it was noted that the intussusception could not be manually reduced, and the decision was made to transect the leading and trailing ends in situ. The afferent limb was first taken about 10 cm from the ileocaecal valve. However, when taking the efferent limb, proximal to the hepatic flexure, the robotic stapler failed to fire across the colon as the device registered colonic tissue that was too thick, mandating an even more distal transection point, past the hepatic flexure. On inspection of the specimen (online supplemental video 1), it was discovered that precisely 47 cm of small bowel had indeed invaginated into the ascending and transverse colon. The initial inability for the robotic stapler to fire across the colon was caused by the unexpected long length of intussusception of the small bowel, essentially attempting to transect through four layers of bowel wall instead of two. Importantly, clinicians must anticipate a lengthy invagination when stapling across bowel, especially when utilising a manual stapler without bowel-wall thickness feedback technology. The commonly used manually deployed EndoGIA stapler is not equipped with such sensing technology and failure to confirm a bowel thickness of only two layers can lead to the dreaded complication of stump or anastomotic leak. Our patient did well with primary anastomosis and was discharged home after appropriate monitoring.

Our report is clinically significant across two accounts. First, it marks one of the largest intussusceptions ever recorded in an adult. A retrospective series by Rea et al1 reviewed 170 adult cases of intussusception identified from 380,999 CT reports over a 6-year period and revealed the largest intussusception of 20.5 cm, which is less than half of the findings in our case. A review of PubMed, OpenEvidence, Google Scholar and Cochrane Library reveals somewhat conflicting results, with one case in 1961 which recorded an intussusception of 100 cm.2 More recent reports, however, yield a record of 33 cm in an adult3 and 50 cm in a child,4 marking our case as one of the largest recently recorded lengths of intussusception across both CT and intraoperative measurements in an adult. Second, our report is significant as it highlights the serendipitous misfiring of the robotic stapler preventing what otherwise would have been a forceful firing of a handheld stapler across four layers of bowel instead of two. Our case alerts clinicians to be uniquely mindful of the leading and trailing ends of the intussusception, especially when the intussuscipiens is colonic tissue, which due to the naturally thicker wall than small bowel, may make it difficult to confirm the inner borders of the intussusceptum.

Learning points

Small bowel intussusception can present with even massive telescoping (47 cm in our case) and can lead to misidentification of the true leading end of the intussusception.
