Repurposing antifungal drugs for Oral squamous cell carcinoma using network pharmacology, molecular docking and molecular dynamics approaches
Authors: Anju Mayadevi Nair, Bounika Esvanth Rao, Abhi Venkatshree Anand Kumar, Satish Srinivas Kondaveeti, Gnanasambandam Ramanathan, Vijayalakshmi Ramshankar, Divyambika Catakapatri Venugopal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remains an important cause of cancer morbidity and mortality, and new multi-target approaches are required to increase therapeutic options. Drug repurposing offers a promising strategy to identify new anticancer applications for clinically approved drugs with established safety profiles. An integrative in silico workflow was designed to identify overlapping targets between selected antifungal drugs and OSCC-associated genes based on network pharmacology and protein-protein interaction analysis. Network pharmacology identified 144 common targets for antifungals and OSCC, and EGFR and MMP9 were selected as druggable hubs based on their contributions to cancer progression. Molecular Docking revealed promising binding, with fluconazole (−8.5 kcal/mol) and itraconazole (−8.2 kcal/mol) showing better binding than the reference inhibitors tanomastat (−8.3) and erlotinib (−6.9) against MMP9 and EGFR, respectively. However, 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations and MM-PBSA calculations showed a different reality. Erlotinib and tanomastat showed the dynamic signature of specific binding, including persistent hydrogen bonds, restricted PCA clusters, and deep free energy minima. In contrast, fluconazole-EGFR showed thermodynamically unfavorable binding (+20.249 kJ/mol), fluconazole-MMP9 showed transient contacts mediated through zinc, and itraconazole-EGFR did not form persistent hydrogen bonds. Notably, the highest RMSD (0.31 ± 0.11 nm), protein destabilisation and unreliable binding energy (±101.005 kJ/mol) were shown by itraconazole-MMP9. The results imply that fluconazole and itraconazole are not specific and direct inhibitors of EGFR or MMP9, and their docking scores are artifacts from zinc chelation and hydrophobic overestimation. This study provides a cautionary computational framework emphasizing that rigorous MD and MM-PBSA validation is indispensable for reliable drug repurposing.

Introduction

Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC) is the most common cancer globally, with 390,000 new cases reported in the GLOBOCAN 2022 reports. The incidence of OSCC globally ranks between 7th and 12th among cancers. India was reported as the highest incidence due to maximum consumption of tobacco/betel among the affected individuals.1, 2 Despite improvements in surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapies, only modest increase in the overall survival of OSCC have been achieved over the last few decades due to local recurrence, metastasis and resistance to therapy.3, 4 This reality underscores the need for new, affordable therapies that can be translated into practice relatively quickly. A practical approach to extend the reach of cancer therapy is drug repurposing, which finds new indications for existing drugs with well-characterized pharmacokinetic and safety profiles.5, 6 Repurposed drugs can be rapidly moved into clinical testing and are often more readily available in resource-limited settings than de novo drug discovery.5, 6, 7 Among the many drug classes being explored, antifungal agents have attracted attention because several of their primary and off-target effects intersect with key cancer-related pathways, including those regulating proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune responses.6, 8

Itraconazole, a triazole antifungal used widely in clinical practice, is one of the best-studied examples. Beyond inhibiting fungal ergosterol synthesis, itraconazole has been shown to interfere with Hedgehog, mTOR, and Wnt/β-catenin signalling, inhibit angiogenesis, and modulate drug resistance in different cancer models. In OSCC and other head and neck cancers, preclinical studies suggested that itraconazole can reduce proliferation and invasion, although the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully mapped.9 Other azoles, such as fluconazole, have also demonstrated context-dependent anticancer activity, but their roles in OSCC remain poorly understood.10, 11

Network pharmacology offers an effective approach to understanding the intricate interactions among drugs, their targets, and disease pathways at a systems level.12 Recently, a few computational studies have identified itraconazole as a potential binder of a mitochondrial fission protein, DRP1, through docking and molecular dynamics.13 However, the role of antifungals in modulating classical oncogenic pathways, especially those involved in proliferation and invasion, is still poorly investigated. Most repurposing studies also focus on target identification without checking the thermodynamic reliability of the binding predictions. This study aims to fill these gaps by exploring the effects of fluconazole and itraconazole on EGFR and MMP9, two key determinants influencing the progression of OSCC, via network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics and MM-PBSA analysis.14 This approach helps to distinguish true binders from false positives and prevent overinterpretation of docking scores. In summary, the study provides a more robust computational framework to support future experimental validation of antifungal repurposing in OSCC (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Repurposing antifungal drugs for OSCC through network pharmacology and molecular docking.Fig. 1

Methodology

Antifungal and OSCC target identification

Based on our previous study,15 itraconazole,16, 17 terbinafine,18, 19 and clotrimazole20 were selected for further investigation because of their reported anticancer potential in OSCC. Further studies identified fluconazole,21 which has been reported to exhibit anticancer activity, miconazole in lung cancer,22 and ketoconazole in renal cell carcinoma.23 The chemical structures of the selected antifungals were retrieved from the PubChem online database24 (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed on August 9, 2024).

Potential target screening of selected ligands and OSCC

Targets for each chosen antifungals were identified through SwissTargetPrediction25 and had a prediction Probability threshold of ≥0.7 (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), where the canonical SMILES was provided, and “Homo sapiens” was specified as the species and had a probability value greater than 0.7. OSCC-associated targets were sourced from databases such as GeneCards26 and had a Relevance score > 10 (http://www.genecards.org/), DisGeNET27 and had a Score ≥ 0.1 (http://www.disgenet.org/), OMIM28 (https://omim.org/), and PharmGKB29(https://www.pharmgkb.org/), all accessed on 10th October 2024. After combining these targets, duplicates were removed. Overlapping targets for the selected ligands and OSCC were then identified using a Venn diagram analysis with the Bioinformatics tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn) on 15th October 2024.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction and analysis

To explore the interactions among shared proteins, the STRING30 database (https://string-db.org/) was used, specifying “Homo sapiens” for organismal context. A PPI network was constructed with a medium confidence threshold of ≥0.4. Cytoscape31 V3.10.1 was employed for visualization of the network, and the CytoHubba32 plugin highlighted key hub genes with high degrees within the network, indicating genes with stronger functional significance. Hub proteins were identified by Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), degree, and closeness.

Analysis of pathways and functional enrichment

Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on hub proteins using the DAVID database33 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), and Gene Ontology was visulized using SRplots.34 GO Enrichment was conducted with significant terms selected based on a threshold p-value of less than 5 × 10–2. Shiny GO35(http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) was used to visualize the enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways with bar and lollipop plots.

Physicochemical, ADMET, and drug-likeness characteristics

To prioritize antifungals for molecular docking, the following multi-parameter scoring approach was employed (i) Lipinski's Rule of Five compliance, (ii) physicochemical properties (molecular weight, TPSA, LogP, hydrogen bond donors/acceptors), (iii) the ADMET profile (human oral absorption, BBB permeability, CYP inhibition, and toxicity), (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/, online database, accessed on August 16, 2024) and (iv) the boiled-egg model to estimate gastrointestinal absorption and brain penetration. This integrated approach enabled us to achieve a compromise between drug-likeness and safety profile while ensuring favorable pharmacokinetic properties for the selected compounds in terms of oral administration, which is relevant for OSCC therapy.

Ligand preparation

The 3D structures of the ligands, itraconazole(PubChem ID: 55283) and fluconazole (PubChem ID: 3365), were downloaded from the PubChem database in MDL SDF 3D format. The ligands were converted to PDB format using PyMOL. The converted ligands were then prepared using AutoDock Tools version 1.5.7.36 Polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges were added to the ligands, and the prepared structures were saved in PDBQT format.

Protein preparation

The target protein structures EGFR (PDB ID:1GKC) and MMP9 (PDB ID: 1M17) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) in legacy PDB format. The protein structures were opened in BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2025 Client, water molecules, heteroatoms (HETATM), and co-crystallized ligands were removed, and the structures were saved in PDB format. The prepared protein structures were then opened in AutoDock Tools version 1.5.7, water molecules were removed and polar hydrogens and Kollman charges were added. The processed proteins were saved in PDBQT format.

Grid box preparation

Based on previously reported literature, active binding residues were identified and marked on the protein structures. Grid boxes were generated with a grid spacing of 0.375 Å to cover the active binding residues. The grid box dimensions and center coordinates for the selected target proteins were defined as follows: EGFR (46 × 46 × 46; center: 22.778, 1.549, 51.650) and MMP-9 (24 × 28 × 58; center: 68.619, 26.934, 113.044).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using the command prompt interface of AutoDock 4.2 to evaluate the binding affinity of the selected ligands, such as itraconazole and fluconazole, toward the MMP9 and EGFR. Each interactions were ran in triplicate. The best poses were selected for further analysis after examining the binding affinities of the resulting docking positions. The docked complexes were ranked based on their binding energies, with lower binding energy values indicating stronger binding interactions. The potential of the antifungals as inhibitors of the target protein was assessed using the docking study.

Molecular dynamics

The complexes with the best binding scores (1GKC-FLU/ITR/TAN; 1 M17-FLU/ITR/ERL) topologies were retrieved using ATB topologies and were added into GROMACS. Ligand topology was selected from the ATB server. Hydrogens were added to the heavy atoms using the GROMACS module pdb2gmx. The steepest descent algorithm was first used to vacuum minimize the prepared systems for 1500 steps. The structures were then dissolved using a water simple point charge (SPCE) water model in a cubic periodic box. After that, the complex systems were kept at the proper salt concentration of 0.15 M by injecting the right amounts of Na and Cl counter ions. A previously published paper served as the basis for the system preparation.37Every structure that emerged from the NPT equilibration phase underwent a final production run in the NPT ensemble for a simulated duration of 100 ns. Lastly, the Gromacs software package's tools, such as the protein root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (RG), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and hydrogen bonding (H-Bond), were used to analyze the simulation's trajectory. The binding free energy (1GKC-FLU/ITR/TAN; 1 M17-FLU/ITR/ERL binding) of an inhibitor with protein throughout simulation time was analyzed using the Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) technique. The binding free energy was estimated using the GROMACS function g_mmpbsa.38 To get an accurate result, the final 50 ns with dt 1000 frames of 1GKC-FLU/ITR/TAN and 1 M17-FLU/ITR/ERL were calculated.

Disease-antifungal target network construction

To understand the mechanistic potential of the selected antifungals, a PPI network was prepared with 144 common targets using the STRING database (Fig. 3). The constructed network included 1028 edges and 143 nodes, with 337 edges predicted by chance, indicating substantially more interactions than expected. 22 important targets were found by Cytoscape's functional enrichment analysis (Fig. 4). The nodes are the target proteins, and the edges are the predicted interactions with a score cutoff of ≥0.4. Hub proteins were identified by Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), degree, betweenness and closeness. A PPI network for these 22 enhanced targets, which were more relevant to the progression of OSCC. In order to enable sophisticated visualization and thorough network analysis, the built network was then loaded into Cytoscape. The significance of the top 10 targets—CASP3, EGFR, BCL2, MDM2, MMP9, GSK3B, MAPK3, MAOB, DRD2, and CYP3A4 was determined by calculating the MCC ratio using CytoHubba (Fig. 5). EGFR is a clinically validated target (cetuximab39 and erlotinib40 are FDA-approved inhibitors), providing a benchmark for computational validation. Both proteins possess well-characterized ligand-accessible binding sites, the ATP-binding pocket of EGFR41 and the catalytic zinc site of MMP9,42 and are therefore amenable to small-molecule targeting. EGFR is an upstream signalling hub,43 and MMP9 is a downstream effector of invasion,44 representing complementary nodes in the OSCC progression. Many of the other genes, such as CASP3,45 BCL2,46 MAPK3,47 MDM2,48 and GSK3B,49 are intracellular proteins, which would need specific inhibitors to modulate. CYP3A4 is a metabolic enzyme,50 DRD2 and MAOB51 are not directly related to the core proliferative/invasive phenotype of OSCC. Physicochemical, ADMET, and drug-likeness characteristics of selected antifungals

Among the six antifungals evaluated, fluconazole and itraconazole were selected for molecular docking and MD simulations based on the following systematic criteria.

As per Lipinski's Rule of Five, Fluconazole is compliant with all criteria (MW 306.27, LogP 0.88, HBA 7, HBD 1). Although it has a higher MW (705.63) and LogP (4.75), it is still within acceptable limits for oral drugs and is a clinically approved drug with well-established safety (Table 1). ADMET profiles of the drugs considered show that fluconazole had the cleanest toxicity profile of all the antifungals, with no CYP450 inhibition (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP3A4), no Ames test toxicity, and favorable human oral absorption (94.96%). Itraconazole showed CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 inhibition but had the highest human oral absorption (100%) and a safer profile compared to clotrimazole, miconazole, and ketoconazole, which showed multiple CYP inhibitions and positive Ames toxicity (Table 2). The SwissADME boiled-egg plot (Fig. 8) confirmed that fluconazole lies in the region of high gastrointestinal absorption with no blood-brain barrier penetration. At the same time, itraconazole shows high gastrointestinal absorption despite limited BBB permeability. This is promising for OSCC therapy, as high BBB penetration is not needed and may contribute to CNS side effects. Terbinafine (LogP 4.92, TPSA 3.24) and miconazole (LogP 5.16) had high lipophilicity and unacceptable TPSA values, indicating poor aqueous solubility and potential formulation problems. Clotrimazole had the most disturbing ADMET profile, exhibiting positive Ames toxicity and profound CYP inhibition in all tested isoforms. Ketoconazole had some CYP inhibition and moderate toxicity issues. Molecular docking

Molecular docking revealed that fluconazole and itraconazole had good binding affinities against MMP9 and EGFR with scores ranging from −8.5 to −7.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 9). Fluconazole had the strongest binding affinity for MMP9 (−8.5 Kcal/mol) compared to the known MMP9 inhibitor tanomastat (−8.3 Kcal/mol), and similarly, itraconazole showed the highest binding affinity toward EGFR (−8.2 Kcal/mol) compared to fluconazole (−7.1 Kcal/mol) and the reference inhibitor erlonitib (−6.9Kcal/mol) (Table 3). Both antifungals interact with functionally important residues. Fluconazole coordinates the catalytic zinc ion (ZN6) in MMP9 and interacts with the hinge region of EGFR (THR101, MET104), while itraconazole occupies the ATP binding pocket with extensive hydrophobic contacts (PHE106, CYS108, TYR112). However, these encouraging docking scores should be taken with caution as scoring functions are were known to overestimate metal chelation (fluconazole-Zn interaction) and hydrophobic interactions (itraconazole). Molecular dynamics

The 200 ns MD simulations revealed a clear difference in the dynamic behavior of the reference inhibitors and the repurposed antifungals. The specific EGFR and MMP9 inhibitors erlotinib and tanomastat, respectively, exhibited (i) low RMSD with minor fluctuations, (ii) site-specific rigidification of active-site residues, (iii) compact Rg and reduced SASA, (iv) stable intermolecular hydrogen bonds (2–4 throughout the simulation), (v) constrained PCA clusters, and (vi) deep and single FEL minima. This dynamic signature was evidence that these compounds form stable, specific, thermodynamically preferred complexes.

In contrast, fluconazole and itraconazole displayed a different dynamic signature, suggesting non-specific or artifactual binding. RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, PCA profiles of Fluconazole-EGFR were very similar to the apo protein, confirming no specific interaction of EGFR with Fluconazole. Fluconazole-MMP9 had low RMSD and low RMSF at histidine residues, but this attributed to zinc chelation and not specific binding. This was demonstrated by the absence of persistent intermolecular H-bonds, wide sampling in PCA, and a flat FEL with several minima.

Itraconazole-EGFR had a low RMSD, similar to erlotinib, but does not form persistent H-bonds, does not reduce SASA sufficiently and has a broad, shallow FEL, suggesting that the apparent stabilization was driven by non-specific hydrophobic collapse rather than ordered binding. Most notably, itraconazole-MMP9 actively destabilizes the protein, as evidenced by the highest RMSD with the largest standard deviation, expanded Rg, elevated SASA, reduced internal H-bonds, increased RMSF, diffuse PCA sampling and an extremely flat FEL with no clear minimum.

In summary, MD simulation showed that erlotinib and tanomastat were true, specific binders while fluconazole and itraconazole were false positives due to docking artifacts (zinc chelation for FLU; hydrophobic overestimation for ITR). This study provides a warning dynamic framework for drug repurposing. RMSD alone was not sufficient, persistent hydrogen bonding, PCA restriction and deep FEL minima were required to confirm true binding specificity (Fig. 10, Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14, Fig. 15, Discussion

OSCC remains one of the major cancers in the Southeast Asian and Indian populations54, 55 because of its high recurrence, metastatic potential, and resistance to conventional therapy,56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62 which increases the relevance of identifying inexpensive multi-targets. Drug repurposing is the most promising solution, as it can accelerate translational advancement by using drugs with established pharmacological properties.63, 64 There is proven evidence that antifungal agents such as itraconazole,16, 17 terbinafine18, 19 and clotrimazole20 affect signalling pathways which are directly linked to the progression of OSCC. Here, a network pharmacology approach65 has been used to identify multiple shared targets between selected antifungals and OSCC, suggesting that these compounds can exert anticancer effects through a multi-target mechanism rather than through a single protein interaction.

EGFR and MMP9 were prioritized among the other hub genes based on their known roles as direct drivers of OSCC hallmarks and their druggability by small molecules. EGFR is overexpressed in 80–90% of OSCC and drives proliferation, survival and angiogenesis,66 while MMP9 facilitates invasion and metastasis by degrading extracellular matrix.67 Importantly, these two proteins are functionally linked. EGFR signalling induces MMP9 expression via MAPK and GSK3β pathways.68 Conversely, CASP3 and BCL2 are intracellular apoptosis regulators and would need specific inhibitors.69 MAPK3 is a downstream kinase with functional redundancy,47 MDM2 is a protein-protein interaction target requiring specialised inhibitors,48 and GSK3β is an upstream regulator with broad effects.49 CYP3A4 belongs to the cytochrome P450 superfamily, which is mainly involved in drug metabolism, and accounts for oxidative metabolism of about 50% of clinically used drugs.70 Its status as a hub gene in the network analysis suggests the metabolic clearance of the antifungal drugs rather than a direct therapeutic target for OSCC. This is in agreement with the known potent inhibition of CYP3A4-mediated metabolism by itraconazole.71 DRD2 and MAOB have been shown to participate in cancer progression in other tumour types, but their direct role in the core proliferative and invasive phenotype of OSCC is poorly characterized.51 Thus, EGFR and MMP9 are the most biologically relevant and therapeutically accessible targets among the identified hub genes.

Fluconazole was chosen for a detailed computational study because it has the best physicochemical properties: the lowest molecular weight (306.27 Da), balanced lipophilicity (LogP 0.88), moderate TPSA (81.65), full compliance with Lipinski's Rule of Five and the most favorable ADMET profile of all the antifungals tested. Fluconazole is commonly used for the management of candidemia in immunocompromised cancer patients, as established by clinical guidelines and observational studies.21 However, its direct anticancer activity remains poorly characterized, with only limited and cell-type-specific reports available in the literature.72 Itraconazole was selected despite its higher molecular weight and LogP because (i) it is potent and widely used clinically; (ii) it has been reported to have anticancer activity in OSCC by inhibiting the Hedgehog pathway; (iii) it has the highest predicted human oral absorption (100%); and (iv) its ADMET profile, which is not as clean as fluconazole but significantly safer than those of clotrimazole, miconazole and ketoconazole that showed positive Ames toxicity and extensive CYP inhibition. Terbinafine and the other azoles were deprioritized due to poorer drug-likeness, higher toxicity predictions, and less favorable formulation properties. Thus, the selection of fluconazole and itraconazole was based on a systematic balancing of drug-likeness, safety, pharmacokinetic potential, and existing biological evidence.

Molecular docking showed that fluconazole and itraconazole could outperform the reference inhibitors of MMP9 and EGFR. But the molecular dynamics simulations at 200 ns showed a quite different picture. Erlotinib and Tanomastat showed the definite signature of specific binding, including low RMSD, persistent hydrogen bonds,41, 42, 73 compact radius of gyration, restricted PCA clusters and deep single free energy minima, validating the computational workflow. Quinazoline-based EGFR inhibitors have demonstrated binding free energies of −25.0 to −23.9 kcal/mol (approximately −104.6 to −100.0 kJ/mol).74 MMP9 inhibitors such as bergenin75 and DB1246542 have shown binding energies of −137.452 and − 115.669 kJ/mol, respectively. The favorable binding energies of erlotinib (−80.878 kJ/mol) and tanomastat (−120.670 kJ/mol) thus confirm the reliability of this computational workflow. In contrast, the fluconazole-EGFR complex behaved exactly as the apo protein, confirming no significant interaction. However, the fluconazole-MMP9 complex showed transient zinc-mediated contacts instead of persistent hydrogen bonds, suggesting an artefact of triazole chelation. The itraconazole-EGFR complex had low RMSD, but did not form persistent hydrogen bonds, and showed a broad, shallow free energy landscape consistent with non-specific hydrophobic collapse. Notably, the itraconazole-MMP9 complex exhibited the highest RMSD (0.31 ± 0.11 nm), enhanced flexibility, increased radius of gyration, decreased internal hydrogen bonds and the most diffuse PCA sampling, which is a clear signature of protein destabilisation. These dynamic parameters offer a decisive way to distinguish true binders from false positives.

In conclusion, although network pharmacology suggested EGFR and MMP9 as biologically meaningful nodes in OSCC, the structural, dynamic and thermodynamic evidence suggest that fluconazole and itraconazole are not direct, specific inhibitors of these targets. Docking alone does not prove binding and scoring functions can overestimate hydrophobic interactions (itraconazole) and metal chelation (fluconazole). Even low RMSD from MD simulations can be misleading if it is not accompanied by persistent hydrogen bonding, limited PCA sampling and a deep minimum in the FEL. This comprehensive approach of dynamic metrics such as RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, hydrogen bonding, PCA, FEL and MM-PBSA thermodynamics provides a robust framework to distinguish the true binders from false positives. This study provides a cautionary computational framework for antifungal repurposing in OSCC and emphasises that rigorous MD and MM-PBSA validation is indispensable for reliable drug discovery. Limitations and future perspectives

The primary limitation of this study is that all findings are computational and require experimental validation. The MM-PBSA method relies on approximations, including continuum solvation models and neglect of conformational entropy, which can overestimate binding for lipophilic compounds like itraconazole. Classical force fields do not adequately model polarization effects or zinc coordination geometry in MMP-9, contributing to artifactual fluconazole binding. The 200 ns simulation timescale may be insufficient to capture slow conformational transitions relevant to kinase activation. Additionally, the truncated protein constructs (EGFR kinase domain, MMP-9 catalytic domain) lack regulatory regions that influence full-length protein dynamics.

Future studies should prioritize experimental validation through a multi-tiered in vitro strategy. Biochemical assays should include EGFR kinase activity assays (HTRF) and MMP-9 fluorogenic peptide degradation assays to directly measure enzymatic inhibition. Biophysical binding studies using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) would confirm binding affinity (Kd). Cellular assays should employ OSCC cell lines (e.g., HSC-3, SCC-25) for MTT viability assays, Western blotting for phosphorylated EGFR (p-EGFR) and downstream signalling (p-AKT, p-ERK), and gelatin zymography for MMP-9 activity. The expected outcomes are that erlotinib and tanomastat will show potent inhibition, while fluconazole and itraconazole will demonstrate weak or no activity, confirming their lack of direct EGFR/MMP9 engagement.
