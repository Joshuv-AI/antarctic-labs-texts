Diversity and resilience of algae in phycoremediation of carbon, nitrogen, and other pollutants from inland and wastewaters
Authors: Thangavel Mathimani, Samuel Lalthazuala Rokhum, Narasimman Kalaiselvan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDomestic, industrial, and agricultural wastewater contains higher concentration of pollutants, including carbon and nitrogen nutrients, heavy metals, and harmful chemicals, which significantly impact human health and the ecosystem. In this scenario, phycoremediation is an eco-friendly and economically viable technique that harnesses the diversity of algae to eliminate elevated carbon and nitrogen elements, as well as other contaminants in aquatic systems. Therefore, this review critically examines recent advancements in the utilization of algae for inland water and wastewater treatment applications, with a focus on carbon and nitrogen removal. Furthermore, this study examines the optimization of pollutant removal using different algae, algal consortium, and bacterial consortia for sustainable water management. Additionally, it highlights the feasibility of algae-based treatment, which enhances water quality while potentially producing biomass for biofuels and various high-value products. The challenges, recommendations, and future directions presented in this review can lay a roadmap for effectively selecting a resilient algal strain for phycoremediation of wastewater, thereby promoting environmental sustainability.

Introduction

Water pollution has become a serious global concern, resulting from different industrial discharges, agricultural runoff, and untreated urban wastewater. Rapid industrialization and urbanization, coupled with insufficient awareness about water’s critical importance, have left approximately 80% of the global population vulnerable to water scarcity and contamination risks. Only about 2.5% of Earth’s water is fresh, out of which just 0.036% is readily accessible for our use; the remaining 1.96% is stored in polar ice caps, underground wells, and aquifers.1 This tiny portion of the freshwater ecosystem has also been significantly affected by human activities, and pollution has become a threat to both life and environmental health.2 Advanced scientific approaches are necessary for maintaining ecosystem health and promoting human well-being, necessitating education and awareness to mitigate risks and preserve this vital resource. Based on recent reports, 11 million tons of plastic enter water reservoir like oceans, rivers, and seas worldwide each year. These plastics can take up to 200 years to degrade. If the trend continues, the total amount of plastic could rise to around 29 million tons by 2040.3 On the other hand, the demand for freshwater is projected to increase by one-third globally by 2050. Therefore, there is an urgent need for proactive measures to address water scarcity and concerns about water quality. Recognizing the importance of these issues, developed countries, such as the US, have implemented certain measures over the past two decades, which reports suggest have shown substantial progress. This is mainly driven by significant increases in the wastewater collection and treatment capacities: exceeding 90% in both urban and rural areas.4 Moreover, in developed countries, nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus are the major contributors (30%–40%) to inland water pollution, originating from agricultural and urban sources. In contrast, in developing countries, this quantum is at much higher levels (up to 70%) due to less efficient agricultural practices and inadequate wastewater treatment.5 Worldwide, key water pollutants include organic pollutants (10%–20% in developed, similar in developing), heavy metals (5%–10% in developed, up to 30% in developing), pathogens (20%–30% in both), and sediments (10%–30% in both), where the relative contribution of each can vary based on differences in industrial practices and regulatory enforcements.6 Water pollution arises from multiple sources. Among them, industrial activities are the primary contributors to contaminating inland waters in all kinds of areas, including rural, urban, and semi-urban areas. Consequently, these activities are known to cause a range of environmental issues over both immediate and extended periods; their negative impacts on communities are also well-documented. In particular, the wastewater released by industries leads to substantial water pollution and poses health risks worldwide. It is reported that each year, industries consume around 865 km3 of freshwater from a total of 3,928 km3. When industrial effluents are insufficiently treated before being released into sewerage systems, they can contaminate groundwater and harm aquatic life. Moreover, these pollutants exacerbate several other environmental problems, including air and soil contamination. This untreated wastewater may make its way into agricultural use, which can damage crops and introduce harmful contaminants into the food supply. The origin of many waterborne illnesses, such as diarrhea, giardiasis, typhoid fever, cholera, hepatitis, jaundice, and cancer, can be traced to such contaminants. Wastewater generated from various sectors can be classified into several types, including sewage, domestic, storm runoff, agricultural, and industrial wastewater.7 In India, 17,000 highly polluting industries collectively discharge over 88,000 million liters of wastewater daily. In urban areas, only about 30% of this wastewater is treated before being released into a water body, whereas in rural areas, treatment facilities are still lacking, and the untreated water has been repeatedly cited as a reason for significant pollution.8 Inland waters are increasingly threatened by heavy metals, microplastics, and organic pollutants, as these are toxic, persistent, and detrimental to both water quality and human health. Effective management of these contaminants is essential for a comprehensive understanding of their sources, environmental behavior, and interactions. Pervasive microplastics exacerbate pollution by carrying organic and inorganic contaminants such as heavy metals and pesticides, emphasizing the need for sustainable strategies that balance environmental protection with economic growth.9 Inland water bodies are affected by multiple pollution sources: from industrial discharges (20%) to agricultural runoff (40%), including nitrogen and phosphorus, and urban sources (25%), such as sewage and stormwater, as well as mining activities (10%) releasing heavy metals and the increasingly prominent climate change impacts (5%) altering nutrient cycles. Therefore, protecting water quality and ecosystem health demands integrated strategies and the implementation of advanced technologies.10 The literature shows that many countries are directing their efforts into water treatment to address water scarcity and pollutant removal, as the focus on effective wastewater management has intensified with population growth and expanding industrialization. Wastewater treatment is also essential for recovering clean water and enabling the sustainable reuse of water resources.11 The treatment of inland water and industrial wastewater is crucial for protecting public health, preserving ecosystems, ensuring water security, and fostering economic growth. Untreated inland water serves as a major source of waterborne diseases such as cholera, typhoid, and dysentery, which collectively affect over 2 billion people globally and cause approximately 1.4 million deaths annually. A proper wastewater treatment safeguards drinking water sources, maintains aquatic balance, and supports wetlands by preventing eutrophication and biodiversity loss.12 Further, it promotes water reuse in agriculture and industry, reducing freshwater demand by 30%–40% and supporting economic stability through efficient resource use. Exploring the potential of sundry algal species for phycoremediation of both inland water and different types of wastewaters imparts an inclusive and complete comprehension of water treatment to the readers. Eco-technologies like phycoremediation offer dual benefits by removing up to 83%–96% heavy metals and 43%–100%, emerging pollutants while enabling resource recovery in the form of biofuels, biofertilizers, and biopolymers making it a cost-effective and sustainable wastewater management approach.13

Most of the conventional N removal proposed using solid carbon source like sugarcane bagasse. One study with supplementing intermittently aerated constructed wetlands (CWs), significantly enhanced nitrogen removal, achieving 98.3% total N removal at an influent C/N ratio of 5 with sugarcane bagasse fermentation liquid (SBFL), and outperformed sucrose in NO3--N and NH4+-N removal. The addition of SBFL further reduce N2O emissions by 17.8%–43.7% and maintained low CH4 emissions.14 Even though conventional treatment is an easy approach, it limited their effectiveness against certain pollutants like heavy metals, high operational costs, energy intensity, chemical dependency, challenges in sludge disposal, and limited adaptability to changing environmental conditions, necessitating exploration of alternative and integrated approaches for more sustainable water management.15 Interest in green wastewater treatments is growing, with methods including phycoremediation (using algae), CWs, biological filtration, bioaugmentation, membrane bioreactors (MBRs), anaerobic digestion, aquaponics, and natural systems such as oxidation ponds. These promising approaches for sustainably decontaminating and restoring ecosystems while minimizing harm to biodiversity.16 Phycoremediation treats elements such as phosphorus, nitrogen, sulfur, and minerals as nutrients for algal growth, rather than contaminants; this alternative approach is key to removing pollutants like nitrates, phosphates, agrochemicals, hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and excess nitrogen and phosphorus from water bodies. Apart from phycoremediation, some other green wastewater treatment like phytoremediation17 (plants like Brassica juncea, Willow trees and Vetiver grass) and mycoremediation (fungi like Oyster mushrooms, Pleurotus ostreatus and White-rot fungus, Phanerochaete chrysosporium) were also reported by many researchers.18 In phytoremediation, pollutants such as heavy metals, pesticides, hydrocarbons, and chlorinated solvents are removed through three main mechanisms: accumulation, degradation, and stabilization. In mycoremediation, the fungi remove pollutants through enzymatic degradation using extracellular enzymes and biosorption via their mycelial network.19 Further, because of shallow root depth, slow growth rates, and the need for biomass disposal the phytoremediation is limited for large scale promotion.20 While mycoremediation faces challenges like environmental sensitivity, uneven fungal growth, and difficulty in large-scale application.21 In contrast, phycoremediation, uses microalgae and cyanobacteria, which offer faster and more efficient pollutant removal, adaptability to aquatic systems, and the added benefit of nutrient recovery and biomass valorization, making it a more sustainable and versatile remediation approach.22 The approach of phycoremediation for inland/wastewater treatment is shown in Figure 1.Figure 1Schematic illustration of bioremediation for inland water and wastewater

The phycoremediation technique also inhibits bacterial proliferation, enhancing water disinfection.23 Additionally, it reduces PO4-P, NH3-N, and COD concentrations in primary-treated effluent by approximately 97.89%, 98.81%, and 88.24%, respectively, demonstrating its strong eco-friendly treatment potential.24 Many researchers have discussed with various algal species like Chlorella, Scenedesmus, Spirulina, Phaeodactylum tricornutum, Nannochloropsis, Dunaliella, and Euglena for water treatment. These microalgae play a crucial role in removing nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus, which significantly contribute to cleaning the water. Among them, Spirulina and Chlorella are known for their potential for biomass production, while Phaeodactylum tricornutum is preferred for lipid production. Dunaliella can survive even in high-salinity environments, and Euglena is also considered a promising potential bioenergy source. Additionally, Microalgae provide a sustainable solution for improving the quality of landfill leachate while producing useful biomass. In a recent study, textile wastewater (TWW) was treated with Chlorella vulgaris (CV) and algal biomass carbon (algal-BC), with the aim of achieving environmental conservation while creating an additional avenue for energy production. In BC helps to increase the adsorption of pollutants and also provides nutrients that support the growth of microalgae, leading to higher biomass productivity and carbon capture (77.57 ± 2.52 mg L−1 day−1).25 A CV-BC-TWW system was demonstrated to achieve over 99% bioremediation by removing pollutants such as MB, COD, nitrates, and phosphates through biosorption and biodegradation processes. Furthermore, this method can be extended to value-added development in bioremediation. This integrated approach also improves biodiesel production (up to 76.79 ± 2.01 mg g−1) due to enriched fatty acid profiles; therefore, it can serve as a sustainable and efficient approach for wastewater treatment and energy generation.26 A recent review emphasizes the remediation of contaminants such as hydrocarbons, pesticides, radioactive substances, and heavy metals from wastewater,27 and it provides an overview of phycoremediation where microalgae are used to clean wastewater by removing pollutants, particularly heavy metals (HMs). Furthermore, the review also discusses various strategies, including rhizofiltration, CWs, algal mats, and waste stabilization ponds, explaining how they enhance water quality by utilizing algae and valorizing algal biomass for biofuel, animal feed, and fertilizers. It also mentions several new biotechnological approaches, such as genetic modifications and metabolic engineering, that can enhance the efficiency and specificity of phycoremediation processes.27 However, unlike that review, our proposed review majorly focuses on phycoremediation, particularly on how algae/cyanobacteria remove of carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) nutrients from wastewater, thus prioritizing nutrient pollution control. In addition, this review examines recent advancements in phycoremediation technologies and outlines their underlying mechanisms for carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) assimilation. It highlights the dual benefits of phycoremediation: (i) effective water purification and (ii) valuable biomass generation via circular economy models. This review also stands out because it addresses resource recovery from wastewater and compares different processes of converting algal biomass into biofuels and high-value bioproducts; hence, this brings out the environmental and economic advantages of algal-based remediation systems. Microalgae are emerged as a promising bioresource for producing high-value products such as proteins, fatty acids, pigments, anti-aging compounds, and bioenergy, owing to their exceptional biomass productivity and nutrient removal capacity. Their cultivation using wastewater offers sustainable, non-competitive resource utilization, presenting vast opportunities for commercial bioindustry development.28 High-value by-products like polysaccharides, pigments, bioactive compounds, and energy products like hydrogen and methane are derived from phycoremediation. High-value chemicals typically offer greater economic returns per unit biomass (up to $5,000–$10,000/ton) but have limited market scale, whereas energy products provide lower economic margins (around $200–$600/ton of algal dry weight) yet support large-scale circular bioeconomy applications through renewable fuel generation and carbon recycling. A recent study reports that, with basic strains Chlorella, Chlamydomonas, and Scenedesmus are ideally suited for phycoremediation, they produce the by-products high protein and lipid contents, biofuel, H2, and electricity generation.23

Overall, this review takes a forward-looking view on sustainable inland water and wastewater treatment by diverse algal species. Further, significance of temperature, pH, and light intensity on the phycoremediation potential will be discussed as these factors influence algal growth rates and biomass productivity and are seen as crucial for optimizing C and N removal efficiency and other integrated applications. A separate section was presented on biofuels and various high-value products extraction from wastewater tolerant algal biomass.

Phycoremediation and significance of algae

In wastewater treatment, various strategies are used to remove nutrients. These typically include biological processes, chemical precipitation methods for removing phosphorus, adsorption techniques which use materials like activated carbon, as well as membrane-based technologies and wetland constructions. These methods are effective for nitrogen and phosphorus, and they catering to diverse treatment needs and environmental conditions.29 Conventional methods for nutrient removal in wastewater treatment, such as aerobic activated sludge processes and chemical phosphorus removal, often face several limitations. They tend to consume large amounts of energy, exhibit unstable treatment, and show poor efficiencies in resource recovery. These shortcomings run counter to the UN Sustainable Development Goals, which emphasize reducing carbon emissions and calls for better use of resources.30 In this regard, a method more in line with these goals is phycoremediation, a technique that employs microalgae for efficient removal of carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) from polluted water bodies. Microalgae are highly versatile organisms with potential applications in areas such as pharmaceuticals, biofuels, and effluent water treatment, as they are known for to their rapid growth and adaptability to varied, even extreme, environments, from deserts to cold climates. Further, algal biomass is rich in valuable compounds like proteins and lipids, making them valuable for many industries.31,32 Microalgae absorb CO2 during photosynthesis. In the process, the final products are biomass and oxygen. This process is key to reducing carbon emissions and improving water quality, as well as being important in the uptake of nitrogen compounds in the soil, like nitrate (NO3−) and ammonium (NH4+), and in the water by mitigating eutrophication through nitrogen reduction. The latter process can occur through direct assimilation into cellular components or together with bacteria that facilitate denitrification.33 This approach offers a more sustainable method for removing nutrients, as it relies on natural biological processes. Although these processes occur naturally, they can be adjusted or optimized based on our understanding of individual algal species and their surrounding environmental conditions. As a heterotrophic organism, algae convert inorganic nutrients into biomass using solar energy. Previous research has reported productivity of algal reactors to be ranging from 40 to 150 tons/ha·year (dry matter) depending on design parameters and environmental factors.34 According to one recent study, achieving high productivity requires considerable amounts of inputs: up to 200 tons of CO2, 10 tons of nitrogen (N), and 1 ton of phosphorus (P) per 100 tons of biomass. In terms of cost, the study notes that these inputs are typically supplied as chemical fertilizers costing around 0.5 €/kg.35 Another study utilized flue gases and wastewater as nutrient sources with the aim of reducing associated costs, thus improving the chances of profitability by recovering valuable nutrients. The integration of microalgae cultivation with wastewater treatment has been extensively investigated for various effluents, including sewage and manure. The main of aim of such integration is to reclaim nitrogen and phosphorus as biomass. Reported biomass production yields can be up to 1 kg of dry microalgae per m3 of sewage and over 10 kg per m3 of manure. These figures highlight microalgal role in nutrient recovery technologies.36 Most of the compounds present in wastewater are subsequently released into the atmosphere, primarily as carbon as CO2 and nitrogen as N2. Phosphorus, on the other hand, typically precipitates; it then needs to be removed with the digested sludge.37 According to a study, conventional wastewater treatment methods can cost as much as 0.2 €/m3 and require approximately 0.5 kWh/m3 of energy, while also releasing CO2 and nitrogen. FCC Aqualia processes 500 Mm3 annually, resulting in the emission of 1,000 kt CO2, 25 kt nitrogen, and 5 kt phosphorus—equivalent to Fertiberia’s fertilizer production. Adopting microalgae cultivation has the potential to generate 500 kilotons of biomass per year, significantly cutting CO2 emissions and saving more than 50% of the energy compared to conventional methods.11 Microalgal biomass production is influenced by solar radiation and the efficiency of photosynthesis. Solar radiation typically ranges from 150 to 350 W/m2 annually, which impacts algal growth. Photosynthetic efficiency can vary from 1% in open systems to 5% under optimized conditions.38 Also, the CO2 fixation capacity spans 40 to 200 t/ha year in low solar areas and 70 to 450 t/ha·year in tropical regions, while nitrogen recovery ranges from 2 to 10 t/ha·year and phosphorus from 0.2 to 1.1 t/ha·year in less sunny regions; tropical areas see 5 to 25 tN/ha·year and 0.5 to 2.5 tP/ha·year38,39 More researches were carried out with nutrients removal and by product development using microalgae. A study reported microalgae and microalgae–bacteria consortia are efficient for CO2 fixation from waste gases, wastewater treatment, and producing value-added products. Also, mixed cultures and consortia enhance biomass yield and nutrient removal efficiency compared to monocultures.40 Microalgae such as Chlorella sp., Pediastrum sp., Scenedesmus sp., and Phormidium sp., among others, are commonly applied, with most strains belonging to Cyanophyta and Chlorophyta. The bacteria present in microalgae–bacteria consortia mainly come from activated sludge and particular sewage sources, integrating the Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle in microalgae boost CO2 fixation and product yield.41 Compared to all nutrients, an efficient carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) removal from wastewater is crucial due to their environmental impacts. Previous studies reported nitrogen removal efficiencies in wastewater treatment plants range from 60% to 90%, impacting water quality and aquatic life (Environmental Protection Agency [EPA], 2023). Excess nitrogen, above 10 mg/L in drinking water, poses health risks like methemoglobinemia in infants (WHO, 2022). Carbon removal, up to 80% in treatment plants, helps mitigate greenhouse gas emissions (USEPA, 2021).42 Generally, in aqueous environments, nitrogen exists as inorganic (NH3, NH4+, NO2−, NO3−, and N2) and organic compounds (amino acids, amino sugars, urea, proteins, uric acid). NH3 and NH4+ equilibrium depends on pH. NO2− is unstable and toxic, quickly oxidizing to NO3−. Biologically, NH3 and NO3− are key forms of nitrogen, with organic nitrogen converted to NH3 for availability.43 The important nitrogen conversion forms are given below.TN=ON+NH3−N+NO3−−N+NO2−−NON=PON+DONTKN=ON+NH3−NTIN=NH3−N+NO3−−N+NO2−−NTON=NO3−−N+NO2−−N+N2O−NTUN=N2+NH3−N+ONwhere: TN, total nitrogen; DON, dissolved organic nitrogen; PON, particulate organic nitrogen; TKN, total Kjeldahl nitrogen; TIN, total inorganic nitrogen; TON, total oxidized nitrogen; TUN, total unoxidized nitrogen.

The carbon (C) to nitrogen (N) ratio is crucial in wastewater treatment as it directly impacts microbial activity and nutrient removal efficiency. Maintaining a balanced C ratio, typically around 20–30:1, promotes optimal microbial growth for effective organic matter degradation and nitrogen assimilation.44 One study investigated the impact of varying N/P ratios (5–30) on biomass growth and nutrient removal from municipal wastewaters using an optical panel photobioreactor (PBR). However, TP removal depends significantly on both N/P ratio and biomass productivity, indicating optimal ratios between 5 and 30 for efficient wastewater treatment.45 Another study optimized a microalgae-based treatment using Scenedesmus obliquus under various agitation and aeration conditions. This aeration enhanced biomass and chlorophyll-a content (725.32–811.16 × 104 cells mL−1, 1.58–1.69 mg L−1) compared to agitation (426.06–465.14 × 104 cells mL−1, 1.48–1.61 mg L−1). Nutrient removal efficiencies were higher in sucrose wastewater under agitation (TP 1.84–2.30 mg L−1, TN 29.62–36.39 mg L−1, 21.33%–30.67% and 44.84%–58.51%, respectively, on day 2) and in both sucrose and humic acid wastewater under aeration (TN 19.56–31.20 mg L−1, TP 0.26–0.30 mg L−1, 13.92%–46.75% and 88.36%–90.50%, respectively, on day 5). Dissolved organic matter (DOM) in the wastewater consisted mainly of humic-like substances, with higher aromaticity and humification in humic acid conditions affecting pollutant removal by algae. This study provides insights for optimizing coupled with other processes.46 Heterotrophic cultivation of Phormidium autumnale in sugarcane vinasse, shows promise due to high biomass yield and contaminant removal. Cultivation at 35°C with a C/N ratio of 16 and pH 7.5 achieved biomass productivity of 127.20 mg L−1 day−1, removing 49% organic carbon, 47% nitrogen, and 28% phosphorus. Batch cultivation improved organic carbon (67%) and nitrogen (70%) removal, with significant bioproducts: 4.26% lipids, 197.53 mg g−1 phycocyanin, 21.33% proteins, and various carbohydrates suitable for exopolysaccharides. P. autumnale effectively treats vinasse, yielding valuable bioproducts alongside wastewater purification.47 A research study reported that biochar derived from Chlorella vulgaris loaded with nZVI (BC/nZVI) reduced algal toxicity, lowering growth inhibition from 98.10% (nZVI) to 42.86%, and mitigating pigment loss (chlorophyll a 62.66%, b 58.84%, carotenoids 45.74%) and oxidative stress (SOD 163.13 U/mg, MDA 73.84 nmol/mg). Overall, BC/nZVI exhibited high reactivity with reduced toxicity, making it suitable for eco-friendly pollutant remediation.48 Further, the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are toxic emerging pollutants causing reproductive, endocrine, and carcinogenic effects in living organisms. It can be removed effectively by microalgae or consortium with bacteria/fungi, offer an eco-friendly and efficient alternative to conventional PAH. Integration of genetic engineering, nanotechnology, and AI/ML tools further enhances the effectiveness of microalgal based bioremediation.49

Reactors used in phycoremediation

The key factors that govern bioreactor performance to enhance pollutant removal efficacy are light intensity, nutrient availability, temperature and pH. Maintaining optimal pH and hydraulic retention time ensures effective algae activity and biomass productivity. The reactor design, including surface area and mixing mechanisms, plays a critical role in maximizing remediation effectiveness.50 Different types of reactors used in algae-based water treatment, namely the conventional activated sludge (CAS), MBR, bio discs sewage treatment, bio-filters, trickling filters moving bed biofilm reactor (MBBR).51 Further, a sustainable water treatment approach extended by algae-based membrane bioreactors (AMBRs), which effectively removes pharmaceuticals, antibiotics, and hydrocarbons, outperforming traditional treatment plants.52 A recent study, reports A novel microalgae membrane bioreactor integrated with an internal circulating fluidized bed demonstrated high treatment efficiency for marine aquaculture wastewater, which achieving NO3−-N of 52%, 85% of NH4+-N, and 80% PO43−-P removal.53 Another study with AMBR with osmotic membrane (OMPBR) under tertiary wastewater attained the removal of TN, 99% and TP, 100%.54 Different wastewater treatment strategies for nutrient recovery are shown in Figure 2.Figure 2Different wastewater treatment approaches for nutrient recovery

Generally, an activated sludge process efficiently treats wastewater using aerobic microorganisms in aeration tanks. It consumes organic matter, achieving high treatment efficiency, followed by effective settling and clarification of the effluent for clean water output. One study discussed, with microalgal-bacterial symbiotic system in SBR achieved, 100% removal of ammonium, high COD removal, and optimal nutrient removal (TN 53.85%, TP 85.13%) with 5:1 algae to sludge ratio. COD removal was consistently high across ratios. It also enriches the Comamonadaceae, Flavobacterium, Paenarthrobacter, Mesorhizobium, and Nitrobacter and highlighting their role in nutrient degradation and symbiosis dynamics during different stages of the reaction.55 Another study reported that a symbiotic co-culture of microalgae and activated sludge, co-culture achieved up to 93% nitrate (NO3−) and 99% phosphate (PO4−) removal, along with 73% COD reduction, with a maximum biomass yield of 7.8 g/L.56 Despite its benefits, such as effective nutrient removal and organic matter reduction, it has several limitations, including high energy consumption for aeration, sensitivity to shock loads that impact treatment efficiency, excess sludge production necessitating disposal, potential odors, and susceptibility to process upsets that affect water quality.57 Further, algae in PBRs efficiently remove pollutants like nitrogen, phosphorus, and heavy metals from wastewater, aiding in environmental cleanup. This system utilized Desmodesmus sp., a green microalgae strain that was originally isolated for this purpose. To enhance nutrient uptake rate, a low cell concentration was maintained during light inhibition periods. Strategies included cell mass recycling during high light availability and adjusting dilution rates. Compared to conventional methods, these strategies increased biomass productivity by 80% in summer and 10% in winter, whereas nitrogen/phosphorus (N/P) removal rates improved by 23% during winter and remained stable in summer.25 Another study with Scenedesmus obliquus in rice mill paddy-soaked wastewater (PSW) PBR demonstrated superior performance with maximum biomass productivity (340 ± 2 mg/L/d), NH3-N removal (96.12 ± 0.21%), and PO4-P removal (97.58 ± 0.18%). PBR also yielded the highest lipid (L ∼12% of biomass), protein (P ∼40%), and carbohydrate (C ∼20%) content compared to raceway ponds and polybags.58 Furthermore, the research extended to paper industry wastewater, which was treated using a consortium of Chlorella sp., Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter calcoaceticus in a batch PBR, achieving a biomass productivity of 3.17 g/L. Removal efficiencies reached 99.95% for total nitrogen and 95.16% for COD, highlighting effective wastewater treatment by the microalgae-bacteria consortium.59 Regarding reactor optimization, the CAS is commonly used as a biological stage in wastewater treatment, which can be easily and effectively integrated with algae culturing units, such as algal ponds, to enhance nutrient removal. The highest removals were observed as 84% DOC under blue light at 300 μmol/m2·s, and 51% DN and 80% DP under red light at 100 μmol/m2·s.60 The energy ranges limited between 0.18 and 0.8 kWh·m−3, aligning with typical wastewater treatment plant values of 0.35–0.65 kWh·m−3. In open raceway ponds (ORPs), the energy demand is 0.10–0.35 kWh m−3 with light energy utilization as 80–250 μmol photons m−2 s−1. Due to its simple construction, it has low CAPEX and OPEX, at nearly 1.98 and 9.69 EUR/kg. However, it is susceptible to contamination and weather fluctuations.61 Furthermore, regarding the high-rate algal pond (HRAP), which operates under 150–400 μmol photons·m−2·s−1 mixed light with an energy consumption of 0.29 kWh·m−3, this system has higher algal growth at a lower cost of €0.04 per m−3.62 A recent study reported an energy consumption of 0.02 kWh/m3 for HRAPs compared to 0.20–0.4 kWh/m3 for activated sludge.63 Closed PBRs are the best alternatives for open ponds, which operate with light intensity of 60–700 μmol m−2 s−, also they offer precise control over environmental and operational parameters.64 A study reports that a twisted tubular PBR design would reduce energy consumption by 38% compared to actual tubular PBR conditions.65 However, their high energy requirements for maintaining optimal conditions significantly increase overall operational costs.

A study reported with Chlorella vulgaris and native sludge bacteria consortium in batch PBRs at 24°C under continuous light achieved approximately 75% removal of total phosphorus and total nitrogen, alongside biohydrogen production rates ranging from 35% to 40%66 Another study cultivated three cyanobacterial strains namely Anabaena oryzae, Nostoc muscorum, and Spirulina platensis, were cultivated in potato industry wastewater and synthetic media. The highest growth rates were observed in the mixed cyanobacterial culture and Spirulina platensis in PIW, achieving chlorophyll contents of 3.75 and 2.30 μg·mL−1, DWs of 1.79 g·L−1, and 1.63 g L−1, and ODs of 0.41 and 0.38, respectively.67 The analysis of the lipid profile showed that the fatty acid methyl ester composition was well-suited for biodiesel production. This finding highlights the potential of C. vulgaris in mitigating environmental impacts resulting from dairy wastewater.68 Furthermore, an algal membrane PBR (AMBR) wastewater treatment with Chlorella vulgaris was investigated to reduce land use, shorten retention times, minimize fouling, and ensure efficient biomass separation, achieving substantial biomass of nearly 7.37 g/m2 and a lipid content of nearly 10.17%.25 Another study with greywater treatment using algal biomass, peaked at 48 mg/L/d with HRT, while maintaining high removal efficiencies: 94% for BOD and 96% for anionic surfactants. The highest total nitrogen (TN) and total phosphorus (TP) removals are 59.5% and 34.5%, respectively. HRT, the system achieved the highest methane yield of 0.332 m3/d and net energy return (NER) of 1.04, suggesting self-sustainability with zero power requirements.69 Another experiment with mixed bioreactors showed significant improvements in biomass production and phycoremediation of secondary urban effluent, with notable reductions in biological oxygen demand (54.34%), total phosphorus (92.4%), ammoniacal nitrogen (97.1%), and total Kjeldahl nitrogen (92.8%). Furthermore, CO2 enhances microalgal growth and cell density by 54.26% in a shorter time. Biomass yields were 0.5324 ± 0.007 g L−1 without CO2 and 0.821 ± 0.014 g L−1 with CO2.70 Furthermore, this study extends to a flat panel PBR for the mixotrophic treatment of dairy wastewater using Tetradesmus sp. SVMIICT4, achieving effective COD and nutrient removal. The biomass accumulated carbohydrate (21.48 mg g−1) and protein (19.52 mg g−1). Also, it Increased chlorophyll content nearly 18.94 mg g−1, enhanced electron transport (ET/Rc) and reduced non-photochemical quenching (NPQ).71 Raceway ponds (RWPs), another type of reactor used in phycoremediation, are characterized by circular or rectangular channels with paddlewheel circulation. These ponds strike a balance between cost-effectiveness and operational efficiency, making them suitable for larger-scale applications. Additionally, the controlled movement of algae cultures in the ponds promotes nutrient absorption and biomass production. A study reported that when impregnated activated charcoal (IAC) was used along with algal species such as Spirogyra and C. pyrenoidosa in RWPs, the system achieved more than 75.7% TN removal. Winter-spring seasons showed higher nitrogen removal, affirming both systems’ effectiveness in mitigating eutrophication from sewage.72 Another study with an 800 L scale inland pond using a native algae consortium effectively removed nutrients, such as ammonia, nitrate, phosphate, and COD, within safe discharge limits in both batch and raceway ponds. The harvested algal biomass was lipid-rich (45.87% total solids, TS). Anaerobic digestion revealed a theoretical methane potential of 745.87 mL CH4 g−1 VS, with an experimental yield of up to 630 mL CH4 g−1 VS.73 A comparative study with anaerobically digested piggery effluent (ADPE) using a thin layer reactor (TLR) and a raceway pond reported with Chlorella sp. and Scenedesmus sp, exhibited a higher ammonium removal rate of 19.23 mg N-NH4+ L−1 day−1) compared to the raceway pond. The ash-free biomass yield is 0.84 g L−1, and the average volumetric biomass productivity is 60 mg L−1 day−1, which is significantly higher by 2–2.5 times in the TLR. Wastewater treatment/inland water treatment by diverse algal species

Generally, the nutrient recovery from water depends on the nature of the species and environmental conditions. Numerous studies have been carried out to explore optimal cellular characteristics and strategies for enhancing water treatment. These investigations focus on understanding the factors such as nutrient availability, light intensity, and temperature conditions. Wastewater and inland water treatment using various algal species involves exploiting their ability to uptake nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus, thereby reducing the concentration of pollutants in the water. Algal species such as Chlorella, Scenedesmus, and Spirulina are some of the commonly employed ones, as they have been found to be efficient in nutrient uptake and biomass production. These species show great potential for water purification and eutrophication control, as the algal photosynthesis can improve water quality, supporting ecological balance and sustainable resource management.75 Phycoremediation uses microalgae to bioremediate pollutants like NO3−, PO4−, and heavy metals from wastewater (WW) sustainably without generating hazardous byproducts. The technique can be a robust alternative to conventional treatments, as it offers the advantages of being cost-effective and eco-friendly, which go a long way in shrinking the carbon footprint. Efforts include immobilization techniques, pretreatment strategies, genetic engineering, and synergistic technologies to maximize phycoremediation and produce value-added products like bioenergy application.27,76 Diatoms excel in wastewater remediation and carbon dioxide capture, while yielding valuable products. Diatom biorefinery offers sustainable wastewater management, producing biomass for biofuels, biofertilizers, animal feed supplements, and pharmaceuticals rich in EPA, DHA, and pigments like fucoxanthin.77 Most of researches were focuses to maximize biomass yield, which serves as a valuable feedstock for various applications including biodiesel production, protein extraction, and biofertilizer development after wastewater treatment. Examination of the amino acids, peptides and fatty acids, derived from microalgae harvested in a pilot-scale phycoremediation of swine wastewater. The system reached a peak microalga concentration of 247 ± 3.4 mg L−1 and achieved complete removal of phosphate and ammonium. Also, it revealed high levels (% of total protein) of leucine (4.1), lysine (2.5), phenylalanine (2.6), and threonine (2.4) in amino acids. Fatty acids included 5.3% polyunsaturated (C18:2 and C18:3) and ∼10% monounsaturated (C16:1 and C18:1).78 One recent study evaluated pollutant removal like COD, TSS, NO3-N, turbidity, P using Chlorella vulgaris in livestock effluent with closed cultivation of 5 L bottles, 100%, 75%, 50%, 25% dilutions (to meet NWQS class II standards for recreational water). Results revealed that 61.97% TSS, 39.1% COD, and 36.4% P removal, respectively.79 Hypersaline pickled mustard wastewater was bioremediated using Chaetoceros muelleri and an indigenous bacterium. Reduction of 82% in COD, 90% in ammonia nitrogen, 94% in total nitrogen, and 96% in total phosphorus were achieved in 10% PMW. Microbial analysis showed increased abundance of Halomonas and Marinobacter, facilitating nutrient recycling by diatoms.80 The use of Chloroccocum humicola and Oscillatoria sp. for nutrient removal and lipid production revealed nutrient recovery rates ranged from 69.98% to 93.72% for phosphorus and 50.55%–85.90% for nitrogen. Heavy metal removal ranged from 24.17% to 88.10% across different wastewater concentrations (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%). C. humicola effectively reduced physico-chemical parameters at 25% and 50% concentrations and achieved a notable 1.61-fold increase in lipid production at a 50% wastewater concentration.81 Another study investigated with integrated approaches with Chlorella minutissima and Chlorella sorokiniana in wastewater, and it reported Chlorella sorokiniana exhibited 19.14% higher lipids than Chlorella minutissima. Also, the biodiesel derived from Chlorella sorokiniana met USA and European standards (89.23 ± 0.21% lipid content). Both species effectively removed total phosphate (TP), total nitrogen (TN), chemical oxygen demand (COD), and total organic carbon (TOC) from wastewater, with TOC removal efficiencies of 95% and 98% for Chlorella minutissima and Chlorella sorokiniana, respectively.82 Further, phycoremediation potential of Chlorella vulgaris is demonstrated with industrial waste water to remove nutrients like 88.6% NH4-N and 91.2% PO4-P removal in synthetic wastewater, and effective reduction of NH4-N (68.6%), NO3-N (74%), PO4-P (71.5%), and COD (90.2%) in secondary treated sewage.83 Another study found that Chlorella fusca cell density was highest in treatments with 50% wastewater concentration, followed by 100%, 75%, the control, and 25%. BOD showed a 75% reduction at 100% wastewater addition. DOC levels decreased by 41% and 40% in 50% wastewater and 100% wastewater, respectively, while total nitrogen (TN) dropped by 55% at a 50% wastewater level.84 Another study reported that Chlorella fusca cell density increased in treatments with 50% urban wastewater (UW) concentration, followed by the treatment with 100% UW, 75% UW, the control, and finally 25% UW. Additionally, the protein content increased to 50.5% at a 75% UW concentration. The biological oxygen demand (BOD5) analysis indicated a 75% decrease in 100% UW concentration. Dissolved organic carbon (DOC) levels decreased by 41% and 40% in 50% UW and 100% UW concentration, and total nitrogen (TN) decreased by 55% in 50% UW concentration.85 Further the nutrient removal can be enhanced using consortium cultivation.86 Six microalgal species, namely Scenedesmus abundans, Chlorella vulgaris, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Coelastrum microporum, Nannochloropsis salina, and Dunaliella tertiolecta, were used for remediation of municipal wastewater reverse osmosis concentrate (ROC). It observed that S. abundans, C. vulgaris, and N. salina showed promise under salinity stress, N. salina adapted well to ROC conditions, while S. abundans demonstrated superior growth and salinity tolerance compared to C. vulgaris. Both S. abundans and N. salina effectively removed nutrients and organic matter by 11.5–18 mg/L/d TN, 7.1–8.2 mg/L/d TP, 8.6–12.4 mg/L/d DOC. Also, biochemical methane potential tests indicated significant methane production from algal biomass up to 422 mL CH4/g VS for N. salina, highlighting its potential for energy production via anaerobic digestion without requiring pretreatment.87 Another study assessed with Chlorella vulgaris efficacy in treating municipal wastewater, using primary treated effluent from a sewage treatment plant. Non-filtered effluent removal efficiencies of 98.32% for phosphate, 97.26% for ammonia, and 84.71% for COD was lower than filtered effluent i.e., 98.53% for phosphate, 98.63% for ammonia, and 89.41% for COD.88 The Chlamydomonas reinhardtii demonstrated the optimum performance and TP removal from 2 mg L−1 to 0.02 mg L−1. It significantly reduced TP and TN in domestic wastewater, achieving removal efficiencies up to 99.15% and 99.07%, respectively. Nutrient removal from different water reservoirs and wastewaters

Various types of wastewater reservoirs include industrial effluent ponds, municipal sewage treatment lagoons, agricultural runoff retention basins, and natural wetlands. Each environment presents unique advantages and disadvantages for using plants to improve water quality by removing pollutants such as heavy metals, organic compounds, and excess nutrients. Residual biomass from extraction processes can be repurposed for energy generation and other valuable products.91 Another study assessed mushroom farm wastewater (MFW) treatment using microalga Chlamydomonas asymmetrica strain SAG70.72 isolated from the Ganga River, with 0%, 50%, and 100% MFW showed significant reductions (p < 0.05) in TDS (87%), BOD (93%), COD (92%), TKN (87%), and TP (97%) in 50% MFW. Higher heavy metal uptake in treatment experimental devices TEDs with combined treatments compared to controls, supported by higher bioaccumulation factors. Biomass of P. australis was greater in TEDs with algae. HM translocation factor (TF) for Cr, Cu, Cd, and Pb remained ≥1, indicating effective phytoextraction.93 Another study examined Spirulina platensis cultivation in textile wastewater (TWW1 and TWW2), demonstrating effective COD reduction by 62.59% for TWW1, 46.68% for TWW2, which aligned with first-order kinetics. Nutrient removal rates showed phosphate reductions as 36.42% for TWW1 and 62.18% for TWW2, and ammonia reductions as 59.34% for TWW1, 69.31% for TWW2. Seed germination tests with Vigna radiata indicated enhanced growth using treated wastewater. This outcome suggests that there exists potential for their in use in irrigation. Increased lipid and carbohydrate content following treatment supports biofuel production. As the biofuel can be put to use, this contribute to the sustainability dimension of phycoremediation, especially in water management.94 Further development of this study into large-scale ponds, specifically using HRAPs, offers an alternative to traditional wastewater treatment methods. Recent advancement in the phycoremediation of inland water reservoir

Recent advancements in phycoremediation techniques for inland water reservoirs have shown promising developments. By-products from treated biomass and techno-economic study

Phycoremediation provides a sustainable and eco-friendly approach to wastewater treatment, effectively removing nitrogen, phosphorus, and metal ions and also yields the products of market interest. Pilot-scale integration of this approach remains unexplored despite demonstrated efficacy in treating swine wastewater. It involves analyzing the costs associated with algae cultivation, harvesting, processing, and product recovery against the benefits of reduced pollutant levels, resource recovery (such as biomass for biofuels or bioproducts), and potential revenue streams. Factors considered include capital investment, operational costs, energy requirements, market value of products, and environmental benefits. Various nutrient removal technologies and their advantages

Special emphasis on mechanism of C and N uptake by algae cell

The mechanisms of C and N uptake by algae cells involve several key processes. First, algae absorb carbon dioxide (CO2) from their surroundings through diffusion and actively transport it using carbonic anhydrase enzymes. The CO2 is then converted it into bicarbonate ions inside the cells of the algae. This is a key process in photosynthesis, as CO2 is fixed into organic carbon compounds, such as sugars.178 Regarding nitrogen uptake, algae mainly absorb nitrogen primarily in the form of ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3−) from their environment. For the uptake of ammonium, transport systems across the algal cell membrane are activated by proton gradients or ATP hydrolysis. Nitrate uptake, on the other hand, involves transport proteins that move nitrate ions into the cell. Once inside, these ions are reduced to ammonium or incorporated into amino acids and proteins. These uptake mechanisms are affected by environmental factors: nutrient availability, pH, light intensity, and temperature are some of the factors reported in the literature. Studies have been conducted to understand these processes, as they are vital for optimizing algae growth in various applications, including wastewater treatment, biofuel production, and bioremediation.179,180 For example, in one study involving FTIR analysis, 13C isotope incorporation was tracked all the way up to individual algal cells. The analysis revealed C- uptake and allocation across carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids. Spectral shifts in Spirogyra indicated significant cellulose incorporation at 1,160 cm−1, 1,108 cm−1, 1,080 cm−1, 1,048 cm−1, and 1,030 cm−1 peaks. Natural phytoplankton studies showed distinct C patterns; for instance, diatoms underwent observable changes in proteins and carbohydrates, while green algae and euglenoids showed fewer shifts.181 However, challenges such as culture contamination and prokaryotic overgrowth hinder productivity and stability in microalgal biorefineries. Automated online flow cytometry has been identified as a powerful tool for optimizing bioprocesses, as it provides real-time, high-resolution monitoring of microbial dynamics. One study has pioneered the application of this technique in microalgal ecosystems. In particular, it used fluorescence emissions to quantify and assess the diversity of Chlorella vulgaris, simultaneously, considering a range of concentrations (2.2–1,002.4 cells ·μL−1).182 The evolution of oxygenic photosynthesis led to reduced atmospheric CO2 level and increased O2 levels, restricting the activity of RUBISCO (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) during photosynthesis. To overcome this, algae and certain plants have developed CO2-concentrating mechanisms. These ensure greater availability of CO2 around RUBISCO, but variations have been noted by researchers among different algal groups.183

C removal mechanism

Algal strains utilize various mechanisms to remove carbon (C) from wastewater, all of which play crucial roles in cleaning and enhancing the quality of aquatic environments. Microalgae employ several mechanisms to remove carbon (C) from wastewater, contributing significantly to the purification of aquatic environments. In this section, key carbon removal mechanisms utilized by microalgae are highlighted.

Photosynthesis

CO2 fixation: microalgae utilize carbon dioxide (CO2) dissolved in wastewater as a carbon source for photosynthesis. Through the action of the enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RUBISCO), CO2 is incorporated into organic molecules, primarily sugars, within the algal cells.184,185 As a byproduct of photosynthesis, microalgae release oxygen (O2) into the wastewater. This process not only supports aerobic microbial activities but also enhances the dissolved oxygen levels in the water, crucial for the survival of aquatic organisms and overall ecosystem health.186

Biochemical assimilation

In addition to CO2, microalgae can also utilize and assimilate various organic carbon compounds present in wastewater. These may include sugars, organic acids, and other carbon-containing molecules that originate from organic pollutants or biomass decomposition.187 The carbon compounds taken up by microalgae are used for their cellular growth, maintenance, and energy production. The assimilation further helps in reducing the organic carbon load in wastewater, contributing to its purification.188 Certain microalgal species, especially coccolithophores and few green algae, have the ability to precipitate calcium carbonate (CaCO3) under specific environmental conditions. This process removes dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC), such as bicarbonate ions (HCO3–) and carbonate ions (CO32–), from the wastewater.189 As part of the biomineralization process, the precipitation of calcium carbonate can occur either within or outside algal cells, depending on the algal species and the availability of calcium and carbonate ions in the wastewater. This mechanism contributes to both carbon removal and carbonate alkalinity regulation in aquatic systems.190

Nitrogen removal mechanisms

Microalgae assimilate nitrogen in wastewater by absorbing ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3−) through specialized transport proteins; NH4+ is directly incorporated into amino acids for protein synthesis,191 while NO3− is first reduced to NH4+ before assimilation. Additionally, nitrification-denitrification involves the microbial conversion of NH4+ to nitrite (NO2−) and nitrate (NO3−) by nitrifying bacteria, followed by reduction to nitrogen gas (N2) by denitrifying bacteria under anaerobic conditions.192 Some microalgae also actively exchange NH4+ across cell membranes, concentrating nitrogen intracellularly.193 Overall, microalgal systems achieve NH4+ removal efficiencies of 70%–90%, NO3− removal of 50%–90%, and NO2− removal of 20%–70%, depending on species, light intensity, and nutrient conditions, thereby enhancing nitrogen cycling and improving wastewater quality.194 Further, the nitrate reductase (NR) and glutamine synthetase–glutamate synthase (GS–GOGAT) pathways play significant roles in algal nitrogen assimilation and removal efficiency.195 The reduction of nitrate (NO3−) into nitrite (NO2−), followed by nitrite reductase converting it to ammonium (NH4+), which is then incorporated into amino acids through the GS–GOGAT cycle. In this process, GS converts NH4+ and glutamate into glutamine, and GOGAT regenerates glutamate using 2-oxoglutarate from carbon metabolism. Optimal coordination between pathways enhances nitrogen removal by facilitating rapid assimilation into cellular biomass, thereby tightly linking carbon fixation and nitrogen metabolism in various algal species.196

Factors affecting algae-based nutrient removal

Algae are photoautotrophic organisms that utilize light to produce essential chemical compounds for growth through the process of photosynthesis. Photosynthesis, stimulated by inorganic carbon supply, light, and temperature, enables the absorption of nutrients.197 Most of the studies were discussed with biomass production under the factors associated with the above. Research investigating the impact of light colors on PBRs using Chlamydomonas reinhardtii indicated that blue and red lights are less effective in generating algal biomass. The highest productivity was observed under warm white and yellow lights. The photoperiod, light intensity, and temperature play a major role in nutrient removal and value products. A research study with Ulva prolifera, conducted under a 12:12 light/dark cycle at 25°C and a light density of 400 μmol m−2 s−1, yields better performance. An increase in light density decreases chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv′/Fm′, NPQ, rETRmax).198 Another study with Coriandrum sativum L. reports an optimal efficiency and yield attained with a light intensity of 200 μmol m−2 s−1 and a 16-h photoperiod.199 Chlorella vulgaris in food processing wastewater treatment achieved 73% COD removal with a biomass yield of 0.44 g/L under a 12H:12H photoperiod. In contrast, a 24 h light cycle resulted in 75% COD removal with a biomass yield of 1.02 g/L.200 Furthermore, Lemna minor and Lemna trisulca exhibited greater growth under higher temperatures (25°C) and longer photoperiods (24 h). Lemna minor achieved maximum biomass and surface coverage under optimal conditions of 25°C and 12 h of light, indicating efficient resource utilization. In contrast, Lemna trisulca exhibited stable growth at lower temperatures 15°C, 12 h light, demonstrating better adaptability to variable physicochemical conditions.201 Scenedesmus sp. cultivated in an outdoor raceway pond achieved a maximum productivity of 12.5 ± 0.6 g m−2 d−1 during summer, which is 30% higher than that observed in winter, attributed to the photoperiod. Seasonal light fluctuations also affect nutrient uptake, with complete ammoniacal nitrogen and 85% phosphorus removal, demonstrating effective coupling of photosynthetic activity with wastewater remediation.202 Additionally, supplementing yellow light with blue light enhanced growth and overall culture capability.203 A recent study reported that the biomass production of Desmodesmus sp. cultures increased with light intensity. However, raising the light intensity from 150 to 300 μE m−2 s−1 did not significantly affect the biomass of Chlorella vulgaris and Scenedesmus obliquus, which were approximately 0.6 and 0.8 g/L, respectively, at 150 μE m−2 s−1 after one week. Thus, 150 μE m−2 s−1 was optimal for biomass production in these two species.204 Light significantly influences algal growth and nutrient utilization efficiency. In controlled indoor systems, artificial light sources can enhance the photosynthetic activity of microalgae. Microalgae photosynthetic systems are most efficient in the blue (around 400 nm) and red (600–700 nm) regions of the light spectrum, optimizing the utilization of nitrogen and phosphorus from wastewater.205 Red light, in particular, stimulates algal growth. Although using artificial light increases the cost of wastewater treatment, light-emitting diode (LED) technology, with a longer lifespan and lower electricity consumption, makes the use of artificial light sources more viable. This enhances photosynthetic activity and nutrient uptake from the water body, improving the overall efficiency of the treatment process.206 Many studies have demonstrated specific light preferences among different microalgae species, Spirulina platensis exhibited a 38% increase in growth rate under red light. Optimal productivity for Chlorella vulgaris was achieved using green light and research on high-density cultures of Scenedesmus bijuga indicated that green light, which is weakly absorbed, enhances photosynthetic efficiency.207,208 In open water systems, light availability varies with depth and can be limited by dense algae and other microorganisms. Proper water circulation is crucial for optimal microalgae exposure, as prolonged exposure to intense surface sunlight can damage photosystem II and reduce photosynthetic efficiency.209 In addition to light, temperature has a strong influence on algae growth in various bioremediation systems. It has been found that algae species thrive best when the temperature ranges between 15°C and 30 °C. However, closed PBRs exposed to sunlight can exceed 40 °C, so cooling systems need to be installed to maintain optimal growth conditions, which in turn increases treatment costs.210,211 At approximately 36 °C, algal growth rates reportedly decrease by 7%–10%—heat stress is thus a detriment to growth. Arctic strains of Chlorella were found to show optimum growth between 6°C and 20 °C (μ = 0.19–0.22 days–1), but tolerated growth up to 30 °C. Another study reported that the tolerable temperature range of Chlorella vulgaris can tolerate temperatures is from 15°C to 40 °C. Overall, microalgae species grow optimally at around 15 °C, with growth inhibition occurring when temperatures exceed their optimal range by 2°C–4°C.212,213 The pH level plays a crucial role in algae-based wastewater treatment systems because it affects many biochemical and physiological processes within the algae cells. In general, algae grow better and take up nutrients more efficiently in neutral to slightly alkaline conditions. For example, Chlorella spp. typically grows best in a pH range between 6.5 and 8.5.214 Additionally, studies indicate that the pH level affects the solubility and availability of nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, in wastewater. At higher pH levels, ammonia (NH3/NH4+) mostly exists in the toxic ammonia (NH3) form, which can inhibit algal growth. Conversely, at lower pH, the ammonium ion (NH4+) becomes more dominant and is generally less harmful to the cells.215 This parameter influences the composition of algal biomass as well, including its lipid content, protein synthesis, and carbohydrate production. For instance, under certain pH conditions, algae tend to increase lipid accumulation, which is beneficial for biodiesel production. However, it is still important to regulate temperature and nutrient concentrations, so that optimum conditions for algal growth and nutrient removal efficiency can be established.216,217,218 In open pond systems, pH alone is insufficient to control pathogen levels effectively. Factors like temperature, dissolved oxygen, and solar radiation must also be managed, as they are closely interconnected and can directly influence pathogen concentrations. Increased solar radiation, for instance, can reduce pathogens in water or stimulate algal growth.219,220 CO2 is a fundamental substrate for photosynthesis in microalgae. During this process, microalgae use CO2 together with water and light energy to produce carbohydrates and release oxygen. In wastewater treatment applications, microalgae remove nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus from the water; simultaneously, they produce biomass, which can be harvested and processed for use in various applications such as biofuels, bioplastics, and animal feed additive.221 During wastewater treatment, algae rely on sources of inorganic carbon, primarily supplied through the aerobic respiration of bacteria within the wastewater. This process releases CO2, which algae utilize for photosynthesis, thereby promoting their growth.222 Additionally, researchers have suggested supplementing with extra CO2 as a proactive measure to enhance algal biomass production. Another critical source of inorganic carbon is bicarbonate ions (HCO3−); these ions are formed during the hydrolysis of CO2, which significantly enhances algal growth rates during water treatment.223

In addition to cultivation factors, the use of nanoparticles, such as magnetic nanoparticles and metal oxide nanoparticles like CeO2, ZnO, Fe3O4, and Al2O3, plays a significant role in enhancing the removal of contaminants. Biochar-loaded nano zero-valent iron (nZVI/BC) improved nZVI stability and effectively removed Microcystis aeruginosa while reducing toxicity to E. coli compared to bare nZVI, where strong ROS-induced oxidative stress caused rapid cell inactivation, indicating that nanoparticle surface modification can balance reactivity and environmental safety.224 In some cases, nanoparticles and pharmaceuticals persist in the water even after treatment. Its presence in a water body for a prolonged duration primarily affects the ecological system due to its toxicity.225 Further, it damages algae and other aquatic organisms, leading to bioaccumulation, trophic transfer up the food chain, and lasting ecosystem damage.226 A study discusses that ZnO has significantly toxic effects on microalgae Chlorella vulgaris and Scenedesmus obliquus, with this toxicity mechanism involving the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which results in the release of toxic zinc ions (ZN2+) and causes physical damage, disrupting essential physiological processes like photosynthesis and nutrient uptake.227 Further study with ZnO and Fe2O3 nanoparticles exhibited dose-dependent toxicity toward Chlorella vulgaris, causing growth inhibition, oxidative stress, and cell membrane damage, with significantly lower IC50 values (0.258 and 12.99 mg L−1) than their bulk forms. The enhanced toxicity of nanoforms highlights their greater ecological risk to aquatic microalgae and potential disruption of aquatic ecosystems.227 Binary exposure of Chlorella vulgaris to ZnO nanoparticles and tetrabromobisphenol A (TBBPA) caused severe cellular damage, oxidative stress, and chlorophyll reduction, while humic acid reduced this synergistic toxicity highlighting that nanoparticle–pollutant interactions can amplify ecological risks and disrupt aquatic primary productivity.228 Genetic/metabolic engineering of algal wild types is an emerging area of research in bioremediation. The limitation of wild strain for pollutant removal is overcome by genetic engineering approach. Genetic modification through plasmid conjugation (overexpressing the CPA1, CPA2, and CPA3 genes) and random mutagenesis (RM) have been employed to improve the salt removal efficiency of microalgae. These approaches also enhance self-flocculation ability and promote higher lipid accumulation, thereby improving overall performance.229 Further, microalgal Metallothioneins and Phytochelatins are majorly used for heavy metal removal. Genetically modified Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and co-cultured with engineered Pseudomonas stutzeri attained superior N2 and NH3 removal through improved CO2/O2 exchange and nutrient cycling.230

Challenges and recommendations

Using phycoremediation to clean water streams and remove nutrients can be a very cost-effective compared to using conventional methods, although large-scale cultivation still poses certain challenges. One of the challenges is selecting suitable microalgae among a million species to identify strains, specifically for industrial and WWT applications; here, high-through-put screening can be helpful. The screening process will then guide the design of algal groups that can adapt to diverse wastewater compositions. During phycoremediation, reducing the hydraulic retention time (HRT) is crucial, as it directly influences the system’s energy efficiency per cubic meter of treated water. To reduce HRT, the integration of attached cultivation with fast-growing microalgae has been proposed as a potential approach, which also reduces operational costs. Another reason for recommending this technique is that research has shown that there is scope for further advancements in, among other aspects, strain selection, consortia design, and operational efficiencies. Like any innovation, algae cultivation also faces challenges in terms of productivity, energy consumption, and economics. For example, the solid-liquid separation process can be quite energy-intensive. For effective dewatering, separation, and drying, specific solutions need to be synthesized. Additionally, cultivation, harvesting, and biomass extraction all contribute to the costs. One factor that favors this technique is that industries worldwide often have ample space available. Establishing large-scale algae cultivation units near water reservoirs is another recommendation that has frequently come up in studies. Additionally, for heavy metal removal, the authors recommend incorporating a separate filtration system, which would further enhance the overall water treatment efficiency. These approaches effectively utilize existing industrial areas while leveraging the natural capacity of algae to purify wastewater, thereby supporting both environmental objectives and regulatory compliance. In the long run, investing in R&D to improve and optimize indigenous algae species can be a long-term strategy for reducing costs, while the by-products generated during wastewater treatment could support national economies. Local algal species are naturally adaptable, and cultivation conditions can be optimized to maximize this trait. Doing so not only improves nutrient removal but also increases biomass output, from which valuable by-products such as proteins, lipids, enzymes, and vitamins can be obtained. Additionally, genetic/metabolic engineering of algal wild types is an emerging area of research in bioremediation. The limitation of the wild strain for pollutant removal is overcome by a genetic engineering approach. Overall, integrating advanced cultivation techniques with targeted genetic engineering strategies can greatly enhance the capabilities of native algae, thereby paving the way for sustainable solutions for inland water remediation in the future.

Conclusion

This review presents a holistic discussion of inland and wastewater nutrient removal through phycoremediation, a process that utilizes nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and minerals as nutrients, while also removing contaminants from water bodies. Key insights derived from this review are presented below. Different algal species (e.g., Chlorella, Scenedesmus, Spirulina, Phaeodactylum tricornutum, Nannochloropsis, Dunaliella, and Euglena) are known for their effectiveness in wastewater treatment. These species adeptly extract nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, significantly aiding in water purification efforts. An integrated ozonation and phycoremediation in treating tannery effluent, achieving 60%–80% COD removal with 60% color removal and 100% odor removal. Among the reactors, fluidized-bed reactors with Chlorella vulgaris or Scenedesmus abundans for treatment of secondary effluent showed efficient removal of TP and TN. Under optimized experimental conditions, the microalgal consortia exhibited 86% phosphate, 70% nitrogen, and 70% P removal, along with concurrent production of biomass and lutein from tolerant biomass. As a biorefinery approach, the lipid content from treated algal biomass can serve as a parent material for high-quality biodiesel production, which contains desirable fatty acids such as C16:0, C16:1, C18:1, C18:2, and C18:3. Algae have the ability to utilize C, N, and P, which are commonly found in inland water, and therefore, inland water/wastewater from different sources can be bioremediated by algae at a point source in an environmentally benign approach. However, genetic/metabolic engineering of wild-type algae represents a rapidly advancing area in bioremediation research. Through targeted genetic modifications, the inherent limitations of natural strains in C and N removal, as well as other pollutant removal, can be effectively overcome. As a path to future study, advanced genome-editing tools, such as CRISPR-Cas9, TALENs, and RNAi, enable the efficient regulation or knockout of genes to enhance nutrient uptake and increase stress tolerance. Additionally, overexpression of antioxidant and osmoprotectant genes strengthens cellular defense mechanisms under adverse wastewater conditions.
