Areca nut extract exposure disrupts myogenesis and metabolism in C2C12 cells
Authors: Chi-Wei Chen, Tsung-Teng Huang, Yan-Ru Chen, Chin-Hung Liu, Chia-Hsun Hsieh, Tai-Lin Chen, Kuo-Chu Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Approximately 10 % of the global population uses betel quid, a preparation containing areca nut, with the practice being concentrated in countries such as India, Taiwan, parts of southern China, and Southeast Asia (Arora and Squier, 2019, Lee et al., 2011). Over the past 25 years, government and nongovernmental organization (NGO) initiatives promoting health education and support for quitting betel nut use in communities, schools, and workplaces, along with regular oral mucosal screenings, have helped decrease the incidence of oral cancer and the proportion of betel nut chewers (Lee et al., 2011, Yang et al., 2020). According to statistics from the Ministry of Health and Welfare of Taiwan in 2018, 2.2–10.7 % of males aged 18–65 years habitually chew betel nut, with the highest proportion found among the age group of 40–49 years (Yang et al., 2020). This habit has contributed to oral and pharyngeal cancers, which consistently rank as the fourth most common cancer among males in Taiwan (Lee et al., 2011). The primary constituents of areca nut (AN), commonly known as betel nut, include carbohydrates, proteins, crude fiber, alkaloids, polyphenols (flavonols and tannins), fatty acids, and certain trace elements (Humans, 2004, Kant et al., 2014, Meutia Sari et al., 2017). Existing research has revealed several health impacts of AN consumption (Garg et al., 2014). AN has been shown to be a carcinogen, and AN chewing is an established cause of oral cancer and oral cancer-related deaths (Mehrtash et al., 2017, Warnakulasuriya and Chen, 2022). AN contains several bioactive components, such as arecoline, procyanidin, luteolin, and catechin, each of which contributes to its physiological and pharmacological effects (Sun et al., 2024). Numerous studies have shown that arecoline has genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity, nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity and neurotoxicity (Liu et al., 2016).

Areca nut extract (ANE) and arecoline can interfere with the differentiation of 3T3-L1 adipocytes by suppressing the expression of key proteins involved in adipogenesis and insulin signaling (Hsieh et al., 2011). This disruption leads to insulin resistance in adipocytes, altering metabolic pathways and causing metabolic dysfunction (Hsieh et al., 2011). Despite the extensive traditional use of ANs and their well-documented bioactive properties, a notable gap in the scientific literature exists regarding their specific effects on myogenesis (Sun et al., 2024). While some studies have explored the general health impacts of AN chewing, particularly its genotoxic and carcinogenic potential, few studies have focused on its influence on muscle cell differentiation and development (Senevirathna et al., 2023). Comprehensive studies investigating how ANE affects the various stages of myogenesis, from myoblast proliferation to myotube formation, are lacking.

Myogenesis is the process of muscle tissue formation during embryonic development and regeneration throughout an organism’s life (Bentzinger et al., 2012). Myogenesis involves several well-defined stages: myoblast proliferation, differentiation, fusion into myotubes, and maturation into myofibers (Chal and Pourquie, 2017). Muscle precursor cells, known as myoblasts, first proliferate and increase in number. Then, they exit the cell cycle and begin to differentiate by expressing specific muscle markers (Lin et al., 2013). Differentiated myoblasts fuse to form multinucleated myotubes and undergo further maturation and alignment to form functional muscle fibers capable of contraction (Alarcin et al., 2021). This process is regulated by a network of transcription factors, including myogenic differentiation protein 1 (MYOD1), myogenin (MYOG), myogenic factor 5 (Myf5), and myogenic regulatory factor 4 (MRF4), which control the expression of muscle-specific genes (Hernandez-Hernandez et al., 2017). In addition, the Notch and Wnt signaling pathways have been reported to be essential for orchestrating the timing and extent of myoblast proliferation and differentiation (Tsivitse, 2010). Understanding myogenesis is fundamental for addressing various muscle-related diseases and conditions, including muscular dystrophy, muscle atrophy, and sarcopenia (Baig et al., 2022).

Metabolic activity is intimately linked to myogenesis, as the energy demands of proliferating and differentiating myocytes necessitate robust metabolic support (Belli et al., 2019). Myocytes rely on glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and lipid metabolism to meet their energy needs during different stages of myogenesis (Sin et al., 2016). Proper metabolic function ensures that myocytes have sufficient amounts of ATP and biosynthetic precursors to support their growth and differentiation (Hargreaves and Spriet, 2020). Additionally, fatty acid oxidation provides an alternative energy source during myogenesis, particularly in the later stages of myotube maturation and muscle fiber formation (Park et al., 2023). Understanding these metabolic pathways and their regulation is essential for understanding how metabolic reprogramming affects myogenesis. Insights into these processes can provide valuable information for developing therapeutic strategies to enhance muscle regeneration and treat muscle-related diseases.

Metabolic activity plays a key role in muscle cell differentiation and function, and any alterations in metabolic pathways can significantly impact myogenesis (Zumbaugh et al., 2022). ANE contains several bioactive compounds that can reprogram cellular metabolism, affecting myocyte differentiation and muscle formation (Sun et al., 2024). However, the related mechanisms by which ANE induces metabolic reprogramming in myocytes remain unclear. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for elucidating the broader implications of AN consumption for muscle health and development. The present research addresses the gap in the literature by investigating the metabolic effects of ANE on C2C12 cells and how these changes influence myogenic differentiation. Furthermore, by revealing the effects of ANE on myogenesis, this study provides insights into how the consumption of AN may influence muscle development and regeneration. The findings from this study could contribute to a broader understanding of how dietary and environmental factors influence myogenesis. Additionally, elucidating the metabolic pathways affected by ANE may inspire new therapeutic interventions for muscle atrophy and other muscle-related disorders.

Materials and methods

ANE preparation

The preparation of extracts of ripe and tender ANs was performed using a previously described method (Huang et al., 2010, Nair et al., 1987) that was kindly provided by Dr. Tsung-Yun Liu (Institute of Food Safety and Health Risk Assessment, National Yang-Ming Chiao Tung University, Taipei, Taiwan). Fresh tender areca nuts, the fruit of Areca catechu L., were purchased from local shops in Taipei, Taiwan. After the husks were removed, the nuts were extracted three times with 80 % acetone (1:10 w/v). The acetone was then removed using a vacuum evaporator (Büchi RE111, Flawil, Switzerland) under reduced pressure. The aqueous residue was subsequently partitioned with n-hexane and ethyl ether to remove lipid materials. After the aqueous layer was lyophilized, the resulting light yellow powder was used as the ANE and stored at − 20°C under argon. The yields of the extracts were 26 and 12 % for tender and ripe ANs, respectively (Liu et al., 1996).

Cell culture

The C2C12 murine myoblast cell line (BCRC 60083) was obtained from the Bioresources Collection and Research Center (BCRC) at the Food Industry Research and Development Institute (FIRDI; Hsinchu, Taiwan). C2C12 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 3.7 g/L sodium bicarbonate (Sigma‒Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA), 1 % antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). C2C12 cells were cultured in a 37 °C humidified incubator with a 5 % CO2 atmosphere.

C2C12 cell myogenic differentiation and treatment

Previous studies have utilized C2C12 cells to investigate myogenesis (Berdichevsky et al., 2010). In this study, C2C12 myoblasts (1.5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in a 24-well culture plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After the incubation, the cells were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove the culture medium. For differentiation into myotubes, the medium was replaced with differentiation medium (DM), which consisted of DMEM supplemented with 2 % horse serum (HS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific); this time point was designated as Day 0. Undifferentiated C2C12 myoblasts were cultured in DM containing ANE for 5 days. In parallel, the cells were incubated in DM for 4 days to induce differentiation, and the DM was replaced at intervals of 48 h. The differentiated C2C12 myotubes (4 days after DM application) were then treated with ANE for 3 days.

Immunofluorescence staining

Treated or untreated myotubes were fixed with 100 % ice-cold methanol, permeabilized using 0.3 % Triton X-100 (Sigma‒Aldrich), and then incubated in PBS containing 2 % bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were subsequently incubated with the primary antibody MYH4-FITC (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 4 °C overnight to label the myotubes and with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for nuclear staining. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope equipped with a digital camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The fusion index was calculated as the number of MYH4+ cell nuclei divided by the total number of nuclei. The myotube diameter and the fusion index were analyzed in a blinded manner, with at least 100 myotubes analyzed per condition using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay

We conducted an SRB assay to determine the effects of the ANE on myoblasts and differentiated myotubes, as described previously. SRB, a bright pink amino xanthene dye, binds to cellular proteins under slightly acidic conditions. The dye selectively binds to basic amino acid residues in proteins and forms a stable complex. The amount of dye bound to the proteins is proportional to the total protein content of the cells, which serves as an indirect measure of the cell density (Voigt, 2005). Briefly, C2C12 cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded into a 96-well culture plate and incubated overnight to allow adherence. Cytotoxicity in myoblasts was evaluated by treating the cells with ANE for 72 h beginning the day after seeding. For differentiated myotubes, cytotoxicity was tested by applying the treatments for 72 h following a 4-day DM incubation. After ANE treatment, the cells were fixed with 10 % trichloroacetic acid (Sigma‒Aldrich), air-dried, and stained with a 0.4 % (w/v) SRB solution. Cell viability was then determined by measuring the absorbance at 565 nm using a VersaMax microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR)

qRT‒PCR was performed according to the MIQE guidelines (Bustin et al., 2009). C2C12 myoblasts and differentiated myotubes seeded in 24-well plates (1.5 × 105 cells per well) treated with or without ANE were lysed with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocols. A total of 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Afterward, the synthesized cDNAs were mixed with the specific primers (250 nM) listed in Supplementary Table 1 and SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Real-time PCR amplification was performed using a LightCycler 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche). The PCR cycling parameters were 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s for 40 cycles. GAPDH was used as a reference gene. The relative gene expression levels were calculated as ΔCt=Ct gene − Ct reference, and the fold change in gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from 1.5 to 2 × 106 cells using PRO-PREP™ Protein Extraction Solution (iNtRON Biotechnology, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea), and the protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were stored at –80 °C until further experiments. Proteins from each sample were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) on 8–12 % gels and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with TBST (Tris-buffered saline/Tween-20) buffer and then incubated with the primary antibodies and corresponding secondary antibodies. The protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent detection system (Millipore), and the membranes were imaged using a ChemiDoc XRS + Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The primary MYOD1 antibody was from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA), and the anti-MYH4 antibody was obtained from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA). The primary anti-MYOG, anti-rabbit HRP and anti-mouse HRP secondary antibodies were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Primary antibodies against p-mTOR (Ser2448), p-p70S6 kinase (Thr389), p62 and LC3-II/LC3-I were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), and the anti-GAPDH antibody was purchased from GeneTex (San Antonio, TX, USA). The band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to that of the loading control.

Metabolomic analysis

A total of 1.5 × 106 C2C12 myoblasts were seeded in 100 mm culture dishes, cultured for 2 days, and subjected to myogenic differentiation and treatment according to the established protocol. At the end of the treatment period, the medium was removed, the cells were washed twice with 10 mL of ice-cold PBS, and then 1 mL of ice-cold 80 % methanol aqueous solution was added and the cells were incubated at –80 °C for 15 min to fully extract metabolic intermediates. The cells were scraped into the solution, transferred to 1.5 mL microtubes, and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was removed for the metabolomic analysis. Sample pretreatment and analysis were performed with the assistance of the Metabolome Core Laboratory of the Center for Healthy Aging Research at Chang Gung University. The samples were blown dry with nitrogen gas and then redissolved in 200 mL of a 1 % acetic acid aqueous solution. A 5 mL sample was injected and analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using a Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometry system (Waters, Massachusetts, USA) with electrospray ionization (ESI) in negative ion mode at −3000 V and a 2.1 × 100 mm BEH C18 column (Waters) at a flow rate of 400 mL/min. The mobile phases were solution A (10 mM tributylamine (TBA) and 15 mM acetic acid in distilled water) and solution B (10 mM TBA, 15 mM acetic acid, and 50 % acetonitrile in distilled water). The data were processed using MassLynx V4.1 software (Waters). The contents analyzed included 57 metabolites related to amino acids, carbohydrate metabolism, lactate, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), energy metabolites, coenzyme A (CoA), the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP), the urea cycle, glutathione, and oxidized glutathione.

Statistical analysis

The data are shown as the means ± standard errors of the means (S.E.Ms.) from at least three independent experiments. SigmaPlot version 13.0 (Systat Software, San Jose, CA) was used to create the figures. The two groups of samples were compared using the two-tailed Student’s t test. Statistical significance is indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, or ***P < 0.001.

Results

Morphological and molecular changes during C2C12 myoblast differentiation

We analyzed the levels of differentiation-related markers, including MYOD1, MYOG, and MYH4 (Ko et al., 2006, Miyake et al., 2011, Tajbakhsh et al., 1997), during myogenesis in the absence of ANE to characterize the baseline expression of myogenic markers. This approach allowed us to address the natural progression of myogenesis without any drug interference. MYOD1 expression decreased significantly on Days 2, 4 and 6. The expression of the MYOD1 protein mirrored its mRNA pattern, with significant downregulation observed by Day 4. MYOG mRNA and protein levels markedly increased on Day 2. MYOG mRNA expression increased significantly by Day 2 but then rapidly decreased. In contrast, the MYOG protein level continued to increase and peaked on Day 4 (p < 0.05). MYH4, a marker of terminal differentiation, displayed steadily increasing mRNA and protein levels throughout the time course, with a significant increase observed on Days 4 and 6 (Fig. 1A). Immunofluorescence staining showed that the expression of MYH4, an indicator of myogenic differentiation, began to increase on Day 2 as the cells started fusing into myotubes, with most cells expressing MYH4 by Day 3 (Fig. 1B). ANE modulates mTOR signaling and autophagy in myoblasts

We analyzed the levels of key markers involved in protein synthesis, degradation, and autophagy to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of ANE on myoblasts and myotubes. In C2C12 myoblasts, treatment with 40 μg/mL ANE significantly reduced the levels of phosphorylated mTOR and p70S6K, both of which are critical components of the mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the autophagy markers LC3-II and p62 were markedly upregulated and downregulated, respectively, following treatment with 20 and 40 μg/mL ANE, indicating increased autophagy activity in myoblasts. In contrast, differentiated C2C12 myotubes showed no significant changes in p-mTOR, p-p70S6K, or LC3 levels after treatment with any of the tested ANE concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S1). These findings indicate that ANE differentially affects the sensitivity of myoblasts and myotubes by modulating mTOR and p70S6K signaling as well as autophagy. Chloroquine (CQ1), an autophagy inhibitor, was applied at a nontoxic concentration of 5 μM in combination with ANE (40 μg/mL) to investigate the role of autophagy in ANE-induced myoblast atrophy. Treatment with ANE alone led to a visible reduction in the myotube diameter, consistent with atrophy. However, compared with treatment with ANE alone, cotreatment with ANE and CQ1 did not restore the myotube thickness (Fig. 4B). Metabolic reprogramming of C2C12 cells upon ANE treatment

Metabolism is a critical factor influencing muscle function (Wust et al., 2018, Yen et al., 2010). Furthermore, we analyzed whether ANE affects muscle cell metabolism by performing a targeted metabolite analysis. As summarized in Table 1, in ANE-treated myoblasts, the levels of asparagine, glutamine, and alanine were significantly reduced, indicating impaired amino acid assimilation and nitrogen metabolism. Glycolysis showed limited changes, with decreased levels of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate but no subsequent accumulation. With respect to metabolites of the TCA cycle, malate levels are reduced, and α-ketoglutarate levels tend to decrease, suggesting that anaplerotic flux was compromised. Oxidative PPP activity was curtailed, as reflected by decreased levels of 6-phosphogluconate, but the level of ribose-5-phosphate increased, indicating a reliance on the nonoxidative branch. Energy nucleotide levels remained stable, but the reduced glutathione (GSH) level was markedly decreased, highlighting impaired antioxidant defenses. Overall, ANE-treated myoblasts displayed vulnerability, with weakened biosynthetic and redox capacities (Fig. 5A). In contrast, ANE treatment significantly altered the metabolomic profile of differentiated C2C12 myotubes, affecting several key metabolic pathways. Glycolysis was upregulated, as evidenced by increased levels of intermediates such as 2-phosphoglycerate, phosphoenolpyruvate, and lactate, despite the reductions in fructose 6-phosphate and glucose 1-phosphate levels. The levels of TCA cycle intermediates, including citrate and cis-aconitate, were significantly increased in ANE-treated myotubes, reflecting potential shifts in mitochondrial metabolism. Energy metabolism was impaired, as evidenced by the significantly reduced ATP levels, highlighting the compromised bioenergetic efficiency following ANE treatment. Alterations in CoA metabolism were observed, with decreased levels of CoA but increased levels of acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA, suggesting a redirection of metabolic flux toward lipid synthesis or acetylation processes. The HBP was also affected, with reduced UDP-N-acetylglucosamine levels and increased N-acetylglucosamine and UDP-glucose levels, indicating potential alterations in protein glycosylation. In the urea cycle, argininosuccinate levels decreased, whereas ornithine levels increased, suggesting imbalances in nitrogen disposal. Amino acid levels, including those of glutamine and aspartate, were significantly reduced, indicating that ANE may disrupt nitrogen metabolism (Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that ANE induces significant metabolic reprogramming in myotubes, impairing energy production, nitrogen metabolism, and biosynthetic pathways.

ANE modulates the expression of metabolic genes in differentiated myotubes

Next, the effects of ANE on the mRNA expression of key metabolic genes were analyzed (Fig. 6). The expression of the enzyme aldose reductase (AKR1B3), which is involved in the polyol pathway (Pastel et al., 2015), was significantly decreased after ANE treatment. With respect to glycerol metabolism, the expression levels of both glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) and glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 2 (GPD2) decreased, whereas the expression of phosphoglycerate mutase 2 (PGAM2), a critical glycolytic enzyme that is highly expressed in skeletal muscle (Zhang et al., 2022), remained unchanged. Under anaerobic conditions, the expression of the key metabolic enzyme lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) was significantly decreased after ANE treatment. Additionally, treatment with 20 and 40 μg/mL ANE significantly reduced the expression of genes involved in nitrogen metabolism, including glutamate dehydrogenase 1 (GLUD1) and arginase 1 (ARG1). Glutamine synthetase (GS) and glutaminase 1 (GLS1), critical regulators of glutamine metabolism, were significantly downregulated in ANE-treated myotubes. Discussion

Chewing fresh areca fruits is the main source of arecoline and total areca alkaloid exposure. According to Ji et al. (Ji et al., 2024), the estimated daily intakes of arecoline and total areca alkaloids for Chinese populations are 1.126 and 2.625 mg/kg BW/day, respectively, for average exposure and 4.411 and 9.739 mg/kg BW/day, respectively, for high exposure (95th percentile). The ANE concentrations used in this study (20 and 40 μg/mL) were selected based on preliminary cytotoxicity assays and the previous literature (Lai and Lee, 2006). These concentrations may exceed typical real-life systemic exposure levels. However, betel quid chewing is widely recognized as a long-term habit that is often sustained for decades, leading to repeated and cumulative exposure to areca nut alkaloids. Over time, such prolonged exposure may result in cumulative biological effects, especially in tissues that are directly or indirectly affected. In the current study, our aim was to evaluate the overall biological effects of ANE and ANE-induced changes in the metabolome, because this treatment mimics the complex exposure that occurs in habitual AN chewers. Therefore, the use of relatively high concentrations of ANE in vitro is justified for exploring potential mechanisms and effects under conditions that simulate chronic, long-term exposure. Further investigations into the specific contributions of individual components, such as arecoline, will provide more mechanistic insights into the observed effects.

In the present study, treatment with nontoxic concentrations of ANE interfered with C2C12 myogenic differentiation. ANE reduced the ability of cells to differentiate and fuse and significantly decreased the expression of the MYOD1, MYOG, and MYH4 genes in myoblasts but had a less pronounced effect on differentiated myotubes. MYOD1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly decreased in ANE-treated myoblasts. However, the changes in MYOD1 mRNA and protein levels in ANE-treated myotubes were not consistent with those in untreated differentiated myotubes, and the differences between groups were not significant. Recent studies have shown that the RNA binding protein Staufen1 controls the translation of MYOD1 (de Morrée et al., 2017, Ravel-Chapuis et al., 2014). Moreover, MYOD1 is an extremely short-lived protein and is degraded by the ubiquitin pathway, with a half-life of ∼30 min (Abu Hatoum et al., 1998). Thus, these discrepancies suggest that MYOD1 expression in ANE-treated myotubes may be regulated by posttranscriptional or posttranslational mechanisms. ANE treatment resulted in metabolic reprogramming and downregulated the expression of key genes involved in glycolysis, nitrogen metabolism, and glutamine metabolism (Fig. 7). Arecoline is the main alkaloid present in AN and has been linked to genotoxic effects, DNA damage, and increased risks of oral and esophageal cancers (Oliveira et al., 2021). Chang et al. showed that arecoline (40 μM) inhibits the myogenic differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts by reducing STAT3 phosphorylation (Chang et al., 2012). Our results revealed that arecoline and catechin (25 and 50 μM) alone did not induce atrophic changes in differentiated C2C12 myotubes (Supplementary Fig. S2). These results highlight the differential sensitivity of these cell types to ANE.

In this study, we performed a SRB assay, which revealed that ANE treatment did not induce cytotoxicity in C2C12 myoblasts. Previous research has shown that the partially purified 30–100 kDa fraction of areca nut extract (ANE 30–100 K) induces autophagy in various cell types, including oral carcinoma OECM-1 cells (Chiu et al., 2020). Consistent with these findings, we observed that ANE induced autophagy in C2C12 myoblasts. We cotreated cells with ANE (40 μg/mL) and CQ1, an autophagy inhibitor, at a nontoxic concentration of 5 μM to explore the role of autophagy in ANE-induced myoblast atrophy. ANE treatment alone led to a marked reduction in the myotube diameter, indicating atrophy. However, compared with ANE alone, the combined treatment with ANE and CQ1 did not decrease the myotube diameter. These findings suggest that autophagy does not directly contribute to ANE-induced myotube atrophy. Instead, we propose that autophagy may be involved in metabolic reprogramming during C2C12 differentiation, serving to maintain cellular homeostasis and prevent cell death.

In C2C12 myoblasts treated with ANE, we observed a pronounced reduction in the intracellular glutamine level, which was accompanied by decreased GSH levels and the downregulation of mTOR signaling. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies showing that ANE and its major alkaloid arecoline induce oxidative stress by depleting GSH and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in epithelial cells, thereby impairing mitochondrial function (Chang et al., 2001). As glutamine is not only a key precursor for GSH biosynthesis but also an essential activator of the mTOR pathway, its depletion may serve as a central link between oxidative stress and impaired anabolic signaling. Furthermore, the ability of N-acetyl-cysteine (NAC) to rescue the arecoline-induced inhibition of C2C12 differentiation (Li et al., 2022) underscores the critical role of a redox imbalance in mediating the detrimental effects of ANE. Together, these results suggest that ANE disrupts both antioxidant defenses and anabolic signaling in myoblasts, potentially compromising muscle cell growth and differentiation.

The gene expression analysis revealed that ANE treatment downregulated the expression of several key genes involved in amino acid metabolism, including GLUD1, ARG1, and GPD2. These findings align with the metabolomic results, which showed reduced glutamine levels and decreased levels of intermediates in the urea cycle, such as argininosuccinate, indicating disruptions in nitrogen metabolism and amino acid turnover. The downregulation of GS and GLS1 expression further suggested impaired glutamine biosynthesis and catabolism, highlighting the potential effects of ANE on the homeostasis of glutamine, a crucial nitrogen donor in muscle cells (Newsholme et al., 2003). GS mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in response to 20 μg/mL ANE treatment, indicating a strong inhibitory effect of this concentration. Although an increasing trend was observed after treatment with 40 μg/mL ANE, the change was not statistically significant, suggesting a potential compensatory response that did not reach significance under the conditions tested. This result may reflect a biphasic or threshold-dependent regulatory pattern in response to ANE treatment. Further experimental validation is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms responsible for this controversial trend.

Lactate accumulation is mediated by an imbalance between its production and clearance (Brooks, 2020). Pyruvate is converted to lactate through enzymatic catalysis by LDHA, and lactate is converted to pyruvate through catalysis by lactate dehydrogenase isoform B (LDHB). Moreover, lactate levels are influenced by lactate clearance and transport mechanisms (Mao et al., 2025) and may not strictly parallel LDHA gene expression, suggesting that lactate accumulation in ANE-treated differentiated myotubes may reflect a combination of isoform compensation, altered clearance, and microenvironmental metabolic reprogramming rather than LDHA expression alone. More experiments will be needed to resolve this discrepancy in the future. Furthermore, the gene expression data revealed a significant reduction in the expression of AKR1B3, a key enzyme involved in polyol metabolism (Nishinaka et al., 2019), suggesting potential alterations in oxidative stress pathways following ANE treatment. However, we did not analyze the levels of intermediate metabolites related to the polyol pathway. The levels of intermediate metabolites and activity of related enzymes need to be analyzed to determine whether ANE affects this pathway. Overall, the combined analysis of gene expression and metabolomic data highlights that ANE treatment induces broad metabolic reprogramming in differentiated myotubes, characterized by disrupted amino acid metabolism, enhanced glycolytic flux, and impaired energy production.

Different species or different muscle groups have different types of muscle fibers. Muscle fibers can be divided into three main types based on their contraction speed and the pathway they use to produce ATP (Schiaffino and Reggiani, 2011). Most human muscles are type I, and mouse C2C12 muscle cells are type IIb myofibers (Schiaffino and Reggiani, 2011). Different muscle fibers have different mitochondrial densities and oxidative phosphorylation activity. The quadriceps muscle mainly consists of type IIb myofibers with fewer mitochondria, whereas the soleus muscle mainly contains type I and type IIa myofibers that have more mitochondria and a high oxidative capacity (TeSlaa et al., 2021). Primary human skeletal muscle cells (HSMCs) mainly rely on glycolysis for energy production, but the mitochondrial content of HSMCs is lower than that of mouse C2C12 muscle cells and rat L6 muscle cells (Abdelmoez et al., 2020). The masseter muscle is dominated by a mixture of type I and type II myofibers (Rowlerson et al., 2005), and the orbicularis oris muscle is dominated by type II myofibers (Burrows et al., 2014).

Clinically, oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) is a chronic, progressive, premalignant condition primarily caused by AN chewing. It is characterized by the juxtaepithelial deposition of fibrotic tissue, followed by muscular degeneration and restricted mouth opening. Kant et al. reported that the cross-sectional thickness of the masseter muscle in patients with OSMF associated with AN and tobacco use is reduced compared to healthy controls (Kant et al., 2014). Preventing or mitigating AN-induced muscle loss is an important clinical concern. In the present study, glutamine levels were significantly decreased in ANE-treated myoblasts and differentiated myotubes. Therefore, glutamine supplementation may be considered for individuals with an AN chewing habit. This result also suggests that AN may have different effects on different types of myofibers, and more experiments need to be performed using other types of myofibers. The precise mechanisms underlying these changes merit further study. Future research should also explore the long-term effects of ANE on muscle health and its effects on other cell types and tissues. Investigating the interactions between ANE and other dietary or environmental factors could provide broader insights into its effects on overall health. Understanding these interactions is pivotal for informing public health recommendations and developing therapeutic strategies for muscle-associated disorders.

Conclusions

This study provides pivotal insights into how ANE suppresses myogenesis by reprogramming cellular metabolism in C2C12 cells. Our findings indicate that ANE reduces myoblast differentiation and fusion, significantly decreases the expression of key myogenic markers, and induces metabolic reprogramming characterized by altered glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in differentiated myotubes. ANE induces significant metabolic reprogramming in differentiated myotubes, impairing energy production, nitrogen metabolism, and biosynthetic pathways. Moreover, the results of the analysis of the mRNA expression of metabolic genes provide a comprehensive view of the metabolic alterations induced by ANE treatment in differentiated myotubes. The findings of this study underscore the potential health implications of AN consumption in muscle wasting and highlight the need for continued research into its biological effects.
