Selected high-entropy alloys for thin film deposition, with improved mechanical and corrosion properties
Authors: Dumitru Mitrica, Laurentiu-Florin Mosinoiu, Arcadii Sobetkii, Stefania Caramarin, Alexandru-Cristian Matei, Laura-Madalina Cursaru, Ioan-Albert Tudor, Beatrice-Adriana Serban, Mihai Ghita, Nicoleta Vitan, Cosmin Romanitan, Elena Olaret, Bogdan Postolnyi, Alexander Pogrebnjak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh Entropy Alloys (HEA) are multi-principal element materials that have been intensively studied over the past decade. In this work, two Al–Fe–Cr–Ni–Cu HEAs were synthesized and processed into thin films by DC magnetron sputtering, and their structural, mechanical and electrochemical properties were systematically evaluated. The coatings exhibited dense, uniform structures with strong adhesion to 304 L stainless steel. Coatings hardness was higher than the substrate material. Electrochemical tests in 3.5 g/L NaCl solution revealed superior corrosion resistance for the I8-10-derived coating, which achieved the lowest corrosion rate (5.54E-05 mm/year) and the highest polarization resistance (5.470.008 Ω), outperforming both the bulk alloys and uncoated substrates.Keywords: High-entropy alloys (HEAs), Thin film deposition, Sputtering, Structural characterization, Corrosion resistanceSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Corrosion is a common degradation process that arises from chemical and electrochemical interactions between engineered materials and their surrounding environments. This phenomenon not only compromises the structural integrity and functionality of components but also generates substantial economic costs worldwide with estimates indicating that corrosion-related expenses may account for nearly 3% of the global gross domestic product (GDP)1. Over the decades, alloy development has focused heavily on optimizing the formation of protective passive films such as Cr2O3, NiO and MoOx to shield the underlying metal from aggressive environments, particularly in chloride-rich or acidic media2.

Due to their multicomponent chemistry and complex atomic arrangements3, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) introduce a fundamentally new paradigm in corrosion resistance. The simultaneous presence of multiple alloying elements promotes the formation of compositionally rich and potentially more stable passive films. Furthermore, the inherent sluggish diffusion and lattice distortion effects in HEAs4 can slow down corrosion kinetics compared to conventional alloys, often outperforming stainless steels or nickel alloys in saline and acidic environments.

The corrosion behavior of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) is strongly influenced by their chemical composition and the synergistic interactions among alloying elements5. In this context, aluminum (Al) and copper (Cu) are two elements whose effects on corrosion resistance are complex and highly interdependent6.

Aluminum is widely recognized for its strong passivation tendency in many alloy systems. In HEAs, Al additions contribute to the formation of dense and stable Al2O3-enriched passive films, which can significantly reduce corrosion rates, particularly in neutral or alkaline environments. The incorporation of Al has also been reported to enhance the pitting resistance of certain Cr-containing HEAs by improving the chemical homogeneity of passive layers7. However, excessive Al content may induce the precipitation of secondary BCC phases and can lead to elemental segregation, which may cause the formation of micro-galvanic couples at grain boundaries and reduce corrosion performance in aggressive environments such as chlorides8.

Copper, on the other hand, is generally considered a noble element that contributes to corrosion resistance in conventional alloys by stabilizing passive films and promoting resistance to biofouling and microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC)9,10. In stainless steel and nickel-based alloys, Cu additions can enhance resistance in sulfuric and phosphoric acid environments11. In HEAs, Cu can enrich the outer layers of passive films but it is also susceptible to segregation or formation of Cu-rich precipitates potentially leading to localized galvanic corrosion or pitting under chloride exposure12. Thus, the beneficial role of Cu is highly sensitive to its distribution and interaction with other alloying elements.

The co-alloying of Al and Cu in HEAs remains a relatively unexplored strategy, with only a few studies addressing their combined effects on corrosion behavior13. It is hypothesized that a balanced addition of Al and Cu could synergistically stabilize passive films where Al improves film density while Cu enhances surface resistance. Achieving such a balance is strongly dependent on processing methods which can affect elemental segregation, microstructural homogeneity and phase stability.

Among the available techniques, induction melting offers several key advantages for HEA production. It enables rapid and uniform heating of large melt volumes, promotes effective mixing of constituent elements and minimizes contamination risks due to its non-contact heating nature. Furthermore, the controlled atmosphere and high-frequency stirring during induction melting can help suppress elemental segregation and enhance chemical uniformity, which are critical for the development of stable passive films and improved corrosion resistance14. Compared to arc melting or powder metallurgy routes, induction melting also allows for greater scalability, making it an attractive option for the synthesis of bulk HEAs with tailored corrosion properties.

In addition to developing corrosion-resistant bulk HEAs, an effective way to further exploit their properties is through the fabrication of coatings, which offers a more economical solution by combining enhanced performance with reduced material usage. Various deposition techniques such as laser cladding15, electro-spark deposition16 and magnetron sputtering17,18 have been successfully employed to produce HEA films on steels, aluminum alloys, and silicon substrates. These coatings can provide superior corrosion protection by forming dense, stable passive layers and high-resistivity barriers which prevent the penetration of corrosive species. Moreover, HEA films often exhibit improved microstructural homogeneity compared to their bulk counterparts19, which can further enhance corrosion resistance, particularly in chloride-rich or marine environments.

The present work aims to investigate the effects of aluminum and copper alloying on the microstructure and corrosion behavior of induction-melted high-entropy alloys designed for potential application in marine environments. Two alloy compositions were synthesized by induction-melting technique Al9Fe29Cr29Ni29Cu4 and Al5Fe43Cr5Ni33Cu14 (at%), with the goal of examining how the composition influences the structural and electrochemical corrosion performance of these materials. Thin films were also prepared using DC magnetron sputtering and characterized in comparison with their bulk counterparts. This study focuses on detailed characterization of phase composition and microstructure using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with particular attention to phase stability, elemental distribution and potential segregation. The corrosion behavior of the alloys (both bulk and coatings) is evaluated in chloride-containing environments through linear sweep voltammetry. In addition, Vickers microhardness measurements were carried out to assess the mechanical properties of the alloys, complementing the electrochemical evaluation and providing a more comprehensive understanding of their suitability for service in aggressive marine environments.

The outcomes of this work are expected to contribute to a more fundamental understanding of Al–Cu co-alloying effects on the microstructural evolution and corrosion behavior of high-entropy alloys, and to support the development of advanced alloy systems with improved performance for application in severe marine environments.

Experimental procedures

Alloy Preparation

The composition selection was made based on antimicrobial requirements (at least 2% Cu) and HEA configuration requirements (elements in equal or appropriate proportions). The percentage of Al, which has a major influence on the processing temperature and material characteristics, was also modified.

The preparation of alloys to obtain deposition targets is carried out by melting in a vacuum induction furnace.

In this case, the presence of highly reactive elements or with high vaporization voltage is not recommended. In this sense, elements with low vaporization temperatures (Zn, Mn) were not used. The CrFeNi triplet is recognized in HEA alloys with high potential for solid solution formation.

The preparation of high-entropy alloys was carried out in a Linn MFG-30 induction furnace, in vacuum and with an inert atmosphere (Ar). For the melting/processing of HEA alloys, zirconium oxide ceramic crucibles stabilized with calcium oxide (Zircoa) were used, with a capacity of 2 L.

Thin-film deposition

The thin film depositions were performed on a hybrid PVD (Physical Vapor Deposition) system. The DC/RF Magnetron Sputtering module used is manufactured by Kurt J. Lesker and consists of a circular TORUS magnetron sputtering gun/cathode with a diameter of 2 inches, a PD500x DC source with a maximum power of 1500 W used for metal targets and an R301 radio frequency source with a maximum power of 300 W used for ceramic targets. Thus, metallic coatings can be obtained by DC Sputtering and oxide coatings by RF Sputtering on flat surfaces with a maximum diameter of 10 cm. The preliminary vacuum of 10e−7 mbar and the working vacuum are provided by a TG-800FRWB turbomolecular pump of 800 L/s. The equipment is also upgraded with a plasma cleaning system for the surface of the parts before deposition called glow discharge system. Silicon and 304 L steel plates with dimensions of 15 × 15 mm and 30 × 20 mm and a glass slide of 80 × 30 mm were used as substrates. The silicon substrates were employed for the SEM-EDS analysis, to avoid interference with the morphological and compositional results. The HEA targets for the alloys I8-10 and I8-11 (with composition revealed in Table 1), had a diameter of 2 inches and a thickness of 6 mm.

Characterization

The chemical composition of the samples was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma spectrometry (ICP-OES) using an Agilent 725 spectrometer (Santa Clara, CA, USA). To highlight the homogeneity of the alloys, samples for chemical analysis were taken from various areas of the sample. The composition values obtained varied by + 1% or -1% from the average composition of the sample.

The structure of the samples was also investigated by SEM-EDS characterization with a SEM scanning electron microscope with energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS), type Quanta 250 FEI, operating at 25 kV and equipped with an EDS detector manufactured by EDAX with an ELEMENT FIX SDD ELEMENT FIX detector.

The crystal structure of the alloys was determined by X-ray diffractometry, using a BRUKER D8 ADVANCE diffractometer, with the help of the DIFFRACplus XRD Commander software (Bruker AXS), by the Bragg-Brentano diffraction method, Θ – Θ coupling in vertical configuration with the following parameters: power 40 kV×40 mA, Cu Kα radiation, rotation speed 30 rpm, removal of the Cu Kβ component electronically, with the help of the SOL X detector. Data processing was done using the DIFFRAC.EVA VER.5 2019 program from the DIFFRAC.SUITE.EVA program package and the ICDD PDF-5 + 2024 database.

Nanoindentation tests were performed using G200 Nanoindenter (KLA Instruments, USA) equipped with a Berkovich diamond tip (20 nm radius of the tip) and operated in Continuous Stiffness Measurement (CSM). Hardness (H) and Young’s modulus (E) were calculated using the Oliver and Pharr method implemented in the NanoSuite software. A Poisson’s ratio of 0.18 was used for modulus calculations. The tests were conducted at room temperature, with a surface approach velocity of 10 nm/s and a strain rate target of 0.05 s⁻¹. Harmonic displacement and frequency were set to 2 nm and 45 Hz, respectively. 12 indentations were performed on each sample, ensuring at least 50 μm distance between them. To capture a stable contact response, indentations were carried out up to a maximum depth of 500 nm. However, in order to minimize substrate influence, mechanical properties were extracted only from the 100–150 nm depth interval, which corresponds to less than 10% of the total film thickness (average thickness of the coatings ~ 2 μm).

The adhesion of the coating deposited on the 304 L steel substrate was evaluated with the scratch test. The test was performed with a “Scratch Test NANOVEA” device - scratch/adhesion indenter: M0-M1250, conical at 120 degrees and 50 micrometers.

The electrochemical corrosion studies were carried out at room temperature in an ASTM corrosion cell with a volume of 1 L (Fig. 36), equipped with 3 electrodes: the working electrode – the material to be analyzed, the exposed surface area 1cm2; the reference electrode – Ag/AgCl in 3 M KCl solution; the counter electrode – platinum wire. The 3 electrodes are coupled to an Autolab PGSTAT 128 N potentiostat/galvanostat (Methrom) connected to a computer equipped with NOVA 2.1 software. The electrolyte solution used was NaCl with a concentration of 0.35 wt%, according to the ASTM G61-86 standard (Reapproved 2003). The experiments were carried out after 60 min of immersion of the electrodes in the studied electrolyte solution.

The working procedure after which the experiments were carried out was linear polarization. This consists of determining the working electrode potential in open circuit (OCP), followed by Linear sweep voltammetry ± 0.1 V around OCP, scan rate 0.001 V/s, step 0.001 V, and stabilization time 5 s. A potential sweep is applied and the resulting current is measured. The polarization resistance can be extracted from the resulting data and the Tafel approximation can be applied to calculate the corrosion rate. The slope of the curve at corrosion potential can be calculated by performing a linear regression tangent to the data from 10 mV cathodic to 10 mV anodic relative to the corrosion potential). The polarization resistance Rp is calculated from inverse of the slope (1/slope): Icorr = B/Rp, where B is Stern -Geary constant. In this case, B = 0,026 and it is calculated from the anodic and cathodic Tafel slopes (βa and βc) of the corrosion system: B = βaβc / (2.303(βa + βc)).

The analysis of the corrosion process was performed using NOVA 2.1 software, by automatically determining the following parameters from the Tafel curve: corrosion potential, Ecorr (V); corrosion current density, jcorr (A/cm2); corrosion current intensity, icorr (A); corrosion rate (mm/year); polarization resistance, Rp (Ω).

Aluminum has a relatively low vapor pressure and can produce losses due to vaporization.

SEM-EDS analysis of sample I8-10 obtained by melting and casting in an induction furnace was studied by electron microscopy (Fig. 1). The alloy presents a well-defined dendritic structure and well-defined inter-dendritic spaces. According to the EDS results all the elements are present in the inter-dendritic zone (Figs. 2 and 3) (Table 3). SEM analyses performed on sample I8-11 (Fig. 4) shows the presence of single-phase microstructures with medium-sized grains (100–200 μm). The mapping image shows a homogeneous distribution of elements in the structure (Fig. 5), which justifies the acceptance of a single-phase structure. Nevertheless, an intergranular segregation of copper is also observed. The EDS point analysis of the I8-11 alloy (Fig. 6; Table 4) shows a homogeneous distribution of elements throughout the mass of the alloy, predominantly Fe, Ni, but also Cu. Small and uniformly distributed impurities are occasionally observed in the material. XRD analysis of Al-Fe-Cr-Ni-Cu HEA-based coating systems deposited on stainless steel substrates by magnetron sputtering is presented in Fig. 12 and reveals distinct structural characteristics for the two compositions. The sample I8-11 S exhibits sharp and well-defined diffraction peaks corresponding to a single-phase face-centered (FCC) structure, and absence of distinct peaks corresponding to the individual constituent elements. This indicates successful atomic-level mixing and compositional homogeneity within the region exposed to the X-ray beam, good crystallinity and the formation of a stable solid solution of the designed HEA. In contrast, sample I8-10 S displays only single broad diffraction peak centered at 44°, suggesting a nanocrystalline or partially amorphous structure with poor long-range order. The broad peak could correspond to either FCC (111) or BCC (110), but its width and low intensity imply limited crystallite size or structural disorder. These differences may be primarily attributed to the compositional variations between two alloys, namely Fe-Ni-Cu-rich matrix favors FCC phase formation, while the higher Al and Cr content may promote phase instability in I8-10 S and inhibit crystallization under the non-equilibrium deposition conditions.

The microhardness of the deposited samples and modulus are presented by comparison in Fig. 13. The mean values and standard deviation for hardness (H) and modulus (E) over the 100–150 nm depth interval, for the two alloy coatings and for the substrate, are presented in Table 6. The load- displacement curves for the specimens are presented in Fig. 14. In line with the “10% rule” for instrumented indentation testing20, H and E values were determined within the 100–150 nm depth interval, which represents ~ 5–9% of the 1.7–1.9 μm coating thickness, thereby minimizing substrate effects. Both coatings exhibit similar hardness (HI8−10 S= 6.79 ± 0.98 GPa, HI811S=6.96 ± 1.34 GPa), slightly higher than the substrate (HSS−304 L=6.48 ± 0.66 GPa), and different moduli (EI8−10 S= 142.52 ± 13.49 GPa, EI811S=190.61 ± 26.87 GPa, and ESS−304 L=216.16 ± 18.82 GPa). A reduction in stiffness, while maintaining comparable hardness, increases the H/E ratio, which has been correlated with improved coating compliance and reduced tendency for cracking under mechanical loading21,22. This suggests that the coatings may provide enhanced toughness despite similar hardness. The load-displacement curves recorded to a 500 nm indentation depth are (Fig. 14) show good reproducibility at low indentation depths (< 200 nm). At large indentation depths (> 300 nm), curves begin to scatter, probably due to the substrate influence.

The higher the polarization resistance (Rp), the more corrosion resistant the studied material is and the corrosion rate over time is lower. As can be seen from Table 7; Fig. 17, both the I8-10 alloy and the I8-10-based coating (I8-10 S) improve the corrosion resistance of 304 L steel in NaCl solutions of 0.35 wt% concentration. Sample I8-10.S.P7, obtained from HEA I8-10 ingots, presents the lowest corrosion rate (5.54E-05 mm/year) and the highest polarization resistance (5470.008 Ω). Samples I8-11 and I8-11 S have also a higher corrosion resistance than uncoated 304 L stainless steel. It can be seen that the HEA coatings studied on 304 L steel have a higher corrosion resistance than the alloys from which they were manufactured. It is worth noting that the corrosion rate and polarization resistance of the I8-11 S based coating are lower than those of the I8-11 alloy. Therefore, it can be concluded that the I8-10 S alloy-based thin film has the best corrosion resistance. The I8-10 alloy also exhibits a reduced corrosion rate and has better properties compared to commercial 304 L stainless steel.

Conclusion

In this work, Al–Fe–Cr–Ni–Cu high-entropy alloy coatings were successfully deposited by DC magnetron sputtering and systematically characterized. Two alloy compositions with low and high Cu percentage were analysed in the as-cast state as target material and in thin film state, deposited by a well know technique. The as-cast alloys presented simple solid solution structures with a single phase configuration identified in the alloy with high Cu content. The XRD analyses of deposited alloys showed simple single phase or near amorphous structure. Alloy thin films exhibited dense structures, in the range of 1–2 μm, with strong adhesion to stainless steel substrates and improved corrosion resistance compared to both bulk alloys and uncoated substrates. Notably, the I8-10-derived coating achieved the lowest corrosion rate and highest polarization resistance, highlighting the beneficial role of Al–Cu co-alloying in enhancing phase stability and passive film formation. The hardness of the deposited films is higher than the stainless-steel substrate. These results demonstrate that sputtered HEA thin films are promising protective coatings for structural components exposed to marine environments.
