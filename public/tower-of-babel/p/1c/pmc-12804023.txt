Mitral Valve Stenosis in Marshall-Smith Syndrome: Expanding Phenotype Spectrum
Authors: Mohanageetha Ardhanari, Shahnoor Jafri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Marshall-Smith syndrome, Mitral stenosis, Mitral regurgitation, Transthoracic echocardiography, Genetic disorder

Introduction

Marshall-Smith syndrome (MSS) is an extremely rare embryonic developmental disorder with a prevalence of less than 1 in 1,000,000. The syndrome is characterized by accelerated skeletal maturation, craniofacial dysmorphism, respiratory complications, and failure to thrive (FTT). Clinical variability of MSS is becoming apparent, and the phenotype is expanding. Cardiovascular findings in MSS reported in the literature include atrial septal defect (ASD), ventricular septal defect (VSD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and aortic root dilation. We are reporting a case of mitral stenosis (MS) in a patient with MSS.

Case Presentation

The patient was born at 37 weeks of gestation to a healthy 26-year-old woman. Prenatal history was complicated with maternal smoking and anemia, which have no relation to development of this disease. There was no family history of MSS. The infant was small for gestational age, weighing 5 pounds and 5 ounces, and was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for temperature instability. At birth, a large umbilical hernia and multiple gluteal and intergluteal clefts were noted on exam. The patient failed the hearing screen. Feeding difficulties were noted early on, which were initially attributed to formula intolerance and difficulty latching. The infant was discharged home after 7 days in the neonatal intensive care unit.

At 2 months the patient developed FTT and also failed the 2-month Ages and Stages Questionnaire; as a consequence they underwent inpatient evaluation for FTT. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) performed as part of evaluation for FTT showed only a tiny patent foramen ovale and otherwise normal cardiac anatomy and function. Laryngomalacia was noted on dysphagiogram, for which the patient underwent supraglottoplasty at 3 months of age. A gastrostomy tube was placed for feeding difficulties during that same hospitalization. The patient was noted to have advanced bone age at 4 months. By 5 months of age, the infant was suspected to have MSS given the constellation of findings including developmental delay, FTT, umbilical hernia, chronic respiratory issues, and advanced bone age, along with classic craniofacial dysmorphism including thick curly hair, short forehead, hirsutism, proptosis, and micrognathia. Extensive testing with microarray, urine glycosaminoglycans, and metabolic testing were all normal. Genetic testing with multigene sequencing with a macrocephaly panel and subsequently whole exome sequence showed a heterozygous likely pathogenic variant in NFIX:c.1459del (p.Asp487 fs) consistent with MSS.

At 11 months, a repeat TTE performed for heart murmur showed findings of abnormal mitral valve (MV) with tethered posterior mitral leaflet and shortened chordae (Figure 1A, Video 1) with severe MS and moderate mitral regurgitation (MR) based on visual estimate (Figure 1B, Video 2). The MV mean inflow gradient was 23 mm Hg. The left atrium was severely dilated, with a left atrial volume indexed to body surface area of 51 mL/m2. Otherwise normal cardiac anatomy and function were observed.

Two-dimensional TTE, apical 4-chamber pediatric (apex-down) view in diastole without (A) and with (B) color-flow Doppler, demonstrates a normal size left ventricle, severely dilated left atrium, and tethered posterior leaflet of the MV (white arrow) with shortened chordae with acceleration across the MV consistent with MS.

The patient therefore underwent MV replacement with a 16 mm mechanical valve in the supra-annular mitral position and closure of the ASD at 1 year of age. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) performed postoperatively to assess MV function showed good mobility of the leaflets and no obvious vegetations. Color-flow and continuous-wave spectral Doppler showed mild residual MS and mild MR (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4; Videos 3 and 4). Postoperative course was uneventful, and the infant was discharged on home propranolol, aspirin, and coumadin.

LV, Left ventricle.

LA, Left atrium; LV, left ventricle.

Continuous-wave spectral Doppler TEE performed postoperatively, midesophageal mitral commissural long-axis (75°-82°) diastolic view, demonstrates a mean gradient of 8 mm Hg consistent with mild MS.

The patient was closely followed as an outpatient; TTE at the most recent follow-up showed a stable well-functioning 16 mm mechanical valve in a supra-annular mitral position (Figure 5A, Video 3) with trivial residual MR and mild MS (Figure 5B, Video 4). The mean gradient of the inflow across the mitral inflow was 10 mm Hg, suggestive of possible mild MS (Figure 6). The patient had unstable international normalized ratio levels and therefore was transitioned from warfarin to low molecular weight heparin. They remained stable from a cardiovascular standpoint at a midterm follow-up at 6 years of age with only mild residual MS, and mild MR.

Continuous-wave spectral Doppler TTE, apical 4-chamber pediatric (apex-down) view in diastole preoperatively (A), demonstrates a mean gradient of 23 mm Hg consistent with severe MS and postoperatively (B), demonstrates a mean gradient of 10 mm Hg consistent with mild MS.

The clinical course was also significant for long-standing hypertension managed with atenolol. Progressive respiratory difficulties from choanal atresia and bilateral vocal cord dysfunction warranted choanal atresia repair, laryngoplasty, and tracheostomy placement. Ventriculoperitoneal shunt was placed for Chiari malformation. Seizures were controlled with levetiracetam. The patient also had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss and scoliosis. Progressive feeding difficulties warranted gastrostomy tube placement. Mild platelet dysfunction disorder was noted following prolonged bleeding following invasive procedures. Bone fragility resulted in nontraumatic bone fractures including femur, for which the patient was on bisphosphonate infusions every 6 months.

Discussion

First described by Marshall et al. in 1971,1 MSS is a rare disorder that is characterized by advanced bone age, orofacial abnormalities, FTT, and respiratory complications. In the literature, there are fewer than 60 patients described to date.2, 3, 4 In 2010, it was discovered that MSS is caused by de novo mutations in the Nuclear Factor I/X (NFIX) gene at the 13.3 locus of the short arm of chromosome 19p13.3 encoding for a transcription factor critical during neurodevelopment and osteogenesis.5 Most patients are thought to have a de novo mutation. Diagnosis of MSS requires a careful review of developmental history, clinical features, and genetic testing.

Facial characteristics include macrocephaly, craniosynostosis, broad forehead, coarse eyebrows, shallow orbits, hypertelorism, underdeveloped upturned nose, cleft palate, micro retrognathia, developed helix, and antihelix of the auricle. The bone age is characteristically advanced at birth. Other characteristic skeletal findings of this syndrome include osteochondrodysplasia, kyphosis, and scoliosis. Respiratory findings include choanal atresia, laryngomalacia, laryngeal hypoplasia, hypoplastic epiglottis, and respiratory difficulties resulting in pneumonia, aspiration, and pulmonary hypertension. Ophthalmologic, neurological, dermatological, gastrointestinal, genitourinary,6,7 and hematological findings have been reported. Cardiovascular findings include ASD,8 VSD, PDA, and dilated aortic root at the sinotubular junction.6

Factors that affect morbidity and mortality in patients with MSS are primarily due to respiratory difficulties that result from upper airway anomalies and recurrent aspiration. Aggressive management of respiratory and feeding difficulties may prolong life expectancy.3,9,10

Abnormalities of the MV have not been previously described in patients with MSS in the literature. In our patient, the initial TTE performed at 2 months of age showed normal MV, and a repeat TTE performed at 11 months of age showed severe MS. The patient did not have any intercurrent illness to suggest the possibility of an acquired secondary cause of MS. As noted earlier, MSS is characterized by accelerated bone maturation. Many authors have asserted that the accelerated skeletal maturation seen in MSS is due to a mesenchymal defect, which results in early mineralization of the secondary ossification centers, as opposed to a true acceleration in bony maturation.1 Authors speculate whether abnormal mineralization of the MV could have resulted in the later finding of severe MS. Further research is needed to understand the precise mechanisms linking MSS to abnormal mineralization and whether it leads to specific imbalances like hypocalcemia or hypercalcemia. Further understanding of the mechanisms and pathophysiology of comorbidities associated with MSS enables future development of anticipatory guidance and treatment guidelines.

Transthoracic echocardiography remains the primary imaging modality for initial and follow-up assessment of prosthetic heart valves11,12 and is a valuable tool for assessing mechanical MV. With the availability of several windows on TTE, recording or checking velocity across the prosthetic MV is readily feasible to evaluate prosthetic valve function. However, TTE has limitations due to acoustic shadowing from the valve’s metal components, hindering visualization of the left atrium and MR. Transesophageal echocardiography is a valuable tool for evaluating mechanical MVs due to its proximity to the heart, allowing for detailed visualization of valve structure and function; assessment of paravalvular leak; detection of possible complications such as presence of pannus, thrombi, vegetations, abscess formation, or processes dehiscence; and guidance during procedures. Overall, echocardiography (both TTE and TEE) plays a crucial role in the initial assessment and ongoing monitoring of mechanical MVs. Cardiac computed tomography and cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging are potential additional tools.

Conclusion

Cardiovascular findings in MSS reported in the literature include ASD, VSD, PDA, and aortic root dilation. The MS noted in our patient with MSS could possibly demonstrate the expanding phenotype. The proposed association will be substantial if there are additional future reports of MV abnormalities in patients with MSS. All patients with MSS need formal cardiac evaluation given the reported cardiovascular findings. Echocardiography remains the mainstay in the initial diagnosis and ongoing monitoring of cardiac valvular pathology.
