Association between insufficient balloon dilatation during endoscopic papillary balloon dilatation and post-ERCP pancreatitis in patients with choledocholithiasis: a multicentre retrospective study in China
Authors: Jiawei Chen, Caihan Duan, Yu Jin, Qi He, Guochen Shang, Zheng Ding, Chaoqun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine whether insufficient balloon dilatation during endoscopic papillary balloon dilatation (EPBD) is associated with postendoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography pancreatitis (PEP) and prolonged hospitalisation and to identify clinical and anatomical factors related to insufficient dilatation.DesignMulticentre retrospective cohort study with propensity score matching analysis.SettingThree tertiary referral hospitals in China: Union Hospital of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, the Gastroenterology and Endoscopy Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University and the First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University.ParticipantsAmong 3341 patients with choledocholithiasis who underwent EPBD between January 2023 and December 2024, 2970 met the eligibility criteria. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 220 patients were included in the final analysis, including 110 with inadequate balloon dilatation and 110 with adequate dilatation.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the incidence of PEP, diagnosed by standardised criteria. Secondary outcomes included the length of postoperative hospitalisation and risk factors for insufficient balloon dilatation.ResultsThe incidence of PEP was significantly higher in the insufficient dilatation group compared with the sufficient dilatation group (24.5% vs 8.2%, p=0.002). Multivariate analysis confirmed insufficient dilatation as an independent risk factor for PEP (OR 4.302, 95% CI 1.874 to 9.879, p=0.001), while endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) was a protective factor (OR 0.391, 95% CI 0.178 to 0.858, p=0.019). Insufficient dilatation was also associated with a longer postoperative hospital stay (p=0.002). Furthermore, the presence of a juxtapapillary duodenal diverticulum (JDD) was strongly correlated with a lower likelihood of insufficient dilatation (OR 0.388, 95% CI 0.210 to 0.716, p=0.002).ConclusionsInsufficient balloon dilatation during EPBD is a significant risk factor for PEP. ENBD was associated with a reduced risk. Patients with JDD are more likely to achieve sufficient balloon dilatation. Recognising this sign of insufficient dilatation can help clinicians stratify risk and tailor postprocedural management.

Introduction

Gallstone disease is a common digestive disease with a prevalence of approximately 6% around the world.1 In China, 5–11% of the population suffers gallstone disease, which causes an accumulated health burden.2 3 Although most cholelithiasis cases are asymptomatic, others might develop cholangitis with upper abdominal pain, fever and obstructive jaundice when the stone obstructs the bile duct.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the primary minimally invasive treatment of choledocholithiasis, and endoscopic papillary balloon dilatation (EPBD) is a useful strategy to remove difficult common bile duct (CBD) stones.4 Post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) is a primary complication of ERCP. EPBD was previously regarded as a risk factor for PEP, which impeded its application.5 However, clinicians gradually realised that balloon dilatation, especially large balloon dilatation, can improve the success rate of stone removal with no significantly different adverse event rate compared with other strategies.6 7 Some operational factors of EPBD rather than EPBD itself are considered risk factors for PEP, such as inflation pressure and mechanical lithotripsy. Furthermore, some studies suggested adequate balloon size results in better efficacy and safety of EPBD, which might be due to that the small balloon cannot sufficiently dilate the papilla and might lead to more operations to remove the stones.6 8 During EPBD, disappearance of the balloon waist under fluoroscopy is commonly regarded as a sign of adequate dilation. In routine practice, however, persistent balloon waist despite application of 75% of the maximal dilation pressure is not uncommon and is defined by the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society (JGES) guidelines as insufficient dilation.9 The clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain. Previous studies have mainly focused on balloon size or total dilation time, whereas limited data are available regarding whether insufficient dilation itself is associated with PEP or which patients are more likely to experience inadequate dilation during the procedure.

In this multicentre retrospective cohort study, we evaluated patients who underwent EPBD for choledocholithiasis using propensity score matching (PSM) analysis. The aims of the study were: (1) to determine whether insufficient balloon dilation, defined by persistent balloon waist, is associated with PEP and prolonged hospitalisation; and (2) to identify clinical and anatomical factors related to unsuccessful dilation, with particular attention to juxtapapillary duodenal diverticula (JDD). Through analysis of this routinely observed intraoperative finding, we sought to better clarify its potential clinical relevance during EPBD.

Methods

Study objects

A total of 3341 patients diagnosed with choledocholithiasis who underwent ERCP with EPBD between January 2023 and December 2024 at the Union Hospital of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, the Gastroenterology and Endoscopy Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University and the First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University were initially screened. After applying the exclusion criteria, 371 patients were excluded, leaving 2970 patients enrolled in the study (116 in the insufficient dilatation group and 2854 in the sufficient dilatation group). Inclusion criteria: (1) Confirmed diagnosis of choledocholithiasis and receipt of ERCP with EPBD treatment for stone extraction. (2) Complete medical records and procedural data, including patient demographics (age, sex), preoperative biochemical indicators (serum lipase, amylase, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBIL)), ERCP procedural details (balloon dilation status, use of mechanical lithotripsy, endoscopic retrograde pancreatic drainage (ERPD) and endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD)) and postoperative outcomes (serum amylase/lipase, complications, length of hospitalisation). (3) Normal preoperative serum lipase and amylase levels to rule out pre-existing pancreatitis and establish a clear baseline for PEP diagnosis. Exclusion criteria: (1) History of previous ERCP (endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST) or balloon dilation), as prior papillary intervention may alter sphincter anatomy and affect both balloon dilatation efficacy and PEP risk. (2) History of prior pancreatic duct stenting (referred to as ERPD in this study), to eliminate the potential protective or anatomical effects of prior pancreatic duct interventions on PEP risk. (3) History of acute pancreatitis secondary to choledocholithiasis, as it may confound the diagnosis of PEP and reflect a pre-existing predisposition to pancreatic inflammation.

Data collection

The characteristics of patients (gender and age), the condition of the gallstone (the diameter of CBD and the stone, the number of stones), biochemical indexes (ALT and TBIL), the operation details (the diameter of expansion, complete stones removal, mechanical lithotripsy, diverticula, operation time, ERPD and ENBD), the complications (pancreatitis, cholangitis, cholecystitis, haemorrhage and perforation) and length of postoperative hospitalisation were collected. According to the JGES guidelines for endoscopic papillary large balloon dilation, the insufficient dilatation was defined as the balloon waist does not disappear even with the application of 75% of maximal dilatation pressure (figure 1).9 The X-ray images of all enrolled patients were reviewed, and the patients were divided into the insufficient dilatation group and the sufficient dilatation group according to the images. No missing data were present for any of the collected variables in the final matched cohort of 220 patients, as patients with incomplete records were excluded based on the inclusion criteria.

ERCP procedures

All ERCP operations were performed by expert endoscopists. A side-viewing duodenoscope is advanced to the major duodenal papilla portions. EST was performed in all patients at left of sphincter. Cannulation of the bile duct is achieved using a sphincterotome and catheter under fluoroscopic and endoscopic guidance, followed by contrast injection to delineate stone location, size and ductal anatomy. EPBD was performed when the bile duct is narrow, and balloon size is selected based on duct diameter and stone size to avoid overexpansion and ductal injury. A wire-guided biliary balloon dilation catheter is advanced over a guidewire into the CBD, positioning the balloon across the papilla or strictured segment. The balloon is gradually inflated using a pressure-controlled syringe to the manufacturer-specified maximum pressure (usually 8–12 atm). Stones are then extracted using standard retrieval balloons or baskets. For larger stones (>10 mm), mechanical lithotripsy is required. ERPD was performed when the guidewire was inserted into the pancreatic duct more than three times during the operation. ENBD was performed when there were residual stones, difficult or repeated cannulation attempts, oedema in the distal bile duct or other risk factors for post-ERCP complications.

Diagnosis of PEP

The diagnostic criteria of PEP are as follows: (1) Duration of symptoms of pancreatitis that is more than 24 hours after ERCP. (3) The level of serum amylase and lipase was three times higher than the normal upper limit. (4) Imaging examinations indicate acute pancreatitis. Patients who satisfy two of the criteria can be diagnosed as PEP.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were first tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean±SD, while non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median with IQR. Comparisons of categorical variables between groups were performed using Pearson’s χ² test. Comparisons of normally distributed continuous variables were conducted using independent samples t-tests, and comparisons of non-normally distributed continuous variables were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. To assess whether the excluded cases due to incomplete clinical or procedural data (n=73) were missing at random (MAR), we compared baseline characteristics (sex, age, ALT, TBIL, CBD diameter and stone diameter) between the excluded group and the included cohort (n=2970). No significant differences were observed for any of these variables (all p>0.05), suggesting that the missing data were likely MAR. Therefore, the exclusion of these cases is unlikely to have introduced substantial selection bias (online supplemental table 1). To control for confounding biases, we performed 1:1 PSM based on the following eight baseline characteristics: age, sex, ALT, TBIL, CBD diameter, stone diameter, expansion diameter and operation duration. The calliper was set at 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score.10 11 The full specification of the logistic regression model used to generate the propensity score, including coefficients and SEs for all covariates, is provided in online supplemental table 2. 110 cases in each group were included after PSM analysis (figure 2).

For this matched cohort (n=220), we employed the following methods to analyse risk factors: Risk factor analysis for postoperative pancreatitis (PEP): (1) Single-factor and multiple-factor logistic regression models were employed. Single-factor analysis was conducted first. Subsequently, variables with p<0.1 in the single-factor analysis and clinically significant variables (particularly those with persistent baseline imbalance postmatching, such as duodenal diverticulum-JDD) were incorporated into the multiple-factor model for adjustment. OR and their 95% CIs were calculated. (2) Analysis of factors influencing length of stay: Multivariate linear regression models were used to analyse the impact of various factors on postoperative hospital days. (3) Analysis of factors influencing inadequate balloon dilatation: Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were employed to explore independent factors associated with inadequate balloon dilatation. To assess the robustness of our findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the entire unmatched cohort (n=2970). In this analysis, we employed multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for the same covariates included in our main model, to evaluate the association between insufficient balloon dilatation and PEP without PSM. The results of this analysis are presented in online supplemental table 3. To evaluate covariate balance, we calculated standardised mean differences (SMDs) for all baseline variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, SMD was calculated using the standard formula based on means and SD. For non-normally distributed continuous variables presented as median (IQR), we estimated the mean by the median and the SD by IQR/1.35. For categorical variables, SMD was calculated using the proportion formula. An SMD absolute value <0.2 was considered to indicate acceptable balance. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.25 software. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 3341 patients were initially enrolled, of whom 371 were excluded based on the predefined criteria. Thus, 2970 patients were initially enrolled, with 116 (3.9%) classified into the insufficient dilatation group and 2854 (96.1%) into the sufficient dilatation group. Before PSM, the insufficient dilatation group was significantly younger (53.9±15.1 years vs 59.0±12.2 years, p=0.044), had higher ALT levels (median 182.5 (IQR 64.5–300.5) U/L vs 145.0 (IQR 59.3–230.7) U/L, p=0.078), longer operation duration (median 36.0 (IQR 28.2–43.8) min vs 29.0 (IQR 22.9–31.5) min, p<0.001) and longer postoperative hospitalisation (median 7.5 (IQR 4.3–10.7) days vs 6.0 (IQR 4.0–8.0) days, p<0.001). TBIL also tended to be higher in the insufficient dilatation group (median 65.2 (IQR 25.0–106.6) μmol/L vs 45.4 (IQR 20.7–69.7) μmol/L, p=0.057) (table 1).

After one-to-one PSM, 220 cases were included, comprising 110 in each group. In the matched cohort, 43.6% and 51.8% of cases were male in the insufficient and sufficient dilatation groups, respectively, with no significant difference. The average age was 55.3 years in the insufficient dilatation group, showing no significant difference compared with the sufficient dilatation group. There were also no significant differences in ALT levels, CBD diameter, maximal stone diameter, expansion diameter, number of stones or ratio of ERPD operation between the two groups. Additionally, operative duration and postoperative hospitalisation were shorter in the sufficient dilatation group (p=0.032 and p=0.041, respectively), and fewer patients required mechanical lithotripsy (p=0.029) (table 2).

More patients in insufficient dilatation groups have PEP

We recorded the five kinds of post-ERCP complications including pancreatitis, cholangitis, cholecystitis, haemorrhage and perforation here. As expected, PEP is the most common complication. 36 of 220 (16.4%) patients developed PEP, and the PEP cases in the underexpanded group were nearly three times more than the sufficient dilatation group (p=0.002) (table 3). Other complications are rare in both groups and no cholecystitis was found. Table 4 summarises the incidence of PEP according to different clinical characteristics in the matched cohort. Briefly, no significant differences were observed in age, sex, CBD diameter, stone number, mechanical lithotripsy or duodenal diverticulum between patients with and without PEP (all p>0.05).

Insufficient dilatation is a risk factor for PEP

To investigate whether the clinical characteristics mentioned above influence the incidence of PEP, we performed a logistic regression analysis. In univariate analysis, only the insufficient dilatation was found to positively associate with PEP development, with an OR of 3.651 and 95% CI 1.627 to 8.193 (table 5). Insufficient dilatation was correlated to PEP with an OR of 4.302 and 95% CI 1.874 to 9.879 (table 5). The ENBD operation also showed an association with PEP in multivariate analysis (OR: 0.391, 95% CI 0.178 to 0.858, p=0.019), although there was no statistical significance in univariate analysis (p=0.070). A sensitivity analysis conducted in the entire unmatched cohort (n=2970) yielded consistent results. In the multivariate model, insufficient dilatation remained independently associated with an increased risk of PEP (OR 3.841, 95% CI 1.671 to 8.832, p=0.002), while ENBD remained a protective factor (OR 0.415, 95% CI 0.188 to 0.917, p=0.029) (online supplemental table 3).

JDD was discovered to correlate to the sufficient dilatation of the balloon

We next investigated which factors might influence the expansion of the balloon through logistic regression analysis. As shown in table 7, the level of TBIL and the JDD were significantly negatively associated with the insufficient dilatation of the balloon in univariate analysis. However, only JDD was still significantly correlated to insufficient dilatation in multivariate analysis with an OR of 0.388 and 95% CI 0.210 to 0.716, which indicated the diverticula is more likely to contribute to the sufficient dilatation of the balloon.

Discussion

In the current study, we investigated the risk factors for PEP in the EPBD operation for choledocholithiasis treatment and found that insufficient dilatation was associated with PEP and increased hospitalisation time.

Previous studies indicated EPBD has lower rates of bleeding and perforation, while its effects on the incidence of PEP were controversial.12 Some factors during EPBD operation were considered risk factors for PEP, such as pancreatic duct injection of contrast medium and pancreatitis history but not balloon dilation itself.12 Higher pressure and longer dilatation may contribute to the lower PEP incidence.6 A recent study identified the dilatation duration of EPBD <3 min is an independent risk factor for PEP.13 Insufficient papillary dilatation was hypothesised to be a potential risk factor for PEP since it might increase the need for mechanical lithotripsy.6 We found the PEP ratio in the insufficient dilatation group was significantly higher than in the sufficient dilatation group, which is consistent with the hypothesis. Due to insufficient dilatation, more patients received mechanical lithotripsy and the stones might not be completely cleared, which is considered an important risk factor for ERCP-related complications.14 15 However, we did not find associations between mechanical lithotripsy and PEP here. The reason for the insufficient dilation associated with the PEP may be a problem with the sphincter of Oddi itself.

The level of serum TBIL was found to be independently associated with the failure of the first ERCP to achieve bile duct clearance.16 Although there was no correlation between TBIL and PEP, we found it contributed to a prolonged length of postoperative hospitalisation, indicating the predicted role of TBIL in choledocholithiasis treatment. The younger age was considered a risk factor for PEP. However, the age was discovered not to be associated with pancreatitis in our regression analysis. This is consistent with several previous studies that indicated age is correlated to PEP but not to post-EPBD pancreatitis.12 17

ENBD after ERCP treatment of CBD stones was demonstrated to decrease the incidence of PEP. A previous study found ENBD reduces the incidence of hyperamylasaemia and decreases the length of hospital stay in EST patients and another discovered ENBD prevents PEP in patients who received EPBD clearance of stones.18 19 In this study, the logistic regression analysis also indicated ENBD as a protective factor for PEP and was associated with hospital stays.

We finally investigated the influence factors of the insufficient dilatation of the balloons and found those with JDD are more susceptible to sufficient dilatation. JDD generally does not induce symptoms itself. Nevertheless, studies indicated the diverticula might obstruct the bile duct following cholelithiasis, and is a risk factor for the development and recurrence of the gallbladder and bile duct stones.20 21 Moreover, it was found that impacted JDD can induce acute pancreatitis, and may associated with PEP.22 23 However, here we found that JDD contributed to the sufficient dilatation. The reason might be that the sphincter beside the diverticulum is weak and relatively easy to dilate.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it carries inherent risks of selection and information bias. Although we compared baseline characteristics between the excluded cases with incomplete data (n=73) and the included cohort and found no significant differences, indicating that missing data were likely MAR, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of non-random missingness due to unobserved factors. Nevertheless, the proportion of excluded cases was relatively small (2.2%, 73/3341), and the comparative analysis supported the MAR assumption, mitigating concerns about selection bias. Second, although PSM was used to balance intergroup differences based on age, sex, ALT, TBIL, CBD diameter, stone diameter, expansion diameter and operation duration, other unmeasured or unaccounted-for confounding factors (eg, papillary morphology, stone hardness, operator experience variation) cannot be excluded as potential sources of residual confounding. Although we adjusted for variables remaining imbalanced after matching (eg, JDD) in multivariate analysis, prospective, large-sample studies remain the gold standard for validating our conclusions. Third, the definition of ‘inadequate balloon dilatation’ relied on visual assessment during intraoperative imaging (disappearance of the balloon waist sign), which inherently carries subjectivity despite adherence to JGES guidelines. Fourth, as a multicentre study, differences in endoscopic techniques and equipment across centres may exist. Moreover, all participating centres are tertiary referral hospitals, so the cohort may not represent patients with mild or uncomplicated choledocholithiasis treated in community hospitals. This limits the generalisability of our conclusions. Although all procedures were performed by experienced specialists, such variations could affect the homogeneity of results. Additionally, data on patient race/ethnicity and systemic comorbidities (eg, hypertension, diabetes mellitus) were not systematically collected in this retrospective study. Although the study population was derived from a racially homogeneous Chinese cohort and no significant associations with PEP were observed in preliminary analyses, the absence of these variables may limit the generalisability of our findings. Future prospective studies should incorporate comprehensive comorbidity assessments. Finally, while the matching process enhanced comparability between groups, it resulted in a significant reduction in sample size, from 2970 to 220 patients. This loss of sample size, while methodologically necessary to control for confounding, inevitably constrains the statistical power of our study. This is particularly relevant for identifying risk factors with small effect sizes and for the stability of our multivariate model. The wide CI for the primary association (insufficient dilatation and PEP: OR 4.302, 95% CI 1.874 to 9.879) reflects this uncertainty. Furthermore, the limited number of events in certain subgroups, such as mechanical lithotripsy, may lead to type II error, potentially explaining why previously reported risk factors did not reach significance in our analysis. Therefore, negative findings should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes, specifically designed to capture these endpoints, are warranted to validate our findings and provide more precise effect estimates.

In conclusion, this study suggests the insufficient dilatation of the balloons in EPBD operation contributes to the development of PEP while ENBD can decrease the risk of PEP. Patients with JDD are more likely to achieve sufficient dilatation of the balloons. These results might help choose therapeutic strategies for treating ERCP patients.
