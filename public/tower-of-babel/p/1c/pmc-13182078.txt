Regional hip bone density patterns in femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures: a single-center retrospective study of 500 older adults in midtown Shanghai
Authors: Yinhua Ma, Xiaoping Xu, Mingying Gu, Yunfei Li, Lan Fei, Xin Zhang, Qingquan Zheng, Yong Yin, You Wang, Zhuocheng Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHip fragility fractures in older adults commonly present as femoral neck or intertrochanteric fractures, which may reflect different regional deficits in proximal femoral bone mineral density (BMD). This study assessed whether QCT-derived regional hip BMD patterns can distinguish femoral neck from intertrochanteric fragility fractures.MethodsRetrospective analysis of 500 patients aged ≥65 years (2020–2024). QCT-derived T-scores for femoral neck (FN), trochanter (FT), intertrochanter (IT), and total hip (TH) were evaluated. Multivariable logistic regression (age, sex, FT T-score, TH T-score) identified predictors; a point-based score was derived.ResultsFracture types: 277 femoral neck, 223 intertrochanteric; mean age 80.2±7.7 years. Intertrochanteric cases were older (82.6±7.2 vs 78.3±7.5, p<0.01) with lower regional T-scores (all p<0.01). FT T-score was the strongest discriminator (OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.11–0.35, p<0.001). Higher TH T-score paradoxically associated with intertrochanteric fractures (OR 2.16, 95% CI 1.35–3.44, p=0.001), indicating focal trochanteric deficit relative to preserved composite density. Men with intertrochanteric fractures showed localized trochanteric loss; women demonstrated more diffuse decline. Final model AUC 0.747 (95% CI 0.704–0.791).ConclusionsQCT-derived regional hip T-score patterns, particularly the trochanteric T-score, help differentiate femoral neck from intertrochanteric fragility fractures. The discordance between low trochanteric and relatively preserved total hip density may provide a useful phenotype for fracture characterization and risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09776-z.

Summary

In older adults with hip fractures, we used CT-based bone density maps to see if patterns differ by fracture type. Lower density around the trochanter best distinguished intertrochanteric fractures. A simple score using age, sex, and regional densities predicted fracture type, suggesting targeted assessment may guide prevention and treatment.

Background

Compared to Shanghai’s central urban areas, Jiading District is a new city that has transformed from a traditional suburb into a highly developed modern area. Statistics show that individuals aged 65 and above account for approximately 27% of the local population. Although this figure is slightly lower than Shanghai’s average of 29%, it still indicates that the district has entered a super-aged society. However, in comparison to the central urban areas, medical resources in Jiading remain relatively insufficient. In the process of urbanization, most local elderly people have gotten rid of heavy physical labor in rural areas. At the same time, their awareness and attention to the prevention of osteoporosis them are relatively lower. Over four years, our medical center located in the district recorded 662 cases of hip fractures due to various causes, among which 500 cases were purely fragility fractures. Therefore, appropriate treatment, intervention, and the prevention of osteoporosis warrant significant attention [1].

Hip fracture is one of the most common osteoporotic fractures in the elderly population and is accompanied by relatively high rates of morbidity and mortality within one year after injury [2, 3]. As in many Asian countries, with the rapid progression of population aging in China, many local medical centers are facing increasing challenges in treating octogenarian and nonagenarian patients with hip fractures. The high prevalence of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and other systemic comorbidities in elderly patients increases both the risk and cost of surgical treatment, and the different surgical procedures for various osteoporotic hip fracture types also have distinct implant‑related complications.

Femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures differ in patient demographics, injury mechanisms, perioperative management, implant selection, complication profiles, and functional prognosis [4]. Femoral neck fractures are increasingly treated with arthroplasty using cemented stems, whereas trochanteric and subtrochanteric fractures are increasingly treated with intramedullary (IM) nailing [4, 5]. The main local complications after total or hemi hip arthroplasty for elderly femoral neck fractures include prosthetic loosening, intraoperative or postoperative periprosthetic fracture, hip dislocation, and acetabular erosion. In contrast, screw cut‑out and varus malunion are common failure modes of intramedullary fixation systems for intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric fractures. These findings are consistent with clinical evidence that treatment efficacy for fractures in different regions of the elderly hip is closely related to the degree of local osteoporosis, which may prevent an implant or fixation device from achieving stable purchase in the proximal femur or may allow it to penetrate the acetabular surface. There have been apparent changes in treatment philosophy and related complication profiles compared with the past in managing femoral neck versus trochanteric region fractures [6]. A rational explanation is that optimal management of elderly hip fractures must be based on a better understanding of localized osteoporosis in the hip region.

Decline in bone mineral density (BMD) around the proximal femur is one of the key indicators and a gold standard criterion for diagnosing osteoporosis [7]. However, its predictive value for hip fractures and for specific fracture locations remains controversial [8]. Previous research has shown that with increasing age, hip BMD gradually declines and the overall incidence of fragility hip fractures increases [9]. However, the relative probabilities of different hip fracture types vary by age and sex. Tanner et al. reported that with increasing age the proportion of intertrochanteric fractures rises in women, whereas the proportion of femoral neck fractures remains higher in older men [10]. Li et al. performed a quantitative analysis of hip BMD in 196 Chinese patients aged over 50 with osteoporotic hip fractures and found that patients with intertrochanteric fractures were generally older and had lower hip BMD than those with femoral neck fractures [6]. In addition to age and sex, torsional and bending‑related local morphological and geometric parameters—such as femoral neck cross‑sectional moment of inertia and trochanteric buckling ratio—also significantly influence the incidence of intertrochanteric fractures. Recent systematic evidence reviews by the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force on osteoporosis screening found that BMD screening in women over 65 has only a small absolute association with reducing hip fractures and offers limited benefit for men and for women under 65 [11]. The discriminatory ability of measuring hip BMD alone to predict whether and where a hip fracture will occur remains weak. Awal’s research further showed that quantitative computed tomography (QCT)‑based three‑dimensional (3D) finite element model analysis is more valuable than BMD alone in predicting the occurrence of hip fractures [12]. However, such methods have shortcomings in practical application, including complex analytical procedures, limited ability to determine the precise future fracture location, and suboptimal feasibility.

QCT provides calibrated volumetric BMD for the femoral neck, greater trochanter, and intertrochanteric regions, offering region‑specific data that complement and correlate with, but are not numerically interchangeable with, DXA areal BMD [13, 14]. We hypothesize that not only is hip BMD associated with the incidence of fractures at different sites, but that the regional distribution of BMD across the femoral neck, greater trochanteric region, and intertrochanteric area may also be associated with the occurrence of femoral neck versus intertrochanteric fractures. In this study, by analyzing QCT profiles of 500 adults aged 65 years and older who sustained hip fractures due to falls, we analyzed the correlation between hip BMD and its regional distribution and the incidence of femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures. This study aims to explore a convenient and feasible diagnostic approach for more precise prevention and treatment of hip fractures.

Methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted at Jiading District Central Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Medicine and Health Sciences, Shanghai, China. The study period spanned from 1 December 2020 through 31 December 2024. Methods and reporting followed the STROBE guideline for observational studies.

Ethics

The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiading District Central Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Medicine and Health Sciences (Approval No. 2025-53,). The ethics committee granted a waiver of individual informed consent because the study was purely retrospective, posed minimal risk to participants, and all data were processed in a de‑identified manner. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).

Participants

During the study, we screened 662 consecutive inpatients aged ≥ 65 years admitted with an acute low‑energy hip fracture (femoral neck or intertrochanteric). Inclusion criteria: (1) radiologically confirmed proximal femur fracture (AO/OTA 31-B [femoral neck] or 31-A [intertrochanteric]); (2) ability to retrieve required demographic data. Exclusion criteria were predefined to improve cohort homogeneity and to ensure the feasibility and validity of contralateral hip QCT analysis. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following: (1) age < 65 years; (2) high-energy trauma (e.g., traffic accident, fall from height); (3) conditions known to substantially alter bone metabolism or QCT-based BMD interpretation, including chronic kidney disease (especially CKD-MBD), rheumatoid arthritis or other chronic inflammatory joint diseases, thyroid disease (hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism requiring treatment), or malignancy; (4) CT data not suitable for QCT analysis of the contralateral (non-fractured) hip, defined a priori as inability to reliably perform semi-automated segmentation of the predefined ROIs due to: (a) prior contralateral hip arthroplasty or internal fixation hardware; (b) severe contralateral hip deformity or advanced destructive hip pathology (e.g., end-stage osteoarthritis with major deformity, femoral head collapse/necrosis, severe dysplasia) preventing consistent ROI placement; (c) incomplete anatomical coverage of the contralateral proximal femur (missing femoral neck/trochanter/intertrochanteric landmarks required for ROI definition); or (d) prohibitive image artifacts (e.g., severe motion or metal artifacts) that would invalidate densitometric measurements. All QCT measurements were obtained from the contralateral hip to minimize fracture-side artifacts and potential early post-fracture remodeling effects. After applying these criteria, 500 patients with unilateral fragility femoral neck or intertrochanteric fractures remained for analysis. (Fig. 1). All patients included in this study presented with a unilateral femoral neck or intertrochanteric fracture. No bilateral hip fractures were identified in the study cohort.

Fracture type classification

Fractures were classified as femoral neck or intertrochanteric using preoperative anteroposterior and lateral radiographs and thin‑slice CT multiplanar reconstructions. Two fellowship‑trained orthopedic surgeons independently reviewed images while blinded to quantitative BMD metrics and patient demographics beyond age and sex. A senior musculoskeletal radiologist adjudicated discordant classifications.

CT acquisition protocol

All patients underwent multi‑detector spiral CT scanning on a single scanner (United Imaging UCT530, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) from the iliac crest to 4 cm distal to the lesser trochanter. The scan mode was helical with a pitch of 0.9875 and a minimum slice thickness of 0.5 mm. Axial images were reconstructed at 1.0‑mm contiguous intervals. Patients were positioned supine with both lower limbs gently internally rotated (~ 15°) when tolerated. All QCT measurements were performed unilaterally on the contralateral (non-fractured) hip.

QCT analysis

Quantitative CT measurements were performed using PL-QCT software (Bones QCT, Bones Technology Limited, Hong Kong, China). Semi‑automated segmentation defined four regions of interest (ROIs): (1) total hip (TH; integral ROI encompassing the femoral neck, trochanteric, and intertrochanteric regions), (2) femoral neck (FN), (3) femoral trochanter (FT), and (4) intertrochanter (IT) (see Supplementary Figure S1 for schematic boundaries). The software’s default integral (cortico‑trabecular combined) volumetric BMD (vBMD) algorithm with partial volume correction was applied; no manual cortical–trabecular separation was performed. Areal BMD (aBMD, g/cm2), volumetric BMD (vBMD, mg/cm3), and ROI‑specific T‑scores (FNT, FTT, ITT, THT) were generated automatically. For patients with unilateral fractures, measurements were obtained from the contralateral (uninjured) hip to minimize fracture-side artifacts and potential early post-fracture remodeling effects. In the present cohort, contralateral ROI segmentation and QCT-derived T-scores were feasible in all included cases; thus, no participant was excluded due to contralateral hip non-feasibility (e.g., prior contralateral hip implant, severe deformity, incomplete coverage, or prohibitive artifacts).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were two‑tailed with α = 0.05 and performed in SPSS (v28.0.0, IBM) and R (2025.05.1, R Foundation). Continuous variables were inspected for distributional form (Shapiro–Wilk test, Q–Q plots) and presented as mean ± SD. Between-group comparisons (intertrochanteric vs. femoral neck) used an independent samples t‑test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared by χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Univariable logistic regression estimated crude odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Candidate predictors for multivariable modeling were pre-specified (age, sex, FNT, FTT, THT, ITT, scanner manufacturer) plus any additional clinically relevant covariates. Multicollinearity was assessed via the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5 considered acceptable); where high collinearity among T-scores occurred, competing models were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and likelihood ratio tests.

Model performance was quantified by discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, AUC, with 95% CI by DeLong), and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit).

Development of the point-based score: Final model coefficients (β) were divided by the smallest absolute non-zero β and multiplied by an integer scaling factor, then rounded to the nearest integer to assign raw points. To retain the effect size hierarchy, sex was weighted ×3 and FT T-score category × 2 (details in Results). The total score equaled 3×(sex points) + (age points) + 2 × FTT + THT. The optimal discrimination cut-off (> 7) was chosen using the Youden index, and sensitivity, specificity, positive/negative predictive values, and likelihood ratios were calculated with 95% CIs.

All p-values are reported to three decimal places; those < 0.001 are shown as < 0.001 without further decimal truncation.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 500 patients were included based on the above-mentioned criteria. The mean age was 80.21 ± 7.68 years. There were 277 femoral neck fractures (84 men and 193 women) and 223 intertrochanteric fractures (72 men and 151 women). The average age of the patients was different in the two groups (femoral neck and intertrochanteric), at 78.33 ± 7.54 years and 82.55 ± 7.22 years (P < 0.01), respectively. Patients with intertrochanteric fractures were older than those with femoral neck fractures, especially in women. Association of age and sex with regional hip T-scores

As presented in Fig. 2A, the correlation analysis revealed that age was negatively correlated with all regional hip T-scores (r = -0.229 for the femoral neck, -0.261 for the trochanter, -0.208 for the intertrochanteric region, and − 0.230 for the total hip; all p < 0.001). In addition, substantial inter-regional collinearity was observed, with pairwise correlations ranging from r = 0.786 (femoral neck vs. trochanter) to r = 0.984 (intertrochanteric vs. total hip), supporting parsimony in subsequent multivariable modeling.

Comparison of demographic and hip densitometric characteristics between femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures

Using independent t-tests for continuous variables (age, site-specific hip T-scores, inter-site T-score differences) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (sex, fracture laterality, osteoporosis prevalence), we found that patients with intertrochanteric fractures were on average approximately four years older (p < 0.01), had a markedly higher prevalence of osteoporosis (p < 0.01), and exhibited consistently lower T-scores across femoral neck, trochanter, intertrochanteric region, and total hip (reductions roughly 0.25–0.5 T-score units, trochanter largest, p < 0.01, small-to-moderate Cohen’s d) (Table 3). Baseline comorbidities were comparable between the intertrochanteric fracture group and the femoral neck fracture group. Sex distribution and side of injury did not differ (both p > 0.6), reducing the likelihood of confounding from these factors. Analysis of inter-regional T-score differences demonstrated a large-effect contraction of the FNT-FTT and a more negative FNT-ITT (both p < 0.001), while the FTT-ITT remained nonsignificant (p > 0.2), localizing the principal densitometric reconfiguration to the neck–trochanter axis rather than within the trochanteric complex itself. Collectively, these statistics delineate a more diffuse yet disproportionately trochanter-centered bone deficit milieu underlying intertrochanteric fractures—potentially reflecting regionally accentuated trabecular deterioration or altered load-transfer mechanics.

Age-stratified analysis of the sex and bone mineral density characteristics between intertrochanteric or femoral neck fractures

In the 65–74 year group, only sex showed a significant difference (p ≈ 0.02). Osteoporosis prevalence, all regional hip T-scores, and all inter-regional T-score differences (FNT–FTT, FNT–ITT, FTT–ITT) were non‑significant (all p > 0.40) (Table S2). In the 75–84 year group, sex no longer differed (p > 0.90); intertrochanteric fractures had a higher osteoporosis prevalence (p ≈ 0.004) and higher FTT, ITT, and THT (all p < 0.05), while FNT remained non‑different (p > 0.50). The FNT–FTT was larger (more negative) in femoral neck fractures, whereas other internal differences contributed little additional separation (Table S3). In the ≥ 85 year group, sex and osteoporosis prevalence were non‑significant (p > 0.18); differences persisted at FTT (p < 0.01) and THT (p < 0.05); FNT and ITT were only borderline (p ≈ 0.06–0.07); internal differences, including FNT-FTT, FNT-ITT, and FTT-ITT, narrowed further (Table S4). The pattern reveals a progression from demographic distinctions based solely on sex in the youngest age group to a middle age phase marked by higher osteoporosis prevalence, regional differences, and notable separation between FNT and FTT. In the oldest age group, group contrasts decrease, with remaining differences mainly in FTT and weaker separation in THT, while FNT and ITT lose clear significance.

Sex-stratified analysis of the sex and bone mineral density characteristics between intertrochanteric or femoral neck fractures

In sex‑stratified analyses (Table S5, S6), intertrochanteric fractures were consistently associated with older age, but the T‑score diverged markedly between men and women. Among men, intertrochanteric fractures occurred 2.7 years later than femoral neck fractures (80.7 ± 8.0 vs. 78.0 ± 6.8; p = 0.02). Regional T‑scores differed focally: FNT were similar (p = 0.43), whereas the trochanter was substantially more demineralized in the intertrochanteric group (p < 0.001), with only a borderline lower THT (p = 0.07) and a nonsignificant ITT difference (p = 0.18). Inter-regional T‑score differences emphasized a topographic shift: the F.N.–F.T. differential inverted (p < 0.001), and the F.T.–I.T. gap widened (− 1.93 vs. − 1.62; p = 0.02), while FNT–ITT remained comparable (p = 0.14), delineating a predominantly trochanter‑centric T‑score deficit in men with intertrochanteric fractures. In women, intertrochanteric fractures presented at an older age (83.4 ± 6.7 vs. 78.5 ± 7.9; p = 0.02) with a markedly higher osteoporosis prevalence (p < 0.001). T‑scores at all sites were lower in that group compared with the non‑intertrochanteric group. Unlike men, women exhibited a more global hip T‑score depression rather than a strictly focal trochanteric decrement. T-score difference analysis showed larger FNT–FTT separation (0.46 vs. 0.25; p < 0.001) and a narrowed FNT–ITT difference (− 1.05 vs. − 1.25; p < 0.001), while FTT–ITT was indistinguishable (p = 0.80), indicating a relative convergence of intertrochanteric subregion loss in the intertrochanteric fracture cohort. Collectively, these data suggest a sex‑dimorphic structural vulnerability: men with intertrochanteric fractures display a localized trochanteric T‑score deficit with preserved femoral neck T‑scores, whereas women present with broader multicompartment hip T‑score depression coupled with a higher categorical osteoporosis burden.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of intertrochanteric and femoral neck fracture

In the stepwise multivariable logistic regression distinguishing intertrochanteric from femoral neck fractures, the final model (Table 4) retained four predictors: sex, age, FTT, and THT (AUC 0.747; 95% CI 0.704 ~ 0.791) (Figure S2). Each additional year of age increased the odds of an intertrochanteric (vs. femoral neck) fracture by 6% (β = 0.06; OR 1.06; 95% CI 1.03 ~ 1.09; p < 0.001). Sex conferred a little over a two‑fold higher odds (β = 0.76; OR 2.14; 95% CI 1.34–3.43; p = 0.001). The FTT showed the strongest inverse association (β=−1.63; OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.11–0.35; p < 0.001). In contrast, the THT entered with a positive coefficient (β = 0.77; OR 2.16; 95% CI 1.35–3.44; p = 0.001), implying that, conditional on trochanteric status, relative preservation of overall hip mineralization increases the probability of an intertrochanteric (rather than femoral neck) pattern. Overall, the model delineates an intertrochanteric fracture profile characterized by older age, sex‑specific susceptibility, and a disproportionate trochanteric T‑score deficit occurring in the context of relatively preserved total hip density.

Discussion

In this study of 500 patients aged ≥ 65 years with low‑energy hip fractures, intertrochanteric fractures—compared with femoral neck fractures—were characterized not by a uniformly lower composite hip bone mineral density, but by a distributional imbalance: a disproportionately reduced greater trochanteric value with relative preservation of the other regions. Prior work has consistently shown that femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures differ in patient profiles, biomechanical loading conditions during a sideways fall, and implant-related complication patterns. However, evidence has been mixed regarding whether conventional single-site BMD measures (e.g., femoral neck or total hip DXA) meaningfully discriminate fracture subtype, which may partly explain the modest clinical utility of BMD alone for predicting where a hip fracture will occur. Studies comparing BMD and hip geometry across fracture types suggest that both densitometric and structural parameters contribute, while more recent work using QCT-derived measures and patient-specific modeling further supports the concept that proximal femoral strength is regionally heterogeneous and not fully captured by composite areal BMD. Our results add to this body of literature by demonstrating that, among older adults with fragility hip fracture, an intertrochanteric fracture phenotype is characterized less by uniformly low composite density and more by a disproportionate trochanteric deficit relative to overall hip density, with a sex-dimorphic pattern (more focal in men, more diffuse in women). These findings provide structural evidence that helps explain the previously limited predictive performance of single‑site BMD measurements.

Prior studies linked overall bone mineral density and demographic factors to hip fracture risk [1–3]. Our QCT-based finding that intertrochanteric fractures are characterized less by uniformly depressed composite hip mineralization and more by a disproportionately low trochanteric T‑score—while the total hip T‑score is comparatively preserved after statistical adjustment—refines earlier DXA observations which predominantly emphasized globally lower total hip or trochanteric BMD in intertrochanteric versus femoral neck fracture cohorts [15]. This “imbalanced distribution” pattern helps explain why single composite measures (total hip or femoral neck BMD alone) deliver only moderate fracture subtype discrimination and why large-scale screening recommendations (e.g., USPSTF) note limited incremental yield from repeating standard BMD in older women beyond identifying overall fracture risk rather than fracture pattern [11]. Collectively, these data support a shift from single-site absolute BMD paradigms toward distribution-sensitive metrics to refine hip fracture phenotyping and potentially guide targeted preventive or implant strategy decisions.

Mechanistic interpretation

The proximal femur is a structurally heterogeneous load‑bearing unit. The femoral neck, with a higher proportion of cortical bone, resists bending and shear, whereas the trochanteric and intertrochanteric regions are rich in metabolically active trabecular bone characterized by faster remodeling and earlier loss of trabecular thickness and connectivity [16, 17]. Early and pronounced trabecular thinning, increased separation, and connectivity loss in the trochanteric region—a trabecular‑rich area—are associated with greater local concentration of impact energy and combined bending–shear stresses during a sideways fall, predisposing to intertrochanteric fracture [18, 19]. Conversely, when generalized low bone density involves the proximal femur globally, concomitant cortical thinning and trabecular deterioration of the femoral neck reduce the mechanical failure threshold and favor femoral neck fracture [20, 21]. Our observation that, for a given low trochanteric T score, a relatively higher total hip T score (indicating that the neck and other regions are comparatively preserved) is associated with intertrochanteric rather than femoral neck fracture supports a load distribution pattern in which focal trochanteric weakening precedes more global deterioration, analogous to initial failure initiating at the most compromised local segment in an engineered structure. Additionally, periarticular muscle atrophy can exacerbate load concentration at a structurally weak trochanteric region: gluteus medius and minimus atrophy has been associated with increased risk of fall‑related hip fractures [22], suggesting that concurrent degeneration of muscle and bone in the trochanteric region magnifies the mechanical consequences of regional mineral deficit.

Primary prevention

In addition to conventional whole-site DXA or FRAX-based assessment, attention to a relative trochanteric deficit on opportunistic CT or dedicated low-dose QCT may help phenotype regional vulnerability and flag some individuals who could be at higher risk for intertrochanteric fracture, even when global thresholds for osteoporosis have not yet been met. Importantly, this information should be viewed as adjunctive rather than as a stand-alone screening strategy, because gaps in primary and secondary fracture prevention are driven largely by health-system and patient-level barriers (e.g., screening coverage, care coordination, treatment initiation, and adherence). In the HEALTH trial femoral neck fracture substudy (n = 2,520), only about 25% of patients initiated osteoporosis medication despite protocol recommendations; during 2-year follow-up, 9% sustained a new fracture and contralateral hip fractures comprised approximately 40% of these events, underscoring the persistent secondary prevention treatment gap [23]. In practice, incorporating a distribution-sensitive trochanteric deficit metric into inpatient or early post-fracture workflows—particularly when CT imaging is already available—may help prioritize timely evaluation and initiation of guideline-approved antiresorptive or anabolic therapy, along with standard measures such as correction of vitamin D deficiency when indicated, which may contribute to mitigating focal structural vulnerability [24].

Sports medicine intervention

The gluteus medius and minimus have been described as a “hip rotational cuff” that contributes to gait stability and postural control [22, 25, 26]. Consistent with this concept, Chi et al. reported that atrophy of these muscles is significantly associated with fall-related hip and pelvic fractures [22]. Building on these prior observations, our finding of focal trochanteric fragility supports a rehabilitation-forward clinical implication: interventions that improve hip abductor capacity and neuromuscular control may be particularly relevant for reducing lateral instability and facilitating postoperative functional recovery. Specifically, we suggest that (1) during early rehabilitation, in addition to conventional resistance training, programs emphasize progressively loaded hip abduction, pelvic stabilization, closed-chain neuromuscular control tasks, and rapid step-initiation drills to enhance abductor torque and dynamic balance; (2) because muscle quality may influence the response to strengthening, assessment of gluteus medius/minimus fatty infiltration (e.g., ultrasound or MRI when clinically indicated) may help tailor exercise dosing and motor-control strategies—while recognizing that such imaging is not routinely required; and (3) training aimed at modifying fall mechanics and dissipating lower-limb impact forces (e.g., soft-landing practice and lateral protective stepping) may reduce peak lateral impact loading at the trochanter during falls [27, 28]. Importantly, these rehabilitation principles are broadly supported in geriatric and postoperative care and should be applied independent of QCT; regional QCT patterns may serve primarily to phenotype risk and prioritize abductor-focused and balance-oriented elements in selected patients.

Impact on surgical strategy

Marked trabecular degradation in the trochanteric region—reflected by reduced local cancellous density—can indicate diminished implant purchase and increased risk of lag screw or blade cut‑out and rotational failure in intertrochanteric fractures [29–31]. Management considerations therefore include: (1) selecting a long cephalomedullary nail to extend the load transfer path in unstable patterns [7]; (2) evaluating polymethylmethacrylate or calcium phosphate augmentation to enhance anchorage where very low local density is present [32, 33]; (3) strict control of Tip–Apex Distance together with a central–inferior head element position to maximize the surrounding three‑dimensional bone stock and reduce cut‑out risk [31, 34]; and (4) in patients (often women) with diffuse proximal femoral bone loss who sustain a femoral neck fracture, choosing a cemented stem at arthroplasty to improve primary stability and reduce early displacement and reoperation or periprosthetic fracture risk [35, 36]. Regional (focal versus diffuse) T‑score patterns derived from subregional hip assessments may serve as supplementary information for procedure selection and planning of implant reinforcement [21].

Generalizability and external validity

This study was performed at a single center in Jiading District, Shanghai, and included hospitalized adults aged ≥ 65 years with low-energy hip fractures who had preoperative CT suitable for QCT-based regional analysis. Consequently, our findings may not be fully generalizable to other demographic or geographic settings with different ethnic composition, anthropometry, comorbidity profiles, osteoporosis treatment rates, fall circumstances, or health-care pathways. In particular, the absolute regional T-scores, the extent of the “trochanter-dominant” deficit pattern, and the performance and optimal threshold of the proposed point-based score may vary across populations and imaging workflows. Nevertheless, the underlying structural concept—regional heterogeneity of proximal femur bone density and a disproportionate trochanteric deficit as a discriminator of intertrochanteric fracture phenotype—has a plausible biomechanical basis and may be broadly relevant. Future studies should conduct external validation in independent multi-center cohorts (including non-Chinese and rural populations) with harmonized QCT acquisition/analysis and calibration procedures to confirm transportability and to recalibrate the score when needed.

Limitations and future directions

In this retrospective study, QCT metrics of the fractured hip were approximated using measurements from the contralateral (uninjured) hip. Although contralateral-hip QCT may be infeasible in some real-world patients with contralateral implants, severe deformity, or substantial artifacts, this issue did not arise in our included cohort (no technical exclusions). Nevertheless, future studies in broader populations should anticipate such scenarios and predefine alternative acquisition/analysis strategies.

Although side‑to‑side femoral BMD is strongly correlated, published Japanese data showed consistent left–right differences of approximately 3–5% at total hip, neck, and trochanter [37]. Future studies should prospectively acquire bilateral hip QCT (in high‑risk or screening cohorts) to derive standardized asymmetry indices for volumetric BMD, microarchitecture, finite element failure load, and gluteus medius/minimus muscle size/fat infiltration, thereby testing their added value for early detection of focal trochanteric fragility and fracture type prediction.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that intertrochanteric fractures in older adults were associated with a pattern of disproportionately reduced trochanteric bone mineral relative to total hip values, while femoral neck fractures were more often associated with more uniform regional reduction. This distribution-based information added discriminatory value beyond age, sex, and a single composite density measure, prioritizing rehabilitation strategies—particularly hip abductor strengthening, neuromuscular control, and balance training—to support fall-risk reduction and postoperative functional recovery. Integration of regional assessment with established risk tools and structured abductor muscle rehabilitation may improve prevention and management strategies for hip fracture subtypes.
