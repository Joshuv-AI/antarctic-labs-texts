Knowledge, attitudes and practices of health care professionals towards pharmacovigilance in Georgia
Authors: Nino Silagadze, Marina Darakhvelidze, Elza Nikoleishvili, Ana Katamadze, Ekaterine Ruadze, Nino Ganugrava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPharmacovigilance (PV) is fundamental for public health, focusing on the detection, evaluation, and prevention of adverse drug reactions (ADRs). ADRs significantly contribute to global morbidity and mortality. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) play a crucial role in ensuring drug safety, as their expertise is essential for the timely identification, management, and reporting of ADRs. Despite the importance of PV, underreporting remains a critical challenge, particularly in developing countries. The PV system in Georgia is ineffective and characterized by underreporting from healthcare professionals.Aim/objectiveTo evaluate the knowledge, attitudes and practices of PV among HCPs in Georgia.MethodsWe evaluated Georgian physicians’ and nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices about PV in this cross-sectional study, which ran from April 2023 to June 2024. A self-administered questionnaire was distributed to 342 doctors and 342 nurses who were chosen by multistage sampling in 16 districts across six regions. The questionnaire was modified from previous research to comply with local laws. Taking into account a design effect and an expected response rate, the sample size was determined using an estimated 20% awareness level of PV procedures. Data analysis was performed using STATA version 18.0, employing chi-square tests, t-tests, and descriptive statistics.ResultsAmong participants, 76.3% encountered ADRs in their practice, but only 12.95% reported them. None of the nurses and only 10.2% of physicians used the official Ministry of Health (MOH) reporting form. The main barriers to reporting were a lack of awareness about the national reporting scheme (91.7%), uncertainty about the reporting process (76.9%), and underestimation of ADR severity (40.8%). Physicians had higher knowledge scores than nurses (mean scores: 19 vs. 16; p < 0.001), while nurses had more positive attitudes toward ADR reporting (mean scores: 6 vs. 5.6; p < 0.001).ConclusionADR reporting among Georgian HCPs is notably low due to insufficient awareness and knowledge of the PV system. This gap delays timely identification and management of adverse effects, impacting patient safety and public health. To address these challenges, strengthening PV education is essential. Introduction

Pharmacovigilance (PV) has evolved substantially, becoming a cornerstone of public health by ensuring medication safety worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other medicine/vaccine related problem [1].

Globally, the high prevalence of ADRs has significantly contributed to increased morbidity and mortality rates in both the hospital and public sectors. On average, an estimated one in 10 patients is subject to an adverse event while receiving hospital care in high-income countries. Available evidence suggests that 134 million adverse events due to unsafe care occur in hospitals in low- and middle-income countries, contributing to around 2.6 million deaths every year. According to recent estimates, the social cost of patient harm can be valued at US$ 1trillion to 2 trillion a year [2–4]. ADRs are a major cause of patient suffering worldwide and are considered one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in many countries [5].

Integrating drug safety systems into public health programs is essential to optimize healthcare resources and prevent medication-related tragedies. A robust pharmacovigilance (PV) system ensures patient safety and the rational use of medicines by detecting and mitigating risks in a timely manner. Ultimately, safe and effective pharmacotherapy not only protects individual patients but also fosters public trust in national health policies; conversely, widespread adverse events can severely undermine the credibility of public health initiatives. Despite global progress in drug safety, ADRs remain a significant public health challenge, particularly in developing countries [6].

While clinical trials are essential for assessing drug safety, they may not reveal all potential side effects. Thus, post-marketing pharmacovigilance is crucial for identifying adverse reactions that only emerge during widespread use [7, 8].

An effectively integrated pharmacovigilance system within the public health framework can reduce costs, enable a more accurate assessment of the risks and benefits of drugs and help health care programs achieve their goals. Additionally, it serves as a form of insurance to prevent the use of counterfeit, ineffective, and substandard medicines while promoting the rational use of resources. The notion that pharmacovigilance is a luxury and available only to developed countries should be changed with the realization that the introduction of a pharmacovigilance system is essential for the rational and safe use of medicines in all countries [6, 9].

Health care professionals play a pivotal role in the pharmacovigilance system, requiring relevant knowledge and expertise to detect, manage, and report drug safety issues effectively. Despite all of the above, there is still a lack of knowledge of pharmacovigilance and its practical application among health care professionals. Health care professionals report only 2–4% of all adverse events and approximately 10% of serious adverse events globally, with even lower reporting rates observed in developing countries. This aligns with findings from various studies, which emphasize that underreporting of ADRs is a significant issue worldwide, particularly in resource-limited settings [10]. Previous studies assessing the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of HCPs in the broader region, such as a recent comprehensive study conducted in Turkey [11], consistently reveal significant deficits in PV awareness and a lack of training, which act as severe barriers to effective ADR reporting.

Georgia’s experience with pharmacovigilance

The legislative foundation for pharmacovigilance in Georgia is primarily provided by the Law of Georgia on Medicines and Pharmaceutical Activities, enacted in 1996. This law outlines the responsibilities of health care providers and regulatory bodies in ensuring drug safety. It mandates the monitoring of side effects of medicines, requiring medical specialists to participate in a unified system of side effect monitoring [12].

The Agency for the Regulation of Medical and Pharmaceutical Activities, operating under the Ministry of Health, is the primary body responsible for enforcing pharmacovigilance regulations. Its duties include granting marketing authorizations, managing the registry of pharmaceutical products, and monitoring the quality and safety of marketed products through pharmacovigilance activities.

Georgia became a full member of the World Health Organization’s Programme for International Drug Monitoring (PIDM) in 2018, collaborating with the Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) to enhance drug safety monitoring. However, Georgia’s PV system is weak and ineffective. Lack of reporting causes a failure to detect, collect, evaluate, monitor and prevent adverse drug events, which in turn increases the risk of harm, morbidity and mortality to patients in both the hospital and nonhospital sectors [13]. PV is not integrated into medical, nursing, or pharmacy education and Continuous Medical Education (CME) programs.

Despite the growing global interest in pharmacovigilance, limited research has assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of HCPs regarding ADR reporting in Georgia. To date, no nationwide study has evaluated the PV readiness of HCPs in Georgia. While evaluating and monitoring drug safety is crucial to prevent patient harm, achieving this requires an effective PV system. Assessing professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) is essential to identify the specific educational and systemic barriers driving adverse drug reaction (ADR) underreporting. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the KAP regarding PV among Georgian healthcare professionals. Ultimately, the findings will enable policymakers to design targeted, evidence-based interventions, thereby improving ADR reporting rates, strengthening the national PV framework, and ensuring optimal patient safety and public health outcomes.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study in randomly selected outpatient clinics and hospitals in 16 districts [6 regions] of Georgia. The study was conducted from April 2023 to June 2024.

The study population was selected based on specific eligibility criteria. Inclusion criteria comprised licensed physicians and nurses currently practicing in the selected outpatient clinics and hospitals who voluntarily consented to participate. Conversely, exclusion criteria applied to healthcare professionals who were on long-term leave during the data collection period, medical and nursing students, and participants who returned incomplete questionnaires.

Development of the questionnaire

A self-administered, structured questionnaire was developed based on insights from a review of the literature [14–16]. However, certain questions were modified to align with the specific regulations and practices within Georgia. For example, questions were tailored to address the existence of a local PV officer or a coordination group within the respondents’ institutions and whether the respondents had ever approached them. This questionnaire included three main aspects: knowledge of PV, attitudes towards ADR reporting, and current practices of ADR reporting (annex1).

Sample size calculation

Considering that the PV system in the country is sporadically implemented, it was believed that the expected number of physicians who had some information about the procedures of the PV system did not exceed 20%.

Sampling methodology

Multistage sampling was used, and all regions participated in the selection process. In the first stage of selection, districts were selected using the probability proportional to size (PPS) method. This meant that districts where more doctors worked had a greater chance of having more hospitals or outpatient clinics included in the study.

After the districts were determined, the probability proportional to size (PPS) method was also used to select hospitals and outpatient centers. Accordingly, hospitals or outpatient centers where more doctors work have a greater chance of being included in the selection process.

Validation of the instrument

The questionnaire underwent a piloting and face validity assessment with 10 doctors and 10 nurses. The evaluation focused on clarity of understanding, time required for completion, and response consistency. Specifically, to evaluate the reliability and strengthen the instrument’s credibility, we qualitatively assessed whether the pilot group interpreted the pharmacovigilance terminology and questions uniformly and without ambiguity, ensuring that the tool consistently measured the intended concepts across different respondents. The feedback from this process, along with the validity evaluations, resulted in minor adjustments to the questionnaires, including the redesign of certain statements.

Distribution of the questionnaire

The final revised questionnaire was personally distributed to the selected physicians and nurses during multiple visits to the designated hospitals and outpatient centers. The participants were asked to complete the questionnaire within the designated period [2 weeks]. During data collection, participants with incomplete questionnaires were excluded and replaced by others randomly selected from the same hospitals and outpatient centers to ensure that the required sample size was met with a complete response rate.

Statistical analyses

The data were collected using paper-based questionnaires and were entered into electronic questionnaire forms developed in KoboToolbox (Kobo Organization, Cambridge, MA, USA), an open-source platform designed for reliable field data collection [19]. Knowledge, attitudes, and practices were analysed separately for nurses and doctors. Percentages were calculated to describe qualitative variables, whereas means with standard deviations (SDs) and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used for quantitative variables. A t test was applied to assess the significance of differences between group means, whereas Pearson’s chi-square [χ²] test was employed to evaluate the significance of differences in percentages between groups.

Each response related to knowledge and attitudes was recorded as either incorrect [= 0] or correct [= 1]. The total number of correct responses for each participant was calculated to determine their overall knowledge and attitude score. The scores were categorized using Bloom’s cut-off points [20]. Participants who scored 80–100% were classified as having “good knowledge” and a “positive attitude.” Those scoring 60–79% were categorized as having “moderate knowledge” and a “neutral attitude,” whereas scores below 60% indicated “poor knowledge” and a “poor attitude.”

All the statistical analyses were conducted.

Results

General description of the study participants

The study included 324 [48.4%] nurses and 346 [51.6%] physicians from 16 districts of Georgia. 12% [12%] [n = 79] of the study participants were male, and 88% [n = 591] were female. A larger proportion of participants were from outpatient medical facilities (52.99% [355]) than from hospitals (47.01% [315]). Overall, most medical personnel involved were based in Tbilisi (63.28% [424]), whereas the remaining participants were from regional areas (36.72% [246]). In terms of work experience, 40.42% (268) of the participants had 11 to 20 years of experience, 36.2% (240) had over 21 years of experience, and 23.38% (155) had less than 10 years of experience (Table 1).

Knowledge

98% (98%) of health care professionals believe that ADRs can occur immediately after drug administration. Additionally, 89.83% recognized that ADRs may result from the pharmacological action of a drug, and 84.71% understood that ADRs can be new or unexpected. However, fewer than half of health care professionals believe that ADRs can be delayed (38.42%) or persist for a long time (40.69%).

Comparison of doctors’ and nurses’ knowledge: univariate analysis

Nearly all medical professionals (97.45%) agreed that ADR reporting is necessary if the reaction is life-threatening, but only 26.59% believed that mild ADRs should also be reported. Notably, there was no statistically significant difference between doctors’ and nurses’ responses regarding the need to report life-threatening or mild reactions. For all other responses, the differences were statistically significant (Table 2).

A majority of respondents knew that ADRs should be reported for newly marketed medicines (88.37%) and vaccines (90.57%). Furthermore, 87.06% agreed that ADR reporting is important when the drug is used in children. However, only 47.13% felt the need to report ADRs caused by complementary medicines (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference in the knowledge of doctors and nurses regarding the need to report ADRs related to vaccines or paediatric use. For all other responses, the differences are statistically significant at the p < 0.001 level (Table 2).

Overall, 92.87% of health care professionals are unaware of whether a local PV officer or coordination group exists in their workplace (91.91% of doctors and 93.83% of nurses). Additionally, 94.43% of health care professionals have never seen the ADR reporting form approved by the MOH of Georgia (Table 2).

Quantitative assessment of knowledge

The average knowledge score for doctors was 19 (SD 7), whereas it was 16 (SD 7) for nurses. This difference in knowledge between doctors and nurses was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Similarly, a statistically significant difference was observed between the knowledge of medical staff in the regions (16, SD 7) and that in Tbilisi (18, SD 7).

However, no statistically significant difference was found between the knowledge of staff working in outpatient clinics (18, SD 7) and that of staff working in hospitals (17, SD 7) (p = 0.829). The difference in knowledge based on years of practice experience approached statistical significance (p = 0.056) (Table 3).

Attitude

Nearly half (48.52%) of health care professionals believe that all types of ADRs should be reported, regardless of the type of information available. Additionally, 47.98% thought that reporting was necessary even if they were uncertain that the reaction was caused by a drug. However, 31.69% find it difficult to identify an ADR, and 11.05% refrain from reporting if they feel it does not represent new medical information.

The majority of respondents agreed that additional actions or recommendations are needed to improve ADR reporting. Both doctors and nurses equally believe that reporting ADRs is a professional obligation and should be mandatory for all medical personnel. Furthermore, they do not perceive ADR reporting as something that could negatively affect their professional reputation (Table 5).

Practice

A significant proportion (76.29%) of HCPs (80.35% of doctors and 72.22% of nurses) mentioned encountering ADRs during their practice. However, the percentage of those who actually reported ADRs remains low, at just 12.95% overall. Among them, 14.16% (49) of the doctors and 11.73% (38) of the nurses reported ADRs. Notably, only 10.2% of the doctors used official reporting forms, whereas alternative mechanisms, such as reporting to physicians, research teams, pharmaceutical companies, supervisors, or COVID-19-specific reporting channels, were more commonly utilized. Strikingly, none of the nurses used official reporting forms.

The primary barriers to ADR reporting include (i) lack of information about the national reporting scheme (91.71%); (ii) lack of knowledge about the reporting process (76.93%); (iii) lack of awareness regarding the need to report ADRs (81.58%); (iv) underestimation of the severity or certainty of ADRs, with 40.82% believing the reactions were not serious enough to report; and (v) uncertainty about the causal relationship between the drug and the reaction (8.19%).

Training on ADR reporting is notably absent for most health care professionals, with 96.53% (334) of doctors and 96.30% (312) of nurses reporting that they had not received any training on this topic. Furthermore, only 5.2% (18) of the doctors and 3.4% (11) of the nurses stated that PV was included in their medical or nursing curricula (Table 8).

Discussion

The results highlight the difference in knowledge scores among doctors and nurses, which could be attributed to several factors: differences in educational background, as doctors typically receive more extensive and specialized education in pharmacology, clinical medicine, and drug safety during their medical education. Additionally, physicians are frequently required to participate in continuing medical education activities. Nurses have fewer opportunities or requirements to engage in such specialized training. Similarly, the study in India revealed that doctors had higher baseline knowledge scores compared to nurses and after an educational intervention, both groups showed significant improvement in their knowledge [21].

The results revealed notable disparities in attitudes between nurses and doctors. The finding that a majority of nurses scored in the highest attitude category, whereas over half of the doctors scored in the lowest category, could be explained by several factors: nurses are often more directly involved in monitoring patients’ responses to medications, which may foster a more positive attitude towards PV. They frequently observe ADRs and understand the importance of reporting them to doctors to ensure patient safety, while doctors are more actively involved in decision-making related to drug therapy, such as evaluating potential drug interactions, benefits, and risks, which may lead to different reporting behaviors and priorities. These findings are consistent with previous studies. For example, Rehan et al. reported differences in pharmacovigilance knowledge and attitudes between doctors and nurses in a tertiary care hospital in India [22].

The geographic disparity in knowledge and attitudes was observed, with healthcare professionals in Tbilisi demonstrating significantly higher scores than their regional counterparts. These findings highlight that health care personnel in Tbilisi might benefit from different resources, training, or professional environments that enhance awareness and foster more positive attitudes towards PV Similar geographic disparities in PV knowledge and attitudes have been documented in the study by Shukla S, et al. emphasizing that resource distribution plays a critical role in surveillance efficacy [23].

This study highlights a significant gap in PV practices, revealing a connection between low ADR reporting rates and several factors: a lack of awareness of the need to report ADRs, underestimation of ADR severity, and insufficient knowledge of local ADR reporting processes and regulations. Although a majority of health care professionals (76.29%) have encountered ADRs in their practice, only 12.95% reported these events. Furthermore, the finding that only 10.2% of doctors utilized official reporting forms underscores the lack of integration and standardization in the reporting process within health care settings.

When comparing our results to existing literature, the 12.95% reporting rate is consistent with findings from other developing countries. Studies conducted in India and Ethiopia have also reported low ADR reporting rates among healthcare professionals, primarily due to limited awareness of pharmacovigilance systems and reporting procedures [24, 25]. This finding aligns with broader evidence showing that only a small proportion of ADRs are reported globally due to underreporting and limited pharmacovigilance awareness [26–28].

Multiple studies have shown that a large proportion of HCPs are unaware of national PV centers, with widely varying levels of awareness reported across studies [29, 30]. Our findings similarly indicate insufficient knowledge about local ADR reporting processes and regulations.

This disparity reflects inadequate knowledge of the PV system and highlights gaps in communication and dissemination of information about the national reporting scheme to health care professionals and insufficient integration of PV practices into daily health care operations.

These similarities and differences allow us to consider our results within a global context. The consistent issues of low ADR reporting rates and gaps in knowledge emphasize that these challenges are widespread among developing countries. However, the specific differences—such as the distinct roles of doctors versus nurses and the pronounced urban–rural divide—indicate areas where targeted interventions could have significant impacts. Improving education, standardizing reporting procedures, and ensuring equitable distribution of resources can strengthen PV systems, ultimately enhancing patient safety on a global scale [31].

The findings of this study have several critical implications for national health policy. To address the systemic underreporting and bridge the knowledge gap, policymakers must transition from passive regulations to active, system-wide interventions. First, PV and ADR reporting training should be integrated as mandatory components of the licensing and recertification criteria for all healthcare professionals. Second, the Ministry of Health should prioritize the development of user-friendly, digital reporting platforms (e.g., mobile applications or integrated electronic health records) to eliminate bureaucratic barriers and simplify the reporting process. Finally, establishing decentralized, regional PV coordination centers is essential to address the urban-rural divide and ensure equitable drug safety monitoring across the country.

While this study provides a foundational quantitative assessment of the PV system in Georgia, future research should focus on qualitative methodologies to explore the deeper behavioral and cultural barriers to ADR reporting, particularly in rural and resource-limited settings. Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of specific educational interventions (such as targeted CME programs) on actual reporting rates over time. Finally, expanding research to include the perspectives of community pharmacists and patients will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the national pharmacovigilance ecosystem.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. First, it represents the first nationwide assessment of pharmacovigilance knowledge, attitudes, and practices among healthcare professionals in Georgia. Second, the use of multistage probability proportional to size sampling across multiple regions enhances the representativeness of the findings. Third, the relatively large sample size including both physicians and nurses provides valuable insight into professional differences in pharmacovigilance awareness and reporting behavior.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as this was a cross-sectional study, we cannot conclude a causal relationship between factors associated with high levels of knowledge, positive attitudes, or good practices. Second, regarding selection bias, although we utilized probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling, the heavy concentration of healthcare professionals in the capital (Tbilisi) may affect the overall distribution. Third, there is a potential for information bias; since the data relies on a self-administered questionnaire, the responses may be subject to recall bias or socially desirable responding. Fourth, regarding generalizability, while the study covers multiple regions across the country, the findings might be more reflective of urban or semi-urban settings and may not fully capture the extreme realities of highly remote rural clinics. Finally, to facilitate a streamlined binary assessment (correct vs. incorrect), the “No” and “Don’t know” responses were grouped into a single category during the analysis. While the raw data allows for these responses to be evaluated separately, they were intentionally combined to clearly emphasize the overall deficit in correct pharmacovigilance knowledge. This grouping does not alter the study’s primary conclusions regarding the existing knowledge gaps.

Conclusion

The results revealed low rates of adverse drug reaction reporting among health care professionals, primarily due to a lack of awareness about the national ADR reporting system. This study also highlights that current PV requirements in Georgia remain on paper and are not largely implemented in practice. These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive educational initiatives and system-wide changes to enhance ADR reporting. Addressing the identified barriers, such as providing training, increasing awareness of reporting mechanisms and simplifying the reporting process is essential. Additionally, incorporating PV and ADR reporting training into healthcare professional licensing requirements, as well as integrating PV into medical and nursing curricula, could significantly strengthen the national PV system.
