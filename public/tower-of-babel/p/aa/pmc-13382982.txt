Double-Negative Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder
Authors: Antonio Malvaso, Francesca Bovis, Giacomo Greco, Chiara Morandi, Pietro Businaro, Stefano Masciocchi, Enrico Marchioni, Diego Franciotta, Maria Pia Sormani, Matteo Gastaldi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesNeuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a severe condition usually associated with aquaporin-4 (AQP4) antibodies. A clinical presentation suggestive of NMOSD can also be associated with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibodies (MOGAD). NMOSD can be diagnosed in the absence of autoantibodies (double-negative NMOSD [DN-NMOSD]), but this subgroup has been poorly investigated. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to define the clinical spectrum, prognosis, and treatment response in DN-NMOSD vs AQP4-NMOSD/MOGAD.MethodsWe searched on PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Google Scholar, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases of studies on patients fulfilling inclusion criteria. Patient characteristics, outcome measures, and treatment regimens were extracted.ResultsWe included 41 of 1,027 articles screened and analyzed 671 patients with DN-NMOSD (median age 38.6 years [range IQR: 32.5–42.85]; female-to-male ratio 1.5:1; median follow-up 44.4 months [range 1–600]), 73.6% of which relapsed. In the meta-analysis, mean annualized relapse rate (ARR) was higher, albeit not significantly, in DN-NMOSD (1.08; 95% CI 0.73–1.43) vs AQP4-NMOSD (0.84; 95% CI 0.45–1.23) and MOGAD (0.61; 95% CI 0.39–0.83, p = 0.08). Administration of maintenance immunosuppression in DN-NMOSD led to a significant ARR reduction (pooled rate ratio 0.19, 95% CI 0.07–0.49; p = 0.001), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 90%, p < 0.0001). In meta-regression, no covariates were associated with ARR reduction, including the administration of specific drugs (rituximab, p = 0.288; azathioprine, p = 0.291; mycophenolate, p = 0.918). The pooled mean difference in pre‑ and post‑maintenance treatment Expanded Disability Status Scale values indicated a significant change in disability in MOGAD (−0.93, 95% CI −1.67 to −0.19, p = 0.02) but not in AQP4-NMOSD (−0.62, 95% CI −1.85 to 0.61, p = 0.27) or DN-NMOSD (−0.52 (95% CI −1.30 to 0.25, p = 0.16).DiscussionDN-NMOSD is a heterogenous, severe and highly relapsing disease, where attacks lead to irreversible dysfunction. The administration of maintenance immunotherapy reduces the relapse risk and should be considered early to prevent further disability.

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is an inflammatory autoimmune disorder of the CNS primarily affecting the optic nerves and spinal cord and, more rarely, the brainstem, diencephalon, and cerebrum.1,2 Most patients diagnosed with NMOSD harbor antibodies to aquaporin-4 (AQP4-IgG). In addition, patients with phenotypes closely resembling NMOSD can be positive for antibodies to the myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG-IgG). The 2015 Wingerchuk diagnostic criteria also allow the diagnosis of NMOSD in patients with a highly suggestive clinical phenotype with no AQP4-IgG and where alternative diagnoses have been excluded. It is now recognized that this exclusion should involve also patients with MOG-IgG, which should be considered as a distinct disorder (MOG-IgG‑associated disorder [MOGAD]).2-4

A recent narrative review attempted to describe the characteristics of DN-NMOSD, highlighting that these patients can show a monophasic or relapsing course and a severity comparable with AQP4-NMOSD.2 Hence, maintenance therapy with immunosuppressors is often used to reduce the relapse risk in patients with DN-NMOSD, but this is supported mostly by nonsystematic data, leaving uncertainty on the ideal management strategy. Through a combined systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to define the clinical, paraclinical, and prognostic features of DN-NMOSD and compare them with those of AQP4-NMOSD and MOGAD. In addition, we aimed to define the effect of maintenance immunotherapy on relapse rate and disability in DN-NMOSD.

Methods

Study Selection and Definitions

Our systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocol 2020 guidelines5 (eMethods). A comprehensive literature search was performed by 2 authors (A.M., M.G.) up to March 15, 2025, in PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Google Scholar, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The detailed search strategy is provided in eMethods, and a flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Study Selection Algorithm According to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Guidelines

We included observational, retrospective, cross-sectional, prospective studies—case series and case reports written in English—on patients fulfilling the following criteria: (1) confirmed diagnosis of NMOSD according to 2006 or 2015 criteria1,6 as explicitly reported by the authors of the study in the Methods section; (2) negative for AQP4 and MOG antibodies in serum and/or CSF, both tested with a CBA (fixed [F-CBA], live [L-CBA], or both [eTable 1]); and (3) sufficient/detailed clinical or radiologic information. Within the same studies, patients with AQP4-NMOSD and MOGAD with NMOSD-like phenotype were also considered for comparative analysis with DN-NMOSD. In addition, patients for which outcome measures (either individual or at a cohort level) including annualized relapse rate (ARR) and/or Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) before and after treatment were available were included in the meta-analysis.

Identified studies were uploaded to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) to merge retrieved titles, remove duplicates, and screen titles and abstracts. Two authors (A.M. and M.G.) independently performed title and abstract screening. We excluded studies not pertaining to NMOSD, reviews or editorials, postmortem analysis, in vitro studies, and animal model studies. All studies deemed eligible by at least 1 author proceeded to full-text review. The same 2 authors performed full-text review, blinded to the other's verdict. Discordant eligibility assessments were resolved by consensus through discussion between the same 2 authors in addition to a third author. The included studies were published between September 2012 and September 2024.

Patients were defined as pediatric (age at onset younger than 18 years), early onset (EO; younger than 40 years), and late onset (LO; 40 years or older).7,8 A “relapsing” course was established if patients experienced at least 2 attacks (including the inaugural attack) separated by at least 3 months. Immunotherapy was divided into “acute phase” given during an attack (including oral/intravenous steroids, plasma exchange, and/or intravenous immunoglobulins) and “maintenance” given to prevent further relapses (oral steroids for >3 months, azathioprine [AZA], mycophenolate mofetil [MMF], methotrexate [MTX], rituximab [RTX], cyclophosphamide [CYP], and tocilizumab [TCZ]). Immunosuppressants indicated for MS were defined as MS disease‑modifying therapies (MS-DMTs). A comparison analysis at baseline and on the effect of treatment was also performed, considering the AQP4-NMOSD and MOG-NMOSD cohorts obtained from the same studies reporting DN-NMOSD information. For completeness, an additional synthetic diagram was reported summarizing demographic, clinical, radiologic, and paraclinical features of AQP4-NMOSD and MOGAD cohorts, according to the most recent data from the literature (eFigure 1).

Risk of Bias Assessment

We assessed the quality of nonrandomized, observational, retrospective case series and case report studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool.9 Risk of bias assessment was conducted by 2 authors (A.M. and M.G.) for all studies included in the systematic review and meta-analysis, and bias scores were categorized into low (7–10 total points), moderate (5–6 total points), and high (0–4 total points). Overall, 35 studies (85.4%) had a low risk, 5 studies (12%) had a moderate risk, and 1 study (2.4%) had a high risk of bias (eFigures 2–3 and eTable 2). For studies included in the meta-analyses, where applicable, risk of bias assessment was performed using to the Risk of Bias (RoB) tool by Cochrane Methods.9,10

Statistical Analysis

Information on mean ARR and mean EDSS scores, along with their SD before and after initiation of maintenance immunosuppressive therapy, were extracted for each selected study. For EDSS, we calculated the mean difference (post-treatment minus pretreatment) and the corresponding 95% CIs. For ARR, the ratio of post-treatment to pretreatment mean values (ARR ratio) and corresponding 95% CIs were computed. A random-effects model was used to obtain the pooled estimates of the mean EDSS difference and the mean ARR ratio between pre- and post–immunosuppressant initiation.

When only medians and ranges (minimum–maximum) were reported, these were converted to means and SDs.11 Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using I2 statistic (the proportion of total variability attributable to between-study differences), and the between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method.12 Group differences in pooled estimates (e.g., DN-NMOSD vs AQP4-NMOSD vs MOGAD) were assessed using Q statistic for subgroup interaction, with a random-effects model incorporating Hartung-Knapp adjustment for CIs and p values.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity and assess the effect of clinical and demographic characteristics on treatment-associated changes in ARR and EDSS, we performed univariable and multivariable weighted random-effects meta-regression analyses. Predefined study-level covariates included mean age at onset, female sex, clinical phenotype at onset, and type of maintenance immunotherapy. Meta-regressions were conducted when at least a moderate degree of heterogeneity was present (I2 ≥ 50%).10 Predictors with a p value < 0.10 in univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable meta-regression model, acknowledging the limited number of studies and exploratory nature of these analyses.

To account for differences in follow-up duration, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which we calculated annualized EDSS changes. For each study, the mean EDSS difference was divided by the mean follow-up duration, and these annualized differences were then pooled using random-effects meta-analysis. Small-study and publication biases were assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and by Egger regression test.13 All qualitative and quantitative statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), R version 4.0.3 (R Core Team, 2020), and RevMan version 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, 2020) softwares.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

Our study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024614404). This study did not require ethical approval because we used data published in scientific articles.

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available for consultation in the Zenodo repository at the following link: doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18011464.

DN-NMOSD Clinical Phenotype and Course

Data on the inaugural attack of the neurologic disease were available for 349 patients, and in 236 patients (67.6%), this affected only 1 neurologic district (ON: 127/349, 36.4%, 19 of which were bilateral; TM: 109/349, 31.2%), suggesting that the DN-NMOSD diagnosis was reached only after a subsequent relapse. ON+TM at the inaugural attack was found in 111 of 349 patients (31.8%). The remaining 2 patients (0.6%) showed a combination of TM+brainstem involvement. An adjunctive pooled prevalence analysis of various neurologic presentations at onset is reported in eTable 4.

Information on the involvement of each neurologic district throughout the whole disease course was available for 577 of 671 patients (86%). The 2 most involved districts were the spinal cord (transverse myelitis [TM]; 399/577, 69.2%) and optic nerve (optic neuritis [ON]; 369/577, 68.6%; bilateral ON; 47/369, 13%). Rarer districts were the brainstem (60/577, 10%; 15 with repetitive vomiting suggestive of area postrema syndrome [APS] and 4 with cerebellar syndrome) and encephalic involvement (19/577, 3.3%).

Finally, information that allowed to define the combination of districts affected throughout the whole disease duration was available for 369 patients, and all of them showed the involvement of ON+TM (Figure 2, B and C). Among these, an attack in additional districts was reported in 61 patients including the brainstem (43/369, 11.5%), APS (15/369; 4%), and cerebellum (3/369; 0.8%) (Figure 2, B and C).

The disease course was relapsing in 458 of 622 patients (73.6%) and monophasic in 164 of 622 (26.4%). Median time to relapse, which was reported in 111 of 458 (24.2%), was 15.6 months (range 1–96), and the median number of relapses was 2.46 [110/458 (24%); range 0–18].

DN-NMOSD Neuroradiologic Features

Only 338 of 671 patients (50.4%) from 24 studies had brain and/or spinal MRI data reported in detail by the authors. Spinal cord MRI showed abnormalities in 228 of 338 (67.5%). Cervical tract was involved in 114 of 228 patients (50.0%), thoracic in 89 of 228 (38.8%), and lumbosacral in 40 of 228 (17.5%). One hundred ten of 228 patients (48%) had lesions spanning >2 spinal segments (longitudinal extensive TM [LETM]).

Brain MRI showed abnormalities in 184 of 338 patients (54%), including nonspecific white matter lesions in 159 of 181 (88%) and tumefactive brain lesions in 21 of 181 (11.6%). Optic nerve MRI alterations data were available in 66 of 184 patients (36%), of whom 14 of 66 (21.2%) reported unilateral involvement, 9 of 66 (13.6%) intraorbital segment, 10 of 66 (15.2%) prechiasmatic segment, 6 of 66 (9%) optic chiasm extension, 3 of 66 (4.5%) optic tract involvement, and 2 of 66 (3%) perineuritis. Lesion extension <50% and >50% of the nerve was reported in 4 of 66 (6%) and 7 of 66 (10.6%), respectively. These anatomical and extension-specific details were not uniformly available for all 66 patients, and frequencies reflect only cases with explicit reporting by the authors.

DN-NMOSD Laboratory Features

Overall, 624 of 671 patients were tested using a L-CBA for both AQP4 and MOG (93%). For 2 patients derived from case reports, the type of CBA used was not explicitly described; 29 patients derived from one retrospective study had only F-CBA performed, whereas 16 patients from a small case series and a retrospective study had F-CBA performed for anti-AQP4 antibodies and L-CBA performed for anti-MOG antibodies. All patients included in the meta-analysis (see below) were tested using a L-CBA. Information on longitudinal retesting were not available.

Sufficient information on the results from CSF analysis was reported in 326 of 671 patients (48.6%); 140 of the 326 patients (43%) had CSF pleocytosis (>5 cells/mm3; range 6–436), and 61 of the 326 patients (18.7%) showed increased CSF protein concentration (>45 mg/dL). Unique-to-CSF oligoclonal bands (OCBs) were found in 52 of the 326 patients (16%). MOG antibodies were tested also in the CSF in 122 patients with a L-CBA and resulted negative.

DN-NMOSD Immunotherapies

Sufficient information regarding immunotherapy was available for 293 of 671 patients (44%) collected from 32 studies. Two hundred sixty-five of the 293 patients (90%) received acute-phase immunotherapy with oral/intravenous steroids, and 41 of the 293 (14%) additionally received PLEX and/or IVIg.

As maintenance immunotherapy, patients received AZA (105/293, 36%), RTX (77/293, 26.3%), MMF (41/293, 14%), mitoxantrone (21/293, 7.1%), MTX (11/293, 3.8%), TCZ (11/293, 3.8%), inebilizumab (9/293, 3%), and CYP (8/293, 2.7%). In addition, 8 of the 293 patients (2.7%) MS -DMTs including fingolimod (1/293, 0.3%), natalizumab (1/293, 0.3%), or interferon-beta (6/293 (2%). Mean time from first relapse to immunosuppressant administration was 63.2 months (range 5–93.2), although this information was available only for 62 of the 293 patients (21%).

Meta-Analysis Results

Overall, we included data from 14 of 41 studies (34%) describing 227 of 671 patients (33.8%) treated with maintenance immunotherapy and from 16 of 41 studies (39%) in which at least a mean pretreatment ARR and/or mean pretreatment EDSS was reported. No patients receiving MS-DMTs were included in the meta-analysis.

Pretreatment Mean ARR and Mean EDSS Score Comparison Between DN-NMOSD, AQP4-NMOSD, and MOGAD Cohorts

We conducted a random-effects meta-analysis of pretreatment mean ARR and EDSS values across serostatus cohorts. ARR data were available for 306 patients with DN-NMOSD (16 studies17-19,23-27,29,31-33,37,41,45,47), 158 with AQP4-NMOSD (7 studies17,19,25-27,33), and 134 with MOGAD with an NMOSD-like phenotype (5 studies17,25,26,33,47).

The overall pooled mean ARR across all cohorts was 0.84 (95% CI 0.65–1.03), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 15.4%; p = 0.72). Subgroup estimates were 1.08 (95% CI 0.73–1.43) for DN-NMOSD, 0.84 (95% CI 0.45–1.23) for AQP4-NMOSD, and 0.61 (95% CI 0.39–0.83) for MOGAD (eFigure 5). Although there was a trend toward lower ARR in the MOGAD cohort, overall differences among the 3 groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08).

For EDSS, pretreatment data were available for 368 patients with DN-NMOSD (16 studies17,19,22-25,27,31,37,38,41,44,46,47,e1,e2), 877 with AQP4-NMOSD (13 studies17,19,22,24-27,37,41,44,46,47,e2), and 66 with MOGAD (6 studies19,25-27,44,47). Pooled mean pretreatment EDSS values were 4.56 (95% CI 3.45–5.67) for DN-NMOSD, 4.00 (95% CI 3.27–4.74) for AQP4-NMOSD, and 3.43 (95% CI 2.14–4.72) for MOGAD (eFigure 6). The overall pooled mean EDSS was 4.04 (95% CI 3.49–4.59), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%; p = 1.00). Differences among serostatus cohorts were not statistically significant (p = 0.43).

Effect of Maintenance Immunosuppression on ARR in DN-NMOSD and Comparison With AQP4-NMOSD and MOGAD

We first evaluated changes in ARR before and after the initiation of maintenance immunosuppression in DN-NMOSD (Figure 3A). Among the 6 studies included,17,27,33,37,41,47 the pooled ARR ratio (post-treatment vs pretreatment) was 0.19 (95% CI 0.07–0.49), indicating a statistically significant reduction in relapse rate under treatment (p = 0.001). Heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 90%, p < 0.0001).

To address the high heterogeneity observed, 2 sensitivity analyses were performed. The first included only studies enrolling patients with a relapsing course,17,27,37,41 while the second excluded the single study that included pediatric patients.33 In both analyses, the magnitude and direction of the treatment-associated reduction in ARR, as well as the extent of heterogeneity, remained largely unchanged (eFigures 7–8).

We then explored potential sources of heterogeneity using meta-regression in DN-NMOSD. No significant associations were found between ARR ratio reduction and age at onset (p = 0.151), ON+TM clinical phenotype at onset (p value = 0.152), female sex (p = 0.612), LETM at onset (p = 0.873), TM at onset (p = 0.491) monolateral or bilateral ON at onset (p = 0.305; p = 0.873), RTX treatment (p = 0.288), AZA treatment (p = 0.291), and MMF treatment (p = 0.918).

We next evaluated pre–post ARR changes in patients with AQP4-NMOSD and MOGAD with NMOSD-like phenotype and compared the 3 disease groups (Figure 3B). For AQP4-NMOSD, data from 3 studies were included.17,27,37 The pooled rate ratio comparing post-treatment with pretreatment ARR was 0.33 (95% CI 0.12–0.95, p = 0.04), indicating a statistically significant reduction in relapse rates, albeit with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 96%, p < 0.0001). For MOGAD, only 1 study was included,17 and the ARR ratio was 0.31 (95% CI 0.04–2.25, p = 0.25).

When pooling all 3 subgroups, the overall ARR ratio was 0.24 (95% CI 0.12–0.46, p < 0.0001), confirming a significant reduction in ARR after initiation of maintenance immunosuppression. Although point estimates varied among subgroups differed numerically between DN-NMOSD, AQP4-NMOSD, and MOGAD, the formal test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.72).

Effect of Immunosuppressive Therapy on EDSS in DN-NMOSD and Comparison With AQP4-NMOSD and MOGAD

We then assessed changes in disability, as measured by EDSS, before and after the start of maintenance immunosuppressive treatment in the 3 cohorts (Figure 4, A and B). In DN-NMOSD, 12 studies17,22-25,27,31,37,38,46,47,e1 contributed data. The pooled mean difference in EDSS score was −0.52 (95% CI −1.30 to 0.25), indicating no statistically significant change in disability after treatment (p = 0.16).

In the AQP4-NMOSD group (8 studies17,22,24,25,27,37,46,47), the pooled mean EDSS difference was −0.62 (95% CI −1.85 to 0.61, p = 0.27), again showing no significant change. By contrast, the MOGAD cohort (7 studies17,22,24,25,27,37,47) showed a significant improvement in disability, with a pooled mean EDSS difference of −0.93 (95% CI −1.67 to −0.19, p = 0.02).

Across all the 3 subgroups, the overall pooled EDSS mean difference was −0.68 (95% CI −1.17 to −0.20; p = 0.008), indicating a modest but significant reduction in disability after treatment when all diseases were combined. Heterogeneity was high in the DN-NMOSD (I2 = 72%) and AQP4-NMOSD (I2 = 91%) subgroups and lower in MOGAD (I2 = 39%). No significant interaction was found between serostatus subgroup and EDSS change (p = 0.66).

To account for variability in follow-up duration (ranging from 19 months to 9 years), a sensitivity analysis was performed using annualized EDSS changes (eFigure 9). In the DN-NMOSD group (8 studies17,22,24,25,27,46,47,e1), the pooled annualized mean EDSS difference was −0.35 (95% CI −0.81 to 0.10; p = 0.10), again suggesting no significant change in disability under treatment. In AQP4-NMOSD (6 studies17,22,25,27,46,47), the annualized mean difference was −0.29 (95% CI −0.59 to 0.01; p = 0.06), and in MOGAD (5 studies17,22,25,27,47), it was −0.26 (95% CI −0.76 to 0.24; p = 0.31), neither reaching statistical significance. However, when pooling all 3 subgroups, the overall annualized mean EDSS difference remained statistically significant (−0.32; 95% CI −0.53 to −0.12; p < 0.01), indicating a small average improvement in disability per year of follow-up across diseases. Heterogeneity was low to moderate across the subgroups (I2 = 41.8% for DN-NMOSD, 0% for AQP4-NMOSD and 0% for MOGAD). A meta-regression restricted to the 12 DN-NMOSD studies contributing EDSS data did not identify any covariates significantly associated with EDSS change.

Publication Bias Analysis

Visual inspection of funnel plots suggested at most mild small-study effects in the meta-analyses or ARR ratio and EDSS change (eFigures 10-14), and statististically significant asymmetry in the DN-NMOSD cohort for ARR ratio (z = 0.14; p = 0.89) or EDSS difference (z = −0.84; p = 0.40) (eFigures 10–14). Given the relatively small number of contributing studies in some analyses, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and such findings do not rule out the possibility of residual publication bias. Sensitivity analysis excluding studies with a small number of patients was performed (eFigures 15–16). Furthermore, potential patient overlap across studies was carefully assessed, and overlapping cohorts were excluded to minimize bias and avoid duplication in the pooled analyses.

Discussion

In this study, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis focused on patients with DN-NMOSD and found that, in this subgroup, the administration of maintenance immunotherapies significantly reduces the ARR, similar to what was observed in AQP4-NMOSD and MOGAD.Therapeutic management of DN-NMOSD is challenging because no dedicated randomized clinical trials have been performed so far.2 Although inebilizumab has demonstrated efficacy in the N-MOmentum trial, the study was not powered to assess outcomes in AQP4 participants, and very few double-negative patients were randomized to placebo, limiting between-group comparisons. As such, results for this subgroup are presented descriptively and should be interpreted as hypothesis generating only.41

Current guidelines suggest that, becase attacks in DN-NMOSD can be severe and with incomplete recovery, patients should be treated with immunosuppressive drugs such as rituximab, but this is largely based on expert opinion.e5 Our data strengthen this indication, even though the heterogeneity between studies remains high. In our study, we thoroughly excluded all the patients who were not tested for MOG and AQP4 antibodies with a CBA, thus granting a more homogenous representation of the DN-NMOSD group.1 It is important to note that because fixed CBA has a lower accuracy for MOG antibodies, and, to a lesser extent, for AQP4 antibodies, all patients included in the meta-analysis had a L-CBA performed for both AQP4 and MOG antibodies, whereas only 0.3% in the systematic review underwent an unspecified CBA, 4.3% only F-CBA, and 2.4% F-CBA for anti-AQP4 antibodies and L-CBA for anti-MOG antibodies. This should have limited the risk of misclassification of patients with DN-NMOSD in our study. We did not find a significantly relevant effect of a specific treatment compared with another, but the data heterogeneity prevented us from performing a proper comparison. Therefore, at present, we cannot provide further indication on which treatment should be preferred in DN-NMOSD.

EDSS was not modified by immunosuppressive treatment in DN-NMOSD and AQP4-NMOSD, while patients with MOGAD showed a clinical improvement. This is in line with previous data and is likely to reflect different pathogenic mechanisms.24,25,49,e1 Lesions in AQP4 NMOSD can lead to irreversible neuronal damage, while in MOGAD, patients can show substantial recovery and, in many cases, radiologic disappearance of the lesions.2-4 Our data suggest that, similar to AQP4-NMOSD, patients with DN-NMOSD accumulate irreversible damage upon attacks. Further studies should explore the likely heterogenous pathogenic mechanisms at play in DN-NMOSD. The limited improvement in EDSS observed in patients with DN-NMOSD may be partly explained by the prolonged mean delay of over 5 years between the first relapse and the start of maintenance therapy, which could contribute to irreversible disability accumulation.

It is still debated whether DN-NMOSD should be considered as a disease (an entity with a unique and identifiable cause) or as a syndrome (i.e., a collection of symptoms without a known identified cause). The latter hypothesis is supported by biomarker studies that show heterogeneity compared with AQP4-NMOSD. For example, most patients with DN-NMOSD show low levels of serum and/or CSF glial fibrillary acidic protein, which, on the contrary, are usually high in APQ4-NMOSD.2,e6-10

In our study, we systematically described the clinical, radiologic, and laboratory features of patients with DN-NMOSD considering them as a unique group. However, the high heterogeneity detected in the response to treatments could favor the “syndrome” hypothesis vs the “disease” hypothesis. Future studies, and the upcoming revision of the Wingerchuk criteria, will need to address this issue.2,e5

The timing of maintenance immunotherapy administration in DN-NMOSD is still debated. Some authors suggested that, given a lower relapse risk compared with AQP4-NMOSD, initiation of maintenance immunotherapy should only be considered in patients with relapsing disease or experiencing a severe first attack.e11 Conversely, recent studies performed on patients included in the MSbase registry, highlighted that the overall relapse rate was similar in both AQP4-NMOSD and DN-NMOSD.e12,e13 In line with this, our study highlighted that patients with DN-NMOSD had very frequent relapses (in 73.5% of cases), and in the meta-analysis, this group showed the highest mean pretreatment ARR. Notwithstanding the heterogeneity of DN-NMOSD, our data suggest that, in the absence of precise biomarkers, early maintenance immunotherapy even after the first attack should be considered to prevent further disability accumulation.

Our study has several important limitations to mention. Data came from retrospective, observational cohorts and/or case series/reports. Standardized classifications, definitions, and diagnostic criteria for neurologic manifestations were not uniformly used or reported, and therefore not suitable for pooling results. Patients were included if they fulfilled the 2015 Wingerchuk criteria. However, because detailed information for individual patients was available for revision only in approximately half the cohort (51.4% for neuroradiologic data and 49.6% for CSF analysis), we cannot exclude a misapplication of the criteria, especially concerning the exclusion of alternative causes such as MS. Because of the vast majority of aggregated demographics and prevalence data, our pooled estimation may not represent entirely the real-world cohort of DN-NMOSD. Accordingly, the high heterogeneity observed across studies may reflect differences in study design, patient populations, and follow-up duration. In addition, the pre–post design without parallel control groups is susceptible to regression to the mean and time-dependent bias, which should be considered when interpreting treatment effects.

Only a minority of patients in our systematic review performed CSF MOG-IgG testing (18.2%). Recent studies highlighted that some patients with a DN-NMOSD phenotype might actually harbor CSF-only MOG antibodies, and current criteria allow the diagnosis of MOGAD in the presence of CSF antibodies and supporting features.e14 Even though this finding is relatively rare,e15-16 we cannot exclude a risk of misclassification of some of the patients included.

Finally, the meta-regression analysis included a limited number of studies, which may reduce the statistical power and reliability of the observed associations. The results obtained indicate that no covariates can significantly predict the treatment effect on mean ARR ratio and EDSS score difference, suggesting that further research with more homogenous study populations may be needed to explore potential covariates.

DN-NMOSD is a heterogenous condition with a high risk of relapse. The attacks mostly lead to the accumulation of irreversible disability. The administration of maintenance immunosuppression is effective in reducing the relapse risk and should be considered early. Future studies should investigate and compare the efficacy of each immunosuppressive drug to help define the optimal treatment strategy. The identification of biomarkers and/or new autoantibody reactivities able to define disease subgroups within DN-NMOSD would also help to develop treatment strategies targeting specific pathogenic mechanisms.
