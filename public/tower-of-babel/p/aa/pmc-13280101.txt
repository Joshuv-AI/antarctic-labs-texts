Incidence of Skin Infections in Patients with Atopic Dermatitis Treated with Topical Medications: A Cohort Study Using Japanese Claims Data
Authors: Akio Tanaka, Rikiya Toda, Takahiro Tsuchiya, Yasuhito Konishi, Miyuki Matsukawa, Takeo Nakayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSkin infections are a common concern in the treatment of atopic dermatitis (AD) with topical medications including topical corticosteroids (TCS), tacrolimus, delgocitinib, and difamilast. However, real-world evidence regarding the incidence of these infections across different topical medications remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate patient characteristics and the incidence rates of skin infections associated with topical medications using Japanese claims data.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using health insurance claims data from June 2022 to June 2023. Patients with AD were classified into three treatment groups: difamilast, TCS, and non-TCS (tacrolimus and delgocitinib). Incidence rates of skin infections were calculated using the person-year method (1000 person-years). Propensity score matching was used to compare treatment groups.ResultsAmong 107,854 eligible patients, 1,844,45,988, and 8256 received difamilast, TCS, and non-TCS monotherapy, respectively. Before matching, the proportions of female patients were 59.8%, 51.7%, and 66.8% in the difamilast, TCS, and non-TCS groups, and the median ages were 19.0, 13.0, and 38.0 years, respectively. After matching, the incidence rate of skin infections was 362.880/1000 person-years in the difamilast group, compared with 306.336/1000 person-years in the TCS group (risk ratio: 1.185; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.934, 1.503). Compared with the non-TCS group, the risk ratio was 1.302 (95% CI 1.005, 1.686). No major differences were observed in the incidence of individual skin infections, except for acne. Among patients who received difamilast monotherapy, the incidence rate of skin infections (340.341/1000 person-years; 95% CI 285.209, 406.129) was consistent with the overall patients prescribed difamilast (349.819/1000 person-years; 95% CI 326.257, 375.084).ConclusionsDifamilast demonstrated a comparable safety profile to other topical treatments regarding skin infections in patients with AD. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring infection risk and ensuring appropriate clinical management when prescribing topical medications for AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01770-6.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Topical medications, Skin infections

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Skin infections often pose a problem in the treatment of atopic dermatitis with topical medications, but studies using real-world data for these infections are scarce.We used real-world data to assess patient characteristics, topical treatment patterns, and infection incidence in Japanese atopic dermatitis patients, with a particular focus on difamilast.What was learned from the study?This study revealed that the overall incidence rate of skin infections associated with difamilast was comparable to that of other topical treatments for atopic dermatitis, with no major differences in individual skin infections.It is important to closely monitor these infections and ensure appropriate clinical management.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic skin condition characterized by relapsing eczema with pruritus as a primary lesion [1]. Topical medications—including topical corticosteroids (TCS), tacrolimus ointment, delgocitinib ointment, and difamilast ointment—play a crucial role in managing AD by exerting antiinflammatory effects through modulation of skin immune cells (for brevity, the term “ointment” is omitted throughout this manuscript when referring to tacrolimus, delgocitinib, and difamilast). These topical medications are listed in the Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Atopic Dermatitis 2024 [1] as first-line treatments to be applied following diagnosis. However, there have been no reports on the actual patient backgrounds or real-world usage patterns for each medication.

Further, skin infections are often a problem in the treatment of AD with topical medications [2–4]. This risk is attributed to impaired skin barrier function, changes in the microbiome, and abnormal immune responses in patients with AD [5], as well as the effects of medications on immune cells [4]. Skin infections are listed as a common adverse effect of TCS, tacrolimus, and delgocitinib in the Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Atopic Dermatitis 2024 [1]. Furthermore, a systematic review paper reporting the safety of these topical medications for AD in children also cited skin infections as frequently reported adverse events [6]. Difamilast is a treatment for AD that exerts antiinflammatory effects by selectively inhibiting phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) within the PDE family [7], but there have been no reports evaluating skin infections in real-world clinical settings.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate characteristics of patients with AD treated with difamilast, TCS (classified by potency), and other non-TCS (tacrolimus and delgocitinib), then subsequently conduct an exploratory evaluation of the incidence rates of skin infections of each treatment group. The secondary objective was to calculate the incidence rates of the skin infections in patients treated with difamilast monotherapy and in overall patients treated with difamilast, including those prescribed difamilast in combination with other topical treatments, phototherapy, or systemic therapies for AD.

Methods

Study Design and Study Population

This was a retrospective cohort study. The index date was defined as the date the target drug was first prescribed within the observation period. The look-back period was defined as the 180 days preceding the index date, and the follow-up period was more than 90 days after the index date.

Patients were included in the study if they met all of the following inclusion criteria during the data collection period from 1 June 2022 to 30 June 2023: (1) patients diagnosed with AD (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] code: L20) and initiated treatment with one of the target drugs—difamilast, TCS (classified by potency), or non-TCS such as tacrolimus and delgocitinib (see code list [Supplementary Table S8])—within the same month, with no prescription of the same drug in the preceding 180 days, (2) patients aged under 75 years, and (3) patients who were continuously observable for at least 180 days prior to the index date.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who experienced any skin infections within 180 days prior to the start of drug administration, (2) patients diagnosed with diabetes (ICD-10 code: E10–E14) within 180 days prior to the start of drug administration, (3) patients used immunosuppressive agents (oral or injectable) within 180 days prior to administration, and (4) patients received AD treatments other than topical medications (e.g., cyclosporine, oral steroids, phototherapy, antibody drugs) at least once within 6 months prior to the index date.

Patients who met all inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria were included in the study. For the secondary objective, the fourth inclusion criterion and fourth exclusion criterion were not applied.

Patients were classified into three treatment groups according to the study purpose: difamilast, TCS, and non-TCS (tacrolimus and delgocitinib). While difamilast is technically a non-TCS medication, it was analyzed independently in this study to distinguish its effects from those of other non-TCS medications.

Data Source

Data sources for this study were the corporate health insurance association, national health insurance data provided by DeSC Healthcare, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), and health insurance claims data for health insurance association members provided by IQVIA (Tokyo, Japan). These data have been matched.

The DeSC database comprises health insurance claims and specific health checkup data provided by several health insurance societies, national health insurance programs, and the medical care system for the elderly (wide area union for the medical care system for the elderly) [8]. It covers all age groups, from pediatric to late-stage elderly populations, thereby ensuring a certain degree of representativeness of the general population. As of January 2022, the database included approximately 4.4 million individuals [8].

This study required tracking safety information that emerged after target drug administration. The database consisted of insurance data, which were highly traceable and allowed for continuous information gathering even after patients transferred to a different medical institution. Therefore, this database was considered appropriate for collecting information on skin infections following drug administration across multiple medical institutions.

Outcome Measures

The outcomes included the overall incidence rate, as well as individual incidence rates of skin infections—specifically folliculitis, acne, impetigo, herpes simplex, Kaposi’s varicelliform eruption, tinea, warts, candidiasis, cellulitis, erysipelas, molluscum contagiosum, furuncle, furunculosis, and carbuncle (see code list [Supplementary Table S9]). Incidence rates were calculated using the person-year method (1000 person-years), in which the number of skin infections that occurred during the evaluation period for the target drug was divided by the total person-years of observation, multiplied by 1000. The evaluation period for each case was defined from the initiation of the target drug to either 90 days after the last prescription date or the end of the data usage period, whichever was earlier. For each patient, only the first occurrence of a skin infection during the observation period was counted, and recurrent episodes were not included in the analysis. A minimum infection‑free interval of 180 days prior to the index date was required for an event to be considered a new infection.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean, standard deviation, and/or median (first quartile [Q1]–third quartile [Q3]), minimum, and maximum. Categorical variables were summarized as number and percentage. The confidence coefficient for the interval estimate was 95%. No imputation was made for missing data.

The primary analysis was limited to patients who received monotherapy with difamilast, TCS (classified by potency), tacrolimus, or delgocitinib—that is, patients who were not treated with two or more topical medications in combination. The analysis involved 1:1 nearest neighbor propensity score matching with a caliper of 0.2 and without replacement. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model, in which the treatment groups (difamilast, TCS, and non-TCS) were used as the dependent variable, and sex [9–11], age [9–13], topical medications for AD used immediately prior to the index date [14], history of allergic rhinitis [12], and history of bronchial asthma [12] were included as independent variables. Standardized differences were calculated as an indicator to evaluate the balance of patient characteristics before and after matching.

Initial matching ratios were set at 1:10 (difamilast versus TCS) and 1:3 (difamilast versus non-TCS); however, due to standardized differences of 0.2 or greater, 1:1 matching was ultimately adopted for the final analysis. In the matched cohort, the incidence rates of skin infections based on person-years and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each treatment group. Additionally, the risk ratio and its 95% CI for the difamilast group relative to the TCS group and non-TCS group were calculated.

As a secondary objective, the incidence rate of skin infections based on person-years and its 95% CI for difamilast monotherapy and difamilast combination therapy with other topical treatments, nontopical AD treatments (e.g., cyclosporine, oral steroids, antibody drugs), or phototherapy was calculated. Additionally, analyses such as those for the primary analysis were also conducted for each TCS subgroup classified by potency, and for the non-TCS medications tacrolimus and delgocitinib. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS® ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical Approval

This study was approved on 17 July 2024 by the Research Ethics Committee of Research and Development Division, Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (approval no.: 240702). As we used secondary anonymized data provided by the DeSC Healthcare and IQVIA, no individual-level informed consent was obtained. The questionnaire survey was conducted by the DeSC Healthcare in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan and Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Study Population

The patient flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. During the data collection period from 1 June 2022 to 30 June 2023, a total of 751,558 patients with AD were identified. Of these, 175,063 patients met all of the following inclusion criteria: (1) patients diagnosed with AD who later started topical antiinflammatory medications, (2) patients aged under 75 years, and (3) patients with an observable period of 180 days prior to the index date. We excluded 59,724 patients who met any of the following exclusion criteria: (1) patients who experienced any skin infection within 180 days before the index date, (2) patients who had a diagnosis of diabetes within 180 days before the index date, or (3) patients who used immunosuppressants within 180 days before the index date. After these exclusions, 115,339 patients remained.

Patient flowchart. AD atopic dermatitis, ICD-10 International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, TCS topical corticosteroids

These patients were used for the secondary objective of investigating the actual incidence rate of skin infections associated with difamilast. After further exclusion of 7485 patients due to receiving AD treatments other than topical medications, 107,854 patients were included in the analysis for the primary objective. Among these patients, 1844, 45,988, and 8256 received difamilast, TCS, and non-TCS monotherapy, respectively. The TCS group stratified by potency consisted of 970, 13,421, 14,817, 14,536, and 2244 patients in the weak, medium, strong, very strong, and strongest potency subgroups, respectively. In the non-TCS group, 4308 patients received tacrolimus monotherapy and 3948 received delgocitinib monotherapy (Fig. 1).

The absolute value of the standardized difference for each patient characteristic after matching was less than 0.1, indicating that the patient characteristics were well balanced between the treatment groups.

Discussion

This study is a new retrospective cohort study of Japanese patients with AD, examining patient background and incidence of skin infections according to the use of different topical antiinflammatory medications. Our findings revealed that the incidence rate of skin infections associated with difamilast was comparable to that of other topical treatments. In the matched cohort, the risk ratios for the overall incidence rate of skin infections for the difamilast group compared with the TCS and non-TCS group were 1.185 (95% CI 0.934, 1.503) and 1.302 (95% CI 1.005, 1.686), respectively. There were no major differences in individual skin infections. Additionally, the incidence rates of skin infections (1000 person-years) were similar between patients treated exclusively with difamilast (340.341) and overall patients treated with difamilast (349.819).

Prescription patterns revealed that difamilast, tacrolimus, and delgocitinib were prescribed to a higher proportion of female patients compared with TCS medications. This may suggest female patients are more frequently prescribed non-TCS medications, including difamilast. In terms of patient age, the median patient age was lowest in the difamilast group (19.0) and highest in the delgocitinib group (41.0) among the treatment groups. In addition, the proportion of pediatric patients (≤ 11 years) was highest in the difamilast group (40.5%), followed by the tacrolimus group (28.1%) and the delgocitinib group (17.2%). This suggests that difamilast tends to be more frequently prescribed in pediatric patients. All drugs were more likely to be prescribed in medical institutions with fewer than 20 beds, suggesting that AD is commonly managed in such settings.

Regarding overall skin infections, no major difference was observed in the risk ratio for skin infections between the difamilast group and the TCS group when comparing incidence rates. Similarly, no major differences were observed for specific skin infections with sufficient evaluable cases, except for acne. After matching, 1322 of the 1836 patients (approximately 70%) in the TCS group received a TCS of “strong” potency or higher, indicating that the majority of patients in this group were treated with a strong-potency TCS. Although the specific application sites were not identifiable in this study, it is plausible that patients using strong or higher potency TCS applied the medications to lesions on the extremities or trunk, rather than the face, in accordance with clinical guidelines [1]. In comparison, non-TCS medications and difamilast may have been applied to the face, which could have influenced the observed incidence rates of acne in these treatment groups. As presented in Table S2, among patients using medium-potency TCS—typically applied to the face—there was no major difference in acne incidence between the difamilast group and the medium-potency TCS subgroup. These findings indicate that the site of application may contribute to the patterns observed in acne incidence.

In the comparison with the non-TCS group, the difamilast group showed no major difference in the risk ratio for overall skin infections among evaluable cases. Additionally, no major differences were observed in the incidence of specific skin infections that occurred in the evaluable cases, except for impetigo. The incidence rates in the difamilast group were 7.952% and 7.536% for overall skin infections and 3.649% and 3.594% for acne when matched with the TCS and non-TCS groups, respectively. These incidence rates of acne were comparable to those reported in the phase III long-term study of difamilast (8/166 [4.8%] in adults and 9/200 [4.5%] in children) [15]. The incidence rates of impetigo in the difamilast group matched with the TCS and non-TCS groups were approximately 1.3% or lower. In children aged 12 years or younger, the incidence rate of impetigo in the difamilast group matched with the medium-potency TCS subgroup was approximately 2.3%. These rates were comparable to the incidence rate of impetigo in adults reported in the long-term study (1.2%), but appeared to be lower than the rate observed in children (15.5%) [15]. Additionally, the incidence rates of folliculitis and contagious molluscum in this study were lower than those reported in the long-term study.

In previous systematic reviews of pediatric AD [6], the incidence rate of skin infections associated with TCS ranged from 0% to 3%, except for certain low-potency TCS. This is approximately half the rate observed in patients ≤ 12 years old in the TCS medium subgroup in our study. Fukuie et al. reported a 28% incidence rate of viral infections with mid-potency TCS, which may be due to the longer observation period and small sample size [6, 16]. Factors contributing to this include the use of disease names from medical claims and the fact that this study only included cases of TCS monotherapy.

The incidence rate of skin infections with tacrolimus is 0–13% in a previous systematic review [6], and the incidence rate of skin infections in the non-TCS group in this study was within this range, suggesting our findings align with previous reports. Compared with the results of long-term trials of delgocitinib in adults and children [17, 18], the incidence rate of acne in this study was slightly lower than 1%, appearing similar to previously reported rates. However, the incidence rate of other skin infections in this study appeared to be lower than those reported in previous studies, which could be due to this study including only monotherapy.

In both the TCS and non-TCS groups, the number of cases of Kaposi varicelliform eruption, candidiasis, cellulitis, erysipelas, furuncles, furunculosis, and carbuncle were very small, and it was considered difficult to evaluate and examine them in this study. Given the very small number of herpes simplex and eczema herpeticum cases observed in this study, the statistical power to detect differences for these outcomes was insufficient.

Overall, this study evaluated the incidence rates of skin infections in patients with AD treated with topical antiinflammatory medications. No major differences were observed among the medications. These findings suggest that to prevent skin infections associated with AD it is important to not only select appropriate topical medications, but also manage the underlying condition and improve the skin barrier to maintain healthy skin [19]. Furthermore, guidance from medical professionals is essential to ensure the proper use of topical medications.

Difamilast is generally prescribed for patients with mild-to-moderate AD; therefore, it is likely that many of the patients included in the study had mild-to-moderate disease. However, as TCS, the comparator, are used across a wide range of severities, and because claims databases lack indicators that verify disease severity, the precise severity of the patients remains unknown.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were derived from a claims database provided by DeSC Healthcare and IQVIA, which includes records from health insurance societies and National Health Insurance. Most of the data originated from health insurance societies primarily cover current employees of large corporations and their dependents. As such, the study population is likely limited to individuals employed in specific industries or occupations, and may not be representative of the general Japanese population. Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to the broader population. Second, although prescription data were available in the database, detailed information such as actual duration of use, application sites, and amounts of topical medication applied was not captured. Additionally, disease names recorded in the claims data are primarily for insurance billing purposes and may not always align with clinical diagnoses documented in physicians’ medical records. Therefore, a certain degree of diagnostic misclassification cannot be excluded. In this insured population, most infections that lead to medical consultation and claims for medical expenses are expected to be captured; however, skin infections that do not result in medical visits or prescriptions are not recorded, and our estimates may underestimate the true incidence of all skin infections. Third, while we adjusted for confounding factors using variables available in the database, the possibility of residual confounding due to unmeasured variables cannot be ruled out. Consequently, not all potential confounders could be fully controlled for in this analysis. Fourth, the database did not contain detailed clinical information such as disease duration or lesion distribution, which may influence the risk of developing skin infections. As a result, residual confounding due to these unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

This recent retrospective cohort study analyzed real-world use of topical medications for AD and the incidence of skin infections in Japan. Patients with AD are prone to skin infections due to impaired skin barrier function, and it has been suggested that use of topical antiinflammatory medications increases the risk of skin infections. It is important to closely monitor each case of skin infection and ensure appropriate clinical management.
