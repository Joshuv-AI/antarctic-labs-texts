Machine learning in AIRR diagnostics: Advances and applications
Authors: Aslı Semerci, Celine AlBalaa, Brian Corrie, Dylan Duchen, Gisela Gabernet, Jinwoo Leem, Enkelejda Miho, Ulrik Stervbo, Justin Barton, Pieter Meysman, AIRR-Community
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recent advancements in sequencing technologies have led to an exponential increase in adaptive immune receptor repertoire (AIRR) data. These receptors, crucial to the adaptive immune system, are believed to have strong potential for diagnostic applications. The immune repertoires represent a wealth of data, creating a growing demand for robust computational methods to analyze and interpret this vast amount of information.In this review, we examine the application of machine learning algorithms for the classification and analysis of AIRR-seq data for different diagnostic applications. We provide a high-level division of current approaches based on their focus on repertoire-level or sequence-level features. We provide an overview of the current state of public AIRR data sets available for model training. Finally, we briefly highlight what lessons can be learned from successful AIRR diagnostic approaches and what hurdles still must be overcome.

Introduction

The immune system is composed of diverse components that provide both specific and nonspecific defense against foreign invaders of the body. Among these, the adaptive immune system represents the most specialized arm, distinguished by its remarkable versatility and capacity to mount highly specific responses. The key feature within the adaptive immune system is its use of variable adaptive immune receptors (AIRs) to recognize a wide diversity of pathogens, intruders, and aberrant cells to generate specific responses. For T-cells, these are the T-cell receptors (TCRs), which primarily bind small linear peptides presented by the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC). For B-cells, these are the B-cell receptors (BCRs) and the antibodies (Abs), which are able to bind a broad spectrum of antigen types (proteins, peptides, carbohydrates, and lipids). An alpha and a beta–or a gamma and a delta–chain constitute the TCR, while two identical heavy and light chains constitute the BCR. The vast diversity of these receptors arises from the recombination variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) during lymphocyte development, a process in which variable, diversity, and joining gene segments are randomly assembled, with additional nucleotide insertions and deletions at their junctions, producing unique receptors with distinct antigen specificities. Flexible loops with high sequence variation in the TCR and BCR structure form the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs 1–-3) in both receptor chains which contribute to binding the target antigen. The CDR3, located at the junction of the V(D)J genes, shows the with the highest variation and is often important for binding. By generating a high diversity of AIRs, the immune system maximizes the chances of obtaining a matching binding receptor for any intruder it may encounter.

An individual’s entire collection of BCRs and TCRs are denoted as their adaptive immune receptor repertoire (AIRR), which is dynamic and changes in response to an immunological challenge such as an infection. During an infection, lymphocytes with a receptor specific to antigenic determinants from the pathogen are activated and proliferate as part of the immune response. In this manner, activated lymphocytes take on a more significant part of the repertoire than their non-activated counterparts. Activated cells transition into a memory phenotype, some long-lived, and respond faster upon re-encounter with the pathogen. Once the pathogen is cleared, the developed response is stored in the small group of memory cells to generate a fast reaction for possible future encounters. Consequently, the immune receptor repertoire contains information on an individual’s history, current immune state, and future response to unencountered pathogen [1]. However, despite these promising results, AIRR-seq-based diagnostics approaches have not yet become routine in the clinic. Traditional approaches, such as serological testing or direct pathogen detection, are currently faster, cheaper, and more standardized, while AIRR-seq requires specialized sequencing, extensive bioinformatic pipelines, and regulatory validation [2]. Overcoming these barriers will be key for the future translation of AIRR-based methods into routine diagnostics, especially for hard-to-diagnose diseases and early diagnosis. Therefore, if we decode the information contained within AIRRs, this information will present an enormous diagnostic potential in the clinic.

An individual’s AIRR is accessible through targeted amplification techniques, which make use of relatively constant regions within the AIRs to sequence the variable CDR3 along with enough information to identify the receptor sequence’s V and J segments [3], as extensively described by Mhanna et al [4]. Sequencing a large number of lymphocytes within a sample approximates the AIR distribution within the whole repertoire, which has been shown to be sufficient for several diagnostic applications. Data quality substantially influences the outcomes of these analyses; thus, it is imperative to consider this factor. However, the significant potential diversity of AIRs means that they are mostly unique for the individual in which they were generated, and the overlapping clonotypes between two individuals are few, even between monozygotic twins [5]. This necessitates dedicated statistical techniques and, in recent years, dedicated machine learning (ML) approaches to translate an individual’s unique and complex AIRR data into an interpretable signal for a diagnostic research question, as described by Pertseva [6].

In this review, we focus on the application of statistical and ML techniques to categorize patients, tissues, and samples using complete AIR repertoire data. Prediction of functionality at the level of individual cells or individual receptors has been previously covered by comprehensive reviews [7,8]. Our goal is instead to describe the state-of-the-art methods for making predictions at the repertoire level, and delineate the existing methodologies into clear categories rather than providing an exhaustive overview. Furthermore, we detail what can be learned from the approaches that have been successful so far and the outstanding challenges that remain.

Applications of immune repertoire analysis

The nature of the adaptive immune response in human diseases has presented a vast space of potential diagnostic applications, which have been explored with AIRR data. These opportunities are not limited to developing diagnostic tools but also with the aim of guiding the development of new therapy strategies with biological agents. Therefore many AIRR classification applications exist that can be broadly categorized as immune repertoire analysis.

Emerson and colleagues built a classifier to distinguish Cytomegalovirus (CMV) seropositive individuals from negatives based on the immune response to the virus detected from bulk TCR repertoire data [9]. This study is one of the pioneering examples of utilizing patterns in AIRR-seq data to identify exposure to infectious pathogens (e.g., CMV). This study’s investigation was based on the analysis of CMV seropositive patients’ evaluation because of the virus’s high prevalence, substantial clonal expansion, and well-characterized epitopes. These features make CMV an ideal proof-of-concept, though they are not representative for other infections [10,11]. Similarly, the COVID-19 pandemic saw the development of an AIRR-seq-based test for past SARS-CoV-2 infection, which was granted Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) by the FDA [12].

Beyond their role in developing diagnostic tools for pathogen-related disorders, AIRR-based analyses are increasingly being applied to predict therapy responses and to elucidate the mechanisms of treatments that interact with the adaptive immune system. Investigating the treatment’s systemic effects is often the first step for improving its efficiency (Fig. 1). With the increasing application of immunotherapies, AIRR-based diagnostics to predict or monitor their responses are becoming more common [13]. Because the composition of T and B cells has a critical impact on the state of adaptive immunity, these lymphocytes are central to the effectiveness of any intervention aimed at stimulating an immune response. Research has indeed shown that the read-outs from AIRR data can be used to distinguish responder subtypes towards various therapies [14–16]. Clonotypes within an individual can be tracked over time and enable monitoring of changes in the immune repertoire in response to immunotherapy [17]. It has been shown that changes in the immune repertoire patterns, such as the diversity of induced receptors or the expansion of therapy-specific receptors, correlate with the quality of the response [18–21].

In addition to the development of diagnostic tools for pathogen-related disorders, AIRR-based approaches have also been applied to predict therapy responses and to understand the mechanisms of treatments that modulate the adaptive immune system. Over the last decade, monoclonal antibodies and other antibody-based biologics have become key therapeutic options in fields such as oncology, autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, frequently demonstrating superior target specificity and clinical efficacy compared to conventional small-molecule drugs [20,22]. However, reprogramming the immune system with an external immune agent can cause unexpected changes; therefore, using AIRR characterization before, during, and after therapy can be a valuable guideline for planning future treatment approaches and developing new treatment strategies. Also, combining clinical information with BCR and TCR sequences can provide many advantages during the decision-making process. The literature offers a wide range of genomic data that has been analyzed thoroughly to understand the correlation between AIRR differentiations and immunotherapy studies, and various databases have been built to improve the characterization capacity [23]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including anti-CTLA4 and anti-PD1 antibodies, sustain T-cell activity by blocking inhibitory receptors, thus promoting anti-tumor responses [24–28]. These therapies drive measurable alterations in TCR/BCR repertoires, such as expansion of specific clonotypes and shifts in diversity [29]. AIRR-seq provides a powerful means to track these immune changes, offering biomarkers for treatment efficacy and immune-related adverse events [22,23].

It is known that some autoimmune diseases are linked to the presence of specific immune receptors that recognize self-antigens. A recent publication showed that targeted depletion of TRBV9+ T-cells held potential as a therapy for ankylosing spondylitis [30]. This example illustrates that the presence of specific receptors, which can be detected through AIRR sequencing, is directly related to disease risk for particular cases. In addition to detecting prior pathogen exposures or inferring an individual’s susceptibility to disease, AIR-based features have also been linked to disease severity. In both infectious and noncommunicable pathologies with an immune component, sequence content, gene usage, and the diversity (i.e., breadth and depth) of the repertoire have all been associated with clinical measures of disease severity [31–34]. In addition, HLA genotypes have been long linked to the occurrence of specific auto-immune disorders [35,36]. MHC molecules are a primary driver in shaping the TCR repertoire due to the TCR selection process, which requires TCR binding to MHC with an appropriate strength. Consequently, statistical processes make it possible to determine the HLA type from a given TCR repertoire [37].

Methodological categories of AIRR diagnostics

The ubiquity of AIRR data and the promise of novel diagnostic applications have driven a growing demand for robust computational methods to analyze and interpret this information. Analysis and classification of repertoires can be broadly divided into two categories. One category utilizes features derived from the shape of the repertoire, based on repertoire-level metrics such as diversity, similarity, and gene usage. The other category focuses on individual sequences within the repertoire, identifying motifs present only in a small subset of receptors associated with one class or another. More recent research has gone into integrating both methods to extract fine-grained sequence-level insights and broader immune system characteristics simultaneously.

Repertoire-level classification

The immune repertoire encompasses all the AIR sequences in an organism; only a small fraction of these sequences are involved in disease progression and resolution [9,38]. However, the properties of the repertoire as whole can already be informative for specific diagnostic applications, beyond the presence or absence of disease-specific sequences. Of particular note, is the repertoire diversity, which can be enumerated for any AIRR sample, which considers the number of unique B- cell clones or T- cell clonotypes and their abundance in the repertoire. In this manner, diversity can be considered a proxy for the range of antigenic targets that these receptors can recognize, which has clinical relevance in several settings.

Commonly used approaches to describe and compare global AIR diversity measures derive from studies in biodiversity and include species richness Shannon entropy and the Gini-Simpson index among others, which are specific cases of Rényi entropy or Hill diversity indices [39,40]. These metrics are based on the number of unique AIR sequences found within the repertoire as well as their relative frequency. Among these, Shannon entropy is one of the most common metrics, calculated by summing the frequencies of each unique clonotype in a sample multiplied by the base 2 log of the same frequency. This entropy calculation is often normalized by the maximum possible entropy to allow comparison between samples. This normalized term is then referred to as the Shannon Equitability Index or Pielou’s Evenness Index. Using such diversity metrics, research has shown that lower TCR repertoire diversity in tumor tissues than paired adjacent normal tissues was associated with a poor prognosis [41,42]. In this instance, the lower diversity might suggest T cells were blocked from infiltrating into tumors or induced apoptosis in the tumor microenvironment, which indicates an insufficient T-cell immune response, and these patients might benefit from checkpoint blockade therapies. These diversity metrics can be calculated using most commonly available AIRR analysis tools, such as VDJtools, MiXCR, and Alakazam [43–45].

The Morisita-Horn similarity index (MH) is a widely used statistical measure for analyzing repertoire overlap. This approach considers the number of shared sequences between two repertoires and the contribution of those sequences to each repertoire, which is sensitive to both rare and abundant sequences. MH similarity index ranges from zero to one, where zero represents no overlap (different repertoires), and 1 represents perfect overlap (identical repertoires). Jin et al. (2018) demonstrated the utility of the MH index in assessing the similarity of the TCR repertoire in the peripheral blood of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) patients and its correlation with their prognosis [42].

In addition to global AIRR diversity comparisons, other methods aim to limit the scope analysis to specific regions of AIRR sequences. For example, the relative abundance of specific AIR-encoding genes can also reflect disease-specific associations. Variable (V) and Joining (J) gene usage analysis in AIRR has become a powerful tool for repertoire classification and diagnosis. This approach leverages the unique patterns of VJ gene segment usage in TCRs and BCRs to understand immune responses and disease states better. Specifically, the V gene is often most relevant as it captures the variation in the CDR1 and CDR2 as well as a part of the CDR3, which contribute to the specificity and affinity of the immune receptor. Differences in the distribution of V genes used in the rearranged repertoire can indicate an antigen-specific response or unusual clonal expansions. In addition, the identification of clonally expanded clusters of TCR or BCR sequences in a repertoire, can reveal disease-associated features and represent an active area of research [46,47]. These approaches are further complemented by repertoire-based global network analysis, which provides an additional dimension by taking into account clusters that exist in the AIRR when represented as a network [48]. AIRR-derived networks inherently follow a power-law distribution, characterized by a few highly expanded clonotypes and many low-frequency ones. This skewed organization, which can be observed even in cord blood samples prior to antigen exposure, reflects the fundamental architecture of adaptive immune repertoires. Immune responses to infections further accentuate this pattern by expanding antigen-specific clonotypes that cluster around highly connected network hubs [49].

Analyses focused on shorter subsequences or motifs across the breadth of the repertoire or within regions of functional importance (e. g., CDR) have also been linked to disease risk and severity [50–52]. For BCR-focused AIRR analyses, comparing somatic hypermutation rates can also be informative in ML-based classification analyses [53].

Sequence-level classification

Sequence-level classification is essential for identifying a set of AIRs that can discriminate between two or more biological conditions. The first demonstration of this approach has been examined by Emerson et al., who developed a classifier based on the presence of specific individual clonotypes to differentiate between CMV-positive and negative individuals [9]. To achieve this, the researchers used public clonotypes, which are TCR sequences shared by many individuals. In most cases, a public TCR clonotype is defined as a clonotype that occurs exactly, i.e., matching both V gene usage and CDR3 amino acid sequence, across more than 1%−5% of the studied cohort. In BCR, public or convergent clones are usually computed as sequences with a certain percentage of CDR3 identity due to the somatic hypermutation that accompanies clonal expansion and introduces targeted mutations in the BCR. Public clonotypes are often close to germline, with a high chance of being generated by the recombination process during lymphocyte maturation [54]. The Emerson-type approach identifies AIR sequences that may be linked to specific diseases, immune responses, or other biological conditions by quantifying the frequency and distribution of public clonotypes across different populations. Its archetypal application involves a Fisher exact test to score TCRs based on their association with a specific condition of interest. Each significant TCR is then retained as either a positive or a negative selected TCR. A diagnostic metric is then calculated by enumerating the occurrence of the positively associated TCRs, normalized by the repertoire size. Further refinements are possible by providing the associated TCRs to a machine-learning model, such as a random forest or logistic regression [55,56].

To integrate private clonotypes for diagnostic research questions, AIR sequence clustering methods have been proposed to group highly similar sequences whose common patterns can be identified across different repertoires [57]. However, research has shown that most clustering methods suffer from a high false positive rate and thus often group functionally irrelevant AIR sequences together, hindering their use for diagnostic applications [58,59]. For this reason, prioritization strategies have been developed to highlight those AIR sequences or clusters that may be disease-relevant. Common strategies entail evaluating their generation probability, as a high frequency but low probability clone indicates antigen-specific expansion and the number of highly similar AIR sequences within the repertoire, as there is often a convergence of similar sequences following antigen stimulation [60–62].

Multiple instance learning

While AIR repertoire-level and sequence-level features have demonstrated their utility in diagnostic tasks, multimodal ML-based approaches that combine gene usage, CDR-related sequence composition, k-mer/motif abundances, and other AIR-derived features can lead to improved diagnostic or classification performance [33,51,53,63]. Recent studies [11,64–67] highlight the importance of leveraging specific AIRR encoding strategies and state-of-the-art ML models, such as Multiple Instance Learning (MIL), to enhance our understanding of immune repertoires to avoid complex data structure-related accuracy decreases. Immune repertoire classification serves as a quintessential example of MIL. In this approach, a group of instances, known as a ‘bag’, is assigned a single label rather than individual labels for each instance. During training, the specific labels of instances within the bag remain unknown; only the overall bag label is provided. Despite several successful applications to AIRR-diagnostic problems, ongoing work aims to resolve the remaining challenges in immune repertoire classification related to the biological properties of TCR and BCR sequences. Key difficulties include: (i) Low witness rate: In MIL, the Witness Rate (WR) is defined as the proportion of discriminating instances within a bag. In the context of AIRR classification, where only a small subset of sequences indicate disease status, identifying relevant sequences becomes particularly challenging because real-world sequencing captures only a small proportion of the total repertoire. For example, in a large dataset of CMV-binding repertoires, each repertoire contained an average of 192,515 unique TCR sequences. However, estimates suggest that only about 100 sequences are associated with CMV [68]. This corresponds to a WR of less than 0.0001%, making it extremely difficult to distinguish the disease-relevant sequences from the vast majority of non-discriminative ones. (ii) Minimal overlap in AIR sequences: The overlap of AIR sequences between individuals is typically less than 1%, even when analyzing millions of sequences per repertoire [69]. (iii) Extensive diversity resulting from V(D)J recombination, leading to many instances. Estimates suggest that this mechanism can theoretically generate up to 10^20 unique TCR sequences [70] and even more for BCR. (iv) Many-to-many AIR-epitope binding: Multiple AIR sequences can bind the same antigen, and an AIR single sequence can bind multiple antigens [71–73].

To address these challenges, AIR MIL-based methods, such as DeepRC, (a) apply transformer-like attention pooling instead of max pooling, allowing the model to learn a classifier on the repertoire representation rather than the sequence representation, (b) pool learned representations rather than predictions, and (c) use more flexible feature extractors, such as 1D convolutions, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or long short-term memory networks (LSTMs). The DeepRC strategy is expertly crafted to analyze large datasets through the innovative “learning to focus” approach. By honing in on key segments of the data, this strategy uncovers significant patterns that are crucial for accurate target identification. Additionally, modern Hopfield networks in DeepRC facilitate the identification of interpretable patterns across large sets of instances [66]. Another strategy involves integrating multimodal data, as implemented in DeepAIR [74]. DeepAIR combines structural, sequence, and VJ gene usage features to improve immune repertoire classification. This multimodal approach enhances classification performance, particularly in cases where sequence-based features alone are insufficient to distinguish between repertoires.

Selecting machine learning approaches for AIRR diagnostics

Selecting the most appropriate ML approach for AIRR diagnostics depends strongly on the data characteristics, cohort size, and intended application to reach accurate information. Traditional models such as logistic regression and random forest approaches are useful for small to moderate datasets, due to their simplicity, interpretability, and robustness to overfitting. These models are particularly suited when the primary features are global repertoire metrics (diversity indices, V/J gene frequencies) or public clonotypes [9]. In contrast, deep learning approaches, including CNN/LSTM-based frameworks, immuneML, and DeepAIR, provide more accurate results when used on large-scale datasets. These methods automatically extract complex non-linear features that can reveal sequence motifs, but require careful cross-validation and external testing to avoid overfitting [67,74,75]. Disease-relevant (low witness rate) cases require analysis with MIL architectures, such as DeepRC, which have become particularly effective to use for these purposes. MIL models identify relevant subsets of sequences without requiring explicit labels for individual sequences by applying attention-based pooling [66,76].

The choice of ML model directly impacts diagnostic utility: interpretable models facilitate biological validation and clinical acceptance, whereas high-complexity models may achieve better accuracy at the cost of transparency. Therefore, model selection should consider (i) dataset size and diversity, (ii) whether interpretability or predictive power is prioritized, and (iii) the intended translation to clinical decision-making.

The importance of data for AIRR diagnostics

A large number of high-quality, reliable datasets is essential for effective machine-learning strategies in diagnostics [77–80]. Progress in the field has been accelerated by the availability of public immune receptor sequencing datasets. These datasets vary in their scale, focus, and applications and broadly fall into two categories: large-scale cohorts that enable population-level analyses and focused disease-specific cohorts that provide deep insights into particular pathologies (Table 1). To promote the reuse of AIRR data, the AIRR Community has developed metadata standards for AIRR-seq datasets and repositories tied together in the AIRR Data Commons (ADC). The AIRR Data Commons [81] is composed of 10 international repositories, contains over 5.2 billion AIR sequences, and is accessible through user-friendly web-based interfaces such as iReceptor [82] and VDJServer [81]. Observed Antibody Space (OAS) [83] and Observed T-cell Space [28] contain over 2.4 billion AIR sequences, also stored in the AIRR standard format, resulting in over 7.5B AIR sequences spanning 1000s of subjects and 100s of studies available to researchers in an interoperable data format and easily accessible for ML diagnostic researchers (Table 1).

These diverse datasets serve multiple research applications spanning diagnostic biomarker identification, disease progression monitoring, treatment response prediction, and public versus private immune repertoire characterization. The complementary nature of large-scale and focused datasets has proven essential for advancing both basic immunological research and clinical applications.

Large-scale cohorts are characterized by their extensive sequence depth and broad subject coverage. The dataset from Emerson et al. exemplifies this category, comprising 114 million AIR sequences from 786 subjects, focused primarily on CMV responses [9]. Datasets like this facilitate the identification of disease-specific TCR signatures across populations and enable robust statistical analyses due to their large sample sizes. On the other hand, datasets such as Zaslavsky et al. are impressive for their breadth [75]. This dataset contains BCR and TCR repertoire data from 593 subjects across both viral infections (SARS-CoV-2, Influenza, HIV) and autoimmune conditions (SLE, Type 1 Diabetes (T1D)), enabling comparative analyses across different disease contexts. Similarly, the dataset from Rawat et al. consists of over 300 million TCR sequences from 2250 HLA-typed subjects across T1D, healthy related, and non-related controls [84]. This study used ML analysis to demonstrate TCR motif enrichment associated with T1D, indicating a potential basis for TCR-based diagnostics and therapeutics. This study is part of the T1D AIRR Consortium, which currently consists of over 400 million AIR sequences across eight studies and 2500 subjects, including T1D and non-T1D controls [85]. These large-scale cohorts are necessary for applying sequence-level classification techniques and more advanced ML-based approaches due to their reliance on large sample sizes. For the sequence-level approaches, this is due to their dependence on public AIR sequences or patterns, necessitating large cohorts to identify those sequences that occur in a small subset. Indeed, research has shown that sequence-level classification requires a critical cohort size to provide any performance, which, for the CMV seropositivity classification using the associated clonotype approach, seems to be 400 subjects [76]. On the other hand, advanced AIRR-based ML classifiers require larger sample sizes to allow proper fitting of their model weights and avoid overfitting, as is typical for any ML application [86] (Table 2).

Disease-specific cohorts, while typically smaller in size, offer detailed characterization of specific pathologies through carefully matched case-control designs. In another study of T1D, Seay et al. identified TCR-beta chains with homology to a known autoimmune TCR in 7 out of just 18 patients [87]. However, in general, the smaller-scale data sets are often restricted to repertoire-level classification approaches as the cohorts do not have sufficient power for other types of analyses. This lack of power prohibits sequence-level classification, as each clonotype to be considered is another variable. This restricts their signal to a specific setting, where, for example, a higher AIRR diversity is indicative of a patient subgroup. However, these disease-specific cohorts often have an additional information level in the form of longitudinal data, where the same subject was sampled at multiple time points. If this was before and after an intervention, changes within the AIRR repertoire can be tracked. In this manner, one can track AIR sequences of interest if they match a relevant frequency profile across time points, for example, a clear expansion after treatment or disease onset. This reduces the amount of AIR sequences to be studied and, in some cases, allows the application of sequence-level classification by tracking these clonotypes of interest. Many of the individual cohort data sets from the studies discussed above (e.g. Emerson, Rawat, Seay) are available to search, analyze, and download from the AIRR Data Commons.

Discussion

AIRR-based diagnostics has progressed in recent years, with substantial scientific leaps in the methods we use to study AIRR data and the insights derived from them. However, more research is still required to unlock the potential of AIRR diagnostics, as the current analysis techniques still need to overcome several common hurdles.

There are different commercial AIRR high-throughput sequencing kits, and each uses its own set of primers and chemistry before even considering more recent single-cell or spatial sequencing techniques [4]. Due to these differences, the resulting data has biases directly related to the sequencing techniques. For example, research has shown that samples from different sequencing protocols can be separated purely based on V/J gene usage [60]. The potential for significant bias exists in classification methods, particularly when merging different data sets derived from blood sample testing. This issue warrants careful consideration to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results. Thus, when combining data sets, one must remain aware that any signal might be specific to the data set, not the diagnostic problem of interest. If ML methods are applied, this can result in information bleed if the same bias is present in both the training and test sets [91]. This type of information bleed can be hard to identify due to the common AIRR issue of a lack of ground truth. Evaluating any AIRR-based diagnostic signal on independent external data sets is thus best practice.

A common assumption is that the most frequent clones should be the most relevant for the diagnostic application of interest. However, the majority of methods that have been developed, including the most performant approaches, do not utilize this information and only consider the presence or absence of biomarkers within the AIR repertoire. It is still an open question whether there is value in using clone frequency, as this adds another information layer, again biased by the experimental technique and utilized computational annotation tools [92].

As mentioned throughout the review, many diagnostic-based methods only consider public clonotypes, which results in a loss of information. In the MIRA dataset, only 2.7% of clones identified were shared in 2 or more subjects [93]. However, representing the diverse AIRs in a universal feature space that best captures their function or relevance is currently an unsolved problem.

Parallel to the field of AIRR diagnostics is the field of AIRR target prediction, which aims to predict the antigenic target of a specific AIR sequence. These methods also hold great value for AIRR diagnostics, as the occurrence of an AIR sequence linked with a target of interest can be a valuable biomarker. In this manner, these methods can enable a bottom-up approach, where one is using molecular annotation to translate to the patient level, compared to the more common top-down approach reviewed in this article, where AIRR patterns are searched in a manner entirely dependent on the subject annotation without their molecular context. Leveraging AIRR target prediction as the basis of bottom-up AIRR diagnostics has been shown to be viable for COVID-19 diagnostics [94]. However, the models developed for AIRR target prediction are currently restricted in their application as most are only performant for targets with sufficient training data [95]. Where sequence-level target annotations are available, these are often derived from clones identified by clonal expansion in an individual or from public clones enriched across individuals. These techniques also have an inherent selection bias that should be carefully considered when selecting training data for repertoire classification or biomarker discovery.

A final concern currently hindering the advancement of ML methods for AIRR diagnostics is the lack of thorough and independent benchmarking at all levels, including processing steps, encoding strategies and computational approaches. This is primarily due to a lack of solid benchmarking data sets with known ground truth. As indicated, several large-scale AIRR data sets are available and commonly used to benchmark novel methods. However, these datasets share two significant downsides that hinder their AI readiness. Firstly, there is no standardized way of processing the datasets or splitting these datasets into train, validation, and test subsets. As indicated before, depending on how this is done, this can result in biases or information bleed, which remain undetected before application to external data sets. In addition, there is also no agreed-upon metric on which to evaluate the performance of novel methods. Therefore, any novel approach currently has to rerun previous methods for comparison on the same data set to ensure that no novel biases are introduced in their test setup. Secondly, the ground truth of these datasets is not known. On the sequence level, the annotation could be wrong as they often depend on other experimental techniques, which have a known error rate. For example, the AIRR samples in the Emerson dataset are frequently classified on CMV serostatus, which itself is based on antibody titer cut-off. Further, it is often also not known which, if any, AIR sequences underlie or associate with a given condition. This means that the accuracy of identified patterns is difficult to assess as they cannot be compared to a known ground truth.

From a future perspective, developments of AIRR-based diagnostics approaches are likely to be useful where current methods fall short. These include early disease detection before clinical symptoms appear, patient stratification for personalized immunotherapies, and monitoring minimal residual disease or immune reconstitution in transplantation. In these scenarios, repertoire-based tests could provide unique actionable insights that conventional assays cannot, but only if they match or exceed existing tools in accuracy, cost, and turnaround time. Identifying and prioritizing these high-impact clinical use cases will be critical for the future development, approval, and integration of AIRR diagnostics into routine practice.

The AIRR Community’s Diagnostics Working Group is committed to advancing AIRR-seq for clinical use, focusing on prognosis, diagnostics, and disease monitoring. By broadly disseminating the existing and potential possibilities for AIRR-seq in the clinic and engaging regulatory bodies, they aim to overcome obstacles in bringing AIRR-seq into clinical practice. Additionally, the newly forming ML Working Group is poised to create competitions for classifying AIRR-seq data, fostering innovation and collaboration within the community. These efforts collectively strive to bridge the gap between technological advancements and practical applications, ensuring that the full potential of AIRR-seq is realized in diagnostic settings.
