Water infiltration and saturated hydraulic conductivity in an agricultural watershed with pedogenetic discontinuity
Authors: Leandro Dalbianco, Jean Paolo Gomes Minella, Gizachew Ayalew Tiruneh, José Miguel Reichert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding soil hydrology is crucial for effective watershed management, particularly in landscapes characterized by pedogenetic discontinuities and gravelly soils. This study investigates soil hydraulic properties within a tobacco-cultivated watershed, focusing on granulometry, bulk density, water retention (from saturation to permanent wilting point), steady-state infiltration rate (Is), and saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat and Keff) across different hillslopes, slope positions, and soil layers. Results show a wide range of Ksat (0 to 387 mm h−1), with the highest conductivity consistently found in surface layers, attributed to intense tillage from ridge construction in tobacco farming. Variability was also high in Is (4 to 402 mm h−1) and Keff (0 to 224 mm h−1). Acrisols exhibited some low infiltration values, while midslope positions generally showed higher Is across soil types. A linear relationship between Keff and Is (r² = 0.67) was observed, though the Ksat of surface soil was not related to Is. More permeable soils (higher Ksat) had lower bulk density and reduced water retention under wet or dry conditions. In surface layers, Ksat correlated positively with water retention near field capacity, Keff related directly to Is and gravel content but inversely to retention at permanent wilting point, and Is correlated positively with fine sand and saturation water retention and negatively with bulk density. This spatial heterogeneity reflects the influence of texture, compaction, land use, and microtopography in sloped terrains. Pedogenetic discontinuities shape soil horizon development and hydrological response, especially under saturation, highlighting the importance of incorporating these factors into hydrological models and watershed management to mitigate subsurface erosion risks. Enhanced understanding of these soil layers aids prediction of infiltration, runoff, and chemical transport, improving land and soil management in similar environments.

Introduction

Water movement in saturated soils is vital to watershed hydrology, influencing runoff, soil erosion, groundwater recharge, and contaminant transport. In areas with pedogenetic discontinuities, i.e., sudden changes in soil horizons due to shifts in parent material or clay migration, these processes become more complex. Such discontinuities can act as hydraulic barriers or create preferential flow paths based on differences in soil texture and structure1. These conditions often promote lateral flow, perched water tables, and increased surface runoff, which may intensify erosion or alter recharge patterns2. Understanding these dynamics is essential for hydrological modeling and sustainable land and water management3.

In addition to natural soil layering, land-use changes and management practices further complicate the spatial variability of soil physical properties. Activities such as tillage, irrigation, fertilization, and organic matter amendments alter soil structure and hydrological behavior at local scales4. For instance, tillage can homogenize surface soil layers, while targeted fertilizer use can influence compaction and porosity. Spatial variability in properties such as texture, structure, and macroporosity is particularly influential because it affects hydraulic conductivity and water flow pathways5. Macropores formed by biological activity or physical cracking facilitate preferential flow, which bypasses the soil matrix. In colluvial or alluvial settings, pedogenetic discontinuities often create low-permeability zones that redirect water laterally, impacting water availability and erosion risk6. Understanding both natural and management-induced spatial heterogeneity is therefore vital for site-specific soil management, precision agriculture, and effective watershed conservation.

Soil hydraulic properties, such as saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat) and water retention, vary significantly across soil discontinuities, which can alter the dynamics of water flow within a landscape7. These alterations influence subsurface water pathways, often redirecting flow toward the soil surface and affecting runoff timing, volume, and direction. As a result, this process can intensify erosion and lead to the loss of nutrients within a watershed8. Understanding how water moves through these complex soil systems is essential for accurate watershed hydrology modeling and for developing effective land and water management strategies.

In sloped watersheds, the distribution of Ksat near the soil surface is particularly critical. This surface-level conductivity determines how much rainfall infiltrates into the soil versus how much runs off, influencing sediment transport and soil erosion rates9. Because of its importance for hydrological modeling10, mapping the spatial distribution of Ksat is key to capturing infiltration processes and variability across the landscape. The uneven distribution of Ksat can create runoff-prone areas, known as hotspots, which are vulnerable to rill and gully erosion11. Consequently, physically-based models like WEPP, LISEM, and SWAT require detailed Ksat data to produce accurate predictions, especially in topographically complex regions12,13. Several studies have applied these models to Brazilian watersheds to evaluate runoff and erosion processes14.

A strong correlation exists between steady-state infiltration (Is), often measured under field conditions, and Ksat obtained from laboratory analysis15. Initially, infiltration is rapid due to capillary suction and gravitational forces, but it slows as the soil becomes saturated, eventually stabilizing at Is, the minimum infiltration rate under saturated conditions. This rate is vital for predicting surface runoff and soil erosion, influencing environmental sustainability and socio-economic resilience. Infiltration behavior is affected by soil texture, structure, mineralogy, and land management16.

Slopes significantly influence hydrological processes such as runoff, infiltration, erosion, and sediment transport, which shape downstream hydrology17. Spatial analysis across hillslopes is vital to understanding how these processes originate and evolve from upper to lower slope positions. This is especially important in areas with heterogeneous soils, where contrasting textures can lead to dominant subsurface flow and saturation-excess runoff. Variations in topographic position affect infiltration rates and hydraulic conductivity due to differences in soil development, compaction, and water redistribution18. These dynamics, shaped by both natural and landscape-driven factors, influence water movement and help identify zones vulnerable to erosion or waterlogging, aiding watershed management19.

The objectives of this study were to evaluate whether hillslope position (upper slope, midslope, and toeslope), soil type (Acrisols, Cambisols, and Leptosols), and pedogenetic discontinuities produce a pattern in steady-state infiltration (Is), saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat), and effective hydraulic conductivity (Keff); and to assess whether effective, profile-scale conductivity Keff predicts field-measured infiltration better than surface-layer Ksat under tobacco cultivation and ridge-tillage management.

Materials and methods

Description of the watershed

The study was conducted in a rural headwater watershed, with a drainage area of 1.19 km2, located in the northeastern region of the state of Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil (latitude 28° 49′ 31" S, longitude 52° 12′ 30" W). The altitude varies from 560 to 740 m, where the upper portion of the basin has gentle relief, and the lower portion is a mountainous portion of the watershed (Fig. 1). The local climate, according to the Köppen climate classification, is of the Cfb type, humid subtropical with hot summers and winter with frequent frosts20. The average annual rainfall is 1938 mm21.Fig. 1Location of the watershed in southern Brazil and sampled hillslopes for soil characterization within the studied watershed.

Location of the watershed in southern Brazil and sampled hillslopes for soil characterization within the studied watershed.

Geology is characterized by volcanic spills of the Serra Geral formation (riodacite), and the soil classes are Acrisols (57%), Cambisols (10%), and Leptosols (33%) (Fig. 2). In general, the soils are characterized by the presence of a coarse-textured A horizon, with the presence of gravel in the surface layer and impediments to water flow in subsurface layers (20–40 cm), due to the presence of an argillic B horizon, saprolite, or rock.Fig. 2Soil map of the studied watershed, with an indication of sampling locations.

Soil map of the studied watershed, with an indication of sampling locations.

In the lower third of the watershed (toeslope), the undulating relief (average slope of 7%) conditioned the formation of more developed soils, with the presence of textural B horizon (Acrisols), associated with less developed soils (Cambisols and Leptosols). In the middle third (midslope), the slightly more slopy relief than in the lower third provides great spatial variability of soils, and there are associations between Acrisols, Cambisols, and Leptosols. In the upper third (upper slope), the mountainous relief, with short hillslopes and embedded valleys, is formed by less-developed soils composed of associations of Cambisols and Leptosols (Fig. 2).

Soil use and management are characterized by tobacco cultivation (Nicotiana tabacum L.) under minimum tillage system (23.3%), tobacco cultivation in conventional system (17.4%), native forest (19.8%), reforestation with eucalyptus (Eucalyptus spp.) (22.8%), pastures (6.6%), shrubbery forest (6.3%), farmsteads (2.9%), and small dams (0.9%). For this study, all sampling sites were under fallow after tobacco cultivation.

Hillslopes, soils, and physical and hydrological characterization of the soil

Five hillslopes in the watershed were selected (Fig. 1), with varying soil toposequences, to study soil granulometry, bulk density, water retention from 0 (saturation or total porosity) to 1500 kPa (permanent wilting point), Ksat, and effective hydraulic conductivity (Keff), and Is. Sampling points followed the hillslope gradient, seeking to cover areas with more developed and less developed soils used for tobacco cultivation. To represent the landscape variability, soil properties were characterized at three positions on each of the hillslope, namely upper slope, midslope, and toeslope positions.

Water infiltration was determined using a double-ring infiltrometer22, with a 5-cm hydraulic head, and readings were taken every five minutes until steady-state infiltration was reached. Three replications were performed with a distance of a maximum of five meters at each sampled point. The Is values were classified as low (< 15 mm h−1), medium (15–50 mm h−1), and high (> 50 mm h−1) infiltration rate23.

At all points where water infiltration tests were performed, four replicates of soil samples with preserved structure were collected with metal rings of 0.04 m in height and 0.057 m in diameter, at the top of four soil layers (0–20, 20–40, 40–60, and 60–80 cm). These samples were used to determine soil bulk density, water retention at different tensions, and Ksat24. Samples with altered structure were also collected (0.5 kg), in the same layers described above, for the determination of granulometry after soil dispersion25 by the pipette method26, and permanent wilting point (1500 kPa) using a WP4 psychrometer27,28. The gravimetric water content was multiplied by the bulk density corresponding to the sampled layer to obtain the volumetric water content.

Soil samples with structural integrity were prepared in the laboratory and saturated through capillarity at 0 kPa, which represents total soil porosity. Afterward, on a sand column29,30, the samples were exposed to tensions of 6 kPa that separate water retained in micropores from retention in macropores, and to 10 kPa to estimate water held at field capacity. Finally, the samples were oven-dried at 105 °C until constant weight to calculate soil bulk density.

Another set of soil samples with structural integrity was used for Ksat determination, using a constant head permeameter31. Then, the Keff of the soil profile was calculated as suggested by32:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Keff=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Li/\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Li}{Ki}$$\end{document}where L is the thickness of saturated layer i, Ki is Ksat of saturated layer i, and m is the number of saturated layers in the soil profile.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance was done for all studies of soil properties. For soil granulometric classes (gravel, coarse sand, fine sand, total sand, silt, and clay), statistical evaluation using the Tukey test at a 5% probability of error was performed to compare, for each granulometric class, the differences between the averages of each relief position on the hillslope, where the averages are composed of the values ​​of the four soil layers, for each slope separately. For soil physical-hydraulic properties, statistical analysis using the Tukey test at 5% was performed with two objectives: comparing soil layers for the same relief position and comparing relief positions considering the average value of the four soil layers for the physical-hydraulic property. For the Is values, statistical analysis using the Tukey test at 10% was performed to compare relief positions on the same hillslope and to compare slopes for the same relief position. Moreover, we performed correlation analysis among soil properties and linear regression analysis (Ksat vs. Is, and Keff vs. Is).

Hillslope 2 (125 m long) is predominantly occupied by Acrisols with a marked textural gradient, reaching depths of up to 80 cm at the upper slope and midslope, while Leptosols occur at the toeslope (Table 1). All positions presented gravelly surface layers underlain by more clayey subsurface horizons (Table 2). Ksat and total sand contents were consistently higher in surface layers than in subsurface layers (Table 3). In contrast to the other hillslopes, Is values were low at the upper slope and midslope and medium at the toeslope (Table 4), indicating a distinct hydrological behavior.

Hillslope 3 (45 m long) exhibited pronounced pedogenetic variability despite its short length. Cambisols occurred at the midslope, whereas Acrisols with textural gradients dominated the upper slope and toeslope (Table 1). Clay content was highest at the upper slope (Table 2). Is was classified as medium at the upper slope, high at the midslope, and low at the toeslope (Table 4).

Hillslope 4 (135 m long) was characterized by Leptosols at the upper slope and Cambisols at the midslope and toeslope (Table 1). Gravel was present in most layers except at the toeslope, where sediment deposition resulted in higher silt contents throughout the profile (Table 2). The highest Ksat values occurred in surface layers (Table 3). Is was high at the upper slope and midslope and medium at the toeslope (Table 4).

Hillslope 5 (125 m long) presented Acrisols at the upper slope and less developed soils (Leptosols and Cambisols) downslope (Table 1). The midslope had a shallow profile (60 cm) with consistently high Ksat across layers (Table 3). Is was high at the upper slope and midslope and medium at the toeslope (Table 4). Field observations confirmed sediment deposition at the toeslope, which coincided with the lowest Is values along this hillslope.

Variability and correlation of soil physical and hydraulic properties

Soil texture, bulk density (BD), saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat), and steady-state infiltration rate (Is) are summarized in Tables 2, 3 and 4for all hillslopes, positions, and soil layers. Gravel was present in nearly all soils, except at the toeslope of hillslope 4. Total sand content ranged from 3 to 57%, silt from 28 to 66%, and clay from 12 to 66%, indicating strong textural heterogeneity across the watershed (Table 2).

Bulk density differed significantly among hillslope positions and soil layers (p < 0.05; Table 3). Mean BD values ranged from approximately 0.86 g cm−3 at midslope positions to 1.35 g cm−3 at toeslope positions. Bulk density generally increased with depth and tended to be higher at toeslopes than at upper and midslope positions, reflecting sediment accumulation and reduced biological disturbance. In surface layers (0–20 cm), the lowest BD values occurred most frequently at midslopes, whereas the highest BD values were observed in deeper layers (40–60 cm) at toeslope positions.

Soil water retention exhibited substantial variability across matric potentials (0, 6, 10, and 1500 kPa) and was strongly influenced by both hillslope position and soil depth (Table 3). At saturation (0 kPa), toeslope soils showed the highest water retention, particularly in surface layers, followed by midslope and upper slope soils. Water retention consistently decreased with depth across all hillslopes. At 1500 kPa (permanent wilting point), water retention declined sharply at all positions; however, toeslope soils maintained relatively higher water contents than upslope positions.

Coefficients of variation (CV), considering all hillslopes, positions, and layers, were low for bulk density (10%), silt (21%), and water retention at different matric potentials (8–30%), with CV increasing at higher tensions (drier conditions). Intermediate variability was observed for clay (47%) and total sand (54%), while gravel (109%) and Ksat (219%) exhibited very high variability. Steady-state infiltration rate (Is) also showed high CVs at most positions (Table 4), even though replicate measurements were conducted within a maximum distance of five meters.

Across hillslopes, Is values were frequently higher at midslope positions; however, these differences were not consistently statistically significant due to pronounced spatial variability. High and medium Is values occurred across all soil types, whereas low Is values were observed only in Acrisols. These results indicate that slope position and soil development jointly influence infiltration behavior, but local heterogeneity limits the detection of uniform spatial trends.

Effective hydraulic conductivity (Keff) exhibited a significant linear relationship with Is (slope = 2.05; r2 = 0.67; p < 0.05), whereas Ksat measured in the surface layer showed no significant relationship with Is (p > 0.05; Fig. 3). Discussion

Hillslope-scale controls on soil physical and hydraulic behavior

The soils exhibited significant variation in physical-hydraulic properties across different hillslope positions (top slope, midslope, and toeslope), which can be attributed to pedogenetic processes, slope-induced erosion, and sediment deposition that influence soil structure, texture, and water retention. These properties are particularly vital for crop water availability in rainfed systems. Higher clay and organic matter content in lower slope positions enhances water retention and provides resilience during dry periods33. Erosion processes on upper slopes often erode fine particles and organic matter, leaving behind coarser, denser soil34, whereas deposition in lower areas improves soil structure and reduces BD35. Furthermore, spatial variability in BD reflects differences in vegetation cover, organic matter, and land use intensity. As noted by36, agricultural activity on higher slopes can exacerbate soil compaction and deplete organic matter, contributing to elevated bulk density levels.

Soil organic matter and aggregation enhance Ksat by improving pore connectivity, especially in degraded or compacted soils37, and well-aggregated soils typically exhibit higher hydraulic conductivity than compacted soils of similar texture38,39. Land management practices such as tillage, cover cropping, and amendments further modify soil structure, thereby altering Ksat dynamically across soil types40. Understanding these variations is crucial for accurate water flow modeling, irrigation management, and minimizing runoff and erosion in agroecosystems.

Ksat varies among soils like Cambisols, Acrisols, and Leptosols due to differences in texture, structure, and porosity. Cambisols, with granular structure and moderate clay, generally have higher Ksat (10–50 mm h−1), favoring infiltration41. Acrisols, rich in kaolinitic clay, often show lower Ksat (< 10 mm h−1) due to compacted structure42. Leptosols, shallow and stony, display variable but frequently low Ksat, restricting continuous flow paths13,43. Understanding this variability is crucial for effective water flow modeling, irrigation management, and minimizing runoff and erosion in agroecosystems. Moreover, organic matter content and land management can significantly alter these baseline values, highlighting the importance of site-specific assessments in understanding hydrological behavior and guiding soil conservation44,45.

Soil compaction and decreased porosity with increasing depth are commonly attributed to reduced root growth and diminished biological mixing, which limit soil structure development46. Furthermore, erosion induced by runoff and the subsequent deposition of materials downslope contribute to the redistribution of finer particles and organic matter, leading to denser soils in lower landscape positions47. The relatively low bulk density values observed on top slopes reflect a more favorable soil structure with better aeration, conditions that support root growth and microbial activity. Enhanced water retention in surface soil layers can be linked to higher organic matter content and root density, which improve soil porosity and aggregation48. Conversely, the reduction in water retention in subsurface layers is likely due to compaction and diminished organic matter. These findings underscore the significant influence of both topographic position and soil depth on soil water retention properties. Consequently, soil management strategies, such as the application of organic amendments and erosion control measures, should consider these spatial variations to optimize water availability, especially in sloped agricultural systems.

Increased water retention in downslope soils is attributed to higher clay content and the accumulation of organic matter resulting from lateral water and sediment movement48. Greater water retention in the lower slope’s top 20 cm soil layer is associated with finer texture, improved porosity, and enhanced soil structure compared to coarser upper-slope soils49–51. Across all slope positions, water retention markedly decreased at 1500 kPa (the permanent wilting point), but the lower slope maintained relatively higher water content, suggesting more available water for plants. The largest difference between water retention at 10 and 1500 kPa occurred in the lower slope, indicating more favorable moisture conditions for plant growth52.

Hydraulic conductivity showed pronounced spatial variability, with Ksat ranging from 0 to 1114 mm h−1 and Is from 4 to 402 mm h−1, highest in surface layers influenced by intense tillage53,54 in the tobacco ridge systems. Such variability reflects the complex interplay of soil texture, compaction, land use, and micro-topography characteristic of sloped landscapes, confirming that infiltration measurements can effectively represent soil hydraulic properties under various management conditions54,55.

Implications of pedogenetic discontinuities for infiltration, conductivity, watershed hydrology, and land management

High variability in Ksat and Is has been widely documented, even across short spatial scales, often obscuring statistically significant differences among treatments or locations54. This variability highlights the necessity of employing numerous replications or applying geostatistical methods to effectively analyze spatial patterns in field data. When estimating surface runoff, alternative infiltration approaches are frequently used to scale point measurements up to hillslope levels. For example, Koppe et al.56 derived infiltration estimates (Ia) by combining the concentric rings method (Ip) and physically-based modeling (Ik). They found that the Ip method tends to underestimate infiltration under high rainfall intensities (> 40 mm h−1) but performs well during less intense rain events, while Ik accurately represents infiltration across all rainfall intensities. Such findings emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate infiltration measurement techniques based on rainfall regimes and study objectives.

Water flow in saturated soils is strongly influenced by vertical and lateral heterogeneity within soil profiles, particularly in watersheds with pedogenetic discontinuities. Well-developed soils, such as Acrisols, often exhibit a textural gradient with coarser particles near the surface horizons (A or E) and finer illuvial clays accumulated in subsurface Bt horizons57,58. Pedogenic discontinuities or variations in lithology between soil horizons, not solely attributable to soil-forming processes, may result from alluvial deposits, colluvial layering, volcanic ash, or anthropogenic activities59. When infiltrating water encounters a finer-textured Bt horizon beneath coarser surface layers, a capillary barrier effect can form, causing water to accumulate and subsequently move laterally downslope60. In sloped terrains like those in many forested or mountainous catchments, such lateral subsurface flow or interflow pathways are critical in driving rapid hydrological responses during rainfall events61.

Soils containing substantial gravel content in their surface layers are common in many ecosystems, especially mountainous regions, where they notably affect soil hydrology. Gravel facilitates preferential flow paths, allowing water to bypass smaller pores in the finer soil matrix during heavy rainfall events. Consequently, water stays less time in the upper soil layers, potentially reducing soil water availability for plants62. However, the influence of gravel depends on its size, distribution, and amount. In some cases, gravel layers can compact or form crusts at the surface, creating a “gravel mulch effect” that restricts water infiltration and promotes surface runoff when rainfall intensity exceeds the infiltration capacity of the underlying soil20. Additionally, gravel affects soil thermal and evaporative properties by shading the surface and reducing wind velocity, which can mitigate evaporation despite the lower water retention capacity of gravelly soils63. These combined effects have significant implications for soil erosion, vegetation water uptake, and groundwater recharge, particularly in areas with irregular precipitation patterns.

The Ksat varies considerably with soil texture, influencing infiltration and water movement. Sandy soils typically exhibit high Ksat, often exceeding 50 mm h−1, due to their large pores and low water retention, which favor rapid infiltration64. In contrast, clayey soils generally have low Ksat values below 1 mm h−1 because of their fine texture and small pore sizes that restrict water flow65,66. Loam soils, with balanced sand, silt, and clay fractions, show intermediate Ksat between 10- and 50-mm h−1, reflecting moderate permeability67,68. Higher Ksat values correlate with increased macroporosity and thus faster infiltration, which reduces runoff and enhances groundwater recharge69. Ksat is influenced not only by porosity but also by pore size distribution and geometry, making soil structure and aggregation critical in determining water flow beyond simple porosity measurements70.

Soil texture commonly changes downslope as a result of erosion and sediment deposition processes, creating a distinct gradient along slopes. Finer particles like clay tend to be eroded from upper slopes and deposited in lower areas, which alters soil texture and influences soil properties such as water retention, nutrient availability, and structural stability71. Alongside texture variation, soil compaction is often more pronounced in mid- to lower slope positions due to increased machinery use and livestock grazing in these zones, leading to reduced infiltration rates and elevated surface runoff72. Land use also varies along slopes, affecting soil conditions; for instance, cultivated upper slopes often experience more severe degradation, whereas lower slopes may benefit from the accumulation of eroded, nutrient-rich sediments, improving fertility73. Furthermore, micro-topography plays a critical role in redistributing water, sediments, and organic matter, resulting in high spatial variability in soil properties even over short distances74.

Variations in Ksat and Is within soil profiles are largely governed by soil texture and structure. Surface horizons generally contain higher sand contents and exhibit greater Ksat values due to improved pore connectivity and reduced tortuosity, facilitating water flow5,75. Conversely, clay-rich subsurface horizons show reduced Ksat because of diminished pore connectivity and increased tortuosity, which hinder water movement and can lead to water accumulation above these layers76,77. Soil compaction and pedogenic processes further reduce structural porosity and permeability in subsurface horizons, while swelling of clay particles upon wetting can clog pores and decrease saturated conductivity78. The current study’s findings align with this, demonstrating a notable decrease in Ksat within clay-enriched subsurface layers, underscoring the impact of pedogenic clay accumulation on soil water dynamics.

Additionally, higher Is and Ksat values observed in midslope areas with steeper gradients suggest enhanced infiltration, consistent with previous work indicating improved water movement in intermediate slope positions due to the interplay of soil structure and water redistribution processes79. The occurrence of lateral subsurface flow in these midslope zones further illustrates the complexity of hydrological responses in sloped landscapes80. These results confirm that combining field infiltration tests and laboratory Ksat measurements effectively captures spatial variability in soil water flow.

Tobacco-cultivated soils exhibit significant differences in hydrological behavior between their surface and subsurface layers, largely influenced by specific agricultural practices such as ridge formation. These ridges increase surface roughness, which in turn affects water infiltration rates by modifying surface porosity and runoff patterns81,82. The intensive tillage typical of tobacco farming generally results in a lower bulk density in the surface soil compared to subsurface layers, particularly across various slope positions. Furthermore, the presence of a notable gravel fraction in the surface soil contributes to increased macroporosity, enhancing infiltration capacity and altering hydraulic properties83,84.

Given the existence of distinct soil horizons with different hydraulic characteristics, hydrological models must account for this layered structure to improve infiltration predictions. For example, the Green-Ampt infiltration model can be adapted for multilayered soils to capture such complexities more accurately85. The Ksat values for the surface layer are typically high (around 100 mm h−1), reflecting its porous nature, while subsurface Ksat values vary widely (from 1 to 20 mm h−1), depending on soil type.

The detailed infiltration measurements and physical-hydraulic soil characterizations provide a valuable dataset for understanding watershed hydrodynamics, complementing existing databases86, and for parameterizing physically based hydrological models. These data improve insights into soil water movement, storage, and availability, which are crucial for simulating runoff, groundwater recharge, and soil moisture dynamics at the watershed scale16. Models like SWAT12 and Hydrus87 require accurate site-specific parameters, including Ksat, soil porosity, and water retention data, derived from field and laboratory analyses to enhance prediction accuracy.

This research supports the refinement of hydrological models by incorporating soil features often overlooked in conventional assessments, providing practical knowledge for soil conservation and land management efforts aimed at reducing erosion and nutrient losses. Understanding the interplay between soil formation processes and hydrological function is increasingly vital, especially as climate change intensifies extreme rainfall events. This work contributes to watershed resilience planning by elucidating mechanisms behind saturation-excess runoff, thereby aiding sustainable management strategies under changing climatic conditions.

Implications for soil management in watersheds

The results of this study have direct implications for soil management in small, sloping watersheds characterized by gravelly soils and strong pedogenetic discontinuities. The observed variability in Is, Ksat, and Keff demonstrates that soil hydraulic behavior is governed not only by surface conditions but, more critically, by vertical soil layering and profile-scale constraints. Consequently, soil management strategies that rely exclusively on surface indicators or uniform soil classes are unlikely to adequately control infiltration, runoff, or erosion in such heterogeneous landscapes.

The weak relationship between surface-layer Ksat and field-measured infiltration, contrasted with the stronger predictive capacity of Keff, indicates that management practices aimed solely at increasing surface permeability, such as intensive tillage, ridge construction, or surface loosening, may have limited effectiveness if subsurface horizons restrict vertical water movement. In watersheds with pedogenetic discontinuities, subsurface layers often act as hydraulic bottlenecks, promoting lateral flow and increasing the risk of saturation-excess runoff and subsurface erosion. Effective soil management should therefore prioritize practices that reduce vertical contrasts in soil hydraulic properties, including minimizing compaction, preserving aggregate stability, and avoiding repeated disturbance that accentuates sharp surface–subsurface differences.

The frequent occurrence of higher infiltration and conductivity at midslope positions suggests that transitional landscape zones can play a disproportionate role in regulating hillslope hydrology. From a management perspective, these areas represent both opportunities and risks: they may facilitate infiltration under favorable conditions, but they can also serve as pathways for lateral subsurface flow when restrictive layers are present downslope. Targeted management at midslope positions, such as controlled traffic, maintenance of soil structure, and conservation practices88–90 aligned with slope direction, may therefore yield greater hydrological benefits than uniform treatments applied across entire fields.

The mismatch between mapped soil classes and field-observed profiles highlights an additional management implication: conventional soil maps may not reliably identify zones with restrictive layers or high runoff potential in complex terrain. For practical soil management, this underscores the importance of field-based assessment, including localized infiltration measurements or soil profile observations, to guide site-specific interventions. Such approaches can help identify critical areas where conservation practices, reduced disturbance, or alternative land use may be most effective.

Finally, the findings emphasize that soil management in similar watersheds should adopt a profile-scale and hillslope-scale perspective. Practices designed to improve infiltration, reduce runoff, and limit erosion must account for soil depth, layering, and lateral connectivity rather than focusing solely on surface conditions. By aligning management decisions with the dominant hydrological controls identified in this study, land managers can improve water regulation, reduce soil degradation, and enhance the long-term sustainability of agricultural production in heterogeneous, sloping watersheds.

Scope and limitations of the study

This study provides a detailed assessment of soil hydraulic behavior in a small agricultural watershed characterized by strong pedogenetic discontinuities, gravelly soils, and pronounced hillslope gradients under tobacco cultivation. Its scope includes the evaluation of soil hydraulic properties across hillslope positions, soil types, and soil depths, combining field infiltration measurements with laboratory determinations of soil physical and hydraulic properties. By integrating surface and profile-scale indicators, the study captures key aspects of vertical and lateral variability in soil water flow that are highly relevant for erosion control, runoff generation, and soil–water management in sloping agricultural landscapes.

Some limitations, however, must be considered when interpreting the results and translating them into management recommendations. First, infiltration and hydraulic conductivity measurements represent conditions at the time of sampling and do not capture temporal variability associated with seasonal changes in soil moisture, crop development, biological activity, and rainfall regime. Consequently, the reported values should be interpreted as indicative rather than as long-term averages, particularly in systems subject to periodic tillage and ridge reconstruction.

Second, the strong spatial heterogeneity introduced by gravel content and pedogenetic discontinuities resulted in high variability of Ksat and Is at short spatial scales, reducing the ability to identify consistent, deterministic patterns solely based on hillslope position or soil type. From a land management perspective, this implies that uniform management prescriptions applied across entire hillslopes or soil mapping units may be ineffective or even counterproductive. Instead, management strategies should acknowledge within-field variability and prioritize zones where hydraulic restrictions or excessive infiltration are most likely to occur. Third, conventional soil survey maps were insufficient to represent the true distribution of soil classes and subsurface horizons, as shown by frequent mismatches between mapped soils and field observations. This limitation has direct implications for land-use planning and conservation practices, as reliance on generalized soil maps may underestimate the presence of restrictive layers that control infiltration and promote lateral flow. Field-based assessments or targeted soil profiling are therefore essential for identifying zones vulnerable to runoff concentration, subsurface erosion, or waterlogging.

Fourth, the dominance of tobacco cultivation under ridge-tillage systems constrains the extrapolation of results to other cropping systems. Nevertheless, the findings highlight that ridge construction and surface soil disturbance substantially increase surface-layer Ksat while leaving subsurface hydraulic constraints largely unchanged. This vertical contrast implies that management practices focused solely on improving surface infiltration may not reduce runoff or erosion if subsurface layers restrict vertical percolation and promote lateral flow. Effective land management should therefore aim to reduce hydraulic discontinuities within the soil profile, for example, by minimizing compaction, preserving soil structure, and avoiding practices that accentuate sharp contrasts between surface and subsurface layers. Fifth, preferential and lateral subsurface flow were inferred from hydraulic indicators rather than directly measured. While this limits precise quantification of flow pathways, the consistent divergence between surface Ksat and profile-scale Keff provides strong indirect evidence that subsurface layers exert dominant control on infiltration outcomes. For land management, this reinforces the need to evaluate soil profiles as a whole rather than relying exclusively on surface indicators when designing conservation practices such as contour farming, terraces, or buffer strips.

Finally, scaling remains a key limitation. Although Keff proved to be a better predictor of field-measured infiltration than surface-layer Ksat, translating profile-scale measurements into watershed-scale hydrological response requires additional integration with modeling and long-term monitoring. Nonetheless, the results clearly indicate that management decisions based solely on surface soil properties are likely to misrepresent infiltration capacity and runoff risk in heterogeneous, sloping landscapes.

Overall, within its defined scope, the study provides actionable insight for land management by demonstrating that soil layering, hillslope position, and management-induced surface disturbance jointly control infiltration and hydraulic conductivity. The findings support land management approaches that emphasize profile-scale assessment, spatially targeted interventions, and conservation practices designed to mitigate the effects of pedogenetic discontinuities, thereby improving water infiltration, reducing erosion risk, and enhancing the sustainability of agricultural production in complex terrain.

Conclusion

The results demonstrate that hillslope position, soil type, and pedogenetic discontinuities do influence the magnitude and spatial distribution of soil hydraulic properties, but they do not generate a single, uniform pattern-response in steady-state infiltration (Is), saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat), or effective hydraulic conductivity (Keff) across the watershed. Upper slope, midslope, and toeslope positions exhibited contrasting hydraulic behaviors that reflected local combinations of soil development, texture redistribution, gravel content, and management effects rather than position alone. Similarly, Acrisols, Cambisols, and Leptosols showed distinct ranges of Ksat and Is consistent with their degree of weathering and profile development, yet substantial within-class variability limited the emergence of clear, deterministic trends.

Pedogenetic discontinuities played a key role in controlling water flow by creating sharp vertical contrasts in hydraulic conductivity, frequently restricting vertical percolation and favoring lateral subsurface flow. These discontinuities weakened the direct relationship between surface-layer Ksat and field-measured infiltration, particularly in gravelly and layered soils typical of the studied hillslopes. In contrast, by integrating the hydraulic resistance of multiple soil layers, Keff showed a stronger and more consistent relationship with steady-state infiltration.

Overall, the findings confirm that profile-scale conductivity provides a more reliable predictor of infiltration than surface-layer Ksat under tobacco cultivation and ridge-tillage management. This highlights the importance of accounting for soil layering and hillslope context when interpreting infiltration measurements and parameterizing hydrological models. The study reinforces that soil hydraulic behavior in heterogeneous, sloping landscapes cannot be adequately represented by surface properties alone, and that effective, depth-integrated indicators are essential for understanding infiltration, runoff generation, and subsurface flow in managed watersheds.
