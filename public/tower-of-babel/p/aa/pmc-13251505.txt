Strengthening animal rabies surveillance through a public-private One Health collaboration: Insights from a field veterinarian workshop in Assam, India
Authors: Parimala Mohanty, Balaji Chandrasekhar, Dinesh Kumar Gupta, Ganesh Daimary, Harish Kumar Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurveillance of dog-mediated rabies in India remains constrained, especially in livestock and other animals by limited diagnostic access, biosafety concerns, fragmented reporting systems, and weak integration between field veterinary services and laboratories. Public-private partnerships (PPPs) and One Health oriented capacity building are increasingly promoted as strategies to address these gaps, yet evidence on such approaches remains limited. A two-day workshop titled “Skill Enhancement of Field Veterinarians to Improve Surveillance of Dog-Mediated Rabies in Livestock and Wildlife in Assam” was organised on 25–26 July 2024 in Guwahati, Assam to strengthen field-level rabies surveillance by building sampling and transport capacity, introducing rapid LFA-based diagnostics, enhancing data reporting practices, and promoting a One Health perspective. This article aims to provide implementation of insights into rabies surveillance through One Health collaboration using a PPP approach.MethodologyThis study presents an implementation-focused qualitative analysis through a PPP-led capacity-building workshop for field veterinarians in Assam, India. Structured group discussions held prior to hands-on training explored perceived barriers, operational challenges, and feasible strategies for improving animal rabies surveillance in resource-limited settings. Discussion outputs were thematically analysed to identify recurring system-level constraints and opportunities.ResultsThirty-two field veterinarians from 18 districts and one NGO participated in the workshop. Thematic analysis of group discussions highlighted five interlinked themes influencing rabies surveillance: (i) biosafety and sampling constraints at field level, (ii) limited diagnostic reach and transport logistics, (iii) uncertainty around rapid test interpretation and confirmatory pathways, (iv) fragmented reporting and feedback mechanisms, and (v) the need for institutional coordination across veterinary, public health, and wildlife sectors.ConclusionsThis implementation report demonstrates how PPP-based One Health capacity building can generate practice-based insights from frontline veterinarians and inform feasible approaches to decentralise and strengthen animal rabies surveillance in resource constrained settings.

Introduction

Rabies is a preventable yet neglected zoonotic disease that continues to pose a substantial threat to both human and animal health [1]. The persistence of the disease in endemic countries reflects critical gaps in surveillance, access to preventive services, and multisectoral coordination [2]. As the majority of human cases result from bites by free-roaming dogs, mass dog vaccination remains one of the most cost-effective and sustainable strategies for interrupting transmission [3], [4]. India, home to one of the world's largest populations of free-roaming dogs, contributes nearly one-third of global rabies burden [5]. This disproportionate impact highlights the urgent need for coordinated, cross-sectoral action to strengthen rabies control efforts, as envisaged under the One Health framework [6].

Over the past decade, India's human health sector has made marked progress through improved availability of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), adoption of intradermal PEP regimens, and enhanced public awareness [7]. Effective rabies elimination depends not only on vaccination coverage but also on timely detection, laboratory confirmation, and information flow across sectors, particularly in settings where human-animal interfaces are complex. However, surveillance mechanism in the animal health sector remains weak and underutilised. Laboratory confirmation of rabies in livestock and free-roaming dogs (FRD) is sporadic, geographically limited, and dependent on a small number of regional diagnostic centres [8], [9]. As a result, the vast majority of suspected animal rabies deaths, particularly in rural and resource-limited settings, go unconfirmed and unreported. This diagnostic gap arises primarily from logistical constraints and limited field-level capacity for the safe, timely, and standardised collection of appropriate samples from animals dying with clinical signs consistent with rabies [10], [11].

Field veterinary professionals are the primary point of contact for animal health events and are therefore pivotal to the effective functioning of rabies surveillance systems. However, sample collection from livestock, free-roaming dogs (FRD), and wildlife deaths suspected of rabies remains irregular. This is largely attributable to limited training in sample collection, handling, and transport protocols, compounded by significant biosafety concerns arising from inadequate access to personal protective equipment (PPE) [12], [13]. In contrast, human rabies surveillance exists but is predominantly syndromic, reflecting socio-cultural and ethical constraints surrounding sample collection from deceased patients [14], [15]. In animals, however, systematic and safe sample collection is operationally feasible if field staff are adequately trained, appropriately equipped with PPE, and supported by rapid diagnostic tools and clear referral pathways, which together can strengthen overall rabies surveillance. Recent advances, including the increasing availability of lateral flow assays (LFAs) and other rapid diagnostic kits facilitate rapid screening of rabies in field settings [16]. These tools do not replace laboratory confirmation but can substantially strengthen surveillance by enabling triage, prioritisation, and timely response. Importantly, the effective use of such technologies depends on skilled personnel and standardised procedures for sample procurement, preservation, and reporting.

In northeastern India, rabies surveillance challenges are further compounded by difficult terrain, high densities of free-roaming dogs, limited laboratory coverage, and constrained veterinary infrastructure [13], [17]. Addressing these gaps requires deliberate capacity-building initiatives that empower field veterinarians with practical, hands-on skills and a deeper understanding of their role in One Health-oriented rabies elimination.

Public-private-partnerships (PPPs) offer an effective mechanism to bridge these gaps by leveraging complementary resources, expertise, and operational capacities from government, academia, and non-governmental organisations. Such partnerships can support technical training, facilitate the use of rapid diagnostic tools, and strengthen laboratory networks to enhance field-level surveillance and promote sustainable impact. Against this backdrop, a skill-enhancement workshop was organised in Assam to strengthen the rabies surveillance architecture through a PPP approach. The workshop focused on building competencies in safe and effective sample collection, appropriate storage and transportation, and the use of rapid diagnostic kits through a collaborative and coordinated approach guided by PPP principles. Additional sessions emphasised biosafety, risk communication, data documentation, and reporting practices aligned with national and global guidelines.

By adopting a PPP approach, the capacity-enhancement workshop aimed not only to build technical skills but also to cultivate a sustained inter-institutional ecosystem for rabies control. Strengthening field-level veterinary capacity is essential for shifting rabies surveillance in India from predominantly reactive reporting to more proactive, evidence-driven detection. Equipping veterinarians with appropriate skills, tools, and intersectoral linkages can enhance sample submission, improve diagnostic confirmation, and generate reliable data to guide targeted interventions. Within this context, the workshop represents a practical step toward operationalizing the One Health approach and supporting India's progress toward the global goal of eliminating dog-mediated human rabies by 2030 (“Zero by 30”), while illustrating how coordinated collaboration among government, academia, and non-governmental organisations can strengthen rabies surveillance and control in Assam. Here we present the implementation insights generated through a PPP-led One Health oriented capacity building workshop for field veterinarians to improve rabies surveillance.

Methodology

Workshop overview

The two-day workshop was organised during 25–26 July 2024 to enhance with an aim to enhancing the sample collection and transportation skills of field veterinary professionals from for improved surveillance of dog-mediated rabies in livestock and dogs. Was organised during 25–26th July 2024. The workshop was part of the broader project “Implementing a comprehensive One Health approach to eliminate dog-mediated rabies” and was organised as a public-private partnership (PPP) led by DBT Wellcome Trust India Alliance project team from the Indian Institute of Technology Guwahati, and in collaboration with Brooke Hospital for Animals (India), Worldwide Veterinary Service (Mission Rabies), JH CreIndia Foundation, and the Directorate of Animal Husbandry & Veterinary Services, Government of Assam. The primary goal was to improve technical knowledge of veterinarians, practical skills, and understanding of rabies surveillance within a One Health framework. The workshop adopted a participatory and multisectoral approach, combining interactive lectures, hands-on demonstrations, and structured exercises. Group discussions (GDs) were used to identify and prioritize locally relevant interventions to improve dog-mediated rabies surveillance. This structure positions the workshop as both a capacity-building initiative and a self-standing qualitative research method, following the phenomenological-hermeneutic approach, which emphasises the interpretation of participants experiences and collaborative knowledge generation.

For this a One Health oriented PPP framework was used to operationalise the idea for rabies surveillance, integrating public sector with academic, non-governmental, and technical partners through the concept of Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV). This model links policy oversight, multisectoral coordination, and field-level service delivery. Pre-workshop preparation

Prior to the practical sessions, resource persons with expertise in minimally invasive brain sample collection via the foramen magnum method and in the epidemiology and burden of dog-mediated rabies in Northeast India delivered preparatory lectures to participants. A pre-workshop discussion document elaborating the objectives, expected outcomes, group activities, discussion guides and deliverables of the workshop was circulated to participants a week before the workshop (Supplementary file 1). The participating field veterinarians were nominated by the Directorate of Animal Husbandry & Veterinary Services, Assam ensuring geographic representativeness. One participant represented a local non-governmental organisation (NGO) engaged in dog population management activities.

Workshop structure and sessions

The workshop was conducted over two consecutive days at Hotel Palacio, Guwahati (Fig. 2). Day one focused on conceptual orientation, including lectures on rabies surveillance, the One Health approach, sample collection, diagnostics, and the rabies-related burden in livestock. Participants were introduced to Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM) and the World Veterinary Services data collection application. Ice-breaking exercises and interactive discussions ensured that all participants had a uniform understanding of rabies dynamics, surveillance strategies, and reporting protocols. Day two emphasised hands-on activities such as demonstrations of sample collection, safe storage and transportation of specimens. Handling of samples upon receipt in the laboratory was demonstrated, and the RRFIT test was also demonstrated. Videos illustrating proper extraction and handling techniques complemented the hands-on exercises. Sessions also addressed the wildlife-livestock-human interface to highlight potential cross-species transmission pathways.

The GD sessions followed a semi-structured format guided by key themes designed to strengthen surveillance activities for dog-mediated rabies in the region. The discussions were conducted interactively and moderated by HKT to keep them focused. Participants were divided into eight small heterogeneous groups from different districts to promote equitable participation and mitigate dominance bias. Key points were aggregated, and consensus was reached through facilitated discussion. The key summary points from discussions, observations, and individual outputs were documented as qualitative data using written notes. This approach allowed the workshop to serve as a research method, capturing the lived experiences, perspectives, and tacit knowledge of field veterinarians, in line with a phenomenological-hermeneutic framework [18].

The workshop concluded with the distribution of personal protective equipment (PPE) kits, rapid LFA diagnostic test kits, and reference materials (information brochure) to reinforce learning and ensure field applicability (Supplementary file 2).

Data analysis

Data from discussions, notes, and observations were systematically analysed using thematic content analysis. Key steps included consolidation with written notes, open coding to identify recurring themes and patterns across groups, categorisation of codes into broader thematic clusters related to sample collection, diagnostic capacity, intersectoral collaboration, and surveillance challenges, and interpretation of themes in the context of PPP-enabled interventions to enhance rabies surveillance. The analysis emphasised participants' subjective experiences, the effectiveness of PPP-driven collaboration, and insights into practical and operational barriers, thus aligning the findings with both research and policy-relevant outcomes. All authors (PM, BC, DG, HKT) were involved in the facilitation process; PM and GD carried out the thematic analysis through mutual consensus following discussion on the interpretations. Any discrepancies were resolved by HKT.

Ethical considerations

The workshop was a part of the overarching project at the Indian Institute of Technology Guwahati for skill enhancement and capacity strengthening of field veterinarians for the surveillance and control of dog-mediated rabies in livestock, other animals and wildlife. Participation in the workshop discussions was voluntary, informed consent was obtained prior to participation, and all collected data were anonymised. The project was reviewed and approved by the Institute Human Ethics Committee of IIT Guwahati (IHEC/2024/03). The study was performed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki ensuring confidentiality and participant welfare throughout the workshop. Participation in the workshop was mandated by the Directorate of Veterinary Services, Government of Assam while attendance in the discussions remained voluntary for attendees.

Surveillance priorities identified in the group discussions

The group discussion (GD) sessions provided a platform for participants to critically examine the feasibility and potential impact of various surveillance approaches for rabies control. Discussions were guided by pre-identified surveillance inputs and enabled participants to prioritize contextually relevant and actionable interventions for strengthening field-level rabies detection and management. The themes that emerged during the discussions are presented below:(a)Predisposing Factors and Transmission Sources

(a)Predisposing Factors and Transmission Sources

Predisposing Factors and Transmission Sources

It emerged from the discussions that the risk of rabies transmission in livestock is shaped by both potential exposure to wildlife in fringe areas and interactions with free-roaming dogs, underscoring the complex epidemiology of the disease at the human–animal–environment interface. Several participants noted that existing surveillance systems often overlook ecological and farm-level management factors, contributing to under-reporting of rabies risk. As one participant observed, “Many livestock farmers' dwellings are close to forest edges, and we often hear wild animals and stray dogs chasing. But no one is recording these interactions officially.” There was broad agreement that mapping wildlife–livestock interfaces and documenting livestock rearing practices could serve as important first steps for improved risk identification.(b)Monitoring Free-Roaming Dog (FRD) populations

(b)Monitoring Free-Roaming Dog (FRD) populations

Monitoring Free-Roaming Dog (FRD) populations

Another critical consideration for rabies control was monitoring FRD populations. Participants emphasised the need for reliable estimates of FRD populations to ensure adequate vaccination coverage and identifying areas with potentially high incidence. Participants elaborated on the importance of understanding dog density, movement patterns, and spatial overlap with bite incidents and livestock rabies cases. One of the participants stated, “We need to know where dogs are concentrated, their movement patterns, and link it with bite reports. Otherwise, vaccination campaigns misses the hotspots.” At the same time, concerns were raised regarding the resource intensity of routine population monitoring. The importance of sight-resight methods for FRD enumeration along with creating a database of the FRD to monitor their abundance in targeted areas was elaborated by HT. Besides photographic surveys, other practical approaches such as visual censuses, and integration of dog population data with bite and rabies surveillance were discussed as feasible alternatives.(c)Intersectoral Communication and Coordination

(c)Intersectoral Communication and Coordination

Intersectoral Communication and Coordination

A recurrent theme across groups was the need for timely and effective communication between veterinary, human health, and wildlife sectors. Participants shared experiences of delayed information flow following suspected rabies cases, which impeded coordinated response. As one field veterinary officer remarked, “Even when we detect a suspected rabies case, the information does not reach the health authorities in time.” Suggested solutions included the use of digital applications, WhatsApp-based communication channels, and automated alert systems to facilitate rapid, district-level information exchange.(d)Targeted and Sentinel Surveillance

(d)Targeted and Sentinel Surveillance

Targeted and Sentinel Surveillance

Participants expressed strong support for systematic screening of samples from deceased FRD to assess the extent of rabies circulation or identify potential disease-free areas. Risk-based surveillance in locations with high wildlife interaction was viewed as a comparatively more efficient than a uniform surveillance approach. Some participants also identified seromonitoring of vaccinated free-roaming dogs as a useful tool to assess vaccine coverage and inform the timing of repeat vaccination campaigns.(e)Data Management and Use of Technology

(e)Data Management and Use of Technology

Data Management and Use of Technology

The importance of spatial and digital tools for surveillance planning emerged as a key theme. Participants were particularly interested in understanding the potential value of GIS-based mapping, centralised databases, and basic predictive analytics for visualising patterns and prioritising interventions in relation to adequate vaccination coverage. As one veterinarian noted, “Mapping cases on GIS will help us see patterns we cannot detect by just looking at spreadsheets.” Digital reporting and real-time monitoring tools were viewed as promising options for improving response times and decision-making. These tools could potentially increase sample collection and lead to better estimate of rabies prevalence in livestock.(f)Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM) and Reporting Systems

(f)Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM) and Reporting Systems

Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM) and Reporting Systems

Discussions underscored the role of Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM) in improving coordination between field veterinarians, district surveillance officers, and public health authorities. Participants emphasised the need for rapid and reliable reporting of bite incidents, supported by mobile-based tools with paper-based backups to ensure data integrity. As one participant stated, “A simple mobile app could help us report bites instantly and coordinate with health authorities for timely PEP administration.” There was consensus that strengthening IBCM mechanisms could enhance early detection and coordinated response to rabies risk.

Discussion

This study illustrates a systems-oriented approach to strengthening surveillance for dog-mediated rabies in free-roaming dogs and livestock through capacity building of the veterinary workforce using a PPP model in Assam, India. The initiative demonstrates how context-specific training, grounded in One Health principles and operationalised through collaborative public-private engagement, can address critical surveillance gaps, enhance diagnostic preparedness, and foster intersectoral coordination. Furthermore, the implementation-focused analysis provides insight into how frontline veterinarians perceive and navigate the practical constraints of rabies surveillance, highlighting context-specific strategies that may not be visible in top-down policy frameworks. The findings highlight key structural and operational dimensions of rabies surveillance in the region and point to the central role of field veterinarians within this ecosystem. By examining veterinarians' perspectives and field-level experiences, the study identifies persistent implementation gaps, limited cross-sectoral integration, and weaknesses in monitoring and feedback mechanisms that constrain effective surveillance.

Rabies surveillance as a systemic public health approach

The structural gaps highlighted during the workshop reflect longstanding impediments similar to those reported from rest of India [19]. The discussions identified fragmented surveillance, inadequate diagnostic infrastructure, and limited coordination between veterinary and human health systems as the major gaps. Similar systemic deficiencies have been reported from other rabies-endemic regions in Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, where passive surveillance and dependence on central laboratories have resulted in delayed confirmation and substantial under-reporting of animal cases [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. The workshop addressed foundational barriers that have historically hindered animal rabies surveillance by enhancing the technical competencies of field veterinarians in collecting brain samples from suspected rabies death cases, rapid diagnostic methods, and biosafety. Hands-on training of the WOAH-recommended occipital foramen brain sampling technique and lateral flow assays (LFAs) for on-site testing represents a shift toward decentralised, field-adapted diagnostics [[25], [26]]. Experiences from comparable programs in Tanzania [23], the Philippines [[25], [26]], and Sri Lanka [27] demonstrate that enabling field-level personnel with simple, validated tools can shorten diagnostic turnaround, increase detection sensitivity, and generate data critical for local decision-making. While formal outcome evaluation was beyond the scope of this study, contemporaneous programmatic reporting by the WOAH documented an increase in animal rabies samples reaching diagnostic laboratory in Assam following decentralised capacity-building efforts, supporting the operational plausibility of such approaches. The evidence showing supports that surveillance decentralization is achievable even in resource-limited and geographically challenging settings when supported by appropriate training and technical guidance [28].

From technical skills to field epidemiological competence

Beyond laboratory and sampling proficiency, the workshop's major contribution lies in enhancing field epidemiological competence among veterinarians. Participants were exposed not only in sample collection but also in data documentation, and the integration of epidemiological information into surveillance workflows. This approach echoes findings from Kenya [22] and Thailand [29], where incorporating geographic information systems (GIS) and data analytics into rabies surveillance improved outbreak detection and optimised vaccine allocation. In Assam, where the accessibility varies widely across districts, integrating spatially informed data systems can substantially improve the precision of surveillance and the efficiency of resource deployment.

Strengthening diagnostic capacity and regional integration

Before the establishment of the Animal Rabies Diagnosis Laboratory at the College of Veterinary Science, Khanapara, animal rabies diagnosis in Assam relied primarily on clinical signs or histopathology, with confirmatory samples transported over a thousand kilometres. This process introduced high logistical costs, risks of sample degradation, and long delays, ultimately undermining timely surveillance. The satellite laboratory has since tested 193 animal brain samples from Assam and neighbouring states, with 88 (45.6%) testing positive. The observed positivity across species such as cattle (87%), dogs (65%), pigs (50%), horses (66.7%), goats (41.7%), and jackals (50%) reveals a far greater endemicity than previously recognised [28].

Comparable findings from community-level surveys in India suggest similar underestimation of the true rabies burden [30]. Hampson and colleagues (2015) estimated tens of thousands of human rabies deaths annually in dog-endemic countries, underscoring the gap between modelled and reported cases [31]. Recent national analyses for India indicate an estimated 5700 human rabies deaths per year [32], reflecting the urgent need for stronger animal surveillance to inform prevention. The Assam data also aligns with global evidence that localised diagnostic capacity substantially improves case detection and situational awareness [28].

From training to transformation: impact of capacity building

Post-training programmatic observations suggested improvements in sample submission practices and reporting quality [28]. The ability of veterinarians to perform field-based sampling and utilize LFAs for preliminary detection enhanced confidence in diagnosis and improved the timeliness of communication with human health departments. These changes are likely to contribute to reduced delays in initiating post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) and strengthened data flow between sectors. Comparable studies in Sri Lanka [33] and the Philippines [26] report that such capacity-building programs directly contribute to reduced diagnostic turnaround times and more consistent animal-human data sharing.

Importantly, these outcomes suggest that field-level empowerment, when coupled with institutional diagnostic support, may contribute to systemic improvements in surveillance sensitivity and response efficiency. Similar models applied in Tanzania have shown up to a 60% increase in confirmed rabies case reporting within one year of decentralised diagnostic training [34]. Assam's early outcomes suggest comparable potential, emphasising the value of sustained investment in local veterinary capacity [28].

Operationalizing One Health collaboration through PPPs

A defining feature of the Assam workshop was its emphasis on translating One Health principles into operational practice. By integrating veterinary, human health, and wildlife stakeholders, the workshop demonstrated a PPP model that facilitates knowledge sharing, rapid case reporting, and coordinated outbreak response. India's National Action Plan for Dog-Mediated Rabies Elimination (NAPRE) explicitly calls for multisectoral collaboration [[35], [36]]; however, implementation has often been fragmented [37]. State-level facilitation of direct communication between animal health laboratories and human health authorities through PPP mechanisms could help bridge this gap. Positive case results should be shared immediately with researcher working in the field and district health departments, enabling faster human PEP administration and community-level risk communication.

Comparable One Health pilots in Indonesia [[38], [39]] and Uganda [21] demonstrated that local intersectoral coordination enhances detection and reduces human exposure through timely interventions. The Assam approach thus operationalises One Health in practice by embedding veterinarians as frontline epidemiologists who link community, animal, and human surveillance streams. The experience also reflects a paradigm shift positioning veterinarians not merely as diagnosticians but as educators, advocates, and community-level epidemiologists.

Participatory prioritisation and public private partnership-enabled surveillance strengthening

Most rabies control initiatives in India are led by single sectors with minimal focus on surveillance mechanisms [[40], [41]]. Through this workshop, we facilitate a PPP model for shared problem-solving across government, academia, and non-governmental organisations. This approach may enhance trust, feasibility, and contextual relevance. The workshop-based group discussions enabled participatory prioritisation of surveillance interventions grounded in local operational realities. Through this process, field veterinarians collectively identified a set of interlinked surveillance priorities reflecting both technical and systemic dimensions of rabies control. These priorities included risk assessment at the wildlife-livestock, livestock-domestic dog, and domestic dog-human interfaces; understanding transmission dynamics; generation of reliable data on free-roaming dog populations and bite incidence; strengthening intersectoral communication and coordination; and implementation of targeted and sentinel surveillance approaches.

Importantly, the convergence of these themes illustrates how PPP-enabled capacity building can address multiple, interconnected layers of the surveillance system simultaneously. Technical competencies such as sample collection and diagnostics were closely intertwined with organisational functions, including data sharing, feedback mechanisms, and coordinated response across sectors. The participatory identification of priorities fostered ownership among frontline veterinarians and aligned field-level practices with institutional objectives, reinforcing the role of PPPs as facilitators of both workforce capacity strengthening and data integration.

Similar participatory and partnership-based frameworks in rabies-endemic settings in Africa and Asia have demonstrated improved adherence, sustainability, and effectiveness of surveillance programmes when frontline implementers are actively engaged in defining priorities and solutions [21]. The Assam experience reinforces this evidence, suggesting that participatory prioritisation within a PPP framework can enhance the coherence, feasibility, and acceptability of surveillance interventions, shifting rabies control efforts from reactive reporting toward more strategic, evidence-informed action.

Institutional sustainability and future directions

Institutional sustainability remains central to translating workshop gains into long-term surveillance capacity. Embedding rabies diagnostics and One Health collaboration into state-level veterinary programmes, and curricula can ensure generational continuity of competencies. Continuous mentoring, quality assurance, and refresher workshops through PPPs as demonstrated in this workshop, are essential for maintaining diagnostic standards and data reliability. Such continuous efforts would highlight the existing burden of the disease at the district or state level.

Nevertheless, persistent challenges remain. Resource constraints in logistics, cold chain maintenance, and reagent supply threaten sustainability. Integration of animal surveillance data into national systems such as the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) remains limited. Experiences from other low- and middle-income countries indicate that without sustained funding and clear governance structures, post-workshop gains may erode over time [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. The PPP workshop model must therefore be institutionalised through dedicated budget lines, standard operating procedures, training, and a formal monitoring framework that includes measurable performance indicators such as sample throughput, diagnostic turnaround time, and PEP actions undertaken.

Implications for policy and health system strengthening

The Assam PPP workshop highlights the potential of decentralised diagnostics, participatory planning, and intersectoral integration in advancing India's “Zero by 30” target. Embedding LFA-based triage and occipital foramen sampling within state surveillance protocols could substantially increase the volume and quality of animal rabies data. However, as the literature cautions, LFAs must be complemented by confirmatory testing (dFAT, dRIT, or molecular assays) to ensure diagnostic accuracy [[26], [46]]. Consistent with national policy frameworks, coordinated engagement among state veterinary services, research institutions, diagnostic laboratories, and administrative structures through PPP mechanisms may strengthen the overall surveillance architecture.

Digital data platforms linking veterinary and human health authorities represent another critical opportunity for system strengthening [[22], [47]]. GIS-based mapping and interoperable dashboards can improve transparency, accountability, and prioritisation across administrative levels [48]. Experiences from Kenya [22], Thailand [29], and Indonesia [38] illustrate how digital tools can enhance active surveillance, guide vaccination targeting, and optimise resource allocation.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, the scope of the work did not include evaluation of pre- and post-workshop competencies among participants. Similarly, changes in rabies surveillance performance could not be assessed within the timeframe and design of this study. The findings are derived from qualitative analysis of group discussions with participating veterinarians and may therefore be influenced by contextual specificities, limiting their generalisability beyond similar settings. Nonetheless, as the first workshop of its kind in the region, combining hands-on demonstration of safe sample collection and transport with structured, open discussions on rabies surveillance priorities, it generated practice-based insights that can inform future evaluations of surveillance effectiveness.

Conclusion

This study illustrates how PPP-led One Health collaborations can serve as platforms to generate field-driven insights into strengthening animal rabies surveillance. By documenting frontline perspectives, the paper contributes to practice-based evidence to inform future surveillance design in resource-limited settings.
