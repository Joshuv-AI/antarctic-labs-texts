Introduction

Zoonotic viruses and the one health imperative

Emerging infectious diseases pose an escalating threat to global health security, and the majority arise at the human–animal–environment nexus. Approximately 60% of emerging pathogens are zoonotic, and wildlife reservoirs are responsible for over 70% of these events [1]. Rapid urbanisation, environmental degradation and climate-driven disruptions are intensifying cross-species interfaces, reshaping pathogen ecology, and increasing the likelihood of pathogen spillover. Identifying reservoir species and understanding pathogen circulation within them is therefore fundamental to proactive surveillance and outbreak prevention within a One Health framework [2]. In densely populated cities, urban infrastructure, sanitation systems, wastewater networks and waste management practices shape environmental exposure pathways that govern pathogen persistence and transmission across interconnected human, animal and environmental systems. Within this context, commensal rats integrate multiple environmental compartments through their daily movement across sewer systems, surface environments and human habitation, making them valuable sentinels of urban environmental viral circulation rather than solely wildlife reservoirs.

Urban rats as synanthropic reservoir hosts

Among mammals living in close association with humans, commensal rats (Rattus norvegicus and Rattus rattus) occupy a uniquely influential ecological role. These species thrive in the anthropogenic environments created by human activity, including sewer systems, food storage networks, informal settlements, waste management sites and built infrastructure [3]. Urban rodent populations experience unique selective pressures linked to sanitation infrastructure, waste streams and human density, making findings from rural or wildlife-focused studies poorly transferable to cities [4]. Their high reproductive rates, mobility and behavioural plasticity facilitate sustained population densities in urban ecosystems worldwide [5].

Rats are known reservoirs for numerous pathogens of veterinary and public health concern, including Leptospira spp. [6], Yersinia pestis [7] and Coxiella burnetii [8].In contrast, viral infections associated with urban rats have historically received less systematic study.

Several viral pathogens detected in urban rats have established or suspected zoonotic relevance, including rat hepatitis E virus/Rocahepevirus ratti [9], and Seoul virus (SEOV) [10]. In addition, SARS-CoV-2 [11], has been investigated in rats because of its broad mammalian host range, evidence of reverse zoonotic transmission in other animal species, and the potential exposure of urban rodents to human-derived waste and contaminated environments. Compared with bacterial and parasitic pathogens, viral agents pose distinct challenges for urban surveillance due to rapid evolution, environmental persistence, and the potential for abrupt cross-species emergence [12], [13]. Moreover, studies have identified a broad diversity of additional viral families, including caliciviruses, rotaviruses, picornaviruses and poxviruses, whose relevance for humans remains unknown [14].

Growing evidence of viral spillover potential

The first confirmed human infections with rat-associated HEV, identified in 2018, highlighted the need for improved surveillance of rodent-borne viruses [15]. Similarly, SEOV outbreaks in Europe, North America and Asia illustrate the potential for spillover from commensal Rattus species [16]. Although SARS-CoV-2 has not been consistently detected in wild urban rats, its capacity to infect multiple mammalian hosts, combined with rats' exposure to human waste and sewage, reinforces the need for targeted surveillance [17], [18].

Knowledge gaps

Despite increasing concern regarding zoonotic emergence, knowledge of viral pathogens associated with commensal rats remains incomplete and uneven. Existing evidence is characterised by substantial gaps that hinder comprehensive understanding of pathogen circulation at the human–animal–environment interface.

First, the available literature is strongly shaped by geographic bias. Most studies originate from high-income regions, particularly Europe, North America and parts of East Asia, while rapidly urbanising areas in Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia and Latin America remain sparsely investigated. These regions are undergoing intense demographic growth and infrastructure expansion, conditions that may amplify opportunities for zoonotic spillover. Second, the existing evidence base is highly fragmented. Studies differ widely in trapping strategies, sample sizes, tissues analysed, diagnostic assays and reporting practices, limiting cross-study comparability and obscuring broader epidemiological patterns. Third, diagnostic capacity remains a critical constraint. Many assays used for hepatitis E virus detection were designed for human-adapted genotypes and may inadequately detect rat-associated strains, while the genetic diversity of hantaviruses and the discovery of novel rodent coronaviruses highlight the need for broader-spectrum detection approaches.

Finally, rat surveillance is rarely integrated into broader One Health monitoring systems. Few investigations link animal data with environmental sampling or human clinical surveillance, leaving key questions unanswered regarding exposure pathways, viral persistence and spillover dynamics. As a result, while individual studies provide valuable local insights, a comprehensive synthesis of rat-associated viral diversity, distribution and zoonotic potential has been lacking.

An important taxonomic distinction concerns hepatitis E viruses detected in rats. Historically, many studies used the broad term hepatitis E virus (HEV), but rat-associated HEV is now recognised as distinct from the human-associated HEV lineages most commonly implicated in classical hepatitis E. Human-associated HEV belongs to Paslahepevirus balayani, whereas rat hepatitis E virus, often referred to as rat HEV or HEV—C1, belongs to Rocahepevirus ratti. This distinction is epidemiologically important because these viruses differ in host association, genomic diversity, diagnostic detectability and zoonotic interpretation. In this review, we therefore use “rat HEV” or Rocahepevirus ratti where studies specifically investigated or genetically confirmed rat-associated HEV, and reserve “human-associated HEV” or Paslahepevirus balayani for detections explicitly assigned to human-associated lineages. Where older studies used only the generic term HEV without sufficient taxonomic resolution, we report the virus as “HEV, not further classified” and interpret such findings cautiously.

Rationale for selecting focal viral groups

This review focuses on three viral groups because they represent distinct but complementary dimensions of rat-associated viral surveillance and zoonotic risk assessment. First, hepatitis E viruses were included because commensal rats may harbour rat hepatitis E virus, commonly referred to as rat HEV or HEV—C1, as well as, more rarely, viruses related to human-associated hepatitis E virus lineages. This distinction is important because rat HEV and human-associated HEV are taxonomically and epidemiologically distinct. Human-associated HEV belongs to Paslahepevirus balayani, whereas rat HEV belongs to Rocahepevirus ratti. Consequently, detections in rats should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of circulation of classical human-associated HEV unless the original study provides sequence-based or otherwise specific taxonomic confirmation.

Second, Seoul virus was selected because it is the hantavirus most consistently associated with commensal Rattus species and is a recognised cause of haemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome in humans. Seoul virus should therefore be distinguished from the broader category of orthohantaviruses or hantavirus-like detections reported in rodent studies. In this review, SEOV detections are analysed separately where the original study specifically identified Seoul virus, whereas other hantavirus, orthohantavirus or family-level detections are collated as additional viral findings.

Third, SARS-CoV-2 was included because the COVID-19 pandemic generated targeted surveillance of synanthropic urban mammals exposed to human-derived waste, sewage and contaminated built environments. Rats are not established maintenance hosts for SARS-CoV-2, but their ecological proximity to humans and urban waste streams makes both positive and negative surveillance findings informative for assessing spillover, reverse zoonosis and environmental exposure pathways. SARS-CoV-2 was therefore included not because rats are confirmed reservoirs, but because rat surveillance provides a useful case study of how urban wildlife monitoring can be rapidly mobilised during a pandemic.

Together, these three focal groups capture different surveillance questions: a rat-adapted virus with emerging zoonotic relevance, rat HEV/Rocahepevirus ratti; a classical rat-associated zoonotic hantavirus, Seoul virus; and a pandemic virus investigated at the human–animal–environment interface, SARS-CoV-2.

Objectives of this review

Building on this rationale, this systematic review synthesises global evidence on viral pathogens detected in commensal rat populations inhabiting urban environments. Specifically, we aimed to: i) characterise the diversity, prevalence and geographic distribution of viral pathogens detected in Rattus norvegicus and Rattus rattus from urban environments; ii) distinguish, where possible, rat hepatitis E virus/Rocahepevirus ratti from human-associated hepatitis E virus/Paslahepevirus balayani, Seoul virus from other hantavirus or orthohantavirus detections, and SARS-CoV-2-positive from SARS-CoV-2-negative surveillance studies; iii) evaluate geographic, temporal and methodological patterns in the evidence base, including sample type, diagnostic method and taxonomic resolution; and iv) assess the implications of rat-associated viral circulation for human, animal and environmental health within integrated One Health surveillance systems.

By integrating these lines of evidence, this review aims to provide a structured overview of viral surveillance in urban rat populations, identify methodological and geographic gaps in the current evidence base, and support the development of standardised rat biomonitoring approaches that can complement wastewater, environmental and clinical surveillance within One Health preparedness frameworks.

Material and methods

Study design and reporting

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they investigated viral pathogens in commensal rat species (R. norvegicus and/or R. rattus) inhabiting urban environments, including cities, metropolitan areas and suburban settings. Eligible studies were required to employ virological detection methods, such as molecular, serological and/or culture-based approaches, and to report original research data on viral prevalence, viral diversity or viral genetic characterisation. Only studies published in English between 1 January 2000 and 30 June 2025 were included.

Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on laboratory-reared rats or examined rat populations from rural or wilderness environments. Publications investigating only bacterial, parasitic or fungal pathogens without a viral component were also excluded. Review articles, editorials, conference abstracts and case reports without systematic sampling were not eligible for inclusion. Studies published in languages other than English, or those lacking sufficient methodological detail to permit reliable data extraction, were also excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across five electronic databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus and WILDbase. The search strategy was developed using a combination of controlled vocabulary terms (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH] and Emtree) and free-text keywords organised into three concept groups:

Concept 1 (Target species): rat, rats, “black rat*”, “brown rat*”, “Norway rat*”, “roof rat*”, “Rattus norvegicus”, “Rattus rattus”.

Concept 2 (Pathogens): virus, viruses, viral, “zoonotic disease”, zoono*, pathogen*, “infectious agent*”, “viral infection*”.

Concept 3 (Environment): urban, city, cities, metropol*, municipal*, “built environment*”, suburb*, “human settlement*”.

Studies limited exclusively to environmental matrices, such as wastewater, sewage, soil or water, without virological data from urban Rattus spp., were outside the scope of this review.

The environmental concept was designed to identify studies of commensal rats inhabiting urban environments rather than studies of environmental matrices per se. We therefore used broad urban-context terms, including urban, city, municipal, built environment, suburban and human settlement. Terms such as sewage and wastewater were not included as independent search concepts because studies based exclusively on wastewater, sewage, soil or water samples were outside the eligibility criteria unless they also reported rat-level virological data. Nevertheless, sewer systems, wastewater networks and waste management sites were considered relevant urban habitats where rat sampling studies could be included if they met the rat-level eligibility criteria.

Study selection

All records retrieved from the database searches were imported into Mendeley reference management software, where duplicates were identified and removed. Study selection was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria. In the second phase, full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were assessed for final inclusion. Screening was performed independently by two reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Data collection process

Data extraction was performed using a standardised form developed and piloted by the review team. Two reviewers independently extracted data from each included study, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Extracted data included study characteristics (authors, year of publication, country, study design and sample size), rat population characteristics (species, capture methods and available demographic information), virological methods (diagnostic approach and target genes or antigens), viral findings (virus identity, number of positive animals, prevalence estimates and reported genetic characterisation), and methodological features relevant to interpretation of results.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes of interest were viral prevalence, defined as the proportion of infected individuals, and viral diversity, defined as the number of distinct viral taxa reported. Secondary outcomes included the geographic distribution of detected viruses and methodological characteristics that may influence viral detection, such as sampling strategy and diagnostic approach.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale adapted for cross-sectional studies of pathogen prevalence. Assessment criteria included representativeness of the sampled rat population, adequacy of sample size, appropriateness of virological methods, completeness of outcome reporting and potential for selection bias. Based on these criteria, studies were classified as having low, moderate or high risk of bias.

Data synthesis

Study characteristics and viral detection outcomes were summarised using descriptive statistics. Viral prevalence was synthesised descriptively by pathogen and geographic region. Prevalence values (percentage of positive animals) are presented with 95% confidence limits (CL95) in parentheses or as error bars in figures and were calculated using bespoke software based on the tables of Sokal and Rohlf [19] as previously described in our previous publications [20], [21], [22]. Although quantitative meta-analysis was initially considered for viral pathogens reported in multiple studies, substantial heterogeneity in study design, sampling strategies, diagnostic methods and reporting practices limited the feasibility of robust quantitative synthesis. Consequently, results are presented as descriptive summaries and crude prevalence estimates to facilitate comparison across studies.

Results

The database search identified 628 unique records, of which 70 studies met the inclusion criteria following title/abstract and full-text screening (Fig. 1). Of these, 33 studies were conducted in Europe, 22 in Asia, 11 in North America, 3 in South America, and one in the Caribbean. Several studies reported data from more than one city or region, and not all studies documented viral infection. Other viral pathogens

Twelve studies screened for viruses other than rat HEV/Rocahepevirus ratti and HEV-like viruses, SEOV and SARS-CoV-2 (Table 4). Cross-regional patterns of viral occurrence

Across continents, several viral pathogens were repeatedly detected in urban rat populations, suggesting shared global patterns of circulation. Rat hepatitis E virus/Rocahepevirus ratti, human-associated hepatitis E virus/Paslahepevirus balayani and Seoul virus were identified in all three major regions examined, Europe, Asia and the Americas, indicating widespread and sustained association with commensal Rattus species. In contrast, SARS-CoV-2 detection was geographically restricted, with evidence limited to the Americas, highlighting the importance of regional epidemiological context in shaping spillover dynamics. Other viral families, including noroviruses, rotaviruses and poxviruses, were detected in multiple regions but with highly variable prevalence and inconsistent reporting.

Methodological and temporal patterns in the evidence base

The included studies were heterogeneous in both timing and methodology. Most studies were cross-sectional or short-term surveys, while repeated longitudinal sampling was uncommon. The evidence base increased over time, with earlier studies primarily focused on targeted detection of established rat-associated viruses, particularly Seoul virus and rat hepatitis E virus/Rocahepevirus ratti, human-associated hepatitis E virus/Paslahepevirus balayani, whereas more recent studies increasingly incorporated broader molecular screening, sequencing and SARS-CoV-2 surveillance; however, true untargeted viral metagenomic approaches remained rare among the included studies.

Diagnostic approaches varied substantially between studies and pathogens. Molecular methods, including PCR, RT-PCR, conventional sequencing and, in a small number of studies, viral metagenomic analysis, were used to detect viral RNA or genomic material in tissues such as liver, lung, intestine, kidney, feces and swabs. Serological methods, including ELISA, IFA, neutralization assays and multiplex immunoassays, were used to detect evidence of exposure, most commonly in serum or blood. These approaches measure different biological endpoints and are therefore not directly comparable.

Sample type also differed markedly between studies. Hepatitis E virus-related studies most frequently analysed liver, feces or serum; Seoul virus studies commonly used lung tissue or serum; and SARS-CoV-2 studies used a mixture of oral swabs, rectal or faecal samples, lung, intestine, blood or serum. These differences may influence detection probability and interpretation of apparent prevalence.

The methodological patterns identified in the Results directly affect interpretation of the observed geographic and pathogen-specific differences. Several limitations were recurrent across the evidence base. These included small sample sizes in some studies, inconsistent reporting of rat species and trapping context, variable taxonomic resolution of viral detections, differences between serological and molecular endpoints, and strong geographic clustering of surveillance in Europe, North America and parts of East Asia. These factors limited the feasibility of quantitative meta-analysis and required a descriptive synthesis.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesises global evidence on viral pathogens circulating in commensal rat populations in urban environments across Europe, Asia and the Americas. Collectively, the findings demonstrate that R. norvegicus and R. rattus host a diverse array of viral agents, including viruses with established or suspected zoonotic potential. Beyond documenting pathogen presence, this synthesis highlights pronounced regional contrasts in viral circulation and exposes substantial gaps in surveillance coverage and methodological harmonisation, which together constrain robust assessment of zoonotic risk in urban ecosystems.

A central finding of this review is that apparent differences in viral prevalence between countries and regions cannot be interpreted independently of study design. The included studies differed substantially in trapping context, sample size, rat species composition, tissue or sample type, and diagnostic approach. Molecular assays applied to liver, lung, feces or swabs indicate detection of viral RNA or genomic material in a specific biological compartment, whereas serological assays detect evidence of exposure and may persist beyond active infection. Consequently, the values reported in this review should be interpreted as study-specific detection proportions rather than directly comparable estimates of infection prevalence. This distinction is particularly important for viruses such as rat HEV and Seoul virus, for which studies used both molecular and serological approaches.

The widespread detection of hepatitis E virus across all assessed regions reinforces the role of urban rats as important reservoirs of rat-associated HEV lineages. Marked differences in apparent prevalence between regions are likely driven by a combination of ecological factors, sanitation infrastructure, rat population density and study design, rather than reflecting true absence or presence alone. Notably, the consistently high prevalence reported in some Asian and North American urban settings suggests sustained viral maintenance within rat populations. Diagnostic limitations remain a critical concern, as many assays were originally developed for human-adapted HEV genotypes and may inadequately detect genetically divergent rat strains [90]. Consequently, current prevalence estimates probably underestimate the true burden of HEV in rat populations, particularly in regions where molecular characterisation remains limited. Earlier studies frequently used broad HEV terminology or assays developed for human-associated HEV genotypes, making retrospective taxonomic assignment difficult. Therefore, apparent HEV prevalence in rats should not be interpreted as prevalence of classical human-associated HEV unless the original study specifically confirmed Paslahepevirus balayani or related human-associated lineages. Most rat detections in this review are better interpreted as rat HEV/Rocahepevirus ratti or HEV-like viruses associated with Rattus hosts.

Seoul virus was detected on all three continents, but with distinct epidemiological patterns. Moderate prevalence and substantial within-country variability in Europe contrast with evidence of more entrenched circulation in parts of Asia and highly heterogeneous patterns in the Americas [10]. These differences likely reflect regional variation in rat ecology, urban infrastructure and human–rat contact rates, as well as historical patterns of viral introduction and persistence. Given that SEOV accounts for a substantial proportion of human haemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome globally, its repeated detection in urban rat populations across continents underscores the need for sustained, geographically expanded surveillance rather than reactive, outbreak-driven investigation [91].

The detection of hantavirus or orthohantavirus-related signals other than SEOV in studies of urban rats requires cautious interpretation. Hantaviruses often show strong, although not absolute, associations with reservoir hosts, and SEOV remains the hantavirus most consistently linked to commensal Rattus species. Therefore, reports of non-SEOV hantavirus detections or serological reactivity in Rattus spp. should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that urban rats are competent maintenance reservoirs for these viruses. Such findings may reflect spillover infection, transient exposure, assay cross-reactivity, limited taxonomic resolution, co-circulation of multiple rodent species in the sampled environment, or incomplete host identification. In this review, SEOV-positive studies were therefore analysed separately, whereas broader hantavirus, orthohantavirus or serological findings were retained as additional detections requiring confirmatory molecular characterisation. This distinction is particularly important for One Health risk assessment, because public health relevance depends not only on detection but also on host competence, viral shedding, persistence within the rat population and opportunities for human exposure.

The recent Andes virus outbreak associated with an international cruise ship illustrates the public health impact that hantavirus events can have in travel-related and multinational exposure settings [92]. However, Andes virus is not a Rattus-associated hantavirus and should not be conflated with SEOV ecology. Rather, this event reinforces the broader need for hantavirus preparedness, accurate reservoir attribution and integrated One Health surveillance across human, animal and environmental interfaces.

In contrast to rat hepatitis E virus/Rocahepevirus ratti, human-associated hepatitis E virus/Paslahepevirus balayani and SEOV, SARS-CoV-2 exhibited pronounced regional heterogeneity. The absence of detectable infection or seroconversion in European rat populations suggests that rats have not acted as a significant maintenance host in these settings to date [18]. However, evidence of infection in rats from the Americas indicates that spillover can occur under specific epidemiological conditions, likely linked to high levels of human transmission and environmental contamination [81]. These observations are consistent with the broad host range and evolutionary plasticity of SARS-CoV-2 and highlight the importance of continued monitoring at the human–animal–environment interface [93], [94], [95], particularly as new variants emerge. Well-designed negative studies are therefore essential for accurate surveillance interpretation and should be consistently reported to avoid misinterpretation of geographic data gaps.

Beyond the focal viruses, this review documents a broad and complex rat-associated virome. While many detected viruses currently lack confirmed zoonotic relevance, their repeated identification across diverse urban settings indicates that commensal rats host viral communities far more extensive than those typically considered in public health surveillance. The high viral diversity reported in Asian studies and in the Caribbean, even when based on limited sample sizes, highlights the potential for substantial undiscovered viral diversity [6], [65], [70]. Because only a small minority of included studies used untargeted viral metagenomic approaches, expanded application of metagenomic and genomic surveillance will be essential to characterise this virome more comprehensively and to identify viruses with spillover potential before human disease emergence [96].

A consistent theme across continents is the uneven distribution of research effort. Surveillance remains concentrated in a limited number of high-income countries, while rapidly urbanising regions, where ecological disruption and infrastructure strain may amplify spillover risk, remain underrepresented. Even within well-studied regions, investigations are often short-term, pathogen-specific and methodologically heterogeneous [97]. This fragmentation limits comparability, complicates synthesis and hampers the development of evidence-based risk assessments. Importantly, the absence of reported infection in many settings should not be interpreted as evidence of absence.

Urban rats occupy ecological niches that place them at the centre of human–animal–environment interactions [16]. Their routine movement through sewer systems, waste networks and human habitation makes them both reservoirs and sentinels of environmental pathogen circulation. Integrating rat biomonitoring into One Health surveillance frameworks, alongside wastewater-based epidemiology, environmental sampling and clinical reporting, offers a practical opportunity to strengthen early warning systems, identify hotspots of transmission risk and inform targeted urban health interventions [98]. Given their ubiquity and demonstrated capacity to harbour zoonotic viruses, commensal rats represent a scalable surveillance target within municipal One Health systems. The findings also indicate that rat biomonitoring should not be interpreted as a standalone measure of urban zoonotic risk. Rather, rat surveillance can provide a complementary signal alongside wastewater monitoring, environmental sampling, vector and wildlife surveillance, and human clinical data. This integrated approach is especially important in cities, where rats move between sewers, waste sites, food distribution networks and human dwellings. However, the current evidence base remains too fragmented to define universal prevalence thresholds for public health action. Standardised protocols, repeated sampling and transparent reporting of sample type, diagnostic method and taxonomic resolution are therefore essential for future surveillance systems.

This review has several limitations inherent to its design and the available literature. Restriction to English-language publications may have excluded relevant studies, particularly from underrepresented regions. Substantial heterogeneity in study methodologies, including sampling strategies, diagnostic platforms and reporting standards, limited direct comparability between studies and constrained quantitative synthesis for several viral pathogens. In addition, the focus on commensal rats in urban environments means that findings may not reflect viral circulation in peri-urban or rural settings, or in other rodent species that may also contribute to pathogen maintenance and spillover dynamics.

Taken together, evidence from Europe, Asia and the Americas indicates that urban rats harbour diverse viral communities, including pathogens of clear relevance to human health. However, surveillance remains fragmented, geographically uneven and methodologically inconsistent. Strengthening routine rat biomonitoring, improving diagnostic standardisation and embedding rodent surveillance within integrated One Health systems will be essential to enhance early warning capacity and mitigate zoonotic risk in an increasingly urbanised world.

Conclusions

This systematic review demonstrates that commensal rats inhabiting urban centres across Europe, Asia and the Americas harbour a diverse range of viral pathogens, including hepatitis E virus and Seoul virus, with prevalence levels that in some regions reach clear public health significance. The detection of SARS-CoV-2 in rat populations in the Americas, despite its absence in Europe, further illustrates that rat–human viral exchange is feasible under specific epidemiological conditions. Collectively, these findings position urban rats as important sentinels of environmental viral circulation and highlight their relevance to zoonotic risk assessment at the human–animal–environment interface.

Across continents, surveillance of rat-associated viruses remains fragmented, geographically uneven and frequently limited to short-term, city-specific investigations. Many major urban centres, particularly in rapidly growing regions of Asia and Latin America, lack systematic monitoring, creating preventable blind spots in global preparedness. Methodological heterogeneity in sampling design, diagnostic approaches and reporting standards further restricts cross-study comparability and hampers early detection of emerging threats.

To address these gaps, rat-based biomonitoring should be formally embedded within national and municipal One Health preparedness frameworks. Priority actions include routine and standardised sampling of rats in high-risk urban environments (such as sewer systems, ports, food distribution corridors and informal settlements), integration of animal surveillance with wastewater monitoring, environmental viral sequencing and clinical reporting, development of rat-adapted diagnostic assays for HEV and hantaviruses, and establishment of open data pipelines enabling rapid cross-sectoral data sharing.

Investing in coordinated rodent surveillance will transform an underutilised urban health signal into an actionable early-warning system, strengthening resilience to future zoonotic threats in an increasingly urbanised world.
