Effectiveness of a multimodal intervention for mothers of newborns hospitalized in the intensive care unit to increase exclusive breastfeeding
Authors: Hernán Manotas, Socorro Moreno, Fabian Gil, Juan Gabriel Piñeros, María Fernanda Roa, Carolina Prieto, Melissa Méndez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, only 40% of newborns admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) are exclusively breastfed, a figure that decreases to approximately 20% in low- and middle-income countries. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of a multimodal intervention on exclusive breastfeeding at NICU discharge and to assess changes in parental stress, breastfeeding self-efficacy, breastfeeding knowledge, and risk of postpartum depression among mothers during their newborns’ hospitalization.MethodsWe conducted a quasi-experimental before-and-after intervention study among mothers of newborns admitted to the NICU of a tertiary care hospital in Bogotá, Colombia. The primary outcome was exclusive breastfeeding at discharge. Secondary outcomes included parental stress, breastfeeding self-efficacy, breastfeeding knowledge, and risk of postpartum depression. The intervention combined parental education, structured support from healthcare providers, and a mobile application delivering breastfeeding-related educational content. Propensity score matching was used to control for potential confounders, and conditional logistic regression was applied to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for the primary outcome. Paired odds ratios (admission–discharge) and Becker’s d coefficient were calculated for secondary outcomes.ResultsNewborns whose mothers received the intervention had substantially higher odds of being discharged with exclusive breastfeeding compared with those whose mothers did not receive the intervention (adjusted OR [aOR] = 10.35; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 4.95–21.7; p < 0.001). In the no-intervention group, the risk of postpartum depression increased significantly during hospitalization (paired OR = 9.4; 95% CI: 3.7–23.6;p < 0.001), whereas a significant reduction was observed in the intervention group (paired OR = 0.04; 95% CI: 0.005–0.29; p < 0.001). No statistically significant changes in parental stress were observed in either group (no intervention: OR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.07–1.65; p = 0.29; intervention: OR = 0.73; 95% CI: 0.30–1.80; p = 0.64). Breastfeeding self-efficacy declined significantly in the absence of intervention (OR = 0.23; 95% CI: 0.11–0.49, p < 0.001) and improved significantly among mothers who received the intervention (OR = 2.73; 95% CI: 1.36–5.42; p = 0.002). Effect sizes for breastfeeding knowledge were − 0.84 in the no-intervention group and 0.65 in the intervention group.ConclusionsThe multimodal intervention was associated with significantly higher odds of exclusive breastfeeding at NICU discharge. Additionally, the intervention reduced the risk of postpartum depression and improved breastfeeding self-efficacy and knowledge among mothers.

Introduction

Globally, only 40% of term and preterm newborns admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) are exclusively breastfed, and this proportion decreases to approximately 20% in developing countries [1]. In Colombia, only about 8% of term and preterm newborns discharged from NICUs receive exclusive breastfeeding [2].

Breast milk provides multiple benefits to term newborns, including protection against respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, reduced mortality, and improved intellectual capacity during childhood and adolescence [3]. In preterm newborns, breastfeeding is associated with a lower incidence of conditions such as retinopathy of prematurity, neonatal sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis, and osteopenia of prematurity [4]. During hospitalization, it provides an essential physical and emotional bond between mother and newborn, promoting attachment and reducing the risk of long-term neurological disorders [5]. Several factors limit exclusive breastfeeding in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), including mother-infant separation after admission, challenges in maintaining breastfeeding, and poor communication between mothers and healthcare providers [1, 4].

Despite these well- established benefits, evidence on effective multimodal interventions that support exclusive breastfeeding among mothers of newborns hospitalized in the NICU remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop and implement a multimodal intervention to increase breast milk feeding and to evaluate its effect on exclusive breastfeeding at discharge among mothers of newborns hospitalized in the NICU. In addition, changes in parental stress, breastfeeding self-efficacy, breastfeeding knowledge, and risk of postpartum depression were assessed in mothers between the newborn’s admission to and discharge from the NICU.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a quasi-experimental, before-and-after study between February 1 and December 31, 2022 (Fig. 1), in the NICU of the University Hospital in Bogotá, Colombia. This is a private, tertiary-level hospital whose NICU allows continuous parental presence, enabling mothers or fathers to accompany their hospitalized newborns 24 h a day. The unit has 23 beds for neonatal care and admits approximately 200 to 250 newborns annually.

In this NICU, during 2021, internal statistics indicated that only 5% of newborns were discharged with exclusive breastfeeding, according to medical record data, despite the availability of support from a breastfeeding support team—composed of nurses with expertise in lactation—for all mothers of hospitalized newborns, as well as the availability of the kangaroo mother care method, a strategy that promotes exclusive breastfeeding in preterm infants [6, 7].

The cohort that did not receive the intervention was recruited between February 1 and July 31, 2022. After the end of control-group recruitment, a predefined 15-day washout period was implemented, during which no new participants were enrolled and all mother–newborn dyads from the control group completed their NICU stay and were discharged before the intervention was introduced. The cohort that received the intervention was subsequently recruited between August 16 and December 31, 2022. Therefore, there was no temporal coexistence of control and intervention dyads within the NICU, minimizing the risk of contamination between groups.

Participants

Mother-newborn pairs were included in the study if the mother could accompany her newborn daily and consented to participate. A consecutive sample of all eligible mother–newborn dyads admitted to the NICU during the predefined study periods was included. Although the NICU admits both term and preterm infants, this study included all newborns meeting inclusion criteria, reflecting the real-world composition of our unit. The study excluded mothers whose native language was not Spanish, newborns with life-threatening diagnoses, those conceived through surrogacy, mothers with a history of breast cancer, multiple pregnancies, or those who had contraindications to breastfeeding such as HIV infection, hepatitis B, active tuberculosis, or uncontrolled hyperthyroidism. Also, dyads with fewer than three days of NICU stay were excluded. Follow-up occurred from the time of admission to the NICU until discharge.

Multimodal intervention

The multimodal intervention consisted of three main components:

Information sharing and parent education through in-person and virtual sessions, which were held individually or in groups, lasting 30 min during the first three days of hospitalization. These sessions were designed to support mothers breastfeeding in the NICU, providing information on the importance of breastfeeding and milk extraction and storage techniques.Support from healthcare personnel: The entire NICU healthcare team participated in three informational training sessions, conducted individually or in groups and lasting up to 30 min, aimed at promoting the importance of human milk feeding. Trained staff subsequently provided brief educational interactions to mothers at least once during hospitalization, with a minimum duration of three minutes, adapted to the NICU context. These interactions focused on conveying essential information and strengthening the mother–staff relationship; interpersonal variability in information delivery was not formally assessed.Mobile App: A mobile app called “ Prematury” was developed to collect sociodemographic and clinical data from mother–newborn dyads and to assess parental stress, breastfeeding self-efficacy, risk of postpartum depression, and breastfeeding knowledge using validated instruments at study entry and discharge in both the intervention and control groups. In the multimodal intervention group, the application included educational videos on breast milk and colostrum expression and storage techniques, additional resources to engage mothers, and a function for recording daily milk extractions. This interactive tool provided mothers with motivation and education through videos and daily reminders, facilitating breast milk expression and enabling them to maintain milk production and stimulation even when newborns could not feed orally due to hemodynamic instability or other medical conditions.

Usage of the mobile application was monitored throughout the study. All mothers in the intervention group had access to all three components of the multimodal intervention: educational sessions, support from healthcare personnel, and use of the mobile application.

Control group

Mothers in the non-intervention group had access to the mobile application to record clinical and demographic characteristics and complete the respective assessment scales. In addition, they received routine care provided in the NICU, which included standard breastfeeding support (e.g., general guidance from nursing staff, assistance with milk expression when required, and access to institutional breastfeeding promotion practices according to usual care protocols).

Variables and data collection

We collected demographic and clinical data on mothers and newborns, including newborn sex, birth weight, and gestational age; length of stay in the NICU; maternal age, education level, body mass index, marital status, religion, and chronic hypertension; mode of delivery; completion of prenatal classes; history of exclusive breastfeeding in previous children; socioeconomic status; and maternal health insurance affiliation (type of health insurance or social security coverage). Additional variables included place of birth, number of live births, and the number and volume of breast milk extractions and feedings. Mothers completed the questionnaires using a mobile application, and any questions were resolved by a research assistant.

According to the World Health Organization criteria, exclusive breastfeeding was defined as exclusively breast milk, with no other liquids or solids, not even water [8]. It was measured through medical records from study entry to NICU discharge. Exclusive breastfeeding at exit from the NICU was assessed considering 24 h before discharge. In this study, ‘exclusive breastfeeding’ was defined as exclusive human milk feeding, regardless of delivery method (bottle, syringe, ordirect latch), consistent with institutional practice and some public health guidelines in settings prioritizing human milk over feeding mode.

Parental stress was measured using the Parental Stress Scale developed by Miles and Funk, which has been validated in Spanish-speaking populations [9]. Breastfeeding self-efficacy was assessed using the adapted version of the Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale–Short Form (BSES-SF), an instrument with validation studies conducted in the Colombian population [10].

Knowledge about breastfeeding in the NICU was assessed using a test created specifically for this study. This test consisted of a nine-item multiple-choice questionnaire. Participants who scored eight or more correct answers were classified as having adequate knowledge, and those with seven or fewer correct answers were classified as having inadequate knowledge. Finally, The risk of postpartum depression was assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), which has been validated in Colombia [11].

Parental stress, breastfeeding self-efficacy, risk of postpartum depression, and breastfeeding knowledge scales were self-administered at study entry and NICU discharge in both the intervention and non-intervention groups.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Comité de Ética en Investigación, Fundación Santa Fe de Bogotá, Colombia) under approval number CCEI-14114-2022, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated a priori using Epidat 3.1, assuming 80% statistical power, a 95% confidence level, and an expected difference in proportions of 15%. Based on an estimated baseline exclusive breastfeeding rate of 5% and accounting for a potential 10% loss to follow-up, the final required sample size was 162 mother–newborn dyads.

The measure of effect of the intervention was an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) derived from conditional logistic regression models. We implemented a propensity score matching (PSM) approach to control for confounding factors. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression, modeling the log odds of receiving the intervention as a function of observed baseline covariates. These included maternal age, mode of delivery, maternal educational level, weight for gestational age, attendance to prenatal courses, risk of postpartum depression, breastfeeding knowledge, parental stress, and breastfeeding self-efficacy at NICU admission.

Matching was initially performed using the nearest neighbor method in a 1:1 ratio; however, adequate matching between the intervention and control groups was not achieved. As an alternative, the optimal matching method was applied in a 1:1 ratio, ensuring adequate balance between the groups and successfully matching all cases (Fig. 2). The balance of covariates before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), calculated with the bal.tab function from the Cobalt package. Covariates with SMD values below 0.1 were considered balanced, in this study, all variables met this criterion. Subsequently, logistic regression models used the propensity scores were fitted to estimate the effect of the intervention.

Distribution of the common support of propensity scores in the two study groups

For categorical secondary outcomes (parental stress, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and maternal risk of postpartum depression), paired classifications at admission and discharge were used to construct within-group contingency tables. Changes over time were assessed by identifying concordant and discordant pairs. Crude paired odds ratios were calculated exclusively from discordant pairs and defined as the ratio between improvement and worsening, according to the directionality of each outcome. Statistical significance of within-group changes was evaluated using McNemar’s exact test. Differential changes over time between the intervention and no-intervention groups were subsequently examined by comparing paired odds ratios across groups.

Group means, and standard deviations were calculated at both time points for the continuous variable of breastfeeding knowledge. Within-group changes were quantified using Becker’s effect size (d), comparing the magnitude of change between the intervention and control cohorts.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 2023), [12] utilizing the MatchIt (version 2011) [13] and Cobalt (version 2023) [14] packages for matching procedures and balance diagnostics.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study included 162 mother–newborn dyads, 81 in the non-intervention group and 81 in the multimodal intervention group. None of the participants were lost to follow-up.

Most mothers were from middle to high socioeconomic backgrounds and held university degrees. The median maternal age was 35 years (range 21–46). Most newborns were full-term and delivered via cesarean section. No cases of extremely preterm or post-term infants were identified (Table 1).

Primary outcome: exclusive breastfeeding at discharge

Mothers who received the multimodal intervention had significantly higher odds of exclusively breastfeeding their newborns at NICU discharge compared with those who did not receive the intervention (crude OR = 14.2; 95% CI: 6.78–33.1; p < 0.001). After adjustment for potential confounders using propensity score matching, the association remained statistically significant, with substantially higher odds of exclusive breastfeeding at discharge in the intervention group (adjusted OR [aOR] = 10.35; 95% CI: 4.95–21.7; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

In the non-intervention group, the baseline assessment showed a risk of postpartum depression in 23 mothers (28%), parental stress in 8 (10%), breastfeeding self-efficacy in 46 (57%), and adequate breastfeeding knowledge in 38 (47%). At discharge, the risk of postpartum depression was observed in 65 (80%), parental stress in 4 (5%), breastfeeding self-efficacy was present in 19 (23.5%), and adequate breastfeeding knowledge in 32 (39.5%).

In the intervention group, the baseline assessment showed a risk of postpartum depression in 23 mothers (28%), parental stress in 13 (16%), breastfeeding self-efficacy in 37 (46%), and adequate breastfeeding knowledge in 60 (74%). At discharge, the risk of postpartum depression was found in 1 (1%), parental stress in 10 (12%), breastfeeding self-efficacy in 56 (69%), and adequate breastfeeding knowledge in 78 (96%).

In the control group, postpartum depression risk worsened significantly during hospitalization, with a predominance of discordant pairs showing deterioration from admission to discharge (paired OR = 9.4; 95% CI: 3.7–23.6; p < 0.001). In contrast, the intervention group showed a significant improvement over time, with substantially more mothers improving than worsening (paired OR = 0.04; 95% CI: 0.005–0.29; p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this quasi-experimental study, mothers who received a multimodal intervention during their newborn’s hospitalization in the NICU had substantially higher odds of exclusive breastfeeding at discharge compared with mothers who did not receive the intervention. In addition, favorable within-group changes were observed in maternal risk of postpartum depression, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and breastfeeding knowledge in the intervention group, while less favorable or null changes were observed in the non-intervention group. These findings should be interpreted as associations, given the observational nature of the study, and the magnitude of effect should be considered in light of the quasi-experimental design and the potential for residual confounding despite propensity score matching.

The observed association between the multimodal intervention and higher exclusive breastfeeding rates at NICU discharge is consistent with previous studies showing that combined strategies—including maternal education, staff engagement, and digital health tools—are more effective than single-component interventions. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported that educational and support-based interventions delivered during NICU hospitalization can improve breastfeeding initiation and continuation, particularly when interventions are tailored to maternal needs and reinforced by healthcare professionals [15–18]. Our findings extend this evidence to a middle-income country setting with historically low baseline rates of exclusive breastfeeding at NICU discharge.

Regarding maternal mental health outcomes, the intervention group demonstrated a marked reduction in postpartum depression risk over time, whereas the non-intervention group showed significant deterioration during hospitalization. These findings are consistent with prior literature indicating that enhanced parental support, education, and engagement in infant care during NICU admission are associated with improved maternal psychological outcomes [19]. Although causal inference cannot be established given the study design, the contrasting trajectories between groups suggest that structured support during hospitalization may contribute to better maternal mental health. The marked increase in depression risk observed in the non-intervention group may reflect the cumulative psychological burden associated with prolonged NICU hospitalization and maternal–infant separation.

Breastfeeding self-efficacy showed divergent patterns between groups, declining in the non-intervention group and improving significantly in the intervention group. This observation is consistent with evidence that confidence in breastfeeding is sensitive to the availability of education, reinforcement, and professional support, particularly in stressful environments such as the NICU. Mobile-based educational tools and repeated interactions with trained staff may help mothers develop and sustain confidence in their ability to provide breast milk, even when direct breastfeeding is initially limited [17, 20].

Parental stress did not change significantly over time in either group. This finding differs from some reports suggesting that targeted NICU interventions reduce parental stress, but may reflect characteristics of the study population, including relatively high educational and socioeconomic levels and the absence of neonatal mortality in both groups. These contextual factors may have mitigated stress independently of the intervention [21, 22].

Breastfeeding knowledge improved in the intervention group but declined modestly in the non-intervention group. The decrease observed among mothers who did not receive the intervention may reflect a lack of reinforcement during hospitalization rather than true loss of knowledge. Additionally, some variability in measured knowledge may be attributable to limitations of the study-specific questionnaire, which is acknowledged in the Methods Sect. [23].

The study was conducted in a tertiary-level NICU within a middle-income country health system. Therefore, results may be most applicable to similar neonatal care settings with comparable resource availability, clinical protocols, and breastfeeding support practices.

This study demonstrates that implementing a multimodal strategy in a NICU setting without an established breastfeeding promotion program is feasible and may be associated with substantial improvements in exclusive breastfeeding at discharge and related maternal outcomes. Given that the population consisted largely of term and late-preterm newborns, these findings may be most applicable to level III NICUs with mixed neonatal populations. This model may be particularly relevant for middle-income settings where resource constraints limit availability of individualized lactation support.

These findings have relevant implications for neonatal clinical practice and breastfeeding promotion policies, particularly in middle-income settings where NICU resources and structured breastfeeding support programs may be limited. The intervention evaluated in this study could be feasibly implemented in similar hospital contexts to improve breastfeeding outcomes among infants. This model may be particularly relevant in settings where resource constraints limit availability of individualized lactation support. These findings may inform scalable breastfeeding support models in resource-constrained NICU settings globally.

Limitations

The main limitations are the lack of random assignment of the intervention and the lack of temporal overlap between groups, which may lead to selection bias and confounding. Although propensity score matching was used to make the intervention and non-intervention groups comparable on factors affecting breastfeeding to achieve the exchangeability necessary for causal inference, unmeasured confounding variables, unassessed changes in maternal behavior, or other factors coincident with the introduction of the multimodal intervention could have influenced the results. Although propensity score matching reduced observable baseline differences between groups, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables such as maternal breastfeeding motivation, family support, or prior breastfeeding experience cannot be completely ruled out. Future randomized studies would help strengthen causal inference. Furthermore, our definition includes bottle-fed human milk, which may overestimate true EBF rates but reflects pragmatic clinical realities in many NICUs. Future research should evaluate the scalability and cost-effectiveness of this intervention across different neonatal care levels and health system contexts.

Conclusion

In this quasi-experimental observational study, a multimodal intervention delivered during NICU hospitalization was associated with higher exclusive breastfeeding at discharge, along with favorable changes in maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy and postpartum depression risk. These findings suggest that structured, multimodal breastfeeding support implemented during hospitalization may be a valuable strategy to support mothers of high-risk newborns. Further studies using randomized or prospective designs are needed to confirm these associations and to evaluate their sustainability after discharge.
