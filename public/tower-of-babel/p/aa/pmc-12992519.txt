Deviation From AI-Predicted Implant Sizes in Total Hip Arthroplasty Is Associated With Increased Complication Risk
Authors: Michael P Murphy, Andrew M Schneider, Cameron J Killen, Daniel R Schmitt, Nicholas M Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient characteristics may predict implant sizes in total hip arthroplasty (THA), but the clinical value and generalizability of such models remain uncertain. This study evaluated the accuracy and relevance of an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven prediction tool for THA sizing in a diverse population.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 2410 primary THAs at 2 academic hospitals (2007-2021). Data included patient characteristics, surgical approach, surgeon fellowship training, and complications (periprosthetic femur fracture [PPF], aseptic loosening within 1 year). An AI model predicted femoral stem and acetabular cup sizes using age, sex, height, weight, and race/ethnicity, and these predictions were compared with implanted sizes. In a subset, predictions were compared to traditional radiographic templating. Multivariate analyses assessed associations between size deviation and complications.ResultsAI-predicted femoral stems and cups were within one size of implanted components in 72.0% and 77.7% of cases, and within 2 sizes in 91.2% and 94.7%, respectively. Excluding complex cases (dysplasia, avascular necrosis, head collapse, conversion THA) improved accuracy (P < .0001). In subgroup analysis, AI predictions outperformed radiographic templating for stems (mean deviation 0.731 vs 1.179; P < .0001) and cups (0.673 vs 1.071; P = .036). Arthroplasty-trained surgeons implanted components significantly closer to AI-predicted sizes (P < .001). Closer alignment with AI-predicted stem size was associated with reduced aseptic loosening and PPF (P < .0001).ConclusionsA free AI-assisted prediction tool (Mortho) demonstrated strong accuracy and generalizability for implant sizing in primary THA, particularly in standard cases. Predictions mirrored arthroplasty-trained surgeons’ selections and outperformed radiographic templating, supporting patient characteristic-based AI as a valuable adjunct for THA planning.Keywords: Total hip arthroplasty, Aseptic loosening, Periprosthetic fracture, Predicting implant size

Introduction

Accurate templating in total hip arthroplasty (THA) is important for streamlining operating room efficiency [1] as well as for avoiding complications [2]. Appropriate execution of the preoperative plan minimizes complications such as leg length discrepancy, failure to restore offset, femoral fracture, instability, and allows for cost savings [3,4]. Meanwhile, recent evidence supports that template-directed instrumentation may additionally decrease operating room time by over 10%, minimize surface area for contamination, and decrease instrumentation costs by over 35% [5]. While alternatives in templating including with EOS (Eos Imaging Inc, Cambridge, MA) and computed tomography have shown improved accuracy in templating [6,7], radiographic templating has remained the standard due to its accessibility, cost, and limited radiation [8,9]. In an era of value-based health care and steadily decreasing reimbursement, delivery of effective patient care in an accurate, timely, and affordable manner will prove paramount [[10], [11], [12]].

As a novel, accurate, and timely solution, patient characteristics, including demographic (age, sex, race/ethnicity) and anthropometric measures (height and weight), have shown promising results in predicting total knee arthroplasty (TKA) component sizes. At no added cost or radiation exposure, studies have shown that these patient-specific inputs can predict TKA sizes within 1 size between 79.3% and 91.6% of the time [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Naturally, when these equations were applied to a new patient population unique from the population used to create the models, they all decreased in accuracy [17]. Thus, when these models are proposed, it becomes critical to assess their generalizability.

Recently, these patient factors were shown to predict THA component size within 1 size 79% and 78% of the time for the femoral stem and acetabular cup, respectively [18]. Templating for THA has shown to be less accurate when compared to TKA templating, and predictions based on patient characteristics appear to follow a similar trend [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Among contributing factors, the complexity in THA preoperative planning has shown to be related to the unique contour of the femoral stem among stem designs, the presence of cysts, osteophytes, differing patient bone quality, dysplasia, and presence of additional hardware, and the issues with templating a 3-dimensional implant on 2-dimensional anteroposterior radiographs. [19,20]. The accuracy of THA size predictions based on patient characteristics will naturally depend on these factors.

Thus, this study aims to evaluate the accuracy and generalizability of component size predictions generated by a specific artificial intelligence (AI)-based tool using patient characteristics in THA. The primary outcome was the accuracy of AI-predicted femoral stem and acetabular cup sizes compared with the sizes implanted intraoperatively. The secondary outcomes included the association between deviation from the AI-predicted size and the risk of major complications, specifically acute periprosthetic fracture and aseptic loosening requiring surgical intervention. By examining the clinical consequences of implanting component sizes that deviate from AI-generated predictions, this study seeks to inform appropriate use of patient-characteristic-based AI tools in preoperative planning, optimal patient selection, and the potential consequences of deviation from predicted sizes in diverse clinical scenarios.

Material and methods

After approval from the institutional review board, we identified a consecutive series of 2410 primary THAs performed between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2021, across 2 academic hospitals. To ensure independent data points, only the first procedure was included for patients who underwent bilateral THA during the study period (n = 105). Only primary THAs utilizing cementless, press-fit femoral stems were included (see Table 1); cemented stems (n = 65) were excluded from the analysis. Patient characteristics were collected for each patient, including age, gender, height, weight, body mass index, ethnicity, race, and laterality. Implant data included manufacturer, prosthesis design, and component size. Complications recorded were periprosthetic fracture within 6 weeks of surgery and aseptic loosening at any time point. Aseptic loosening was defined as revision surgery performed for clinically diagnostic femoral stem subsidence, as documented in the operative report.

Mortho is a free phone and website application that predicts implant sizes for total hip, total knee, and partial knee arthroplasty using these patient characteristics alone or in combination with preoperative imaging. The application’s initial predictions are derived from crowd-sourced data, while AI is used to continuously refine these predictions based on user feedback, thereby enhancing accuracy over time. While specific algorithmic details are proprietary, the model was developed using approaches similar to previously published prediction models in arthroplasty, which link implanted component sizes to patient characteristics in large retrospective datasets [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. For the purposes of this study, however, the algorithm was operated offline to maintain patient confidentiality of those involved in this study and to prevent adaptive learning during evaluation. The algorithm’s predicted acetabular cup and femoral stem sizes were then compared with the actual sizes implanted.

As part of institutional protocol, radiographic templating was performed for all cases, primarily by trainees under attending supervision. However, due to the retrospective nature of the study and variability in documentation, radiographic templating records were retrievable for only a subset of cases. In these cases, a secondary analysis was performed to directly compare the accuracy of radiographically templated component sizes with patient-characteristic-predicted sizes for both the femoral stem and acetabular cup. For each method, mean deviation from the implanted size was calculated and compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Standard deviation (SD) and interquartile range (IQR) are also reported where relevant. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and variability in record keeping, this analysis was limited to cases with documented templating data.

We assessed several variables for their potential effect on the accuracy of the AI-predicted sizes based on patient characteristics. Operative reports were reviewed for surgical indications including avascular necrosis (AVN), dysplasia, and conversion THA. Preoperative radiographs were evaluated for femoral head collapse, defined as Ficat stage IV [21]. Both the training background of the operating surgeon (fellowship-trained in adult reconstruction or not) and the surgical approach (anterior or posterior) were recorded for each case. The association of each with the risk of periprosthetic fracture or aseptic loosening was evaluated. Because there was significant overlap (confounding) between surgical approach and surgeon fellowship training status, both variables were first included in the multivariate model to assess their relative contributions to complication risk. To account for this confounding, the variable with the lower predictive value was removed first, allowing assessment of the independent effect of the remaining variable.

The relationship between the difference in implanted vs model-predicted component size and complication rates was analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the predicted and actual intraoperative femoral and acetabular component sizes, while categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test. A multivariate generalized linear model was used to assess the association between relevant covariates including preoperative diagnosis, surgeon, demographic and anthropometric characteristics, implant type, and component size with the risk of complications. Covariate selection was performed using backward stepwise elimination, with variables exhibiting a P value greater than 0.1 sequentially removed until only statistically significant predictors remained in the final model.

A priori power analysis indicated that a minimum of 787 patients would be required to achieve 80% power (type I error = 0.05) to detect a small effect size (f 2 = 0.01) in the generalized linear model. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS, version 26 (Armonk, NY), with significance set at P < .05.

Study population

The mean age of the patient population was 63.3 years (SD: 12.2 years), 55.9% were female, mean height was 66.8 (SD: 4.2) inches, mean weight was 195.5 (SD: 51.3) pounds, and 51.6% of hips were performed on the right side. The mean follow-up interval was 3.4 ± 3.1 years.

A total of 114 complications required surgical intervention during the study period. Of these, 37 (1.6%) involved either subsidence within 1 year or acute periprosthetic fracture (PPF) of the femoral stem or acetabular cup. Specifically, there were 5 cases (0.2%) of femoral stem subsidence or aseptic loosening, 6 cases (0.3%) of acetabular cup aseptic loosening, 24 cases (1.0%) of intraoperative or acute (<6 weeks) low-energy periprosthetic femur fractures, and 2 cases (0.1%) of intraoperative periprosthetic acetabular fractures. Analysis of periprosthetic femur fractures excluded 2 cases (0.1%) of greater trochanteric fractures, as these were assumed to be unrelated to implant size. Of the 24 acute femur fractures, 12 (0.5%) were intraoperative fractures managed with cable fixation without stem revision, 2 (0.09%) were acute postoperative fractures treated with cables, and 8 (0.3%) were acute postoperative fractures requiring stem exchange.

Of the 37 major complication cases, 29 (78.4%) occurred in patients who underwent the anterior approach, while 8 (21.6%) occurred in those with the posterior approach. Most anterior approach procedures were performed by fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons, resulting in confounding between approach and training status. In multivariate analysis, arthroplasty training status was marginally more predictive of periprosthetic fracture risk than surgical approach.

Results

The accuracy of AI-predicted implant sizes for the full cohort was within 1 size of the implanted component in 72.0% of femoral stems and 77.7% of acetabular cups, and within 2 sizes in 91.2% and 94.7%, respectively. The mean absolute error for femoral stem size prediction in the full cohort was 1.11 sizes.

When the analysis was restricted to cases performed by arthroplasty-trained surgeons, prediction accuracy was significantly higher than in cases performed by non-arthroplasty-trained surgeons (P < .001). Similarly, exclusion of cases involving conversion THA, dysplasia, AVN, or femoral head collapse resulted in a significant improvement in prediction accuracy compared with the full cohort (P < .0001). In this restricted cohort (n = 1711; 74.2% of cases), mean absolute error improved to 1.08 sizes, with accuracy increasing to 75.1% and 79.6% within 1 size and 93.8% and 95.5% within 2 sizes for femoral stems and acetabular cups, respectively (Figs. 1 and 2, Tables 2 and 3).

In a subgroup of 592 cases with available radiographic templating data, AI-predicted sizes were significantly more accurate than radiographic templating for both femoral stems (mean deviation 0.731 vs 1.179 sizes; P < .0001) and acetabular cups (0.673 vs 1.071 sizes; P = .036).

Association between prediction deviation and complications

Deviation of the implanted femoral stem size from the AI-predicted size was strongly associated with an increased risk of aseptic loosening or acute periprosthetic femur fracture (P < .0001). This association remained significant after adjustment for patient characteristics, surgeon training status, surgical approach, and preoperative diagnosis (P < .0001). In contrast, deviation from the AI-predicted acetabular cup size was not associated with acetabular complications (P = .803).

Among the 37 cases of major complications, the direction of deviation from the predicted component size was closely associated with the type of complication observed (Fig. 3). In 34 of 37 cases (91.9%), implanting a component smaller than the predicted size (undersizing) was associated with aseptic loosening or subsidence, whereas implanting a component larger than the predicted size (oversizing) was associated with periprosthetic fracture. This pattern was observed in all acetabular cup complication cases. The 3 exceptions included 2 periprosthetic fractures following postoperative falls and 1 case of aseptic loosening in which the implanted femoral stem size matched the predicted size.

In multivariate analysis, deviation from the AI-predicted femoral stem size was the strongest independent predictor of major complications. No patient characteristic, including age, sex, or body mass index, remained independently predictive after adjustment.

Discussion

Several patient factors have shown to be related to the accuracy of preoperative planning [19,20]. This study aimed to guide surgeons in patient selection for AI-based implant size prediction in THA. The results demonstrate improved prediction accuracy when implant sizing was performed by an arthroplasty-trained surgeon. Additionally, the results support that patients having an indication of AVN, having femoral head collapse, or involving a conversion THA involved the implantation of a larger femoral stem. Similarly, femoral head collapse and dysplasia were statistically related toward implanting a smaller acetabular cup, with femoral head collapse and dysplasia cases also being most associated with acetabular cup acute periprosthetic fracture or loosening. Meanwhile, conversion THA was associated with implantation of larger acetabular cup sizes. Exclusion of these cases resulted in a statistically significant improvement in prediction accuracy, as measured by absolute sizing error and a threshold of ±1 and ±2 implant sizes (P < .0001).

When the AI-based prediction model was applied to an independent patient cohort, prediction accuracy was modestly lower than that reported in the development dataset. In prior work, predicted component sizes were within 1 size in 79.1% of femoral stems and 78.2% of acetabular cups, and within 2 sizes in 94.3% and 94.6% of cases, respectively [18]. In contrast, the present study found that, in an outside patient series, accuracy within 1 size was 72.0% and 77.7%, and within 2 sizes was 91.2% and 94.7%, respectively. A similar decrease in accuracy when applying predictive models to external populations has also been observed in TKA studies [17]. These findings underscore the importance of validating predictive models on independent datasets and suggest that future approaches may benefit from algorithms that can securely learn and adapt to new institutional data to optimize accuracy.

As a supporting factor for AI-predicted sizes, these results do not depend on radiographic technique. Namely, there exists supporting evidence that presence and placement of the calibration marker ball correlate with templating accuracy [22,23]. In cases of improper placement or absence of a calibration marker, a standard magnification may be applied with varying accuracy [20]. In this study, patient-characteristic-based predictions reliably estimated component sizes independent of radiographic technique.

In the present study, smaller deviations between the implanted femoral stem size and the patient-characteristic-based prediction were associated with lower odds of acute periprosthetic femur fracture or aseptic loosening requiring surgical intervention (P < .0001). The relation between implanting a relatively larger femoral stem and increased acute periprosthetic femur fracture has been shown [24,25]. The surgeon must balance this with the risk of aseptic loosening. The results of the present study support the work of Bergin et al., where it was observed that patients with larger femoral stem sizes and lower bone to stem ratios had improved implant survival related to aseptic loosening [26]. Given this complex relationship, the findings of this study suggest that patient-characteristic-based size predictions may serve as a useful adjunct to surgeon judgment in THA.

Furthermore, this study identified that cases involving femoral head collapse and dysplasia were statistically associated with the use of smaller acetabular cups, as well as a higher risk of acute periprosthetic fracture or loosening of the acetabular component. This likely reflects the challenging native anatomy in these patients. In developmental dysplasia of the hip, the acetabulum is often shallow, underdeveloped, and exhibits deficient bone stock, particularly in the superolateral region [27]. Likewise, femoral head collapse can result in altered acetabular morphology and compromised bone quality, further complicating cup placement and fixation [28]. In such cases, medialization of the acetabular cup has been described as a technique to achieve better bone coverage and initial stability, with recommendations to avoid oversizing the cup [29]. However, excessive medialization can lead to complications such as protrusio acetabuli and altered hip biomechanics, and therefore must be approached with caution.

In a post hoc analysis, distal engaging femoral stem designs were associated with higher odds of periprosthetic fracture or aseptic loosening (P < .001). However, this analysis included a limited number of stem designs and was not a primary objective of the study. Given potential patient selection bias and heterogeneity in stem design features, this finding should be interpreted with caution.

The same size association was not identified for the acetabular cup (P = .803). Rather, acute periprosthetic fracture or aseptic loosening of the acetabular cup was most associated with femoral head collapse (P = .003) or dysplasia (P = .069). These results are supported by current literature, showing an association with dysplasia and decreased consistency in cup [30] and femoral head collapse associated with an increased proportion of aseptic loosening [31]. Furthermore, acetabular cup design was not associated with loosening or fracture (all P > .517).

This study has several limitations. The overall complication rate was relatively low, which limited power for certain subgroup analyses, although similar complication rates have been reported in prior arthroplasty series [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. The analysis included a limited number of femoral stem designs, and findings may not be generalizable to other implant systems. Component size predictions were compared with the implanted component size, assuming the implanted size was optimal; postoperative imaging was not used to independently assess oversizing or undersizing. Additionally, this study was retrospective and conducted at two academic centers within a single geographic region, which may limit external generalizability. The subgroup analysis comparing radiographic templating with AI-based prediction was constrained by the retrospective and non-consecutive availability of templating data, with templating often performed by trainees, and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, confounding between surgical approach and surgeon fellowship training status, along with low event rates, limits conclusions regarding approach-specific complication risk. Prospective studies are warranted to further evaluate causality and validate these findings across broader patient populations.

Conclusions

The results of this study report on the accuracy of patient-characteristic-based THA sizes in a patient cohort unique from that which it was derived from. This study supports improved accuracy when applied to patients without diagnosis of dysplasia, femoral head collapse, AVN, involving conversion THA, and with improved accuracy if performed by arthroplasty-trained surgeons. In this retrospective analysis, closer alignment between the implanted femoral stem size and the patient-characteristic-based prediction was associated with a lower rate of major complications.
