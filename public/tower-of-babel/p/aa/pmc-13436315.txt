Co-application of biochar and cow manure enhances growth, yield and soil chemical properties under spinach production
Authors: Cosmas Wacal, Ronald Buwa, Jackson Nyakahuma, Patrick Kirungi, Thaddeo Kahigwa Tibasiima, Jacob Kaingo, Deogratius Luyima, Joseph Okello, Geoffrey Levuason Candia, Bruce Robin Nyamweha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeclining soil fertility remains a major constraint to vegetable production in tropical regions, necessitating sustainable and efficient nutrient management strategies. This study evaluated the effects of biochar, cow manure and their co-application on growth, yield and soil chemical properties of spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) under field conditions in western Uganda. A field experiment conducted in 2025 tested four treatments: control, biochar alone (50 t ha−1), cow manure (20 t ha−1), and co-application of biochar (25 t ha−1) and cow manure (10 t ha−1), arranged in a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD). ResultsResults indicate that the co-application of biochar and manure produced the tallest plants (45.6 cm), approximately 120% taller than the control (20.7 cm), and increased plant height by 35.7% compared to biochar alone. Similarly, the highest yield (22.5 t ha−1) was obtained under the co-application of biochar and manure. Specifically, the co-application of biochar and manure significantly increased spinach yield by 78% compared to biochar alone, while a non-significant increase of 31.1% was observed relative to manure alone. Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed strong positive associations among growth and yield parameters and soil total N, K, EC and pH. ConclusionOverall, the co-application of biochar and manure enhanced spinach productivity and soil fertility in tropical systems.

Introduction

Soil fertility decline evidenced by limited nutrient availability is still a major constraint to vegetable production in many tropical regions including Uganda [1]. Sustainable soil amendment strategies are therefore needed to improve soil chemical properties, enhance crop growth and increase yields while reducing the over reliance on synthetic fertilizers [2]. Biochar, a carbon rich product obtained from the pyrolysis of organic biomass under limited oxygen conditions, has emerged as a promising soil amendment due to its stable carbon, high porosity, and strong nutrient retention capacity [3]. Based on this, biochar can improve soil pH, enhance water holding capacity, increase cation exchange capacity (CEC), and promote nutrient retention, consequently leading to enhanced soil fertility and crop performance in degraded or nutrient deficient soils [2, 4].

Despite these benefits, the effectiveness of biochar as a standalone amendment can be limited by its relatively low nutrient content especially nitrogen (N) immediately after application although this depends on the feedstock used for its preparation [5]. Consequently, co-application with organic amendments such as manure has been proposed as a strategy to harness synergistic benefits that include nutrient supply from organic manure combined with the structural and chemical enhancement from biochar [6]. This integrated approach can therefore improve soil nutrient availability, microbial and overall soil health more effectively than either amendment alone [7].

Several studies have demonstrated the positive effects of combined biochar and organic amendments on soil and crop performance [4, 8–10]. For instance, a field experiment in which maize cob biochar in combination with farmyard manure was applied, the co-application of 10 t ha− 1 biochar and 5 t ha− 1 farmyard manure produced the highest cucumber yield compared to sole applications [8]. The authors reported that the co-application increased cucumber yield by 60.5% compared to the control, exceeding sole biochar and manure by 27.7% and 20.3%, respectively. Moreover, the yield gain was associated with improved soil fertility, notably higher total N (0.36%) and enhanced phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) availability due to better nutrient supply, retention, and amelioration of acidic soil conditions. Furthermore, another study has shown that co-application of neem-based biochar with poultry manure at a rate of 2 t ha− 1 biochar with 15 t ha− 1 manure significantly increased the yield of cucumber plants by 45.8% in the co-application (20.8 t ha− 1) compared to the control (14.27 t ha− 1) [10]. This suggests that co-application of biochar and poultry manure could serve as a sustainable means of crop production especially in cucumber. The authors concluded that co-application of biochar and manure in cucumber production is an efficient nutrient management strategy that enhances cleaner production by promoting carbon sequestration and reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, thereby contributing to climate change mitigation. In another study, co-application of 50 t ha− 1 of biochar and poultry manure at 5 t ha− 1 significantly increased the root weight of radish by 192% while biochar alone increased by 25% and poultry manure by 47% compared to the control [11]. The authors also reported that co-application of biochar and poultry manure improved soil pH, organic matter (OM), nitrogen (N), phosphorous (P), exchangeable cations potassium (K), calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg). Thus, the co-application of biochar and manure could be beneficial for soil fertility enhancement.

Moreover, Zafar-ul-Hye et al. [9] reported that co-application of 0.5% sugarcane bagasse biochar and 0.5% farmyard manure significantly outperformed the sole application of these amendments in terms of maize growth, yield and biomass improvement. The authors observed an increase in grain yield by 20% in the co-application of 0.5% sugarcane bagasse biochar with 0.5% farmyard manure compared to the control, outperforming sole application of biochar (7.81%) and sole manure (4.69%). The increase in growth and yield was attributed to improved soil chemical properties such as CEC, porosity and nutrient retention under biochar and farmyard manure treatment compared to the control.

More still, co-application of biochar at a rate of 10 t ha− 1 with organic manure at 7.5 t ha− 1 increased the dry matter and grain yield of rice by 69% and 88% compared to the control (un-amended), and above their sole applications [7]. The authors observed that co-application of biochar with manure increased total N and available P by 34% and 26% respectively compared to the control (un-amended). In addition, there was significant increase in the soil pH, organic carbon and exchangeable potassium (K) under co-application of biochar and manure. Similarly, Hameeda et al. [12] found that co-application of biochar at 5 t ha− 1 with manure at 10 t ha− 1 significantly increased the fruit yield of tomato as well as improving P and N use efficienciesand soil pH. Overall, these studies underscore the potential of co-application of biochar and manure to ameliorate soil chemical constraints and enhance growth and yield of vegetables under challenging soil environment. Moreover, the beneficial effects of co-application of biochar and manure could offer a viable pathway for producing climate-smart soil amendments that supports sustainable intensification.

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) is a widely consumed leafy vegetable valued for its high nutritional quality and short growth cycle [13]. However, spinach production in many tropical soils is limited by poor fertility affecting its potential yield [1, 14]. Whereas the beneficial effects of biochar and manure on soil and crop responses have been established for crops like maize, rice and cucumber [8, 10], few studies have specifically examined the combined effects of biochar and cow manure on soil properties and spinach growth and yield, particularly under unique soil and climatic conditions of Uganda. This represents a significant knowledge gap given the importance of leafy vegetables in local diets and the potential for organic soil amendments to support sustainable production.

Therefore, this study aimed to address this research gap by assessing the effects of co-application of biochar and cow manure on soil chemical properties and the growth and yield of spinach. The findings will provide valuable insights into sustainable soil fertility management practices for tropical vegetable production systems.

Materials and methods

Study area

The field experiment was carried out during the months of March and May of 2025 (one season) at the Faculty of Agriculture and Environmental Sciences of Mountains of the Moon University (0°41’28.9"N 30°14’56.4"E, altitude; 930 m above sea level). The land at this farm was at least three years under fallow prior to the experiment. The soil at the experimental site is dominantly Andosols according to the FAO classification system. The initial soil chemical properties were characterized by soil pH of 5.8, EC of 0.65 dSm− 1, N of 0.12%, P of 17.1 mg kg− 1 and K of 6.92 cmol kg− 1. This area generally experiences a bimodal rainfall pattern with one peak during April–May and the other in August–October although these weather patterns have changed over the years due to climate change. The average annual rainfall in this area varies between 1000 and 1400 mm with average minimum and maximum temperatures of 22.5 °C and 25.5 °C respectively.

Plant materials

The spinach variety used in this study was “Fordhook Giant”, a commonly cultivated variety in Uganda. The seeds were supplied by East African Seed Company Limited (EA SEED), purchased from a registered agro-input store located in Fort Portal, Uganda. Prior to the field establishment, the seeds were sown in a nursery bed in a greenhouse at the Faculty of Agriculture and Environmental Sciences, Mountains of the Moon University, where they were raised under standard nursery management practices. The seedlings were maintained for four weeks and transplanted into the experimental plots at the stage of four fully developed true leaves, ensuring uniform and healthy planting materials for the experiment.

Establishment of the field experiment

The experiment was conducted using biochar from maize stovers, which was selected because of its high abundance on farms after maize harvest in this area [15]. The biochar was prepared using a small-scale traditional open pit method [2]. Cow manure was obtained from Agroecology farm of Mountains of the Moon University. The cow manure was decomposed for three months before use in the experiment. The nutrient composition of biochar and cow manure is indicated in Table 1.

The experiment was conducted using a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) to evaluate the effects of biochar and cow manure on spinach growth and yield. The experimental layout was established across a slope, and divided into three blocks. Each block contained four plots measuring 1 m by 2 m, each separated by a 1-meter buffer to minimize inter-block interference. The treatments, including a control (T1), which received neither biochar nor manure, biochar at 50 t ha− 1 (T2), cow manure at 20 t ha− 1 (T3), and a combination of biochar (25 t ha− 1) with cow manure (10 t ha− 1) (T4), were randomly assigned to the plots. The biochar and cow manure were uniformly spread on each plot after preparing a fine tilth and then manually mixed to 15 cm depth using a hand hoe.

Spinach seedlings were then transplanted at a spacing of 45 cm between rows and 45 cm between plants, providing adequate space for growth. All plants were kept well-watered to avoid transplanting shock and enhance seedling establishment. To mitigate soil erosion, particularly important in the slopy area of the field, erosion control bunds were established along the lower edges of the garden. Weeding commenced three weeks after transplanting to eliminate competing vegetation and ensure optimal resource availability for the spinach plants. Thereafter, regular weeding was maintained throughout the growing season to minimize competition and promote healthy spinach growth.

Growth and yield analysis

At 90 days after transplanting, plant height, number of leaves per plant, leaf area, leaf length, leaf width, and root length were recorded from 10 selected plants in each plot. Briefly, plant height was considered as a vertical measurement from the base of the stem at the soil surface to the tip of the highest point of the spinach plants. This measurement was achieved by the aid of a flexible tape measure. Number of leaves was determined by manually counting all fully expanded leaves on each selected plant at the time of data collection. All senescent leaves were excluded from the count. On the other hand, leaf length and width were measured on the fully expanded leaf of each selected plant using a tape measure. The leaf length was recorded from the base of the leaf blade to the tip of the leaf along the midrib. Leaf width was measured at the widest portion of the leaf blade.

Leaf area was estimated by multiplying the measured leaf length and width and then adjusting using a correction factor of 0.75 to account for the natural shape of the leaf blade [16].\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{Leaf area}\left(\mathrm{cm}^{2}\right)=\text{Leaf length}\times\text{leaf width}\times{0.75}$$\end{document}

Stem girth was measured manually using a vernier caliper at a consistent position of 2 cm above the soil surface to ensure uniformity across all sampled plants. The caliper jaws were gently closed around the stem without compressing the tissues, and readings were recorded in millimeters (mm) before conversion to cm.

The root length was determined at harvest by carefully excavating the plants and loosening the surrounding soil to minimize mechanical damage to the root system. The uprooted plants were gently washed under running water to remove adhering soil particles without breaking the root system, and the distance was carefully recorded. All measurements were recorded in centimeters (cm).

Spinach yield was determined by harvesting and weighing the marketable leaves from ten (10) randomly selected plants per plot using a digital weighing balance. The fresh weight obtained per plant was recorded in kilograms and summed to obtain the total yield for the sampled plants. The average yield per plant was extrapolated to a per-hectare basis. Yield was subsequently expressed in tons per hectare (t ha− 1).

Soil sampling and analysis

At harvest time, rhizosphere soil samples were collected using an auger to a depth of 15 cm. The samples were air-dried, ground, and passed through a 2 mm sieve to retain only the fine earth fraction. All laboratory analyses were conducted following standard procedures described by Okalebo et al. [17]. Soil potential of hydrogen (pH) and electrical conductivity (EC) were measured in a 1:2.5 (w/v) soil: water ratio using a pH meter (Model pH 700 m, Eutech Instruments, Singapore) and EC meter (Model Meter HQ40d, Hach Co., UK) respectively. Soil total N was determined using the micro-Kjeldahl digestion method followed by distillation and titration of the ammonia liberated. The exchangeable K was extracted in a 1 N ammonium acetate (pH 7.1) solution and then analyzed using a flame photometer (Model; Jenway PFP7, Cole-Parmer Co., USA). Soil available P was extracted using the Bray 1 solution, and P was measured by the ammonium molybdate–ascorbic acid method using a spectrophotometer (Model UV-6300 PC, VWR International Co., USA).

Statistical analysis

All data collected on growth, yield and soil chemical properties were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using IBM SPSS statistics (version 25.0) to examine the differences among treatments. Mean separation was performed using Tukey’s HSD at a significance level of p < 0.05 to determine significant differences among treatment means. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to explain the total data variability with respect to correlations among growth, yield and soil chemical properties under different amendments.

Results

Effect of biochar and cow manure on the growth parameters of spinach

The one-way ANOVA analysis revealed a highly significant treatment effect on spinach plant height (p < 0.001), indicating that the growth performance of spinach varied considerably depending on whether the plants received biochar, cow manure, their combination, or no amendment (Fig. 1). The tallest plants were observed under the co-application of biochar and cow manure, with a mean height of 45.6 cm, which was approximately 120% taller than plants in the control plots (20.7 cm). Cow manure alone followed closely with a mean height of 39.2 cm, representing about 89% increase over the control, while biochar alone produced plants averaging 33.6 cm, which was still 62% taller than the control. Overall, the results show that co-application of biochar and cow manure increased plant height by 35.7% compared to biochar alone and 16.2% compared to the manure alone although there was no significant difference between combined application of biochar and manure and manure alone.

The cow manure treatment produced the longest leaves (31.4 cm), closely followed by the combined biochar + cow manure treatment (30.5 cm). Furthermore, the leaf area of spinach was significantly influenced by the different soil amendment treatment. The highest leaf areas were observed under cow manure (302.20 cm2) and the combined biochar + cow manure treatment (288.08 cm2), which were statistically similar and significantly greater than the control and biochar alone. Similarly, the biochar + cow manure treatment had the longest roots (13.3 cm), followed closely by cow manure alone (12.4 cm), biochar alone (10.2 cm), and the control (7.84 cm).

Effect of biochar and cow manure on the yield parameters of spinach

Results show that the biochar + cow manure treatment had the highest fresh weight (301 g per plant), followed by cow manure alone at 229 g per plant, biochar at 168 g per plant, whreas the control recorded only 49.4 g per plant (Fig. 2). This represents a more than six-fold increase in fresh biomass between the control and the best treatment.

Furthermore, spinach yield was significantly influenced by the different treatments (Fig. 3). The control plots, which received no amendments produced lowest yield at 3.7 t ha− 1, significantly lower than all other treatments (p < 0.05). Application of biochar alone increased yield to 12.6 t ha− 1, representing a 3.4-fold increase over the control, while cow manure alone resulted in a yield of 17.2 t ha− 1, a 4.6-fold increase compared to the control. Soil analysis results demonstrated a statistically significant trend in nutrient contents following the application of different treatments. Soil total N was significantly increased by cow manure (0.325%) compared to the control while no significant difference was observed between biochar and control. In addition, there was no significant difference among co-application of biochar with manure (0.304%) and cow manure alone (0.325%). Relationship among growth, yield and soil chemical properties under spinach as influenced by biochar and cow manure

The principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear relationships among treatments, soil chemical properties, and spinach growth and yield attributes (Fig. 4). The first two principal components explained a substantial proportion of the total availability in the dataset, with PC1 accounting for 64.98% and PC2 accounting for 11.97%, giving a cumulative explanation of approximately 76.95% of the total variation.

Fig. 4Principal component analysis (PCA) showing relationship among growth, yield and soil chemical properties under spinach production

Principal component analysis (PCA) showing relationship among growth, yield and soil chemical properties under spinach production

On the variable biplot, plant growth and yield related parameters such as plant height, leaf weight, leaf length, leaf number, stem girth, root length, leaf area, and yield were strongly and positively associated with PC1, indicating that these traits contributed most to the variation in spinach performance. Soil chemical properties such as N, K, electrical conductivity (EC), and pH also loaded positively on PC1. On the other hand, P showed a weaker (opposite loading) relative to the growth parameters, indicating a comparatively lower contribution to variation under the conditions in this experiment.

The treatment-based PCA clustering clearly separated the different soil amendments. The biochar-only treatment clustered on the positive side of PC2, indicating a strong association with improved vegetative growth parameters. The co-application of biochar and cow manure treatment clustered on the positive side of PC1, showing a strong relationship with yield-related traits and nutrient availability. The cow manure only treatment grouped separately, moderately associated with growth variables but less strongly than co-application or biochar-only treatment. On the other hand, the control treatment clustered on the negative side of PC1 and PC2.

Overall, the PCA indicates that the organic amendments, particularly co-application of biochar and cow manure positively influenced the growth and yield of spinach.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that biochar, cow manure and their co-application significantly enhanced growth and yield parameters, including plant height, leaf number, leaf area, stem girth, root length and plant weight (Figs. 1 and 2, Tables 2 and 3). More specifically, the combined application of biochar and manure significantly increased plant height and yield compared to the control and biochar alone. These observations are attributed to the beneficial effects of biochar-manure integration in improving soil nutrient availability and overall soil fertility as evidenced by increase in soil N and K (Table 3). These findings are consistent with numerous studies reporting synergistic and beneficial effects of biochar-manure combinations on vegetable growth [7, 10, 11, 18]. Moreover, the superior performance observed under the co-application treatment could be explained by complementary and mechanistically linked functions of biochar and cow manure in soil systems [7, 19]. Cow manure supplies readily mineralizable organic matter and essential nutrients such as N, P, K and micronutrient that directly support rapid vegetative growth and leaf expansion. This explains the strong response observed in leaf number, leaf length, and leaf width under manure-only treatments (Table 2). Similar responses have been reported in leafy vegetables where manure increased chlorophyll content photosynthetic capacity and biomass accumulation through improved nitrogen availability [20, 21].

Biochar on the other hand, contributes primarily through physical, chemical and biological soil conditioning mechanisms rather than immediate nutrient supply. Its higly porous structure enhances soil aeration, water-holding capacity, and root penetration, which explains the significant increase in root length and stem girth observed in this study [22]. However, in the present study, the significant increase in root length was more strongly associated with manure application, while the biochar-only treatment did not differ significantly from the control (Table 2). This suggests that nutrient availability from manure play a more direct role in stimulating root elongation, whereas biochar likely contributed indirectly through improvement in soil physical properties. Improved root systems allow plants to explore a larger soil volume for nutrients and water, supporting sustained growth. Similar improvements in root architecture following biochar application have been widely reported [23]. Improvement in the rooting systems of spinach because of organic amendment application allows enhanced absorption of nutrients. Therefore, the overall improvement in vegetative growth parameters directly translate into higher photosynthetic capacity, better canopy development, and improved marketable yield for leafy vegetables such as spinach. These improvements imply higher productivity per unit area and enhanced income potential without heavy reliance on synthetic fertilizers [24].

Furthermore, spinach yield responded mostly strongly to the co-application of biochar and cow manure, increasing yield by 78% compared to biochar alone. Similar yield increments due to co-application of biochar and manure have been reported in lettuce, maize and tomatoes when biochar is combined with other organic amendments [12, 25–27]. In this study, the substantial yield improvement could be associated with enhanced vegetative growth, particularly the significant increase in plant height observed under the combined treatment. Research indicates that in leafy vegetables such as spinach, yield largely depends on shoot development, thus improvements in growth parameters directly translate into higher marketable biomass [28]. Additionally, the co-application also significantly increased leaf number, leaf length, leaf width, and leaf area compared to biochar alone, resulting in broader and well-expanded leaves with greater photosynthetic surface area, a plausible explanation for the yield increment. Moreover, enhanced light interception could have promoted higher assimilate accumulation in the leaves, leading to increased plant weight at harvest [29]. The study also reveals that root growth was improved, as indicated by increased root length, although differences were not statistically significant compared to sole biochar or cow manure. Nevertheless, improved root development by biochar and cow manure likely enhanced nutrient uptake, supporting vigorous shoot growth and maximizing yield [30]. Moreover, the strong relationship between the growth and yield parameters is clearly illustrated by the PCA (Fig. 4), which shows strong positive correlations among plant height, leaf traits, plant weight, and yield, confirming that enhanced vegetative growth under co-application directly contributed to increased spinach productivity.

The increase in spinach yield could also be attributed to the synergistic effects of biochar and manure [31, 32]. This synergy arises because biochar acts as a nutrient retaining and stabilizing matrix for manure-derived nutrients. Biochar surfaces possess a high cation exchange capacity and functional groups capable of adsorbing ammonium, potassium, calcium, and organic compounds released during manure mineralization [33]. This adsorption reduces nutrient losses through leaching and volatilization while allow gradual desorption synchronized with plant demand [34–36]. Additionally, biochar serves as a habitat for soil microorganisms due to its porous structure and large surface area [37]. Therefore, when combined with cow manure, the biochar becomes charged implying pre-loading its pores with nutrients and microbial substrates, transforming it into a slow-release nutrient carrier. Due to the stimulated microbial biomass and enzymatic activities within the charged biochar, nutrient mineralization, nitrification, and phosphorous solubilization are enhanced, thereby improving nutrient availability throughout crop growth period. The strong association between yield traits and soil N, K, EC and pH in the PCA analysis supports this mechanism. Moreover, the yield gains observed suggest that co-application of biochar and manure can function as high efficiency organic fertilizers, capable of matching or surpassing conventional inputs under tropical conditions. This has direct implications for sustainable intensification, especially in smallholder systems where synthetic fertilizer access is limited.

Although soil pH differences among treatments were not statistically significant, a consistent trend towards slightly higher pH values under biochar and manure treatments was observed. This aligns with previous findings showing that biochar buffers soil acidity through the presence of alkaline ash components and surface functional groups [38, 39]. Maintaining soil pH near neutrality (6.2–6.3) is agronomically important because it maximizes nutrient availability and microbial activity in most tropical soils. On the other hand, the EC increased mostly under amended treatment but remained within non-saline, and agronomically safe ranges. Additionally, the elevated EC under biochar and manure reflects increased levels of highly soluble minerals in the biochar used rather than salinity stress [22]. This is also congruent with the findings reported by Agegnehu et al. [24] who attributed moderate increase in soil EC under organic amendments to improved nutrient supply rather than salinization. In terms of physiology, spinach is moderately sensitive to salinity, particularly during early stages of growth. Therefore, the maintenance of EC within safe thresholds indicates that nutrient enrichment occurred without exceeding the osmotic tolerance of the crop.

Overall, the moderate increase in EC was however, within safe thresholds implying that the co-application of biochar and cow manure can safely increase soil nutrient intensity while maintaining osmotic balance in the root zone.

The study also shows that soil total N increased significantly under treatments containing cow manure. Both manure alone and the combined biochar and manure treatment significantly increased soil total N compared to the control, whereas biochar applied alone did not differ significantly from the control (Table 3). Research indicates that biochar prepared from maize applied at a rate 30 t ha− 1 significantly increased N [40]. This improvement in N availability could be linked to the N content in the biochar itself and the nutrient retention capacity of the biochar. On the other hand, cow manure is known to be rich in N, especially ammoniacal N [41]. The co-application of the biochar with manure treatments therefore confers the benefits of manure being a direct N source and biochar as a nitrogen retainer. Conversely, the slightly lower N content in the co-application treatment compared to manure alone likely reflects enhanced plant uptake rather than reduced availability, as supported by higher yield (biomass production) of spinach. Although the uptake of N by spinach was not determined in the present study, it could be hypothesized that its intake was enhanced by the co-application of biochar and cow manure. Nitrogen is one of the most important macronutrient required for growth and development of leafy vegetables since it is directly involved in chlorophyll formation as well as in the process of photosynthesis and protein synthesis [42, 43]. Therefore, its enhanced uptake implies improved physiological processes consequently increasing spinach growth and spinach yield.

Exchangeable K on the other hand, was markedly higher under biochar alone and the co-application of biochar and cow manure treatments, which is consistent with the high K content of many biochars and their ability to retain cations on exchange sites [1, 2, 44]. This supply is normally based on the content of K in the biochar [45]. In this study, the biochar was rich in ash and K content as well reducing its leaching and stimulating the release of K from clay minerals in the soil [46]. Additionally, manure in the combined treatment could have contributed to exchangeable K due its appreciable cation exchange capacity (CEC), which enhances the retention of cationic nutrients in the soil. This higher CEC likely improved K availability by reducing leaching losses and maintaining K in exchangeable forms accessible to plants. Therefore, supplying the required K in soil improves spinach growth and yield. Adequate K supply is known to improve its uptake, enhance resistance towards drought and disease attacks leading to high quality of vegetables [47, 48]. Therefore, co-application of biochar and cow manure is expected to promote growth and quality of spinach as well as tolerance towards abiotic stress.

Interestingly, available P did not differ significantly among treatments, and slightly lower values were observed under the co-application of biochar and cow manure. This may reflect increased P uptake by spinach due to enhanced root development and microbial activity rather than reduced availability. Biochar is known to adsorb phosphate under certain conditions, temporarily immobilizing it while reducing losses and improving long-term availability [49, 50]. Thus, the improved nutrient retention and balanced availability could reduce fertilizer losses, enhance nutrient use efficiency, and minimize environmental contamination. These benefits are particularly critical in tropical agroecosystems characterized by intense rainfall and nutrient leaching.

Overall, the PCA results provide strong integrative evidence of the relationships among the treatments, soil chemical properties, and plant performance. The strong positive loading of growth and yield together with N, K, EC, and pH on PC1 indicates that improved soil fertility directly enhanced spinach productivity. The clustering of the biochar and cow manure treatment on the positive side of PC1 confirms its superior influence on both soil quality and crop performance. The separation of the biochar-only treatment along PC2 suggests that biochar primarily influences structural and physiological growth attributes, while the combined treatment more effectively integrates nutrient supply especially N and K for spinach production with physical improvement of the soil. The control treatment’s isolation on the negative side of both PCs highlights the limitation of un-amended soils in supporting productive vegetable growth. These multivariate relationships reinforce the concept that integrated soil fertility management, rather than single-input strategies, is essential for optimizing crop productivity and soil health [51, 52].

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that co-application of biochar and cow manure led to substantial and consistent improvement in growth and yield of spinach. Plant height, leaf number, leaf length, leaf width, and leaf area, plant weight, and yield were significantly increased by the co-application of biochar and cow manure compared to the sole application of biochar. The increase was mainly attributed to the improvement in soil chemical properties, especially soil total N and exchangeable K. However, the lack of significant difference between co-application and cow manure alone indicated that the major growth response was largely driven by nutrient supply from manure, while biochar contributed complementary soil-conditioning effects. Thus, the study demonstrates clear beneficial effects of the co-application of biochar and cow manure on crop growth, yield and soil fertility. Moreover, these results highlight that biochar can be effectively “charged” with cow manure, thereby enhancing its agronomic performance compared to sole biochar application. Charging biochar with manure pre-loads its porous structure with nutrients and microbial substrates, transforming it into a slow-release nutrient carrier that improves nutrient availability and retention in soil. Additionally, these results suggest that biochar-manure blends represent a promising strategy for the development of highly effective organic fertilizers that integrate nutrient supply and soil health improvement, particularly in nutrient-depleted and acidic soils in the tropical regions. However, further studies should focus on quantifying plant nutrient uptake to elucidate soil-plant nutrient dynamics and evaluating the long-term effects of repeated biochar and manure applications on soil health and crop productivity.
