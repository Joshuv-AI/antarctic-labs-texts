Butorphanol premedication improves postoperative lung function and analgesic outcomes in laparoscopic surgery: a randomized dose-response study
Authors: Xiaofeng Yang, Zhenyu Wu, Yiming Zhuang, Taowu Gong, Yi Liu, Yuhang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn laparoscopic surgery, balancing effective analgesia with preservation of pulmonary function remains a clinical challenge. Butorphanol, a κ-opioid receptor agonist and partial µ-opioid receptor agonist, may offer advantages in respiratory protection and anti-inflammatory effects.MethodsThis prospective, randomized, double-blind, three-arm, single-center clinical trial was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University. Between June 2021 and December 2023, a total of 81 patients undergoing elective laparoscopic surgery were enrolled, including 28 in the control group, 27 in the low-dose butorphanol group (1 mg), and 26 in the high-dose butorphanol group (2 mg). All patients received an intramuscular injection of the assigned study drug 30 min before anesthesia induction. The primary outcome was intraoperative pulmonary gas exchange, including arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2₂), and end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2). Secondary outcomes included intraoperative ventilatory mechanics, perioperative pulmonary function, inflammatory markers, postoperative analgesia, anesthetic drug consumption, and follow-up outcomes up to 9 months after surgery.ResultsCompared with the control group, butorphanol—particularly at 2 mg—significantly improved intraoperative pulmonary gas exchange. The high-dose group showed greater benefits in reducing airway pressures and anesthetic requirements, improving postoperative pulmonary function, suppressing inflammatory responses, and enhancing analgesic effects. During the 9-month follow-up, 70 patients completed all assessments, and the high-dose group consistently demonstrated superior recovery of pulmonary function, better quality of life, and lower levels of chronic pain.ConclusionPre-induction butorphanol enhances intraoperative pulmonary gas exchange and improves secondary outcomes, with 2 mg showing the most consistent benefits for ERAS protocols.Clinical trial registrationThe study has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, registration number: ChiCTR2100047900. Introduction

With advances in modern medical technology, postoperative recovery, particularly pulmonary function recovery, has become a crucial clinical concern. Effective pulmonary function recovery directly affects patients’ quality of life, hospital stay duration, and complication rates, especially in elderly patients or those with comorbidities who face greater challenges [1]. The choice of anesthetic agents, dosage, and analgesic strategies is pivotal in determining outcomes.

Butorphanol is a novel anesthetic analgesic that acts as a partial agonist at the µ-opioid receptor (MOR) and an agonist at the κ-opioid receptor (KOR), with weak activity at the δ-opioid receptor (DOR) as a secondary target, providing analgesia while reducing the need for conventional opioids [2–4]. However, its impact on pulmonary function remains insufficiently studied. Current evidence focuses mainly on analgesic effects in acute pain and anesthesia-related analgesia [5–8], with limited exploration of its role as a preconditioning agent before anesthesia induction.

Studies suggest that butorphanol can alleviate postoperative pain, reduce opioid consumption, and modulate inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [9, 10]. These findings provide a theoretical basis for potential benefits in postoperative pulmonary function recovery. However, systematic evaluations of dose-dependent effects on pulmonary function, inflammatory response, and analgesic efficacy are lacking. Existing research primarily investigates low-dose regimens, neglecting potential differences between high and low doses [9–11].

General anesthesia itself often suppresses postoperative pulmonary function, with opioids contributing to respiratory inhibition and risk of complications [12, 13]. Given butorphanol’s unique receptor profile, clarifying its preconditioning role, particularly the comparative effects of different doses on pulmonary function, inflammation, and analgesia, represents a significant gap in anesthesiology research [11].

This study aims to investigate the dose-dependent effects of butorphanol administered before anesthesia induction on postoperative recovery. The primary outcomes are intraoperative pulmonary gas exchange parameters measured at defined time points. The secondary outcomes include pulmonary ventilation indices, perioperative pulmonary function tests, and inflammatory markers assessed through routine blood tests and cytokine analysis. By clearly defining these primary and secondary targets, this study seeks to clarify the preconditioning role of butorphanol, address current evidence gaps in pulmonary function recovery and inflammation control, and provide a scientific basis for optimizing anesthesia and analgesia strategies in clinical practice, particularly for high-risk surgical patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective, randomized, double-blind, parallel-controlled, single-center clinical trial. The study has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, registration number: ChiCTR2100047900.

Case selection

This study enrolled patients who underwent elective laparoscopic surgery at the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University between June 2021 and December 2023. The majority of cases were laparoscopic cholecystectomy and appendectomy (> 85%), with the remainder including procedures such as laparoscopic exploration and hernia repair, to ensure consistency in surgical trauma and baseline pain levels. All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia with tracheal intubation, and standardized positioning was adopted according to surgical type: reverse Trendelenburg (head-up 15°−20°) for cholecystectomy, and supine or mild Trendelenburg (head-down 5°−10°) for appendectomy, to minimize the influence of positioning on respiratory mechanics. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Approval No. KLL-2020-041), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Patients aged 18–65 years, with an equal distribution of males and females; (2) Body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 30; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I-III classification, requiring general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation; (4) Patients without neurological or psychiatric disorders; (5) No significant abnormalities in laboratory tests; (6) Surgery duration between 30 min and 3 h.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Patients with drug allergies or allergic diseases; (2) Pregnant or breastfeeding patients; (3) Patients who have had respiratory infections or used antibiotics within 2 weeks before surgery; (4) Patients who have used analgesics within 2 weeks before surgery; (5) Patients with poorly controlled hypertension, diabetes, or endocrine disorders; (6) Patients with significant liver, kidney, coagulation, respiratory function, or electrocardiogram abnormalities; (7) Patients deemed unsuitable for inclusion by the researchers.

Exclusion Criteria for Withdrawal: (1) Patients who failed endotracheal intubation three times; (2) Patients who experienced severe pulmonary complications during surgery (such as pulmonary edema, pulmonary embolism) or significant airway pressure fluctuations (airway pressure changes greater than 30% from baseline); (3) Patients who experienced severe intraoperative hemorrhage, used corticosteroids, pulmonary circulation improving drugs, or other analgesics required for non-general anesthesia maintenance; (4) Patients who required emergency critical care during the observation period (Fig. 1).

Blinding and unblinding

This study is designed as a double-blind study. The blinded researchers are responsible for observing the study indicators, while the unblinded researchers perform random group allocation, drug preparation, and administration. During the study, both the subjects and the blinded researchers remain blinded, and the research drug information is only disclosed after the study is completed and all data are collected, except in cases of emergency.

Unblinding is prohibited unless an emergency occurs (such as allergic reactions, shock, or other life-threatening adverse events). In the case of emergency unblinding, the subject’s research medication is revealed, and the subject should be treated as per the pre-established protocol for withdrawal from the study.

Drugs, reagents, and instruments

Butorphanol Tartrate Injection, specification: 1 ml: 1 mg. Batch numbers: 201223BP, 210313BP; approved drug registration number: National Drug Approval No. H20020454, produced by Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Midazolam, sufentanil, etomidate, rocuronium bromide, propofol, remifentanil, sevoflurane. T8-type monitor, anesthesia machine (Drager), PCIA pump, and fully automatic multi-functional enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader.

Random grouping and anesthesia method

After signing the informed consent, the study participants were randomly selected using a simple random sampling method. Based on the preliminary postoperative lung function test results, with FEV1% as the endpoint, the sample size was calculated using PASS 26.0 software. Setting α = 0.05, β = 0.1 (test power 90%), and choosing a difference test method, the sample size for each group was calculated to be 25 cases. Considering a 10% dropout rate, 28 cases were required per group, and the total sample size was 84.

Butorphanol pre-treatment

Thirty minutes before anesthesia induction, patients in the low-dose group (LD group), high-dose group (HD group), and Control group (Con group) were administered intramuscular injections of equal volumes of Butorphanol (1 mg for the LD group, 2 mg for the HD group) and placebo (0.9% saline) in the deltoid muscle. The high-dose group (2 mg) corresponded to the standard clinical preoperative regimen for adults, consistent with commonly recommended intravenous or intramuscular administration (https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=4352). The low-dose group (1 mg) was defined as a subclinical dose, intended to evaluate whether analgesic efficacy is retained below the recommended level and whether the risk of adverse effects is reduced, thereby allowing systematic assessment of the dose–response relationship of butorphanol.

Anesthesia induction

Anesthesia was induced with sequential intravenous administration of midazolam 2 mg, sufentanil 0.3 µg/kg, etomidate 0.2 mg/kg, and rocuronium 0.9 mg/kg. Tracheal intubation was performed 3–5 min later, followed by volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg, respiratory rate of 12–14 breaths/min, and an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:2. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) was uniformly set at 5 cmH2O to maintain alveolar recruitment and reduce the risk of postoperative atelectasis.

Anesthesia maintenance

Anesthesia was maintained with continuous intravenous infusion of propofol (5–10 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min), combined with 2% sevoflurane inhalation. This multimodal regimen was designed to achieve balanced anesthesia, with synergistic effects of intravenous propofol and inhaled sevoflurane enhancing intraoperative hemodynamic stability, while minimizing the required doses of individual agents to reduce adverse effects such as respiratory depression and hypotension, thereby facilitating postoperative recovery. Rocuronium was intermittently supplemented at 0.2–0.3 mg/kg as needed to maintain muscle relaxation.

Monitoring

Airway pressure, end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2), and oxygenation parameters were continuously monitored throughout surgery. Hemodynamic variables were maintained within ± 20% of baseline, and patient positioning was adjusted according to surgical requirements to ensure ventilation efficiency and stable oxygenation.

Postoperative analgesia

At the end of skin suturing, inhalational anesthetics and intravenous anesthetic drug infusion were stopped, and the PCIA pump was connected. The PCIA pump formula: Sufentanil 2 µg/kg + Ketorolac Tromethamine 120 mg + Ondansetron 8 mg + 0.9% saline, diluted to 100 ml. The background infusion rate, self-controlled analgesia amount, and lockout time were set to 2 ml/h, 1.5 ml/shot, and 15 min, respectively. Postoperative pain management was performed using the NRS method. If NRS > 4, Sufentanil 5 µg was administered intravenously for rescue analgesia.

Observational indicators

Basic data

Patient age, height, weight, BMI, modified Mallampati score, thyromental distance, mouth opening, etc.

Primary indicators

Intraoperative pulmonary gas exchange parameters, including partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), and end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2), measured at five time points: T0 (30 min before anesthesia), T1 (5 min after anesthesia), T2 (surgery start), T3 (1 h after surgery onset), and T4 (end of surgery).

Secondary indicators

(1) pulmonary ventilation parameters at the same time points [maximum airway pressure (Pmax) and mean airway pressure (Pmean)], (2) perioperative pulmonary function tests [maximum voluntary ventilation percentage (MVV%), forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity percentage (FEV1/FVC%), and forced expiratory volume in 1 s percentage (FEV1%)], and (3) inflammatory markers, which were assessed by complete blood count [white blood cell count (WBC) and neutrophil percentage (N%)] before and after surgery, as well as by serum cytokine analysis: 5 mL of peripheral venous blood was collected at 30 min before anesthesia and 24 h postoperatively, serum was separated and stored in EP tubes, and IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α were quantified using ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Inflammatory evaluation indicators

Preoperative and postoperative blood routine [white blood cell (WBC), N%] were collected; 5 ml peripheral venous blood was collected 30 min before anesthesia and 24 h after surgery, and the supernatant was separated and stored in EP tubes for ELISA to detect serum IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α levels, according to the instructions of the kit.

Analgesia indicators

NRS scores at different postoperative time points (2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h); total PCIA presses within 48 h; total rescue analgesic doses of Sufentanil within 48 h.

Other indicators

Perioperative anesthetic drug usage, including the amounts of Propofol and Remifentanil used during surgery, and the rescue analgesic doses of Sufentanil administered after surgery.

Follow-up design and data collection

To further assess the impact of Butorphanol pre-treatment on mid-term postoperative lung function recovery and quality of life, a 9-month follow-up was added. The follow-up time points were 1 month postoperatively (T1), 3 months (T2), 6 months (T3), and 9 months (T4). At each follow-up, the following indicators were recorded: FEV1%, MVV%, and FEV1/FVC ratio measured with a pulmonary function testing device to evaluate the trend of lung function recovery. The Short Form 36 questionnaire (SF-36) was used to assess the quality of life over different postoperative periods, including physical health, mental health, and social functioning dimensions (Appendix 1). Chronic pain levels were recorded using the NRS, with scores ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain). Chronic respiratory diseases, rehospitalization occurrences, and the frequency of analgesic use were also documented.

Patients were encouraged to return to the hospital at each follow-up time point for pulmonary function tests and to complete the quality of life questionnaire. For those unable to attend in person, quality of life and pain data were collected via phone, email, or online survey platforms. All follow-up data were recorded in standardized forms and uploaded to an electronic database for management and analysis. A total of 14 patients were lost to follow-up. A total of 14 patients were lost to follow-up, yielding a final completion rate of 83.3% (70/84), with no significant difference in dropout distribution among groups (p > 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (X ± S), and categorical variables as frequencies or percentages. Within-group comparisons were performed using paired t-tests, while between-group comparisons were analyzed with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); when variances were unequal, Fisher’s exact test was applied. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test, and non-parametric rank-sum tests were used for skewed distributions. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was used to evaluate time-dependent changes in pulmonary function indices. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 84 patients were screened, of which 3 were excluded due to abnormal preoperative electrocardiograms, intraoperative and postoperative use of corticosteroids and pulmonary circulation-improving drugs, or personal refusal to cooperate. Thus, 81 patients were ultimately included in the study. The included patients were divided into three groups (Con group: 28 cases; LD group: 27 cases; HD group: 26 cases) and compared. The differences in basic characteristics—such as sex ratio (M/F), age, height, weight, BMI, modified Mallampati score, thyromental distance, inter-incisor distance, body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure—were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

There were no significant differences in preoperative lung function indicators (FEV1 (%), MVV (%), FEV1/FVC (%)) among the groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2A, B). Follow-up results

During the 9-month follow-up, a total of 84 patients were included, of whom 70 completed all follow-up assessments (completion rate 83.3%). Fourteen patients did not complete follow-up due to failure to return or loss of contact. The distribution of missing data was balanced among groups (p > 0.05), with no significant impact on the analysis.

Pulmonary function testing showed that the HD group had significantly higher FEV1%, MVV%, and FEV1/FVC ratios at 1 month (T1), 3 months (T2), 6 months (T3), and 9 months (T4) compared with the Con and LD groups (p < 0.05, Fig. 5A-C). RM-ANOVA indicated a significant time-dependent improvement in pulmonary function across all groups (p < 0.05), with the HD group showing a significantly greater degree of improvement compared with the other groups (p < 0.05).

Regarding postoperative complications, the incidence of chronic respiratory disease and readmission was significantly lower in the HD group than in the Con group (p < 0.05). Analgesic drug use in the HD group decreased progressively over time, with analgesic requirements nearly absent by 9 months postoperatively.

Discussion

This prospective, randomized, double-blind controlled trial demonstrated that pre-induction administration of high-dose butorphanol (2 mg) significantly improved postoperative pulmonary function (FEV1%, MVV%), reduced serum inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α), and enhanced analgesic efficacy (lower NRS scores and reduced PCIA press counts) in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery. These findings expand the perioperative pharmacological profile of butorphanol and provide new evidence for optimizing postoperative recovery strategies. The innovation of this study lies in the systematic evaluation of dose–response effects, integrating pulmonary function, inflammatory markers, and 9-month follow-up outcomes, and confirming the superiority of the higher dose (2 mg) over the lower dose (1 mg).

Compared with previous work, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the dose-dependent effect of butorphanol, as a pre-induction conditioning agent, on postoperative pulmonary function recovery. Earlier research mainly focused on its analgesic properties and opioid-sparing effect [14–16]. By jointly assessing pulmonary function and inflammatory indices, we demonstrated the multi-mechanistic synergy of butorphanol. Our results showed that high-dose butorphanol significantly improved ventilation (PaO2, PaCO2, Pimax) and suppressed inflammation compared with the low-dose group, challenging the traditional notion of a “ceiling effect” [9] and suggesting that appropriate dosing may yield broader clinical benefits.

The improvement in pulmonary function may be attributed to κ-opioid receptor (KOR) activation. Previous studies have shown that KOR agonism suppresses NF-κB signaling, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine release (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α) [3], mitigating alveolar epithelial barrier damage and pulmonary edema. Butorphanol may also activate the AMPK pathway to attenuate oxidative stress [9] and directly relax bronchial smooth muscle, improving airway patency. In this trial, the high-dose group demonstrated superior oxygenation and ventilation efficiency at T3 and T4, further supporting this mechanism. Animal models of LPS-induced acute lung injury likewise confirm that butorphanol exerts KOR-dependent anti-inflammatory activity by suppressing TLR4-MyD88 interaction, downregulating NF-κB/MAPK pathways, and shifting macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotype, thereby alleviating pulmonary inflammation [9, 17, 18]. These mechanistic insights align with the cytokine reductions observed in our cohort.

Clinical evidence also supports the respiratory protective effects of butorphanol. In a randomized bariatric surgery trial, butorphanol improved oxygenation (higher a/A ratio), reduced dead-space ventilation, and lowered pulmonary-injury biomarkers, consistent with our observed PaO2 improvements and reduced PaCO2/ETCO2 [19]. Moreover, butorphanol reduced intraoperative propofol and remifentanil requirements or partially substituted for µ-agonists during induction, thereby reducing µ-mediated respiratory depression while maintaining analgesic efficacy, consistent with our drug-sparing results [20].

In terms of analgesia, this study confirmed that high-dose butorphanol reduced NRS scores and PCIA press counts while decreasing intraoperative intravenous anesthetic consumption [7, 21]. Furthermore, butorphanol pretreatment prolonged analgesic duration and improved medium- to long-term pulmonary function and quality of life, supporting its role in enhanced recovery after surgery pathways. Previous studies have similarly reported that butorphanol reduced 24-hour postoperative nausea and vomiting and improved recovery comfort in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery, attenuated emergence agitation and coughing in functional endoscopic sinus surgery [22], and alleviated visceral pain without cardiopulmonary adverse effects in randomized trials [23]. Meta-analytic evidence also shows that butorphanol provides effective analgesia with a lower incidence of adverse effects, such as PONV, compared with traditional µ-agonists [23, 24], in line with our findings of lower NRS and PCIA use.

This study, however, has several limitations. First, as a single-center randomized controlled trial with a relatively small sample size (n = 84), and with patients limited to elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy or appendectomy, the external generalizability remains restricted and cannot be directly extrapolated to other high-risk populations such as thoracic surgery, elderly patients, or those with chronic lung disease, obesity, or obstructive sleep apnea. Second, only fixed doses (1 mg vs. 2 mg) were tested, without adjustment for body weight or pharmacokinetic variability; moreover, intramuscular administration may introduce variability in absorption and plasma concentration. Future studies should incorporate weight-based dosing and PK/PD monitoring to refine dose–response characterization. Third, inflammatory markers were assessed only at two time points (baseline and 24 h postoperatively), lacking dynamic monitoring, and did not include anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10), lung-injury markers (e.g., sRAGE, SP-D), or oxidative stress indices, thereby limiting mechanistic insight. Fourth, while pulmonary function and inflammatory improvements were demonstrated, postoperative pulmonary complications (e.g., atelectasis, pneumonia, reintubation) and full adverse-event profiles (e.g., nausea, delirium, respiratory events) were not systematically recorded, nor were validated patient-reported outcomes such as SGRQ for respiratory status or BPI for chronic pain. Fifth, although long-term follow-up showed beneficial trends, chronic pain was assessed only via NRS scores, which may not fully capture emotional and functional dimensions. Lastly, despite the double-blind design, mild sedation induced by butorphanol was not quantified, posing a potential risk of unblinding.

Future research should conduct multicenter, large-sample validation trials across diverse surgical types, especially in high-risk populations. Mechanistic investigations should include multi-timepoint inflammatory, oxidative stress, and lung injury biomarkers to clarify KOR-mediated pathways. Clinically, standardized endpoints of postoperative pulmonary complications, safety outcomes, and multidimensional patient-reported measures should be incorporated to fully assess the perioperative value of butorphanol. Additionally, further exploration of combination strategies with other anesthetics or analgesics is warranted to optimize multimodal analgesia and enhance ERAS protocols.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that pre-induction administration of butorphanol significantly improved postoperative pulmonary function (FEV1%, MVV%), reduced inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α), and enhanced analgesic efficacy (lower NRS scores and fewer PCIA presses). High-dose butorphanol (2 mg) was more effective than the low dose (1 mg) in improving pulmonary function and suppressing inflammatory responses. In addition, butorphanol pretreatment reduced intraoperative consumption of propofol and remifentanil. Therefore, butorphanol as a preconditioning agent before anesthesia shows clear benefits in improving postoperative pulmonary function, attenuating inflammation, and enhancing analgesia, with the higher dose demonstrating greater efficacy.
