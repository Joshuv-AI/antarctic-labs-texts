Quality of artificial intelligence‐generated responses on pediatric celiac disease: Comparative assessment of Open AI ChatGPT and Google Gemini
Authors: Ashwin Agrawal, Miri Goodman, Alexander Schosheim, Jonathan E. Teitelbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMany individuals use artificial intelligence tools such as Open AI's ChatGPT and Google's Gemini to assess their symptoms or learn about a disease. However, studies to evaluate responses provided by artificial intelligence tools are lacking. This study aims to evaluate and compare ChatGPT and Gemini in answering pediatric celiac disease‐specific questions in terms of performance across accuracy, safety, completeness, and understandability.MethodsWe developed 34 questions that mimicked questions that parents ask about pediatric celiac disease in four categories: disease symptoms and definition, diagnosis, gluten challenge, and management. Questions were posed to ChatGPT 5.0 and Gemini Pro 2.5. Responses were compared with North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) and European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) guidelines, or clinical best practices when guidelines were absent. Three board certified Pediatric Gastroenterologists rated each response on a 5‐point Likert scale across four domains: accuracy, safety, completeness, and understandability. Word count and Flesch–Kincaid grade level (FKGL) were recorded.ResultsAverage scores were ≥4.5 for ChatGPT and ≥4.3 for Gemini across all four domains. Gemini responses were significantly longer and had higher FKGL. Interclass correlation coefficients showed good agreement for all rating domains except for Gemini safety ratings and understandability ratings for both models.ConclusionsChatGPT and Gemini both generated highly accurate, safe, complete, and understandable information for common questions about celiac disease. Performance decreased when guidelines were lacking or there was clinical ambiguity. However, readability scores (FKGL) for both models exceeded standards for patient education recommendations for sixth to eighth grade reading level or below suggesting there may be barrier to understandability for the general population.

INTRODUCTION

Since their release for public utilization in late 2022, the use of generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI) and large language models (LLMs) such as Open AI's ChatGPT and Google's Gemini has increased exponentially. 1 , 2 , 3 Companies developed Gen AI and LLM to aid the public in answering questions, solving problems, and summarizing information. 4 , 5 One common use for artificial intelligence (AI) has been as a tool for adults to assess their medical symptoms and learn about disease. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Up to 75% of adults have used AI tools to get more information about their health. 6 A small study of 185 parents revealed that up to 74% used AI in pediatric healthcare and that the majority (93.1%) thought AI was partially or very accurate. 7 The massive use by patients emphasizes the need to evaluate the accuracy, safety, clarity, and understandability of the information provided by AI tools.

Celiac disease (CD) is an autoimmune disease characterized by an immune‐mediated response of the small intestine to dietary gluten, which is a protein found in many grains such as wheat, barely, or rye. 8 , 9 CD has been labeled as a primary gastrointestinal disorder, which can present at any age, and its manifestations can vary widely between patients involving either the classic gastrointestinal symptoms or more widespread systemic disease. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 CD occurs in genetically susceptible individuals, with the highest risk being in patients with HLA‐DQ2/DQ8 alleles and results in lifelong disease characterized by an immune response to gluten causing autoimmune destruction of the small intestine. 8 , 10 , 11 The path to CD diagnosis can be complicated by the varied presentations of the disease and children diagnosed with CD must make large adjustments to their diet and lifestyle to adopt a gluten‐free diet (GFD). Pediatric gastroenterologists help parents navigate these transitions, but with the increased use of AI, parents may turn to AI tools to help understand the process of CD diagnosis and management.

Researchers have investigated the efficacy of Gen AI and LLMs for use in healthcare in fields such as colonoscopy preparation, taking a new medication, and basic questions about the treatment of patients with a variety of illnesses. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 In a study on patients taking new medication, researchers found that the AI tools were mostly accurate but left out some critical information that patients may require. 12 Another research study on the accuracy of AI medical information yielded similar results. 14 Regarding patients asking AI questions about preparing for a colonoscopy, one study found that AI generated results were accurate and complete and offered a viable outlet for patients to ask medical questions. 15

To date, no study has compared ChatGPT and Gemini in answering pediatric CD‐specific questions. The European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) and the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) have released recommendations for the diagnosis and management of pediatric CD. 10 , 16 , 17 , 18 In this study, we utilize the recommendations and guidelines from ESPGHAN and NASPGHAN to compare the accuracy of answers from AI tools to those guidelines.

This study aims to evaluate and compare OpenAI's ChatGPT (ChatGPT) and Google's Gemini (Gemini) performance in answering parental questions about pediatric CD across four main domains: accuracy, safety, completeness, and understandability.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was self‐certified as non‐human subject study utilizing Rutgers University's online Non‐Human Research Self‐Certification Tool (HRP‐310b).

Study design

We developed 34 questions that mimicked common questions that parents ask about pediatric CD. These questions were grouped into four categories: (1) disease symptoms and definition, (2) diagnosis, (3) gluten challenge, and (4) management. Three board‐certified pediatric gastroenterologists (Ped GIs) confirmed that these questions were similar to those asked in the clinic.

Questions were posed to ChatGPT 5.0 (OpenAI) and Gemini Pro 2.5 (Google) between August 13–18, 2025. Each question was presented to the AI system in a separate session. In addition, both model's settings were changed to not recall history of past questions to reduce bias by the AI models when answering questions.

Responses were compared with NASPGHAN and ESPGHAN guidelines. If no guidelines were present for a question, responses were compared to clinical best practices. Questions and AI responses can be found in Supplementary Digital Content S1.

Three Ped GIs independently rated each response on a five‐point Likert scale across four domains: (1) accuracy, (2) safety, (3) completeness, and (4) understandability. The Peds GIs were all American Board of Pediatrics certified in both general pediatrics and pediatric gastroenterology. One Ped GI has been practicing for more than 25 years and is the division chief, one Ped GI is the medical director of the organization's CD center, and one Ped GI is a general mid‐career Ped GI. All three Ped GI have expertise and take care of many patients with CD each year. All reviewers are co‐authors of this manuscript and practice at the same academic institution.

To promote uniformity in interpretation of the Likert scale, definitions for each numerical value were provided. Word count and Flesch–Kincaid grade level (FKGL) were also recorded. FKGL is a widely used method of determining written content's readability grade level. As there is no validated scoring measure for AI‐generated responses, we developed our own 5‐point Likert scare which was reviewed by three Peds GIs who discussed the scales to ensure consistent grading across all domains with 5 being the highest (best) score and 1 being the lowest (worst) score. Supplementary Digital Content S2 provides the Likert scales utilized.

Microsoft Word was utilized to do a word count on each AI‐generated response. In addition, Microsoft Word was utilized to calculate FKGL for each response.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS v31 were utilized for data analysis. The reviewer's scores were averaged for each question and domain. Student T‐test assuming equal variances was conducted between the average scores of each domain to determine if significant differences were found between ChatGPT and Gemini. P value less than 0.05 was considered significant. Interclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated to determine if there was good agreement between raters. ICC greater or equal to 0.5 was considered good agreement.

RESULTS

Average scores (Table 1) were ≥4.5 for ChatGPT and ≥4.3 for Gemini across all four domains with no significant differences between models. Gemini responses were significantly longer at an average word count of 513 words compared to ChatGPT at 318 words (p < 0.05). In addition, Gemini responses had higher FKGL (p = 0.03). On average, FKGL for Gemini was 11.4 grade level compared to ChatGPT at 10.5 grade level.

DISCUSSION

Though LLM and Gen AI are being utilized widely by the general public, there have been few studies assessing the quality of AI responses. We present the first study that assessed and compared two GenAI systems responses to parental questions about pediatric CD to determine the safety, accuracy, and understandability of responses.

Our study demonstrates that overall accuracy and safety of responses regarding pediatric disease is quite good with an average rating of 4.3–4.7 out of 5. This is especially true when there are published guidelines regarding the question asked (Table 4). Assessment of scores that were considered low (average score less than 4) shows that accuracy and safety scores were lower when there was no concrete NASPGHAN and/or ESPGHAN guideline or there was some clinical ambiguity or controversy to the best answer (Table 3). 10 , 17 For example, some of the lowest average scores for accuracy were questions regarding the use of separate pans/utensils to cook gluten‐free foods, the use of a shared fryer or use of a shared toaster for gluten‐free foods. The Ped GI raters also found the AI responses safe and unlikely to cause harm. At times, some raters felt that certain responses were somewhat unsafe because they may cause heightened fear and anxiety due to how strict the GFD wording could be. For example, at times responses stated “even a tiny amount of gluten can cause a reaction and intestinal damage” or “even tiny traces of gluten from crumbs, flour dust, or residue on cookware can cause a reaction” which could cause unnecessary and excessive vigilance for a family.

The study also shows that although the AI‐generated responses were complete, the understandability of the responses was variable. The only category where the raters showed poor agreement on scoring was on understandability. We believe this is because each rater has a different understanding of what may be understandable by a patient/parent based on their own experiences. To improve the assessment of understanding, we added an assessment of the FKGL which is a widely used measure of assessing grade level. FKGL is a readability score that indicates the number of years of education a person needs to understand written material. Though Gemini average FKGL (11.4) was slightly higher than ChatGPT average FKGL (10.5), there was no statistically significant difference. This indicates most readers would require a high school reading level (grades 10–12) to understand the answers. Interestingly, multiple organizations including the National Institute of Health, Center of Disease Control, Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality recommend that patient directed educational material be between sixth to eight grade level. 19 , 20 This indicates that AI‐generated responses from both ChatGPT and Gemini may not be understandable by the general population and limit the utility of the information.

This study also demonstrated that ChatGPT responses were on average shorter in word count (318 words) than Gemini answers (513 words). This similarity was found in a review article by Fattah et al. 21 Interestingly, all average scores for completeness that were considered low (Table 3), were for ChatGPT. There were no Gemini average scores for completeness less than four. We postulate this may be due to the direct and more concise answers provided by ChatGPT compared to Gemini.

Though our study did not assess empathy of responses, all raters thought that ChatGPT added more empathy and sounded more like human responses. For example, a response from ChatGPT would periodically say “great question” or “that's a really thoughtful question” or “you're right.” In addition, ChatGPT would often end responses asking if there were follow up questions such as “Would you like me to explain what the next testing steps usually are when total Immunoglobulin‐A (IgA) is low?” or “Would you like me to outline what screening usually looks like for siblings and at what ages it's typically done?” maintaining a conversational style. Other times ChatGPT would try to make the next step for the parent easier. For example, “Do you want me to summarize the exact amounts in terms of common foods (like slices of bread, pasta, crackers) so it's easier to picture what a gluten challenge diet looks like?” or “If you'd like, I can put these into a one‐page “doctor's visit” checklist that you can print and bring, so nothing gets forgotten during the appointment. That way you walk out with a clear plan for the first year after diagnosis.” These responses added a human dimension to responses and helped show empathy. Gemini tried to interject some empathy phrases at times, but it was not as successful as ChatGPT.

While empathic and affirming language is a positive for a parent/patient to read, it also raises concerns regarding authority bias. The empathic responses may increase trust in information that may be incomplete or incorrect. Overreliance on an AI system that is perceived as knowledgeable and empathetic may pose potential risks to the patient if medical advice is accepted without physician verification.

Our study adds to the literature that shows that Gen AI and LLM provide mostly accurate information. A review study by Fattah et al evaluated the accuracy of ChatGPT and Gemini through 11 medical specialties found that certain specialties had higher accuracy of responses than others. 21 For example, overall average accuracy for AI responses for radiology questions was 87.43% for ChatGPT versus 71% for Gemini; however lower for other specialties such as hematology where accuracy ranged from 44% to 63%. 21 A meta‐analysis of ChatGPT generated medical responses showed that overall accuracy of 56% over 17 studies, which reveals the variable accuracy AI chatbots can have depending on the type of question and Gen AI utilized. 22 This variability of accuracy demonstrates that the general population should be cautious of accepting medical advice from Gen AI/LLMs. Interestingly, a recent study from Iran assessing the accuracy of ChatGPT‐3.5 in answering 20 questions related to CD also found high accuracy. 23 However, it should be noted that the study 23 was limited in that it did not address pediatric‐specific questions and only assessed one domain, compared to this study which assessed multiple domains and two AI models.

In this study, AI responses were generally found to be safe and accurate, however, the potential for AI‐generated falsification and hallucinations exist. This is when incorrect or fabricated information is presented with high confidence which can be misleading to users. Additionally, the proprietary nature of LLM model training limits transparency regarding how responses are generated. As a result, AI‐generated responses should not always be equated with factual accurac,y and independent verification of medical information remains essential. Importantly, information generated by AI systems should be considered supplementary and should not replace discussion with a Ped GI for management and diagnostic decisions.

Our study has a few limitations. First, the evaluation was limited to only two GenAI models at one point in time. Given that these models are updated and many other GenAI models exist, their performance may vary compared to our study. Second, only thirty‐four questions were included which may not capture the full breath of parental concerns. In addition, the questions were designed to mimic parental questions without any true patient derived question. Additionally, a limitation may be that this is based on North American parents, and parents in other countries may have different common questions. Presenting each question in an isolated session may not reflect real‐world use, where conversational context and prior queries can influence subsequent AI responses. Third, the ratings relied on a newly developed Likert scale which was discussed and standardized among raters, but has not been validated. In addition, as all reviewers were co‐authors from the same institution, this introduces the potential for institutional and professional bias. Future studies may benefit from geographically and professional diverse reviewers. Finally, our study did not evaluate the real‐world impact of these AI responses. It would be interesting to see how parents would rate the responses as well which could be evaluated in a future study.

CONCLUSION

In summary, our study demonstrates that ChatGPT and Gemini both generated highly accurate, safe, complete, and understandable information for common questions parents may have about CD. Performance decreased when guidelines were lacking or there was clinical ambiguity. Despite strong ratings overall, readability scores (FKGL) for both models exceeded recommendations for sixth to eight grade reading level or below, suggesting there may be barrier to understandability for the general population. Given the potential for error in AI outputs, families should be encouraged to discuss AI‐generated healthcare information with their Ped GI. These tools should complement, not replace, clinician‐guided care and medical decision‐making.
