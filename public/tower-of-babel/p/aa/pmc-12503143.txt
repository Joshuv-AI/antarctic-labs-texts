High Reoperation Rate After Osteosynthesis of Proximal Periprosthetic Tibial Fractures Around Medial Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Stefan Kastalag Risager, Anders Troelsen, Bjarke Viberg, Martin Lindberg-Larsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:A periprosthetic tibial fracture (PPTF) is a known complication of medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (mUKA). Treatment options include conversion to total knee arthroplasty (cTKA), open reduction internal fixation (ORIF), and nonoperative treatment. The risk of reoperation after initial treatment of PPTFs is not well examined. Given the rise of cementless mUKA and its association with early (≤4 months), likely surgery-related fractures, this group is particularly interesting. The aim of this study was to (1) report the 2-year risk of reoperation according to initial treatment and (2) report the need for delayed conversion to TKA (dcTKA) after ORIF or nonoperative treatment, or the need for revision TKA (rTKA) after cTKA.Methods:Using data from the Danish Knee Arthroplasty Register and the Danish National Patient Register, all PPTFs after mUKAs between 1997 and 2022 were included and stratified by treatment. Subsequent reoperations and need for dcTKA or rTKA within 2 years were outcomes.Results:We identified 177 PPTFs after mUKA with complete 2-year follow-up. When the treatment of the PPTF was cTKA (69 cases), 5 cases (7%) underwent reoperation and fewer than 5 required rTKA. For nonoperative treatment (46 cases), 13 cases (28%) underwent reoperation, with 10 cases requiring dcTKA. When treated with ORIF (62 cases), 25 cases (40%) underwent reoperation, with 19 cases requiring dcTKA. PPTFs after cemented mUKA were mostly late PPTFs (70%) and were often treated nonoperatively, where PPTFs after cementless mUKA were often early PPTFs (74-78%) and very rarely treated nonoperatively. Fixation of mUKA was not associated with the risk of reoperation within each treatment group. Among 74 early PPTFs after cementless mUKA, 35 cases were treated with cTKA with fewer than 5 reoperations within 2 years and 0 cases needing rTKA. In contrast after ORIF (39 cases), 16 cases (41%) underwent reoperation, with 12 cases requiring dcTKA.Conclusion:PPTFs treated with ORIF were associated with higher rates of reoperation and need for dcTKA compared with PPTFs treated with cTKA and nonoperative treatment. These data suggest that careful consideration is needed before using ORIF as treatment. However, the unknown fracture severity precludes definitive attribution of reoperation to the treatments.Level of Evidence:Prognostic Level III. Introduction

A proximal periprosthetic tibial fracture (PPTF) is a known complication of medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (mUKA) and can be challenging to treat1,2. PPTF is rare with a 3-year risk of 0.5% after cemented and 0.9% after cementless mUKAs; notably, the risk of early (≤4-months) and likely surgery-related PPTFs after cementless mUKAs is 0.7%3.

A recent study, conducted on a nationwide cohort in a country with high usage of mUKA, identified PPTF as a leading cause of early reoperations after mUKA with a risk of 0.7% within 1 year4,5. Furthermore, the National Joint Registry (NJR) report 20246 for England, Wales, Northern Ireland, and the Isle of Man showed that PPTFs accounted for 2% of all registered revisions after cemented mUKAs and 7% of all registered revisions after cementless mUKAs.

There are 3 main treatment options for PPTF: nonoperative management, open reduction internal fixation (ORIF), and conversion to total knee arthroplasty (cTKA)1,2,5. However, evidence on outcomes remains limited, and management recommendations are largely based on small case series2. Nonoperative treatment is typically used for un- or minimally displaced fractures with a stable implant1,2. ORIF, using cancellous screws or a buttress plate, is applied when the implant is stable and bone fragments are sufficient for fixation1,2. Although considered a good treatment option, some studies has shown the need for delayed conversion to TKA (dcTKA) after ORIF treatment2,5. cTKA is typically reserved for fractures with an unstable implant or small fragments1,2, with outcomes resembling those of revision TKAs (rTKA) for other indications more than primary TKAs7,8.

As the choice of treatment for PPTFs after mUKA and the risk of further reoperations is not fully understood, we aim to1 examine the risk of reoperation according to the initial treatment of a proximal PPTF and2 investigate the need for dcTKA and rTKA after the initial treatment.

Given the trend toward cementless mUKA, the treatment and reoperation rates of early and likely surgery-related PPTFs in this group are particularly relevant and will also be analyzed separately4,6,9,10.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This register study investigated all PPTF after mUKA from 1997 to October 1, 2022, using data from nationwide registers. Follow-up continued until October 1, 2024. Throughout this period, clinicians or medical secretaries prospectively recorded the data in national databases. This study builds on a previous analysis of the same cohort, which identified PPTFs and estimated the risk of PPTF after cemented and cementless mUKAs3. The study adheres to the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) guidelines11.

Data Sources

The Danish Civil Registration System (CRS) assigns a unique Central Person Register number to all individuals born in Denmark or residing for more than 3 months12. This number is used across all national registries, enabling the linkage of individuals12-14. The CRS also records information on births, emigration, and deaths in Denmark12. CRS data were available from 1997 to 2024.

Established in 1997, the Danish Knee Arthroplasty Register (DKR) is a nationwide clinical database that tracks the epidemiology and treatment quality of knee arthroplasties in Denmark15. Since 2009, it has maintained a high completeness rate of over 95%4. DKR data were available 1997 to 2022

The Danish National Patient Register (DNPR) has recorded all healthcare contacts in both public and private sectors since 197716. It contains clinical information, including diagnosis codes based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-1017 and procedure codes from the NOMESCO Classification of Surgical Procedures (NCSP)18. DNPR data were available from 1997 to 2024.

Participants

The study cohort comprised all patients aged 40 years or older who sustained a PPTF after mUKA, had complete 2-year follow-up, and were treated with conversion to TKA (cTKA), ORIF, or managed nonoperatively. Data on the mUKA procedures and treatment of the PPTFs were obtained from the DKR and the DNPR. No sample size calculation was performed.

Variables

All codes used to identify the different variables are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Inclusion criteria

A PPTF registered after a mUKA. The mUKA was identified in the DKR, and the ipsilateral proximal tibial fracture was identified through the DNPR using ICD diagnosis codes or NCSP procedure codes, or recorded as the reason for revision in the DKR3.

Exclusion criteria

mUKA patients were excluded if there were duplicate registrations, incorrect component codes, unknown cement use, or side discrepancies between the DKR and DNPR. Patients with prior major knee surgery were also excluded, including revision arthroplasty (unless for fracture), arthrodesis, prosthesis removal, amputation, or reoperation for tumors or infection. Last, patients with a PPTF after mUKA were excluded if they had surgery within 30 days that was not cTKA or ORIF, or were lost to follow-up due to death or emigration within 2 years.

Exposure

Treatment after PPTF was the exposure variable, and patients were grouped by treatment options: cTKA, ORIF, or nonoperative, defined using NCSP codes in the DNPR or an cTKA recorded in the DKR within 30 days. If no procedure was registered within 30 days, treatment was considered nonoperative. If both ORIF and cTKA were registered the same day, treatment was classified as cTKA. Procedures after 30 days were considered reoperations, including after initial nonoperative treatment. Fracture classification remained unknown to us, as the registries do not provide these details, and it could not be accounted for in the assessment of the different treatments.

Outcomes: Outcomes were identified through DKR and DNPR. The first outcome was reoperation and defined as any knee operation registered within 2 years of the initial treatment of the PPTF, or within 2 years of nonoperatively treated PPTFs. The second outcome was delayed conversion to TKA (dcTKA) following ORIF or nonoperative treatment, as well as the need for revision TKA (rTKA) after cTKA, occurring within 2 years of the initial treatment, regardless of other reoperations. Only codes specifying conversion to a full dcTKA or rTKA was used for this outcome. Infection within the first 2 years of treatment was also reported.

Data Analyses

Patients with bilateral PPTF after mUKA (n < 5) were included twice, once per knee, with separate follow-up. Follow-up continued for 2 years after initial treatment or from the fracture date if treatment was nonoperative.

Patient characteristics are presented as means with standard deviations for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Outcomes are reported as raw numbers and percentages with 95% confidence intervals (CI). As a sensitivity analysis, a binomial logistic regression including treatment choice, mUKA fixation, and PPTF timing (early vs. late) was performed to assess if fixation of the mUKA and timing of the PPTF was associated with the risk of reoperation. Given the limited sample size and full follow-up, further analysis was not warranted.

We present outcomes with fewer than 5 observations as “<5” in accordance with the regulations of the Danish Health Data Authority.

Data Access and Cleaning Methods

Researchers accessed data on all knee arthroplasty patients from 1997 to 2022, with DNPR and CVR data extending to 2025. Data were processed within a secure research platform using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Ethics

This study received approval from the Danish Data Protection Agency (Journal No. 22/17,275). Since it was noninterventional, ethical approval was not required. The research was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki22.

Results

Using data from 22,080 mUKAs, we identified 177 PPTFs after mUKA (101 cementless and 76 cemented mUKAs) with complete 2-year follow-up who underwent cTKA, ORIF, or nonoperative treatment (Fig. 1). The initial treatments were cTKA in 69 cases (39%) (9 in combination with ORIF), nonoperatively in 46 cases (26%), and ORIF in 62 cases (35%) (48 treated with plating and screws, 14 with cancellous screws alone). Early PPTFs after cementless mUKA were treated with cTKA in 35 cases and ORIF in 39 cases (33 treated with plating and screws, 6 with cancellous screws alone). Fewer than 5 were treated nonoperatively and were not included in Table II or the results. Thus, 74 cases treated with cTKA or ORIF were analyzed.

Patient characteristics were comparable across treatment groups, both for the entire cohort of PPTFs after any mUKA and for the subset of early PPTFs after cementless mUKA (Table I). Infection were registered in fewer than 5 cases, all of which occurred after ORIF and all subsequently requiring dcTKA.

All PPTF After Any mUKA

When the treatment of the PPTFs was cTKA (69 cases), 5 cases (7%) underwent reoperation within 2 years and fewer than 5 cases required rTKA (Table II). The mean time to reoperation was 315 days (standard deviation [SD] 237). When the initial treatment was nonoperative (46 cases), 13 cases (28%) underwent reoperation within 2 years, with 10 cases requiring dcTKA (Table II). The mean time to reoperation was 193 days (SD 155). When the initial treatment was ORIF (62 cases), 25 cases (40%) underwent reoperation, with 19 cases requiring dcTKA (Table II). The mean time to reoperation was 277 days (SD 211). Risk of reoperation was similar between ORIF types, with cancellous screws alone being associated with a slightly lower risk. However, the numbers were too small to report in detail.

Among the 101 PPTFs after cementless mUKA, 43 cases (43%) were treated with cTKA, 44 cases (44%) with ORIF, and 14 cases (14%) nonoperatively. Among the 76 PPTFs after cemented mUKA, 26 cases (34%) received cTKA, 18 cases (24%) ORIF, and 32 cases (42%) nonoperative treatment. Between 74 and 78 (74-78%) of the PPTFs after cementless mUKA were early PPTFs, whereas 53 (70%) of the PPTFs after cemented mUKA were late PPTFs. The risk of reoperation differed by treatment type but were similar within each treatment group regardless of mUKA fixation or PPTF timing. However, the numbers were too small to present in detail. The sensitivity analysis showed an odds ratio (OR) of 0.6 (95% CI: 0.3-1.5) for cemented mUKA and an OR of 0.7 (95% CI: 0.3-1.6) for late PPTFs, indicating that neither mUKA fixation nor PPTF timing was associated with an increased risk of reoperation.

Early PPTF after Cementless mUKA

When the treatment of early PPTF after cementless mUKA was cTKA (35 cases), there were fewer than 5 reoperations within 2 years and 0 cases needing rTKA. By contrast, when the initial treatment was ORIF (39 cases), 16 cases (41%) underwent reoperation within 2 years, with 12 of these requiring dcTKA. The mean time to reoperation was 249 days (SD 181).

Discussion

This nationwide cohort identified 177 PPTFs after 22,080 primary mUKA with 2-year follow-up, for the incidence of PPTF after cemented and cementless mUKA, we refer to the article which this study builds3. The difference in treatment choice between PPTFs after cemented and cementless mUKA is likely explained by PPTF timing, with most late PPTFs occurring after cemented mUKA and most early PPTFs after cementless mUKA. There was no significant difference in reoperation risk by fixation of the mUKA or timing of the PPTF within each treatment type. However, because most cTKA cases (35/69, 56%) and most ORIF cases (39/62, 63%) involved early PPTFs after cementless mUKA and most nonoperative cases (28/46, 61%) involved late PPTFs after cemented mUKA, it is difficult to determine whether reoperation risk is driven by treatment type, fixation of the mUKA, or PPTF timing, which is further discussed below.

The highest reoperation and dcTKA rates in this study were found after ORIF: 40% and 31% overall and 41% and 31% for early (≤4 months) PPTFs after cementless mUKA. These findings align with a recent national audit of early reoperations after mUKA, where 5 of 6 periprosthetic fractures treated with internal fixation later required revision surgery5. This poor outcome may partly reflect that most ORIF procedures were for early PPTFs after cementless mUKA (39 of 62). These fractures are typically atraumatic and more related to the primary surgery than external trauma23. As a result, they may develop gradually and be diagnosed after callus formation, making anatomical reduction difficult or impossible. This can cause malalignment and instability, altering knee biomechanics and compromising prosthesis function, thereby increasing the risk of failure and need for dcTKA. dcTKA is further complicated by axial deformity, soft tissue contracture, and periarticular bone changes, warranting caution when selecting ORIF as treatment2.

In this study, nonoperative treatment was commonly used for late PPTFs, which were more frequent after cemented mUKA, and was rarely used for early PPTFs. This suggests that late PPTFs may differ from early PPTFs, which are often nontraumatic, typically involve medial plateau displacement with an unstable prosthesis, and therefore require surgery23,24. The reoperation rate and need for dcTKA after nonoperative treatment of all PPTFs following any mUKA in this study align with a recent meta-analysis by Burger et al.23 where 10 of 44 nonoperatively treated cases required reoperation, and 9 eventually received a TKA. These findings confirm the importance of appropriate first-line treatment also in fall-related PPTFs happening later after mUKA as patients requiring dcTKA after failed nonoperative PPTF treatment may face longer recovery than if cTKA had been performed initially.

Although reoperation risk was lowest in PPTF patients treated with cTKA, this option still carried some risk of re-revision, with fewer than 5 of 69 cases requiring rTKA within 2 years. This aligns with the reported re-revision risk of 8.8% for all revision knee arthroplasties at 3 years6. Thus, prosthesis survival of cTKA for PPTF after mUKA resembles rTKA for other indications and may not be comparable with prosthesis survival after a primary TKA7,8.

Strengths and Limitation

This study uses data from 2 comprehensive national registries, the DKR and DNPR, both with high data completeness, reducing the risk of information bias16,25. The nationwide design reduces selection bias and enhances the applicability of findings to comparable healthcare systems. However, the predominantly Scandinavian ethnicity of the Danish population may limit generalizability to more diverse populations outside northwestern Europe. While the cohort size does not allow for robust statistical analysis, all eligible patients were included, and to our knowledge, this represents the largest study to date examining reoperations after PPTF.

A common limitation of registry-based studies, including this one, is reliance on diagnostic and procedural coding. However, as both exposure and outcomes were defined by surgical procedures, and accurate coding is essential for departmental reimbursement, data quality is presumed high. Another limitation is the lack of fracture classification and radiographic data, preventing severity assessment and warrants cautious interpretation of the results. The indications for both dcTKA and rTKA were unknown to us, as most indications were recorded simply as “mechanical complication to prosthesis” or “other.”

Conclusion

ORIF as initial treatment of PPTF was associated with higher rates of reoperation and dcTKA (40 and 31%, respectively) compared with both cTKA (7% risk of reoperation) and nonoperative treatment (28% and 21%, respectively). This trend remained consistent when analyzing early PPTFs after cementless mUKA separately. These data suggest that careful consideration especially before using ORIF as treatment in cases that may not be reduced anatomically. However, as fracture severity was unknown, the elevated failure rates after ORIF cannot be conclusively attributed to the treatment method.
