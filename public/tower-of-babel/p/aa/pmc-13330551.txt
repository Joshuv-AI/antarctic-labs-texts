Ultrasound modified flaxseed protein-rice starch composite system for texture-modified soft gels: structural, functional, and swallowing properties
Authors: Cong Min, Guanglan Li, Huifeng Yang, Deyin Pan, Peng Guo, Wenxian Zeng, Hongsheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Swallowing disorders, known as dysphagia, pose a significant health challenge for a growing elderly population and individuals with neurological impairments [1]. To address the challenges of dysphagia management, texture-modified foods (primarily gel systems) with tailored structural and functional properties are in high demand, where the regulation of biopolymer interactions is critical for gel formation [2]. Such texture-modified foods must simultaneously provide essential nutrition and possess mechanical characteristics that ensure easy, residue-free swallowing without posing a choking or aspiration hazard. Among the various food macromolecules, starch and protein are two fundamental biopolymers widely used as gelling agents and texture modifiers in food systems. Their interactions critically govern the formation, stability, and oral processing behavior of the gel matrix [3]. Consequently, a thorough understanding and rational manipulation of protein–starch interactions are essential for designing tailored food matrices for specific dietary needs, including dysphagia management.

Starch, particularly from rice, is a common choice for thickening and gelling due to its neutral flavor, high digestibility, and excellent gel-forming properties upon heating and cooling [4]. However, starch-based gels alone often suffer from syneresis, retrogradation and insufficient mechanical strength, which limit their ability to maintain a stable, cohesive bolus during swallowing [5]. Conversely, plant proteins can form elastic gels and contribute to water binding, potentially compensating for the shortcomings of starch gels [6]. Flaxseed protein (FP), an emerging plant protein, constitutes 32%-49% of flaxseed cake and is noted for its excellent nutritional profiles, including richness in arginine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and an essential amino acid profile similar to soy protein, along with multiple health benefits [7]. However, its high denaturation temperature (115–141 °C) prevents sufficient unfolding during typical heating, and this results in a moderate-to-weak gelling ability, presenting a key challenge for its application [8]. According to previous studies, the combination of plant protein and starch could theoretically lead to a composite gel with superior textural and nutritional properties [9], [10]. Nevertheless, the nature of the interactions between FP and rice starch (RS) within a composite gel system remains poorly understood, and the final gel properties are highly dependent on the processing conditions employed [11]. There is therefore a clear need to identify processing strategies that can adequately unfold FP and promote its synergistic interaction with RS, thereby overcoming their individual weaknesses and yielding a composite network with the cohesive, moldable, and low-adhesive texture required for safe swallowing.

In recent years, non-thermal physical processing technologies, such as ultrasound, have gained considerable attention for their ability to modify the structural and functional properties of biopolymers [12]. High-intensity ultrasound generates cavitation effects, which induce mechanical shear, local thermal gradients, and chemical reactions in liquid media [13]. For protein-polysaccharide mixtures, these ultrasonic effects can disrupt the tertiary/quaternary structure of proteins, break down protein aggregates, and fragment starch granules, thereby regulating biopolymer interactions and subsequent gelation. These modifications can profoundly influence the subsequent gelation process and the final gel network. For instance, ultrasonication has been shown to strengthen gels in various systems, including individual proteins, starches, and macromolecular composites [14], [15], [16]. However, the most of these investigations have focused on ultrasonic modification of common macronutrients such as soy and pea proteins [17]. A systematic investigation into how ultrasonic pretreatment modulates the co-gelation of FP and RS, as well as the underlying molecular forces governing the formation of their composite gel network, is still lacking. Moreover, despite the growing adoption of the International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) framework for evaluating swallowing safety [18], ultrasonically modified composite gels have rarely been assessed against its specific classification levels. This knowledge gap hinders the targeted application of ultrasound in fabricating functional food ingredients for specific populations.

Therefore, the present study aims to elucidate the impact of ultrasonic pretreatment on the structural, functional, and swallowing properties of the FP-RS composite gel, with a particular focus on its potential application in texture-modified dysphagia diets. It was hypothesized that controlled ultrasonic pretreatment would partially denature FP and fragment RS granules, thereby promoting more intimate biopolymer interactions, facilitating the formation of a denser and more homogeneous three-dimensional gel network, and ultimately regulating the structural and textural properties. The thermal stability, textural properties, swallowing characteristics, molecular forces and microstructure were investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning calorimetry analysis (DSC), textural profile analysis (TPA), IDDSI tests, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM) and spectroscopic techniques. This work provides fundamental insights into the ultrasonic modulation mechanism of biopolymer co-gelation, and offers a theoretical basis for the rational design of plant-based composite gel systems via green non-thermal processing.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemical reagents

RS (with a purity ≥90%, CAS No. 9005-25-8) and FP (81% protein) flour were purchased from Shaanxi Kangduopu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, Shaanxi, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Preparation of the samples

The gel samples were prepared according to the method reported by Min et al. [19] with minor adaptations. Both RS and FP powders were sieved through a 100-mesh sieve. The FP powder was dispersed in deionized water in a 100 ml beaker. The aqueous suspension of FP was then added to a beaker containing the RS powder to prepare a final suspension with concentrations of 15% (w/v) RS and 2% (w/v) FP. The suspension was stirred at 300 rpm using a magnetic stirrer at room temperature (22 ± 1 °C) for 20 min, followed by ultrasonic treatment. The ultrasonic treatment was performed using an ultrasonic bath (SB-5200DTD, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China) operating at a fixed frequency of 40 kHz with a nominal ultrasonic power of 360 W and a tank capacity of 10 L (internal dimensions: 300 mm × 240 mm × 150 mm) at 25 °C for 0, 20, 40, 60, or 80 min. The estimated volumetric power density was approximately 54 W/L. Subsequently, the samples were stirred again with a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for 20 min, heated at 95 °C for 30 min, and then stored at 4 °C overnight to form gels.

Rheological properties

Rheological measurements were performed on the gel samples using an AR2000 rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA). A parallel plate geometry (40 mm diameter) with a gap of 1 mm was used, and each gel sample (20 mm diameter, 15 mm thickness) was carefully loaded onto the lower plate. After an equilibration time of 120 s, oscillatory frequency sweep tests were carried out over an angular frequency range of 0.6283 to 62.83 rad/s at a fixed strain amplitude of 1.0%.

Water-holding capacity (WHC)

The WHC of different gels was determined as follows. A 5.0 g sample of each gel was centrifuged at 10,000×g and 4 °C for 15 min. After discarding the supernatant, the remaining gel was blotted with filter paper to remove excess surface moisture. The weight of the gel sample along with the centrifuge tube was recorded at room temperature. The WHC was calculated as the ratio of the gel weight after centrifugation to the original gel weight.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

The texture profile of different gel samples was analyzed using a CT3 texture analyzer (Brookfield, Middleboro, MA, USA). Gel sample with a diameter of 20 mm and a height of 15 mm was equilibrated for 30 min prior to testing. Subsequently, compression tests were conducted using a probe under the following parameters: a test speed of 1.0 mm/s, a target deformation of 3.0 mm, and two compression cycles.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal properties of the gel samples were investigated using thermogravimetric analyzer (Q50, TA Instruments Inc., New Castle, DE, USA). Approximately 10.0 mg of the freeze-dried gel powder (lyophilized for 48 h) was placed in an open alumina crucible. An empty crucible of the same type was used as a reference. The analysis was performed under a continuous nitrogen purge (flow rate: 50 mL/min) by heating the sample from 30 °C to 600 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C/min. The mass change signals were recorded throughout the heating process.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis

The freeze-dried gel powder samples were further subjected to DSC analysis using a analyzer (Q2000, TA Instruments Inc., New Castle, DE, USA). Approximately 3.0 mg of each powder sample was accurately weighed and hermetically sealed in an aluminum pan. All samples were heated from 30 °C to 200 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. The onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc), and enthalpy change (ΔH) associated with the thermal transitions were determined from the resulting thermograms.

IDDSI tests

The swallowing properties of the different gel samples were evaluated according to the IDDSI testing methods [20]. For the spoon tilt test, a gel sample was placed on the surface of a spoon. Any initial change in the sample's appearance was first observed. The spoon was then tilted, and the sample was examined for ease of slipping off and for any residue left on the spoon surface, with the results documented photographically. For the fork compression test, a gel sample was placed on a flat laboratory bench. A fork was used to compress the sample with the thumb, applying pressure until the thumbnail blanched (which corresponds to a pressure of approximately 17 kPa, simulating the tongue force during swallowing and mimicking the mechanical action in the oral cavity). Following the test, the deformation and particle size of the gel were observed and recorded photographically. The gel behavior was classified based on the IDDSI framework descriptors. All assessments were performed independently by two trained researchers. Prior to formal testing, both researchers underwent repeated practice following the IDDSI framework guidelines to consistently achieve the thumbnail blanching endpoint, which corresponds to an approximate force of 17 kPa. Each sample was evaluated by both researchers independently, and any discrepancies in classification were resolved through discussion with a third assessor.

Particle size and zeta potential tests

The particle size and zeta potential of the gel samples were analyzed using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 analyzer (Malvern Instruments, Ltd., Worcestershire, UK), following the method described by Min et al. [21]. Briefly, the freeze-dried gel powder was dispersed in deionized water at room temperature for measurements. The instrument parameters were set as follows: temperature at 25 °C, equilibration time of 3 min, and the number of runs was 3.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The FTIR spectra of different gel samples were recorded using a Vertex 70 FTIR spectrometer (Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany) operated in transmission mode according to the method of Min et al. [22]. Prior to analysis, the gel samples were freeze-dried for 48 h and ground into fine powder. The powdered samples were then mixed with KBr (CAS No. 7758-02-3) and compressed into pellets. Spectral data were collected in the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution set at 4 cm−1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The relative crystallinity of different gel samples was determined using an X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean, PANalytical, Almelo, The Netherlands). The freeze-dried samples (lyophilized for 48 h) were ground into powders and uniformly packed into the XRD sample holder. The powder was then carefully pressed to form a smooth and flat surface for measurement. The diffraction patterns were acquired under the following operational parameters: a 2θ range from 5° to 40° with a constant scanning rate of 5°/min. The relative crystallinity was quantitatively estimated using JADE 6.0 software, defined as the ratio of the crystalline peak area to the total area, the latter comprising both the crystalline and amorphous regions.

Contact angles measurements

Static contact angle measurements were performed on the gel samples using an OCA20 optical contact angle analyzer (DataPhysics, Filderstadt, Germany) employing the sessile drop technique. Prior to measurement, the freeze-dried gels were compressed into smooth pellets. A 3 μL droplet of deionized water was automatically deposited onto the pellet surface, and the static contact angle was recorded. Three separate measurements were performed on different surface locations of each sample to ensure reproducibility, and the values were averaged.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructural characteristics of different gel samples were examined using an Thermo Apreo 2S scanning electron microscope (SEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Oregon, USA). The freeze-dried gels were mounted on sample stage using conductive adhesive tape and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold prior to imaging. The coated samples were then transferred into the SEM chamber, and representative micrographs were captured under high vacuum conditions using the secondary electron imaging mode at a magnification of 350× to document the structural features.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

The AFM observations were conducted according to the method described by Min et al. [23]. The gel samples were freeze-dried for 48 h and ground into a fine powder, which was then passed through a 100-mesh sieve. The powdered sample was dissolved and diluted in deionized water to a concentration of 10 μg/mL. Subsequently, 5 μL of the sample solution was deposited onto a freshly cleaved mica substrate (approximately 15 mm in diameter) and vacuum-dried for 3 h. AFM imaging was conducted using a Dimension Icon atomic force microscope (Bruker Co., Bremen, Germany). The scans were performed over a 5 μm × 5 μm area in tapping mode, with a cantilever spring constant of 0.4 N/m and a resonance frequency of 1 Hz.

Data analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's test in SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A probability value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Rheological properties

After ultrasonic treatment, all tested samples formed opaque, smooth, and soft gels. The rheological measurements revealed distinct viscoelastic properties for the FP-RS composite gels subjected to different ultrasonic pretreatment times, as shown in Fig. 1. All gel samples exhibited a predominantly elastic behavior, with storage modulus (G') values significantly higher than the corresponding loss modulus (G'') across the entire frequency range, indicating the formation of stable three-dimensional network structures. Notably, the G' values of all groups increased slightly with increasing angular frequency, displaying a weak frequency dependence. This behavior is characteristic of cross-linked biopolymer gels, where the three-dimensional network can resist structural breakdown under low-to-moderate oscillatory shear, but shows slight reinforcement at higher frequencies due to reduced relaxation time of molecular chains [18].Fig. 1The storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G'') of flaxseed protein (FP) and rice starch (RS) composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

The storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G'') of flaxseed protein (FP) and rice starch (RS) composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

Interestingly, the untreated control gel (0 min) exhibited significantly higher G' and G'' values over the tested frequency range compared to all ultrasonicated samples. The considerably higher viscoelastic modulus of the control gel suggests that native, untreated FP and RS formed a relatively stiff, likely starch-dominated network with high elastic modulus, as discussed by Min et al. [19]. However, this network may be characterized by poor water retention and internal cohesion. In contrast, ultrasonic treatment disrupted the original coarse network, leading to a decrease in G'. However, the extent of this decrease was highly dependent on treatment time. Specifically, at 40 min of ultrasonication, the G' value was approximately 2.4-fold lower than that of the control gel, but 3.0-fold higher than that of the 20 min gel. This phenomenon might be attributed to the disruption of large starch granules and protein aggregates by moderate ultrasonication, followed by their reassembly into a homogeneous and dense three-dimensional network [16]. Further prolongation of ultrasonication to 60 and 80 min resulted in progressively lower G' values. This decline suggests that excessive ultrasonic energy induced structural degradation, possibly due to protein over-aggregation and excessive fragmentation of starch chains, resulting in a weaker and less cohesive gel network [13], [15].

WHC

The water-holding capacity of the composite gels was significantly modulated by the duration of ultrasonic pretreatment, exhibiting a clear non-linear dependence. As shown in Fig. 2A, the WHC values significantly improved from 83.66% in the untreated control (0 min) to a maximum of 94.98% after 40 min of treatment. This represents a notable enhancement in the gel's ability to immobilize water within its network. Hu et al. [24] also confirmed that ultrasonic pretreatment improves the dispersion uniformity of cassava starch within the composite gel of starch and myofibrillar protein through intense shear forces and turbulent effects, thereby further enhancing the water-binding capacity in the gel system. However, further extension of ultrasonic treatment led to a significant reduction in WHC. The values decreased to 92.34% and 91.89% for the 60 min and 80 min treatment groups, respectively. A comparable phenomenon regarding WHC was reported in the work of Ji et al. [25], whose research on tunable rice protein-starch soft gels echoes this finding.Fig. 2The water-holding capacity (WHC) (A), TGA and DTG curves (B), degradation temperature and weight loss rate (C), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves (D) of flaxseed protein (FP) and rice starch (RS) composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time. This trend arises from the dual effects of ultrasound on the gel network. Moderate ultrasonication (20–40 min) promoted a finer, more homogeneous three-dimensional network, as partial denaturation of flaxseed protein exposed hydrophilic groups and starch fragmentation increased water-binding sites, thereby enhancing WHC [26]. In contrast, prolonged treatment (60–80 min) induced structural degradation, with excessive protein aggregation and starch over-fragmentation leading to a coarser network and larger pores that reduced water entrapment [27]. The ultrasonic pretreatment time plays a critical role in modulating the WHC of FP-RS composite gels by influencing colloidal interactions and inducing structural reorganization, with 40 min representing a key threshold in this mechanistic transition.

Textural characteristics

The ultrasonic pretreatment time significantly affected the textural properties of FP-RS composite gels, which are crucial for its potential application in texture-modified diets [3]. The TPA parameters, including hardness, gumminess, adhesiveness, and cohesiveness, exhibited distinct, non-linear trends (Table 1). Hardness increased with prolonged ultrasonication, reaching a peak of 0.86 N at 40 min, representing an approximate 31% increase compared to the untreated control. The initial increase (0–40 min) was attributed to moderate ultrasound-induced partial protein denaturation and starch fragmentation, which resulted in a denser, more cross-linked gel matrix capable of withstanding greater force [26], [28]. In contrast, hardness decreased after 60 min, suggesting that excessive ultrasonic energy led to network weakening, likely due to polymer chain over-degradation or protein over-aggregation [13], consistent with the observed decline in WHC. The gumminess, which represents the energy required to disintegrate a semi-solid food into a swallowable bolus, followed a pattern similar to hardness. It increased significantly, peaking at 23.07 for the 40 min sample. This indicates that gels formed from optimally treated mixtures exhibit greater resistance to mechanical disintegration during oral processing [23]. From a structural perspective, this parameter must be carefully balanced, excessively high gumminess may hinder bolus formation, while insufficient gumminess could lead to premature network breakdown under shear [30]. The adhesiveness, representing the work required to overcome attractive forces between the gel surface and contact material (e.g., oral mucosa), remained relatively stable or slightly decreased from 0 to 60 min before sharply increasing to a maximum of 0.64 at 80 min. This late-stage rise suggests a shift in surface and bulk properties upon prolonged ultrasonication. While moderate treatment strengthens the internal network (increasing hardness), excessive treatment may promote the exposure of adhesive protein residues or generate a more surface-active polymeric blend [24]. This elevated adhesiveness at 80 min reflects a change in surface characteristics that could influence oral processing behavior [31]. The cohesiveness, reflecting the internal strength of the gel and its ability to withstand a second deformation relative to the first, also reached its maximum value of 0.26 at the 40 min pretreatment. This indicates that the network formed under optimal sonication is not only strong but also uniformly bonded, allowing it to undergo deformation rather than fracture [19]. The subsequent reduction in cohesiveness at longer times suggests the formation of a more brittle or heterogeneous network with reduced internal structural integrity.

Taken together, ultrasonic pretreatment enables precise modulation of the textural properties of the FP-RS composite gel through alterations in network formation. A treatment duration of 40–60 min markedly modulates gel hardness and gumminess, reflecting changes in network integrity. Notably, 40 min of treatment serves as a critical inflection point, yielding a gel with distinct hardness, gumminess, and cohesiveness-characteristics associated with a cohesive network structure [3]. The decline in certain textural parameters after prolonged ultrasonication (80 min) reflects a transition in the structural regime, highlighting the sensitivity of the gel network to acoustic energy input.

Thermal characteristics

TGA

The TGA was employed to elucidate the effect of ultrasonic pretreatment on the thermal properties of FP-RS composite gels. Results revealed that the duration of ultrasonic pretreatment exerted a significant and nonlinear regulatory effect on the thermal behavior of the composite gels (Fig. 2 B and C). As the ultrasonication time increased from 0 to 40 min, the initial degradation temperature of the gel rose significantly from 293.4 °C to 298.3 °C, while the corresponding weight-loss rate decreased from 78.7% to 76.2%, indicating enhanced thermal stability. This improvement can be attributed to the changes in protein molecules and starch granules induced by moderate ultrasonic treatment, as discussed earlier, which strengthens the intermolecular interactions and forms a denser gel network. This reinforced network acts as a thermal barrier, effectively delaying the thermal cleavage of polymer chains [32].

However, when the ultrasonication time was extended to 60 and 80 min, the initial degradation temperature showed a decreasing trend (e.g., 295.4 °C at 80 min), and the weight-loss rate increased again. This suggests that excessive ultrasonication may compromise the integrity of the gel network and introduce structural defects by altering molecular structures, thereby reducing its thermal stability. Thirunavookarasu et al. [28] also reported that moderate ultrasound treatment could promote the formation of soy protein isolate–rice starch complexes and enhance their thermal stability, whereas excessive ultrasound treatment resulted in a loose structure of the complexes and a decline in thermal stability. The DTG curves further indicated that the main weight-loss stage for all samples occurred in the range of 250–350 °C, corresponding to the cooperative thermal decomposition of starch and protein components. Notably, the 40-min sample exhibited the flattest weight-loss curve in this high-temperature region, confirming that its network structure possessed optimal uniformity and heat resistance.

DSC

The DSC was used to investigate the influence of ultrasonic treatment on starch gelatinization within the composite system (Fig. 2D and Table 2). The onset temperature (T0) increased slightly from 65.9 °C (0 min) to a maximum of 67.6 °C at 20 min of ultrasonication, then decreased progressively to 62.1 °C at 80 min. The peak temperature (Tp) followed a similar trend, rising from 84.7 °C to 86.6 °C at 20 min and declining to 81.6 °C at 80 min. The initial increases in T0 and Tp at short ultrasonication times likely reflect the preferential disruption of amorphous starch regions, which transiently increased the proportion of more thermally stable crystalline domains [33]. At longer treatment times, the progressive fragmentation of crystalline lamellae and enhanced molecular mobility led to reduced thermal stability of the residual native crystallites, as reflected by lower T0 and Tp values. The enthalpy (ΔH) change exhibited a pronounced non‑monotonic dependence on ultrasonication time. The ΔH value decreased from 38.2 J/g (0 min) to 33.1 J/g at 20 min and reached a minimum of 32.8 J/g at 40 min. The decrease in ΔH indicates that moderate ultrasonic treatment effectively disrupted the native crystalline structure of RS, reducing the total amount of ordered regions that need to be melted during gelatinization [28]. Nikolaou et al. [35] also demonstrated that ultrasound treatment decreased the enthalpy values of the potato starch–pea protein composite system. However, at 60 min, ΔH increased sharply to 45.2 J/g, exceeding even the untreated control. This anomalous rise is attributed to extensive starch chain scission induced by prolonged ultrasonication, which generated short linear fragments with enhanced molecular mobility [36]. These fragments could readily reassociate into densely packed ordered structures or starch–protein complexes that require greater energy to dissociate during the DSC heating scan [27]. Concurrently, the conclusion temperature (Tc) at 60 min was significantly higher (116.7 °C) than all other groups, corroborating the formation of more thermally resistant ordered domains. At 80 min, ΔH partially declined to 36.7 J/g, and both T0 and Tp dropped to their lowest values, suggesting that excessive ultrasonication ultimately compromised the structural integrity of starch to an extent that limited further ordered reassociation.

It should be noted that the elevated ΔH at 60 min does not necessarily reflect a higher degree of pre‑existing ordered structure in the original gel. Rather, it may largely originate from the reassociation of extensively fragmented starch chains into densely packed ordered domains during the DSC heating scan itself [32]. These reassembled structures require additional energy to dissociate, contributing to the apparent ΔH without translating into improved gel functionality. The native gel at 60 min was already compromised by protein over‑aggregation and starch degradation, as evidenced by the concurrent decline in WHC and mechanical properties. Therefore, the high ΔH reflects the reassociation potential of the fragmented chains under thermal scanning, rather than the structural quality of the gel at ambient conditions.

The interpretation of the ΔH trend is supported by the FTIR and XRD results presented in later sections. The FTIR intensity ratio of the bands at 1047 and 1022 cm−1, a marker of short-range molecular order, decreased progressively up to 40 min and then stabilized, indicating that fragmented starch chains reassembled into new short-range ordered domains [27]. Concurrently, the XRD relative crystallinity increased progressively with ultrasonication time, suggesting that prolonged treatment promoted long-range structural reorganization [32]. Notably, this structural reorganization did not translate into improved functional properties, as the concomitant network heterogeneity and protein over-aggregation led to deteriorated WHC and texture.

These findings demonstrate that 40 min of ultrasonic pretreatment is a critical threshold in modulating starch thermal behavior within the FP–RS composite system. At this condition, the balance between crystalline disruption and molecular reorganization yields the lowest ΔH, indicating minimal residual ordered structure requiring thermal dissociation, while maintaining adequate gelatinization temperatures. This distinctive thermal behavior aligns with the performance of the 40‑min sample in terms of WHC and textural strength. In contrast, the elevated ΔH at 60 min, despite reflecting newly formed ordered structures during heating, is associated with deteriorated WHC and texture, indicating that the nature and formation pathway of the ordered domains critically influence the macroscopic gel properties.

IDDSI

The International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) framework has established a standardized framework comprising consistent terminology and practical measurement methods to evaluate the flow and textural properties of liquids and foods specifically designed for individuals with dysphagia [20]. The IDDSI methods were employed to evaluate the swallowing safety level of the composite gels. The results, presented in Fig. 3, demonstrate that the ultrasonic pretreatment time significantly influenced the final IDDSI classification, exhibiting a trend of initial improvement followed by a decline.Fig. 3The IDDSI tests of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

The IDDSI tests of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

Untreated gels and those subjected to short treatment (20 min) were classified as IDDSI Level 4 (Pureed). These gels need pass the spoon tilt test and fork drip test, i.e., samples could hold the shape on spoon, not firm and sticky, and be easily fell off when spoon was tilted, little sample left on spoon; samples could sit in a mound or pile above the fork, a small amount may flow through and form a short tail below the fork, but did not dollop, flow or drip continuously through the fork prongs [20]. They exhibited sufficient cohesion to hold a shape on cutlery but showed slight flow and stickiness, sliding off as a whole when tilted. This indicates a relatively loose internal network with insufficient cohesiveness and hardness, consistent with the texture profile data [37]. Interestingly, Ren et al. [38] found that rice starch and soybean protein can also form Level 4 food using high concentrations of calcium salts (gluconate or chloride) as mediators.

Following ultrasonic pretreatment for 40 and 60 min, the IDDSI level improved to Level 5 (Minced & Moist). These gels must pass the fork pressure test, spoon tilt test and fork drip test, i.e., samples were soft enough to squash easily with fork, don’t need thumb nail to blanch white; samples could hold the shape on spoon and be easily fell off when spoon was tilted, very little sample left on spoon, not sticky; samples could mound on fork and did not fall through the fork [20]. They were characterized by improved moldability, allowing the samples to be easily mashed with a fork, and optimized cohesiveness and structural integrity, enabling them to remain stable in a mound on a fork without falling through while also sliding off a spoon cleanly when tilted [39]. This indicates the formation of a homogeneous network that balanced adequate cohesive strength with appropriate adhesiveness. This improvement corresponded to the peak hardness, gumminess, and cohesiveness values (Table 1), as well as the optimal WHC (Fig. 2A). Several recent studies have reported gel systems that achieved comparable or higher IDDSI levels. Specifically, Lv et al. [40] achieved IDDSI Levels 5 ∼ 6 by softening surimi gels with added polysaccharides, while Yan et al. [41] reached IDDSI Level 5 through synergistic gelation of a scallop male gonad hydrolysates/soy protein isolate binary system. In contrast, extending ultrasonication to 80 min led to regression to Level 4, consistent with declining textural parameters. This suggests that over-processing induced network weakening or excessive aggregation, thereby compromising structural homogeneity and cohesive strength required to maintain Level 5.

The IDDSI assessment confirms that ultrasonic pretreatment for 40–60 min can effectively elevate the swallowing level of the FP-RS composite gels from Level 4 to the Level 5. This change stems from the ultrasound-induced formation of a homogeneous gel network characterized by suitable hardness, good cohesiveness, and low adhesiveness [42]. On the other hand, both IDDSI Level 4 and Level 5 foods impose low demands on oral processing capability, requiring little to no mastication or merely minimal mastication during ingestion. Consumers can achieve bolus transportation solely by virtue of lingual movement, thereby endowing these two categories of food with high swallowing safety [20]. These findings provide direct empirical evidence supporting the potential application of ultrasonically modified FP-RS composite gels in dysphagia diets tailored to specific IDDSI levels. Notably, although both the 40 and 60 min ultrasonic treated gels meeting IDDSI Level 5, differences in textural properties were evident. The 40 min gel showed higher WHC, hardness, gumminess, and cohesiveness, reflecting a more homogeneous network that may better maintain bolus integrity during oral processing. The 60 min gel, though slightly lower in these parameters, still satisfied all Level 5 criteria and could offer a softer texture for patients with very weak tongue pressure. Importantly, this within-level tunability, achieved solely by adjusting ultrasonication time, might enable personalized texture design using a single clean-label formulation. Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. The IDDSI assessments rely on empirical simulations and cannot fully replicate the complex neuromuscular coordination, dynamic bolus breakdown, and salivary interactions during oral processing, further clinical validation remains necessary [18]. Additionally, neither the eating experience (taste, flavor, and mouthfeel) nor practical attributes (storage stability, syneresis, and in vitro digestibility) were evaluated, which may affect patient acceptance and compliance. These aspects warrant investigation in future studies to fully establish the practical suitability of the gels.

Particle size and zeta potential

The mean particle size of the FP-RS composite gels exhibited a significant and progressive decrease with increasing ultrasonic treatment time, as shown in Fig. 4A. The particle size value after 80 min of ultrasonication was approximately 30% lower than that of the untreated (0 min) control, with each incremental treatment duration resulting in a statistically significant reduction. This indicates that the particle size of the reconstituted freeze‑dried powders decreased monotonically with increasing ultrasonication time. It is important to note that these measurements were performed on rehydrated suspensions prepared from lyophilized gels, not on the intact hydrated gels. Therefore, the particle size primarily reflects the redispersibility and colloidal stability of the dried material after reconstitution, rather than the direct mechanical strength or fragmentation propensity of the original gel network. Feng et al. [43] reported that the conjugates formed between mung bean protein and mung bean starch after ultrasonic treatment also exhibited a significantly decreased particle size. The progressively smaller particle size with prolonged ultrasonication may be attributed to enhanced electrostatic repulsion upon rehydration [44], as supported by the zeta potential results discussed below, which limits particle aggregation and improves dispersion.Fig. 4The particle size (A), zeta potential (B), FTIR spectra (C), and protein secondary structure fractions (D) of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time. Different letters (a–e) indicate significant differences (P < 0.05).

The particle size (A), zeta potential (B), FTIR spectra (C), and protein secondary structure fractions (D) of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time. Different letters (a–e) indicate significant differences (P < 0.05).

Concurrently, the surface charge of the particles, as indicated by the zeta potential, showed a significant shift toward more negative values with prolonged ultrasonication (Fig. 4B). The zeta potential decreased from −5.51 mV (0 min) to −8.56 mV (80 min). The values for the 60 min and 80 min treatment groups were not significantly different from each other but were significantly more negative than all other groups. This increase in net negative surface charge is likely attributable to the exposure of previously buried anionic amino acid residues (e.g., from aspartic and glutamic acids) following ultrasound-induced partial denaturation and unfolding of flaxseed protein molecules [35], [44]. The stronger electrostatic repulsion associated with the more negative zeta potential would limit particle aggregation during rehydration, thereby contributing to the improved redispersibility and smaller apparent particle size observed for gels subjected to longer ultrasonication times. It should be emphasized that the particle size and zeta potential data characterize the colloidal properties of the reconstituted powders and do not directly inform on the structural integrity of the hydrated gel network, which is more reliably assessed by the rheological, textural, and microscopic analyses presented in other sections.

FTIR

The FTIR analysis was employed to elucidate the molecular-level structural changes and interactions within the FP-RS composite gels induced by ultrasonic pretreatment. The results showed no new and distinct peaks in the spectra, indicating that ultrasound primarily induced physical modifications rather than new covalent bonding between FP and RS (Fig. 4C). Significant changes in shape and intensity was observed in the broad band in hydroxyl stretching region (3000–3600 cm−1), which was resulted from O–H and N-H stretching vibrations of water, starch, and protein [19]. With moderate ultrasonication (20–40 min), the band became noticeably sharper and shifted slightly to lower wavenumbers, reflecting stronger and more ordered hydrogen bonding within the gel matrix. This enhancement in hydrogen bonding network integrity was attributed to the increased availability of hydroxyl groups from fragmented starch chains and the exposure of polar protein groups (e.g., –OH, –COOH, –NH2) due to unfolding [17], [44]. Thirunavookarasu et al. [28] also reported that ultrasound treatment in the soy protein–rice starch system strengthened hydrogen-bonding capability via conformational alterations and the exposure of reactive residues including glutamine. This strengthened hydrogen-bonded network is the molecular foundation for the improved water immobilization (higher WHC). Prolonged treatment (>40 min) caused band broadening again, suggesting a less homogeneous or partially disrupted hydrogen-bonding environment, consistent with the degradation of functional properties upon over-processing.

The Amide I (1600–1700 cm−1) and Amide II (1500–1600 cm−1) bands, primarily arising from the C=O stretching (Amide I) and N–H bending/C–N stretching (Amide II) vibrations of protein [22], exhibited clear alterations with ultrasound treatment. The Amide I band centered at 1650–1660 cm−1 were mainly attributed to α‑helix, accompanied by components corresponding to β‑sheet (1610–1640 cm−1), β-turn (1660–1695 cm−1) and random coil (1640–1650 cm−1) [45]. The compositional changes in protein secondary structure were determined through deconvolution and multi-peak fitting of the Amide I band (Fig. 4D). The β-sheet content increased progressively from 38.24% (0 min) to a maximum of 45.12% at 40 min, while the α-helix content decreased from 24.67% to 19.48%, and the random coil content declined from 22.15% to 18.26%. Similar changes in the α-helix and β-sheet contents were also observed by Xu et al. [46] in ultrasound treated curdlan-pea protein gel networks. This trend indicates that moderate ultrasonication promoted the conversion of α-helical and random coil structures into β-sheet conformations, likely through ultrasound-induced protein unfolding that exposed hydrophobic patches and facilitated intermolecular hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic association [42], [43]. The elevated β-sheet content is consistent with the formation of a more extensively cross-linked and homogeneous gel network, as supported by the enhanced WHC, hardness, and cohesiveness observed at 40 min. At prolonged ultrasonication times (60–80 min), the β-sheet content decreased slightly while α-helix and random coil fractions partially recovered, suggesting that excessive ultrasonication induced protein over-aggregation, which disrupted the ordered intermolecular β-sheet alignment and led to a less organized network [46]. This secondary structure transition pattern corroborates the non-monotonic changes in gel functionality and microstructure discussed in earlier sections.

The bands between 1000 and 1200 cm−1, associated with C–O and C–C stretching vibrations in the starch glucopyranose ring, reflected ultrasound-induced starch modification [47]. The intensity ratio of the bands at 1047 cm−1 to 1022 cm−1, an indicator of starch ordered structure, decreased with increasing ultrasonication time up to 40 min. This indicates a reduction in short-range molecular order due to cavitation-induced fragmentation of starch granules and molecular chains [48]. This disruption of starch's native ordered structure increased free hydroxyl groups and enhanced hydration capacity, contributing to the gel’s water-binding properties. Additionally, spectral changes in this region suggested increased molecular mobility and compatibility between disordered starch chains and denatured protein, facilitating their interaction [28]. These findings demonstrated that ultrasonic treatment strengthened non-covalent interactions, particularly hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, between FP and RS, which likely underlies the formation of a finer and more homogeneous gel microstructure.

XRD

X-ray diffraction was employed to investigate the influence of ultrasonic pretreatment on the crystalline or ordered structural features within the FP–RS composite gels. The XRD patterns (Fig. 5) exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks at approximately 2θ = 7.5°, 13°, and 20°, confirming that the composite gels possess a certain degree of semi-crystalline organization arising primarily from the starch component [28]. The low-angle peak at 7.5° suggests a layered or lamellar structure, while the strong peak at 20° is characteristic of B- or V-type crystalline patterns [44], [49]. With increasing ultrasonication time, the relative crystallinity gradually increased from 13.99% (0 min) to 16.68% (80 min). No significant peak broadening or peak shift was observed, indicating that the average size and type of crystalline domains remained largely unchanged. The increase in relative crystallinity with prolonged ultrasonication, despite the reduction in absolute crystallinity suggested by DSC (decreased ΔH), suggests that ultrasound preferentially disrupted amorphous regions of starch while leaving crystalline domains relatively intact [50]. At 40 min, the moderate crystallinity contributed to optimal gel network formation by providing sufficient ordered domains as physical cross-linking points without compromising network homogeneity. However, at 80 min, the excessive relative crystallinity, potentially associated with protein over-aggregation or starch retrogradation, correlated with functional deterioration, including reduced WHC and decreased IDDSI level. These findings suggest that an optimal balance between ordered and amorphous regions is critical for achieving desirable gel functionality.Fig. 5The relative crystallinity of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

The relative crystallinity of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

The persistence of crystallinity under ultrasound can be explained by two competing effects: cavitation-induced disruption of native structures, which reduced long-range order, and enhanced molecular mobility and intermolecular alignment, which could facilitate the nucleation and growth of new ordered structures [15], [17]. The observed peak pattern suggests that moderate ultrasonication may optimize this balance, leading to a gel network integrating amorphous hydrated regions (responsible for high WHC and soft texture) with ordered domains (contributing to gel hardness and cohesiveness). This structural duality enables the combination of high water retention with sufficient mechanical strength, which is beneficial for maintaining favorable palatability and bolus integrity during swallowing [23].

Contact angles

The surface wettability of the composite gels, as directly assessed by static contact angle measurements, was profoundly and non-monotonically influenced by the duration of ultrasonic pretreatment (Fig. 6A). The untreated control gel exhibited a contact angle of 38.6°, confirming its intrinsically hydrophilic nature. A significant decrease to 33.2° was observed after 20 min of treatment. Most notably, the contact angle reached a minimum of 20.4° at the 40 min pretreatment mark, representing an approximately 47% enhancement in surface hydrophilicity. This sharp decline suggests that moderate ultrasonication drastically altered the gel-air interface. It might be attributed to the optimal exposure of polar groups, such as hydroxyl groups from starch and ionizable carboxyl/amino groups from partially denatured flaxseed protein, to the surface, driven by ultrasound-induced molecular rearrangement as discussed earlier [44]. However, this enhancing effect was not sustained with prolonged treatment. The contact angle rebounded to 32.1° and 36.2° for the 60 min and 80 min samples, respectively, indicating a reduction in surface hydrophilicity. This reversal likely signifies the onset of structural over-processing, where excessive ultrasound may induce the alteration of polymer structure, leading to the re-burial of hydrophilic moieties and a consequent shift in surface energy [17], [47].Fig. 6Contact angles on the surfaces (A) and SEM images (×350) (B) of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

Contact angles on the surfaces (A) and SEM images (×350) (B) of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

Crucially, the trend in surface wettability aligns coherently with the bulk functional properties. The point of maximum surface hydrophilicity (40 min) corresponds exactly with the sample exhibiting the peak WHC. This correlation underscores a unified mechanism: ultrasound pretreatment at its optimum simultaneously creates a highly hydrophilic interface and a highly water-retentive bulk network. Both phenomena likely stem from the same molecular event—the balanced and extensive exposure of water-binding sites on the biopolymers [17]. Conversely, the decline in surface hydrophilicity following over-processing parallels the deterioration in WHC, providing complementary evidence that excessive ultrasonic energy ultimately compromises the gel's overall hydrophilic character and structural integrity.

Microstructure

SEM

The microstructure of FP–RS composite gels was examined using SEM (Fig. 6B). The untreated gel (0 min) exhibited a relatively coarse and heterogeneous structure with irregular pores (ranging from a few to tens of micrometers) and pronounced aggregates, indicative of weak protein–starch interactions and a poorly organized network [51]. After 20 min of ultrasonication, the gel structure became more homogeneous, characterized by an increased number of pores, a more uniform pore size distribution, and a finer, less aggregated network, suggesting that moderate ultrasound promoted molecular interactions and network homogeneity. At 40 min, the gel network was further optimized, displaying larger, interconnected pores and a more continuous three-dimensional architecture, which underpinned the observed improvements in WHC, mechanical properties, and stability. This optimal structure resulted from balanced ultrasonication-induced protein unfolding, moderate starch fragmentation, and uniform dispersion, facilitating the formation of a stable interpenetrating network through enhanced hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S1) [44]. However, prolonged ultrasonication (60 and 80 min) led to adverse structural changes, including localized collapse, heterogeneous aggregation, and partial fragmentation, despite the overall network integrity being maintained. These effects were attributed to excessive disruption of molecular structures induced by prolonged ultrasonication, which compromises the stability of the gel network [17]. Additionally, excessive ultrasonic treatment may generate an overabundance of free radicals, potentially triggering adverse reactions such as oxidation of proteins and starch, thereby negatively affecting the structure and properties of the composite gels [52]. These results demonstrate that ultrasonic treatment modulates the gel microstructure in a time-dependent manner, with moderate treatment promoting a uniform and stable network, whereas excessive treatment causes structural degradation, consistent with the observed variations in gel functionality.

AFM

The nanoscale surface topography, molecular chain height variations, and roughness of the FP-RS composite gels are depicted in Fig. 7. The two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) height images, along with quantitative roughness parameters, revealed a non-monotonic effect of ultrasonication time on the surface morphology of FP-RS composite gels. The untreated gel (0 min) exhibited a relatively rough and heterogeneous topography with noticeable undulations and the presence of nanoscale aggregates and depressions. After 20 min of ultrasonication, the surface roughness decreased, large aggregated protrusions were reduced, and structural uniformity was improved. Notably, the gel treated for 40 min displayed a smooth and homogeneous surface, characterized by a continuous layered structure with negligible nanoscale protrusions or depressions. This visually demonstrates that moderate ultrasonic energy promotes dispersion and interaction between protein and starch molecules, leading to the formation of a dense and uniform nanoscale network [44]. However, when the treatment was further extended to 60 and 80 min, although the surface morphology remained relatively smooth, re-emergent nanoscale particulate structures and localized cluster-like protrusions and depressions could be observed. This suggests that excessive ultrasonication may induce local rearrangement of molecular chains or microdomain aggregation [15], [17].Fig. 7The atomic force micrographsm, molecular chain height profiles, and surface roughness parameters of FP-RS composite gels with different ultrasonic pretreatment time.

The height distribution analysis (Fig. 7) supported this visual assessment. Minimal height variation was observed at 40 min, indicating that ultrasonic forces modified macromolecular structures, promoting molecular dispersion and interpenetration to form a dense and flat network. Prolonged treatment (60–80 min) led to a slight increase in roughness and height heterogeneity, likely due to the reorganization of excessively denatured protein chains into smaller aggregates or the non-ideal reassembly of fragmented starch molecules [17]. Root-mean-square roughness (Rq) and average roughness (Ra) followed a similar trend, decreasing significantly from 15.1 nm and 5.78 nm (0 min) to minima of 1.4 nm and 0.93 nm at 40 min, respectively, confirming the smoothing effect of optimal ultrasonication. Beyond 40 min, both parameters increased slightly (e.g., Rq = 1.83 nm at 80 min), although remaining lower than the control, indicating a partial loss of nanoscale homogeneity.

These nanoscale evidences suggest that 40 min of ultrasonic pretreatment is optimal for constructing a uniform, dense, and continuous FP-RS gel network. This optimal nanostructure, characterized by minimal surface roughness and homogeneous height distribution, forms the physical foundation for the concurrently observed enhancements in WHC, textural strength, and thermal stability. Conversely, the subtle nanostructural deterioration upon over-processing aligns with the decline in these macroscopic functional properties, underscoring the critical link between nanoscale organization and overall gel performance.

Schematic mechanism

Based on the aforementioned findings, this study proposes a potential schematic mechanism to elucidate the impact of ultrasonic pretreatment on the FP-RS composite gels (Fig. 8). The core of the mechanism lies in the dual and time-dependent effects of ultrasonic cavitation and mechanical shear forces on the conformational state of FP, the structural integrity of RS, and their subsequent interactive assembly. In the absence of ultrasonic treatment, the composite gel exists in a relatively “loose” and heterogeneous state. Only weak non-specific interactions occur between native FP and RS, forming a coarse, uneven, and porous network (evidenced by SEM and AFM results). This results in lower WHC, softer texture, inferior thermal stability, and classification as IDDSI Level 4.Fig. 8Schematic diagram involved in the gelation of FP-RS composite gels with ultrasonic pretreatment.

Schematic diagram involved in the gelation of FP-RS composite gels with ultrasonic pretreatment.

Moderate ultrasonic treatment, particularly at 40 min, causes partial denaturation and unfolding of the FP globular proteins. This exposes previously buried hydrophobic regions and, crucially, increases the accessibility of hydrophilic groups from peptide backbones and polar side chains [17]. Simultaneously, cavitation leads to the physical fragmentation of RS granules and disruption of their partial crystalline domains, increasing the number of free hydroxyl groups and reducing molecular order [15]. These modifications enhanced intermolecular hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding between the two biopolymers, as evidenced by FTIR spectral shifts and molecular forces test (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S1) [44]. Consequently, a homogeneous and densely cross-linked three-dimensional network was formed, which showed minimal nanoscale roughness and uniform continuity. A limited comparison with RS-only gels at 20 min ultrasonication (Supplementary Table S1) showed that FP incorporation substantially altered the color and surface hydrophilicity of the gel matrix, consistent with FP–RS interactions rather than independent phase behavior. FP alone could not form a self-supporting gel under the same conditions, confirming that both biopolymers are required for network formation. This optimized microstructure significantly alters the gel's macroscopic functionality. Enhanced light scattering from the homogeneous network increases gel brightness. The reinforced and cohesive network maximizes water immobilization and confers superior textural properties. The dense network acts as a thermal barrier, improving thermal stability. The balanced textural profile elevates the gel to IDDSI Level 5 (Minced & Moist). The partial disruption of starch crystallinity (lower ΔH) and the potential formation of new stable microdomains (persistent XRD diffraction peaks) collectively contribute to this robust yet deformable structure.

Excessive ultrasonic treatment triggers structural degradation. Overly intense shear and cavitation forces cause over-aggregation of denatured FP molecules and/or excessive scission of starch chains. This disrupts the balance of interactions, potentially burying hydrophilic sites and creating structural heterogeneity. The consequence is a weakened or coarser network with larger and less uniform pores, reflected in the decline of WHC, textural parameters (hardness, cohesiveness), thermal stability, and changes in IDDSI level. The increased surface adhesiveness and reversion in FTIR bands further support this over-processed state.

In summary, ultrasonic treatment serves as a powerful physical tool for modulating the conformational and colloidal states of FP and RS in a nonlinear and time-dependent manner. The 40-min treatment proved optimal, maximizing hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions while promoting the self-assembly of a cohesive, homogeneous, and highly water-retentive gel network. This optimized gel had enhanced mechanical strength, water retention, thermal resilience, and swallowing safety. Conversely, deviation from this optimal duration, whether due to insufficient or excessive treatment, failed to achieve this synergistic structural integration, resulting in gels with compromised functionality. These findings provide a mechanistic foundation for the tailored design of protein-starch composite gels using ultrasound, particularly in the field of texture-modified soft foods.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that ultrasonic pretreatment is an effective physical strategy for precisely tailoring the multiscale structure and functional properties of FP–RS composite gels for dysphagia management. Ultrasonic treatment modulated the gel network in a time‑dependent, non‑monotonic manner. The optimal duration of 40 min promoted partial FP unfolding and moderate RS fragmentation, which maximized intermolecular hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. This resulted in a homogeneous, densely cross‑linked gel network. At 40 min, the WHC peaked at 94.98%, hardness and cohesiveness reached 0.86 N and 0.26 respectively, and the initial thermal degradation temperature increased from 293.4 °C to 298.3 °C with reduced weight loss. Dynamic rheology of gels confirmed the highest storage modulus at this condition, independently validating the formation of the most elastic and structurally stable network. The balanced textural profile obtained at 40–60 min elevated the composite gel to IDDSI Level 5. Prolonged ultrasonication (60–80 min) induced protein over‑aggregation and starch chain scission, leading to network heterogeneity, reduced WHC, weakened mechanical strength, and regression to IDDSI Level 4. These findings provide a scientific basis for the rational design of plant protein-starch composite systems using ultrasound technology, with direct implications for the development of texture-modified soft gels. Sensory evaluation with suitable consumer groups is needed in future work to confirm the applicability of these findings.
