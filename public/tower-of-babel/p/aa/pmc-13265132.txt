Transient gonadotropin suppression by exogenous testosterone decreases INSL3 in early puberty in boys with constitutional delay of growth and puberty
Authors: Taneli Raivio, Mila Tanner, Kirsi Vaaralahti, Matti Hero, Päivi J Miettinen, Tero Varimo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe investigated circulating insulin-like peptide 3 (INSL3) in boys with constitutional delay of growth and puberty treated with exogenous testosterone (T) or oral letrozole (Lz). Twenty-eight boys were randomized to Lz (2.5 mg/day; n = 15) or intramuscular T (1 mg/kg every 4 weeks; n = 13) for 6 months and followed up to 12 months. Blood samples were collected at baseline and at 3, 6, and 12 months to measure INSL3 and other pubertal hormones (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01797718). At baseline, INSL3 levels were similar between groups (T: 1.4 ng/mL [0.8], Lz: 1.9 ng/mL [1.8], P = .39). T suppressed luteinising hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and INSL3 between 0 and 3 months (P < .01), and changes in INSL3 correlated with changes in LH (r = 0.61, P < .001) and FSH (r = 0.67, P < .001). Between 3 and 6 months, INSL3 and gonadotropins rose in the T group, while both increased from 0 to 6 months with Lz (P < .001). At 12 months, INSL3 levels did not differ between groups. Exogenous testosterone thus transiently suppresses INSL3 via gonadotropin inhibition in early puberty.

Boys with constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP) are often treated with testosterone to accelerate pubertal onset, but this transiently suppresses pituitary gonadotropins. Insulin-like peptide 3 (INSL3), a constitutive marker of Leydig cell function, has rarely been examined in this setting. In our randomized trial, testosterone reduced both gonadotropins and INSL3, whereas aromatase inhibition with letrozole increased them. These findings provide novel evidence that INSL3 secretion is already gonadotropin-dependent in early puberty, offering new insight into the regulation of testicular maturation.

Delayed puberty (DP) is characterized by the absence of pubertal development at the age of 13 years in girls and 14 years in boys [1]. DP affects approximately 2% of adolescents and is most commonly related to constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP), a condition defined by delayed onset of puberty but subsequent normal progression [2, 3]. In CDGP boys, low-dose testosterone therapy is widely employed to promote puberty [4], whereas we recently introduced aromatase inhibitor letrozole as a new potential treatment option [5]. These 2 treatment modalities, once given to boys with early signs of puberty, induce opposite effects on pituitary gonadotropin secretion: low-dose testosterone transiently suppressed pituitary gonadotropins, whereas letrozole activated the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis [5]. This provides an opportunity to investigate the impact of transient testicular suppression on markers of the seminiferous epithelium, such as inhibin B and AMH [6], as well as on the marker of interstitial Leydig cells, insulin-like peptide 3 (INSL3) in early puberty. INSL3 serves as a marker for Leydig cell differentiation and function and in peripuberty, its levels increase before, simultaneously with, or after testosterone levels rise in puberty, and it may serve as a marker for distinguishing between CDGP and congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism [7, 8]. The physiological role of INSL3 in humans is not fully understood, although its receptor RFXP2, may be involved in the testicular descent during fetal development [9-11]. RFXP2 is also implicated in osteoblast differentiation, matrix deposition and osteoclastogenesis [12, 13]. In adult men, administration of exogenous testosterone, particularly in high doses or over prolonged periods, suppresses gonadotropins and INSL3 levels [14, 15]. Furthermore, INSL3 levels in transgender girls decrease after GnRH agonist treatment, but in early puberty, the relationship between INSL3 and gonadotropins is not known [7, 16].

Herein, we thus compared the changes in circulating INSL3 levels in boys with CDGP treated either with exogenous testosterone or the aromatase inhibitor letrozole [5].

Patients and methods

This randomized controlled trial was conducted between 2013 and 2017 [5] and included 28 boys with CDGP (mean age 14.7 years, SD 0.6). Participants were assigned to receive either the aromatase inhibitor letrozole (2.5 mg/day; n = 15) or intramuscular testosterone (1 mg/kg/month; n = 13) for 6 months, with follow-up lasting 12 months. Assessments at 0, 3, 6, and 12 months included height, weight, growth velocity, testicular volume, and pubertal stage [5]. Morning blood samples were collected at each visit. In the testosterone group, serum samples were collected approximately 1 week (at 3 months) and 4 weeks (at 6 months) following the testosterone injection.

Total serum testosterone concentrations were measured using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS; API3000 or API3200, AB Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada). Serum estradiol was quantified by LC–MS/MS (TQ-5500, AB Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada) [5]. Gonadotropins (luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)) were determined using an immunoelectrochemiluminometric assay on an automated immunoanalyser (Modular Analytics E170; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) [5]. Serum inhibin B concentrations were measured using a commercially available ELISA (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) [5]. Serum AMH concentrations were determined using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Elecsys® AMH, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) [6]. Serum INSL3 was measured by commercial ELISA kit (Catalog # EK-035-27, RRID: AB_3750345) with reported intra-assay variation <10% and inter-assay variation <15% [17].

The study was approved by the Finnish National Committee on Medical Research Ethics and the Finnish Medicines Agency, and the study was registered to ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01797718). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

Changes in INSL3 and gonadotropin levels within each treatment group were assessed with repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by paired samples t tests. Prior to ANOVA analysis, assumptions of normality were evaluated, and the data met the criteria for parametric testing. Homogeneity of variances was verified, and the assumption of sphericity was assessed using Mauchly's test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing. Data are shown with mean and SD. Spearman's rank correlations were calculated for changes in INSL3, and gonadotropin levels and respective P values are given. Analyses were performed with SPSS for Windows (version 25, Chicago, IL). P-value less than .05 was accepted to indicate statistical significance.

Exogenous testosterone transiently suppressed LH, FSH, and INSL3 at 3 months (P = .001-.005), followed by a significant increase between 3 and 6 months (P = .001-.002). are is shown as mean +/− SEM. Data on serum LH, FSH, testosterone, and testicular volume are from the original study [5].

At the start of the study, circulating INSL3 levels did not differ between the treatment groups (T: 1.4 ng/mL [0.8], Lz: 1.9 ng/mL [1.8], P = .39). Repeated measures ANOVA indicated significant changes in both study groups (for T P = .002 and Lz P < .001). In all CDGP boys, between 0 and 3 months the changes in serum INSL3 levels correlated significantly with changes in serum LH (r = 0.61, n = 28, P < .001), FSH levels (r = 0.67, n = 28, P < .001), and testicular volume (r = 0.65, n = 28, P < .001). Exogenous T suppressed LH, FSH, and INSL3 levels between 0 and 3 months (P < .01), whereas between 3 and 6 months, INSL3 (P = .002) and gonadotropin levels (P ≤ .001) rose in the T group.

In the Lz group, however, gonadotropin and INSL3 levels increased from 0 to 6 months (P < .001). At 12 months, INSL3 levels did not differ between the groups (T: 2.2 ng/mL [0.7], Lz: 3.4 ng/mL [3.0], P = .18).

Discussion

The prerequisite of this work was to investigate how exogenous testosterone, given to expedite CDGP and known to transiently suppress gonadotropins [5], affects circulating INSL3, and whether increased LH and FSH secretion brought about by peroral letrozole [5], increases it. Our results unequivocally show that the steep decline in gonadotropins due to exogenous testosterone given for 3 months [5] was accompanied by a decline in INSL3 levels. This transient suppression was followed by recovery of gonadotropin secretion and concomitant rise in circulating INSL3 occurring 4 weeks after the last testosterone injection in this study (ie, at the 6-month visit).

This finding agrees with the previous reports in adult men [7, 14, 15, 18] and suggests that either exogenous testosterone interferes with Leydig cell maturation, or more probably, that circulating INSL3 in early puberty reflects gonadotropin-dependent changes. While these possibilities are not mutually exclusive, several observations support the latter interpretation. Changes in INSL3 closely paralleled those in LH and FSH in both treatment groups, and recovery of gonadotropin secretion in the testosterone group between 3 and 6 months was accompanied by a concomitant rise in INSL3. Importantly, 6 months after testosterone treatment, INSL3 levels in boys with CDGP did not differ significantly from pretreatment values. Testicular suppression may indeed be the prerequisite for Leydig cells to respond to gonadotropin stimulus in terms of INSL3 secretion, since Bay et al demonstrated that eugonadal men increased INSL3 secretion in response to hCG, but only following gonadotropin suppression [14]. They also demonstrated that, after prolonged gonadotropin suppression, INSL3 did not recover to pretreatment values suggesting that it may be more sensitive than T to reflect altered Leydig cell function. Similarly, Albrethsen et al reported that both GnRH analog–induced gonadotropin suppression and exogenous testosterone administration resulted in rapid suppression of INSL3, despite divergent testosterone levels, highlighting INSL3 as a marker of Leydig cell function rather than circulating androgen exposure [15]. In support of the former, rodent models show that selective ablation of adult Leydig cells also abolished circulating INSL3, and conversely, Leydig cell regeneration recovers INSL3 secretion. supporting a link between INSL3 and Leydig cell mass [19, 20]. Additionally, human histological data demonstrates that age-related reduction in Leydig cell number is paralleled by decreased INSL3 mRNA expression, while the intrinsic steroidogenic capacity of Leydig cells remained preserved [21]. Thus, in adults, INSL3 expression appears to reflect the number of Leydig cells. Previous studies and current findings suggest that short term letrozole treatment is not associated with adverse effects, but the evidence remains insufficient to recommend its use outside carefully selected cases and research setting.

In our cohort, discontinuation of aromatase inhibitor therapy, at the median Tanner genital stage of 3, did not alter circulating INSL3 concentrations, despite a marked fall in LH and FSH from high-normal or slightly supraphysiological levels to the mid-normal range. By contrast, testosterone levels decreased in parallel with gonadotropins. This divergence supports the concept that INSL3 reflects size of the Leydig cell population rather than their acute steroidogenic activity once pubertal maturation has progressed beyond the early stages, ie, when Leydig cell number and differentiation are sufficiently established. At the same time, testosterone secretion remains more directly responsive to moderate reductions in gonadotropin drive. These observations are also consistent with the previous finding that INSL3 shows less diurnal variability, and therefore less covariation with gonadotropins, than testosterone [7].

Given that the boys in the current work already had the first signs of central puberty, our results did not necessarily capture the physiological increase in serum INSL3 at the transition from prepuberty to puberty [22].

To conclude, our RCT provided an excellent opportunity to investigate INSL3 secretion in boys in early puberty in a prospective setting and to correlate these findings with treatment-related gonadotropin dynamics. Gonadotropin suppression by exogenous testosterone transiently declined circulating INSL3 levels and during the study, changes in INSL3 and gonadotropin levels correlated. These findings add a new piece to the understanding of the complex interplay between pituitary signaling and the testes in boys in early puberty.
