Impact of smokeless tobacco on psychological and oxidative stress in unemployed indian youth
Authors: Anurag Mishra, Rishabh Kumar, Satya Narayan Mishra, Sivakumar Vijayaraghavalu, Girish C. Shukla, Munish Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In India, tobacco (nicotine) addiction among youth has increased, leading to substantial socioeconomic burdens, mortality, and morbidity. While minimal short-term nicotine consumption may have antioxidant effects, chronic exposure results in various adverse health outcomes. This study examines the impact of chronic nicotine consumption on cellular oxidative stress and psychological stress, and their correlation with Homocysteine (Hcy) levels in unemployed tobacco consumers. This case-control study included 156 healthy, educated, unemployed male volunteers aged 20–40 years, divided into nicotine-addicted (n = 80) and non-addicted (n = 76) groups. Psychological stress was assessed using perceived stress scales (PSS) and coping self-efficacy (CSE) scales. Oxidative stress markers, including Malondialdehyde (MDA), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), and Catalase, were measured. Hcy levels were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Nicotine-addicted participants exhibited significantly higher perceived stress (p = 0.0001) and lower coping self-efficacy (p = 0.0001) compared to non-addicted individuals. MDA levels in erythrocytes were significantly increased (p = 0.0006), while SOD (p = 0.0001) and Catalase (p = 0.02) activities were significantly decreased in the addicted group. Nicotine intake influenced Hcy concentrations, with 55% of addicted individuals falling into moderate, 27.5% into intermediate, and 7.5% into severe Hcy categories. Chronic nicotine intake also reflected the hematological parameters (WBCs, RBCs, HGB, and Platelets). Chronic tobacco consumption induces oxidative stress and perceived psychological stress, leading to elevated Hcy levels in nicotine consumers. The study highlights the detrimental effects of nicotine addiction on cellular defensive mechanisms, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to address this growing health issue among unemployed Indian youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-024-03416-3.

Introduction

Globally, India is the second-largest producer and consumer of tobacco products, including Gutkha, Khaini, Zarda, Betel quid, Bidi, Cigarettes, and Hookah. According to the National Family Health Survey India Report (2019–2021), approximately 253 million adults above the age of 15 years are addicted to tobacco or related products. Nearly 1.2 million deaths were reported in India and 8 million deaths worldwide annually due to tobacco consumption. The estimated economic burden attributed to tobacco consumption, related diseases, and mortality in the Indian population was INR 1773.4 billion (US $27.5 billion) for the year 2018–2019 (Singh et al. 2023; Tripathi et al. 2023; St Claire et al. 2020). The prevalence of tobacco consumption varies significantly between rural, urban, and slum populations, necessitating diverse policy approaches to control its usage and associated mortality (Gupta et al. 2010; National Tobacco Control Programme (NTCP) | Ministry of Health and Family Welfare | GOI Available online 2021;). Despite various efforts, the ratio of tobacco consumption among unemployed youth has increased (Grover et al. 2020). Millions of educated adults are unemployed or at risk of joblessness, often turning to tobacco (nicotine) as a stress reliever due to its affordability and accessibility (Hobkirk et al. 2018; Rate and 1991–2021 Available online 2021).

Nicotine, the addictive substance found in all tobacco products, is known for its potential to cause various non-communicable diseases, such as heart disease, lung disease, and cancer (Bonnie et al. 2015). Addiction is characterized by compulsive engagement in rewarding stimuli despite adverse consequences, significantly altering brain function and leading to neuroadaptation (Grant et al. 2010). This adaptation increases the number of binding sites on nicotinic cholinergic receptors in the brain, contributing to nicotine tolerance and dependence. Symptoms of craving and withdrawal emerge when desensitized receptors, such as α4β2 nicotinic cholinergic receptors, become responsive during periods of abstinence (Fig. 1) (Benowitz 2010; Kuhn et al. 2019; Zaveri et al. 2015).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the mechanism of nicotine action; the symptoms of craving and withdrawal begin in smokers when desensitized receptors nicotinic cholinergic become responsive during periods of abstinence. Structurally, nicotine is similar to acetylcholine (Ach), which is an important class of neurotransmitters involved in systems concerned with physical and mental arousal, learning memory, and several aspects of emotions. Ach receptors are traditionally classified as nicotine receptors and muscarine receptors. Primarily, nicotine stimulates the central nicotinic cholinergic receptors, inducing the release of several neurotransmitters in the brain like dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), etc. These lead to arousal, mood modulation, performance enhancement, analgesics, and weight-loss effects

Chronic nicotine addiction induces both psychological and oxidative stress. While minimal short-term nicotine consumption may have antioxidant properties and enhance memory and executive functions, chronic exposure leads to numerous adverse effects (Barreto et al. 2014; Emma et al. 1829). Oxidative stress is marked by significant biomarkers such as Malondialdehyde (MDA), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), and Catalase (Grabowska et al. 2023; Shaik et al. 2021; Agarwal et al. 2019). Previous research indicates that nicotine’s direct influence on MDA is not extensively studied, although it is known to impact oxidative stress (Hu et al. 2014). Furthermore, chronic nicotine use may disrupt Homocysteine (Hcy) metabolism, resulting in Hyperhomocysteinemia and Homocystinuria, which are linked to several diseases including cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, cognitive decline, osteoporosis, and various neurodegenerative disorders (Iqbal and Yakub 2013; Tawfik et al. 2021).

This study aims to examine the effects of chronic nicotine consumption on oxidative and psychological stress and to explore the correlations between these stress markers and Homocysteine levels in unemployed Indians who consume nicotine. We focused exclusively on smokeless tobacco users to isolate the effects of nicotine from those of other harmful substances found in cigarette smoke, such as carbon monoxide, which significantly influences Hcy levels through the formation of carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) (Ren and Zhou 2022). Our inclusion criteria ensured that all study volunteers had similar food habits and sleeping patterns to minimize the influence of these variables on stress levels. By comparing the psychological and oxidative stress markers between nicotine-addicted and non-addicted individuals, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of how chronic nicotine consumption affects overall health and contributes to the socioeconomic burden.

In summary, this study investigates the chronic effects of tobacco consumption on unemployed Indians, focusing on the interplay between oxidative stress, psychological stress, and Homocysteine levels. By elucidating these relationships, we hope to highlight the need for targeted interventions to address tobacco addiction and its associated health risks.

Materials and methods

Study design

This case-control study has been designed to evaluate the effect of chronic tobacco addiction on unemployed Indians. Chronic tobacco consumers were randomly recruited from Indian domiciles. The levels of oxidative stress and psychological stress were measured among the young Addicted and Non-addicted unemployed Indians.

Selection of volunteers

156 healthy, educated, unemployed, youth volunteers, age (20–40 years), were recruited randomly in Prayagraj, Uttar Pradesh, India. All the participants were surveyed for tobacco (nicotine) addiction or consumption in smokeless forms (Gutkha, Khaini, Zarda, and Betel Quid). The enrolled participants were divided into two group viz., 1). The control group; never consumed tobacco, and 2). The tobacco-consumers consumed tobacco at least four times per day in any available form before two years of enrollment in the study. All the study participants (cases and controls) were screened through a pre-listed health history questionnaire for any medical complications such as diabetes, asthma, Cardiovascular disease, depression, anxiety, and other mental disorders. The study objective was clearly explained to the volunteers, and their written consent was obtained prior to recruiting for the study. The following inclusion and exclusion criteria of study have been listed below.

The inclusion criteria

Smokeless tobacco users with following inclusion criteria were included in the study: Ethnic North Indians, Age group 20–40 years, Male, Educated, Healthy, Unemployed, and tobacco consumption (at least 2 years) were considered as a study group (cases). Similarly, Non-addicted volunteers with the aforementioned criteria were considered as controls. The inclusion criteria also ensured that all study volunteers had similar food habits and sleeping patterns and did not have any other chronic illnesses.

The exclusion criteria

Individuals over 40 years of age, illiterate, with chronic illnesses, mental or physical disorders, female, and transgender were excluded from the study. The study also excluded those under medical supervision or taking medications. Information regarding secondhand smoke exposure was obtained from participants and excluded if found involved. Study volunteers and/or control volunteers who were not willing to participate in the study were excluded. This study has been approved by the population resource and research center, institutional ethics committee, University of Allahabad, Prayagraj, UP, India, with the reference number – IERB Id: 2022-30DOBC.

Anthropometric measures

All the enrolled volunteers underwent anthropometric measurements; their body weight in kilograms (kg), and height in centimeters (cm) were measured. Body Mass Index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated. According to international standards, a BMI between (18.5–24.9) was considered normal; (25–29.9) were considered overweight; > 30 considered obese, class I (30–34.9); class II (35–39.9); and class III (> 40) (Nuttall 2015).

Blood sample collection, processing, and storage

Venous blood was collected from both groups (Addicted and Non-addicted) recruited in the study at aboard certified pathology by an experienced phlebotomist. EDTA coated anticoagulant vials were used for hematological assessment and plasma separation, whereas plain vials were used for serum separation. Separations, including blood cells, plasma, and serum, were performed at 4 °C temperature, collected in the required volume, and frozen at -80 °C temperature for later use (Lei and Prow 2019).

Preparation of hemolysate

Hemolysate was prepared from the fresh blood; the packed RBCs obtained from whole blood were lysed, and all intracellular contents were collected. In methodology, 20 µL of RBCs were taken in a tube and washed with a 0.9% NaCl solution 2–3 times repeatedly, centrifuged at 3000 rpm (revolution per minute) for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and pellets were collected carefully. In pellets, stabilizing solution was added up to the final volume of 400 µL, and stored at -80 °C temperature for further quantification of MDA and SOD (Huntsman 1974).

Biochemical measurements

Hematological assessment (complete blood count)

A complete blood count was assessed on an automated hematology analyzer (Agappe, India). Hematological parameters, including Leukocytes (WBC, MID, Lymphocytes, and Granulocytes), Erythrocytes (RBCs, HGB, HCT, MCV, MCH, MCHC, and RDW), and Platelets (PLT, MPV, PDW, P-LCC, and P-LCR), were evaluated in a given volume of fresh blood. According to International Standards, CBC was expressed as WBC count (103/µL), RBC count (106/µL), HGB (g/dL), and PLT count (103/µL) (Brereton et al. 2016).

Quantification of plasma proteins

The measurement of protein quantity in each plasma sample was done by the Folin-Lowry method. A standard protein solution of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) was used for calibration. Absorbance was recorded in a UV-Vis double beam spectrophotometer at 660 nm wavelength (Waterborg and Matthews 1984).

Quantification of homocysteine

Plasma Hcy levels were measured in all samples as described earlier, with minor modifications (Pfeiffer et al. 1999). This quantification was performed on high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Shimadzu, Japan). Chemicals and standards used in HPLC are Tri-n-butylphosphine, 4-(aminosulfonyl)-7-fluoro-2,1,3–benzoxadiazole (ABDF), Cysteamine, L-Homocysteine, and L-Cysteine (source: Merck, India). Hcy concentrations are expressed in µmol/L; concentrations between 5 and 15 µmol/L were considered normal, and > 15 µmol/L was considered elevated in the body’s fluids.

Quantification of lipid peroxidation product (MDA) in plasma and RBCs

Plasma and erythrocytes’s MDA was measured with a slight modification of the method given by (Senthilkumar et al. 2021). MDA was considered as an index for lipid peroxidation. For quantification, plasma/hemolysate was mixed with 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and TBA-TCA-HCl solution. The whole mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for 30 min at 100 °C, and centrifuged to obtain a clear supernatant. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded in UV-Vis double beam spectrophotometer at 532 nm wavelength. The MDA content was calculated using the extinction coefficient (ɛ = 156,000), and concentrations were expressed as nmol/g of Hb or nmol/mL of RBCs.

Quantification of superoxide dismutase (SOD)

Superoxide dismutase activity in RBCs was measured with a slight modification of the method given by (Marklund and Marklund 1974). Tris-succinate buffer (pH 8.2) and Pyrogallol (light-sensitive) were used to measure SOD activity in RBCs. The blank tube contained only 3 mL of tris-succinate buffer, whereas the reference tube contained the solution mixture (2.85 mL buffer + 50 µL distilled water + 100 µL Pyrogallol) and the sample solution mixture contained (2.85 mL buffer + 50 µL hemolysate + 100 µL Pyrogallol). Absorbance was recorded in a UV-Vis double beam spectrophotometer at 420 nm for 4 min (with an interval of 30 s), and the catalytic activity of SOD was expressed as (nmol/min/g of Hb).

Quantification of catalase

The plasma Catalase activity was measured as described earlier by (Sinha 1972). A 0.01 M phosphate buffer (pH 7) was used to make the dilution of H2O2. As for the blank solution, 2.5 mL of unitary phosphate buffer (PB) solution was used, while a solution mixture (1.45 mL of PB + 50 µL of H2O + 1 mL of diluted H2O2) was taken as a reference analyte, and in the test sample (1.45 mL of PB + 50 µL of plasma sample + 1 mL of diluted H2O2) was used. Absorbance was recorded at 240 nm wavelength for 3 min (with a 30 s interval). Due to the activity of H2O2 in the test sample mixture, the absorbance was recorded in decreasing order with time. Catalase activity was expressed as IU (International Unit; denotes one unit decomposes 1 µmole of H2O2 per min at pH-7 and 25 °C).

Psychological assessment

Perceived stress (PS)

PSS is one of the validated and widely accepted scales used for psychological assessment. In general, it measures the degree to which situations in one’s life are appraised as stressful. Volunteers responded to a questionnaire having 10 items based on the frequency of emotional feelings and thoughts during the preceding month. By assessing the overall individual score, all volunteers were categorized into three perceived stress categories: low stress (< 13), moderate stress (14–26), and high stress (27–40) (Cohen et al. 1983).

Coping self-efficacy (CSF)

The CSE scale is one of the validated and standardized lists of 26 items given by Chesney et al. 2006 used to assess someone’s coping efficacy against life’s challenges and threats when conditions are not going well or are difficult. All volunteers were asked to carefully rate each item (0 to 10) based on their perceived self-efficacy. The range of ratings was categorized as 0 points = cannot do all, 1–5 points = moderately can do, and 6–10 points = certain can do.

Statistical analysis

The comparisons were made between the socio-demographic, clinical, and laboratory measurements using appropriate parametric tests where data were normally distributed. The statistical data processing of the results was carried out using Microsoft Excel 2019 and the GraphPad Prism version-9 program. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was also calculated among the groups. The result has been presented as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). The statistical significance of the difference was verified using the t-test at 95% confidence and p-value at (p < 0.05).

Effect on homocysteine concentration

Homocysteine is a sulfur-containing, non-proteinogenic, toxic amino acid derived from the metabolism of Methionine (Met). Generally, it is considered a predictive risk marker for several disorders, including cardiovascular disorders, cerebrovascular disorders, neurodegenerative illnesses, bone disorders, etc. Tobacco consumption influences the plasma Hcy level, which worsens the cellular biochemistry and causes various lifelong medical complications.

As given in (Table 3), approximately 55% of the Addicted expressed moderate, 27.5% expressed intermediate, and 7.5% expressed severe Hcy concentrations, whereas in the Non-addicted, nearly 55.3% of volunteers were placed in low, 36.84% in moderate, and 7.8% in intermediate Hcy categories, respectively. Elevated plasma Hcy concentration was quantify in the tobacco addicted group, which shows an indication of severe damage. Effect on MDA and cellular defensive mechanisms

Regular tobacco consumption induces oxidative stress that damages significant biomolecules, including lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, in the human body. Elevated MDA concentration is a predictive sign of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage.

As depicted in (Fig. 6a), we measured significantly increased MDA (0.229 ± 0.006) content in the Addicted group; the same was found reduced in Non-addicted (0.197 ± 0.007) group. Discussion

Nicotine addiction is extensively employed as a stress reliever in today’s contemporary lifestyle; however, long-term consumption is hazardous for both physical and mental health. Nicotine addiction affects brain activity, leading to neurodegenerative illnesses and degrading the nervous system’s potential (Bonnie et al. 2015; Widysanto et al. 2024). The stress levels of adult tobacco users have been observed to be significantly higher than those of non-tobacco users. Given the potential involvement of nicotine addiction in oxidative stress (Caliri et al. 2021), the goal of this study was to investigate the long-term effects of nicotine on psychological stress and Hcy metabolism. We primarily examined the interaction of nicotine addiction with perceived stress, coping efficacy, and oxidative stress markers (MDA, SOD, and Catalase), and analyzed their correlations with Hcy concentration among the enrolled volunteers.

In our survey, nicotine users often reported that their consumption helped relieve feelings of stress, divert their thoughts, and improve work performance. Perceived stress (PS) was adversely associated with coping efficacy in the nicotine-addicted group, supporting our hypothesis and aligning with existing findings. Individuals’ coping strategies under stressful situations were lower in the nicotine-addicted group, consistent with previous studies indicating a negative correlation between PS and coping self-efficacy (CSE) (Guruprakash et al. 2018; Farooq; Iti, J. 2018; Wróbel et al. 2023).

Nicotine addiction promotes oxidative stress both in vivo and in vitro, causing peroxide/antioxidant imbalances in blood cells, plasma, and tissues (Agarwal et al. 2019; Marques et al. 2021). Our study aligns with previous findings that nicotine addiction substantially stimulates MDA levels (Safyudin and Subandrate 2016). We observed a significantly higher MDA concentration in the RBCs of the nicotine-addicted group, indicating oxidative damage, though MDA concentration in plasma samples was not consistently elevated. This higher MDA content in RBCs provides important evidence of oxidative damage in nicotine-addicted respondents (Kashinakunti et al. 2011).

Additionally, we examined SOD concentration in study participants. SOD, a primary antioxidant enzyme, counteracts superoxide radicals produced in cells, serving as a crucial defensive mechanism. Our study found that SOD activity was significantly decreased in the RBCs of the nicotine-addicted group compared to the non-addicted group. Similar findings were observed for plasma Catalase activity. These observations suggest that non-addicted respondents have developed better defensive efficacy against oxidative damage and perceived psychological stress (Agarwal et al. 2019; Naz and Ali 2018).

Nicotine addiction also influences Hcy metabolism, affecting either the remethylation pathway or the transsulfuration pathway, leading to elevated Hcy concentrations (Hyperhomocysteinemia) in blood plasma and serum. In cellular biochemistry, nicotine consumption disrupts remethylation cycle, interrupt the Methionine biosynthesis. Additionally, tobacco intake influences transsulfuration pathway, reducing the conversion of Hcy to Cysteine, further contributing to Hyperhomocysteinemia (Sobczak 2003; Singh 2016). Our study found that approximately 55.0%, 27.5%, and 7.5% of nicotine-addicted respondents had moderate, intermediate, and severe Hcy levels, respectively. This aligns with existing literature indicating that nicotine can interfere with these metabolic pathways, resulting in Hyperhomocysteinemia (Omoike et al. 2020; Haj Mouhamed et al. 2011).

Our study focused exclusively on smokeless tobacco users, hence carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) levels were not investigated, as COHb is primarily a concern for smokers exposed to carbon monoxide (CO). The exclusion of smoking participants ensures that the observed effects on Hcy levels and oxidative stress are attributable to nicotine and other components of smokeless tobacco, rather than the confounding effects of smoking-related COHb. However, it is important to acknowledge the potential role of COHb in influencing Hcy levels. COHb, formed from the binding of CO to hemoglobin, can significantly impact physiological functions (Ren and Zhou 2022). CO, a byproduct of tobacco combustion in cigarettes, binds with high affinity to hemoglobin, forming COHb. This reduces hemoglobin’s oxygen-carrying capacity, impairing oxygen transport (Carrola et al. 2023; Dorey et al. 2020).

The impaired oxygen transport caused by COHb can induce oxidative stress, an imbalance between the production of free radicals, like reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the body’s ability to detoxify these harmful byproducts or repair the resulting damage. Elevated COHb increases ROS, which can damage cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and DNA, disrupting various metabolic functions (Carrola et al. 2023; Hydzik et al. 2023).

One affected pathway is the metabolism of Hcy. Elevated ROS can interfere with the enzymes involved in the remethylation and transsulfuration pathways of Hcy metabolism. The remethylation pathway converts Hcy to Methionine, while the transsulfuration pathway converts Hcy to Cysteine. Disruption of these pathways can lead to an accumulation of Hcy, resulting in Hyperhomocysteinemia (Hayden and Tyagi 2004). Several studies have documented the relationship between COHb and elevated Hcy levels. For example, increased COHb levels are associated with higher Hcy concentrations in smokers, indicating CO-induced oxidative stress may play a crucial role in elevating Hcy levels. This relationship underscores the complex interactions between different components of cigarette smoke and their cumulative impact on metabolic health (Omoike et al. 2020; Haj Mouhamed et al. 2011; Hawale et al. 2021).

Although our study did not measure COHb levels, focusing on smokeless tobacco users, it is essential to consider the broader implications of tobacco use. The connection between COHb and elevated Hcy levels highlights the multifaceted impact of tobacco use on health. Understanding these interactions provides a comprehensive picture of the risks associated with different forms of tobacco consumption, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions. Our exclusive focus on smokeless tobacco users provides specific insights into the impact of this form of nicotine consumption. The exclusion of smokers ensures that our findings are specifically attributable to smokeless tobacco. However, we acknowledge the importance of subgroup analysis in understanding the differential impacts of various nicotine delivery methods. Future research should consider separating smokers and smokeless tobacco users to provide a detailed understanding of the distinct metabolic and physiological effects.

Previous studies have linked elevated Hcy levels with various health risks, including cardiovascular diseases, cognitive dysfunction, and metabolic disorders (Koklesova et al. 2021; Azzini et al. 2020; Son and Lewis 2024). Our findings contribute to this body of knowledge by highlighting the specific impact of smokeless tobacco on Hcy metabolism and oxidative stress, underscoring the need for targeted interventions for nicotine users.

Conclusions

In conclusion, short-term nicotine consumption positively affects memory and executive functions. In contrast, chronic consumption induces perceived psychological stress and reduces CSE in nicotine consumers. Higher perceived psychological stress stimulates oxidative stress, undermining cellular defensive mechanisms and posing a risk of severe damage in unemployed nicotine users. In erythrocytes, MDA content was significantly increased in the nicotine-addicted group. Enzymatic measurements showed significantly decreased SOD and Catalase activity in the addicted group compared to non-addicted unemployed individuals, indicating the initiation of oxidative damage in the study group. Oxidative stress may also lead to clinical symptoms and lifelong medical complications. Similarly, hematological parameters were found significantly worsen in addicted individuals. Moreover, nicotine intake, elevated PS, and oxidative stress collectively disrupt Hcy levels, measured in high proportions in the addicted group.
