Personality Disorder Severity–ICD-11 Scale and CPTSD ICD-11 Scale: examining possible psychometric properties and diagnostic concept overlap
Authors: Ohad Gilbar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The new way of categorising ICD-11 Personality Disorder (PD) moved from categorical PD types to a dimension of PD severity. This change has raised a debate regarding the question of whether the new PDS-ICD-11 possibly overlaps with Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (CPTSD) clusters, specifically the Disturbances in Self-Organization (DSO) cluster. Both disorders, PD and CPTSD, contain self and interpersonal impairment; moreover, as both are trauma-based disorders, it is unclear if different trauma risk factors apply to each.Objective: The current study had two aims: first, to use the new PDS-ICD-11 severity scale (PDS) to assess the psychometric properties of PD in a unique population of male perpetrators of intimate partner violence who suffer from high levels of PD and CPTSD: second, to examine whether exposure to different types of traumatic events and attachment styles would be associated differently with PD and with CPTSD clusters.Method: Participants were 116 males receiving treatment at 18 domestic violence centres. Data were collected using the International Trauma Questionnaire and the PDS-ICD-11 (Hebrew versions).Results: Confirmatory factor analysis supported the factorial validity of PDS-ICD-11, after removing the self-injury symptom. Cumulative childhood trauma and anxiety attachment were associated with PD, PTSD, and DSO, while recent exposure to trauma and avoidance attachment was associated only with DSO.Conclusions: The study supports the psychometric of construct distinction within the new PDS-ICD-11 scale’s definitions of PD and CPTSD. Specifically, it supports the distinctions even where there is similar content of DSO and PD dysfunction of the self and interpersonal impairment. The absence of significant differences in risk factors relating to childhood trauma exposure and psychological injuries support the understanding that all diagnoses shared the same aetiological risk factors.KEYWORDS: Personality Disorder Severity Scale: PDS-ICD-11, ICD-11; complex post-traumatic stress disorder: CPTSD; trauma; attachment; PTSD

The International Classification of Diseases 11th Version (ICD-11) introduced a novel approach to categorising Personality Disorder (PD) by defining it based on the severity of five personality disturbance features: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, and anankastia (Bach et al., 2021). Negative affectivity is a tendency to experience frequent and intense negative emotions; detachment is marked by emotional and social withdrawal; dissociality involves disregard for the rights and feelings of others; disinhibition reflects impulsivity and lack of self-control; and anankastia is characterised by rigid perfectionism and excessive orderliness (Bach et al., 2017). This new conceptualisation has received construct validity in several studies using the Personality Disorder Severity–ICD-11 Scale, the PDS-ICD-11 (Brown & Sellbom, 2023; Lorentzen et al., 2024; Sellbom et al., 2024; Simon et al., 2023). Recent studies using clinical samples have also supported this conceptual model (Zimmermann et al., 2023). However, the conceptualisation of this redefinition as a diagnostic tool has sparked significant debate regarding PD’s potential overlap with another new trauma-based disorder in ICD-11: Complex Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (CPTSD), defined by the core symptoms of PTSD alongside Disturbances in Self-Organization (DSO). This debate has centred around the question of whether or not these disorders share similar symptoms and related interpersonal problems (Felding et al., 2021; White & Hudson, 2023). As an example, Powers et al. (2022) found a possible overlap between Borderline Personality Disorder and CPTSD. The question of overlap in the self-report scales of these disorders has been partially tested in the literature.

Some of those arguing that there is an overlap, focus on the disturbances in self-organisation (DSO). There are three factors of DSO (i.e. emotion dysregulation, negative self-concept and disturbances in relationship) that are claimed to overlap significantly with two Personality Disorder factors – self impairment and interpersonal impairment. The overlap of the factors of CPTSD and PD themselves, then create an overlap in the tools by which they are measured: the Personality Disorders Scale (Felding et al., 2021) and the ICD-11 CPTSD self-report scale.

This potential overlap has significant clinical implications in the field of mental health (Bach & Mulder, 2022; Swales, 2022; White & Hudson, 2023). The implications and effects of mental health disorders are perhaps nowhere more marked than among perpetrators of violence. For that reason, this study examined these questions among male perpetrators of Intimate Partner Violence (IPV). This population often exhibits significant levels of posttraumatic stress symptoms (Hoyt et al., 2012), as well as Disturbances in Self-Organization and Complex PTSD, due their history of chronic exposure to traumatic events in childhood (Gilbar et al., 2020). Furthermore, these individuals may present with PD traits, particularly Cluster B disorders such as Borderline, Narcissistic, and Antisocial disorders, which are characterised by interpersonal disturbances and affective instability (Krause-Utz et al., 2021; Pugliese et al., 2024). Therefore, answering this question of overlap can lead to more accurate clinical assessments and inform appropriate intervention in this field.

The main overlap debate in the symptomatology construct of both CPTSD and PD, as measured by their respective scales, hinges on specific aspects of severe and pervasive problems in the affect regulation (Felding et al., 2021; White & Hudson, 2023). DSO is a main component of CPTSD that encompasses affect regulation. The issues in affect regulation in DSO include heightened reactivity to minor stressors, emotional numbing, and inability to experience pleasure or positive emotions. Personality Disorder, in contrast, encompasses a broader spectrum of emotion regulation deficits that vary in their pervasiveness, severity, and chronicity and manifest in emotional, cognitive, and behavioural aspects of personality dysfunction. The emotional manifestations of PD include: (1) range and appropriateness of emotional experience and expression; (2) tendency to be emotionally overreactive or under-reactive; and (3) inability to recognise and acknowledge unwanted emotions (e.g. anger and sadness) (Felding et al., 2021). These emotional manifestations can also bring about impulsive behaviour, self-harm, or violence (Bach & Mulder, 2022; Felding et al., 2021). Although some distinctions can be identified between PD and DSO symptoms, such as the severity of self-related problems and interpersonal difficulties (e.g. self-harm or violence in PD, whereas DSO primarily involves rage), it has been argued that these symptoms significantly overlap (Felding et al., 2021).

The overlap between the factors of PD and CPTSD was recently examined by Jowett in his 2024 study. Jowett et al. (2024) employed a three-factor model of PD and CPTSD symptoms. Among other findings, discussed below, Jowett et al. (2024) found two PDS items, emotional control and expression (Factor Loading .46) in the DSO factor. This finding has raised the need for further studies evaluating this possible overlap. Such studies would prove particularly important in the understanding and treatment of male perpetrators of intimate violence. Because male perpetrators of IPV can manifest a wide range of deficits in emotion regulation (Neilson et al., 2023), examination of the shared structural components of these disorders can bring clearer answers about the properties of the new PDS-ICD-11.

An additional area of overlap in the symptomatology construct of both CPTSD and PD, as measured by their respective scales, relates to self-functioning, specifically self-esteem (Felding et al., 2021). In CPTSD, this manifests as feelings of shame, guilt, or failure related to disorganised self (Brewin et al., 2017). In contrast, PD is characterised by an impaired ability to maintain an overall sense of self, and personality problems may be consistently evoked by particular circumstances (Swales, 2022). Although PD is associated with more pervasive problems in identity features and self-perception, it can be argued that these issues stem from the same underlying self-functioning deficits and therefore may fall under the same structural framework in related disorder scales, an area that has been less studied (Jowett et al., 2024). Jowett et al. (2024) found among the community population in Denmark one PDS item, self-perception, (Factor Loading .39) in the DSO factor. This finding has raised the need for further studies by evaluating this possible overlap. More specifically, this question is relevant among male perpetrators of IPV who can vary widely in their self-functioning, specifically regarding self-esteem (Cunha et al., 2024).

Another potential area of overlap between CPTSD and PD relates to interpersonal impairment. The DSO component of CPTSD is associated with difficulties in maintaining relationships and feeling close to others, while PD similarly involves an impaired ability to develop and maintain close and mutually satisfying relationships. While PD is characterised by more severe interpersonal difficulties, such as a tendency to be emotionally over- or under-reactive (Bach et al., 2021), these issues may still be related to the same underlying construct. The potential overlap in these interpersonal domains has yet to be thoroughly investigated. Jowett et al. (2024) found that in a three-factor solution (e.g. PTSD, DSO and PD) the factor loading of one item of the interpersonal domains, harm to others, was low. This finding has raised the need for further studies by evaluating this possible overlap, specifically among the population of male perpetrators of IPV whose interpersonal domain includes harm to others (Brassard et al., 2023).

As part of the testing of the diagnostic concept of ICD-11 PD, there is a need to investigate the possible differences of risk factors of the other trauma-related disorders. Whereas PTSD (e.g. re-experiencing, avoidance, and sense of threat), is defined as caused by an extremely threatening or horrific event or series of events (WHO, 2018), mainly in adulthood (Cruz et al., 2022). CPTSD in ICD-11 is specifically defined by psychological distress that typically occurs following most commonly prolonged or repetitive events traumatic exposure (WHO, 2018). The ICD-11 neither requires nor rules out traumatic exposure for a diagnostic finding of PD; generally, PD is hypothesised to stem from very early childhood trauma occurring in the first developmental period. Thus, unlike PTSD, PD and CPTSD risk factors tend to look to childhood events.

Despite this tendency, there are mixed results in the risk-factor studies to date. Many studies have found a positive association between chronic childhood trauma exposure and a CPTSD symptom profile than those with PTSD (Brewin et al., 2017). In addition, studies of PD have shown that in some instances it may result from early traumatic experiences (Yen et al., 2022). In addition, exposure to childhood traumatic experiences revealed ICD-11 PD traits in an individual study (Bach et al., 2022a) and in a systematic review (Back et al., 2021). These interesting risk factor findings can be compared to Jowett’s et al. (2024) study, which did not find early traumatic experiences associated with PD. Indeed, in the case of male perpetrators of IPV, whether diagnosed with PD- or PTSD-related disorders, an association has been found between childhood traumatic events, with small-to-moderate effect size of (Zhu et al., 2024).

Some theoretical literature regarding the contribution of childhood history to the possible differences in the development of PD and CPTSD, suggested that the disorders may differ in the specific nature of the attachment deficit from which they stem (Ford & Courtois, 2021). Such theoretical literature suggested that PD may result from traumatic events during attachment development, while CPTSD occurs later in childhood and adolescence (Ford & Courtois, 2021). Ford and Courtois (2021) proposed two distinct attachment deficits for these disorders: rejection in PD (i.e. borderline personality disorder) and betrayal in CPTSD. However, they did not more specifically examine differences between the disorders regarding attachment style (e.g. avoidance, anxiety). To our knowledge, no study has examined the possible differences in attachment style between these two disorder constructs (White & Hudson, 2023). This question has received heightened attention when diagnosing male perpetrators of IPV. While borderline personality disorder has been proposed as a primary personality structure underlying male perpetrators of IPV – often viewed as a response to attachment deficits – recent perspectives emphasise trauma responses, including both PTSD and disturbances in self-organisation, as foundational to intimate partner violence behaviours (Gilbar et al., 2019). Therefore, the current study aims to investigate this direction as well: to examine the possible overlap on the concept level of the possible risk factors of the differentiated attachment styles.

The current study

Confirmatory factor analysis was used in several previous studies (Bach & First, 2018; Bach et al., 2021; Lorentzen et al., 2024) to test the psychometric properties and diagnostic concept of the self-report PDS-ICD-11. Specifically, to date, five studies have examined the content and construct validity of the ICD-11 PD diagnosis, demonstrating good model fit across diverse Western cultural populations (e.g. in Germany (Zimmermann et al., 2023); Denmark (Bach et al., 2023); Spain (Gutiérrez et al., 2023); Norway (Lorentzen et al., 2024); and Turkey (Aydın-Seyrek et al., 2024)) between 0.92 and 0.95 Comparative-Fit index, and the Tucker-Lewis Index. However, further cultural examinations of this diagnostic concept are needed (Hualparuca-Olivera & Caycho-Rodríguez, 2023). Likewise, clinical and non-European samples are notably absent and needed (Back et al., 2021). In addition, only one study examined this model in relation to the properties of ICD-11 CPTSD (Jowett et al., 2024). Therefore, the current study undertook to examine an expanded construct in a clinical population and across a unique cultural context: the population of mandated patients of intimate partner violence in a Middle Eastern country. That population has been found to exhibit more symptoms of PTSD, CPTSD and PD than the entire general population; among the male perpetrators of IPV, the possible overlap of these disorders lends itself to exploration for profiling this population and to adapt related intervention (Pugliese et al., 2024).

Therefore, the first aim of the study was to test the latent structure of the PDS-ICD-11 model alongside with CPTSD-11 symptoms via the Hebrew version of the International Trauma Questionnaire. The clinical study sample was comprised of males who had perpetrated IPV and were being treated by social service agencies. The second aim was to examine the association between types of traumatic events and the C/PTSD clusters versus symptoms of PD. Specifically examined were the potential differential contributions of exposure to child abuse and cumulative traumas both over a lifetime and in recent proximity to the diagnosis in order to assess the risk for C/PTSD clusters versus PD. We also differentiated the attachment types underlying the trauma. The following hypotheses were formulated and tested:

Hypothesis 1: Measurement models of PDS-11 alongside CPTSD-11 scale symptoms (i.e. PTSD and DSO clusters) will exhibit a good model fit.

Hypothesis 2: The Hebrew version of the PDS ICD-11 will possess satisfactory internal reliability.

Hypothesis 3: Compared to PD and CPTSD, specifically in CPTSD’s DSO sub-category, recent traumatic events in adulthood will be predicted by greater severity of PTSD.

Hypothesis 4: Childhood and cumulative traumas, including life-long attachment deficits, predict PD and CPTSD, specifically CPTSD’s DSO sub-category, more than they predict PTSD.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Data collection took place from January 2022 to May 2024 as part of a large randomised controlled trial study evaluating IPV intervention (for detailed description of the study see Gilbar et al., 2025). Participants were 116 males from the Jewish population in Israel who received treatment at 18 domestic violence prevention centres. The majority of participants sought help after a legal or social service intervention. Data was collected before intervention to prevent IPV perpetration. The socio-demographic variables revealed that the average age was 42 years (SD = 10.50). Regarding education, 54% had completed only high school or below. Regarding religiosity, 25% were traditional (observing some Jewish rituals but not seeing themselves as religious), 35% secular, 23% religious, and 16% Ultraorthodox. In terms of income, most participants, 53%, earned below a $2200 monthly income margin. The mean number of direct exposures to traumatic events after age 18 was 3.63 (SD = 2.63); the mean number of recent traumatic events in the last 6 months was .67 (M = 1.34); and the mean number of exposures to childhood trauma was 1.64 (SD = .56). The average of the PTSD symptoms was .80 (SD = .73, Range = 0-3), and the average of the DSO symptoms was .97 (SD = .97, Range = 0-4). The average of the avoidance attachment was 3, and for anxiety attachment was 3.14 (Table 1).

Measures

Background Variables:

Age, education, income, and level of religiosity were assessed.

The Personality Disorder Severity–ICD-11 Scale

The Personality Disorder Severity–ICD-11 Scale (PDS-ICD-11; Bach et al., 2021, 2023) is a self-report scale that measures personality dysfunction according to the ICD-11 definition of personality dysfunction (i.e. self-functioning, interpersonal functioning, emotional, cognitive, and behavioural manifestations). The PDS-ICD-11 includes 13 items, not including the additional Item 14 capturing the general diagnostic requirement of psychosocial impairment. The first 10 items are measured using a 5-point bipolar scale where participants indicate which of five statements best reflects their functioning. The middle response reflects normal functioning (scored 0), the immediately adjacent response options reflect mild impairment (scored −1 and +1), and the most peripheral response options reflect the most severe impairment (scored −2 and +2). For instance, ‘Interest in Relationships’ may involve social isolation on one pole and dysfunctional socialisation on the opposite pole. These items were recoded as binary items where scores of 0 were retained to reflect ‘normal’ functioning, and all other scores reflect ‘dysfunction’ (1). Items 11–13 were scored on a standard 4-point unipolar Likert scale where higher scores reflect greater disturbance. For example, response options for the item ‘Experience of reality during stress’ included 0 (I never harm myself). The total PDS-ICD-11 score can range from 0 to 13, with higher scores indicating greater symptomatology and severity. Descriptions of all items can be found in Table 2. The psychometric properties of the scale are well supported (Zimmermann et al., 2023).

CPTSD and PTSD: international trauma questionnaire

The CPTSD and PTSD: International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) (Cloitre et al., 2018) is a 12-item self-report measure – currently under development – for screening ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD symptomatology. The PTSD cluster contains six items that represent the three clusters of PTSD (Re-experiencing, Avoidance, Sense of Threat). There are two Re-experiencing items, two Avoidance items, and two Sense of Threat items. Symptom endorsement is scored on a Likert-type scale, indicating how bothersome a symptom has been over the past month with scores ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). Six items represent the three DSO clusters (Affect Dysregulation 2 items; Negative Self Concept 2 items; Disturbance in Relationship 2 items), where endorsement of items indicates how typical the problem is to the individual, with scores ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). The scale can be used to estimate a self-reported ICD-11 PTSD or CPTSD diagnosis by re-coding the Likert scores into six binary variables. These represent each of the three PTSD and DSO symptom clusters based on the following cut-off scores: A diagnosis of PTSD requires a score of ≥ 2 (moderately) for at least one symptom in each of its three clusters.

Exposure to traumatic events: life events checklist (LEC-5)

The Exposure to Traumatic Events: Life Events Checklist (LEC-5) (Weathers et al., 2013) (LEC-5) measure is a 17-item self-report measure designed to screen for potentially traumatic events in a respondent’s lifetime. The LEC-5 was originally used to assess criteria A in ‘The Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale’ (CAPS) (Blake et al., 1990). The validation of the LEC has been supported by the exhibition of adequate temporal stability and shows good convergence with an established measure of trauma history (Gray et al., 2004). This measure assesses lifetime exposure to 15 traumatic events (e.g. natural disaster, sexual assault, life-threatening illness/injury), plus two more – ‘any other very stressful event/experience’ and ‘serious injury, harm, or death you caused to someone else’ – that can be used to indicate exposure to a trauma that was not listed. In this study, specific items related to the Israeli context were added, for example: ‘Were you exposed to combat zones as a civilian? For example: rocket fire on residential areas.’ For each item, the respondent checks, in regard to the event: (1) ‘happened to me,’ (2) ‘witnessed it happening to somebody else,’ (3) ‘learned about it happening to someone close to me,’ (4) ‘was part of my job,’ (5) ‘not sure it applies,’ or (6)’ doesn't apply to my experience.’ To create a summed total that represents the number of different life events that the respondent has experienced, the items were re-coded into binary variables with ‘happened to me’ responses being coded as 1 and all other responses coded as 0. This coding produced a single total cumulative index variable with possible scores ranging from 0 to 23. Cronbach’s alpha reliability for the LEC-5 in this study is .78.

Childhood trauma: exposure to childhood trauma questionnaire (CTQ)

The Childhood Trauma: Exposure to Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) (Bernstein et al., 2003) consists of 28 items referring to five maltreatment forms: sexual abuse, physical abuse, emotional abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. Participants were asked to rate whether the items were true for them on a 5-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging between 1 (never true) and 5 (very often true). An example of the items are as follows: ‘hit severely enough that bruises were noticed’ (physical abuse), ‘was touched sexually’ (sexual abuse), ‘was called names by family members’ (emotional abuse), ‘wore dirty clothes’ (physical neglect), ‘felt loved’ (reverse scored, emotional neglect). This questionnaire provides a summary of each of the five different scores. Previous findings have established the CTQ's validity (Bernstein et al., 2003), as well as the Hebrew version's validity (Talmon, Shilo & Tsur, 2024). Cronbach's alphas for the total score in the current sample were high .90.

Attachment anxiety and avoidance: experiences in close relationships-short form

The Attachment Anxiety and Avoidance: the short version of Experiences in Close Relationships-Short Form scale (Wei et al., 2007) is a 12-item self-report scale designed to measure attachment anxiety and avoidance, based on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). In this sample Cronbach’s α were α = .82 for anxiety and α = .61 for avoidance.

Statistical analysis

Measurement model

Before testing the structural models and examining hypothesis 1 (‘Measurement models of PDS-11 alongside CPTSD-11 scale symptoms (i.e. PTSD and DSO clusters) will exhibit a good model fit’), we built a measurement model (confirmatory factor analysis) to test psychometric properties of construct model of ICD-11 PDS and ICD-11 CPTSD (Martens, 2005). The latent structure of the ICD-11 PDS was assessed within a CFA framework based on complete responses (no missing values were observed) to the two focal instruments. The maximum likelihood estimator was applied (Brown 2015). For goodness of fit we showed the comparative fit index (CFI), and the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), where values greater than 0.90 for the Comparative-Fit index, and the Tucker-Lewis Index are considered acceptable (Marsh, Hau & Wen, 2004). Additionally, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) was reported, with a value less than 0.05 indicating close fit and values up to 0.08 indicating reasonable errors of approximation (Hu & Bentler, 1999). To examine hypothesis 2 (‘The Hebrew version of the PDS will possess satisfactory internal reliability’), we calculated composite reliability based on the actual loadings of the confirmatory factor analysis modelling results (Raykov, 1997), which allows for the reliability of a smaller set of variables to be estimated than is possible with Cronbach’s alpha (Table 3).

Structural model

In order to examine hypotheses 3 and 4, the CFA model was expanded to include associations between the extracted factors and background indicators (Multiple Indicators Multiple Causes; Chang et al. 2020). A range of predictors was added to the model to assess their differential predictive effects on the identified latent variables. Ten predictors were added to the CFA model. The predictors were first entered into the model with the best CFA solution, and each factor was regressed onto each predictor with the regression. Three predictors related to trauma history: the mean number of direct exposures to traumatic events after age 18; recent traumatic events in the last 6 months; and exposures to childhood trauma. Two predictors were related to attachment style: avoidance; anxiety.

Results

The CFA of the ICD-11 CPTSD and PDS-ICD-11 showed a reasonable model fit: CFI = .943, TLI = .936; RMSEA = .054, 95% CI [.037,.065], SRMR = .076; χ2 = 323.80, df = 242, p < .001. Factor loadings for the higher-order model are reported in Table 2.

All symptom indicators loaded onto their respective two-order latent factors in an expected manner with the loadings being positive, high (>.45 in all cases), and statistically significant (p < .001). Only one symptom of PDS, the self-injury, received less than .45. Moreover, the Re-experiencing, Avoidance, and Sense of Threat factors all loaded strongly onto the PTSD higher-order factor. Likewise, the Avoidance, Negative Self Concept, and Disturbances in Relationships factors loaded strongly onto the DSO higher-order factor. We calculated the composite reliability, which showed high levels for the two PTSD factors (.879; .886, respectively), whereas the composite reliability of each of three PDS-11 dimensions was low. Thus, these latter three were presented as a second-order factor, for which we show high reliability (CR = .890). We also calculated the average variance extracted to show distinct performance of each factor. These were high above the maximum shared variance of all pairs of factors (Discriminant validity; Henseler, Ringle & Sarstedt, 2015) (Table 3).

For the further MIMIC model, we also received fair values for the fit indices: CFI = .916, TLI = .904; RMSEA = .059, 95%CI [.046,.071], SRMR = .065; χ2 = 486.54, df = 348, p < .001. The mean number of exposures to childhood trauma and anxiety attachment style were associated with all three clusters of PTSD symptoms (β = .22, p < .05 and β = .28, p < .5, respectively), with DSO symptoms (β = .17, p < .5 and β = .50, p < .001, respectively), and with PDS symptoms (β = .34, p < .01 and β = .50, p < .001, respectively). However, recent traumatic events in the last 6 months, exposures to childhood trauma, and avoidance attachment style were also associated with DSO symptoms (β = .14 p < .01 and β = .30, p < .001, respectively) but not with PTSD symptoms and PDS symptoms (Figure 1; Table 4).

Discussion

There were two study aims. The first aim was to use the new PDS-ICD-11 severity scale to assess the psychometric properties of PD in a unique population of male perpetrators of intimate partner violence who suffer from high levels of PD and CPTSD. The second aim was to examine whether exposure to different types of traumatic events and attachment styles would be associated differently with PD and with PTSD and DSO clusters. To examine the first and primary study aim, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis of PDS-ICD-11 alongside CPTSD-11 symptoms using the Hebrew version of the International Trauma Questionnaire and the Personality Disorder Severity–ICD-11 Scale among our population of perpetrators of IPV. The first hypothesis (H1) (‘Measurement models of PDS-11 alongside CPTSD-11 scale symptoms (i.e. PTSD and DSO clusters) will exhibit a good model fit’) was confirmed. The second hypothesis (H2) (‘The Hebrew version of the PDS will possess satisfactory internal reliability’) was also confirmed. Neither of the two hypotheses related to the second study aim was proved. Specifically, hypothesis 3 (‘Recent traumatic events in adulthood will be predicted by greater severity of PTSD’) was not confirmed. Likewise, hypothesis 4 (‘Complex traumatic exposure, namely, greater exposure to child abuse and neglect, as well as the relative contribution of attachment deficit and cumulative traumas during lifetime will be a stronger predictor of the PD and DSO rather than PTSD symptom cluster’) was not proved.

The Confirmatory Factor Analysis results corroborate findings from two recent studies employing similar methodologies, further supporting the psychometric properties of the construct of the Personality Disorder Scale (PDS) (Bach et al., 2021; Bach, & First, 2018). A construct model of the PDS together with CPTSD was conducted once previously (Jowett et al., 2024). The current study represents only the second investigation examining this model alongside Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, reinforcing the conceptualisation of the PDS as a distinct construct and clinical entity. These results additionally support the internal reliability of the PDS (Hualparuca-Olivera et al., 2024). Specifically, these results support this model among IPV perpetrators populations characterised by PD or CPTSD.

Our findings also differ in important ways from Jowett et al. (2024) groundbreaking examination of the PDS scale and CPTSD’s symptoms. While Jowett et al. (2024), used the three-factor exploratory structural equation modelling solution of the PD and CPTSD symptoms to reach the finding of a potential overlap between self-dysfunction in PDS and Disturbances in Self-Organization, the current study’s findings support the ICD-11’s proposed construct differences. The current study is more nuanced; although it highlights similar content in DSO and PDS self-dysfunction, it also maintains their distinctiveness. Furthermore, this study expands the cultural use of the PDS construct, which has previously been tested only in European populations (e.g. in Norwegian community; Lorentzen et al., 2024). The current study examined the PDS construct for the first time among a Middle Eastern population using the Hebrew language. These results enhance the generalisability of the ICD-11 PDS construct and its applicability in the main Israeli cultural context.

Some of the results of this study can be understand based on the characteristics of its sample. The good fit psychometric properties of the model construct of the results is strengthened by the clinical population under study: male IPV patients mandated to receive psychological intervention and characterised as PTSD/CPTSD and as having personality disorders (Dutton, 1995). Our findings within the specific population of IPV perpetrators support the idea that in this population there are differences in the symptoms category of self-disorganisation (Brassard et al., 2023). This can help to identify different groups of IPV patients who seek referral to therapy. However, a less beneficial finding in identifying patients arose in this study in relation to self-injury. It seems that the dysfunction problem of self-injury, which is part of the PD definition, is under-reported in the unique population in which the study was conducted. Due to the low factor loading of this item, the study’s model construct of psychometric properties was reached after self-injury was removed from the analysis. Although suicide and threat of self-harm has been found among IPV perpetrators (Kafka et al., 2024), it is more common among female IPV perpetrators with borderline disorder who attempted suicide than among male IPV perpetrators (Guzmán et al., 2024). The low factor loading of harm to self was also presented in the first study comparing PDS factors with CPTSD factors among a trauma-exposed community sample in Denmark (Jowett et al., 2024).

The second aim of this diagnostic concept study is to examine the potential differences in the aetiological risk factors of these disorders. To do so, the study investigates the association between types of traumatic experiences and CPTSD clusters versus PDS symptoms. Although PDS may be claimed as a deficit resulting from traumatic events during attachment development, and CPTSD is thought to develop later in childhood and adolescence (Ford & Courtois, 2021), the findings identify no differences in relation to the predictors of cumulative childhood trauma and anxious attachment on PD, PTSD, and DSO. Therefore, the current study results strengthen the notion that all disorders (PD, and C/PTSD) are trauma-related disorders, specifically linked to early childhood experiences and are differentiated mostly by the symptom profile rather than the individual’s trauma history.

More specifically, the only differences related to recent exposure to trauma and avoidant attachment are associated solely with DSO. These results align with the theoretical concept that DSO is a central consequence of trauma exposure both recently and in the past. This suggests the central role of attachment problems in the symptoms related to disturbances in self-organisation (Karatzias et al., 2022). The association between recent trauma exposure, avoidant attachment, and DSO symptoms supports the conceptualisation of DSO as a dynamic construct that can be influenced by different traumatic events and not only in childhood. The lack of significant differences in the predictors of cumulative childhood trauma and anxious attachment across PD, PTSD, and DSO symptoms highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how various types of traumatic experiences contribute to different symptom clusters across these disorders. There is a specific need to further investigate a broader sample of male perpetrators of IPV, comprising equal groups diagnosed with personality disorders (PD) and PTSD-related disorders.

This study has several limitations. The methodology is cross-sectional; therefore, caution must be exercised in the robust use of the model construct and in assuming causal explanations of the correlates associated with the PD and CPTSD factors. The study sample includes a small number of participants, which may affect the study results. In addition, neither the Life Events Checklist nor the Exposure to Childhood Trauma Questionnaire include frequency (i.e. one event or several events) or length of any of the traumatic experiences. The role of chronic trauma as compared to a one-time event, as predictors of these diagnoses, can be understood in the future by employing more exact measurements. In Israel, there are many and varied cultural groups; the fact that we did not examine all of them in this study might have impacted the constructed model and the reliability of the Hebrew International Trauma Questionnaire version. It is therefore essential to examine the construct properties of the ICD-11 diagnoses of both PD and CPTSD among additional cultural groups in Israel. Additionally, if social desirability was a goal for this mandated population (Chan, 2011), they might have reported less suffering than they experienced; low endorsement of symptoms related to their distress may have impacted the low frequency of factors such as the self-injury. Therefore, future research should focus on mental health clinical populations in order to complete the construct validity process of the PDS compared to CPTSD measurements. In addition, further studies should compare face-to-face clinical interviews with self-report. To answer the possibility of aetiological differences between the two disorders, longitudinal studies are needed.

This study’s findings provide strong support for the psychometric properties of the model construct of both ICD-11 PD and CPTSD self-report diagnoses, drawing from a unique population in a previously unexplored cultural context. This research bolsters the utility of the WHO diagnostic framework for clinicians and researchers seeking to elucidate the nuanced distress experienced by trauma-exposed individuals. Furthermore, this investigation offers valuable insights into a previously under-studied clinical population, potentially enhancing assessment practices. The current study results should encourage clinicians to implement discrete screening procedures for PD and CPTSD symptomatology. The differentiations between PD and CPTSD emphasise the need for developing and implementing targeted therapeutic interventions specifically designed to address the distinct symptom profiles associated with each of these conditions. For example, trauma-focused interventions should be further considered for both disorders (i.e.. prolonged exposure therapy; Hoeboer et al., 2024) with specific concern to the high level of inner and interpersonal severity among PD such Dialectical Behavior Therapy; DBT (Bach & Mulder, 2022). More specifically, trauma-informed interventions would support the possibility of different types of psychological characteristic of IPV men: for instance, personality Type B traits (discussed in this study’s literature review and in the PDS definitions) (Pugliese et al., 2024). In addition, this study supports the trauma informed approach to understanding both those who present PD and CPTSD symptomatology among IPV perpetrators (for example Strength at Home (Taft et al., 2016)).
