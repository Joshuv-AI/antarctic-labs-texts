Ionic Conductivity of Composite Polymer Electrolyte: Clarifying the Role of the Interface with Nonconductive Particles
Authors: Guillaume Navallon, Federico Monaco, Katharina Märker, Peter Cloetens, Jakub Drnec, Duncan Atkins, Lionel Picard, Sandrine Lyonnard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolid‐state electrolytes are considered as an enabler for batteries with extended energy density. Polymers are promising materials but show insufficient ion transport properties, a limitation that can be lifted by the fabrication of composite polymer electrolytes. Nevertheless, the role of the inorganic particles in tuning the conduction properties is often unclear, especially when inert fillers are added. Herein, poly(trimethylene carbonate) and alumina particles: α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH and γ‐Al2O3 are mixed by two dispersion methods to prepare a series of composites. The correlations between microstructure, surface chemistry, and transport properties are studied, which are evaluated by combining synchrotron nanotomography, solid‐state nuclear magnetic resonance, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, respectively. An increase of the conductivity by a factor of 1.9 ± 0.6 (1.4 ± 0.4) is observed for α‐Al2O3 (γ‐AlOOH) while no beneficial effect of dispersion is seen for γ‐Al2O3. It is found that dispersion of the particles is crucial, with an interfacial area between polymer matrix and inorganic fillers increased by a factor of 1.3–3 when the particles are dispersed. Moreover, the density of surface groups is estimated at the interface and correlations are found to the changes in conductivity, showing that interactions at the interface of the particles are one key parameter driving the composite performance.Keywords: batteries, interfaces, nanoparticles, nanotomography, organic–inorganic hybrid composites

Introduction

The sector of electric mobility pushed state‐of‐the‐art lithium‐ion technology to its limits as an energy storage device. In fact, high energy density and safety standards requested by the automotive market are hardly attainable with the actual materials and components.[ 1 , 2 ] Hence, the development of alternative technologies that would perform better than lithium ion and overcome its limitation has become a research priority.[ 3 , 4 ] In theory, a great increase in energy density would be possible using metallic lithium as the negative electrode, but this pathway requires to avoid lithium dendritic growth while functioning. Indeed, conventional liquid electrolyte (LE) cannot suppress Li plating which reduces efficiency and in the worst case causes short circuits.[ 5 , 6 ] LEs are also a concern for carmakers in terms of safety because of their high flammability and the intrinsic risk of leakage.

The use of metallic lithium electrode could be enabled by the utilization of solid‐state electrolytes (SSEs).[ 7 ] The solid nature of such type of material could hamper the formation of dendrites. SSE can be inorganic solid electrolytes (ISEs) such as ceramic or glass materials, or solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs). ISEs present relatively high ionic conductivity at room temperature but generally their brittle nature makes them difficult to process in thin separator and they are more likely to exhibit contact issues at the interface with the electrode.[ 8 , 9 ] Conversely, SPEs are relatively easy to produce with a lower cost; they are chemically more versatile and can be made flexible for an optimal contact with the electrodes.[ 10 , 11 ]

However, in practice, SPEs still lack sufficient transport properties at room temperature. As an example, the ionic conductivity of SPEs made of polyethylene oxide (PEO) stands between 10−7 and 10−5 S cm−1,[ 12 , 13 , 14 ] while for the most studied ISEs such as NASICON‐like structures (e.g., Li1.3Al0.3Ti1.7(PO4)3)[ 15 , 16 ] or garnets (e.g., Li7La3Zr2O12),[ 17 , 18 ] the ionic conductivity is two orders of magnitude higher (10−5 to 10−3 S cm−1).[ 19 ]

PEO has been for almost three decades the most studied polymer as an electrolyte.[ 20 ] Its ionic conductivity was first reported at the end of the 1970s after addition of alkaline salt in the polymer matrix.[ 21 , 22 ] Since then, extensive research was conducted to understand the ion transport mechanism and improve its performance. It is now broadly accepted that in most SPEs, the transport of ions between the coordination sites along the polymer chains is supported by segmental motion of the polymer and formation of a neighboring free volume.[ 23 , 24 , 25 ] The major drawback for PEO‐based SPE is the strong dependence of its properties on temperature. Indeed, PEO Li‐salt matrixes are usually semicrystalline, and the fastest ion conduction is achieved only above 60 °C, the melting temperature of the crystallites.[ 13 ] Moreover, the strong affinity of PEO with water and the chemical nature of the end groups can result in a reduced stability during operation.[ 26 ]

Extensive effort is made to overcome these limitations by synthesizing polymers with new chemistry or copolymers.[ 10 ] One promising strategy is to change the ethylene group for a carbonate group. Similar to the solvent used in LE, the carbonate group has a stronger dielectric constant for enhanced salt dissociation and a weaker chelating strength to Li+ as compared with ethylene groups.[ 27 , 28 , 29 ] This could be advantageous for ion transport in the SPE given that the polymer has an aliphatic backbone providing sufficient segmental mobility. Following this strategy, it has been reported that solid‐state Li batteries using a poly(trimethylene carbonate) (PTMC)–poly(ε‐caprolactone) copolymer[ 30 , 31 ] or poly(ethylene carbonate) (PEC)[ 32 ] as a SPE can perform well.

Another strategy to improve the transport properties of SPEs is to fabricate composite polymer electrolytes (CPEs) by mixing particles inside a SPE. In some conditions, the fabrication of CPEs by adding particles inside the polymer matrix can enhance the ionic conductivity.[ 19 ] To our knowledge, this strategy was first introduced by Croce et al.[ 33 ] The authors observed improved ionic conductivity of a PEO‐based electrolyte after addition of non‐conductive particles (Al2O3 and TiO2). More recently, this strategy has also been applied using ion conductive (e.g., ISE) particles to further improve the ionic conductivity of SPEs and mitigate the contact issues of pure ISEs with the electrode.[ 34 ] Despite the idea being two decades old, there is still an ongoing debate in this research area due to inconsistent and contradictory results.

The first studies reporting conductivity enhancement after the addition of non‐conductive fillers proposed two complementary hypotheses. In the first one, broadly accepted in the literature, the presence of particles in the semicrystalline PEO matrix reduces the degree of crystallinity of the electrolyte,[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] hence the larger amount of amorphous phase retained allows an improvement of the overall ionic conductivity. However, increase in conductivity of CPEs above the melting temperature (T m) of PEO was also reported in the early studies supporting this scenario, which cannot be ascribed to a larger amount of the favorable amorphous phase since above T m the whole system is amorphous.[ 39 ]

The complementary hypothesis is that beneficial interactions for ion transport happen at the interface between the polymer–salt phase and the particles. These presumed beneficial interactions would create a space charge region at the interface with particles, improving transport properties.[ 34 , 40 , 41 ] The interactions are proposed to originate from the acidic nature of the surface of the particles that would have Lewis acid–base interactions with the anions and the coordinating groups of the polymer chain.[ 36 , 37 , 39 , 42 , 43 , 44 ] Such Lewis acid–base interactions may favor Li salt dissociation and trap counter‐ions, hence increasing the number of free ions and the transport number of the electrolyte. They would even weaken the Li+ coordination with the polymer.[ 45 ] In the region around the particle surface the polymer matrix is also constrained, creating a free volume that promotes the ion mobility.[ 46 , 47 ] These effects combine into the presence of an interphase region around the particle. Note that similar interphase regions have been also suggested for CPEs containing conductive particles. In these systems, where particles supply, in principle, the fastest conduction pathway for ion transport, it was often reported that the conduction at the interface is the main factor behind the conductivity enhancement.[ 34 , 48 , 49 , 50 ]

Through the years, these primary hypotheses have been continuously challenged by experimental studies reporting limited—or even negative—effects of added particles on the transport properties of the CPEs. Moreover, the dynamics of the polymer do not appear to be significantly affected by the presence of particles.[ 51 , 52 , 53 ] Reproducibility of the enhancement of the conductivity seems to be a concern as suggested by Fullerton et al. in their study on the role of water uptake.[ 54 ] Computational studies, challenged by the extremely complex nature of CPEs, also suffer from inconsistent findings. Some have reported an increase in the conductivity with the addition of particles,[ 55 , 56 ] while others have reported the opposite effect.[ 57 , 58 ] The lack of understanding of the underlying role of the particles constitutes a limitation for optimization of CPEs since it is unclear how to tailor the electrolyte composition for the best performances. Hence, an in‐depth investigation of the fundamentals of ion transport in CPE is still essential.

Here, we designed a series of model CPEs composed of poly(trimethylene carbonate) (PTMC) and three transition alumina particles: α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH, and γ‐Al2O3 to investigate the role of interfaces between particles and polymer–salt phase on the transport properties of the electrolyte. Our objective is not to optimize the performance of novel polymer‐based electrolytes toward application, but to shed light on the effect of local surface interactions on the ionic conductivity of archetypal organic–inorganic hybrid materials. We chose PTMC because it is fully amorphous once mixed with LiTFSI salt to form an electrolyte. Studies reporting the use of this polymer and adding ceramic particles to form a CPE are scarce.[ 59 ] However, the amorphous nature of PTMC in electrolytes is of great interest compared with the more popular and semi‐crystalline PEO, as changes in ion transport properties of the CPE cannot be ascribed to a reduction of crystallinity. Using PTMC as the polymer matrix, we isolate the effect of surface interactions with the added particles on the ion transport mechanisms. We chose α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH, and γ‐Al2O3 as these materials do not conduct Li+. Hence, the ionic conductivity of the resulting CPEs is mostly affected by interactions at the interface between particles and the polymer–salt matrix. The three types of particles have been primarily selected for their crystallinity and their presumed influence on ion transport due to specific surface properties. Indeed, γ‐Al2O3 is often regarded as an acidic substrate while α‐Al2O3 is more neutral in comparison.[ 60 ] γ‐Al2O3 is hence expected to have the strongest influence on ionic conductivity of the CPE. γ‐AlOOH was chosen for the prominent presence of polar —OH groups, which could form Lewis acid–base interactions with the polymer–salt matrix.[ 36 ] The effect of particle dispersion before their addition to the polymer solution has been carefully taken into account in our study. Dispersion and breaking of the particle aggregate prior to the addition inside the polymer–salt matrix should increase the number of beneficial interactions and, in turn, improve the transport properties of the CPE. Moreover, the particles should be more homogeneously distributed in the volume of the electrolyte, which will reduce the extent of polymer regions outside of the influence of the particle.

By combining physicochemical characterization of the particle properties and their morphology, and synchrotron nanotomography of the composite, we qualify and quantify the interphase region and discuss the possible correlation with transport properties of the electrolyte. In view of the estimated density of hydroxyls groups at the surface of the particles, we clarify the role of the particles and stress the importance of their state of dispersion when preparing advanced composite electrolytes.

Experimental Section

Electrolyte Preparation

Poly(trimethylene carbonate) (PTMC) was synthesized by cationic ring opening polymerization of trimethylene carbonate (4MedChem) using phenylpropanol as an initiator. The polymer was obtained after successive precipitations and washing with methanol. End groups of the polymer chains were then protected by reaction with benzoyl chloride (see Supporting Information 1 for more details). The final polymer with an average molecular weight (Mw) of ≈10 kg mol−1 was dried under vacuum for a week and then stored in an Ar‐filled glove box. Note that we have used two batches of pure PTMC synthesized in a sequential manner to cover the material needs for preparing sufficient amounts of electrolyte and perform all characterization campaigns, including material‐hungry reproducibility tests. LiTFSI is used as the lithium salt and dried in vacuum at 120 °C for 72 h and stored in an Ar‐filled glove box. Series of composite electrolyte were prepared inside a glove box by adding the particles to a solution of PTMC and LiTFSI in anhydrous acetone. The concentration of particles was set to 2 % in volume versus the dried polymer (ρPTMC=1.14g.mL−1). The molar ratio of carbonate groups (CO3) and Li+ was 14:1. Three types of particles were used: α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH, and γ‐Al2O3 (American Elements) with respective nominal size and shape: 50 nm spherical, 1–2 μm spherical, and 2–6 nm × 200–400 nm nanorods (for X‐ray diffraction (XRD) diffractograms and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images, see Supporting Information 2). Before use in the preparation of electrolytes, the particles were spread in a glass petri dish and dried in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for a minimum of 72 h. The dried particles were stored in an Ar‐filled glove box. For some electrolytes, dispersion of the particles was conducted prior to the addition to the solution of PTMC and LiTFSI. To do so, particle aggregates were broken down and suspended in acetone using ball‐milling at 300 rpm for 15 min in a bowl of 50 mL with 100 beads made of agate. The particle suspension was then added to the solution of PTMC and LiTFSI to prepare CPE with so‐called dispersed particles. Once the particle added, excess acetone was first evaporated on a heat plate under constant magnetic agitation. Final removal of the solvent residue was completed by vacuum drying in a Büchi oven for more than 72 h at 100 °C. The 2 vol% concentration of particles was calculated assuming the density of PTMC and LiTFSI was 1.14 and 1.33 g mL−1, respectively. Density of the powders was measured with a He pycnometer, and this value was used for the weighting, see Table 1 .

Ion Transport Measurements

Ionic conductivity of the CPE was determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The impedance cell consisted of a CR2032 coin cell where a pellet of the electrolyte was centered in a poly(ethylene) spacer ring with a thickness of 60 μm and an inside diameter of 12 mm and squeezed between two blocking stainless‐steel electrodes. The cells were prepared inside an Ar‐filled glove box before being taken out for measurements on a Biologic VPM300 analyzer. To ensure good contact between the electrolyte and the blocking electrodes, the assembled coin cells were first annealed at 60 °C for 24 h. The experiments were carried out in the frequency range of 1 MHz–100 mHz with an amplitude of 30 mV at temperature ranging from 80 to 10 °C with 1 h plateaus every 10 °C. An equivalent circuit with a constant phase element in series with a resistance and a second constant phase element both in parallel was adjusted to the experimental impedance spectra. Conductivity results are reported in the form of Arrhenius plots with each point being the average of three different coin cells and 5 to 10 EIS measurements on the same temperature plateau.

Li+ transference number was measured following the Bruce–Vincent method.[ 61 , 62 ] The cell was identical to the one described for EIS measurements, except that the two stainless‐steel electrodes were replaced by two discs of metallic lithium.

X‐ray Nanotomography

X‐ray phase contrast imaging was carried out at beamline ID16a of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF, France).[ 63 , 64 ] Before illuminating the sample, the high‐energy beam (33.35 keV) is first nanofocused on a circular point of few tens of nanometers by two Kirkpatrick–Baez mirrors.[ 65 ] These mirrors and the sample holder are both placed in a vacuum chamber under 1 × 10−8 mbar. The sample holder is controlled by piezo motors allowing stable and nanometric positioning of the sample inside the beam.[ 66 , 67 ] Samples mounted on the holder are placed behind the focal point in a cone beam configuration allowing to control the final image resolution with ratio of distances between focal point, sample, and detector. To avoid loss of information in the image due to contrast extinction, a holographic reconstruction with four different samples to focus distances was used and for each distance 2000 tomography projections were obtained rotating the sample over 180° with a pixel size of 50 nm and exposure time of 50 or 75 ms.[ 68 ] For each projection, phase retrieval was carried out using in‐house codes developed in GNU Octave and ImageJ. The tomographic reconstruction was performed using the filtered back‐projection algorithm in the software PyHST2[ 69 ] to obtain the final volumes of 100 × 100 × 100 μm3 with a voxel size of 50 nm3. Image segmentation was done following Otsu algorithm[ 70 ] and microstructural parameters were obtained with MATLAB function regionprops3. Subsequent image analysis was conducted in ImageJ. Subvolumes called regions of interest (ROI) of 36–60 μm were extracted from the entire reconstruction. At least two ROIs for each sample have been studied to obtain statistics for the microstructural parameters.

Solid‐State NMR Experiments

To quantify the number of hydrogens in the dried particle powders, solid‐state 1H‐NMR spectra of the three types of particles were recorded under magic‐angle spinning (MAS, V R = 10 kHz) on a Bruker Avance Neo 500 MHz spectrometer (B 0 = 11.7 T), using a Bruker 4 mm CPMAS probe. All spectra were acquired with a rotor‐synchronized Hahn echo pulse sequence. 1H pulses were applied at 77 kHz radiofrequency field strength and the length of each echo period was one rotor period. For each sample, the T 1 relaxation time constant was measured in a saturation‐recovery experiment, and quantitative recycle delays (5 ⋅ T 1) were then used for the Hahn echo spectra. The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) signal of 1H is directly proportional to the number of protons in the samples. An empirical relation between the molar concentration of protons and their corresponding signal integral in the 1H NMR spectra was established through calibration with three adamantane (C10H16):KBr samples of known concentration. The integral was computed between −120 ppm and 100 ppm, including all spinning sidebands. The empirical relation and the calibration results are given in the Supporting Information (Table S1, Supporting Information) as well as the spectrum for the three particles (Figure S13–15, Supporting Information). All species used for calibration (adamantane, KBr) as well as the three types of particles were dried in a Büchi oven at 120 °C under vacuum for 72 h to remove any trace of humidity. They were then packed in 4 mm NMR rotors in a glove box.

Analytical Techniques

The size exclusion chromatography (SEC, Figure S1, Supporting Information) was performed at 35 °C in THF using a Viscotek GPCmax VE 2001 system equipped with a set of four Malvern columns: T2000 (300 × 8 mm; exclusion limit, 5 kDa), T3000 (300 × 8 mm; exclusion limit, 7 × 104 Da), and two T6000M (300 × 8 mm; exclusion limit, 2 × 107 Da) and a Viscotek TDA 305 detector at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. The polydispersity (M w/M n) of the polymers was calculated based on a polystyrene calibration. XRD of the alumina powder was performed on a Brucker AXS D8 with a Cu X‐rays source and the crystal phases were identified using DIFFRAC.SUITE EVA software (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Images of the particles were acquired by SEM with a Zeiss SIGMA300 (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Dynamic light scattering was performed to assess particle size distribution of dispersed and undispersed γ‐Al2O3 in acetone suspension with a Zetasizer Nano from Malvern‐Panalytical (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The density of dried particle was measured by He pycnometry. The powder is placed in a measurement cell of calibrated volume; the corresponding mass is reported in the analysis software. The powder volume is measured 50 times at a constant temperature of 21 °C. The specific surface of the particles and their pore properties was analyzed by gas absorption and the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) or Barrett‐Joyner‐Halenda (BJH) method, respectively, using a Micrometrics TriStarII Plus instrument.

Results and Discussion

A four‐step experimental workflow was applied, as schematized in Figure 1 . First, we prepared series of CPEs using three types of particles, which were dispersed or not before being added to the polymer–salt solution (Figure 1a). The conductivity of the various electrolytes was evaluated (Figure 1b), and their microstructure further imaged via synchrotron phase‐contrast nanotomography performed on the ID16a beamline at the ESRF (Figure 1c). Finally, we correlated the interactions in the interphase region with the conductivity by combining the obtained microstructural parameters to chemical information measured by solid‐state NMR.

Characteristics of the Composite Polymer Electrolyte

For the preparation of the CPE, we selected a fixed molar ratio of LiTFSI salt with carbonate groups (PTMC) and LiTFSI salt (CO3:Li+), as 14:1 (or 20 wt% of LiTFSI), which is close to a previously reported optimal concentration for the conductivity of PTMC‐based SPE.[ 71 ] We also fixed a constant particle content of 2 vol% (or ≈5.5 wt%) to prepare all CPEs. We did not explore other volume ratios because particles aggregation might vary differently for the three types of particles. This would add a variable that is beyond precise control and can affect the attempts to examine potential correlations between the amount of interface and some selected functional properties as ionic conductivity. Hence, with our formulation strategy, we control the polymer, salt, and filler ratios, and therefore focus on the impact of two parameters only: 1) the dispersion state of the particles depending on the preparation method and 2) their chemical properties on the surface.

The synthesis protocol and the characterization of the PTMC are detailed in Figure S1, Supporting Information. Three types of aluminum oxide and oxyhydroxide particles have been chosen: α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH, and γ‐Al2O3. Some of their properties of interest are reported in Table 1. SEM images and XRD diffractograms are provided in Figure S2, Supporting Information.

These three types of particles were selected for their various surface properties and the presumed influence on ion transport. Indeed, γ‐Al2O3 which has an acidic surface is expected to give the best improvement of ion conductivity, whereas α‐Al2O3 is more neutral in comparison.[ 60 ] γ‐AlOOH contains polar —OH groups within its crystal lattice and as surface groups, which should interact with the polymer–salt matrix.[ 36 ] A schematic of Lewis acid–base interactions supposedly taking place at the surface of the particles is presented on the schematic Figure 1d.

To promote the number of interactions between the polymer–salt matrix and the particles, we pursued a step of dispersion and breaking of the aggregates before adding the particle suspension in the PTMC–LiTFSI solution, as illustrated in Figure 1a. This step was done by mildly ball‐milling the particles in acetone for 15 min at 300 rpm. The reduction of the size of the particles and aggregates in the resulting suspension was controlled for γ‐Al2O3 by dynamic light scattering measurements. The result shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information, confirms a reduction of the median size of particles and aggregates from 900 to 400 nm after this dispersion step. We assumed that this protocol is similarly reducing average size of the two other types of particles.

Transport Properties

We assessed the influence of particle addition on the ionic conductivity of the CPE using EIS and computing the ionic conductivity. Data were acquired during a cooling ramp between 80 and 10 °C, using three different coin cells for each sample to evaluate reproducibility of the measurement, hence necessitating the use of two batches of PTMC. The typical variation for three coin cells is shown in Figure S4, Supporting Information. Results presented thereafter are averaged. The ionic conductivities, computed by fitting an equivalent circuit to the EIS spectra, are shown in Figure 2a,b,c for CPE containing α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH, and γ‐Al2O3, respectively. Some variability in the molar mass distribution of pure PTMC arise from distinct batch origin (see Figure S1, Supporting Information). Also, we noticed an ageing phenomenon observed on the conductivity of our samples (more details given in Supplementary Information 5). Therefore, to avoid potential bias induced by batch provenience or storage times, we adopted a relative approach and compared each composite with the reference SPE (labeled Ref. 1 or Ref. 2) prepared at the same moment with the same PTMC. As shown in Figure 2a, the presence of α‐Al2O3 never improved the conductivity of CPE with respect to the corresponding SPE reference. However, it is worth noting that the dispersion of this type of particles reduced their detrimental influence. The conductivity results for CPE containing γ‐AlOOH are shown in Figure 2b where in all cases the addition of the particles significantly improved the electrolyte conductivity in comparison with the corresponding SPE. In Figure 2c, we can see that no—or little—increase in conductivity was achieved with γ‐Al2O3. Surprisingly, the sample with undispersed particles performed better. In all cases, the presence of alumina oxide or oxyhydroxide seems to have a moderate influence on the ability of the PTMC‐based electrolyte to conduct ions, and the achieved conductivities remain much below the required values of >1 mS cm−1 for an application in real systems.

We also fitted the conductivity results with the Vogel–Tamman–Fulcher behavioral law to compare the pseudo‐activation energies of each composite. The resulting fits are shown in Figure S6, Supporting Information, together with the difference in pseudo‐activation energy with respect to the reference SPE. We found that only the presence of γ‐AlOOH and γ‐Al2O3 reduced the pseudo‐activation energy of the CPEs. No trend regarding the effect of the dispersion state of the particle could be established.

To account for our sample variability, we introduce the ratio R=σCPEσSPE ref. This quantity allows comparing the influence of the particles in the various composition. These results, shown in Figure 2d, highlight the changes in conductivity due to the presence of particles and their dispersion state. As a general trend, we note that the influence of particles, regardless of their nature or dispersion state, is more pronounced at low temperatures. This observation is illustrated when examining the fitted pseudo‐activation energy (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The CPE with the strongest temperature dependence also presents the largest change in pseudo‐activation energy compared with the SPE. The CPE containing α‐Al2O3 never performed better than the corresponding SPE reference. In average, over the studied temperature range, the conductivity of the electrolyte is halved (R = 0.51 ± 0.15) when α‐Al2O3 particles are undispersed, while there are almost no changes in conductivity (R = 0.90 ± 0.06) for dispersed particles. For the CPE prepared with γ‐AlOOH, the conductivity increased up to a factor of 4 at low temperatures for the sample containing dispersed particles. In average, over temperatures ranging from 10 to 80 °C, the measured conductivity increased by a factor of 2.0 ± 0.1 when the particles are not dispersed, and a factor of 2.8 ± 0.9 when they are dispersed. For CPE containing γ‐Al2O3, the conductivity increase is less pronounced, especially for dispersed particles. In comparison with the corresponding SPE in the range 10–80 °C, the conductivity is increased by a factor of 1.8 ± 0.4 in average, and 1.1 ± 0.2 for dispersed γ‐Al2O3. These results indicate that the overall impact of adding inert fillers in a concentration of 2 vol% is moderate and point to the difficulty in establishing a clear correlation between the nature of nonconductive particles, the mixing protocols, and obtained conductivities.

To specifically quantify the effect of the dispersion and compare its significance for the three types of added particles, we introduce another metric, R disp., which is the ratio between the conductivity of the composites with dispersed and undispersed particles, each normalized by the conductivity of their corresponding reference SPE, as expressed in Equation (1).

This ratio allows the quantification of the sole effect of particle dispersion, as shown in Figure 2e and visually highlights the gains or losses in ionic conductivity. Now, we see that the dispersion of α‐Al2O3 improved the conductivity of the CPE up to a factor of R disp = 3 at low temperature and, in average, by a factor of R disp = 1.9 ± 0.6. In the case of γ‐AlOOH, dispersion of this particle improved the conductivity to a lesser extent with an average increase, in the temperature range 10–80 °C, by a factor of R disp = 1.4 ± 0.4. Finally, as shown in Figure 2c, the dispersion of γ‐Al2O3 was detrimental to the conductivity of the resulting CPE with a decrease by almost two‐thirds (R disp = 0.63 ± 0.4).

In conclusion, addition of γ‐AlOOH was beneficial to the electrolyte while α‐Al2O3 was not, and γ‐Al2O3 had a minor effect. Furthermore, these results stress the importance of the particles dispersion state, which can either improve the conductivity of the electrolyte in some cases (by a factor of 1.5–2), or mitigate detrimental effects, as for α‐Al2O3 particles. However, this beneficial effect of dispersion is not verified for γ‐Al2O3.

Additionally, we performed transference number measurements following the Bruce–Vincent method[ 61 , 62 ] (see Figure S7, Supporting Information) on CPEs containing α‐Al2O3 and γ‐AlOOH. We found that TLi+ is reduced by the dispersion from around 0.7 for the SPE and the CPE with undispersed particles to about 0.5 for CPE with dispersed particles for both α‐Al2O3 and γ‐AlOOH. This shows that Li+ mobility is affected not only by the presence of the filler but also by its dispersion state.

Overall, these measurements of the ionic transport properties illustrate the complexity of the effects of added particles inside a polymer electrolyte. Even though the studied system was simplified by using an amorphous polymer–salt phase and nonconductive particles, understanding the role of the particles is not straightforward. Because the added particles do not provide alternative pathways to the polymer–salt phase for ion transport, the observed differences in conductivity probably arise from differences in the physicochemical interactions at the surface of the particles. Hence, either the amount of surface interactions with the particles or the chemical nature of these interactions influences the final conductivity of the composite. We further investigated these potential correlations between the observed changes in conductivity and the microstructure of the CPE by probing the dispersion state of the particles with X‐ray phase contrast nanotomography.

CPE Microstructure Studied by X‐Ray Phase Contrast Nanotomography

To evaluate the microstructure of the CPEs, we performed X‐ray phase contrast nanotomography at the ID16a beamline of the ESRF. The principle of the measurement is schematized in Figure 1c. When an X‐ray beam is transmitted through a material, not only its amplitude is altered but also its phase. Contrary to more conventional X‐ray imaging techniques based on the adsorption phenomena, phase contrast imaging relies on changes in the phase of the beam. These phase changes are related to the real part of the refraction index which is proportional to the electronic density of the various domains inside the imaged sample.[ 72 ] In the investigated CPE, two domains with distinct electronic densities are present: the low electronic density phase in the polymer–Li salt regions and the high electronic density phase formed by alumina particles and aggregates. The full 3D volumes were reconstructed and then split into subvolumes called ROI to enable statistics over the whole sample (see Experimental Section 2.3). Each ROI was segmented to assign the various phases and quantify their main characteristics. Visualization in three dimensions of selected ROIs is shown in Figure 3 for all CPEs samples prepared with α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH, and γ‐Al2O3, with and without dispersion. The high electronic density phase (the inorganic phase) is shown in color while the low electronic density phase (the polymer matrix) is removed from the 3D rendering.

These 3D images give an overview of the composite microstructure. The microstructures of CPEs prepared with undispersed particles are shown in the left column (Figure 3a,c,e) and the ones for dispersed samples in the right column (Figure 3b,d,f). The influence of dispersion on the CPE microstructure is clearly visible when comparing the two sets of data. Indeed, CPEs prepared with the dispersion step (right column) present an apparent larger number of smaller objects (particles or aggregates). When comparing the types of particles, we can see that CPEs containing α‐Al2O3 and γ‐AlOOH present larger objects, meaning that these particles probably form larger aggregates when undispersed compared to γ‐Al2O3 (Figure 3a,c,e respectively). When the particles have been dispersed in the CPE, the size of the objects seems visually homogeneous among the three types of particles (Figure 3b,d,f).

To assess quantitatively the quality of the particle dispersion, we computed the distance to the closest inorganic object in any point of the polymer–salt phase. Such result, called distance map (see Figure S8, Supporting Information, for more details), is also shown in Figure 3 for all six samples. Regions close to an object (particle or aggregate) are represented with dark colors. In those regions, the polymer phase is, to a certain extent, under a stronger influence of the inorganic phase. Polymer and lithium ions in the regions represented in lighter colors (orange and yellow) are further away of the objects and hence less affected by the alumina. The state of dispersion of the particles induced visual changes in the distance map, as further quantified by the corresponding histogram of distances shown on Figure 3. The reported histogram represents the relative number of positions in the volume within a given distance to the closest object. On the same graph, the corresponding cumulative count is also plotted. The analysis of the distances to the closest object shows that the dispersion of the particles narrows the histograms, which means that objects are better distributed in the volume of the CPE. This result is obvious for α‐Al2O3 and γ‐AlOOH and less pronounced for γ‐Al2O3. Indeed, for CPE prepared with α‐Al2O3, in most of the polymer phase the closest object is found between 1 and 3 μm. In contrast, for the CPE prepared with dispersed particles, the dispersion of the distance to the closest object is much narrower, ranging from 0.5 to 1 μm. The cumulative count, shown with the colored line, is steeper for dispersed samples because of the narrower histogram. It also gives the median distance to the closest particles, which is reduced by a factor of 1.8, 1.55, and 1.13 for dispersed α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH, and γ‐Al2O3, respectively. This diminution of the distance to the closest particle inside CPEs which had a dispersion step means that particles or aggregates of particles are reduced in size on average and more homogeneously distributed in the volume of the electrolyte, which is helpful to connect regions of polymer under the influence of particles.

To further evaluate the microstructure of the composites, we extracted three parameters of interest that define the inorganic phase: 1) an equivalent size for each object, defined as the diameter of a sphere with an equivalent volume, 2) the total volume occupied by the objects, and 3) the total surface area of the objects.

These parameters were obtained for each individual ROI to evaluate their representativeness. We found little differences, confirming that the chosen size for the ROIs is large enough to be representative of the sample (see Figure S9–S11, Supporting Information). In the following, we average values over the ROIs. Note that the size of the ROIs was dependant on samples to optimize the amount of the usable data acquired. As quantified parameters like total surface area and total volume occupied by the particles depend on the size of the ROI, the total surface area was normalized by the ROI volume into the surface developed by the objects for a unit volume of electrolyte. In the same manner, the total volume occupied by the objects was normalized into a volume fraction of the particles. The different sizes of ROIs, the corresponding average number of objects in those ROIs, as well as the total volume and total surface area of the objects are given in Figure S12, Supporting Information.

The ROI‐normalized and averaged microstructural parameters are reported in Figure 4 . The average volumetric fraction of the particles (Figure 4a) is ranging from 2.1 to 8.3 vol%. Surprisingly, the computed values are quite far from the expected value of 2 vol%, as prepared, and seems to depend on the type of particle. Our hypothesis to explain this discrepancy is a bias due to resolution effects in the analysis of the nanotomography images. First, we must consider that the unit element of the images, i.e., the voxels, is cubes that do not accurately represent the real shape and surface curvature of the inorganic objects. Second, the size of these voxels (the resolution) is 50 × 50 × 50 nm, which means that solid particles and porosities smaller than 50 nm cannot be resolved in the images. Given those two limitations and the fact that the objects are mostly particle aggregates, it is possible that the tomography cannot account for the porosities present inside the aggregates and/or at the surface of the particles. Hence, both the volume and the surface area of the objects are overestimated, which results in volume fractions larger than the nominal 2 vol%. In a further attempt to explain the differences in volume fraction between the three particles, we evaluated their surface porosities by gas adsorption techniques and the BJH method.[ 73 ] Results are given in Table 1. We observe a great variability between particles with porosity volumes of 0.127, 0.178, and 0.756 cm3 g−1 for α‐Al2O3, γ‐AlOOH. and γ‐Al2O3, respectively. Those porosity volumes show the same trend as the computed volumetric fraction from X‐ray tomography, which would indicate a relation between the porosity of the particles and their probably overestimated volume in the CPE.

In Figure 4b, the average median diameter of the objects for each CPE is shown, with values ranging from 410 nm for the composite containing dispersed α‐Al2O3 to 533 nm for the composite with undispersed γ‐AlOOH. The median size of particles and aggregates in each sample are relatively close to each other and do not seem to strongly depend on the type of particle. We also performed sphericity and shape factors evaluations, but they did not reveal significant dependence on particle types or state of dispersion. Note that, as individual particles form large aggregates of irregular shapes with an extended range of size distribution, the impact of nanoscale versus microscale sizes (α‐Al2O3 vs γ‐AlOOH) or the influence of aspect ratio (rods for γ‐Al2O3) cannot be investigated precisely. Nevertheless, the effect of dispersion is clearly visible for all three particles with a significant reduction of the median size and a narrower standard deviation.

The average specific surface of objects (Figure 4c) is the ratio between the objects’ surface and their volume. This parameter evidences the increase of particle surface due to the dispersion procedure. Dispersing the particles significantly increased their average surface by a factor of 1.9 for α‐Al2O3 and 1.6 for both γ‐AlOOH and γ‐Al2O3. The parameter of interest for comparing the electrolyte is the surface developed by the particles per unit volume of electrolyte, called normalized interface area in Figure 4d. The average normalized interface area between particles and the polymer–salt phase ranges from 0.05 to 0.349 μm2 per unit volume of electrolyte depending on the type of particle. α‐Al2O3 shows the smallest interface area, while γ‐Al2O3 bears the largest and γ‐AlOOH lies in between. This observation brings a first level of understanding to the conductivity achieved with the CPEs. Particularly, we found that if the interfacial area between the polymer and the particle is too little, as in the case of α‐Al2O3, the nonconductive particles seem to be detrimental to the overall conductivity of the CPE. Regarding the effect of dispersion, the interface available for interactions is significantly increased by factors of 3.0 for α‐Al2O3, 1.6 for γ‐AlOOH, and 1.3 for γ‐Al2O3 when the particles are dispersed. It is worth mentioning that these ratios fall in the same range as what we observed for the conductivity increase of the various compounds (Figure 2e).

Generally, the microstructural study supports a correlation between the surface developed by the particles and the measured changes in conductivity of the CPEs. The interface area seems a relevant parameter to estimate the possible amount of interaction between the polymer–salt phase and the particles. However, the true number of interactions also depends on the amount of surface groups on the particles. To access this chemical information, we further performed solid‐state 1H‐NMR.

Quantification of the Chemical Interactions

After drying the particles (see Experimental Section 2.1), we expect the surface of the aluminum oxide and oxyhydroxide to be composed of polar hydroxyl groups (—OH, bounded to one or several aluminum) or bare Al atoms (bounded to one or several oxygens).[ 60 ] This surface chemistry is directly involved in the interactions occurring between the particles and the other species in the electrolyte (PTMC and/or dissociated LiTFSI). Those interactions are supposedly responsible, at least partly, for the changes in ionic conductivity measured for the composite electrolytes. With a simple calibration procedure (see Experimental Section 2.4, Table S1–S2 and Figure S13, Supporting Information), the integration of the spectra measured with solid‐state 1H‐NMR allows a direct quantification of the amount of hydrogen atoms in the powder samples. Detailed procedure to compute the amount of H in the particles along with spectra for all three particles is shown in Figure S13–16, Supporting Information. If we assume that all H atoms occur at —OH groups and that all —OH groups are located on the particle surface, then the hydrogen resonance area is proportional to the number of surface hydroxyls. However, in the case of γ‐AlOOH, —OH groups are also located between the layers of the crystal structure. Therefore, the number of —OH found with this method should be regarded as an upper limit value; the real number of —OH at the interface is likely to be smaller especially for γ‐AlOOH. Nevertheless, this method still enables a relative comparison between the particles, and the estimated amounts inform on the chemical state of the particle surfaces.

The concentration of hydrogen atoms per grams of particles is given in Table 2 as well as the estimated —OH surface density. α‐Al2O3 particles present the largest amount of hydroxyls groups, with 15.8 —OH groups per nm2 while γ‐AlOOH and γ‐Al2O3 have more similar density of surface hydroxyls with, respectively, 10.5 and 12.7 OH nm−2. In comparison with previously reported values for the well‐studied γ‐Al2O3 particle,[ 74 , 75 ] our results are one order of magnitude higher but in those experiments the surface chemistry of the particles is carefully controlled through several steps of calcination, rehydration, and dehydration. We believe that the drying procedure followed in the present work is sufficient to remove physically adsorbed water molecules, but it probably does not significantly affect the amount of chemically bounded hydroxyls that remain abundant on the particle surface. Note that the surface state of the particles might also be modified once dispersed in acetone during the preparation of the CPEs.

Discussion

To evaluate the true number of interactions between the polymer–salt phase and the particles, the chemical information obtained with 1H‐NMR was combined with the geometrical information extracted from the nanotomography, yielding an estimated amount of surface hydroxyls groups per volume of electrolyte. The values for all six CPEs studied with nanotomography are shown in Figure 5 . Since the estimated —OH surface density for the three particles is rather similar, the trend already observed for the interface area between the types of particles (Figure 4d) is not strongly affected when converted into the number of surface groups at the interface. However, this number is an interesting parameter as it enables to draw conclusions regarding the role of the particles on the measured conductivity: 1) First, as suggested from the observation of the interface area, it seems that a too small interface resulting in a too low number of surface groups available for interaction cannot be beneficial for the ionic conductivity of the CPE. This is what we observed in the case of undispersed α‐Al2O3 with only 7.95 × 105 OH per μm3 of electrolyte (see Figure 5, green bars), a value 1.7–5.6 times smaller than for the other samples, associated with a reduction of the CPE conductivity with respect to the SPE (R=σCPEσSPE ref=0.7). 2) Second, our results also suggest that the conductivity does not linearly scale as the estimated amount of surface hydroxyls per volume of electrolyte, but that there is an optimum amount of interactions to occur in the electrolyte volume. This is deduced from the case of γ‐Al2O3 (orange bar), where the interface and amount of —OH at the interface of the CPE is the largest (3.54 × 106 and 4.42 × 106 OH μm−3 of electrolyte for nondispersed and dispersed samples, respectively), while the conductivity increase is found to be quite moderate (a factor 2) or even almost inexistent (a factor 1.2) for the dispersed sample. We believe identification of the species from the polymer–salt phase involved in the interactions (Li counter‐ion or coordinating group from the polymer backbone) would help for a better understanding of this nonlinear behavior. 3) Finally, the amount of surface groups is not the only relevant parameter to explain the conductivity because CPEs with similar amount of surface —OH did not exhibit similar conductivity results. Indeed, the CPEs prepared with dispersed α‐Al2O3 and dispersed γ‐AlOOH both contain relatively close amount of —OH per μm3 of electrolyte (2.37 × 106 and 2.19 × 106 OH μm−3, see Figure 5, hatched green and hatched blue bars, respectively) but the first showed no change in conductivity compared to the SPE while the second had an increase by a factor of 2.8 ± 0.9 in average. We believe this discrepancy indicates that the surface groups of both particles do not interact with the same strength due to difference in their degree of activity (or acidity level).[ 60 ]

Correlations between conductivity and estimated amount of surface hydroxyls. a) Ratio between the conductivity of the CPE and a reference SPE at 20 °C. b) Number of OH μm−3 of electrolyte. CPEs with dispersed particles (hatched bars) and undispersed particles (plain bars).

We find that the conductivity changes resulting from the addition of particles in the polymer electrolyte are at least partly correlated with the estimated number surface hydroxyls per volume of electrolyte. This supports the idea that the acidic nature of surface hydroxyls making Lewis acid–base interactions with the polymer coordinating groups or the anions and forming an interphase region around the particles is beneficial for ion transport. As shown by the distance map, dispersion of the particles is key to allow a good distribution of the particles in the electrolyte and hence homogenize the influence of the particles through the whole volume of the electrolyte. Dispersion of the particles is also key to increase the total amount of beneficial interactions between the particles and the polymer–salt phase by simply increasing the available interface. However, there seems to be an optimum number of interactions or an optimal strength of the interactions to produce the largest possible beneficial effect on the ionic conductivity of the CPE.

Conclusions

Determining the underlying mechanisms for changes in conductivity of a CPE due to the addition of inorganic particles requires a precise knowledge of the state of dispersion of the particles inside the electrolyte and their surface chemistry. Due to the multiple interdependent factors affecting ionic conductivity of composites after particle addition, it is difficult to design simple guidelines for the improvement of polymer electrolyte conductivity with nonconductive particles. However, we showed that the combination of microstructural parameters from X‐ray phase contrast nanotomography with chemical information from solid‐state 1H‐NMR was a successful approach to clarify the role of nonconductive particles in the conductivity of CPEs. The 3D reconstructions evidenced the effect of particle dispersion which results in a significant increase of the interface area between the particles and the polymer–salt phase. The estimated number of surface groups thanks to the integration of the 1H resonance peaks from solid‐state NMR supplements the microstructural parameters with a chemical information. The quantity of surface interaction is beneficial for the CPE conductivity up to a certain level and the nature of the surface groups or supporting particle seems to affect this beneficial influence.

Ionic conductivity is not the only property to consider when one would use a polymer‐based electrolyte inside a Li–metal system. Transference number, resistance to dendrite growth, and electrochemical stability, to cite only a few, are other important properties which may also be affected by the presence of particles in the polymer matrix. Detailed knowledge of the composite microstructure thanks to direct imaging or indirect techniques as small‐angle scattering could be of invaluable help to further clarify the synergies at play and help designing optimized composite electrolytes.
