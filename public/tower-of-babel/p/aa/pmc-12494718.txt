Mapping postpartum physical activity, sedentary time, and sleep: assessing the impact of prenatal physical activity interventions in the FitMum randomized controlled trial
Authors: Anne Dsane Jessen, Saud Abdulaziz Alomairah, Ida Karoline Bach Jensen, Signe de Place Knudsen, Caroline Borup Roland, Jane M. Bendix, Stig Molsted, Ellen Løkkegaard, Bente Stallknecht
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Postpartum physical activity (PA) is linked to improved mental health and sleep. Yet, many women do not return to pre-pregnancy PA levels, and sedentary behavior may increase. This study mapped PA, sedentary time, and sleep from early pregnancy to one year postpartum, and examined if prenatal PA interventions affect these outcomes postpartum. In the FitMum randomized controlled trial, 220 healthy, inactive pregnant women (< 15 + 0 weeks gestation) were assigned to standard care (CON), supervised exercise (EXE), or motivational PA counselling (MOT). Participants wore wrist-worn activity trackers from inclusion to one year postpartum and completed the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. We found that postpartum daily steps increased by ~ 2,000 compared to late pregnancy (p < 0.001), while moderate-to-vigorous-intensity PA (MVPA) declined (p < 0.001). Sedentary time remained similar. Sleep duration dropped postpartum in the first three months (p < 0.05), while sleep quality improved (p = 0.006). Prenatal PA interventions had minimal impact postpartum, however, EXE participants reported slightly better sleep than MOT at one year. In conclusion, PA patterns and sleep changed postpartum, with more daily steps, less MVPA, and reduced sleep duration. Prenatal PA interventions had limited effect. Longitudinal studies are needed to optimize timing of interventions for maternal health. The study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT03679130;20/09/2018.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18186-5.

Background

The postpartum period is challenging for healthy living as parenting takes up both attention and time1–3. Engaging in physical activity (PA) during the postpartum period is associated with positive outcomes, including improved mental health4,5enhanced sleep quality6and reduced weight gain or retention7. Despite its significance, the postpartum period remains overlooked in public health guidelines and research8. Currently, international guidelines for PA in the postpartum period are similar to the guidelines for PA in other adults: 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous-intensity PA (MVPA) per week, with local differences, for example, Danish recommendations are 210 min of MVPA per week9,10. For adults, sedentary time should be limited, and sleep recommendations for adults are 7–9 h per day11,12. However, it is important to acknowledge that infant care and feeding significantly affect daily routines, such as sleep and PA, making it difficult for many postpartum women to meet the recommendations consistently13.

The proportion of postpartum women meeting PA guidelines is low globally, and everyday barriers for PA, such as lack of social support, time, or uncertainties about PA options, can challenge engaging in PA2,3,8,14,15. Few studies have mapped the pattern of PA during pregnancy and postpartum, and the findings are inconsistent16–19. Postpartum PA generally decreases compared to pre-pregnancy, with variations influenced by factors such as the infant’s age and the number of other children18,19. Notably, adherence to international PA guidelines is lower among Danish mothers with infants and toddlers compared to those with older children, with non-adherence rates ranging from 60 to 70%20. Additionally, postpartum women often do not return to their pre-pregnancy PA levels, and sedentary behavior may remain stable or increase8,21. Mapping PA, sedentary time, and sleep from pregnancy through postpartum is essential given their combined impact on maternal physical and mental health. Evidence shows that prenatal PA interventions can improve behaviors like exercise adherence and diet during pregnancy, yet their effects postpartum are often modest or not maintained, particularly among women with low exercise adherence or higher BMI22,23. Additionally, PA during pregnancy has been linked to reduced postpartum depression and anxiety, highlighting potential psychological benefits that may extend into the postpartum period24. However, few studies have longitudinally tracked these behaviors together to understand patterns of change and the potential carry-over effects of prenatal interventions into the postpartum period. Understanding these trajectories can inform tailored strategies to support postpartum health and prevent weight retention, poor sleep, and mental health challenges.

In the FitMum randomized controlled trial (RCT), the main aim was to investigate strategies to increase PA during pregnancy among women with low PA25. Participants provided PA data continuously from inclusion (before gestational age (GA) week 15 + 0) to one year postpartum, making it possible to detect daily changes in health behaviors across pregnancy and the postpartum period. Participants in the FitMum RCT received either standard care (CON), structured supervised exercise (EXE), or motivational counselling on PA (MOT) during pregnancy25. Providing EXE was more effective than CON in increasing MVPA among pregnant women, whereas MOT did not achieve the same effect26. Furthermore, during pregnancy, the EXE intervention improved sleep quality and reduced sedentary time compared to CON, as assessed by both self-report and continuous activity tracker data27. Qualitative interviews made during the FitMum study showed differences in the understanding of PA between intervention groups, as participants in MOT perceived everyday life activities as PA, whereas participants in EXE regarded structured exercise, but not everyday life activities, as PA28. These differences, along with the lack of knowledge regarding postpartum health behaviors, highlight the need for understanding the long-term impact of prenatal interventions and health behaviors on postpartum health behaviors. Thus, there is a need to map postpartum health behaviors by objective methods21,22,29,30.

Aim

This study aimed to (1) assess how PA, sedentary time, and sleep change during the first year postpartum, and (2) explore whether PA interventions during pregnancy influence PA, sedentary time, and sleep during the postpartum period.

Methods

Participants, design and measurements

The present study is a secondary analysis of the FitMum randomized controlled trial conducted at Copenhagen University Hospital – North Zealand, Hilleroed, Denmark, from 2018 to 2022, aiming to increase PA among healthy, inactive pregnant women25. The inclusion criteria were: ultrasonic confirmed intrauterine pregnancy, ≥ 18 years of age, gestational age (GA) ≤ 15 + 0 weeks, BMI of 18.5–45 kg/m2, and ability to wear a wrist-worn activity tracker. Exclusion criteria were previous preterm delivery, obstetric or medical complications, structured MVPA ≥ 60 min/week during pregnancy, multiple pregnancies, inability to speak Danish, or alcohol or drug abuse25. A total of 219 participants were randomized before gestational age week 15 into three groups (ratio 1:2:2): standard care (CON), structured supervised exercise training (EXE), or motivational counselling on PA (MOT), in a parallel-group, superiority design to test whether the two interventions (EXE and MOT) were superior to standard care (CON). EXE participants were offered two one-hour exercise sessions (30 min of stationary biking and 30 min of strength training) per week and one one-hour swimming exercise (15 min swimming, 45 min water exercises) per week, equal to three hours of moderate-intensity PA per week throughout pregnancy. MOT participants were offered three group sessions (one one-hour, and two two-hour sessions) and four individual counselling sessions (one hour each) during pregnancy, with the Self-Determination Theory as the theoretical basis28,31.

The aim for participants in both intervention groups was to achieve 30 min of moderate-intensity PA (65–80% of age-predicted maximal heart rate from the activity tracker) per day. Interventions were delivered throughout pregnancy, with no further intervention provided postpartum. MVPA until GA week 28 was the primary endpoint in the FitMum randomized controlled trial. During the COVID-19 pandemic, due to the total lockdown on 11 March 2020 in Denmark, the intervention shifted to an online format and remained so until the intervention conclusion. A mixed methods evaluation of the FitMum study investigated if interventions were well delivered, showing high fidelity in both the original physical and the altered online COVID-19 study setups28. The FitMum design has been described elsewhere and the protocol published25.

Participants were asked to wear a Garmin vívosport activity tracker 24/7 from inclusion until one year postpartum. The activity tracker was worn on the non-dominant wrist and measured activity through accelerometry and heart rate monitoring32. Proprietary algorithms in the activity tracker calculated PA, sedentary time, and sleep. Moderate and vigorous PA levels were captured if the activity lasted for at least 10 consecutive minutes. Sedentary time was calculated by subtracting sleep time from total sedentary time, with awake sedentary time reflecting periods of minimal movement, such as sitting or resting. Participants uploaded activity tracker data to a research platform (Fitabase) and were reminded if synchronization was missing for seven days. This ensured minimal data loss within the device’s one-week battery lifespan. Activity tracker data from each participant was included in statistical analysis if a week included at least four days with a minimum of 12 h of daily wear time (from 6 a.m. to 12 a.m.)33.

The Danish version of the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ-DK)34 was administered digitally at baseline (GA < 15 weeks), GA 28 weeks, late pregnancy at GA 34 weeks, and one year postpartum. The PPAQ-DK assesses PA across daily activities using 33 questions, classifying activities as Metabolic Equivalent of Tasks (METs). Free-text responses were assigned MET values based on the Compendium of Physical Activities35. Sedentary time was calculated from data of PPAQ’s original sedentary activities (e.g., watching TV) and additional activities (e.g., crafts, driving, studying) as recommended36–38. For postpartum, pregnancy-specific terminology was adapted to suit the context.

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was administered digitally at baseline (GA < 15 weeks), GA 28 weeks, late pregnancy at GA 34 weeks, and one year postpartum. The PSQI consists of 19 questions that assess sleep quality and disturbances, generating a global score from 0 to 21. A lower score indicates better sleep, with scores < 5 classified as “good sleepers”39.

Ethics

The FitMum study was approved by the Regional Committee on Health Research Ethics (30/08/2018, #H-18011067), and the Danish Data Protection Agency (12/09/2018, #P-2019-512). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Participants received oral and written information about the study and gave written informed consent before inclusion in the study.

The FitMum study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT03679130; 20/09/2018.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as frequencies and proportions, mean ± standard deviation (SD), mean and 95% confidence intervals (CI), or median and interquartile range (IQR), with statistical significance defined as a p-value of < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2023) and Microsoft Excel. PA data from the activity trackers were analyzed according to the intention-to-treat principle including all randomized participants with available data. Missing data due to non-wear were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), generating 25 imputed datasets. Data were assumed missing at random. The imputation model included baseline characteristics (maternal age, gestational age at inclusion, working status, education, parity, weight and height), group allocation, and all available PA measurements. Analyses covered the period from randomization until the last day the activity tracker was worn postpartum or until dropout. The analysis was adjusted for baseline PA. To compare study groups, constrained linear mixed models were fitted with time as a factor for the activity tracker outcomes, PPAQ-DK, and PSQI. The main effects in the constrained linear mixed model were time, group, interactions between time and group, but no main effect of group as a constraint due to the randomized design. The model was estimated using generalized least squares with an unstructured correlation matrix across participants matrix and visit-specific variances. The model was fitted for each of the 25 imputed datasets, and the estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rule. Postpartum outcomes were considered secondary outcomes in the FitMum study, and the study was not powered to detect group differences in these analyses. The analyses are therefore exploratory in nature.

Results

Participants

Participants were randomized to either CON (n = 45), EXE (n = 87), or MOT (n = 87). After the data collection was completed, one participant withdrew consent, and MOT, therefore, contains information on 86 participants at baseline. Baseline characteristics of randomized participants are shown in Table 1.

From inclusion to delivery, 42 (19%) participants were lost to follow-up (personal reasons (n = 14), adverse event (n = 4), dissatisfaction with group (n = 4), time constraints (n = 11), did not want to wear activity tracker (n = 5), unknown reasons (n = 4)), and there were no differences in follow-up proportions between the three groups. In this study, 174 participants wore the activity tracker for at least one month postpartum, while 136 participants wore the activity tracker for a year postpartum. Compliance with wearing the activity tracker 24/7 declined throughout the study. During pregnancy, participants wore the tracker 77% of the time27. In the first six months postpartum, participants wore the activity tracker 59% (CON), 53% (EXE), and 45% (MOT) of the time, respectively. Over time, fewer participants contributed with data (Fig. 1). Across the entire postpartum year, participants wore the activity tracker about one third of the time, and the groupwise wear time percentages were 41% (CON), 35% (EXE), and 32% (MOT).

Daily steps, moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity, sedentary time, and sleep measured by activity tracker

Figure 2 presents unadjusted mean activity tracker outcomes for all participants from inclusion to one year postpartum, including daily steps, daily minutes of MVPA, and sedentary time and sleep time in hours per day. The number of steps and hours of sleep decreased throughout pregnancy, whereas MVPA increased until late in the third trimester but declined before delivery. For sedentary time, participants in general had stable levels, until late in the third trimester, where an increase in sedentary time was observed.

In the postpartum year, there was a marked increase in daily steps, surpassing late pregnancy levels with around 2,000 steps per day, except for the first month postpartum (Table 1S and Table 2S in supplementary information). Minutes of MVPA per day postpartum remained low compared to late pregnancy (Table 2S in supplementary information). Sedentary time was increased for two months after delivery compared to late pregnancy (p < 0.001), then returned to similar levels as during pregnancy in the third month postpartum and decreased compared to late pregnancy in the fourth through eleventh month postpartum (Table 2S in supplementary information). Sleep duration decreased somewhat during postpartum with women sleeping 0.93 (p < 0.001), 0.42 (p < 0.001), and 0.13 (p = 0.038) hours less per day in the first, second, and third month postpartum, respectively, compared with late pregnancy. Sleep duration in the fourth through eleventh month postpartum was similar to late pregnancy, but participants slept 0.17 h less in the twelfth month postpartum (p = 0.008) (Table 2S in supplementary information).

Measures of physical activity by the pregnancy physical activity questionnaire

The PPAQ-DK was answered by 147 participants at one year postpartum. Means and differences between study groups are presented in Table 4 S and Table 2, respectively. At one year postpartum, no significant differences were found between study groups in any of the PA intensities or activity types (Table 2).

MET hours per week spent in total, light, moderate, and household activities increased from late pregnancy to one year postpartum in all three study groups (p-values all < 0.001), and sports activities decreased by 4.3 MET hours per week for participants in EXE (p = 0.001) (Table 5 S in supplementary information). The increased sports activities in participants in EXE compared to both participants in CON and participants in MOT in late pregnancy26 were no longer visible one year postpartum (Table 2).

Measures of sleep by the Pittsburgh sleep quality index

PSQI was completed by 138 participants at one year postpartum. Means and differences between study groups are presented in Table 6 S and Table 3, respectively. At one year postpartum, participants in EXE slept more (0.12 h/day, p = 0.012), had better sleep efficiency (−0.21 score, p = 0.011), and had a better (lower) global PSQI score compared to participants in MOT (Table 3). There were no differences between participants in CON and EXE or participants in CON and MOT in the PSQI at one year postpartum (Table 3).

Differences in the PSQI scores during pregnancy are described in detail previously27. For the participants combined, from late pregnancy to one year postpartum, the global PSQI score decreased, i.e., sleep quality improved (p = 0.006) (Table 7 S in supplementary information). Sleep quality also improved significantly in participants in CON and EXE from late pregnancy to one year postpartum (Table 7 S in supplementary information). Yet, all three groups retained scores above 5, i.e., having poor sleep quality. Participants in all three study groups experienced fewer sleep disturbances one year after delivery compared to late pregnancy (p < 0.001) (Table 7 S in supplementary information). Participants in the MOT group fell asleep faster at one year postpartum compared to late pregnancy as their sleep latency was improved (p = 0.034) (Table 7 S in supplementary information). There were no significant changes in self-reported sleep duration, subjective sleep quality, sleep efficiency, sleep medication or daytime dysfunction from late pregnancy to one year postpartum in each of the three study groups separately (Table 7 S in supplementary information).

Discussion

This study mapped PA, sedentary time, and sleep during the first year postpartum and found that PA changes in the postpartum period compared to late pregnancy, with participants achieving more daily steps, less minutes of MVPA and more self-reported PA. That participants took more steps postpartum than in late pregnancy, yet had fewer minutes of daily MVPA, may seem contradictory, but this could be because walking does not necessarily elevate heart rate to MVPA levels. Also, participants slept better at one year postpartum compared to late pregnancy based on self-report. In line with the second aim, we found that the prenatal PA interventions did not influence postpartum PA, sedentary time, or sleep markedly measured by either activity tracker or self-report. Our findings emphasize the importance of mapping PA, sedentary time, and sleep across the perinatal period, as these behaviors collectively impact maternal physical and mental health. Although prenatal PA interventions, such as in the FitMum RCT, improved MVPA, sleep quality, and reduced sedentary time during pregnancy, these effects did not persist postpartum. This gap highlights the need for extended strategies beyond pregnancy to support sustained health behaviors and address postpartum risks like weight retention, poor sleep, and mental health challenges. Our study is unique, as no other study has evaluated the longitudinal effects of prenatal PA interventions on postpartum PA levels, sedentary time, and sleep with both continuous objective measurements and questionnaires.

Postpartum patterns of PA, sedentary time, and sleep

A few observational studies have reported patterns of PA, sedentary time, and sleep from pregnancy and throughout the postpartum period, but the findings are inconsistent. The Pelotas Birth Cohort Study investigated changes in leisure-time PA and sitting time from pregnancy to up to 48 months postpartum using structured interview questions about daily lives and sports participation40. The study revealed that 15.7% of women engaged in leisure-time PA during pregnancy, dropping to 7.9% at 12 months postpartum, and the tendencies of less leisure-time PA postpartum is somewhat similar to our findings, i.e., the decrease in MVPA. In the Pelotas Birth Cohort, the average daily sitting time started at 6.4 h during pregnancy and decreased to between 4.2 and 4.4 h during the first 48 months postpartum. This decline in sitting time is inconsistent with our findings of little change in sedentary time between pregnancy and the postpartum period, maybe due to differences in measurement methods. The proportion of women engaging in any leisure-time walking declined from pregnancy to postpartum in the Pelotas Birth Cohort, which is interesting as the number of steps postpartum surpassed pregnancy levels in our study.

The LIFE-Moms study, which explored maternal movement patterns across pregnancy and postpartum, is another comparable study. The study included data from wrist-worn accelerometers to measure PA, sedentary behavior, and sleep for one week during early pregnancy, one week during late pregnancy, and one week at one year postpartum. In the LIFE-Moms study, sedentary time increased from early to late pregnancy and decreased postpartum, and both light PA and MVPA increased postpartum compared to during pregnancy, which contradicts our findings41. This can be due to several factors, including populations differences (overweight/obese in LIFE-Moms vs. healthy inactive in FitMum), the PA interventions during pregnancy in our study, the use of control data only in LIFE-Moms, and differences in measurement tools (snapshot of actigraphy vs. continuous wrist-worn tracking). The study found that sleep duration decreased from early pregnancy (7.9 ± 0.5 h/day) through late pregnancy (7.6 ± 0.5 h/day) to postpartum (7.2 ± 0.1 h/day), which resembles our findings.

Effect of prenatal PA interventions on postpartum PA and sedentary time

Participants in EXE had been offered structured supervised exercise training throughout pregnancy, and although they did not on average attend half of the sessions, they reported spending significantly more time doing sports during pregnancy than the other two study groups28. However, EXE participants did not maintain sports activities at one year postpartum and differences between groups during pregnancy were not sustained. During pregnancy, offering structured supervised exercise training significantly increased MVPA levels26 and reduced sedentary time27 compared to offering standard care to participants in CON. However, EXE participants did not maintain this difference in the postpartum period. On the contrary, EXE walked fewer steps than CON at six months postpartum, which is difficult to explain, but it may reflect that EXE participants focus on structured exercise over daily activity, e.g. walking. The participants in CON originally signed up for a study investigating PA but did not receive an intervention during pregnancy. All participants were free to engage in PA in the postpartum period, and maybe the motivation of the CON participants was reflected in their step counts postpartum, though they did not report more time spent in light or transportation PA than EXE according to the PPAQ. A relatively high motivation among CON participants was indicated by many women wearing their activity trackers in the postpartum period. Throughout the postpartum year, the average compliance rate for wearing the activity tracker was 41% in CON (EXE: 35%, MOT 32%) suggesting generally high motivation to engage in PA.

The GeliS cluster-randomized trial performed face-to-face counseling sessions during pregnancy to encourage pregnant women to eat a healthy diet and increase their PA and analyzed health behavior 6–8 weeks and one year postpartum using PPAQ22. In accordance with our findings, no differences between study groups were found at 6–8 weeks and one year postpartum. Another study examined the impact of a prenatal lifestyle intervention on PA levels in late pregnancy and up to one year postpartum and found that while the intervention significantly increased PA levels at GA 36 weeks, no significant differences between groups were present at one year postpartum23. Therefore, PA interventions during pregnancy can increase the activity level of pregnant women, but increased PA apparently does not carry over to the postpartum period.

Effects of prenatal PA interventions on postpartum sleep

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that exercise interventions postpartum significantly improved sleep quality and reduced daytime fatigue compared to no exercise42. However, we did not identify any studies that implemented lifestyle interventions from early pregnancy until delivery and subsequently conducted follow-up studies to observe the effects of prenatal PA interventions on postpartum sleep. In the FitMum RCT, we found that sleep quality, as measured by the PSQI, was better for participants in the EXE group compared to CON at GA week 28, and both EXE and MOT showed better sleep outcomes than CON by GA week 34. Despite the self-reported improvements in sleep quality, activity tracker data revealed no significant differences between study groups in terms of sleep duration during pregnancy27. In the present study, sleep continued to decline to one year postpartum. On the other hand, participants self-reported improved sleep quality and fewer sleep disturbances at one year postpartum compared to late pregnancy. These findings underscore the importance of distinguishing between sleep quantity and quality. This suggests that while prenatal interventions may benefit perceived sleep during pregnancy, they may not be sufficient to sustain objective improvements postpartum, which are influenced by unique physiological demands, caregiving responsibilities, and psychosocial factors. The mechanisms linking PA and sleep remain not fully understood, but PA may enhance sleep through hormonal, thermoregulatory, and neurological pathways, as well as by regulating circadian rhythms, which together promote relaxation and improve sleep timing and quality43,44. Moderate-intensity, structured PA performed 1–3 times per week has also been shown to improve sleep quality during pregnancy45–47.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, no other randomized controlled study has examined PA levels, sedentary time, and sleep from early pregnancy through delivery to one year postpartum with both objective and subjective methods. A strength of this study is the continuous measurement by the activity tracker from delivery to one year postpartum, which gives a better reflection of health behaviors than shorter measurements at specific timepoints. A limitation of this study is that measurements of steps from a wrist worn accelerometer might not be accurate, as steps are recorded less precisely when keeping the wrist stable, for example, when pushing a stroller. This may have affected the number of steps obtained in all three study groups. This study tracks physical behaviors across major life transitions (pregnancy, postpartum, parenthood), which are accompanied by changes in work status and likely also in sleep. These factors may influence behavior through shifting internal standards (e.g., adapting to less sleep), rather than through intentional behavior change. Furthermore, this study was powered to investigate PA outcomes until GA week 28; thus, later dropout rates could affect the robustness of the statistical models. The inclusion of healthy, inactive, Danish women may limit the generalizability of our findings to more diverse or international populations. Also, the PPAQ was designed for pregnant women, not postpartum, but we wanted to use the same instrument at postpartum for comparability. Future studies are needed to map the timing and type of interventions to support and sustain postpartum maternal health13. Moreover, studies should apply longitudinal designs that track PA, sedentary behavior, and sleep from early pregnancy to the postpartum period. Lastly, we acknowledge the lack of data on postpartum PA barriers as a limitation and recommend addressing this in future research.

Conclusion

This study mapped PA, sedentary time, and sleep during the first postpartum year, and found that daily steps surpassed pregnancy levels while MVPA remained low; postpartum sedentary time was similar to pregnancy levels, whereas sleep time decreased. Self-reported PA and sleep scores improved one year postpartum compared to late pregnancy. The prenatal PA interventions did not influence postpartum PA markedly, as measured by either activity tracker or self-report. However, participants receiving structured supervised exercise training during pregnancy self-reported improved sleep at one year postpartum compared to participants who received motivational counselling on PA during pregnancy. Even if the effects of prenatal PA interventions do not carry over to the postpartum period, there could be health benefits to both mother and child not captured in the present study.
