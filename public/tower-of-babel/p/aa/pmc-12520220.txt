Relationship Between Phenotyping and Individualized Absolute Risk Differences in Sepsis: A Secondary Analysis of Two Approaches in Two Multicenter Trials
Authors: Victor B. Talisa, Sachin P. Yende, Derek C. Angus, Rinaldo Bellomo, Chung-Chou H. Chang, Gregory F. Cooper, David A. Harrison, Alisa Higgins, Jason N. Kennedy, Florian B. Mayr, Paul Mouncey, Sandra L. Peake, Kathy Rowan, Lu Tang, Sofia Triantafyllou, Donald M. Yealy, Christopher W. Seymour, Faraaz Ali Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Sepsis trials likely include patients who vary in response to therapeutic interventions. The optimal approach to identify such differences in treatment response remains unclear. Estimating individualized absolute risk differences (iARDs) to model treatment response at an individual patient level using supervised effect models applied to randomized trial data may be informative. We explored the relationship between two subgrouping approaches and a recently published iARD model for the effect of early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) resuscitation in sepsis.DESIGN:Secondary analysis of the Protocolized Care for Early Septic Shock (ProCESS) and Australasian Resuscitation in Sepsis Evaluation (ARISE) trials. We applied clinical subtypes (α, β, γ, δ) to 829 ProCESS and 1588 ARISE patients and biologic “hyperinflammatory” and “nonhyperinflammatory” subphenotypes to 363 ProCESS patients with biomarker data using established methods. We predicted iARDs with supervised learning using clinical variables as predictors and 90-day mortality as the primary outcome. We evaluated iARD variability within subgroups.SETTING:Eighty-one sites worldwide.PATIENTS/SUBJECTS:Adults with septic shock.INTERVENTIONS:EGDT or usual care.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:The average treatment effect of EGDT appeared to vary within both clinical and biologic subphenotypes. EGDT appeared potentially beneficial in the β and nonhyperinflammatory subphenotypes but harmful in the γ and hyperinflammatory subphenotypes. However, the predicted iARDs within each subgroup ranged from considerable harm to considerable benefit. For example, for the β-subtype, the average mortality reduction from EGDT was 8.5% (95% CI, –0.4 to 17.5), but the iARDs ranged from a 29% increase to a 16% reduction in mortality, with 39% of patients predicted to be harmed.CONCLUSIONS:Although both clinical and biologic phenotyping may identify subgroups whose average treatment effect is beneficial or harmful, individual risks and benefits within subgroups still vary dramatically, raising concern that phenotyping may not reliably or safely personalize sepsis care.

KEY POINTS

Question: Sepsis subphenotypes that differ in prognosis and, potentially, in response to treatment have been recently identified. We used individualized absolute risk differences (iARDs) to investigate whether residual variability in treatment response to early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) exists within previously described sepsis subphenotypes.

Findings: Prior published studies of the Protocolized Care for Early Septic Shock (ProCESS) and Australasian Resuscitation in Sepsis Evaluation (ARISE) randomized clinical trials of EGDT identified heterogeneity of treatment effect in clinical sepsis subtypes (α, β, γ, δ), in biologic subphenotypes (hyperinflammatory, nonhyperinflammatory), and at an individual level using iARDs. In this study, we compared subphenotypes and iARDs and determined that every clinical subtype and biologic subphenotype (regardless of the average treatment effect) included both participants predicted to benefit from EGDT and participants predicted to have been harmed.

Meaning: Treatment response to EGDT varied within previously described sepsis subphenotypes. Treatment strategies targeting treatments based on subphenotype membership alone may still deliver treatment to individuals who will be harmed and withhold treatment from individuals who would benefit.

Treatment response in sepsis is heterogeneous across all patients (1, 2). One approach to address such treatment-response heterogeneity involves grouping septic patients into subtypes or subphenotypes based on shared patterns of measurable characteristics (3, 4). This approach has led to the identification of clinical subtypes (5). Such subtypes are derived using only clinically available data. However, biologic subphenotypes (6–8), which incorporate biomarker data can also be identified. Although these subtypes and subphenotypes have meaningful differences in clinical outcomes and responses to treatment, several challenges remain. First, there is lack of agreement between subtypes and subphenotypes derived using different methods (9, 10). Second, individual patients within subtypes and subphenotypes may still experience variable levels of benefit or harm from a given treatment.

The variability in treatment response within subtypes is well recognized in other fields. For example, a landmark randomized clinical trial (RCT) of carotid endarterectomy was performed in a targeted subtype of patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis and found significant variation in treatment response in this subtype (11). The benefit from endarterectomy was concentrated in patients at highest risk for immediate stroke and lowest risk of surgical complications, while the remaining patients were harmed (12). Thus, by analogy, solely utilizing subtypes in sepsis to guide treatment without understanding further variability may lead to suboptimal or even harmful treatment decisions.

An alternative approach to understand treatment response heterogeneity in septic patients is to estimate individualized treatment effects (ITEs) based on clinical covariate profiles (13). ITEs predict how individual patients respond to treatment and estimate the magnitude of potential benefit or harm, often using novel computational or machine learning models. Accordingly, we sought to test the hypothesis that ITEs would reveal otherwise hidden heterogeneity in treatment response within previously described sepsis subtypes and subphenotypes. In this study, we leveraged data from two RCTs of early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) in sepsis, where we had previously identified clinical subtypes and biologic subphenotypes, and we estimated individualized absolute risk differences (iARDs), a measure that reflects the predicted magnitus of ITEs, to test this hypothesis.

METHODS

Overview

We analyzed data from the Protocolized Care for Early Septic Shock (ProCESS) (14) and Australasian Resuscitation in Sepsis Evaluation (ARISE) (15) RCTs, which tested EGDT resuscitation against usual care and did not find significant overall difference between treatment arms in clinical outcomes. However, clinical subtypes (α, β, γ, δ) were recently identified in ProCESS. Of these, the β subtype predicted the greatest benefit from EGDT and the δ subtype predicted the greatest harm (5). Biologic subphenotypes (hyperinflammatory and nonhyperinflammatory) were also identified in ProCESS and the hyperinflammatory subphenotype predicted harm from EGDT (16). For this study, we built on a prior analysis of iARDs (17), which provides granular estimates of treatment response. We then used iARDs to investigate variability in EGDT effect across clinical subtypes (in ProCESS and ARISE) and biologic subphenotypes (in ProCESS alone). This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board under protocol number STUDY21050180 (Knowledge Network to Individualize Glucocorticoid Hormone Therapy in Sepsis [KNIGHTS]) on June 25, 2021. All analyses were completed in accordance with guidelines in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Trial Characteristics

Details of the ProCESS and ARISE RCTs are previously published (14, 15). Briefly, ProCESS randomized 1341 patients presenting with septic shock from 31 U.S. emergency departments to EGDT, usual care, or a separate protocolized resuscitation arm and did not demonstrate a significant difference in the primary outcome of 60-day in-hospital mortality (21.0% for EGDT vs. 18.9% for usual care). ARISE randomized 1588 patients presenting with septic shock from 51 emergency departments in Australia, New Zealand, Hong Kong, or the Republic of Ireland to EGDT or usual care and similarly did not demonstrate differences in 90-day in-hospital mortality (18.6% for EGDT vs. 18.8% for usual care). ProCESS and ARISE investigators harmonized trial procedures and standardized outcomes prior to trial initiation to improve consistency and facilitate combining results (18). Our analysis cohort combined patients from ProCESS (n = 895) and from ARISE (n = 1588) who were randomized to EGDT or usual care.

Primary Outcome

We selected 90-day mortality as the primary outcome in our study consistent with the primary outcome of the ARISE trial and a meta-analysis of three contemporary EGDT trials (18).

Clinical Subtypes and Biologic Subphenotypes

Each patient in the analysis cohort was assigned to one of the four clinical subtypes (α, β, γ, δ) by previously described methods using 29 variables (eTable 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B563) available to clinicians (5). Biologic subphenotypes (hyperinflammatory or nonhyperinflammatory) were assigned to 363 ProCESS patients with available blood samples in a prior latent class analysis using 20 pre-intervention clinical and biomarker variables as previously described (16) (eTable 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B563). Additional details on subtype and subphenotype classification are in the Supplemental Methods (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B563).

Individualized Treatment Effects

We recently derived and validated a local linear random forest R-learner iARD model to predict response to EGDT using clinical variables for ProCESS and ARISE patients randomized to EGDT or usual care (17). iARDs from our model represent differences between covariate-adjusted risk of mortality with EGDT vs. usual care for an individual patient. Positive iARDs signify a predicted reduction in mortality with EGDT compared with usual care for an individual patient (benefit from EGDT). Negative iARDs signify a predicted increase in mortality with EGDT (harm). Further details of our iARD methods and management of missing data are included in eTable 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B563).

Statistical Analysis

First, we compared patient characteristics and 90-day mortality across each clinical subtype and biologic subphenotype. Second, we estimated absolute risk reductions in 90-day mortality and 95% CIs with EGDT compared with usual care for the four clinical subtypes and the two biologic subphenotypes and tested for interaction with treatment. Third, we assessed variability in treatment responses by estimating the range and distribution of iARDs within each clinical subtype and biologic subphenotype. Fourth, we identified the variables that were the most important for determining clinical subtypes, biologic subphenotypes, and iARDs. Fifth, we determined the importance of clinical and biologic variables for subtype or subphenotype membership by calculating the absolute value of the mean standardized differences of the primary outcome between subtype pairs and taking the average of all such pairwise differences as the final variable importance. Sixth, we calculated variable importance for the iARD model by using global Shapley additive explanation scores, as described previously (17, 19). Finally, we compared the eight most important variables for each method. All statistical analyses were performed using R, Version 4.3.0 (R foundation, Vienna, Austria).

We assigned 28.9% of the combined ProCESS and ARISE cohorts to the α subtype, 14.9% to β, 26.3% to γ, and 30.0% to δ. Distribution of iARDs in Clinical Subtypes and Biologic Subphenotypes

Overall, iARDs in the combined study population ranged from –29.4% (suggesting harm from a 29.4% increase in 90-d mortality from EGDT compared with usual care) to 18.8% (suggesting benefit). ProCESS and ARISE patients with iARDs less than zero (predicted harm from EGDT) had a lower observed mortality with usual care compared with EGDT. Conversely, patients with iARDs greater than zero had a lower observed mortality with EGDT (eFig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B563).

Within each clinical subtype, there was substantial variability in iARDs with some patients predicted to be harmed by EGDT (iARDs < 0) and some patients predicted to benefit from EGDT (iARDs > 0) (Fig. 1). Within clinical subtypes, the β subtype had the highest average benefit from EGDT compared with usual care (subtype absolute risk reduction of 8.5% [95% CI, –0.4% to 17.5%]). However, more than one-third of patients (38.9%) in this subtype were predicted to be harmed from EGDT based on iARDs, which ranged from –29.4% to +15.6%. The γ subtype had the highest average harm from EGDT, with an average absolute risk reduction of –3.5% (95% CI, –10.2% to 3.1%). However, 58% of γ subtype patients were predicted to benefit from EGDT, and iARDs ranged from as low as –22.7% to as high as +15.6%.

Within biologic subphenotypes, iARDs ranged from –18% to 12% in the hyperinflammatory subphenotype and –25% to 11% in the nonhyperinflammatory subphenotype (Fig. 2). In patients with the hyperinflammatory subphenotype, there was an association with harm with EGDT with an absolute risk reduction of –11.5% (95% CI, –50.0% to 26.9%), but two thirds of patients (61.5%) were predicted to benefit compared with usual care (range of iARDs were –18.3% to +7.8%). For patients with the nonhyperinflammatory subphenotype, EGDT was associated with a smaller but positive effect with an absolute risk reduction of 4.2% (95% CI, –6.1% to 14.5%). However, the iARDs model showed that half were predicted to benefit, and the other half were predicted to be harmed by EGDT (55.2% and 44.8% had iARDs greater and < 0, respectively).

Variable Importance Analysis

Less than half of the variables that were most important for determining clinical subtype or biologic subphenotype membership overlapped with those important for iARD determination (Table 3). For example, neither creatinine nor bilirubin—the most important variables for determining clinical subtype and biologic subphenotype membership, respectively—were among the top eight most important variables for iARD prediction. Albumin was the most important variable for determining iARDs but was only third most important for clinical subtype membership and seventh for biologic subphenotype membership.

DISCUSSION

Key Findings

In this secondary analysis of two trials of EGDT in sepsis, we demonstrate that predictions of individual responses to treatment can vary widely, ranging from benefit to harm, within clinical subtypes and biologic subphenotypes regardless of the average treatment effect of the particular subtype or subphenotype. Additionally, the variables that predicted subtype or subphenotype membership and those identified as important for treatment response by iARDs had limited overlap.

Relationship to Previous Studies

Three multicenter RCTs did not find a significant difference between EGDT and usual care in reducing mortality, leading many to hypothesize that EGDT did not provide benefit (14, 15, 18, 20). Recent studies using subtypes and subphenotypes suggested a more nuanced relationship whereby the trials included both subgroups of patients who benefitted from EGDT and subgroups who were harmed (5, 16). Although targeting EGDT based on clinical subtypes or biologic subphenotypes would have been an advancement for precision medicine, our findings suggest that this approach may still misallocate treatment—administering EGDT to patients who will be harmed and withholding it from those who benefit. To our knowledge, our study is the first to assess variability of predictions of individual responses to treatment within subtypes and subphenotypes in critical illness. However, our findings align with research in other fields demonstrating that individuals can experience both benefit and harm within groups previously identified as responders (12) and that subgroup level heterogeneity of treatment effect alone should not be used to guide individual treatment decisions (21).

Implications of Study Findings

Investigation of ITE models, such as our iARD model, is increasingly common in medicine (22–26) and has the potential to further advance personalization of care (13). ITE models directly estimate differences in outcome across treatment modalities and provide predictions of the direction and magnitude of treatment response for individual patients with insight into the clinical or biologic variables driving treatment response. Recent critical care studies using ITE models have identified heterogeneous treatment effects for glucocorticoids in septic shock (27), anticoagulation strategies in COVID-19 (28), fluid infusion rates (29), and oxygenation targets in critical illness (30), similar to our findings for EGDT, but require further validation.

Thus, the best approach for delivering precision medicine in critical care remains an open problem, with both subtype- and ITE-based strategies having pros and cons. Subtype-based approaches have been supported by multiple studies in sepsis and ARDS (3, 13). Subtypes have shown favorable responses to multiple therapies (5, 22–25) and allow for easy assignment of group membership through electronic health records or rapid diagnostics. However, recent research suggests poor overlap between subtypes and subphenotypes identified by different methods (9). In this regard, our results showing variability in response to treatment within subtypes and subphenotypes highlight the concerns of relying solely on this approach. ITE-based approaches may help individualize delivery of care, but are intervention-specific, require large sample sizes, and are susceptible to overfitting bias (13, 29–31). Precision medicine strategies in critical care may ultimately involve a combination of subtype- and ITE-based approaches to target therapeutics to individuals most likely to benefit and away from individuals who will likely experience harm.

Study Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of our study include the use of clinical sepsis subtypes and biologic subphenotypes that have been validated across multiple cohorts and of an iARD model that has been validated across two independent interventional trials. An important limitation is that while our study shows that iARDs vary widely within subtypes and subphenotypes with regards to EGDT in sepsis, such variability may be intervention-specific, and iARDs for other interventions may not vary significantly within subtypes. Additionally, protein biomarker data were only available for a subset of ProCESS participants and were not available for any ARISE participants limiting our analyses of biologic subphenotypes. Notably, no sepsis study to date has prospectively validated a precision medicine approach based on either subtypes or subphenotypes or using iARDs.

CONCLUSIONS

Predictions of individual treatment responses to EGDT varied between large harm and large benefit within previously derived clinical subtypes and biologic subphenotypes. While developing targeted treatment strategies based on subtypes or subphenotypes may be sufficient for some therapies, incorporating ITE-based approaches may be necessary to personalize other treatments.
