Comparative outcomes and clinic utilization of traditional Chinese medicine and Western medicine in integrated health care in Taiwan
Authors: Shao-Chang Ho, Chi-Jeng Hsieh, Lun-Chien Lo, Jaung-Geng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimTaiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) implemented the Integrated Health Care (IHC) program in 2016, with Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) incorporated into the program in 2019 as a complementary home-based care model. This study aimed to evaluate the utilization patterns and clinical outcomes of TCM-based integrated health care (IHC-TCM) compared with Western Medicine-based integrated health care (IHC-WM).Experimental procedureA retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) from 2019 to 2021. Patients receiving IHC-TCM were compared with those receiving IHC-WM. Propensity score matching (1:1) was applied to balance baseline characteristics. Logistic regression models were used to estimate mortality outcomes.Results and conclusionMortality rates at 90-, 180-, and 365-day were 2.7% vs. 0.8%, 5.0% vs. 3.5%, and 7.3% vs. 6.4% for IHC-TCM and IHC-WM, respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed at 90-, 180-, and 365-day follow-ups. However, a non-significant trend toward higher 90-day mortality was observed in the IHC-TCM group (p = 0.057). In terms of healthcare utilization, IHC-TCM was associated with fewer WM outpatient department (OPD) visits, increased TCM utilization, and different ER utilization patterns. IHC-TCM demonstrated comparable long-term mortality outcomes to IHC-WM, although a potential early-phase mortality difference cannot be excluded. Distinct healthcare utilization patterns suggest a substitution effect between TCM and WM services.Keywords: Integrated health care (IHC), Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), Outpatient department (OPD), Mortality

Introduction

Taiwan officially became an aging society in 2018, with more than 14.5% of the population aged 65 years or older.1,2 This demographic transition has substantially increased the demand for long-term and home-based healthcare services, particularly for disabled and homebound individuals.3 According to the WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy (2014–2023), published in 2013, the current health system is facing an increase in the number of patients with chronic diseases and rising medical costs.4 In response, Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) implemented the Integrated Health Care (IHC) program in 2016 to improve care coordination and reduce fragmentation in healthcare delivery.

Home health care (HHC) enables dependent elderly individuals to remain in their homes while receiving necessary medical care5, 6, 7. As Taiwan transitions toward a super-aged society, HHC, community-based care, and long-term care have become critical components of the healthcare system8, 9, 10, 11, 12. Unlike traditional clinic-based care, HHC emphasizes patient-centered, partnership-based care delivered within the patient's living environment, fostering improved communication and trust between healthcare providers and patients.

Under Taiwan's NHI system, HHC services include home visits, diagnosis and treatment, medical supplies, and nursing support such as catheter and ostomy care. To enhance the efficiency and integration of these services, the IHC program was introduced to promote multidisciplinary team-based care and improve patient outcomes. Multidisciplinary approaches have also been shown to improve quality of life and clinical outcomes among patients with chronic conditions13, 14, 15, 16. Previous studies have demonstrated that IHC is associated with reduced mortality and improved healthcare utilization compared with conventional home health care.17

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) was incorporated into the IHC model in 2019, allowing patients to receive home-based acupuncture and herbal medicine treatments. This integration represents a unique model within Taiwan's dual healthcare system. In addition, the WHO has emphasized the role of traditional medicine in addressing the growing burden of chronic diseases and rising healthcare costs.4

Although IHC based on Western Medicine (WM) has been widely implemented and its effectiveness well documented, evidence regarding IHC-TCM remains limited. In particular, it is unclear whether IHC-TCM provides comparable clinical outcomes and healthcare utilization patterns to IHC-WM in real-world settings.

Therefore, this study aimed to compare mortality outcomes at 90-, 180-, and 365-day, as well as healthcare utilization patterns—including outpatient and ER visits—between patients receiving IHC-TCM and those receiving IHC-WM. These findings may provide important real-world evidence on the role of TCM in integrated home-based care systems.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data for this study were obtained from the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) via the Applied Health Research Data Integration Service of the National Health Insurance Administration (NHIA), Taiwan. The NHIRD contains de-identified and encrypted medical claims data covering nearly the entire Taiwanese population, ensuring comprehensive representation and minimizing selection bias.

Study design and population

The IHC pilot program was initiated in 2016 for WM clinics and expanded to TCM clinics in 2019. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using NHIRD claims from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2021.

A total of 71,373 patients who received IHC services during the study period were initially identified. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) missing information on sex, birth year, or comorbidities, or follow-up duration <90 days; (2) duplicate records within the study period (only the first occurrence was retained); and (3) receipt of WM-based IHC within 180 days prior to the index date to ensure inclusion of new cases. After applying these criteria, 14,331 patients were included in the final analysis.

Group classification

Patients who exclusively received IHC-TCM were assigned to the IHC-TCM group, whereas those who exclusively received IHC-WM were assigned to the IHC-WM group. Individuals who received both TCM and WM during the same episode were excluded to avoid treatment overlap. The index date was defined as the first recorded date of IHC service utilization. Flowchart of patient selection and propensity score matching.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 90, 180, and 365 days after the index date. Mortality was determined using validated NHIRD criteria: (1) inpatient discharge status recorded as death; (2) termination of catastrophic illness certification due to death; or (3) withdrawal from the NHI program without re-enrollment. Patients were followed from the index date until the earliest of death, loss to follow-up, or 365 days. Individuals without a recorded death were censored at 365 days or their last known claim date, whichever came first.

Secondary outcomes included healthcare utilization, defined as the number of ER visits and outpatient department (OPD) visits (including WM, dental, and TCM OPD visits) within 90-, 180-, and 365-day follow-ups.

TCM interventions

In the IHC-TCM group, interventions were delivered by licensed TCM physicians during scheduled home visits. Treatment plans were individualized based on the patient's clinical conditions and included Chinese herbal medicine, acupuncture, or both. More than 90% of interventions involved acupuncture, commonly used for chronic pain, post-stroke rehabilitation, or cognitive decline. Treatment frequency and intensity were determined at the physicians' discretion and were subject to policy and workforce constraints in the home-care setting.

Covariates and comorbidities

Demographic and clinical variables included sex, age, and baseline comorbidities identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes. Key comorbid conditions included diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), dementia, and Parkinsonism.

Disease severity was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which incorporates 17 disease categories with weighted scores, providing an overall measure of comorbidity burden.18,19

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. To improve comparability between groups and reduce potential confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) with 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed. The propensity score was estimated using a multivariate logistic regression model, incorporating demographic variables and baseline comorbidities as covariates.

Covariate balance between groups was assessed using statistical tests, including chi-square tests for categorical variables. Adequate balance was defined as the absence of statistically significant differences after matching.

Logistic regression models were subsequently applied to estimate the association between treatment group (IHC-TCM vs. IHC-WM) and mortality outcomes at fixed follow-up time points (90-, 180-, and 365-day). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Given the observational nature of the study, no causal inference was assumed, and findings were interpreted as associations.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China Medical University Hospital, Taichung, Taiwan.

Results

Study population

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 14,331 patients were eligible for analysis. Following 1:1 PSM based on age, sex, comorbidities, and index year, 962 patients (481 in each group) were included in the final matched cohort.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the study population before and after PSM are presented in Table 1, Table 2. In the unmatched cohort, significant differences were observed between the IHC-TCM and IHC-WM groups in both demographic characteristics and multiple comorbidities. Specifically, age and sex distributions differed significantly between groups, and several comorbid conditions—including cancer, hemorrhagic stroke, ischemic stroke, dementia, Parkinsonism, Alzheimer's disease, spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were more prevalent in the IHC-TCM group before PSM, whereas diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease were more prevalent in the IHC-WM group. In addition, CCI scores also differed significantly, indicating substantial baseline imbalance.

After PSM, these differences were no longer statistically significant, suggesting that the matching procedure achieved good balance between the two groups. Demographic characteristics, including age and sex, became comparable, and all measured comorbidities and CCI scores were comparable between groups, indicating adequate baseline comparability between the IHC-TCM and IHC-WM groups.

In the matched cohort, the majority of patients were older adults, with individuals aged ≥60 years accounting for 80.1% of the IHC-TCM group. Patients aged 80–89 years constituted the largest subgroup in both groups (30.1% in IHC-TCM and 30.4% in IHC-WM), reflecting the predominantly elderly population receiving integrated home care.

Mortality outcomes

As shown in Table 3, the 90-day mortality rate was higher in the IHC-TCM group than in the IHC-WM group (2.7% vs. 0.8%), with a borderline statistical significance (OR: 3.42, 95% CI: 0.97–12.17, p = 0.057). However, this difference diminished over time. At 180-day, the mortality rates were 5.0% and 3.5%, respectively (OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 0.63–2.46, p = 0.522), and at 365-day, the rates were comparable between the two groups (7.3% vs. 6.4%; OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.60–1.75, p = 0.930), indicating comparable long-term mortality between IHC-TCM and IHC-WM users.

Healthcare utilization

Healthcare utilization patterns, including ER visits and OPD visits, were compared between the IHC-TCM and IHC-WM groups. At the 365-day follow-up, the mean number of visits was as follows: ER visits, 1.10 (IHC-TCM) vs. 0.79 (IHC-WM); WM outpatient visits, 28.67 vs. 31.24; dental outpatient visits, 0.79 vs. 0.66; and TCM outpatient visits, 13.98 vs. 2.11. Compared with the IHC-WM group, the IHC-TCM group showed fewer WM outpatient visits, increased TCM outpatient visits, and slightly higher ER visit rates (Fig. 2). Detailed numerical data, including mean visit frequencies and corresponding statistical comparisons, are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Overall, the IHC-TCM group demonstrated a distinct pattern of healthcare utilization compared with the IHC-WM group. In particular, patients in the IHC-TCM group tended to have fewer WM outpatient visits across follow-up periods, while exhibiting higher utilization of TCM outpatient services. ER visits were slightly higher in the IHC-TCM group at the 180- and 365-day follow-ups; however, the absolute differences were small.

At 90-day, the incidence of upper respiratory infection (URI) was significantly higher in the IHC-TCM group (8.3% vs. 4.0%, p = 0.005), while urinary tract infection (UTI) did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.061).

At 180-day, both UTI (17.0% vs. 11.2%, p = 0.010) and URI (13.5% vs. 6.2%, p < 0.001) were significantly more frequently observed in the IHC-TCM group. Pressure ulcer (PU) also demonstrated a higher occurrence (4.0% vs. 1.7%, p = 0.032).

At 365-day, the differences remained significant for UTI (26.6% vs. 18.7%, p = 0.003) and URI (21.2% vs. 10.8%, p < 0.001). Other conditions did not show statistically significant differences between groups.

Discussion

Principal findings

The global trend in aging societies has shifted healthcare delivery toward value-based, patient-centered, and home-based care models. Home health care has been shown to provide significant benefits across a wide range of diseases20, 21, 22 and has become a cornerstone of healthcare systems in aging populations. Within this context, TCM home care can be conceptualized as an extension of outpatient TCM services into the home setting.

In Taiwan, nearly 90% of TCM physicians practice in primary care clinics, positioning TCM as a potentially accessible and community-based healthcare resource. While IHC-WM has been widely implemented and demonstrated effectiveness in reducing mortality, infection rates, and healthcare expenditures, IHC-TCM remains in an early developmental stage. Since its official launch in 2019, IHC-TCM utilization has increased substantially, although its implementation scale remains considerably smaller than that of IHC-WM.23 Previous studies have shown that integrated home care models are associated with reduced mortality and improved healthcare utilization compared with conventional home health care.17

The present study provides real-world evidence on the utilization and clinical outcomes of TCM within Taiwan's IHC system. Our findings indicate that IHC-TCM yields comparable long-term mortality outcomes to IHC-WM in a home-based care setting. These findings support the potential role of IHC-TCM as a complementary and sustainable care model in aging societies.

Mortality outcomes

In this study, a higher mortality rate was observed in the IHC-TCM group at 90 days, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. This finding may reflect differences in baseline disease severity or patient selection, as patients receiving IHC-TCM might represent a more complex or advanced clinical population in real-world practice.

Further investigation is warranted to determine whether the observed early mortality trend is attributable to clinical differences, patient characteristics, or system-level factors. One possible explanation relates to the relatively limited number of home-based care cases managed by TCM compared with WM, which may reflect differences in implementation scale and accumulated clinical experience.

In addition, TCM practitioners may have had relatively less exposure to the modern IHC framework during its early implementation phase, particularly in managing acute conditions and infection-related complications among homebound patients with complex care needs. This may partially reflect structural differences in training systems, as formal subspecialty training and standardized clinical pathways for home-based care are less established in TCM compared with WM.

From a policy perspective, these findings underscore the importance of strengthening workforce development in TCM. This includes the development of more structured training programs, greater integration with multidisciplinary care models, and the establishment of clinical competencies relevant to home-based and complex care. These observations may also reflect broader workforce challenges in TCM, including limited availability of physicians with specialized training in home-based and complex care.

Importantly, the difference in mortality diminished over time, and no statistically significant differences were observed at 90-, 180-, and 365-day follow-ups. These findings suggest that IHC-TCM was not associated with a statistically significant increase in long-term mortality compared with IHC-WM, supporting its potential role in home healthcare settings.

These findings are consistent with the concept that TCM may play a supportive role in long-term care, particularly in symptom control, functional maintenance, and holistic patient management. Several factors may explain this observation. First, IHC-TCM remains in an early stage of implementation, and providers may have had limited experience managing complex or acute conditions in home settings. Second, selection bias may exist, with patients receiving TCM potentially having different clinical characteristics, disease severity, or care preferences. Third, treatment intensity—particularly acupuncture frequency—may have been constrained by workforce and policy limitations.

Patient characteristics and confounding

The higher incidence of infection-related conditions and pressure ulcers observed in the IHC-TCM group is likely multifactorial and should not be interpreted as a direct consequence of TCM interventions. These differences may reflect variations in baseline functional status, disease severity, and referral patterns.

Patients receiving IHC-TCM may represent a distinct clinical subgroup, potentially characterized by greater disability, prolonged immobility, or chronic neurological conditions. Such factors are known to increase the risk of infections and pressure ulcers and may not be fully captured in claims-based datasets.

Infection outcomes

The higher occurrence of UTI, URI, and PU observed in the IHC-TCM group should be interpreted with caution. Rather than indicating inferior treatment effects, these findings are more likely to reflect differences in patient characteristics and care patterns.

Patients receiving IHC-TCM may represent a subgroup with greater clinical complexity, higher frailty, or more advanced chronic conditions, which inherently increase the risk of infections and related complications. In home-care settings, infection risk is strongly influenced by factors such as hygiene practices, catheter use, feeding tube management, and the level of caregiver support, all of which may vary across patient populations.

In addition, more frequent or closer clinical contact in the IHC-TCM model may contribute to higher detection rates of medical conditions, leading to an apparent increase in recorded incidence. The consistent pattern observed across multiple follow-up periods further suggests that these differences are unlikely to be incidental, but rather reflect underlying care needs and surveillance intensity.

These findings highlight the importance of strengthening infection prevention strategies in TCM-based home care, including routine screening, PU risk assessment, and the integration of multidisciplinary care. Therefore, the results of Table 4 should not be interpreted as adverse outcomes of IHC-TCM, but rather as indicators of a population with higher healthcare demand and more complex care needs.

Healthcare utilization

In terms of healthcare utilization, IHC-TCM was associated with fewer WM-OPD visits, increased TCM-OPD utilization, and distinct ER utilization patterns, suggesting a potential substitution effect rather than duplication of services. This pattern may reflect continuity of care within the TCM system, patient preferences, or differences in clinical practice models. However, partial overlap between TCM and WM services cannot be excluded, particularly within Taiwan's dual healthcare system. Further research is needed to clarify the drivers of these utilization patterns.

Role of acupuncture

A defining feature of IHC-TCM is the predominance of acupuncture, which was administered in more than 90% of home visits. Acupuncture is commonly used for pain management, neurological rehabilitation, and cognitive support in patients with chronic conditions. Previous studies have demonstrated its potential benefits in improving cognitive function, reducing stroke recurrence, and modulating immune response24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29.

However, optimal treatment frequency (typically two to three times per week)30,31 is often not feasible in the current IHC-TCM model due to workforce and policy constraints, which may influence treatment effectiveness.

Healthcare system perspective

From a healthcare system perspective, the integration of TCM into home-based care represents a unique model within Taiwan's dual healthcare system. Understanding how TCM contributes to patient outcomes and healthcare utilization may provide valuable insights for optimizing resource allocation, improving care coordination, and enhancing healthcare delivery in aging societies.

Study implications and future research

This study provides important real-world evidence but also highlights several areas for further investigation. Future studies should include larger multi-center cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and more comprehensive clinical variables. Prospective designs and subgroup analyses may help identify patient populations that benefit most from IHC-TCM.

In addition to mortality, future research should incorporate broader outcome measures, including quality of life, functional status, caregiver burden, and healthcare expenditures, to better evaluate the overall impact of TCM in home-based care.

Overall, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of a developing care model. While IHC-TCM demonstrated comparable long-term mortality outcomes to IHC-WM, observed differences in early outcomes and clinical events likely reflect variations in patient characteristics, care complexity, and system-level factors rather than direct treatment effects. As the IHC-TCM program continues to evolve, further research and system-level improvements will be essential to fully elucidate and optimize its role in integrated home-based care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that IHC-TCM yields comparable long-term mortality outcomes to IHC-WM in a home-based care setting. Although no statistically significant differences were observed at 90-, 180-, and 365-day follow-ups, a potential early-phase trend toward higher mortality in the IHC-TCM group was identified and should be interpreted with caution.

IHC-TCM was associated with distinct healthcare utilization patterns, characterized by reduced WM outpatient visits and increased use of TCM services. A higher incidence of infection-related conditions was also observed, which may reflect differences in patient characteristics and care contexts rather than direct treatment effects.

These findings highlight the complexity of integrating TCM into home-based care and underscore the need for further large-scale, prospective studies to clarify its clinical role and optimize its implementation in aging healthcare systems.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective cohort study based on administrative claims data from the NHIRD, the findings are subject to potential residual confounding. Detailed clinical information, such as functional status, disease severity, lifestyle factors, and socioeconomic variables, was not available in the database and therefore could not be adjusted for in the analysis.

Second, the NHIRD does not provide information on certain clinical outcomes, such as the etiology or place of death, which may limit the interpretation of mortality-related findings. In addition, information regarding caregiver characteristics and family support, which may influence outcomes in home-based care settings, was not accessible.

Third, treatment intensity, adherence, and variability in TCM interventions could not be fully assessed. Differences in physician practice patterns and resource availability may have influenced the observed outcomes.

Fourth, IHC-TCM remains in an early stage of implementation, and the relatively small sample size of patients receiving IHC-TCM may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Finally, due to the observational nature of this study, causal relationships cannot be established, and the results should be interpreted as associations rather than direct effects.
