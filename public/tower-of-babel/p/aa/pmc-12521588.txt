Enhanced programming efficiency in vertical NAND flash using self-boosting hot carrier injection
Authors: Moonchurl Kim, Jiwook Hong, Choasub Kim, Soo Jin Kim, Sungyung Hwang, Seung Jae Baik, Jongwook Jeon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Fowler–Nordheim (FN) tunneling scheme is constrained by its high operating voltage requirement and poor energy efficiency, posing significant technical challenges. In response, Hot Carrier Injection (HCI) - based programming methods have been explored as promising alternatives for achieving efficient programming at reduced voltages. This study investigates three fundamental issues inherent in the conventional Self–boosting channel–based HCI approach : program disturbance occurring in cells adjacent to the drain select line (DSL), non-uniform charge injection into the Charge Trap Layer (CTL), and programming efficiency (PGM efficiency) variations depending on cell location. To address these challenges, we propose a novel Bi-lateral HCI programming (HCI-BiPGM) scheme that induces hot carriers from both directions. By symmetrically shaping the lateral electric field around a centrally positioned floating channel region, the proposed scheme ensures uniform charge injection and consistent PGM efficiency irrespective of cell location. Technology Computer-Aided Design (TCAD) Simulation results confirm that the proposed HCI approach achieves approximately 60% higher PGM efficiency compared to FN tunneling methods. Furthermore, it exhibits improved voltage balancing characteristics, highlighting its potential as an advanced low-power NAND flash programming technology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19858-y.Keywords: NAND flash, FN tunneling, Hot-carrier injection, Program, Self-boosting channelSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In the modern information society, NAND flash memory has emerged as a key storage device owing to its non-volatile nature, high-speed operation, high integration density, and low power consumption. Specifically, stacking hundreds of layers in 3D V-NAND and the recent migration from TLC to QLC operation have boosted density and sharply increased per-bit energy demand, because tighter threshold-voltage windows require longer program-verify loops and higher cumulative charge1–3. As a result, the 3D V-NAND architecture has significantly contributed to establishing NAND flash as a core technology in modern data storage4,5. Nevertheless, recent studies have shown that as integration continues to scale, device reliability decreases, and various approaches to mitigate these issues have been explored6,7. At the same time, the power and voltage limitations of conventional programming schemes have become more evident, motivating the emergence of new programming methods8,9. Despite these advancements, NAND flash memory still predominantly employs FN tunneling as its primary programming mechanism10. Nonetheless, this approach has consistently raised concerns related to reliability degradation and increased fabrication complexity due to the high operating voltages required11,12. These challenges have become even more critical with the rising demand for low-power operation in applications such as mobile devices and data centers, underscoring the growing necessity for research into low-voltage, energy-efficient NAND flash programming technologies13.

From this viewpoint, one notable technique is the HCI method. In recent studies, analyses and modeling based on the physical characteristics of HCI have been actively conducted, leading to a deeper understanding and broader applications of HCI physics14,15. By employing this approach, unlike the conventional FN tunneling mechanism that requires high gate voltages, HCI directly exploits the kinetic energy of electrons accelerated by the lateral electric field within the channel, enabling efficient programming even at low gate voltages. Although previous research on Uni-lateral HCI Programming Method (HCI-UniPGM) has been conducted16,17most of these studies have primarily focused on analyzing unintended negative effects associated with HCI, such as the program disturb phenomenon incidentally caused by HCI during FN programming18,19or the read disturb phenomenon induced by HCI during repetitive read operations20–22. In contrast to these earlier works, this study focuses not on these unintended adverse impacts but instead on intentionally exploiting HCI as an advanced programming method.

Specifically, we identify critical limitations overlooked in previous HCI-UniPGM methods and propose a novel HCI-BiPGM scheme overcome these issues. We propose a HCI-BiPGM scheme that maintains the core concept of self-boosting channel while utilizing switch wordline (SWL) on both sides of the program wordline (PWL) to establish symmetrical electric field conditions.

In this approach, the floating region is formed beneath the PWL, ensuring uniform channel potential regardless of cell position. This programming method facilitates uniform HCI through Bi-directional Band-to-Band tunneling (BTBT), effectively mitigating issues such as program disturbance in cells adjacent to the DSL, non-uniform HCI, and variations in programming performance based on cell location. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed HCI-BiPGM scheme achieves faster programming speed compared to the FN tunneling method, while also exhibiting comparable performance to the HCI-UniPGM approach. Importantly, HCI-BiPGM outperforms in terms of charge distribution uniformity, location independence of programming performance, and suppression of interference in adjacent cells. These findings address previously overlooked limitations of self-boosting channel HCI programming method, suggesting that the HCI-BiPGM method holds promise as a next-generation low-power, high-reliability programming technology for NAND flash memory.

Simulation results and discussion

Uni-lateral HCI programming method

Figure 2 illustrates the operating principle and bias sequence of self-boosting channel HCI UniPGM scheme20. Figure 2a shows the NAND string structure and the corresponding energy band transitions during the setup and PGM phases. The string is separated into grounded and floating regions by the SWL. In the setup phase, the grounded region remains at 0 V, while the floating region potential increases due to the floating wordline (FWL) bias, forming a lateral electric field near the SWL. This field causes band bending that enables BTBT, generating hot electrons. As the operation enters the PGM phase, these electrons move into the floating region and induce HCI. Impact ionization takes place at the transition from setup to PGM, when the SWL is turned on. At this point, the previously isolated grounded and floating regions are connected, and carriers traveling along the new conduction path are accelerated and undergo collisions in the silicon lattice, leading to impact ionization. Figure 2a highlights these mechanisms with BTBT and HCI (red arrows) and impact ionization regions (blue dashed lines).

(a) Energy band diagram and lateral field-driven HCI mechanism in HCI-UiPGM, (b) Voltage timing diagram for setup and PGM phases.

Figure 2b shows the time-domain voltage sequence applied to the SWL, FWL, ground wordline (GWL), PWL, SSL, and DSL. HCI occurs mainly between T₂ and T₃, after which FN tunneling becomes the dominant injection mechanism23.

This programming method leverages HCI to precondition the channel or supply additional energy prior to FN tunneling, thereby significantly enhancing overall PGM efficiency. Figure 3a illustrates the variations in channel potential beneath the SWL as a function of VGWL, while Fig. 3b shows that a lower VGWL facilitates the formation of a stronger electric field beneath the SWL, resulting in a larger lateral potential difference in the channel. These results underscore the importance of optimizing the GWL bias for effective boosting. Figure 4a shows the influence of the FWL voltage on the SWL. As VFWL increases, the coupling effect in the floating channel region raises the channel potential. However, when examining the channel potential directly beneath the spacer region between the SWL and PWL, it is observed that once VFWL​ exceeds approximately 9 V, the potential saturates and no longer enhances the boosting effect. As a result, once VFWL exceeds 9 V, the channel potential may even decrease, ultimately reducing the efficiency of HCI. Unlike the GWL, the FWL is not located directly adjacent to the SWL but is separated by the PWL. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 4b, when VFWL exceeds 9 V, it can reduce the magnitude of the electric field at the SWL through the intervening PWL, thereby disrupting the SWL intended function. This indicates that due to cell-to-cell interference, the fringe field can become distorted around the SWL, directly impacting the critical lateral potential difference formation pathway. Consequently, both VFWL and VGWL serve as key parameters that must be carefully optimized during the setup phase to ensure efficient programming. However, the HCI-UniPGM method inherently involves several technical limitations. The first issue is the variation in PGM efficiency depending on the cell location within the NAND string. Figure 5a presents Simulation results of the charge trapping profiles in the CTL for PWL located at the 4th, 15th, and 25th positions, highlighting the spatial dependence of trapped electron density. As shown in Fig. 5b, Cells located in the middle of the string (15th WL) and those at the edges (4th WL and 25th WL) exhibit differences in channel boosting magnitude, with programming at the 4th WL involving more wordline segments boost the channel, thereby raising its potential and consequently enhancing BTBT, impact ionization, and PGM efficiency. Conversely, programming at the 25th WL involves fewer wordlines, resulting in lower channel potential and reduced programming efficiency. Thus, the cell’s position within the string significantly affects the lateral electric field strength and duration of elevated channel potential, leading to variations in programming efficiency.

Bi-lateral HCI programming and inhibit method

In this study, we propose the HCI-BiPGM scheme to overcome several limitations inherent in the conventional HCI-UniPGM approach. As shown in Fig. 8, the key concept of the proposed method is to simultaneously induce HCI from both ends of the program cell’s channel, ensuring uniform programming performance. Similar to HCI-UniPGM, HCI-BiPGM operates in the setup and the PGM phases. However, the distinguishing feature is the placement of two SWLs symmetrically on both sides of the PWL, confining the floating region strictly to the program cell area. Figure 9a schematically depicts the operational principle of the proposed HCI-BiPGM scheme. In the setup phase, both SWLs remain in the off state, electrically isolating the PWL channel region completely from external potentials. Consequently, only the channel region beneath the PWL is floating, while the channels beneath GWLs outside the program cell remain grounded through connections to the BL and SL. This arrangement establishes a clear and symmetric potential difference between the floating and grounded regions. In the PGM phase, both SWLs turn on simultaneously, forming a conduction path between the previously isolated floating region and the grounded regions. The resulting strong lateral electric field accelerates electrons from the grounded regions, leading to impact ionization and the generation of additional electron-hole pairs. The high-energy electrons generated by this process subsequently drift back toward the floating region, where they are injected into the CTL. Meanwhile, the SSL switches to the off state and as a result of the ground potential applied to the BL, the entire channel is pulled down to ground region. Programming

Voltage

The FN method operates within a relatively higher voltage range (12 V ~ 21 V) and exclusively utilizes FN tunneling for program operations. In contrast, HCI-based programming methods leverage both FN tunneling and HCI mechanisms, enabling programming at a reduced voltage range (12 V ~ 18.5 V) and thus delivering enhanced PGM efficiency compared to the FN method. Specifically, the HCI-UniPGM scheme demonstrates the highest PGM efficiency (168.3%) but is susceptible to issues such as program disturbance and asymmetric programming. The HCI-BiPGM method partially mitigates these issues but shows a slightly lower efficiency (165.7% vs. 168.3%) than HCI-UniPGM. Additionally, both HCI-based methods require an additional setup phase distinct from FN programming.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a novel HCI-BiPGM method to Simultaneously address the high operational voltage and reliability limitations associated with conventional FN tunneling, as well as various critical drawbacks inherent in the existing HCI-UniPGM approach. TCAD Simulations confirm that the proposed HCI methods achieve approximately 60% higher PGM efficiency compared to FN tunneling, and the Similar efficiency observed between HCI-BiPGM and HCI-UniPGM arises because HCI-BiPGM has a smaller floating potential but this is offset by dual side HCI. More importantly, the HCI-BiPGM method effectively resolves the key drawbacks of the HCI-UniPGM approach, such as program disturb near the DSL, non-uniform charge injection, and PGM efficiency variation at different WLs, thereby greatly enhancing spatial uniformity and device reliability. Consequently, the proposed HCI-BiPGM scheme represents a promising solution for next-generation low-power 3D NAND flash memory technology, delivering superior PGM efficiency, spatial uniformity, and device reliability.

Methods

To realistically capture charge transport and trapping phenomena in the channel, a single grain boundary (GB) is assumed to exist at the center of each WL segment. The GB acts as both a carrier scattering site and a trap site. Therefore, exponentially distributed grain-boundary-trap (GBT) states and uniform interface-trap densities at the channel/tunnel-oxide and channel/inner-oxide interfaces are included. The average grain size is set to 44 nm, and a constant mobility model with µ = 10 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ is adopted25. The bulk trap density in the charge-trap nitride layer was set to 5 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, consistent with widely adopted values in 3D NAND literature17. In addition, acceptor-like and donor-like states were introduced in the energy gap at the interface between the poly-Si channel and tunnel oxide, as well as between the poly-Si channel and filler oxide where a uniform distribution and a density of 2 × 1012 cm−2eV−1 were used26. As depicted in Fig. 16a, the I − V characteristics obtained from VNAND flash string simulations exhibit excellent agreement with the experimental measurements. The BTBT current model was calibrated using the electric field-dependent tunneling current density extracted from a reverse-biased PN junction, and the calibration results corresponding to this process are shown in Fig. 16b, ensuring accurate reproduction of field-driven BTBT behavior27.

To analyze self-boosting channel based HCI programming in detail, we incorporated key physical models into the TCAD deck to describe charge generation, transport, and injection under high electric fields. The horizontal potential difference between the floating and grounded regions —formed around the SWL—induces sharp band bending in the channel. This establishes favorable conditions for hot carrier generation, leading to sequential electron acceleration and injection into the insulating layer. In this context, BTBT serves as an effective electron source, particularly in regions where the lateral electric field is concentrated. In these regions, the band gap narrows, facilitating the generation of electron–hole pairs through tunneling. To accurately capture this phenomenon, a nonlocal BTBT model is employed in this study, which simultaneously considers the potential distribution and tunneling path length28.

Although impact ionization is not the primary injection mechanism, it plays a supplementary role by expanding the energy distribution of hot carriers29. High-energy electrons collide with lattice atoms, creating additional electron–hole pairs and influencing the injection boundary conditions. To quantify these complex generation-and-transport phenomena, we employ a Spherical Harmonics Expansion (SHE) model derived from the Boltzmann Transport Equation (BTE). The SHE model expands the non-equilibrium electron distribution function into higher-order terms in energy and directional space, enabling precise numerical approximation30. This approach allows for an accurate description of the spatial and energetic injection characteristics of high-energy electrons—phenomena that cannot be adequately captured using the conventional drift-diffusion framework .

The HCI model based on the SHE framework integrates the electron energy distribution function (), density of states (), carrier velocity (), and the tunneling transmission coefficient Γ() to calculate the injection current at the insulator interface. This framework enables quantitative, spatially resolved evaluation of hot-electron injection. Using this model, we quantitatively evaluated the variations in injection efficiency depending on the program cell location, as well as the injection tendencies influenced by the lateral and vertical electric field configurations.

It should be noted, however, that the present TCAD simulation inevitably relies on several simplifying assumptions. For example, fixed average grain size, constant mobility model, and uniformly distributed interface trap density cannot fully capture the stochastic variability of poly-Si grain morphology and localized trap distributions in fabricated devices. Similarly, the bulk trap density in the nitride layer was set to a widely adopted constant value, while in practice it may vary with process conditions. In addition, while the proposed HCI-BiPGM scheme demonstrates promising improvements, challenges such as capacitive coupling, charge redistribution in extremely long strings remain outside the scope of this study. These aspects represent important directions for future experimental validation and extended TCAD modeling.
