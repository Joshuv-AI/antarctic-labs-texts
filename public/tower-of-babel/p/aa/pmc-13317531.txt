Improving Scientific Research: Renegotiated Principles of Science, Proposals, Failures, and Successes
Authors: John P. A. Ioannidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTScience is a self‐correcting process, and scientific research aims to improve adequacy, accuracy, and utility. However, improving scientific research is a demanding task, especially when currently some key principles of science are challenged and renegotiated. Key challenges in the current environment include the increasing loss of trust in scientists; the production of most of the published scientific literature in countries without full democracy and/or in countries without fundamental freedoms, e.g., freedom of the press; limited public availability and transparency as most research is funded by non‐public sponsors that do not prioritize or even seek publication of results; and rapid developments on the frontier of artificial intelligence where non‐human agents can supplement and/or replace human researchers. Concurrently, there have been many proposals on how to improve research. Among a plethora of suggestions and guidance, some may not be useful or may even be harmful, and most lack evidence. Revisiting some key proposals shows mixed track records of failures and successes. Examples are provided from efforts to enhance collaboration, team science, and large studies; replication culture; registration and open science; containment of conflicts of interest; and statistical, computational, and informatics improvements. The optimal stages to improve research (early and/or late in the scientific process) may be debated, and the role, function, and mode of optimal peer review are also under scrutiny. Eventually, science and scientific research are demanding, hard enterprises. Genuine progress requires openness, honesty, and selflessness.

Improving Scientific Research—What Does It Mean?

Improving scientific research may sound extremely commonplace and self‐evident. By its very nature, by definition, science must have the property of self‐correcting itself, that is, improving. This means meeting at least three complementary features. First, it must become more adequate, explaining more adequately phenomena from the natural world. Second, it must become more precise, measuring phenomena with greater precision and less random and systematic error. Third, one hopes that it will become more useful, more beneficial for all of us. However, nowadays even the basic definitions of what scientific research is and how it is done, with what rules and with what fundamental principles, are in constant renegotiation.

Renegotiating Fundamental Principles of Scientific Research: Trust

While traditionally scientific research enjoyed almost universal recognition and acceptance by the public, lately we have seen growing questioning. The intensity of the phenomenon varies across countries. While the USA is usually highlighted as the country where trust in scientists is declining, in fact the USA is among the 12 countries with the highest trust in scientists in a recent survey [1]; only one European country (Spain) scored better. Most European countries did far worse. Among 68 assessed countries, Greece, Italy, and Israel ranked very low (56th, 57th, and 62nd) [1].

Many suspect that science has been sold out to the powerful, e.g., to callous economic interests that are contemptuous or even contrary to social interest. Or to false political leaders, callous to the point of disgust so long as they stay in power. Or to ideological obsessions that trample the rational scepticism of the scientific method. Or to the elitism of some privileged academic intelligentsia that is cut off from the real needs and vital anxieties of the many, the increasingly impoverished, marginalized masses of second‐class citizens. Many may accuse us that, while the world is going up in flames, in our academic towers our brainpower is wasted on cheap regurgitation of alleged progressiveness, wooden speeches and egocentric vanity.

The criticism may be harsh and sometimes clearly unfair, rhetorically exaggerated, populist, and excessive. However, the criticism must be heard, discussed, and utilized if we want to improve our science and its image among the wider public. Calling out misinformation [2] and enemies of science everywhere is a facile simplification, but transforming this creeping crisis into a persecution paranoia does not help. Perhaps, when we see enemies everywhere, we should reflect on whether our worst enemies are ourselves and our own incompetence, and we should return with prudence, wisdom, self‐denial, and empathy to serving knowledge, our fellow human beings, and society with epistemic humility [3].

Renegotiating Fundamental Principles of Scientific Research: Freedom

Science in recent years has not enjoyed the security offered by its traditional social spaces, the protected nurseries where it flourished supported by institutions of freedom, tolerance, and honesty. The classical sociology of science, for example, was developed on the principle that science needs freedom as a condition for flourishing: freedom of thought, speech, and action, and a strong, healthy, functioning democracy as social background [4]. When I published my first article 32 years ago, experts and non‐experts would have shown self‐evident, hard bibliographic data that scientific research is produced and published almost exclusively by open institutions in democratic countries [5]. Today, none of this is true. As I recently published with Jeroen Baas [6], 78% of scientific publications come from countries that are not full democracies and 92% come from countries that do not have good press freedom. Leaving aside totalitarian regimes like China and Russia, the 2026 ranking of the Reporters without Borders [7] shows depressing conditions for (lack of) freedom of the press in countries like the USA (ranked 64th), Italy (ranked 56th), and, worst among EU countries, Greece (85th).

I lived and worked for many years in Greece, and I want to reap this opportunity for self‐awareness. What I present in Box 1 is a comparison of different countries based on a combination of hard data [7, 8, 9, 10, 11] along with some highly personal views.

Renegotiating Fundamental Principles of Scientific Research: Public Availability

If questions are already raised about whether one can trust scientific research when its literature comes from countries where their freedom is either cruelly and authoritatively strangled or artfully and brazenly stolen, even more questions arise when we consider that most scientific research is now opaque and leaves no imprint on the literature [12, 13]. It is a universe of non‐visible dark matter and dark energy. The public funding that usually demands (even with moderate success) the publication of results in the open, transparent community space has been limited to a small percentage of scientific research funds [14]. Most research and development efforts are based on private funds that have clear, specific goals and priorities: maximizing sponsor profits. Certainly, for big pharmaceutical companies, scientific publication remained a useful, desirable tool until recently, as it could offer evidence for licensing and wider use of their products. Nor am I trying to diminish the undoubted, enormous value of healthy entrepreneurship in translating research effort into useful technologies and products. However, for the Big Tech giants and artificial intelligence (AI) companies that dominate the markets today, scientific publication is usually not a priority. Exceptions like AlphaFold for predicting the structure of proteins that led to a Nobel Prize [15] simply confirm the rule. Fabrication of reputation and advertising have now largely replaced information. Dominance in the market and in society does not even need to be judged by peers, to be published, to become common knowledge. Companies with huge capitalization and estimated value are content to claim maximum profits and maximum technological control of everyday life without being held accountable anywhere, let alone in the slow‐moving, often even arteriosclerotic, forums of scientific journals. With this development, concurrently almost all computing power is concentrated outside traditional academic institutions.

If this pattern escalates, we academics may continue to publish in scientific journals, but truly impactful research and technology will be realized elsewhere. Our prestigious scientific journals may become playgrounds where children play. Scientific papers will be just pieces of paper—kind of like the cards and stickers one finds in wafers and chewing gum: if one can collect enough wafer cards or stickers, one may become a university professor.

Renegotiating Fundamental Principles of Scientific Research: The (Non‐)human Factor

Even who and what are and will be the researchers and research of the present and future are currently renegotiated. With the rise of AI, almost all human activities can now be performed by non‐human agents and robots that combine perception, reasoning, planning, and action. More than 50% of online content is now written or translated by AI, not humans [16]. Scientific articles, for example, can now be written within seconds. Perhaps the quality of these fabricated materials is justifiably questioned, but by comparison, the “genuine” human‐generated bibliography is not a heap of diamonds. It looks more like a garbage dump where here and there some remarkable achievements are found among the debris. The annual number of fake articles produced by AI may now exceed a million annually (one in 7 submissions) [17]. The number of trivial articles that we don't even need to ask ourselves if they are wrong or fake (since they are simply useless) is probably many millions.

Even for the most serious research, we already have the option to let teams of AI agents carry it out [18, 19]. Why not even judge it? Why not even publish it? Are we moving towards mixed scientific societies of human scientists and robot scientists? Or perhaps a model of ghettoization of scientific research? Will serious scientific research be conducted, evaluated, and used by AI agents, while human scientists can barely survive with their scientific journals in some insignificant ghetto? Much like, in a zoo, monkeys also need some small artificial lake and some trees to hang out on and pass the time? I dread to think about it, and I hope I won't end up hanging from some insignificant scientific branch eating my ripe research banana.

Proposals to Save Scientific Research—Beware the Saviours

With this explosive mix of possibilities and uncertainty, proposals for how to improve—or even save—scientific research multiply. We have already seen many proposals for many years. As my favourite poet [20] says: Wo aber Gefahr ist, wächst Das Rettende auch. When there is danger, salvation also appears. Unfortunately, many saviours have appeared in science, and the rescue efforts are often contradictory and confusing. With the passage of time and availability of some empirical data, we must always remain open to criticism about whether these proposals have succeeded or failed. Have they all failed and that is why we have arrived where we are? I hope not—there were, undoubtedly, some successes. Who would not accept that science has had and still has moments of peak achievements and vital contributions? However, any successes achieved were not easy to achieve. They required hard work, broad participation of the scientific community and many other interested stakeholders: funding agencies, universities, research institutions, journals and publishers, and the public who can only gain when science makes real progress but also suffers when science suffers.

Indicatively, 12 years ago, in an article titled “How to make more published research true” [21], I compiled proposals for improving scientific research in several clusters of ideas. I wish to look back with a critical spirit whether several of these proposals have led to failures or successes in the meantime.

Collaboration, Team Science, and Large Studies

Collaboration and the emphasis on large studies involving many scientists have borne fruit. From genetics to physics, major scientific endeavours would not have been possible without rallying large groups of scientists to common goals. Possible disadvantages are the large investment that such efforts often require and the potential creation of powerful narratives that are difficult to challenge by lone maverick outsiders. However, the cumulative loss in money, materials, and effort from fragmentation into small research efforts can be much worse even when large coalitions of team science do not lead to spectacular results. Perhaps a useful lesson from the creation of large coalitions with concomitant transparency is that spectacular results are rare. For example, where we expected population genetics to revolutionize medical practice [22], we may have found thousands of genetic associations, but their utility and penetration into medical practice remain insignificant [23].

Replication Culture

The call to develop a culture of verification with more replicability studies has led many scientific fields to engage in systematic soul‐searching exercises [24]. Efforts to examine the robustness of study findings across disciplines have shown that in about three‐quarters of published studies, the information available is insufficient to replicate the corresponding experiments and calculations [25]. When replications can be launched, results are different about half the time [26] and robustness in different analyses can vary [27]. On average, the size of the findings shrinks by half or more [28]. Is this disappointing? Is the glass half full or half empty? What matters is that we now have some indicative estimates of where we are and where we are heading. In some fields, there is some evidence that they are improving based on replication criteria [29]. Perhaps we are doing something right—though there is no agreement on what exactly that is.

Registration and Open Science

Pre‐registration of studies and their protocols, and the pressure to make available and more open data, algorithms, methods, software, and other materials that form the backbone of research efforts has led to a large increase in freely available information and corresponding research material. We have also seen the transformation of a large percentage of research efforts into in silico work—to manage this vast amount of available information. However, most research remains opaque and impossible to openly assess in essence, as already mentioned. Only superficial assessments are feasible for most scientific studies, instinctive rather than rational. Moreover, unfortunately, the battle for open science was distorted to serve other, not that noble, purposes. For example [30], publishers were able to justify asking authors for thousands of Euros, sometimes even over 12,000 Euros, to publish a scientific article. An open article is simply a pdf file. This is a major fallacy and distortion and clearly leads many researchers to despair, especially when research funding is meagre (i.e., in almost all countries and settings).

Containment of Conflicts of Interest

The attempt to limit conflicting interests has probably been crowned mostly with failures. On one hand, several organizations have taken some steps towards transparency and independence from powerful interests, e.g., they have limited obvious distortions in expert committees that undertook the writing of influential guidelines shaping medical practice [31]. It has become less tolerable now that committees are shamelessly bought off by sponsors. On the other hand, loopholes have multiplied. For example, a committee may well be formed through panel stacking [32], including experts who are all known to agree with certain positions, even if they have no obvious financial benefit. Their profit is simply the fact that they acquire the role of powerful dictators. Science by committee is a glorious field for business, political, and ideological interference.

Statistical, Computational, and Informatics Improvements

Use of improved statistical, computational, and informatics methods has created a methodologically stronger bibliography in several scientific fields. Concurrently, many scientists who in the past had never left their laboratory bench or their clinic have found that their research efforts will remain immature or even childish without the cooperation of modern informational conquests. A possible side effect, however, is the availability of extremely complex models and software to a wider set of users who do not know how to use them—but still use them. Large language models can confer the allure of an expert methodologist even to the least knowledgeable misuser. The result is the emergence of information‐dense articles with sophisticated applications that, on closer inspection, turn out to be exercises in high‐level nonsense. In these cases, errors and biases can multiply rather than decrease. Developing a larger pool of experienced data scientists and being able to inject sound statistical and computational techniques into the broader research effort is an ongoing challenge.

Optimal Stages to Improve Research: Early and Late

Improving research can occur at all stages [33]: conception, development, protocol, conduct and data collection, analysis, presentation and publication, and dissemination and practical use of scientific information. I have participated in efforts to influence all these steps. The aftertaste is bittersweet. For example, through the EQUATOR coalition, we have gathered many hundreds of validated efforts to construct guidelines for how studies with different designs should be reported [34], e.g., randomized trials, observational studies, meta‐analyses, diagnostic test studies, predictive models, and so on. These efforts seem to improve the completeness of the reporting that characterizes how a scientific study was conducted, analysed, and commented on. But perhaps a complete (and hopefully, also honest) account of what happened is not enough. A poorly designed study presented in detail and full clarity is still a poorly designed study. Over time, we have also begun to promote guidelines for study design [35]. It may be better to intervene earlier, but it may still be too late if the overall agenda is misguided [36].

Peer Review

The same can be said for interventions related to improving the process of peer review [37]. It is widely known that the process of scientific judgement in science is problematic and biased. However, hundreds of proposals for what else we can do have led to meagre results [38]. Perhaps when peer review occurs, it is already too late. Perhaps our judgement is human, mediocre, and fallible. Perhaps we need some fair judgement earlier [39], or later, or continuously [40]. Yet, we should also not overdo it. Science cannot only involve secondary judgement and criticism; it must also have primary work to show. High‐risk and unpopular ideas should be given space to breathe.

Concluding Thoughts

Perhaps this rumination over improving scientific research has not been enlightening and no clear answers have been given. However, there are no easy answers. Science and scientific research are demanding, hard enterprises. They do not fit easily into moulds and there are no easy recipes. If there were, someone would open a cookbook, follow the steps of the recipe and receive a Nobel Prize with the same ease and certainty as someone makes a plate of meatballs. Scientific research possesses the elements of surprise and uncertainty, no matter how much we want to be certain. It has indeterminacy, no matter how much we try to determine. It is strewn with mistakes, setbacks, non‐linear paths, wandering in the jungle without even being assured of an exit from the dense rainforest of our errors. We may well get lost in this forest along with our students, our patients, and our society that expects a lot from us. I say all this not as a connoisseur, but as someone who, I am confident, has made many mistakes while searching. As a scientist, I hope to be able to continue searching and discover that I have made mistakes. The alternative scenario is not that we do not make mistakes—that would be impossible. The alternative scenario is that we make mistakes and simply do not recognize them, that we are blinded by our biases. May some scientists continue to make great discoveries and help society and fellow human beings. However, since this blessing will happen to few, I hope at least that all of us who strive in scientific research can make mistakes, many mistakes, and recognize them in a timely manner, with frankness, honesty, and selflessness.

Science, education, competitiveness: a tale of different countries.

The five countries directly above Greece in a better position (81–85th) in the Reporters without Borders ranking are the Central African Republic, the internationally not recognized state of Northern Cyprus, Albania, Kosovo, and Mongolia. The tragedy for Greece is even 100 times worse, because unlike these other 5 comparators, Greece is a full democracy [8] and has produced hundreds of times more well‐trained, excellent scientists [9] who struggle practically in a state of exile within their own country trying to do research with very limited funding under heroic conditions, or become self‐exiled abroad to materialize themselves.

How Greece, with such top scientific potential, has managed to be in a state of severe lack of freedom of expression and, in my view, with a distorted appreciation of reality in the public sphere without serious resistance, is a rather unique phenomenon. One comparator that comes to mind is Singapore. The tiny Asian country also has top scientific potential in terms of human resources and shares a very low score in press freedom (even lower, ranked 123rd) [7]. It shares with Greece also a regime of democracy governed by one or a few powerful families (de facto thus an oligarchy) that monopolizes democratic power for decades. The difference is that while in both countries the publicly provided and disseminated information has been massaged or blatantly distorted to the benefit of the respective regime, Singapore has managed to achieve a per capita income 4 times higher than Greece ($107,758 vs. $29,696). Moreover, differences in educating the young generation are stark: in the PISA ranking for the educational skills of 15‐year‐old students, Greece is 44th in mathematics, 44th in natural sciences and 41st in reading comprehension (well below the average of OECD countries), while Singapore ranks first in all subjects [10]. Moreover, Singapore is the world leader in competitiveness while Greece is barely in 59th place in the respective Competitiveness Rankings [11]. Singapore with its often suffocatingly humid weather attracts some of the best scientists, entrepreneurs, investors, and creators from around the world, while beautiful Greece tries to attract only tourists to beaches with low‐budget overtourism. Unfortunately, despite some myths about recent brain gain, in my view, power structures in Greece have traditionally supported mediocrity that practically expels from the country young people dreaming of something better.
