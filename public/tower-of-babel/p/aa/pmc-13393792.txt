Chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic seizures in temporal lobe epilepsy: insights from stereo-EEG in three patients
Authors: Jun Zhuang, Lingxia Fei, Hua Li, Kaihui Li, Qinghua Tan, Shaochun Li, Qiang Guo, Meiling Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo characterize the clinical and electrographic features of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) presenting with chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic seizures and to delineate the underlying seizure network using stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 102 consecutive patients with drug-resistant TLE who underwent presurgical SEEG evaluation between January 2021 and October 2024 at a single tertiary epilepsy center. Three patients with brief, stereotyped chorea–athetosis-like movements during seizures were identified. Clinical, neuroimaging, SEEG, surgical, and follow-up data were analyzed, focusing on the seizure-onset zone, early propagation pathways, and the temporal relationship between ictal activity in cortical and deep structures and the emergence of hyperkinetic movements.ResultsAll three patients had focal seizures with auras and/or impaired awareness accompanied by transient chorea–athetosis-like movements. Structural MRI demonstrated unilateral temporal abnormalities in each patient. [^18F]FDG-PET showed predominantly ipsilateral temporal hypometabolism concordant with the epileptogenic zone and, in one patient, additional contralateral temporal hypometabolism. SEEG demonstrated seizure onset in the ipsilateral temporal pole or mesial temporal structures, with rapid propagation to the insula and posterior orbitofrontal cortex. Hyperkinetic movements invariably followed cortical ictal onset and coincided with widespread high-frequency activity in temporal and insular cortices. In one patient, the caudate head and thalamus were secondarily recruited without evidence of primary deep seizure onset. All patients underwent tailored temporal (± partial insular) resections and achieved Engel class I outcomes with complete resolution of chorea–athetosis-like seizures at 1–2 years.ConclusionsChorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic seizures in TLE appear to be cortically driven, with seizures arising from temporal cortex and propagating through a temporo–insular–orbitofrontal network before recruiting basal ganglia–thalamic structures. These findings highlight the importance of adequate SEEG coverage of temporal and insular regions to avoid misdiagnosis and to optimize presurgical evaluation and surgical planning.

Introduction

Chorea–athetosis is a hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by the coexistence and intermingling of random, irregular, flowing choreic movements and slower, writhing athetotic movements of the limbs and other body parts [1], [2], [3]. It most commonly arises from genetic neurodegenerative disorders such as Huntington's disease, vascular lesions, autoimmune conditions, metabolic disturbances, or drug exposure, which converge on dysfunction within the basal ganglia–thalamocortical circuits [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. In this context, chorea–athetosis is typically regarded as a primary movement disorder.

By contrast, chorea–athetosis-like movements occurring as an ictal manifestation of epilepsy are rare and easily misdiagnosed as primary or secondary movement disorders. In particular, the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying chorea–athetosis-like seizures remain poorly understood. Previous work has largely relied on scalp EEG combined with functional imaging (SPECT, PET, or fMRI), which offers only indirect inferences about the relative timing and causality between cortical and subcortical structures [10], [11], [12]. Direct intracranial recordings simultaneously sampling cortical and deep nuclei in this context are scarce. To date, SEEG studies specifically addressing hyperkinetic seizures are very limited and have predominantly involved frontal or insular lobe epilepsies, providing little direct evidence regarding temporal lobe onset and its relationship with basal ganglia–thalamic activation.

Stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG) provides a unique opportunity to delineate seizure onset zones (SOZ) and early propagation pathways at high temporal resolution, including simultaneous sampling of deep nuclei such as the caudate nucleus and thalamus [13], [14]. Here, we report three patients with drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) whose predominant seizure semiology consisted of brief, stereotyped chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements. All three arose from a cohort of 102 TLE patients who underwent presurgical SEEG evaluation at a tertiary epilepsy center. To our knowledge, there are no prior systematic SEEG case series that combine temporal lobe onset, chorea–athetosis-like semiology, and concurrent sampling of basal ganglia–thalamic structures. By integrating semiology, structural and functional imaging, SEEG findings, and surgical outcomes, we aimed to characterize the ictal network underlying these rare seizures, with a particular focus on the temporal–insular–orbitofrontal cortex and basal ganglia–thalamic loop. Our goal was to provide direct electrophysiological evidence for a cortically driven mechanism of epilepsy-related chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic seizures and to inform clinical recognition, SEEG planning, and surgical strategy.

Materials and methods

Patients

This single-center retrospective case series was based on consecutive patients admitted to our tertiary epilepsy center for presurgical evaluation. We reviewed all patients with drug-resistant epilepsy who underwent SEEG between January 2021 and October 2024. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) a definite diagnosis of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) established by comprehensive clinical assessment and long-term video-EEG and/or SEEG monitoring; (2) prominent chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements as a main ictal manifestation; and (3) completion of SEEG monitoring with available clinical, neuroimaging, surgical, and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) chorea–athetosis attributable to metabolic or autoimmune diseases; (2) drug-induced dyskinesias; and (3) severe motor artefacts precluding reliable SEEG interpretation. During the study period, 102 patients with TLE underwent presurgical SEEG evaluation. Of these, three patients (3/102) fulfilled all inclusion and exclusion criteria and formed the final study cohort. Drug-resistant epilepsy was defined according to the ILAE criteria as failure of at least two appropriately chosen and tolerated antiseizure medications (ASMs) at adequate doses [15]. Clinical, imaging, SEEG, surgical, and outcome data of the three patients.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number: 2024–01-022). All patients or their legal guardians provided written informed consent for invasive monitoring, surgical procedures, and the use of anonymized clinical data for research and publication.

SEEG implantation and recording

SEEG implantation strategies were determined by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) comprising epileptologists, neurosurgeons, neurophysiologists, and neuroradiologists. Implantation plans were individualized based on seizure semiology, scalp video-EEG findings, structural MRI, [^18F]FDG-PET, and neuropsychological assessment, with the primary goal of adequately sampling the suspected temporal epileptogenic zone and its early propagation pathways.

All three patients were implanted with multiple depth electrodes in the anterior temporal neocortex and mesial temporal structures (amygdala and hippocampus) on the presumed epileptogenic side, as well as in the ipsilateral insular cortex. In two patients, additional orbitofrontal electrodes were placed to assess involvement of the posterior orbitofrontal cortex. In one patient (Patient 3), depth electrodes were also implanted in the ipsilateral head of the caudate nucleus and thalamus (anterior and medial nuclei) to evaluate the role of the cortico–basal ganglia–thalamic loop during seizures.

All SEEG electrodes were stainless-steel multicontact depth electrodes (diameter ≈ 0.8 mm, 8–16 contacts per electrode, individual contact length 2 mm, intercontact spacing 1.5 mm), inserted along predefined trajectories using a stereotactic frame or robotic guidance. Post-implantation CT was co-registered with preoperative high-resolution MRI to confirm electrode positions and precise anatomical localization of each contact within cortical regions and deep nuclei.

Continuous video-SEEG monitoring was performed using the Nihon Kohden EEG-1200 system with a sampling rate of 1024 Hz and a bandpass filter of 0.05–600 Hz. Antiseizure medications were gradually reduced as clinically appropriate to increase the likelihood of capturing habitual seizures. Bipolar montages were primarily used for seizure onset and propagation analysis.

Data collection and clinical assessment

Demographic data (sex, age, handedness), age at seizure onset, epilepsy duration, antiseizure medications, and seizure frequency were collected. Seizure semiology was characterized based on long-term video-EEG/SEEG recordings, with particular attention to the presence, onset body region, evolution, duration, and stereotypy of chorea–athetosis-like movements, as well as associated auras, automatisms, and changes in awareness. Seizure semiology was prospectively categorized according to the Semiological Seizure Classification (SSC), which is routinely used in our SEEG presurgical evaluation program [16].

All patients underwent structural MRI and [^18F]FDG-PET as part of presurgical evaluation. MRI findings were reviewed for structural abnormalities in the temporal lobe and related regions. PET scans were visually assessed for focal or regional hypometabolism, particularly in the temporal lobe, insula, and orbitofrontal cortex.

Comprehensive neuropsychological assessment included measures of global intellectual functioning and memory, with full-scale IQ (FIQ), verbal IQ (VIQ), performance IQ (PIQ), and memory quotient (MQ) reported where available.

Surgical procedures were planned based on the integrated interpretation of clinical, neuroimaging, and SEEG findings. Resection extent, histopathology, and postoperative seizure outcomes (Engel classification) were recorded at 1–2 years of follow-up.

SEEG analysis

For each patient, at least three habitual seizures with complete clinical and SEEG documentation were selected for detailed analysis. SEEG traces were visually reviewed by two experienced epileptologists, who independently assessed seizure onset patterns, onset contacts, and early propagation, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Seizure onset patterns were classified according to established SEEG criteria [17], [18], [19]:Low-voltage fast activity (LVFA): 20–30 Hz rhythmic activity with amplitude <10 μV; Rhythmic spikes: >13 Hz rhythmic spikes evolving into LVFA; Burst of polyspikes: high-voltage polyspike bursts evolving into LVFA; Slow-wave evolution: slow waves evolving into LVFA; Rhythmic spike–slow waves: 2–4 Hz rhythmic spike–slow waves or polyspike–slow waves; Theta/alpha evolution: gradually increasing theta or alpha activity evolving into LVFA. For propagation analysis, bipolar montages were used. The onset zone was defined as the set of contacts showing the earliest unequivocal ictal pattern. Propagation sequences were determined by comparing the time of ictal pattern emergence across contacts in the temporal pole, anterior temporal neocortex, mesial temporal structures, insula, posterior orbitofrontal cortex, caudate head, and thalamus. The temporal relationship between these electrophysiological changes and the clinical onset of chorea–athetosis-like movements was carefully examined.

Results

Clinical characteristics and seizure semiology(Table 1)

All three patients had normal interictal neurological examinations without tremor or other pre-existing movement disorders. The chorea-athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements described below occurred exclusively during epileptic seizures.

Patient 1 was a 12-year-old right-handed boy with onset of seizures at age 10. Initial seizures consisted of experiential auras described as “flashes” of previously lived scenes, followed by brief flinging or waving limb movements and sometimes chewing or swallowing automatisms, lasting several tens of seconds. Approximately 90% of seizures occurred during sleep, and in these nocturnal events the experiential aura consistently awakened the patient before the emergence of chorea–athetosis-like movements and automatisms. Seizure frequency progressed from 1 to 2 per month to multiple per day despite oxcarbazepine and topiramate at full therapeutic doses, consistent with drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Neurological examination was unremarkable. Scalp video-EEG showed frequent interictal left temporal spikes and spike–slow waves. Three habitual nocturnal seizures were captured, with semiology of “experiential aura → chorea–athetosis-like movements → left hand and oral automatisms”. Ictal EEG showed left hemispheric spike–slow waves (maximal in the temporal region) at onset, followed by voltage attenuation over the left hemisphere and subsequent evolution into bilateral rhythmic slow activity.

MRI revealed a benign tumor-like lesion in the left anterior temporal lobe with ipsilateral hippocampal sclerosis and cortical thickening in the left insula. [^18F]FDG-PET showed hypometabolism in the left temporal pole, anterior temporal lobe, mesial temporal structures, and posterior orbitofrontal cortex, with additional hypometabolism in the inferior left insula (Fig. 1). Neuropsychological testing indicated mild intellectual disability with marked memory impairment (FIQ 64, VIQ 62, PIQ 74, MQ 50). Patient 2 was a 30-year-old right-handed man with seizure onset at age 14, initially presenting with nocturnal generalized convulsions lasting 1–2 min. With antiseizure medication, the seizures evolved into focal events characterized by an aura of palpitations followed by flinging limb movements, sometimes accompanied by chewing or swallowing automatisms and later autonomic symptoms (e.g., tachycardia). Seizures initially occurred every 1–2 weeks and progressed to once every 2–3 days despite valproate, oxcarbazepine, clonazepam, and topiramate, all administered at full therapeutic or maximum tolerated doses. Neurological examination was normal. Scalp video-EEG showed normal background with interictal spikes predominantly over the right frontal–temporal regions. Three habitual seizures were recorded with semiology of “palpitations → chorea–athetosis-like movements → oral automatisms → autonomic symptoms”.

MRI demonstrated right amygdala hypertrophy. [^18F]FDG-PET showed hypometabolism in the right mesial temporal structures, right temporal pole, and inferior right insula (Fig. 1). Neuropsychological testing revealed normal global intellect (FIQ 101) with PIQ > VIQ (VIQ 87, PIQ 118) but severely impaired memory (MQ <51). These data supported a right temporal epileptogenic zone, and SEEG was performed.

Patient 3 was a 41-year-old right-handed woman with an initial nocturnal generalized tonic–clonic seizure (GTCS) at age 28, shortly after her second childbirth. Approximately three years later, she developed a new seizure type: sudden episodes of head shaking and bilateral upper limb hyperkinetic movements resembling chorea–athetosis, accompanied by incomprehensible speech and unresponsiveness, lasting about 10 s (Fig. 2). These occurred during both wakefulness and sleep, increasing from 1 to 2 per month to almost daily, despite levetiracetam and carbamazepine at full therapeutic doses. Neurological examination was normal. Scalp video-EEG showed interictal spike–slow waves predominantly over the right centro-parietotemporal regions. Multiple habitual hyperkinetic seizures were recorded, with semiology of “chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements → gelastic laughter”. SEEG implantation

In all three patients, SEEG electrode arrays were designed to cover the presumed temporal epileptogenic zone and its main propagation pathways. Electrodes were implanted in the anterior temporal neocortex and mesial temporal structures (amygdala and hippocampus) on the affected side in all three patients. The ipsilateral insular cortex was sampled in all cases. Based on semiology and imaging, orbitofrontal electrodes were added in two patients to sample the posterior orbitofrontal cortex. In Patient 3, additional electrodes were implanted in the ipsilateral head of the caudate nucleus and thalamus (anterior and medial nuclei) to characterize the involvement of the cortico–basal ganglia–thalamic loop. Post-implantation CT–MRI co-registration confirmed correct positioning of all electrodes and allowed precise mapping of each contact to cortical and subcortical structures.

Stereo-EEG findings in individual patients

Patient 1.

SEEG revealed seizure onset in contacts located in the left anterior temporal neocortex. Within 2–4 s, ictal activity propagated sequentially to the left insula, posterior orbitofrontal cortex, and mesial temporal structures. Chorea–athetosis-like movements consistently emerged after this early propagation phase, at a time when high-frequency rhythmic activity was simultaneously present in the left anterior temporal cortex, perisylvian cortex, insula, posterior orbitofrontal cortex, and mesial temporal structures. These findings suggested that synchronous recruitment of this temporal–insular–orbitofrontal–mesial network was closely associated with the expression of chorea–athetosis-like seizures.

Based on SEEG findings, the patient underwent resection of the left temporal pole and anterior temporal neocortex together with mesial temporal structures and partial insular cortex. Histopathology confirmed a ganglioglioma of the left temporal pole. At two years' follow-up, all epileptic seizures, with chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements as the predominant clinical manifestation, had completely resolved (Engel class Ia). All ASMs had been completely withdrawn without seizure recurrence.

Patient 2.

In Patient 2, SEEG showed seizure onset in the right mesial temporal structures, with earliest ictal changes in contacts within the amygdala–hippocampal complex. Ictal activity then propagated along the longitudinal temporal axis and laterally to involve the right temporal pole and anterior temporal neocortex, followed by spread to the right insular cortex. Chorea–athetosis-like movements consistently appeared only after ictal discharges had spread from mesial temporal structures to the lateral temporal cortex and insula. At that moment, high-frequency rhythmic discharges were observed simultaneously in the right mesial temporal structures, lateral temporal cortex, perisylvian cortex, and insula.

A tailored right mesial temporal resection combined with right anterior temporal and partial insular resection was performed. Histopathology showed neuronal disorganization of the right amygdala and FCD type Ib in the right temporal pole. At two years' follow-up, all epileptic seizures, with chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic events as the predominant ictal manifestation, had completely resolved (Engel class Ia). All ASMs had been completely withdrawn without seizure recurrence.

Patient 3.

In Patient 3, SEEG allowed detailed reconstruction of the spatiotemporal evolution of seizures across cortical and deep structures. Ictal discharges first appeared in the right temporal pole neocortex. Within 2–3 s, ictal activity rapidly spread to the right insula and posterior orbitofrontal cortex, followed by involvement of the right mesial temporal structures (amygdala and hippocampus). Subsequently, ictal discharges propagated to the right thalamus and head of the caudate nucleus. Shared clinical and SEEG features

Across the three patients, the core ictal manifestation was a brief, highly stereotyped chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movement pattern. In most seizures, these hyperkinetic movements were preceded by auras (experiential déjà vécu-like phenomena or palpitations) and were frequently accompanied by oral automatisms and, in some events, impaired awareness. Across all captured events, the chorea–athetosis-like movements typically lasted for just over 10 s, involved all four limbs, and appeared largely symmetric, without a consistent lateralizing predominance.

Structural and functional imaging consistently demonstrated unilateral temporal lobe abnormalities concordant with the SEEG-defined epileptogenic zone: a temporal pole ganglioglioma with ipsilateral hippocampal sclerosis and insular cortical thickening (Patient 1), right amygdala hypertrophy (Patient 2), and focal cortical dysplasia in the right temporal pole (Patient 3). [^18F]FDG-PET showed predominant hypometabolism in the temporal lobe ipsilateral to the structural lesion, involving mesial temporal structures and/or the temporal pole and often extending to the inferior insula and posterior orbitofrontal cortex. In Patient 3, there was additional, less pronounced hypometabolism in the contralateral temporal pole.

SEEG findings revealed a common pattern in which seizures originated in the ipsilateral temporal cortex (temporal pole or mesial temporal structures) and then rapidly propagated to the ipsilateral insula and, when sampled, the posterior orbitofrontal cortex. Chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements always occurred after cortical ictal onset. At the time of the hyperkinetic semiology, high-frequency rhythmic activity was present in temporal and insular cortices and mesial temporal structures in all patients.

In Patient 3, additional sampling of the caudate head and thalamus demonstrated that these deep nuclei were not part of the primary seizure-onset zone but were secondarily recruited shortly after temporal cortical onset. The timing of ictal activity increases in the caudate head and thalamus closely matched the clinical onset of chorea–athetosis-like movements, without evidence of independent deep-origin seizures. This pattern supports a role of the basal ganglia–thalamic loop as a downstream amplifier and integrator of cortically driven ictal activity in the expression of hyperkinetic seizures.

Surgical outcomes

All three patients underwent individualized resections targeting the SEEG-defined temporal epileptogenic zone and involved cortical regions, including anterior and mesial temporal structures and, in two cases, partial insular cortex. Histopathology confirmed structural abnormalities concordant with preoperative imaging: ganglioglioma of the temporal pole (Patient 1), right amygdala neuronal disorganization with FCD type Ib in the right temporal pole (Patient 2), and FCD type IIb in the right temporal pole (Patient 3).

At last follow-up (1–2 years after surgery), all three patients were seizure-free (Engel class Ia) with complete resolution of chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic seizures, with two patients having discontinued all antiseizure medications and one patient maintained on reduced-dose antiseizure medications. These outcomes suggest that the identified network was an effective surgical target for both seizure control and elimination of the hyperkinetic semiology.

Discussion

Main findings and novelty

This study describes three patients with drug-resistant TLE in whom the predominant ictal semiology consisted of chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements. All three patients had concordant unilateral temporal lobe structural abnormalities on MRI and corresponding hypometabolism on [^18F]FDG-PET. SEEG consistently showed seizure onset in the ipsilateral temporal cortex—either the temporal pole/anterior temporal neocortex or mesial temporal structures—with rapid propagation to the ipsilateral insula and, when sampled, the posterior orbitofrontal cortex. Chorea–athetosis-like movements invariably emerged after cortical ictal onset, at a time when high-frequency rhythmic activity was present within a temporo–insular–orbitofrontal–mesial temporal network.

In the patient with concomitant sampling of the caudate head and thalamus, these deep nuclei were clearly recruited after temporal cortical onset. The increase in their ictal activity was tightly time-locked to the appearance of chorea–athetosis-like movements, with no evidence of primary seizure onset within these structures. In this patient, SEEG provided a clear temporal sequence of “temporo–insular cortical onset → early recruitment of caudate head and thalamus → emergence of chorea–athetosis-like movements”, arguing against a primary basal ganglia or thalamic origin and supporting a cortically driven mechanism.

The main novelty of this work lies in the use of SEEG to obtain simultaneous ictal recordings from temporal cortex, insula, orbitofrontal cortex, and deep nuclei, thereby delineating a dynamic “cortex-origin–basal ganglia/thalamus-mediated–hyperkinetic output” pathway for epilepsy-related chorea–athetosis-like seizures. To our knowledge, SEEG studies combining temporal lobe onset, chorea–athetosis-like semiology, and concurrent sampling of basal ganglia–thalamic structures are extremely limited. Our data provide rare direct electrophysiological evidence for a cortically driven mechanism in TLE patients with chorea–athetosis-like seizures.

Pathophysiological considerations

The observed chorea–athetosis-like movements are consistent with the concept that hyperkinetic movement disorders result from imbalances within basal ganglia–thalamocortical circuits [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. Classical basal ganglia physiology posits that cortico–striatal projections modulate the balance between the direct and indirect pathways, thereby regulating the inhibitory output of the internal segment of the globus pallidus (GPi) to the thalamus. Relative overactivity of the direct pathway or underactivity of the indirect pathway can disinhibit thalamocortical motor circuits and give rise to choreic and athetotic movements. Within this framework, the basal ganglia do not necessarily have to be the primary locus of pathology; instead, they may function as cortically driven “amplifiers” and “gating circuits” for abnormal motor outputs [14], [20].

Our SEEG findings suggest a more specific cascade in TLE with chorea–athetosis-like seizures: seizure onset in temporal cortex (temporal pole or mesial temporal structures); rapid propagation to insular and orbitofrontal cortices; subsequent recruitment of the caudate head and thalamus; and the emergence of chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic movements, likely mediated by disinhibition within basal ganglia–thalamocortical motor circuits (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Schematic of the seizure network underlying chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic seizures in temporal lobe epilepsy. In all three patients, temporo–insular cortical onset clearly preceded the appearance of hyperkinetic semiology. In Patient 3, increased ictal activity in the caudate head and thalamus lagged behind temporal cortical onset but was closely synchronized with the onset of chorea–athetosis-like movements, without evidence of independent deep-origin seizures. This “cortex-first, basal ganglia–thalamus-later” temporal pattern supports the view that abnormal epileptic discharges arising from temporal and insular cortex drive cortico–striatal projections, engage basal ganglia networks, and ultimately disinhibit thalamocortical motor output pathways.

Compared with previous studies on the pathophysiology of chorea, athetosis, and other hyperkinetic movements—most of which have relied on scalp EEG and functional imaging [21], [22], [23], [24]—our SEEG data provide more direct information about the relative timing of cortical and subcortical activation. Simultaneous recordings from temporo–insular cortex, caudate head, and thalamus in the same patient showed that deep nuclei were secondarily recruited by cortical ictal activity rather than acting as primary seizure generators. These findings complement existing models of basal ganglia–thalamocortical dysfunction by adding a specific cortically driven mechanism for epilepsy-related chorea–athetosis-like seizures in TLE.

Clinical implications

Our findings have several clinical implications for the recognition and management of epilepsy-related movement disorders.

First, chorea–athetosis-like seizures, although rare, represent a distinctive seizure semiology in TLE and should be actively considered in the differential diagnosis of brief, recurrent, stereotyped hyperkinetic episodes. In patients with short-lasting chorea–athetosis-like movements associated with auras, impaired awareness, or other ictal features, an epileptic origin should be strongly suspected to avoid misdiagnosis as primary or secondary movement disorders.

Second, the SEEG results provide practical guidance for electrode implantation and surgical planning. Adequate sampling of the suspected temporal epileptogenic zone—including the temporal pole, anterior temporal neocortex, and mesial temporal structures—together with ipsilateral insula and orbitofrontal cortex appears crucial for delineating the ictal network underlying chorea–athetosis-like seizures. In selected patients with prominent hyperkinetic semiology and acceptable safety margins, additional electrodes in basal ganglia structures such as the caudate head may be considered to assess their downstream involvement.

Although Patient 3 showed that the caudate head and thalamus can be passively recruited during seizures, seizure control in all three patients was achieved by resection of the temporal epileptogenic zone without any destructive intervention in basal ganglia or thalamus. This supports the concept that temporal cortical epileptogenic tissue should remain the primary surgical target, whereas basal ganglia and thalamus are best understood as downstream nodes in a cortically driven network rather than priority targets for lesioning.

Finally, the demonstration of downstream basal ganglia–thalamic recruitment raises hypotheses for future neuromodulation strategies. Given the apparent role of these structures in amplifying and gating hyperkinetic manifestations, nodes such as the caudate head or thalamus might represent potential targets for deep brain stimulation or closed-loop cortico–subcortical stimulation aimed at suppressing cortically driven hyperkinetic seizures. Such approaches, however, require validation in larger, systematically studied cohorts with detailed electrophysiological and interventional data.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective case series with only three patients, and only one patient had SEEG electrodes in both the caudate head and thalamus. Consequently, conclusions regarding the role and timing of basal ganglia and thalamic involvement in chorea–athetosis-like seizures should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Second, our SEEG analysis relied primarily on visual assessment of ictal onset and propagation patterns. We did not perform quantitative connectivity or network analyses, nor did we integrate SEEG with advanced multimodal imaging (such as diffusion tractography or functional connectivity MRI) to corroborate structural–functional relationships within the network.

Third, although our findings support a cortically driven mechanism in TLE-related chorea–athetosis-like seizures, they may not be generalizable to other epileptic syndromes, extratemporal hyperkinetic seizures, or primary movement disorders with comorbid epilepsy.

Future studies should aim to enroll larger cohorts of patients with epilepsy-related chorea–athetosis-like semiology, ideally in a prospective multicenter setting. Combining dense SEEG sampling of temporal, insular, orbitofrontal, and basal ganglia–thalamic structures with quantitative network analyses and multimodal neuroimaging could yield a more precise characterization of the cortico–basal ganglia–thalamic network. Such work may refine presurgical evaluation, optimize SEEG implantation strategies, and guide the development of targeted neuromodulation approaches.

Conclusions

Chorea–athetosis-like hyperkinetic seizures represent a rare but clinically meaningful seizure semiology in temporal lobe epilepsy. In this case series, seizures consistently originated from temporal cortical regions and propagated through a temporo–insular–orbitofrontal network before recruiting the basal ganglia–thalamic loop. SEEG recordings indicated that the basal ganglia and thalamus acted as downstream nodes that were secondarily recruited by cortical ictal activity rather than as primary epileptogenic sources. Accurate recognition of chorea–athetosis-like semiology—particularly when associated with auras and impaired awareness—and appropriate SEEG sampling of temporal, insular, and related networks may reduce misdiagnosis and improve presurgical evaluation. Tailored temporal resections guided by SEEG resulted in excellent seizure control and complete resolution of hyperkinetic manifestations in all three patients. Taken together, these findings support a cortically driven mechanism for epilepsy-related chorea–athetosis-like seizures and provide a network-based rationale for both surgical treatment and the development of future neuromodulatory interventions.
