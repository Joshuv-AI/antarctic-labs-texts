Antimicrobial resistance and biofilm-forming ability in Staphylococcus aureus causing clinical bovine mastitis in Chitwan, Nepal
Authors: Sujan Adhikari, Swagat Khanal, Aakash Marasini, Prakash Panthi, Akash Adhikari, Himal Luitel, Yagya Raj Pandeya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis cross-sectional study, conducted from August to December 2024, investigated the antimicrobial resistance and biofilm-forming abilities of Staphylococcus aureus from clinical bovine mastitis in Chitwan, Nepal. Out of 134 California Mastitis Test-positive milk samples, 32 (23.9%) were confirmed as S. aureus by biochemical tests and species-specific nuc gene PCR. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of the 32 isolates against 12 antibiotics revealed high resistance rates, particularly to ampicillin (78.1%), nalidixic acid (75.0%), and enrofloxacin (62.5%). The prevalence of multidrug resistance (MDR), defined as resistance to ≥3 antibiotic classes, was alarmingly high, with 27 (84.4%) isolates classified as MDR. Presumptive methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), detected via cefoxitin resistance, was identified in 14 (43.8%) isolates, all of which were also MDR. Biofilm-forming abilities were assessed qualitatively and quantitatively, with 5 (15.6%) isolates classified as strong biofilm producers. Fisher’s exact test revealed no significant association between biofilm formation and overall MDR status (p > 0.05). However, a statistically significant correlation was found between strong biofilm formation and high-level MDR (resistance to ≥ 4 classes) (p < 0.05), as well as between strong biofilm formation and presumptive MRSA status (p < 0.01). These findings highlight the co-existence of high-level resistance and strong virulence phenotypes in S. aureus from bovine mastitis in Nepal, underscoring the urgent need for robust antimicrobial stewardship, enhanced surveillance, and the development of strategies to mitigate biofilm-associated treatment failures.Keywords: Bovine mastitis, Staphylococcus aureus, Biofilm, MDR, MRSA, Nepal

Introduction

Bovine mastitis (BM) is a persistent inflammatory disease of the mammary gland that poses a major threat to the global dairy industry. It has detrimental effects on milk production, milk quality, and animal welfare and can greatly increase the treatment costs, making it economically significant (Azooz et al., 2020; Cheng & Han, 2020; Morales-Ubaldo et al., 2023). Among the various pathogens responsible for BM, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a leading cause. It is often associated with subclinical and clinical mastitis and is known for causing persistent intramammary infections (Egyedy & Ametaj, 2022). S. aureus is an opportunistic pathogen and can easily spread between cows during milking. It may also persist in the farm environment, contaminating bedding, equipment, and other surfaces, which complicates control and eradication efforts (Kerro Dego & Vidlund, 2024).

Antibiotic therapy remains the primary treatment for BM. It accounts for the highest volume of antibiotic use in the dairy sector (Cheng & Han, 2020). This widespread and often indiscriminate use of antibiotics has resulted in a rapid increase in antibiotic resistance rates among bacterial pathogens (Ahmed et al., 2024; Muteeb et al., 2023). The emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR) S. aureus strains is a growing concern in managing BM (). This can significantly complicate treatment protocols, diminish therapeutic efficacy, and escalate associated costs.

A key virulence factor contributing to the persistence of S. aureus infections and its recalcitrance to therapy is its ability to form biofilms (Pedersen et al., 2021). Biofilms are structured communities of bacteria encased in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance. This matrix shields the embedded bacteria from host immune defenses and significantly reduces the penetration and efficacy of antimicrobial agents (Grari et al., 2025). Isolates from chronic mastitis cases that form strong biofilms are often linked to treatment failure and recurrent infections (Gebreyohannes et al., 2019; Pokharel et al., 2022).

Furthermore, the emergence of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in livestock and its potential spread to the human population is a growing public health concern. The strains of MRSA, which carry resistance to nearly all β-lactam antibiotics, pose a significant zoonotic threat due to their ability to transmit through livestock to humans via contact or the food chain (Abebe & Birhanu, 2023; Algammal et al., 2020). The treatment of infections caused by MDR-MRSA strains is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality, extended duration of hospitalization, and increased cost of healthcare due to the need for expensive and potentially toxic alternative antibiotics (Saseedharan et al., 2024).

While antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in S. aureus from mastitis has been reported in Nepal, there remains a critical gap in our understanding of the interplay between resistance and key virulence factors like biofilm formation. Reports on the prevalence of biofilm-producing S. aureus and its association with high-risk phenotypes such as MDR and presumptive MRSA are particularly scarce in the Nepalese context. This study aimed to investigate S. aureus in clinical bovine mastitis cases in Chitwan, Nepal, by determining its prevalence, assessing antimicrobial resistance, measuring biofilm-forming ability, and examining how biofilm formation is related to antimicrobial resistance. The findings are intended to provide crucial baseline data to inform local antimicrobial stewardship programs and guide future mastitis control strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design, area, and duration

A cross-sectional study was conducted between August and December 2024. The study took place in Bharatpur Metropolitan City, Chitwan district (27° 40′ 59.99″ N, 84° 25′ 59.99″ E), a major dairy production hub in Nepal.

Sampling and sample collection

A non-probability purposive sampling strategy was employed to select dairy animals showing clinical signs of mastitis. This sampling method was chosen to maximize the likelihood of isolating clinically relevant pathogens. A total of 134 milk samples were collected from 53 dairy cows and buffaloes across 27 smallholder dairy farms. Only individual quarters that exhibited visible clinical signs of mastitis and tested positive by the California Mastitis Test with gel formation were sampled. After disinfection of the teat end with 70% ethanol, approximately 10 mL of milk was collected aseptically into sterile containers. Samples were transported to the laboratory in a cold chain and processed within 6 h.

Isolation, identification, and molecular confirmation of S. aureus

1 mL of each milk sample was first inoculated into 9 mL of Buffered Peptone Water for pre-enrichment and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following enrichment, a loopful of broth was streaked onto Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) (M118, HiMedia, India) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Presumptive S. aureus colonies (golden-yellow) were sub-cultured onto Tryptone Soy Agar (M290, HiMedia, India) to obtain pure colonies. Isolates were preliminarily identified based on colony morphology, Gram staining (Gram-positive cocci in clusters), and positive catalase and tube coagulase tests. The tube coagulase test was performed using rabbit plasma, with clot formation within 4 h considered positive.

Following biochemical identification, all 32 presumptive S. aureus isolates underwent molecular confirmation by targeting the species-specific thermonuclease (nuc) gene. DNA was extracted from pure cultures using a standard boiling method. PCR was performed using the primers nuc-F: 5′-GCGATTGATGGTGATACGGTT-3′ and nuc-R: 5′-AGCCAAGCCTTGACGAACTAAAGC-3′ (Haq et al., 2024). The presence of a 270 bp amplicon when visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed the isolates as S. aureus.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test (AST)

AST was performed for all confirmed S. aureus isolates using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton Agar, following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024). The antibiotic panel included 12 agents from 7 classes, selected based on their clinical relevance in treating bovine mastitis in Nepal: ampicillin (10 µg), cephalexin (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), enrofloxacin (5 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), nalidixic acid (30 µg), doxycycline (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), and sulphamethoxazole/trimethoprim (25 µg). Inhibition zone diameters were measured after 18 h of incubation at 37 °C and interpreted as Susceptible (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R) according to CLSI M100-ED34 breakpoints. Isolates resistant to cefoxitin (zone diameter ≤ 21 mm) were classified as presumptive MRSA. Isolates resistant to agents from three or more antimicrobial classes were defined as MDR.

Assessment of biofilm formation

Qualitative assessment of biofilm formation

Congo red agar (CRA) and the tube method were employed for the qualitative screening of biofilm formation. CRA medium was prepared by using brain heart infusion broth (37 g/L) supplemented with sucrose (50 g/L), agar A (10 g/L), and Congo red dye (0.8 g/L). Bacterial isolates were then streaked onto the CRA plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Strong slime-producing bacteria were expected to form black colonies with a dry, crystalline appearance, whereas non-slime producers develop smooth-surfaced red or pink colonies (Freeman et al., 1989).

For the tube method, a loopful of bacterial culture from overnight plates was inoculated into TSB with 1% glucose. The tubes were incubated at 37 °C while shaking at 225 rpm for 24 h. The broth was then decanted, and tubes were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before being air-dried. The dried tubes were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, followed by washing with deionized water. The tubes were then dried in an inverted position. Positive biofilm production was indicated by a visible film lining the walls and bottom of the tube. The intensity was recorded as strong, moderate, weak, or no biofilm formation based on the subjective evaluation of the density of visible film (Christensen et al., 1985).

Quantitative assessment of biofilm formation

Biofilm formation was quantified using the microtiter plate (MTP) assay, following Christensen et al. (1985) with slight modifications (Manandhar et al., 2018). Briefly, bacterial colonies were grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB) with 1% glucose at 37 °C for 24 h, then diluted 1:100 in fresh TSB. 200 µL of this dilution were added to each well of a sterile 96-well plate, with uninoculated TSB as a negative control. After 24 h incubation, wells were gently emptied, washed four times with PBS, air-dried, and fixed with 2% sodium acetate for 15 min. Wells were stained with 1% crystal violet for 20 min, rinsed with deionized water, and dried again. Biofilms were then solubilized with 95% ethanol for 30 min, and 150 µL of the resulting solution was transferred to fresh wells. Optical density (OD) at 570 nm was measured using an ELISA plate reader (ThermoFisher Multiskan SkyTM).

OD values were used to categorize biofilm producers as no, weak, moderate, and strong biofilm formation based upon the following interpretation:

Cut-off OD (ODC) = Average OD of negative control wells + 3 × standard deviation of negative control wells

Data analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using R (version 4.3.3) via RStudio. Descriptive statistics were used to calculate prevalence and resistance rates. To account for the sample size, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for key proportions were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson exact method. Fisher’s exact test was used to assess associations between strong biofilm formation (as determined by the MTP assay) and key AMR phenotypes (MDR with resistance to ≥3 classes, MDR with resistance to ≥4 classes, and presumptive MRSA status). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence and identification of S. aureus

Of the 134 milk samples from clinical mastitis cases, 32 (23.9%; 95% CI: 17.0–32.0) were confirmed to be positive for S. aureus. All 32 isolates showed characteristic growth on MSA, were Gram-positive, catalase-positive, coagulase-positive, and were definitively identified by the amplification of the 270 bp nuc gene (Fig. 1).

Antimicrobial resistance profile

The highest rates of resistance were observed against ampicillin (78.1%), nalidixic acid (75.0%), and enrofloxacin (62.5%). In contrast, isolates showed the highest susceptibility to ceftriaxone (56.3%) and doxycycline (43.8%). Resistance to cefoxitin, the surrogate marker for methicillin resistance, was observed in 14 (43.8%) of the isolates (Table 1).

MDR and presumptive MRSA

Based on resistance to agents from seven different antimicrobial classes, 27 of 32 isolates (84.4%; 95% CI: 67.2–94.7) were classified as MDR (resistant to ≥ 3 classes) (Table 2). Notably, 14 isolates (43.8%) were resistant to cefoxitin and were thus classified as presumptive MRSA. All 14 of these MRSA isolates were also MDR.

Evaluation of biofilm formation

The biofilm-forming ability of the 32 isolates varied across the different methods (Fig. 2). In the CRA method, only 7 (21.9%) of isolates had a dry, black, crystalline appearance i.e., exhibited positive results for biofilm formation. In the quantitative MTP assay, 5 (15.6%) isolates were strong biofilm producers, 4 (12.5%) were moderate, and 11 (34.4%) were weak producers. Association between biofilm formation and AMR

Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the relationship between strong biofilm formation (n=5) and AMR phenotypes. There was no statistically significant association between strong biofilm formation and the overall MDR status (resistance to ≥3 classes) (p = 0.65). However, a significant association was found between strong biofilm formation and high-level MDR (resistance to ≥4 classes) (p = 0.041). Furthermore, a highly significant association was observed between strong biofilm formation and presumptive MRSA status (p = 0.007).

Discussion

This study provides a crucial update on the prevalence, AMR profiles, and biofilm-forming capacity of S. aureus from clinical bovine mastitis in Chitwan, Nepal. The prevalence of S. aureus was found to be 23.9 % (n = 134). Our observed prevalence was higher than the 15.2 % (n = 191) found in an earlier study conducted in Chitwan (Shrestha et al., 2021). The varied rate is probably due to differences in milk samples analyzed, though S. aureus is established as a major mastitis pathogen. In India, Shah et al. (2025) reported an overall S. aureus prevalence of 42.5% (n = 200), which is notably higher than ours. In contrast, Liu et al. (2022) observed a prevalence of about 24.8% (n = 125) among clinical mastitis cases from a Chinese dairy farm. Such variation may result from differences in the type of milk samples (clinical vs. subclinical) used for bacterial isolation as well as regional differences in pathogen distribution.

The AMR profile of our isolates is highly concerning. Resistance to commonly used antibiotics was strikingly high, with 78.1% of isolates resistant to ampicillin. Similar elevated resistance was shown to nalidixic acid (75.0%) and enrofloxacin (62.5%). These findings likely reflect the intense selective pressure created by the widespread and often unregulated use of these drugs in veterinary practice across the region (Acharya & Wilson, 2019). The situation is further complicated by the exceptionally high prevalence of MDR, observed in 84.4% of isolates, which severely limits the range of effective empirical treatment options available to veterinarians. Such resistance not only increases the likelihood of treatment failure and prolonged infection but also exacerbates economic losses for dairy farmers due to decreased productivity and increased culling rates (Bose & Siva Kumar, 2025).

MRSA was detected in 43.8% of our isolates, which is substantially higher than some previous reports from Nepal (Adhikari et al., 2023; Shrestha et al., 2021). These data suggest a potentially worsening resistance scenario and has important implications for both animal and public health. Given the zoonotic potential of MRSA, such a high prevalence poses a significant threat to farm workers, veterinarians, and milk handlers. The fact that all presumptive MRSA isolates were also MDR underscores the severity of the problem. These “superbugs” are difficult to treat and have the potential to spread between animals and humans, amplifying the risk of hard-to-control infections (Ippolito et al., 2010).

Notably, we observed a link between virulence and antimicrobial resistance. Although not all of the MDR isolates produced robust biofilms, the strongest biofilm phenotypes were shown by the most resistant isolates. This pattern included those classified as high-level MDR (resistance to ≥4 classes) and those with presumptive MRSA profiles. The trend suggests a synergistic relationship in which the most resistant isolates are optimally adapted to persist in the mammary gland.

Biofilms are known to enhance antimicrobial resistance through several mechanisms, such as acting as a physical barrier to antibiotic penetration, sheltering metabolically inactive persister cells, and facilitating horizontal gene transfer (Samrot et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2023). These results suggest that robust biofilm-forming isolates may be more likely to acquire or retain multiple resistance determinants and induce a cycle of enduring infection alongside resistance. These strains pose such a serious threat due to their combination of persistent treatment failure and an increased ability to sustain chronic mastitis.

Limitations of the study and future research directions

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The use of a cross-sectional design allows for a single time-point assessment which hinders the ability to monitor temporal changes in antimicrobial resistance or biofilm prevalence. The sampling site was limited to the Chitwan district, which, along with the non-probability purposive sampling method, may restrict the external validity of the results to other dairy-producing regions of Nepal. While the species identity of S. aureus was confirmed molecularly with nuc gene PCR, the identification of MRSA in the strain was phenotypically based on CLSI recommended cefoxitin disk diffusion and lacks molecular confirmation of the mecA or mecC genes. Additionally, biofilm formation was evaluated phenotypically without analysis of associated genetic determinants, such as the ica operon.

Regardless of these constraints, the study is a valuable source of phenotypic surveillance data from a resource-constrained setting, which is complemented by molecular confirmation of the S. aureus species identity. Future research should broaden the sampling to include several other key dairy-producing regions to provide a more nationally representative picture of the dairy industry. Investigating the genetic determinants of AMR, virulence factors, and biofilm formation alongside more advanced techniques such as whole genome sequencing could provide valuable insights. Other promising avenues include the development of innovative control strategies, such as bacteriophage therapy, anti-biofilm agents, alternative antimicrobials, and vaccines targeting MDR and biofilm-forming S. aureus, which are urgently needed (Kumar, Mahato, Ch & Kumbham, 2025)

Conclusions

Our findings provide critical evidence of the dual threat posed by S. aureus in bovine mastitis in Chitwan, Nepal. We identified a high prevalence of MDR (84.4%) and presumptive MRSA (43.8%) isolates. Crucially, strong biofilm formation was significantly associated with high-level MDR and presumptive MRSA. This co-occurrence suggests that standard antibiotic susceptibility tests may fail to predict clinical outcomes for these persistent, biofilm-forming pathogens. These findings underscore the urgent need for an integrated One Health approach, combining strict antimicrobial stewardship, enhanced farm hygiene practices, and the development of novel anti-biofilm strategies to effectively control bovine mastitis and reduce associated public health risks in the region.
