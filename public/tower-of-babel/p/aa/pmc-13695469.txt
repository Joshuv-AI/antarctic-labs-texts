Disinformation and misinformation in epilepsy: An analysis of multiplatform short‐form social media video content
Authors: Maggie St‐Pierre, Jimmy Li, Dènahin Hinnoutondji Toffa, Alexis Robin, Dang Khoa Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveShort‐form social media content is increasing in popularity but is at risk for propagating health‐related disinformation/misinformation. We aimed to quantify epilepsy‐related disinformation/misinformation on three such platforms: TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts.MethodsWe used the term “epilepsy” on the native search function of TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts to construct a videobase of 250 videos per platform. Videos unavailable at time of analysis were removed. Three evaluators watched the remaining videos to extract data and met to resolve disagreements. Off‐topic, duplicate, and animal videos were excluded. Videos were then categorized as follows: stigmatization, educational, medical advice, personal experience, joke/entertainment, oblique reference, opinion, and advice. Videos were further classified based on if there was any disinformation/misinformation. Videos that presented clinical spells were classified based on if these were likely epileptic seizures or functional/dissociative seizures (FDS). Disinformation/misinformation and FDS prevalence were compared using 95% confidence intervals (CI) among platforms.ResultsA total of 677 videos were analyzed (2016–2025). After exclusion criteria, 563 videos remained, 167 from TikTok, 196 from Instagram Reels and 200 from YouTube Shorts. Most videos were categorized as personal experience or educational. In total, 102 videos (18.1% [14.9–21.3]) were classified as containing disinformation/misinformation; TikTok has the highest proportion (31.7% [24.7%‐38.8]). Furthermore, 115 videos presented any form of clinical spell. Among these, 78 (67.8% [59.3–76.4]) were likely FDS. Instagram Reels had the most PNES videos (88.5% [76.2–100.0]).SignificanceDisinformation/misinformation regarding epilepsy is seen in almost a fifth of videos on short‐form content platforms, rising to almost a third on TikTok specifically. More than half of videos depicting spells are FDS. More research is required on the consequences of these videos on public perceptions of epilepsy and on how individuals understand their epilepsy/FDS diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Disinformation refers to the deliberate dissemination of false information with the intent to enforce a certain agenda. 1 Misinformation, on the other hand, refers to the involuntary dissemination of such false information. 1 Both are common on social media platforms and can involve different topics, including healthcare. Short‐form video platforms, such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, have especially risen in popularity in the last few years, becoming potential vehicles for health‐related disinformation and misinformation.

There are reports of disinformation and misinformation being spread on epilepsy across social media platforms. 2 Epilepsy is a common neurological condition characterized by a predisposition for unprovoked seizures that requires appropriate medical support and counseling. 3 People with epilepsy (PWE) may develop an erroneous understanding of their condition when exposed to false information, which may affect how they decide to manage their epilepsy. The propagation of disinformation and misinformation can also lead to further stigmatization of PWE.

The impacts and prevalence of epilepsy disinformation and misinformation on newer short‐form content platforms are not well understood. Short‐form content consists of videos typically lasting <3 min, designed to convey messages concisely and engagingly, aligning with the evermore rapid content consumption habits on social media platforms. 4 This format is ubiquitous on platforms like TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, which encourage the creation and virality of this type of content. 5 The short content format is well‐suited to mobile platforms and fosters instant engagement through likes, shares, and comments. Short‐form content platforms are particularly popular among young people. 5 Given that the incidence of epilepsy is high in this age group, better characterizing how epilepsy information is disseminated on these platforms is important. 6 In this study, we aimed to characterize the prevalence and key characteristics of epilepsy‐related disinformation and misinformation on short‐form content platforms, comparing TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data sources

Short‐form video platforms have become increasingly popular in the last decade and are mostly used by younger demographics. 7 We chose to analyze TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, the most popular short‐form content platforms in the Western world.

TikTok, a social media platform launched in 2017 by ByteDance, 8 allows users to create and view short videos with music and effects. It has rapidly gained worldwide popularity, including millions of active users in North America, where it is especially popular among young people. Instagram Reels is a feature on Instagram that allows users to create and share short videos, typically ranging from 15 to 90 s, 9 essentially providing an alternative to TikTok while being embedded within a large, pre‐existing social media platform. YouTube Shorts performs the same function as Instagram Reels but on YouTube. YouTube provides its creators with an opportunity to reach a wide audience while monetizing their content through the YouTube Partner Program, which now includes revenue sharing from advertisements displayed on Shorts. 10

The analysis of TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts is essential due to their growing popularity and significant influence on information dissemination. These social media platforms have become key channels for sharing medical information. With their large audiences and viral sharing mechanisms, these platforms can amplify the reach of information, whether accurate or misleading.

Data selection

TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts all have a search function where the user may input a term and search for videos which share the same term in their titles, keywords, hashtags, audio, captions, and user activity, with some variance in the algorithm between the three platforms. Results are then filtered based on relevance and personalized to the interests of the user. We searched TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts for epilepsy short‐form videos using the free text term “epilepsy.” We chose such a broad, English search term to capture the widest and most international range of videos concerning epilepsy.

For this search, we used a new, blank account. This approach was preferred so that results were least influenced by browser cookies or the usual content preferences associated with regular use of these apps. Starting with a blank account eliminates personalized biases that could otherwise skew the data and enhances reproducibility. 11 A total of 750 video URLs were recorded, 250 from each social media platform, and this prespecified number was chosen to surpass sample sizes achieved by past similar research. 12 , 13 This URL recording phase was conducted on June 8th, 2025, using an in‐house Python code to automate the procedure.

With the 750 URLs, the total number of videos which were analyzed was lower since 73 were unavailable at the time of analysis. The remaining 677 videos were analyzed, and several exclusion criteria were applied. Videos were excluded if there (1) only involved non‐human subjects, (2) were completely off‐topic (e.g., a video which used an epilepsy tag only to gain more viewers but that did not describe epilepsy or epilepsy‐adjacent subjects in the slightest), or (3) were duplicates of others.

Data extraction

Three evaluators (M. S‐P., J.L., and A.R.) independently viewed each video. These evaluators included a student living with epilepsy, a resident physician in adult neurology, and an epileptologist. A standardized Excel form was used by each evaluator to collect the following data: (1) whether the video seemed to contain disinformation or misinformation (yes or no) and (2) the category of information provided in the video. Possible video categories were as follows, adapted from a similar work on YouTube videos 12 : stigmatization, educational, medical advice, personal experience, joke/entertainment, oblique reference, opinion, and advice (see Table 1 for a detailed description of each). One same video could have several categories attributed to it. We did not differentiate disinformation from misinformation, as to do so would require knowledge of the intentions of the person disseminating the information, which we did not have. One evaluator was additionally tasked with retrieving the number of views each video received, the video title, the date of publication, the video length, and the social media platform where each video was published.

After data were extracted by each evaluator, disagreements were handled to create a finalized, homogeneous dataset. Videos were classified as containing disinformation/misinformation if they contained information in contradiction to clinical recommendations. Frequent scenarios included, for example, promotion of unverified treatments, confusion between epilepsy and functional neurological disorders without clinical nuance, and claims presented without scientific basis as medical advice. Of note, if a video depicted a functional/dissociative seizure (FDS) without explicitly branding it as an epileptic seizure, this was not considered disinformation/misinformation. For whether a video contained disinformation/misinformation or not, if the senior evaluator and one more evaluator agreed, that which was agreed upon would automatically proceed to the final dataset. If only the two junior evaluators were in agreement, disagreeing with the senior evaluator, the three evaluators would meet to rewatch the video and make a choice. The same procedure was applied for disagreements in regard to whether a video should be excluded or not. As for video categorization, when at least two evaluators attributed a specific category to a video, this category would proceed to the final dataset. If only one evaluator attributed a category to a video, this category would be dropped. The exception to this rule was when a video only had divergent categories (i.e., every evaluator disagreed with one another). In these cases, the evaluators would meet to make a choice. An interrater agreement with a 95% Wilson confidence interval was calculated for disinformation/misinformation decisions between the three evaluators.

Lastly, the senior evaluator was tasked with tagging which videos presented clinical spells and evaluating whether these spells were likely epileptic seizures or FDS.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were first carried out to summarize all the extracted data, first as a whole and then by social media platform. Continuous data are presented as median (interquartile range), and categorical/binary data are presented as count (proportion). In our comparative analyses, we calculated the proportion of videos containing disinformation or misinformation, by platform and by video category, with 95% confidence intervals. We compared the proportion of videos showing FDS (among the videos presenting clinical spells) between platforms, with 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were carried out using R (version 4.5.2). 14 Ethics approval was waived due to all data being publicly available on the Internet.

RESULTS

The initial videobase was composed of 677 videos; 211 (31.1%) were from TikTok, 244 videos (36.0%) were from Instagram Reels, and 222 (32.8%) were from YouTube Shorts. From these, after exclusion criteria were applied (see Table 2 for details), 562 videos remained: 167 (29.7%) from TikTok, 195 (34.7%) from Instagram Reels, and 200 (35.6%) from YouTube Shorts. Across all platforms, the most frequent reason for exclusion was off‐topic content, accounting for 85 videos (74.6% of all excluded videos).

Descriptive analysis

The publication dates of the included videos ranged from February 12th, 2016, to June 5th, 2025, with a median publication date of August 22nd, 2024, indicating that the majority of the analyzed content was published in the last years (see Figure 1).

Videos published per 6‐month period by platform. No videos published between July 2016 and December 2019 were included in our videobase.

Video duration was comparable across platforms. On TikTok, the median video length was 46.0 s. Instagram videos showed an identical median length of 46.0 s, while YouTube videos were slightly shorter, with a median length of 42.5 s (see Table S1, Figures S1 and S2).

Clear differences were observed in audience reach across platforms (see Table S1, Figures S3 and S4). TikTok videos had the highest engagement, with a median of 230 800 views. In contrast, Instagram videos had a median view count of 49 100, whereas YouTube videos received substantially fewer views, with a median of 2096 views.

Content analysis

Table 3 and Figure 2 present the categorization of video content type across the three platforms. In brief, the most prevalent category was personal experience (45.3%; 95% CI: 41.2–49.4), followed by educational (35.7%; 95% CI: 31.7–39.7), oblique reference (13.5%; 95% CI: 10.7–16.3), and joke/entertainment (6.7%; 95% CI: 4.7–8.8). All other categories were infrequent (<5%). This general pattern held true for TikTok and Instagram Reels but not YouTube Shorts, where educational content (63.5%; 95% CI: 56.8–70.2) was the most common, followed by personal experience (28.0%; 95% CI: 21.8–34.2) and the rest of the content categories.

Disinformation and misinformation analysis

A total of 102 videos (18.1%; 95% CI: 14.9–21.3) were classified as containing disinformation or misinformation. The proportion of disinformation or misinformation varied across platforms. TikTok has the highest proportion (31.7%; 95% CI: 24.7–38.8), followed by Instagram Reels (16.8%; 95% CI: 11.6–22.1), and YouTube Shorts (8%; 95% CI: 4.2–11.8), as shown in Figure 3.

Proportion of videos containing disinformation or misinformation. TikTok had the highest proportion of videos with disinformation or misinformation, followed by Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts.

After excluding the advice and opinion categories, both of which only being applied to a single video each, the highest proportion of disinformation or misinformation was observed in the medical advice category (72.7%; 95% CI: 46.4–99). This was followed by personal experience (25.1%; 95% CI: 19.8–30.4), stigmatization (20%; 95% CI: 0–55.1), oblique reference (11.8%; 95% CI: 4.6–19.1), educational (10.4%; 95% CI: 6.2–14.7), and joke/entertainment (2.6%; 95% CI: 0–7.7), as shown in Table S2 and Figure S5.

The interrater agreement between the three evaluators for disinformation/misinformation was 83.5% (95% CI: 80.6–86.0).

FDS analysis

A total of 115 videos presented any form of clinical spell. Among these, 78 (67.8%; 95% CI 59.3–76.4) were deemed to be likely FDS (see Figure 4). Among the 26 videos depicting spells on Instagram Reels, 23 (88.5%; 95% CI: 76.2–100) were likely FDS, which was proportionately the highest. TikTok had 49/72 (68.1%; 95% CI: 57.3–78.8), and YouTube Shorts had 6/17 (35.3%; 95% CI: 12.6–58).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we sought to quantify the amount of disinformation and misinformation about epilepsy, comparing the short‐form video content platforms TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, using a sample of 200–250 videos per platform. Our findings highlight clear differences in the prevalence of disinformation and misinformation across the platforms, with certain variations according to the type of content displayed in the videos. These differences reflect not only the specific characteristics of each platform but also how users consume, produce, and engage with health‐related information.

TikTok has been growing much faster than Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts in recent years, generally boasting higher view counts and stronger user engagement. All three platforms share similar user demographics, with the most prevalent age group being young adults. 15 Our findings reflect the popularity of TikTok, with epilepsy content garnering a much higher median view count on TikTok vs. the two other platforms. The type of epilepsy content posted on these three platforms also tended to differ. Videos posted on Instagram Reels and TikTok predominantly showed personal experiences regarding epilepsy, while YouTube Shorts features a higher proportion of educational content. This trend aligns with observations from other researchers who have documented the prevalence of personal narratives on short‐video platforms, particularly for chronic or stigmatized health conditions. 16 Personal experience–based content serves a different function than educational material, often aiming to foster emotional connection, reduce feelings of isolation, and increase visibility of lived experiences with epilepsy. However, these benefits may come at the cost of reduced scientific accuracy, particularly when health information is communicated without medical expertise. Other types of content, such as stigmatizing content and content aimed at sharing an opinion, were proportionally rarer on all platforms.

Health‐related disinformation and misinformation are prevalent in general on short‐form content platforms. These platforms prioritize rapid engagement, emotional appeal, and simplified messaging. 17 In this context, nuanced medical information can be compressed, oversimplified, or easily taken out of its clinical context. Conversely, a longer format allows for more detailed explanations, making it better suited to educational content and potentially reducing the risk of misinformation. 18 The nature of epilepsy as a medical condition may further amplify these patterns. Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder that is often misunderstood, stigmatized, and surrounded by persistent myths regarding its causes, triggers, and management. Individuals sharing their experiences may seek validation or awareness rather than accuracy, which can inadvertently reinforce misconceptions. 19

Overall, 18.1% of the videos we analyzed contained some form of disinformation or misinformation. Examples included videos promoting non‐evidence‐based treatments, videos depicting likely FDS as epileptic seizures, and educational videos with erroneous information. Disinformation and misinformation were significantly more common in TikTok vs. the other two platforms, with almost a third of TikTok videos containing such information. YouTube Shorts, on the other hand, had only 8% of its videos tagged as disinformation or misinformation. Litterature is sparse regarding disinformation and misinformation about epilepsy on short‐form content platforms. Two smaller studies have reported that approximately half of epilepsy content on TikTok was misleading when analyzing videos showing spells and videos teaching first aid seizure management. 2 , 13 To our knowledge, no studies have specifically investigated Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts in this regard. The mechanisms behind the discrepancy in prevalence of disinformation and misinformation between platforms are complex. This discrepancy is likely due to differing userbases, algorithms to promote videos, platform culture, and content distribution. For instance, our study showed that videos presenting personal experiences were more likely to contain disinformation and misinformation than educational videos. Since TikTok videos were mostly based on personal experiences whereas YouTube Shorts videos were mostly educational, the distribution of content likely helped skew the quality of information in favor of YouTube Shorts.

The high prevalence of epilepsy disinformation/misinformation observed across various platforms can have significant clinical consequences. Exposure to inaccurate claims about antiseizure medications or alternative cures can impair treatment adherence, a key factor in seizure control. 20 Furthermore, misleading portrayals of seizures can influence perceptions of the condition even before medical consultation, thus delaying help‐seeking. These findings underscore the need for clinicians to proactively address patients' exposure to social media content and for epilepsy advocacy groups to actively disseminate accurate and accessible information on short‐video platforms. 21 Platform‐level moderation policies could also benefit from medical oversight tailored to each condition to limit the spread of dangerous neurological misinformation.

Our study showed that FDS were frequent (67.8% of all videos showing spells) on short‐form content platforms, more frequent than epileptic seizures on all platforms except for YouTube Shorts. Only one previous study to our knowledge investigated the misrepresentation of seizures as FDS on TikTok, with a proportion of 55%. 13 The case of FDS being misrepresented as epileptic seizures on these platforms is of particular importance, since awareness and acceptance of a FDS diagnosis are factors strongly tied to the eventual cessation of these events. 22 People using these platforms tend to be younger, which also aligns with the epidemiology of FDS. 23 FDS are events that may appear clinically similar to epileptic seizures but do not result from abnormal electrical brain activity associated with actual epilepsy. 24 Falsely disseminating information misrepresenting seizures as FDS can probably have a detrimental effect on viewers with FDS, reinforcing that their symptoms may rather be due to epilepsy and demoting trust in healthcare professionals. People with FDS often undergo a lengthy, gradual acceptance of the non‐epileptic nature of the events before appropriate psychotherapeutic care can begin. 24 This acceptance process can likely be affected by false information spread by social media influencers. In individuals with FDS, exposure to online narratives that present the diagnosis as illegitimate or purely psychological can worsen acceptance of the diagnosis and adherence to recommended treatment. 25 The presentation of paroxysmal spells without a clear distinction between FDS and epilepsy seizure can also reinforce the misconception among the general public that any visual manifestation resembling a seizure is necessarily an epileptic seizure. Without a detailed neurological explanation or medical context, such simplification risks perpetuating or amplifying misconceptions about epilepsy, particularly among audiences without medical training. This in turn may contribute to the stigmatization of PWE.

Our study featured a few limitations. First, although we sought to analyze 250 videos per platform, we analyzed a lesser number in practice due to several videos becoming unavailable at time of analysis. Still, our videobase was comprised of more than 200 videos per platform, which is a higher sample size than similar previous published works. 2 , 13 Second, we saved the URLs videos based on a blank account for each platform, but in reality, most users will be searching and watching videos tailored to their consumption patterns. One could therefore expect that a person who frequently consumes health‐related disinformation or misinformation may be more likely to be exposed to similar such content. Our results are therefore not wholly generalizable to the average user. Third, we could not reasonably make a distinction between disinformation and misinformation, given that the distinction relies on the intent of the information disseminator, which lies beyond our data collection capacities. Fourth, it is sometimes difficult to differentiate based on semiology alone FDS from epileptic seizures (e.g., frontal lobe seizures). This is why we chose to phrase spells as likely FDS or likely epileptic seizures. Also, only the senior evaluator, who is a board‐certified epileptologist, was tasked with making this distinction. Fifth, categorizing videos based on their content can be a somewhat subjective process. We attempted to counteract this by having three independent evaluators screen the videos.

CONCLUSION

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological condition that often remains poorly understood by the general public. Social media platforms now play a central role in the dissemination of information, both true and false, related to this condition. In this study, almost a fifth of epilepsy‐related videos on short‐form content platforms contained disinformation or misinformation, and this was worse on TikTok vs. other platforms. Type of content also varied; TikTok and Instagram Reels were largely dominated by personal experience–based videos, while YouTube Shorts was characterized by a strong predominance of educational videos. When videos presented clinical spells, more than half of the time, these were likely FDS. The exception was YouTube Shorts, which had more epileptic seizures than FDS. Overall, these findings highlight the need for greater availability of reliable, scientifically validated educational content on epilepsy across short‐form social media. The consequences of epilepsy disinformation and misinformation on social media are probably far‐reaching, from increasing stigmatization of PWE to impeding the process of diagnosis acceptance in people with FDS. One can imagine that consequences may be even more drastic if harmful medical advice is misrepresented as legitimate.
