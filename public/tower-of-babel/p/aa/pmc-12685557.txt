Protic small molecule bioregulators
Authors: Amanda G Davis, Michael D Pluth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall molecule gases such as nitric oxide (NO), carbon monoxide (CO), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) have long been recognized as endogenous signaling molecules with diverse physiological roles. Often described as “gasotransmitters”, these molecules complement other small molecule bioregulators (SMBs) that exert biological function across all kingdoms of life. One underappreciated distinction, however, is that many of these molecules – irrespective of whether or not they are gases in their native states outside of biology – exhibit similar molecular signaling potential mediated by protonation-dependent chemical speciation. In this review, we propose the new cross-cutting classification of protic small molecule bioregulators (PSMBs) to describe molecules in which biological function and reactivity are modulated by protonation state. Examples of PSMBs include the canonical gasotransmitter H2S, emerging gasotransmitters (H2Se, HCN), small molecule crosstalk species (e.g., SNO−, SSNO−, SO42−, ONOO−, NO2−, SCN−, OCl−), and other species where protonation state modulation is accessible at physiological pH. Importantly, these species exist in equilibrium between their neutral and anionic forms, with speciation governed by local pH and molecular environment, directly impacting their membrane nucleophilicity, permeability, redox activity, and interaction with metal centers. We describe the evolutionary origins, biosynthesis, and crosstalk of PSMBs, including roles in redox signaling, post-translational modification, and mitochondrial regulation. Reframing these important molecules in a class defined by their protic ability rather than gaseous state does not diminish prior gasotransmitter designations, but rather serves to recognize commonalities in chemical characteristics that drive the unique biological chemistry and regulation of these species.

Introduction

The prebiotic geologic landscape was much different than our current terrestrial environment, yet hints at the types of simple molecules that have been present throughout evolution. Although contemporary metabolism functions in an oxygen-rich environment, the prebiotic atmosphere was more reducing and likely abundant in volcanic gases, such as CO2, SO2, H2S, and CO. These and related small molecules likely served as foundational feedstocks that contributed to the emergence of early complex biomolecules. The reducing atmospheric conditions also impeded the accumulation of UV-blocking ozone in the atmosphere, enabling rich photochemical reaction pathways that would otherwise be absent under contemporary atmospheric conditions. Work aimed at probing accessible reaction pathways under prebiotic conditions support pathways for nucleobase and amino acid synthesis ranging from photochemical reactions to catalysis by simple metal-supported motifs. For example, HCN formed through atmospheric UV-irradiation and high energy electrical storms, likely served as a significant feedstock for prebiotic nucleobase synthesis [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Similarly, classic experiments, such as the eponymous Miller-Urey experiment, have demonstrated that a simulated primordial reducing atmosphere containing methane (CH4), ammonia (NH3), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and hydrogen (H2) can provide the chemical feedstocks for prebiotic amino acid synthesis [[5], [6], [7], [8]].

Expanding beyond these atmospheric gases, the high-pressure and anoxic conditions found in mineral- and sulfide-rich hydrothermal vents may have generated iron-sulfur clusters as early examples of enzymatic cofactors [9]. These iron-sulfur clusters likely provided prebiotic catalytic subunits and opened new opportunities for redox transformations [10,11]. Over time, these coordination environments supporting iron-sulfur clusters evolved to house ferredoxins found in early archaea and modern day bacteria, now playing important roles in metabolism, photosynthesis, and N2/CO2 fixation and reduction [12,13]. As archaea evolved under otherwise anoxic conditions, the development of microbial photosynthesis led to the transformation of the weakly reducing atmosphere into a more oxidizing one with abundant free oxygen (O2) [14,15]. Referred to as the Great Oxidation Event, this rise of atmospheric O2 eventually led to the devastation of anaerobic microbes allowing for the rise of early aerobic eukaryotes [16,17]. Despite these evolutionary shifts, modern biology continues to rely on small molecule building blocks to support a wide array of physiological processes.

Small molecule precursors are essential for building larger biomolecules and structures, for cellular signaling, and for maintaining cellular homeostasis. For example, ATP synthase catalyzes the conversion of free phosphate (HPO42−) and ADP into ATP for molecular energy storage. Broadening its relevance, when phosphate is trapped in a Ca/OH matrix it forms hydroxyapatite, which is the primary structural component of bone. Beyond structural roles, a variety of endogenous small molecules play roles in diverse signaling pathways across different organ systems. For example, NO, CO, and H2S are often referred to as “gasotransmitters” because they are endogenously generated and act on molecular targets at biologically relevant concentrations. The biological roles of these molecules are broad, with many investigations demonstrating key roles in cardioprotection and inflammation as well as both independent and codependent roles in biological processes including vasodilation and angiogenesis [18,19]. While this “Gasotransmitter Club” is rather exclusive, other small molecule bioregulators (SMBs) also play fundamental and crucial roles across different organisms. For example, methane (CH4), carbonyl sulfide (COS), hydrogen selenide (H2Se), hydrogen cyanide (HCN), and other small molecule gases are receiving increased attention for the established or proposed roles in contemporary biology [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]].

One challenge in including other molecules within the gasotransmitter classification, is the requirement of a natural gaseous physical state and demonstrated biological activity within the organism of generation. Despite the abundance of SMBs, not all meet these specific requirements. Clearly O2 is an important SMB but is not considered a gasotransmitter since it is not produced endogenously. Similarly, many small di- and tri-atomic molecules are important SMBs but are not gases, so are also excluded. Rather than focusing on a specific physical state outside of biology, the chemical properties that influence biological function, such as protonation state, redox reactivity, and membrane permeability may be more useful criteria in grouping SMBs. Taking H2S as an example, the lipophilicity of H2S in its neutral diprotic form likely enhances membrane permeability, whereas its more prevalent anionic form (hydrosulfide, HS−) significantly enhances nucleophilicity and metallophilicity, enabling participation in redox reactions and interaction with metal centers. Further supporting this idea, the partitioning coefficient for H2S (pKa = 7.0) in octanol/water at pH 3.8 is 2.1, meaning that it is about twice as soluble in organic solvents than water. Moving to pH 7.4, the partitioning coefficient drops to 0.64, which is consistent with the anionic HS− being more soluble in water than organic solvent [25]. These factors contribute to the high membrane permeability coefficient for H2S (3 cm s−1), which further supports the importance of the hydrophobic neutral H2S state in passive diffusion from the site of origin. One could also argue that H2S should not be considered a gasotransmitter since it is primarily in its anionic, non-gaseous HS− form at pH 7.4. This protonation-dependent modulation of function is distinct from the other canonical gasotransmitters NO and CO, but aligns more closely with other SMBs in which the protonation state impacts function and activity. Based on these considerations, we propose the new classification of protic small molecule bioregulators (PSMBs) as an under-recognized important class of small molecules in which chemical and physiological function are governed by direct protonation (or deprotonation) (Fig. 1).

Notably, many important small molecule bioregulators (including gasotransmitters) are not gases under common physiological conditions. Rather, a common feature of many small molecule bioregulators is their protic nature, which allows for changes in charge and ionization upon protonation or deprotonation. Although physiological pH is often regarded as ∼7.4, various cellular compartments expand this range from pH 4.7 in the lysosome to pH 8 in mitochondria. Moreover, local microenvironments within proteins or membranes can substantially alter the apparent pKa of functional groups and further impact speciation. For example, cysteine residues can be significantly acidified by providing electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions to complement the stability of the deprotonated thiolate [26]. Similarly, different physiological events (e.g., ischemia, which can decrease the tissue/cellular pH to ∼6.5), can result in tissue acidification an likely impact speciation and diffusion rates across membranes.

At physiological pH, many PSMBs are either fully or partially deprotonated. Considering the full physiological pH range, it becomes clear that many of the anionic conjugate base forms of PSMBs are chemically accessible (Fig. 2) and are likely to exist at sufficient concentrations to drive accumulation or reactivity by Le Châtelier's principle. For example, a PSMB that has a pKa that is three units more acidic than the pH of an organelle would still have 0.1 % speciation in the neutral protonated form, which should be readily sufficient to drive transport. This equilibrium could be further shifted, however, depending on the local molecular environment allowing for greater accumulation or reactivity of either the neutral protonated or anionic deprotonated form. Table 1 highlights the expected speciation in various subcellular compartments based on the local pH values and pKa of a selection of PSMBs. The coexistence and interconversion of these protonation states likely contributes to their capacity to serve as signaling molecules and enables both membrane permeability (through neutral species) and enhanced reactivity (through anionic forms). This dual functionality enables molecular translocation, post-translational modifications, and intermolecular crosstalk, all of which are defining characteristics of PSMBs. In this review, our goal is to highlight the importance of PSMBs by outlining the defining characteristics, intermolecular crosstalk pathways, and physiological relevance of select species. We propose that PSMBs represent a key and underappreciated class of small molecules with biological functions that are fundamentally shaped by their inherent acid-base properties.

Nitric oxide and related reactive nitrogen species (RNS)

Since its discovery as the endothelial derived relaxing factor (EDRF), the biological roles of NO have been demonstrated to include immune system signaling, mitochondrial respiration, cellular adhesion, platelet function, cardioprotection, and a wide array of other functions [18,19]. These and other wide-ranging biological activities have been reviewed in detail in prior reports [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. Mammalian NO is generated endogenously from l-arginine by three different nitric oxide synthases (NOS) isoforms. These include neuronal NOS (nNOS) found in neuronal tissue where it regulates neurotransmission, smooth muscle relaxation, and vasodilation; inducible NOS (iNOS) found in immune and inflammatory cells where it mediates host defense and immune responses; and endothelium NOS (eNOS) found in vascular endothelial cells and responsible for maintaining vasodilation and blood pressure homeostasis [34]. This combination of enzymatic synthesis and action on molecular targets at physiological concentration made NO the first recognized gasotransmitter and sparked interest in investigating other reactive small molecules (and gases) in biology.

The biological chemistry of nitric oxide is also inherently intertwined with other small molecules. For example, some NO cardioprotective pathways are codependent on H2S, which suggests the formation of potential S/N hybrid crosstalk species (vide infra) [35]. One well-established example of NO/sulfur crosstalk is the nitrosylation of biological thiols to generate nitrosothiols (RSNO), which serve as both more stable NO carriers and as reservoirs of NO. Nitrosothiols have longer cellular lifetimes than free NO and also participate in redox-sensitive signaling pathways. Perhaps more importantly, S-nitrosylation is an important NO-mediated oxidative post-translational modification involved in various processes ranging from nitrosylation of hemoglobin during hypoxic vasodilation to the activation of inflammatory responses through nitrosylation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB).

In addition to interactions with adjacent small molecules, the inherent oxygen sensitivity of NO enables a rich redox landscape of reactive nitrogen species (RNS), ranging from the highly reactive dinitrogen trioxide (N2O3), which mediates many S- and N-nitrosation reactions, to more stable anions such as nitrite (NO2−, pKa (HNO2) = 3.3) and nitrate (NO3−, pKa (HNO3) = −1.3). The physiological effects of NO and NO2− are closely linked, and the ease of interconversion between the two species often complicates efforts to parse specific biological roles. Under high O2 tension and more basic pH, NO2− predominates and functions as a “hypoxic buffer,” regulating hypoxic vasodilation, hypoxic mitochondrial respiration, and ischemia-reperfusion tissue injury [36]. Highlighting the impact of protonation state, work with chloroplast inner envelope vesicles has shown a pH dependent rate of NO2− transfer, and membrane permeability coefficients in the chloroplast envelope were measured to be 0.15 cm s−1 and 2 x 10−6 cm s−1, for HNO2 and NO2−, respectively [37,38]. Moreover, NO2− has been demonstrated to increase cyclic GMP (cGMP) levels, inhibit cytochrome P450 activity, and modulate protein expression, all of which are also associated with NO [39]. This biochemical overlap is further complicated by analytical challenges, with widely used methods for NO detection, such as the Griess Assay, primarily measuring NO2− rather than NO directly. Similarly, many fluorescent probes for NO rely on N-nitrosation reactions, which stem from N2O3 or other electrophilic oxidized NO-derivatives rather than NO directly [40,41].

Moving to the fully oxidized form of NO, NO3− is abundant in the environment and is primarily acquired from dietary sources. Although some evidence supports eukaryotic nitrate reductases [42], a primary method for reducing NO3− to NO2− occurs via nitrate reductases found in symbiotic oral bacteria [43]. This NO2− can then be further reduced to NO under hypoxic conditions, but also by endogenous nitrite reductases as well as by non-enzymatic, acid-dependent mechanisms in the stomach (Scheme 1) [44]. In addition, NO can also be oxidized to NO3− by oxygenated hemeproteins via NO-dioxygenation [45,46].

Different oxidized and reduced forms of NO-related species.

One property that distinguishes NO from other gasotransmitters is that it is a radical. NO is connected to several 1e– reduced and oxidized species, including nitroxyl (HNO), nitroxide anion (NO−), and nitrosonium (NO+), with the latter rapidly converting to NO2− under aqueous physiological conditions (Scheme 1) [31]. The deprotonation of HNO to NO− is unusual in that the pKa depends on the spin-state of both the acid and conjugate base. The spin-forbidden 1HNO to 3NO− deprotonation has a pKa of 11.3, whereas the spin-allowed deprotonations of 1HNO to 1NO− (pKa = 23) and 3HNO to 3NO− (pKa = −1.8) differ significantly [47]. More broadly, HNO has in some cases been postulated as an alternative signaling agent for NO with analogous cardiovascular functions [[48], [49], [50]]. Endogenous formation of HNO can derive from the reaction of NO with hydroxylamine (NH2OH) [51] or through reactions with N-hydroxyguanidine [52], ubiquinol [53], or even free Fe(II) [54]. Additionally, Fe(II) has been demonstrated to produce nitrous oxide (N2O), better known as laughing gas, via reduction of NO through an NO− intermediate [54]. Additionally, HNO can react with thiols in a sulfhydryl oxidation to form a RSNHOH intermediate [55]. Highlighting this diverse reactivity and interaction with adjacent small molecules, NO− can also react with molecular oxygen (O2) to generate peroxynitrite (ONOO−), which is a potent oxidant involved in immune response (vide infra) [56].

The breadth of redox chemistry and spin-state versatility of NO supports a broad suite of RNS, many of which can interact directly with other SMBs, protein thiols, and reactive oxygen species (ROS). This extensive reactivity contributes both to the diversity of NO-dependent signaling as well as its central role in crosstalk with other regulatory pathways. These properties likely explain why NO was the earliest member of the gasotransmitter family and remains a cornerstone in the study of reactive signaling molecules.

Hydrogen sulfide and related reactive sulfur species (RSS)

H2S is unique amongst the canonical gasotransmitters in the breadth of oxidation states available to sulfur, ranging from −2 (H2S) to +6 (SO42−), which allows access to diverse chemistry across a large swath of biologically relevant organic and inorganic compounds. As a relic from Earth's ancient anoxic environment, sulfate-reducing microorganisms (SRMs) play an essential role in the sulfur and carbon cycles by metabolizing various sulfur compounds [57]. SRMs can reduce oxidized sulfur species, such as sulfate (SO42−), sulfite (SO32−), and thiosulfate (S2O32−) through various processes to produce H2S [57,58]. Even today, SO42− is one of the most abundant anions in human plasma (300 μM) and plays important roles in detoxification pathways, substrate activation, and tissue and brain development [59].

Building from this evolutionary past, it is perhaps unsurprising that H2S, classically known for its signature rotten egg smell, is also an established gasotransmitter [60]. Although often written in the neutral, diprotic form, H2S has a pKa of 7.0, meaning that under physiological conditions, the majority of H2S exists as hydrosulfide (HS−). As outlined earlier, the neutral H2S form is more membrane permeable than anionic HS− [25,61], likely allowing for greater passive diffusion through membranes and away from the site of origin. In its anionic SH− form, however, sulfide can participate in additional reactivity not available to neutral H2S, including reaction with disulfide bonds, nitrosothiols, sulfenic acids, and other electrophiles. In eukaryotes, H2S is formed endogenously by both enzymatic and nonenzymatic pathways involving cysteine or other oxidized sulfide (Scheme 2). H2S is formed endogenously primarily by three primary enzymes: cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE), cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (3-MST). CSE produces H2S through the conversion of l-cysteine or homocysteine into the corresponding persulfide, which is further reduced to form free H2S [62]. CBS has been shown to produce H2S through the condensation of cystathionine with homocysteine by a β-replacement reaction [63]. Alternatively, 3-MST, enabled by cysteine aminotransferase (CAT) metabolism, produces H2S via cysteine and α-ketoglutarate [64]. Recent studies also show that cysteinyl-tRNA synthetases (CARSs) can generate cysteine hydropersulfides, which can be subsequently reduced downstream to generate H2S [65]. Nonenzymatic H2S production occurs through the reduction of oxidized sulfur species, including persulfides (RSSH), polysulfides (RSn > 2H, H2Sn≥ 2), and thiosulfate (S2O32−) via NADH and glutathione mediated pathways.

Enzymatic and nonenzymatic pathways of H2S generation and related RSS.

As the biological activities of H2S continue to expand, it is now clear that H2S plays important and well established roles in cardioprotection and angiogenesis as well as supporting anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory systems [66]. For example, upregulation of endogenous H2S and/or treatment with exogenous sources improves cardiac function and reduces complications relating to various cardiovascular pathologies [66]. In porcine models of myocardial I/R injury, exogenous H2S treatment suppresses cardiomyocyte apoptosis and autophagy [67]. Highlighting the intertwined nature of H2S with O2 and reactive oxygen species (ROS), H2S has been proposed to be a potential O2-sensor by contributing to mitochondrial ROS scavenging [68]. H2S can also be oxidized by sulfur quinone oxidoreductase (SQR) to form a persulfide, which can then be oxidized to SO32− and eventually to S2O32− by sequential oxidation by sulfur dioxygenase (SDO) and SQR [69]. These oxidized sulfur species can ultimately be reduced back to H2S by endogenous reductants, completing the overall redox cycle [70].

H2S can also be oxidized to inorganic polysulfides (polysulfanides, H2Sn≥ 2), which can occur through various pathways and may also be mediated by enzymes like superoxide dismutase [71]. Once formed, polysulfides provide soluble and diffusible forms of reductant-labile H2S as well as sulfane sulfur sources. H2S2 has reported pKa values of 5.0 and 9.7, with H2S3 having values of 4.2 and 7.5, for the first and second deprotonations [72]. First pKa values as low as ∼3.5 have been reported for H2S8 [73]. This differential speciation at physiological pH is consistent with polysulfides also being classified as PSMBs. Because the acidity increases with increasing sulfur chain length, longer chain polysulfanes are likely found in the Sn2− state at physiological pH [74]. Aqueous solutions of polysulfides are unstable under aerobic conditions and oxidize to form S8 and S2O32−, including more complex reactivity in the presence of other thiol species. Furthermore, polysulfides, as well as other sulfane sulfur species, undergo rapid equilibration [75]. Taken together, these factors suggest that polysulfides are likely to contribute to a pool of soluble and rapidly interconverting sulfane sulfur species, rather than having distinct reactivity associated with specific signaling pathways.

H2S also engages in antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways through activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathways, mitigating ROS induced ferroptosis and mitochondrial apoptosis [76]. The role of H2S in modulating inflammation can occur by multiple mechanisms. For example, the above pathways can mediate inflammation by modulating response from inflammatory cytokines [77], and H2S can also activate K+ ion channels or inhibit Ca2+ and Cl− ion channels, which are essential for maintaining ionic homoeostasis related with inflammation [78]. Modulation of these ion channels can also attenuate heart arrhythmias and promote cell viability [79]. H2S also promotes angiogenesis [80] by upregulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and activating key kinases such as protein kinase B and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) [81]. Through its interaction with these cytokine pathways, ROS, and redox-responsive proteins, H2S functions not only as an important reactive sulfur species, but also as key regulator of redox-dependent signaling cascades.

H2S/NO Crosstalk Species: (Per)thionitrite (SNO−/SSNO−)

As outlined above, certain physiological processes are codependent on NO and H2S [35,[82], [83], [84]], implying the formation of intermediate crosstalk species. Two such candidates are thionitrous acid (HSNO) and perthionitrous acid (HSSNO), which have been proposed to act as reservoirs for both H2S and NO and release after decomposition or activation [[85], [86], [87]]. Both of these hybrid species are anions under physiological conditions, with estimated pKa values of 2.6–7.0 and 1.0–5.0 for HSNO/SNO− and HSSNO/SSNO−, respectively [88,89]. One example of this crosstalk involves the reaction of S-nitrosothiols with H2S to liberate thionitrite (SNO−) and NO [86,90,91]. Additionally, H2S and NO can react directly under oxidative conditions to form HSNO [92]. Exogenous nitrosothiols, such as S-nitrosopenicillamine (SNAP), have been shown to react with HS− to produce SSNO− through an SNO− intermediate (Scheme 3). Further expanding on this reactivity, SSNO− and its decomposition products, including NO and polysulfides (HSn−, Sn2− Sn•–, etc), stimulate soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC) and activate the Keap-1/Nrf2 pathway, both of which are traditionally considered NO- or H2S/NO-mediated pathways [[93], [94], [95]]. The high reactivity and limited stability of SNO− and SSNO−, including the decomposition of SSNO− (minutes to hours) in semi-aqueous conditions, likely prevents bulk accumulation of these species in free solution and instead favors the transient formation of these hybrid motifs at the intersection of NO and H2S signaling [96,97].

A selection of simplified reactions to generate and interconvert between SNO− and SSNO−.

Pathways for interconversion between SNO− and SSNO− have also been investigated under various conditions (Scheme 3). Prior work from our group using model systems has demonstrated that either protonation of SNO− or reaction with gaseous carbonyl sulfide (COS) can generate SSNO−, along with other downstream signaling molecules including N2O (via HNO), H2S, NO, and S0 (via polysulfides) [97]. Similarly, persulfides in the presence of NO2− can generate SSNO− through polysulfide and NO intermediates [98]. Additionally, prior work has shown that SNO− can be generated from SSNO− upon reaction with HS−, cyanide (CN−), and glutathione (GSH) [99]. These findings demonstrate the increasingly rich reactivity landscape of SNO− and SSNO−, which underscores their potential as transient signaling intermediates and highlight the need for further investigations into their abundance, specific signaling relevance, and participation in regulatory pathways.

Dioxygen and related reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Oxygen (O2) is one of the most studied gaseous small molecules, due to its central role in aerobic respiration and other physiological processes. Although not classified as a gasotransmitter, O2 is clearly an important gaseous transmitter with intertwined roles with other SMB signaling pathways. O2 is also not a PSMB, but associated metabolic or crosstalk byproducts, such as superoxide (O2•–), peroxynitrite (ONOO−), and hydroperoxide (HOO−), do fall within this classification based on their acid-base equilibria. The corresponding protonated forms of these species are hydrogen superoxide (HO2•, pKa = 4.9), peroxynitrous acid (HONOO, pKa ∼6.7), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, pKa = 11.6) (Scheme 4).

Example reactions involving generation and consumption of reactive oxygen species.

Many ROS were initially thought to be solely harmful metabolic byproducts but are now recognized as important regulators of signaling pathways and immune response. In addition, oxidative stress is mediated by redox signaling and plays important fundamental roles in biology, influencing processes such as hypoxia, inflammation, apoptosis, muscle contraction, and wound healing, among many others [[100], [101], [102]]. For example, O2•– is generated by NADPH oxidases through the reduction of O2 via NADPH oxidation. This toxic and highly reactive radical plays key roles in phagocyte host defense [103]. Excess O2•–, however, can cause DNA damage and deactivate iron-sulfur clusters leading to downstream generation of other ROS [104,105]. High and unregulated levels of O2•– can lead to oxidative stress and subsequent inflammation, with a number of enzymes, including superoxide dismutase II (SOD2), being responsible for efficient scavenging of O2•– to generate less toxic H2O2 or O2.

Beyond enzymatic pathways, NO can also react with O2•– to form the strong oxidant ONOO− [50]. Alternatively, ONOO− can be generated through reaction of NO− and O2 [56,106]. Protonation of ONOO− yields unstable peroxynitrous acid (ONOOH), which subsequently decomposes into other radical products such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2•) and HO•. The neutral ONOOH species has measured permeability coefficients of 0.4–1.3 x 10−3 cm s−1 depending on membrane model, which are ∼106 faster than the anionic ONOO− [107]. These relatively modest passive diffusion rates are likely complemented by active diffusion facilitated by aquaporins, with AQP6 and AQP9 being likely candidates for ONOO− transport, and aquaglyceroporins AQP3 and AQP8 likely candidates for facilitating ONOOH diffusion [108]. ONOO− is a potent oxidant, contributing to nitration protein tyrosine residues [106,109] as well as the oxidation of iron-sulfur clusters [105], zinc fingers [110], and various enzymes [111]. In fact, protein nitration can be used as a biomarker for certain neurodegenerative disease states such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [112,113]. In addition to the above pathways, ONOO− can react directly with CO2 to form an unstable nitrosoperoxylcarbonate (ONO2C(O)O−) intermediate, which can rearrange and release NO3− and CO2 or alternatively homolyze to form NO2• and carbonate radical anion (CO3•–) [[114], [115], [116]].

Hydrogen peroxide is the 1e– reduced derivative of O2•– and is generated endogenously as a byproduct of leaky mitochondrial respiration, superoxide dismutase, and NADPH or xanthine oxidase activity [117,118]. H2O2 also functions as a redox mediator by oxidizing specific protein thiols, and permeate membranes through both passive and active diffusion, with the latter often being mediated by aquaporins [119]. Various transcription factors [120] and protein tyrosine phosphatases [121] are also modulated by H2O2. Peroxiredoxins serve as endogenous H2O2 scavengers and have also been demonstrated to propagate downstream oxidative signaling to the redox-regulated transcription factor STAT3 [122]. Heme-based myeloperoxidases (MPO) also scavenge H2O2 and catalyze its reaction with Cl− to form hypochlorous acid (HClO, pKa = 7.6), which is a potent oxidant and chlorinating agent vital for immune response and pathogenic defense. Misregulation of HOCl, however, is a biomarker for various disease states including atherosclerosis, chronic inflammation, and some cancers [123]. HOCl can also damage tissue by chlorinating protein thiol and amino groups or by binding to heme [124]. Taken together, a recurring theme in O2 biology is its capacity to engage in redox reactions with other small molecules, modulating both transient and sustained signaling events, which significantly influence broader redox networks and crosstalk with other SMB pathways.

Hydrogen selenide and related reactive selenium species (RSeS)

Continuing down the chalcogen group, selenium is an essential dietary micronutrient and is found in trace amounts across biology. Despite the similarities to sulfur in terms of atomic size and electronegativity, selenium is substantially more reactive [125,126], likely due to its greater polarizability and distinct redox properties [127,128]. Similar to the other gasotransmitters, hydrogen selenide (H2Se) is a gas in its neutral form, is endogenously produced, and plays important roles in cardiovascular health [129], bone homeostasis [130], cancer [131,132], and other disease states [[133], [134], [135]]. Notably, H2Se (pKa = 3.9) is much more acidic than H2S, meaning that nearly all H2Se exists as hydroselenide (HSe−) at physiological pH. A number of recent reviews have highlighted the emerging molecular biology, redox biology and metabolism of selenium and related species (H2Se, Se-derived amino acids, and selenoproteins, etc) [128,132,[136], [137], [138]], and our focus here will be on the more narrow scope of small molecule reactive selenium species (RSeS).

Most selenium is acquired through diet in the form of inorganic selenate (SeO42−) and selenite (SeO32−), with smaller contributions from selenomethionine (SeMet). SeMet is metabolized in the transsulfuration pathway, resulting in the intermediate formation of selenohomocysteine (SeHcy) and selenocysteine (Sec) prior to the generation of H2Se and subsequent incorporation into selenoproteins [139]. Fully oxidized SeO42− is reduced into the intermediate SeO32−, and subsequently to elemental Se0 or H2Se via glutathione-mediated pathways along with other enzymatic and nonenzymatic processes (Scheme 5) [140,141]. Once formed, H2Se functions as a key intermediate in the biosynthesis of selenophosphate (HSePO32−), which is subsequently incorporated into Sec in selenoproteins by selenophosphate synthetase [[142], [143], [144]]. In addition, the conversion of H2Se and ATP to HSePO32− not only enables downstream Se incorporation, but also likely serves as a reservoir for bioactive selenium [145].

Summary of relevant reactive selenium species and associated biological chemistry.

Given the high reactivity and O2-sensitivity of H2Se, substantial pools of “free” H2Se are unlikely to exist in vivo, which underscores the necessity for tightly regulated biosynthetic and scavenging machinery to control endogenous H2Se levels. Disruption of this regulation, or excessive H2Se accumulation, is likely involved in disease states associated with selenoprotein biogenesis as well as Se bioaccumulation and toxicity. Although H2Se remains less well understood than NO, CO, or H2S, growing evidence supports its role as an emerging gasotransmitter. Much like H2S, however, the majority of H2Se is not a gas under physiological conditions, by rather a reactive and diffusible anion, making it clearly fit within the framework of PSMBs.

Hydrogen cyanide and related cyano species

Perhaps the most infamous of the small molecules discussed here, cyanide (CN−) is well known for its acute toxicity. Highlighting the toxicity as well as importance of protonation state, gaseous HCN is a common product from the combustion of carbon and nitrogen containing synthetic plastics and textiles and constitutes a major inhalation risk during fires, with toxicity after inhalation associated with binding of anionic CN− to Cytochrome c oxidase [146]. Despite this reputation, hydrogen cyanide (HCN, pKa = 9.4) has been recently shown to stimulate certain physiological pathways, including mitochondrial bioenergetics, at low concentrations [147]. HCN is produced endogenously through the HOCl-mediated oxidation of glycine (Scheme 6), and can also be acquired through diet with many plants, such as stone fruit, bitter almonds, and apples, containing cyanogenic glycoside natural products that are produced as a defense mechanism against herbivores [148,149].

Reactive cyano-compounds and their modes of generation.

Complementing these pathways, HCN is produced endogenously through a variety of pathways including as a byproduct of phagocytosis and within neuronal tissues [[150], [151], [152]]. Cyanide can also be produced by bacterial synthases or generated as downstream byproduct of myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity, which also has potential for subsequent formation of cyanate (OCN−, pKa (HOCN) = 3.9) [150,153,154]. Physiological CN− levels are tightly regulated through detoxification pathways, including scavenging by rhodanese to generate thiocyanate (SCN−, pKa (HSCN) = 1), and by additional routes involving conversion to formate or CO2 [155]. We note that the pKa of HSCN has been reported from −1.7 to 4.0, with the most common values appearing near pKa = 1. Highlighting the impact of protonation state on HSCN/SCN− membrane permeability, studies with egg phosphatidylcholine-decane bilayers under acidic conditions (pH ∼1–3) showed permeability coefficients of 2.6 cm s−1 and 5 x 10−9 cm s−1 for HSCN and SCN−, respectively [156]. These results highlight the impact of the neutral state in membrane transport and also support a pKa value near the range of investigation.

At high concentrations, CN− induces cytotoxic hypoxia by competitively binding to heme and inhibiting Cytochrome c oxidase (Cco/COX) within Complex IV in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, thus shutting down aerobic production of ATP [[157], [158], [159]]. Excess CN− can induce nuclear and mitochondrial DNA fragmentation [160,161], disrupt microtubules [162], and interfere with transcription factors leading to apoptosis [163,164]. Cyanide is also particularly damaging in the central nervous system by disrupting mitochondrial membrane potential [165], causing intracellular acidification [166], and inducing the release of other neurotransmitters from damaged cells, thereby exacerbating neurotoxic effects [167,168].

Despite this toxicity, more recent studies have also demonstrated that low levels of CN− are cytoprotective. These effects include calcium mobilization, NMDA receptor activation, and stimulation of neurotransmitter release [169,170]. Additionally, CN− can act as a mitochondrial activator by stimulating COX activity, elevating ATP levels, and increasing cell proliferation [147]. CN− also has intertwined roles with the other SMBs. For example, NO influences CN−-mediated COX inhibition, whereas CN− can upregulate NOS expression [169]. Cyanide also participates in detoxifying crosstalk reactions. For example, it can react with sulfide and S2O32− via 3-MST metabolism to generate SCN− (Scheme 6) [171]. SSNO− has also been reported to react with CN− to generate SNO− and SCN− (Scheme 6) [99]. Additionally, the oxygen analogue OCN− is a spontaneous byproduct of urea decomposition, an abundant metabolite, and substrate for cyanase enzymes in microbes involved in cyanate detoxification [172]. Cyanate is harmful because neutral isocyanic acid (HCNO) can react with amine groups, resulting in carbamoylation and subsequent changes to protein structure, charge, and function [173]. Furthermore, oxidized selenium sources (SeO42−/SeO32−) have also been shown to react with CN− to form selenocyanate (SeCN−), although the biological relevance of this pathway remains underinvestigated [174].

Much like H2Se, HCN is an emerging gasotransmitter. Recent data supporting the cytoprotective effects at low concentrations, as well as its interconnectivity with H2S and NO, strongly support this growing role of HCN in biology. More broadly, and given its strong pH-dependent speciation as well as modulated nucleophilicity and metallophilicity, HCN clearly fits within the category of PSMBs.

Conclusion

Although the “gasotransmitter” classification has drawn significant attention to the biological chemistry of a growing collection of small molecules, its criteria remain narrow and focus primarily on nonbiological physical state rather than mechanistic function. Similarly, when considering NO, CO, H2S, H2Se, and HCN, it becomes evident that most of these molecules rely on reversible protonation/deprotonation to access the chemical forms responsible for their biological activity. This shared feature underscores the need for a broader conceptual framework that captures the fundamental reactivity of such species.

Focusing on the chemical underpinnings associated with biological function, our proposal to classify these species as protic small molecule bioregulators better captures the protonation-dependent chemistry of H2S, H2Se, and HCN and distinguishes them from NO and CO, while also accommodation other important PSMBs outside of the traditional gasotransmitter scope. Moreover, this distinction removes the requirement of a gaseous state, instead prioritizing function and mechanism over physical form. The ability of PSMBs to exist in different protonation states allows for dynamic regulation by local environments, which directly impacts their speciation and biological activity. Moreover, because the PSMB designation focuses on the protic nature, rather than their elemental makeup or oxidizing/reducing propensity, we anticipate that there will be fewer inadvertent assumptions that members of this group have interchangeable reactivity properties, as is often the case with the "ROS" or related groupings of molecules.

Many PSMBs, including the canonical gasotransmitters, are well known for their acute toxicity. As highlighted here and elsewhere, however, the dose makes the poison. At physiological concentrations (often nM – μM), these species act as key signaling agents. Their high reactivity allows for on-demand generation, selective interaction with biological targets, and effective removal by scavenging to maintain homeostasis. Many PSMBs also participate in post-translational modifications, which in many cases can be tuned by the local environment. For example, the conversion of a nucleophilic thiol into an ambiphilic persulfide serves roles in redox signaling, H2S storage, and structural modification.

The versatility of PSMBs is further exemplified by the unique ability of these molecules to traverse barriers associated with biological compartmentalization. Simple protonation or deprotonation alters polarity and hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity of PSMBs, allowing for significant changes in membrane permeability and facilitating movement across concentration gradients often without dedicated transporters. Recognizing a subset of gasotransmitters and SMBs as PSMBs does not diminish the utility of prior classifications. Instead, it reflects the continued refinement of understanding how chemical properties can be translated into biological activity. As research continues to advance across this important class of small molecules, we anticipate that the scope of PSMBs will grow to include additional molecules with new functions and regulatory mechanisms. Furthermore, we view that focusing on the innate chemistry of PSMBs will help guide new insights into functions, regulatory mechanisms, and fundamental biological chemistry driving advances and new understandings of how these important small molecules exert biological function.
