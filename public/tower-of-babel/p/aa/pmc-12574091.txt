Sexual dimorphism in colon is mediated by an androgen-IL33+ stromal cell axis
Authors: Haoyu Wang, Baowei Jing, Juan Zou, Tian Lan, Mengxin Hu, Lan Lin, Hanhua Cheng, Rongjia Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMales and females exhibit pronounced disparity in the epidemiology, clinical progression, and therapeutic outcomes of colonic diseases, but the underlying mechanisms that regulate sexual dimorphism of colon remain poorly understood.ResultsWe determined the colon as a pivotal androgen metabolic hub, where gonad-derived androgens drive sex-dimorphic levels, while androgen-metabolizing enzymes maintain androgen homeostasis in colon. We identified IL-33+ colonic stromal cells as the dominant AR-expressing population in colon. Mechanistically, sex-biased androgen levels govern the nuclear translocation of androgen receptor and further assembly of AR liquid-liquid phase-separated condensates in the immunomodulatory stromal cells of male colon. Notably, we uncovered AR-directed transcriptional programs via nuclear AR phase separation underlying sex-biased expression of key factors, including SerpinA3N and MT1, thereby defining molecular base for sex disparities through gonad-colon axis.ConclusionThese findings provide molecular and cellular base for sex disparities through an androgen-IL33+ stromal cell axis in colon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01493-9.Keywords: Sex, Disparity, Gonad, Androgen receptor, Colon

Introduction

There are differences not only in sexual characteristics to affect physiology or endocrine, behavior, and metabolic aspects between the sexes, but also, more importantly, in the development and prevention of diseases, the awareness and presentation of symptoms, and the effectiveness of therapy. Both males and females exhibit distinct preference or differences in many distinct types of diseases, for instance, immune responses and disease susceptibility [1, 2], cancer incidence and mortality [3], cardiovascular diseases, Alzheimer disease, type II diabetes [4, 5], even in survival threats [6]. Precision medicine requires consideration of sex differences in the onset, progression, prevention, and therapy of diseases, to achieve the goal of tailoring medicine to individual patients in the immediate future. There is an urgent need to elucidate the factors responsible for sex differences in health and disease.

Accumulating evidence reveals pronounced sex disparities in the epidemiology, clinical progression, and therapeutic outcomes of colonic pathologies. Males exhibit earlier-onset, higher incidence (12.4% in male; 9.2% in females) and mortality (5.5% in males; 4.0% in females) in colorectal cancer, while females show a higher proportion of right-sided tumors [7–9]. Consistent with human data, animal models demonstrate an accelerated disease severity in colorectal cancer in males [10, 11]. Within inflammatory bowel diseases, sex differences are striking in Crohn’s disease but modest in ulcerative colitis: western populations show female-predominant Crohn’s disease prevalence, contrasting with male predominance in Asian cohorts [12–14]. Females display heightened extraintestinal manifestations and divergent treatment responses [15–17], while male murine models develop more severe experimental colitis [18]. In addition, male patients with ulcerative colitis had lower T cell fractional abundance, compared to female patients, suggesting sex-dependent differences in resistance to TNF inhibitor therapy [19]. Among functional gastrointestinal disorders, irritable bowel syndrome exhibits robust sex dimorphism, with females experiencing higher prevalence, increased symptom burden, and frequent comorbid conditions (e.g., anxiety, depression, fibromyalgia) [20, 21]. Of note, post-pubertal males harbor significantly elevated androgen levels, in comparison with females [12], suggesting androgen involvement in colonic disease pathogenesis. Human studies examining androgen-disease associations remain limited and inconclusive [22–36], whereas mouse data imply paradoxical roles: androgens exacerbate colitis and colon carcinogenesis, yet ameliorate irritable bowel syndrome [11, 37–39]. Critically, the mechanistic basis for these context-dependent effects remains elusive, hindering the development of sex-tailored therapeutic interventions.

Androgens, primarily synthesized by the Leydig cells in testis in and theca cells in ovary, exert their effects via the androgen receptor (AR), a ligand-dependent transcription factor. In the unliganded state, AR remains cytoplasmic through sequestration by heat shock proteins. Upon ligand binding, AR dissociates from heat shock proteins, is translocated to the nucleus, undergoes phase separation to form transcriptional condensates in prostate cancer cells, and regulates target genes by binding androgen response elements (AREs) [40–42]. While androgen/AR signaling is well-characterized in male-specific organs (e.g., prostate and testes), its regulations and functions in sex-shared organs (e.g., kidneys, adrenal glands, and liver) remain elusive. Emerging evidence identifies androgens/AR as key drivers of sex-biased gene expression in these shared organs [43–45]. Recent single-cell transcriptomes studies have extended this inquiry to the colon [46]; however, technical limitations in resolving distinct colonic cell populations have precluded identification of androgen-dependent sex-biased genes in this tissue, hindering mechanistic understanding of sex disparities in colonic pathologies.

The gut is involved in androgen metabolic regulation. Hepatically derived testosterone glucuronide undergoes biliary secretion into the intestinal lumen, where gut microbiota-mediated β-glucuronidase hydrolyzes testosterone glucuronide to liberate bioactive free testosterone (T) [47, 48]. This activated androgen is subsequently reabsorbed across the intestinal mucosa, completing the enterohepatic recirculation [49]. Critically, emerging evidence implicates intestinal microbiota in androgen synthesis and degradation [49–52]. However, the intrinsic capacity of colonic cells to participate in androgen metabolic pathways remains poorly defined.

Intestinal stromal cells, a heterogeneous cell population located beneath the epithelial layer, not only provide structural support but also regulate intestinal immunity and stem cell homeostasis [53, 54]. In small intestinal stromal cells, the AR signaling pathway is involved in maintenance of stem cell homeostasis by negative regulation of BMP signaling [55]. In colon, AR signaling has been shown to promote colonic motility [39]. Thus, AR signaling could be involved in colonic sexual dimorphism through stromal cells, but the underlying mechanisms that regulate sexual dimorphism of colon remain elusive.

In this study, we delineate pronounced sex differences in colonic androgen levels, with significantly elevated concentrations in male in comparison with female mice. This male-dominant profile primarily stems from gonadal androgen synthesis. Although colonic epithelium lacks de novo T biosynthesis capacity, it is blessed with an uncommon ability to catalyze active androgen degradation via the enzymes hydroxy-delta-5-steroid dehydrogenase, 3 beta- and steroid delta-isomerase 2/3 (HSD3B2/3) and hydroxysteroid (17-beta) dehydrogenase 2 (HSD17B2). Strikingly, AR expression was predominantly localized to interleukin 33-positive (IL-33+) stromal cells in colon. Elevated androgens in males drove enhanced formation of the nuclear AR phase-separated condensates in colonic stromal cells in males, but not in females, ultimately promoting sex-biased expression of AR-target genes (e.g., Serine (or cysteine) peptidase inhibitor, clade A, member 3 N (Serpina3n); Metallothionein 1 (Mt1)). These findings elucidate novel mechanisms governing androgen-dependent sexual dimorphism of the colon, provide a molecular foundation for understanding sex disparities in colonic pathophysiology and potential therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

C57BL/6J mice were raised in the Experimental Animal Center of Wuhan University. Mice were housed under a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle at 25 °C and 55% humidity in the animal center. All animal experiments and methods were performed following the relevant approved guidelines and regulations, as well as under the approval of the Ethics Committee of college of life sciences at Wuhan University (wdsky0201703-2).

Antibodies

Primary antibodies used were as follows: Anti-AR (A19611, ABclonal), Anti-HSD17B2 (A1983, ABclonal), Anti-HSD3B2/3 (A1823, ABclonal), Anti-IL-33 (AF3626, R&D systems), Anti-SerpinA3N (AF4709, R&D systems), Anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), Anti-Ki67 (14-5698-80, Thermo Fisher).

The secondary antibodies used were as follows: HRP-goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1706515, Bio-Rad), HRP-goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1706516, Bio-Rad), FITC-goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (AS011, ABclonal), FITC-goat anti-rat IgG (H + L) (SA00003-11, Proteintech), FITC-monkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (AS042, ABclonal), TRITC-monkey anti-goat IgG (H + L) (SA00007-3, Proteintech).

Plasmid constructs

Mouse Ar CDS (NM_013476.4) was cloned and inserted into pcDNA3.0 using KpnI and XhoI to generate pCMV-Ar and inserted into pEGFP-N1 using XhoI and BamHI to generate Ar-egfp. Mt1 (NM_013602.3) promoter sequence was amplified from mouse genomic DNA and inserted into pGL3-basic (E1751, Promega) using XhoI and HindIII to generate pGL3-Mt1. Serpina3n (NM_009252.2) promoter sequence was cloned and inserted into pGL3-basic using KpnI and XhoI to generate pGL3-Serpina3n and serial deletion constructs (pGL3-Serpina3n-1 to pGL3-Serpina3n-6). Site-directed mutagenesis of the AREs was performed using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture and transfection

CHO cells were obtained from China Center for Type Culture Collection (GDC0018) and cultured in DMEM/F12 (C3130-0500, Vivacell) supplemented with 10% FBS (C04001-500, Vivacell). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Plasmid transfection was performed using polyethylenimine “Max” (9002-98-6z, Polyscience) according to the manufacturer’s standard protocols.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

CHO cells in each well of a 48-well plate were transfected with 0.5 µg recombinant constructs and 10 ng pRL-TK (E2241, Promega). Luciferase activities were subsequently measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system (E1960, Promega) and recorded by a Single Tube Multimode Reader (Modulus, Turner Biosystems), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Living cell analysis and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)

Living cell analysis was performed as described in our previous study [56]. CHO cells were cultured in 35 mm glass-bottomed dishes (BS-20-GJM, Biosharp) and transfected with 1 µg of the Ar-egfp plasmid when the cell density reached approximately 50%. 24 h post-transfection, the cells were treated with a medium containing dihydrotestosterone (DHT) (521-18-6, AbMole) for 1 h. Images were then captured using Zeiss confocal fluorescence microscopy (LSM880, Zeiss) equipped with an environmental chamber that maintained a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For FRAP experiments, the FRAP module of the Zeiss confocal microscope system was utilized. The AR-EGFP condensates of interest were selected and bleached with a 488 nm laser beam. Time-lapse images were subsequently acquired and the data were analyzed using ZEN software (v3.6, Zeiss).

Orchiectomy and Sham surgical procedures

Six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 300 mg/kg tribromoethanol (T903147, Macklin). Orchiectomy and sham surgical procedures were performed as described [39]. The surgical area was disinfected with 75% ethanol. A midline incision of approximately 0.5 cm was made in the scrotum, and the fascia and perididymis were sequentially dissected to expose the testis. Sterile sutures were used to ligate the spermatic cords in the castrated mice. The testis was excised, and the remaining tissue was returned to the scrotum. The same procedure was repeated to remove the contralateral testis through the same incision. The incision was then closed with sterile sutures. For the sham operation, the same scrotal incision was made, and the testes were exposed, but no ligation or removal of the testes was performed. The testes were returned to the scrotum, and the incision was closed. Four weeks post-surgery, both the castrated and sham-operated mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation for subsequent experiments.

Isolation and culture of mouse colonic stromal cells

The isolation and culture of mouse colonic stromal cells were performed as previously described [57]. Colon from 10-week-old mice were resected, washed with pre-cooled PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.68 mM KCl, 12 mM Na2HPO4, 1.76 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4), incised longitudinally, and segmented into approximately 1-cm-long pieces. The pieces were incubated in PBS buffer containing 2% FBS, 10 mM HEPES (A1023, Fonsber), 5 mM EDTA (15575-038, Invitrogen), and 1 mM DTT (A1059, Fonsber), oscillated at 37 °C for 10 min, and then transferred to fresh buffer for another 10 min. After gentle washing, the pieces were further cut into 5 mm fragments and digested in HBSS (H1025, Solarbio) with 2 mg/mL collagenase IV (40510ES60, Yeasen), 10% FBS, and 100 µg/mL DNase I (BS137, Biosharp) at 37 °C for 1 h. The digested tissue was shaken and filtered through a 100 μm strainer. The filtered cell suspension was centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min, then resuspended in medium (DMEM (C3113-0500, Vivacell) with 12% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (03-03101B, Vivacell)), and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Suspended cells were removed by PBS washing, and the adherent cells (stromal cells) were further cultured in fresh medium for 4 days before subsequent analysis.

Reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT–PCR)

RT-PCR and qRT-PCR analysis were performed as described in our previous study [58]. Total RNA was extracted from mouse colon tissues using TRIzol Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a poly(T)18 primer and the MMLV system (M1701, Promega). RT-PCR was employed to amplify the target genes, with Actb as an internal control. qRT-PCR was performed in the real-time PCR system (StepOne, Applied Biosystems) using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712, Vazyme). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as described in our previous study [59]. Proteins extracted from colon tissue were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.5) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C. The membranes were then washed 3 times with TBST for 5 min each. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with appropriately diluted secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, followed by washing with TBST 4 times for 5 min each. Protein bands were visualized by incubating the membranes with the ECL kits (K-12045-D50, Advansta).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Immunofluorescence analysis was performed as described in our previous study [60]. Mouse colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, dewaxed, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Cells were cultured on 24-well plates with coverslips. The coverslips were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. Tissue sections or coverslips were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each. Samples were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (A1050, Fonsber) at room temperature for 20 min, and then washed 3 times with PBS. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (A1035, Fonsber) at 37 °C for 30 min, the samples were incubated with an appropriate concentration of primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Following 5 rounds of PBS washes, the samples were co-incubated with DAPI (C1022, Beyotime) and fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. After 5 additional PBS washes, the samples were imaged using confocal microscopy (SP8, Leica) to obtain high-resolution images.

Determination of T and AD levels by LC-ESI-MS/MS system

Liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) analysis of T and androstenedione (AD) in mouse colon were performed as previously described [61]. Colon tissues (0.05 g) were homogenized and mixed with 400 µL of methanol (1.06007.4008, Merck). The mixture was vortexed for 10 min, incubated on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, concentrated at 20 °C until completely dry. The dried sample was redissolved in 100 µL of methanol, vortexed for 5 min, and centrifuged. Then, 80 µL of the supernatant was collected for further LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis. The LC-ESI-MS/MS system consisted of an ultra-performance liquid chromatography instrument (ExionLC AD, SCIEX) coupled with a mass spectrometer (QTRAP 6500+, SCIEX) equipped with an ESI source (Turbo Ionspray). The liquid chromatography separation was performed on a Kinetex C18 column (1.7 μm, 100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d.) (AJ0-8782, Phenomenex) at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. Mass spectrometry detection was performed in positive ESI mode using multiple reaction monitoring mode. Detailed mass spectrometry parameters for T and AD detection are provided in Supplementary Table S2. AD (C10255030, Dr. Ehrenstorfer) and T (C17322500, Dr. Ehrenstorfer) were used as the standard reference. Data were acquired and processed using Analyst Software (v1.6.3, AB Sciex).

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Total RNAs from colon tissues were isolated and cDNA libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (NR606, Vazyme). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were processed with fastp (v0.23.2) for quality control, and clean reads were aligned to the mm39 reference genome using STAR (v2.7.8a). Gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts in subread (v2.0.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between comparison groups were identified using DESeq2 (v1.38.3), based on a fold change (FC) > 1.3 and a P-value < 0.05. Results were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.4.4).

Analysis of single-cell transcriptome data

The previously generated single-cell transcriptomes from healthy mouse colon (GEO accession number GSE264408 [62]) were retrieved and analyzed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0). Cells with < 20,000 transcripts and < 10% mitochondrial transcripts were retained for further analysis. Data normalization and log transformation were performed using the “LogNormalize” function in Seurat. The top 2000 variable genes were selected based on the “FindVariableFeatures” function for principal component analysis (PCA). The top 30 principal components were chosen according to the Jackstraw approach and visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). DEGs for each cluster were identified using the “FindConservedMarkers” function in Seurat. Clusters were annotated based on previously known markers and cluster-specific genes. Visualization of results, including violin plots, feature plots, and dot plots, was performed using the Seurat package.

CUT&Tag-seq and data analysis.

A total of 150,000 primary colon stromal cells were collected after treatment with 10 nM DHT for 24 h. CUT&Tag experiments and library preparation were conducted using the NovoNGS CUT&Tag 4.0 High-Sensitivity Kit (N259-YH01, Novoprotein) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed with fastp (v0.24.0) to filter out poor-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the mm39 reference genome using bowtie2 (v2.5.4). The aligned reads were sorted and filtered using samtools (v1.21). Duplicated reads were removed using picard (v3.3.0). Peak calling was performed using the de-duplicated bam file through macs2 (v2.2.9.1). The bam file was converted into a bigwig file using bamCoverage provided by deeptools (v3.5.5). The bigwig file was visualized using the IGV genome browser (v2.19.1, Broad Institute).

Analysis of ATAC-seq data

The bigwig files of the assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) data of mouse colonic stromal cells 1 (MRISC-ATAC_rep1) and stromal cells 2 (CD34 SP-ATAC_rep1) were obtained from GEO datasets under accession number GSE156245 [57]. The accessible chromatin at Ar loci was visualized using the IGV genome browser (v2.19.1, Broad Institute).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard. Statistical analyses were performed using the following tests: (1) two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test for comparisons between two independent groups; (2) one-way ANOVA for comparisons among more than two groups. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v9.0, GraphPad). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sex differences of androgen levels and its homeostasis maintenance in colon

To determine whether sex differences exist in colonic androgen levels, we first quantified endogenous T and AD levels in colon of both male and female mice by LC-ESI-MS/MS. Based on the standard curve (Coefficient of determination R2 over 0.99), colonic T and AD concentrations were significantly higher in colon of adult males compared to age-matched females (Fig. 1a–f). To explore whether the difference in colon was determined by gonadal androgen levels, bilateral orchiectomy (ORCH) was performed. Colonic T and AD concentrations were significantly decreased compared with sham surgery (SHAM) controls, and the difference of androgens levels in colon were no significant between ORCH and female mice (Fig. 1g, h). These results indicated that gonadal secretion is the predominant determinant of male-biased colonic androgen levels.

Colonic epithelial cells express androgen-metabolizing enzymes HSD3B2/3 and HSD17B2 that convert active androgens to inactive metabolites

To explore metabolic homeostasis of androgen in colon, expression patterns of androgen metabolic enzymes were analyzed. Colon transcriptomes of both males and females showed that the metabolic enzyme genes Hsd3b2, Hsd3b3, and Hsd17b2 were expressed in the colon of both sexes, but no sex differences, while cytochrome P450, family 11, subfamily a, polypeptide 1 (Cyp11a1), cytochrome P450, family 17, subfamily a, polypeptide 1 (Cyp17a1), and hydroxysteroid (17-beta) dehydrogenase 3 (Hsd17b3) mRNAs were not detectable (Fig. 1i). RT-PCR and western blots confirmed the results (Fig. 1j–l). In adults, expressions of Hsd3b2, Hsd3b3, and Hsd17b2 were higher in colon, liver, and kidney relative to other tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1). In addition, the protein sequences of HSD3B2 and HSD3B3 were highly homologous with similarity of 95.2% and identity of 92.2%. Single-cell transcriptomes (GEO accession number GSE264408 [62]) was used to re-cluster cell types of colon. Using UMAP combined with established marker genes, we identified 15 distinct cell types, including three stromal cells clusters (Stromal cells 1–3), lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), vascular endothelial cells (VECs), smooth muscle cells (SMCs), pericytes, two B cells clusters, plasma cells, T cells, group 1and group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1s and ILC2s), myeloid cells, and epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. S2). Dot plot showed that Hsd3b2, Hsd3b3, and Hsd17b2 were specifically expressed in the epithelial cells of colon (Fig. 1m). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the results (Fig. 1n). These data suggested that the colon did not undergo de novo synthesis of androgens from cholesterol. Instead, androgens are synthesized in the gonads and transported to the colon via circulation, resulting in higher androgen levels in colon of males compared to females. Moreover, the colonic epithelium expressed androgen-metabolizing enzymes that could convert androgens into inactive metabolites (Fig. 1o–p).

Androgen receptor is expressed in the population stromal cell 1 with the marker IL-33+ in mouse colon

Androgen exerts their biological functions through the nuclear AR to regulate expression of downstream genes. Therefore, we first investigated the expression pattern of AR in the colon. Real time qRT-PCR showed that colonic Ar mRNA levels were gradually decreased from postnatal day 0 (P0) to P28, then stabilized with no further change at P56 (Fig. 2a). This expression pattern was similar with that of the mesenchymal marker Vim (Vimentin) but differed from those of the epithelial marker Muc2 (Mucin 2) and the immune cell marker Ptprc (protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type C/CD45) (Fig. 2b–d). Immunofluorescence staining showed AR was expressed in mesenchymal cells, and expression signaling was broadly distributed across colonic mesenchymal cells at P0 and then restricted to the mesenchymal cells beneath the crypts at P28 and P56 (Fig. 2e), which was consistent with proliferation of the mesenchymal cells (Fig. 2f). Further single-cell transcriptomes (GSE264408 [62]) were used to determine cell types of Ar expression. Feature plot and violin plot showed that Ar was mainly expressed in the cluster stromal cell 1, with minor expression in stromal cell 2, ILC2s, and LECs (Fig. 2g–h). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the predominant localization of AR to stromal cell 1 (IL-33+), indicating AR expression in IL-33+ cells (Fig. 2i). In addition, ATAC-seq data for stromal cell 1 and stromal cell 2 (GEO accession number GSE156245 [57]) showed accessible chromatin at Ar loci in both cell types, with significantly stronger signals in stromal cell 1 (Fig. 2j), indicating active Ar transcription in these type of cells. Taken together, these data suggested that the stromal cell 1 was the primary AR-expressing cell type with the distinctive marker IL-33+ in the colon.

Sex difference of the nuclear phase separation of AR in colon

The AR is a ligand-dependent nuclear receptor, with phase-separated condensate formation regulated by androgen in prostate cancer cells [40, 41]. Since AR expression showed no sexual dimorphism at both transcriptional and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S3), we explored AR transcriptional condensates in gene expression control in colons of both males and females. Immunofluorescence revealed that AR was exclusively localized in the nuclei of the colonic stromal cells with distinct condensates in males, whereas it was uniformly distributed in both the nuclei and cytoplasm in females (Fig. 3a). To investigate the mechanism of this sexual dimorphism, we analyzed AR condensates in colons from bilateral orchiectomy (ORCH) and sham surgery controls (SHAM) mice. Immunofluorescence showed that AR condensates were nearly abolished in colon after ORCH, compared to SHAM mice (Fig. 3b), implicating gonadal androgen as a critical regulator in colon. Consistent with this, in vitro androgen treatment of primary colonic stromal cells induced a complete nuclear translocation and condensate formation of AR (Fig. 3c). Further live-cell imaging revealed that androgen treatment induced rapid formation of dynamic AR-EGFP nuclear condensates, with observable fusion events occurring within 60 s (Fig. 3d). FRAP analysis confirmed the liquid-like nature of the condensates, as evidenced by fluorescence recovery within 10 s post-bleaching (Supplementary Fig. S4). Collectively, these data suggested that AR undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation in a male-biased manner in the colon, a process governed by gonadal androgen signaling.

Sex-biased genes regulated by androgen-AR in colon

To further investigate androgen-AR regulated downstream genes in colon, we performed colon transcriptomes analysis of pre-pubertal (2-week-old) and post-pubertal (10-week-old) of both male and female mice, as well as ORCH and SHAM mice (Fig. 4a). Sex difference of gene expression was more obvious at post-pubertal compared to pre-pubertal stage (Fig. 4b), suggesting a potential regulatory role of androgen in the sex-biased expression of colonic genes. At threshold (fold change >1.3, P < 0.05), we identified 573 male-biased and 680 female-biased differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in post-pubertal mice (Fig. 4c). While most DEGs were autosomal, some sex chromosome-linked genes showed sex-biased expression, including X-linked inactive X specific transcripts (Xist, X-inactivation regulator), eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2, subunit 3, structural gene X-linked (Eif2s3x, inactivation escape gene [63]), and lysine (K)-specific demethylase 6 A (Kdm6a, inactivation escape gene [64]), and Y-linked ubiquitously transcribed tetratricopeptide repeat containing, Y-linked (Uty), DEAD box helicase 3, Y-linked (Ddx3y), lysine demethylase 5D (Kdm5d), eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2, subunit 3, structural gene Y-linked (Eif2s3y) (Fig. 4d). To identify sex-biased genes regulated by androgen in colon, we performed transcriptomes analysis of the colons in both male and female mice, as well as SHAM and ORCH mice. After bilateral orchiectomy, 46 male-biased DEGs were downregulated, indicating that these genes were androgen upregulated DEGs, while 14 female-biased DEGs were androgen downregulated (Fig. 4e–f).

Through an integrative approach combining single-cell transcriptomes (GSE264408) with AR transcriptional regulation analysis (utilizing JASPAR database, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and cleavage under targets & tagmentation sequencing (CUT&Tag-seq)), we identified Serpina3n and Mt1 as androgen-AR regulated DEGs that are directly regulated by AR-mediated transcription (Fig. 5a). Single-cell transcriptomes profiling revealed predominant expression of Serpina3n in the clusters stromal cell 1 and stromal cell 2 (Fig. 5b). Immunofluorescence co-expression analysis showed that SerpinA3N (cytoplasm) and AR (nuclei) proteins were present in the same cells of the colon (Fig. 5c), suggesting potential AR-mediated regulation of Serpina3n expression. Furthermore, we analyzed the regulatory effects of AR on the promoter of Serpina3n using the dual-luciferase reporter system. Overexpression of AR in CHO cells significantly enhanced the promoter activities of Serpina3n (Fig. 5d). Luciferase reporter assays of serially truncated Serpina3n promoter constructs indicated that ARE was located within the −198 to −52 bp region of the promotor (Fig. 5e). JASPAR predicted a putative ARE from −79 to −63 bp within this region (Fig. 5f–g). Site-directed mutagenesis of the ARE in the Serpina3n promoter resulted in a significant decrease of luciferase activity compared to the wild-type construct (Fig. 5g). CUT&Tag-seq analysis also demonstrated that AR bound to the promoters of Serpina3n in colonic stromal cells (Fig. 5h). In addition, Mt1 was demonstrated as an androgen regulated gene through the same strategy and methods (Supplementary Fig. S5). Taken together, these data indicated that androgens-AR signaling drive sex-biased expression of their target genes in colon (Fig. 5i).

Discussion

Sex differences in the clinical presentation of colonic pathologies—including colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, and irritable bowel syndrome [7, 12, 21]—are well established, but their mechanisms remain unresolved. Elucidating androgen-mediated regulatory mechanisms is thus critical for developing sex-tailored therapies for colon diseases. Here, we suggest the colon as a pivotal androgen metabolic hub, where gonad-derived androgens drive sex-dimorphic tissue concentrations. Androgen-metabolizing enzymes were expressed in colonic epithelium, which could convert androgens into inactive metabolites to maintain androgen homeostasis in colon. Through single-cell transcriptomes analysis, we identified IL-33+ colonic stromal cells as the dominant AR-expressing population and revealed that sex-biased androgen levels govern the nuclear translocation of AR and assembly of AR liquid-liquid phase-separated condensates within this immunomodulatory stromal cell 1 population in colon. Consequently, we uncovered AR-directed transcriptional programs underlying sex-biased expression of key genes—including Serpina3n (a serine protease inhibitor modulating cell death/survival and inflammation) and Mt1 (a metal-binding chaperone mitigating oxidative stress)—thereby defining molecular base for sex disparities in colonic health and disease susceptibility.

Sexual dimorphism in colonic pathologies arises from multifaceted contributors, including sex chromosomes [65], gonadal hormones [66–68], and gut microbiota [69]. Our data show that male mice exhibit significantly elevated T and AD levels versus females—a disparity primarily driven by heightened testicular (vs. ovarian) androgen biosynthesis. This gonadal-colonic axis implicates androgen signaling as a key regulator of sex-biased colonic disease phenotypes. Critically, androgens not only exert direct effects via AR but also orchestrate gut microbial composition [70, 71]. Supporting this paradigm, testosterone governs gut microbial dysbiosis in the azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium-induced colorectal cancer model [72], revealing androgen-microbiota modulation as a novel mechanism underpinning sex disparities in colonic disorders.

Our study suggest AR as predominantly expressed in IL-33+ colonic stromal cells—a recently characterized subset essential for colon homeostasis, alternatively termed MAP3K2-regulated intestinal stromal cells (MRISCs) [57], trophocytes [73], crypt-bottom fibroblasts 2 (CBFs2) [74], or RSPO3+GREM1+ fibroblasts (RGFs) [75]. These cells constitute the primary source of niche-modulating factors, secreting WNT agonists and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) antagonists critical for maintaining epithelial stemness [73]. Consistently, genetic ablation of R-spondin 3 (Rspo3) or mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 2 (Map3k2) in IL-33+ stroma disrupts stem cell homeostasis and exacerbates colitis [57, 75]. Emerging evidence indicates that androgens suppress ILC2s proliferation across multiple tissues—including skin, lung, heart, lacrimal glands, liver, pancreas, salivary glands, spleen, and stomach—resulting in significantly elevated ILC2s abundance in females versus males [1, 46, 76]. This disparity underpins sexual dimorphism in pathologies like asthma [77, 78]. Notably, we detected robust Ar expression in colonic ILC2s, suggesting androgen-mediated regulation of colonic immunity in a sex-dependent manner. Critically, IL-33 serves as a key activator of ILC2s in mice and humans, potently enhancing their proliferation and cytokine secretion [79]. The IL-33–ILC2s axis confers protection against colitis in murine models [80–82]. Intriguingly, IL-33+ stromal cells are the dominant IL-33 source in colon, indicating essential crosstalk between AR-expressing stromal cells and ILC2s in colonic immune homeostasis—a mechanism with profound implications for sex-biased vulnerability to colonic diseases.

As a core member of the nuclear receptor family, AR exhibits strictly ligand (androgen)-dependent regulation of its phase separation behavior. Studies in prostate cancer—a classic androgen-dependent malignancy—demonstrate that androgen-induced AR phase-separated condensate formation significantly enhances transcriptional activation of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) gene [40, 41]. In addition, anti-androgen drugs (e.g., enzalutamide) effectively suppress AR condensate formation and markedly reduce their transcriptional regulation of PSA [40, 83]. Although AR expression levels show no sexual dimorphism in colonic tissues, sexual dimorphic androgen levels of colon lead to more frequent AR condensate formation in male colonic tissues via gonadal-colon axis. This study not only provides a novel molecular mechanism underlying sex disparities in colorectal cancer incidence, but also suggests that anti-androgens drugs like enzalutamide may exert preventive effects against male-predominant colorectal cancer by modulating AR phase separation. Thus, these findings provide new understanding of gonadal-colon axis, which is helpful for developing sex-specific precision prevention strategies in treatment of colorectal cancer.

Enhanced formation of nuclear AR liquid-liquid phase-separated condensates in colonic stromal cells of male versus female mice drives sex-dimorphic transcriptional regulation of androgen-responsive genes (e.g., Mt1, Serpina3n). Metallothioneins (MTs; including MT1)—cysteine-rich metal chaperones—orchestrate essential metal homeostasis (Cu/Zn) and detoxify toxic metals (Cd/Hg) [84], while modulating redox balance, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress responses [85]. Although MTs exhibit context-dependent roles in colitis [86–89], their sex-biased regulation by AR remains unexplored. In addition, MT deletion exacerbated high fat diet-induced obesity [90]. Given that android fat mass was higher in men relative to women [91], MT1 is probably involved in sex difference of intestinal fat metabolism in an androgen-AR-regulated manner. Similarly, SerpinA3N (a secretory serine protease inhibitor) broadly targets cathepsin G, elastase, granzyme B, and cysteine proteases [92]. Critically, SerpinA3N preserves epithelial barrier integrity by inhibiting elastase [93] and represents the sole extracellular granzyme B inhibitor [92, 94]—a pivotal immune checkpoint molecule for NK/T-cell-mediated tumor clearance in colorectal cancer [95–97]. Thus, androgen-dependent male-biased expression of Mt1 and Serpina3n elucidates a molecular basis for sex disparities in colonic disease progression, positioning these genes as key regulators in sex-specific therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

This finding provides molecular and cellular base for sex disparities in the colon through an androgen-IL33+ stromal cell axis and helpful for understanding sex disparities in colonic pathophysiology, informing targeted therapeutic strategies.
