An integrated review of monkeypox: from pathogen and epidemiology to diagnostics, control, and challenges
Authors: Wujian Li, Qiyuan Yang, Wenyu Xie, Wenqian Dong, Sandra Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMonkeypox (Mpox) is a zoonotic disease caused by MPXV and remains a threat to global public health. MPXV Clade Ib has higher mortality, whereas Clade IIb exhibits widespread international transmission. WHO has twice designated mpox epidemics as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC). This highlights that the virus is not only pathogenic but also has the potential to cause a global pandemic, posing a challenge to the international public health system. The rapid transmission of MPXV between and within hosts is related to two key virus particles: IMV and EEV. Antigenic proteins (A29L, M1R, B6R, and A35R) on these particles mediate viral invasion, dissemination, and immune evasion. Clinical management of mpox remains supportive, and there is a lack of specific antiviral drugs. Although smallpox-related drugs (such as tecovirimat, cidofovir, and brincidofovir) show potential, their clinical efficacy needs to be verified. The existing vaccines recommended by the WHO are facing challenges, such as side effects or limited duration of immune protection in application, prompting the research and development of novel vaccines and antibody therapeutics. Research on the mechanism of invasion and surface proteins of viruses will continue to provide a scientific foundation for the development of medications and vaccines. Prevention and control rely on a stronger monitoring system, timely risk warnings, and public health education. This article reviews the recent progress in MPXV detection, vaccine development, and antibody-based therapy, providing new insights for mpox prevention and control.KEYWORDS: Mpox, virology, vaccines, antibody drugs, zoonotic infection

Introduction

The monkeypox virus (MPXV) is the cause of mpox and has caused two pandemics since 2022. The World Health Organization's (WHO’s) most recent report, released on 26 March 2026, declared that from 1 January 2022 to 28 February 2026, 181,164 laboratory-confirmed cases were reported globally, with 492 related fatalities recorded in 144 afflicted countries. MPXV was first isolated in Denmark in 1959 from monkeys imported from Singapore. In 1970, the Democratic Republic of Congo reported the world's first case of human infection, but it was misdiagnosed as smallpox at that time [1]. There are two primary viral clades, clade I and clade II, which have been detected in Central and Western African, respectively [2]. There is a 0.5% difference in genome sequence between these clades. Divergent virulence-associated genes underlie the significant disparity in clinical outcomes between mpox virus clades, with Clade I exhibiting a case fatality rate of up to 10.6% compared with less than 3.6% for Clade II. Both clades comprise subclades A and B. Clade IIb, also known as Clade III, which generated significant epidemics, contains lineages A.1, A.1.1, A.2, A.2.1, A.2.2, A.3, and B.1. Its enhanced transmissibility in nonendemic regions suggests that adaptive virological evolution drove its accelerated dissemination.

The global dissemination of the MPXV has emerged as a public health concern of international importance, driving the development and application of a range of prevention and treatment technologies. In the field of diagnostics, various molecular diagnostic assays and immunological testing methods have been widely deployed. These techniques not only exhibit high sensitivity and specificity but also enable viral genotyping, providing robust support for epidemic surveillance and early containment efforts. With respect to vaccines, multiple candidate vaccines based on diverse technological platforms, including recombinant protein vaccines, attenuated live vaccines, novel mRNA vaccines, and nanoparticle vaccines, are being actively developed [3,4]. These candidates have demonstrated promising immunogenicity and protective efficacy in preclinical studies, with some having progressed to clinical trial phases. Significant strides have also been made in the research of antibody-based therapeutics: neutralizing antibodies targeting key antigenic epitopes of the virus have shown therapeutic potential through detailed mechanistic studies, offering novel strategies for passive immunization. Moreover, efforts in small-molecule drug development have focused on critical stages of the viral life cycle. Leveraging structural biology and computer-aided design, numerous compounds with inhibitory activity have been developed, demonstrating broad-spectrum antiviral potential. Nevertheless, several key issues remain in current research. The differences in cellular entry efficiency between distinct viral particles (IMV and EEV) and the precise molecular mechanisms underlying immune evasion have yet to be systematically elucidated. To better understand viral pathogenesis, the application of organoid systems is warranted to dynamically track the pathological progression triggered by infection in specific target cells and organs. Clinically, long-term cohort follow-up is needed to clarify individual variations and potential contributing factors associated with post-incubation relapse and chronic infection. Regarding transmission, the infection efficiency and pathogenic mechanisms of routes beyond direct contact and respiratory droplets, specifically sexual and vertical transmission, await further characterization. In terms of clinical therapy, the cross-protective efficacy of broad-spectrum vaccines against different viral clades remains inadequately evaluated, and investigations into the durability of post-infection humoral immunity, inter-individual variability, and the coordination between antibody and cellular immune responses are still in their early stages. In the development of small-molecule therapeutics, functional validation of certain drug targets and detailed elucidation of their binding interactions with candidate compounds require more intensive study. Furthermore, systematic surveillance data on viral persistence and transmissibility in the environment are lacking, and the potential for transmission by asymptomatic individuals further compounds the complexity of outbreak containment.

This review systematically synthesizes recent advances in diagnostic technologies, vaccine development, antibody therapies, and small-molecule drugs, while identifying current research gaps. Finally, it examines the challenges impeding the containment of MPXV transmission and epidemic spread, and discusses potential countermeasures to mitigate these challenges.

Pathogen

The genus OPXV comprises several well-known species, including ectromelia virus (ECTV), cowpox virus (CPXV), vaccinia virus (VACV), variola virus (VARV), and monkeypox virus (MPXV). OPXV is characterized by a large and complex proteome consisting of over 200 proteins [5]. MPXV, in particular, is an enveloped double-stranded DNA virus with a dumbbell-shaped core and an outer tubular surface structure measuring approximately 200–250 nm in diameter [6]. Like other OPXV, MPXV has a genomic architecture comprising inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) composed of tandem repeats, variable terminal regions, and a highly conserved central core (as shown in Figure 1). Owing to their genetic and antigenic similarities, infection by one OPXV species can confer substantial cross-protection against others. This principle underpins the use of VACV-based vaccines, which are effective against MPXV, CPXV, and VARV infections. With the elimination of smallpox and the discontinuance of smallpox vaccination, cross-protective immunity has waned in recent decades, necessitating the development of MPXV-specific preventative and therapeutic medicines.

The IMV is brick-shaped or oval, approximately 200–250 nm in diameter, and consists of a lipoprotein envelope, internal lateral protein bodies, and a thick-walled core containing double-stranded DNA. The core houses the viral genome along with enzymes essential for replication and transcription, such as RNA polymerase and capping enzymes. Key surface proteins of the IMV include M1R, which participates in membrane fusion, and A29L, which mediates viral attachment by binding to glycosaminoglycans. The EEV, built upon the complete IMV structure, possesses an additional outer lipid membrane derived from the host cell membrane. Its characteristic surface proteins primarily include A35R, which is involved in cell-to-cell spread, and B6R, which is associated with complement regulation and immune evasion. (B) The MPXV genome is a linear double-stranded DNA of approximately 197 kb, structurally organized into a central conserved region, two flanking variable regions, and terminal inverted terminal repeats (ITRs). The central conserved region encodes core proteins essential for viral replication, transcription, and assembly (such as the DNA polymerase F8L), ensuring the fundamental viral life cycle. The variable regions, in contrast, encode a large array of immunomodulatory proteins (such as the complement inhibitory protein MOPICE and the nuclear immunosuppressive protein P2), which serve as the molecular basis for immune evasion, cross-species transmission, and differential virulence.

Multiple important core enzymes and key proteins are involved in the replication, infection, and immune evasion processes of MPXV. Recent cryo-electron microscopy studies have revealed multiple functional states of the MPXV DNA polymerase holoenzyme (F8/A22/E4). Structural data show that the holoenzyme can form a hexameric autoinhibitory conformation, which is dissociated into an active trimeric form upon dsDNA binding, exposing the DNA-binding site [7]. In the replicative state, E4 and F8 form a “forward sliding clamp” that encircles the single-stranded template, enabling processive synthesis [8]. In the editing state, the mismatched primer 3′ terminus enters the exonuclease domain, where it is recognized and excised by conserved aromatic residues, ensuring replication fidelity. The inhibitor Ara-CTP acts as a chain terminator by competitively inhibiting the polymerase through the formation of an additional hydrogen bond with Asn665 and altering the orientation of the 3′-OH group. Furthermore, E4 possesses uracil DNA glycosylase activity and excises template uracil via a “clamp-push-pull” mechanism, coupling base excision repair with the replication process [9]. These findings elucidate the mechanisms of assembly, activation, fidelity maintenance, and inhibition of the MPXV polymerase holoenzyme, providing a structural basis for the development of antiviral drugs.

MPXV enters the host through the respiratory mucosa or damaged skin, with the epithelium of the oral and respiratory mucosa serving as the primary infection site. The specific viral ligands and host receptors involved in viral entry remain unclear; however, the complement regulatory protein MOPICE encoded by MPXV is known to influence its cell and tissue tropism [10]. During the early stage of infection, MPXV interferes with host innate immunity through multiple proteins: the A47 protein inhibits TLR3/TLR4 signalling pathways, attenuating inflammatory and antiviral responses [11]; MOPICE inhibits complement activation, assisting the virus in evading complement-mediated clearance. Additionally, MPXV utilizes OMCP (the product of the N3R gene) as a soluble NKG2D ligand to inhibit NK cell killing of infected cells [12].

The central conserved region of the MPXV genome encodes enzyme systems involved in DNA replication, transcription, and RNA synthesis [13]. During replication, the host APOBEC3 enzymes induce GA to AA and TC to TT mutations, driving accelerated viral evolution, suggesting that host factors exert significant mutational pressure on viral replication [14]. MPXV interferes with host signalling pathways through multiple proteins to promote replication: ankyrin-like proteins (e.g. J3L, D1L) and Bcl-2-like proteins (e.g. A47R, B13R) inhibit the NF-κB pathway, reducing inflammatory responses [15]. H1L inactivates STAT1, while C6R blocks STAT2, collectively inhibiting JAK/STAT-mediated IFN signalling [16]. In terms of antiviral responses, the F3 protein of MPXV is unable to bind dsRNA due to N-terminal truncation, yet it still evades PKR-mediated translational inhibition through an unknown mechanism [17].

Throughout its replication cycle, MPXV generates four distinct forms of viral particles: the intracellular mature virus (IMV), the intracellular enveloped virus (IEV), the cell-associated enveloped virus (CEV), and the extracellular enveloped virus (EEV). These particles represent sequential stages of viral morphogenesis and egress, of which play specific roles in cell-to-cell spread and systemic infection (as shown in Figure 2). The IMVs are the first assembled infectious particles, maturing within viral factories and remaining in the cytoplasm of infected cells until cell lysis occurs, after which they play important roles in transmission between hosts. However, to disseminate within the host, a subset of IMVs acquires additional membranes via the trans-Golgi or endosomal compartments, forming IEVs. Tecovirimat, the only drug approved for the treatment of smallpox and mpox, blocks this wrapping step. IEVs subsequently utilize actin-tail propulsion to reach the plasma membrane, giving rise to CEVs that remain attached to the cell surface and EEVs that are released into the extracellular space to facilitate disseminated infection. The F13L gene encodes the VP37 protein, which plays a key role in viral maturation and envelope formation; its inhibition blocks viral release from infected cells [18]. During dissemination, MPXV evades adaptive immunity through multiple mechanisms: the B10 protein interferes with MHC-I peptide loading and intracellular transport, impairing CD8+ T cell recognition; D6L encodes an IL-18 binding protein that inhibits IFN-γ induction; J2L (CrmB) acts as a TNF-α decoy receptor, suppressing inflammatory responses; and B16R functions as a soluble IFN-α/β/γ receptor, blocking IFN signalling [19,20].

Life cycle of MPXV. Intracellular mature virions (IMVs) are assembled within viral factories, where the viral core becomes enveloped by a lipid membrane derived from the endoplasmic reticulum. A subset of IMVs is subsequently wrapped by host transport vesicles, such as those originating from the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) or early endosomes, thereby transforming into double-membraned intracellular enveloped virions (IEVs). These IEVs are then trafficked along microtubules to the cell periphery, where the outer vesicular membrane fuses with the plasma membrane, exposing the virion on the cell surface as a cell-associated enveloped virions (CEVs). Ultimately, CEVs are released into the extracellular space either through budding or exocytosis, becoming extracellular enveloped virions (EEVs) that mediate systemic dissemination.

Each particle type possesses a unique membrane protein composition, which determines their distinct roles in immune evasion and dissemination within the host. B6 and A35 are key envelope proteins on the surface of the EEV. B6 is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein, homologous to B5 in VACV. Although no experimental crystal structure is available, AlphaFold2 predicts that it consists of four short consensus repeat (SCR) domains, an acidic stalk region, a transmembrane domain, and a short cytoplasmic tail [21]. B5 is involved in virus wrapping, intracellular transport, actin tail induction, and non-fusogenic rupture of the EEV outer membrane, with the stalk region interacting with A34, and SCR1-2 together with the stalk region forming important neutralizing epitopes. A35 is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein, homologous to A33 in VACV. Its crystal structure (PDB: 3K7B) reveals a disulphide-linked homodimer, with the ectodomain containing a flexible stalk and a C-type lectin-like domain (CTLD) [22]. Although A33 is not essential for replication, it is critical for B5 transport, actin tail formation, and cell-to-cell spread. Antibodies targeting the conserved distal dimer groove of the OPXV A35/A33 protein dimer interface recognize highly conserved conformational epitopes across subunits, effectively inhibiting intercellular spread of EEV, thereby mediating broad-spectrum cross-protective efficacy. This makes them key targets for next-generation universal OPXV vaccines and immunotherapies.

M1, A29, E8, and H3 are envelope proteins on the surface of the IMV. M1 is an IMV surface protein associated with the entry-fusion complex (EFC). Its crystal structure reveals an ectodomain consisting of a five-helix bundle stabilized by three conserved disulphide bonds and containing a hydrophobic cavity that accommodates the N-terminal myristate group [23]. N-terminal myristoylation is essential for infectivity, and the “myristate switch model” suggests that its conformational change mediates membrane fusion. Neutralizing antibodies (e.g. 7D11) bind to the distal membrane region, potentially blocking the active conformational transition [24].

The A29 protein is homologous to the VACV A27 protein and is highly conserved among poxviruses. Its C-terminus folds into a long α-helix, with a structure similar to the crystal structure of VACV A27 (PDB 3VOP). The A27 protein plays multiple roles in the viral life cycle, including binding to cell surface heparan sulphate, regulating membrane fusion, and mediating IMV transport to form EEV [25].

E8 is homologous to the VACV D8 protein and is a glycosaminoglycan (GAG)-binding protein that binds to cell surface chondroitin sulphate, which is critical for the key step of IMV adsorption to host cells during viral entry [26]. The crystal structure of the D8 protein reveals an inactive carbonic anhydrase (CAH) fold domain, a C-terminal helix, a transmembrane region, and a cytoplasmic tail, with the CAH domain binding chondroitin sulphate E through a conserved pocket and the C-terminal helix mediating oligomerization [26].

H3 mediates viral attachment through binding to heparan sulphate via a positively charged surface and forms an attachment protein network with A17, A27, and others, promoting virus-host binding and potentially affecting viral entry efficiency [27]. The crystal structure of the H3 protein reveals a glycosyltransferase fold, containing a conserved ExD motif for UDP-glucose binding, and its C-terminal domain may undergo conformational stabilization upon ligand binding [28].

Epidemiology

Although MPXV is a DNA virus that evolves at lower rates than RNA viruses, it can nevertheless undergo mutations, deletions, and recombination during transmission. Epidemiological data indicate an overall case fatality rate (CFR) of 8.7% for MPXV infections, with distinct variations between viral clades: Clade I (formerly the Congo Basin clade) has a CFR of 10.6%, whereas Clade II (historically the West African clade) has a significantly lower CFR of 3.6% [29]. From a virological perspective, the stronger virulence of MPXV clade I is due to the preservation of additional genes associated with host defence evasion in the central region of its genome. While clade IIb has undergone extensive large fragment gene deletions (e.g. at the OPG015 locus) during its evolution. Although these deletions may have reduced its pathogenicity, the sustained mutations driven by APOBEC3 have enhanced its ability to adapt to long-term transmission among humans.

In 2003, the first documented outbreak of mpox outside Africa was reported in the Americas, involving 47 human cases infected through exposure to virus-carrying rodents imported from Ghana, West Africa [30]. Subsequently, sporadic mpox cases have been reported outside Africa, including one confirmed case in Israel (2018), one travel-associated case in Singapore (2019), and 2 epidemiologically linked cases in the U.S. (2021). As of 2022, a total of 142,242 mpox cases with 328 fatalities have been documented across 133 countries and territories globally. Notably, the number of confirmed mpox infections continues to increase worldwide. In July 2022, the WHO declared the global spread of mpox a PHEIC [31]. On August 14, 2024, the WHO subsequently reinstated this designation following a surge in MPXV Clade I infections across Central Africa. Following an 80% decline in African cases from the peak and widespread vaccination, the WHO announced that mpox no longer constituted a PHEIC. This contrasted with the Africa CDC's decision to reaffirm the public health emergency of continental concern, prompted by rising infections in several countries.

According to WHO surveillance data, approximately 96.5% of the 94,331 globally reported mpox cases outside Africa since 2022 have occurred in male individuals [32]. A retrospective analysis of 282 cases (1980–1985) revealed an age range of one month to 69 years, with 90% of cases occurring in children younger than 15 years [33]. For decades, mpox has spread from the hyperendemic Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) to other countries, with the median age of cases increasing from children to young adults [2]. While most patients recover within 2–4 weeks, severe complications disproportionately affect children, pregnant individuals, and immunocompromised individuals [34]. Smallpox vaccination may confer partial cross-protection against mpox infection. However, following the global eradication of smallpox in 1980, routine vaccination programmes were discontinued worldwide, resulting in limited population-level immunity through vaccination in current demographics.

The natural reservoir of MPXV remains undetermined; however, small mammals play a critical role in viral transmission. Evidence of OPXV infection has been detected in six rodent species (two Funisciurus spp., Graphiurus lorraineus, Cricetomys emini, Heliosciurus sp., Oenomys hypoxanthus) and the four-toed elephant shrew (Petrodromus tetradactylus). The global emergence of mpox in 2022 established human-to-human transmission as a primary driver of the outbreak, constituting an epidemiologically significant paradigm shift in viral dissemination dynamics.

The transmission of MPXV occurs via zoonotic and anthroponotic routes. Zoonotic transmission involves animal bites, contact with infected bodily fluids or lesions, or consumption of infected bushmeat, accounting for 72% of cases in Africa. Human-to-human transmission occurs through exposure to respiratory secretions, blood, or skin lesions, particularly via prolonged face-to-face respiratory droplet exposure in healthcare or household settings [35]. Emerging research indicates that synergistic interactions among anthropogenic drivers, such as conflict zones, agricultural expansion, deforestation patterns, climate change impacts, and human mobility, have increased the permeability of the zoonotic interface between MPXV-infected wildlife reservoirs and human populations. This dynamic constitutes a principal factor driving mpox endemicity in ecological transition zones such as Nigeria. Nosocomial transmission can be mitigated through stringent infection control measures [36]. Virological surveillance of residential surfaces occupied by two mpox cases revealed ubiquitous MPXV DNA contamination in all high-contact fomites, with direct hand transfer efficiency correlated with detectable viral genomic loads. However, the epidemiological relevance of this environmental persistence remains indeterminate, as the minimum infectious dose through fomite-mediated transmission has not been empirically established [37]. Vertical transmission, including transplacental transmission and postnatal contact, has been documented. A 2007–2011 study of four pregnant individuals infected with MPXV highlighted adverse fetal outcomes associated with maternal infection [38]. Sexual transmission has emerged as a significant route, with numerous cases reported among men who have sex with men (MSM) during the 2022 outbreak.

Epidemiological models have provided key evidence for quantifying the enhanced human-to-human transmission capability of MPXV since 2022. A systematic review and meta-analysis based on 47 studies showed that the pooled basic reproduction number (R0) for monkeypox was 2.13 (95% CI 1.63–2.62), and the pooled effective reproduction number (Rt) was 2.51 (95% CI 2.05–2.97), both significantly above 1, confirming that the virus has acquired sustained human-to-human transmission capability [39]. Stratified analysis further revealed that the Rt among MSM (3.08) was significantly higher than that in the general population (2.20), suggesting that sexual contact networks are a core factor driving enhanced transmission [39]. Early estimates by Branda et al. in multiple European countries also found R0 values as high as 2.44–3.62 within the MSM population, while the transmission risk in the general population remained extremely low [40]. Bragazzi et al. employed a risk-structured model that distinguished between sexual and non-sexual transmission routes, estimating that the difference in R0 between high-risk and low-risk populations could reach two orders of magnitude, and noted that the underreporting rate across countries ranged from 25 to 93%, reflecting transmission heterogeneity and limitations in surveillance systems [41]. Collectively, the modelling evidence indicates that the “enhanced human-to-human transmission” of monkeypox virus during the 2022 outbreak was not solely attributable to viral evolution per se, but rather stemmed primarily from a fundamental shift in transmission routes towards sexual contact networks, coupled with the amplifying effect of highly connected individuals within these networks.

Research indicates that sustained human-to-human transmission of MPXV is driven by multiple factors. At the molecular level, after the virus enters the human population, sustained activity of the host antiviral factor APOBEC3 becomes a major driver of its evolution; although it restricts the types of mutations, it generates sufficient genetic diversity for adaptation to the human host. At the transmission dynamics level, the virus primarily exploits “heavy-tailed” sexual networks, wherein highly connected individuals (e.g. MSM) were rapidly infected during the early phase of the outbreak, driving exponential growth while also contributing to subsequent declines due to network saturation [42]. In terms of host tropism, the virus exhibits pronounced sex- and behavior-based preferences. The 2022 clade IIb outbreak was predominantly concentrated among MSM populations, whereas the 2024 clade Ib and clade Ia outbreaks clearly involved sex worker populations and featured close-contact transmission within households, including among children [43]. This spread from specific high-risk groups to household networks poses a formidable challenge to public health interventions.

Clinical symptoms

Mpox syndrome is characterized by a biphasic clinical course marked by successive systemic and dermatological manifestations. After an incubation period of 1–21 days, the initial febrile phase (lasting 1–3 days) presents with pyrexia, lymphadenopathy, and constitutional symptoms such as cephalalgia, myalgia, and profound asthenia. This is followed by a dermatological phase, which typically begins with macular papular eruptions that progress sequentially through papular, vesicular, and pustular stages, eventually culminating in crust formation over a period of 2–4 weeks. In some cases, cutaneous manifestations may constitute the primary clinical presentation.

The pathology of mpox is defined as a disseminated vesiculopustular rash that evolves through distinct morphological stages, starting with enanthem and advancing through macular, papular, vesicular, and pustular phases before concluding with desquamation, during which scabs detach, signalling lesion resolution [44]. In severe and fatal cases, mucocutaneous lesions exhibit prominent necrotizing and proliferative epithelial changes. Autopsy findings further revealed extensive involvement of the digestive tract, systemic tissue necrosis, and thrombotic vasculopathy affecting lymphoid tissues, lungs, and other solid organs. Common pulmonary complications include bronchopneumonia and acute lung injury. The widespread presence of the MPXV antigen and DNA across affected tissues confirms systemic viral dissemination.

The global mpox outbreak in 2022 (clade IIb) was characterized predominantly by genital, perianal, and oropharyngeal lesions, often accompanied by lymphadenopathy, proctitis, and other symptoms. The clinical course was relatively mild, with a low mortality rate (approximately 0.13%). Transmission occurred mainly through sexual contact, and young adult males (particularly MSM) constituted the primary affected population [45]. In contrast, during the 2024 clade Ib outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), adults predominantly presented with involvement of the urogenital system (89% had genital lesions), with complications mainly affecting the urogenital tract, whereas children exhibited more widespread, non-genital-predominant rashes. Pregnant individuals faced a high risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes (67%). Overall, the hospitalization complication rate was elevated, yet the mortality rate remained low (0.5%) [46]. Both outbreaks demonstrated a close correlation between transmission patterns and clinical manifestations; however, clade Ib exhibited more complex age distribution and clinical features within the context of sexual transmission.

The disease spectrum ranges from self-limiting infections to severe or fatal outcomes, with mortality influenced by factors such as viral strain, adequacy of medical care, exposure dose, and host immune status. Vulnerable populations, including paediatric patients, pregnant women, and immunocompromised individuals, especially those with uncontrolled HIV infection, are at elevated risk of complications and mortality. A frequent sequela in survivors is persistent scarring following the resolution of skin lesions. These clinical and pathological features underscore the necessity for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and vigilant clinical management to mitigate adverse outcomes.

Diagnostics

Amid the global mpox outbreak, the implementation of precise and reliable diagnostic methodologies enables early viral detection, facilitating timely intervention strategies to disrupt transmission chains and mitigate epidemic progression. In the laboratory testing of MPXV, the collection, preservation, and transportation of clinical samples must strictly adhere to standardized procedures to ensure testing sensitivity and reliability. Skin lesion material is the core specimen for MPXV laboratory diagnosis [47]. During the acute phase, priority should be given to collecting vesicle contents and nasal/oropharyngeal swabs, which should be placed in viral transport medium and transported to the laboratory under refrigeration (2–8°C) or frozen (−20°C) within 48 h. In the convalescent phase, alternative samples such as urine can be additionally collected to monitor persistent viral shedding. Sampling strategies should be adjusted for different populations: for MSM, collection of semen and perianal vesicle contents is recommended; for HIV-positive patients, due to potentially delayed viral clearance, follow-up sampling should be extended (at least 2 weeks). Furthermore, when skin lesion samples cannot be obtained, urine and saliva can serve as effective alternative materials [48].

Accurate diagnosis of mpox requires a combination of molecular assays, serological testing, and clinical evaluation. PCR is the gold standard for mpox detection, offering high sensitivity and specificity when combined with clinical and epidemiological data. The E9L, B6R, G2R, and F3 proteins could be used as key targets in real-time PCR [49]. The E9L gene encodes the viral DNA polymerase and represents a highly conserved genomic region within the OPXV. It is present in all OPXV, including MPXV, VARV, and CPXV. By designing specific primers and probes, E9L can be used to distinguish MPXV from other OPXV, offering broad utility, particularly in the detection of Eurasian OPXV. The B6R gene, located in the MPXV genome, encodes a viral envelope protein and serves as an MPXV-specific target. It exhibits high conservation across different MPXV clades (e.g. the West African and Congo Basin clades) and effectively differentiates MPXV from non-OPXV pathogens with strong specificity. The G2R gene resides in the terminal variable region of the MPXV genome and is a specific target for the West African MPXV clade, with sequence differences present in the Congo Basin clade. The F3 gene encodes an early viral protein and is relatively conserved within the MPXV genome. It lacks homologous sequences in other common pathogens, thereby ensuring detection specificity and minimizing the risk of cross-reactivity. Real-time fluorescent PCR (RT PCR) is currently the gold standard for the clinical diagnosis of mpox, and has a sensitivity and specificity that can approach 100%. It is capable of detecting viral nucleic acids in various clinical samples, including skin lesions, blister fluid, scabs, and oral or nasopharyngeal secretions. Alternative molecular techniques, such as recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), are currently under development [50]. These methods allow rapid detection and simplified operational procedures; however, they generally exhibit moderately lower sensitivity and specificity than RT PCR does.

Virus isolation, which is conducted in biosafety level-3 (BSL-3) laboratories, remains a definitive approach for species identification via electron microscopy and subsequent strain characterization. However, this method is highly resource-intensive, time-consuming, and restricted to specialized facilities. Proteomic mass spectrometry, which utilizes viral protein profiling, has potential as an emerging diagnostic tool but has not yet been widely implemented in clinical practice. Additionally, serological detection of IgM and IgG antibodies becomes viable approximately 5–8 days after infection [51]. Serological assays can be used to assess the actual exposure level, transmission range, population immune status, and epidemic dynamics of MPXV, providing a basis for public health responses. The immunological cross-reactivity among OPXV enables the preliminary diagnosis of MPXV under BSL-2 or lower laboratory conditions, but precise and specific diagnosis among viruses of the same genus still requires careful design. The A33, E3, and A56 proteins of MPXV can serve as target proteins for serological diagnosis. A synthetic peptide combination designed based on specific epitopes can efficiently distinguish MPXV infection from VACV or VARV exposure in an ELISA, achieving approximately 86% sensitivity and 90% specificity [52]. Enhanced diagnostic capabilities not only support rapid case identification but also inform targeted containment protocols, thereby optimizing resource allocation and strengthening evidence-based public health responses.

Prevention and control

Vaccines

It is believed that immunization against smallpox will induce humoral immunity for up to 75 years and provide around 85% protection against MPXV. There are three primary vaccinations used worldwide to prevent mpox (as shown in Table 1). Developed from the modified Vaccinia Ankara (MVA) virus, the MVA-BN vaccine (Jynneos) is a widely used third-generation nonreplicating attenuated live vaccination with great immunogenicity and high safety. It usually takes two doses separated about four weeks apart. It has received official regulatory approval for mpox prevention in regions like the United States, the European Union, and Canada, and it is approved for people 18 years of age and older who are at high risk of mpox exposure (such as close contacts of confirmed cases, healthcare workers, and laboratory personnel). MVA vaccination elicited anti-MPXV neutralizing antibodies in young people, with seroprevalence rates of 52 and 63%, respectively, according to recent real-world studies [53]. One dose of the MVA vaccine showed effective at about 80% in men or people at high risk of mpox [54,55]. LC16-KMB, another third-generation vaccine derived from the Lister strain, is a minimally replicating vaccine that is suitable for both adults and children and offers the benefit of single-dose protection with an excellent safety profile. It is currently approved mainly in Japan for the prevention of monkeypox, and the World Health Organization (WHO) advises its use in high-risk populations. The ACAM2000 vaccine, on the other hand, is a second-generation replicating smallpox vaccine that also offers strong immunogenicity and immune protection against mpox. However, its administration method, which usually requires percutaneous or subcutaneous inoculation, can cause local reactions and carry a risk of rare but serious adverse events; as a result, it is primarily authorized in the United States under Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) for preventive vaccination in high-risk groups [56].

In addition, four monkeypox vaccines are now being clinically evaluated. BNT166 (a multivalent mRNA vaccine) and mRNA-1769 (an mRNA vaccination that encodes four MPXV surface proteins) have both entered Phase I/II clinical studies. Complete clinical efficacy data have not yet been released. Preclinical studies indicate that BNT166 boosts antibody and T-cell responses and protects against viral challenge in mouse and non-human primate models [57]. Compared to MVA, non-human primate studies show that mRNA-1769 decreases lesion count and viral replication while producing higher neutralizing and functional antibodies [58]. The MVA strain-based live attenuated monkeypox vaccine has acquired clinical trial approval from China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), making it the first monkeypox vaccination allowed for clinical testing in China. A replication-defective monkeypox vaccine, created by removing 26 gene segments from the Tian Tan strain (VTT) of the smallpox vaccine, has received NMPA clinical study permission [59]. Preclinical research indicates that it generates cross-neutralizing antibodies against MPXV. Currently, there is no domestically manufactured and marketed mpox vaccine in China. But the MVA vector-based live attenuated mpox vaccine is currently undergoing clinical trials and is expected to bridge this gap in the future.

In preclinical studies, novel mRNA and nanoparticle vaccination platforms were used to generate mpox vaccines. The favoured antigens include A35R, B6R, M1R, A29L, H3L, and E8L, among others, and efficient expression is accomplished via the lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery system [66,68]. Several vaccine candidates, including the pentavalent AR-MPVX5, the quadrivalent MPXV-1103, the trivalent AAL/AALI, the bivalent fusion VGPox series, and the single-chain trivalent AMAB series, have shown strong humoral and cellular immune responses in animal models, inducing high titres of MPXV-specific IgG and cross-neutralizing antibodies (against VACV) [60,61,63,69,70]. In terms of protective performance, most vaccinations have achieved 100% survival in mice models infected with VACV or MPXV, considerably lowering viral loads in the lungs and multiple organs and mitigating pathology. Notably, AR-MPVX5 has demonstrated strong immunogenicity and safety in models of immunodeficient primates infected with SIV and non-human primates (NHPs). Moreover, single-cell sequencing and TCR/BCR repertoire investigations have demonstrated vaccine-induced B-cell and T-cell clonal proliferation, specific CDR3 patterns, and long-lasting memory responses [64,70]. Certain vaccines, such as MPXV-1103 and MV2, continue to offer total protection 280 days after the initial shot, according to long-term immunity evaluations [61,62]. Recent research has used the SpyTag/SpyCatcher system to multivalently display IMV antigens (M1R, E8L) and EEV antigens (A35R, B6R) using self-assembling nanoparticles like mi3, ferritin, and lumazine synthase (LuS) [65]. Candidate vaccines such as bivalent mi3-AM, trivalent VLPs, and multivalent LuS nanoparticles induced strong humoral and cellular immunity in mice, creating high-titre cross-neutralizing antibodies, 100% protection against VACV challenge, and substantial reductions in tissue viral levels. Remarkably, the trivalent VLP produced higher neutralizing antibody levels in rhesus macaques than JYNNEOS. Passive serum-transfer effectively protected mice. LuS nanoparticles showed that multivalent combinations provided greater protection than monovalent formulations, with EEV-neutralizing antibodies exhibiting a stronger inverse correlation with viral loads. While the mi3-AM vaccine maintained complete protection up to 160 days post-immunization.

Currently, mpox vaccines also face multiple challenges, such as the durability of immune responses, rational antigen design, applicability to special populations (e.g. people living with HIV), and cross-protective efficacy against emerging viral variants. Overcoming these challenges will depend on more clinically relevant animal models, standardized immune evaluation systems, and systematic clinical trial designs.

Monoclonal antibodies

In recent decades, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have demonstrated significant efficacy against infectious diseases as both preventive and therapeutic agents. A critical application in OPXV infections is their ability to confer postexposure protection even after the onset of disease symptoms, closely mirroring the clinical progression of human infections and therapeutic intervention points [71]. As disease severity increases, so do the requirements for robust antibody potency testing. The high safety profile of modern mAbs positions them as viable alternatives to vaccinia immune globulin (VIG), currently the only approved immunoglobulin-based therapy for OPXV infections.

Vaccinia immunoglobulin (VIG) is the only commercially available antibody preparation in clinical use, exerting non-specific neutralization through polyclonal antibodies. However, its application is limited: qualified donors are scarce, with the U.S. CDC stockpile in 2022 sufficient for only about 500 severely ill patients. Additionally, VIG suffers from batch-to-batch variability in efficacy, the risk of blood-borne pathogen transmission, and the potential for anti-antibody responses (HACA) upon repeated administration [72].

These limitations have spurred a global impetus for the development of novel antibody-based therapeutics, driving innovations across multiple fronts, including antibody formats, target selection, and production platforms (as shown in Table 2). Surface proteins such as M1R, B6R, A29L, A28, and A35R were identified as the main neutralizing antibody targets. Highly neutralizing antibodies targeting M1R, such as M1H11, M3B2, and A138, were obtained by immunizing mouse or human antibody libraries. The mechanism study showed that these antibodies blocked the fusion and entry process of the virus and host cells mainly by binding to the conformational epitope of M1R. In particular, A138 plays a strong neutralizing role by binding to the circular region of M1R; the bispecific antibody bis-M1M3 achieved synergistic neutralization activity by simultaneously targeting two different epitopes on M1R and showed excellent protective effect in the mouse model. The human antibodies hMB621 and hMB668, targeting B6R, neutralized VACV with PRNT₅₀ values of 99.3 and 93.1 ng/mL, respectively, in a complement-dependent manner [73]. They exert non-competing neutralization by recognizing distinct domains (SCR3 4 and SCR1 2). The M1R targeting antibody hmMM1 40 neutralized VACV with a PRNT₅₀ of 0.15 μg/mL by blocking virus receptor interaction via a conformational epitope [74]. In animal models, these antibodies, alone or in combination, significantly reduced viral loads in tissues, with the hmMM1 40 and hMB621 combination showing particularly potent efficacy against MPXV. Highly neutralizing antibodies such as B7C9 and B026 were obtained by immunizing mouse or human antibody library [21,75]. These antibodies play a neutralizing role in the presence of complement by recognizing the SCR or stalk domain of B6R. The mechanism involves the deposition of C1q and C3, thus preventing the virus from spreading between cells. In parallel, monoclonal antibodies, including 3A1 and 9F8, were developed against the A29L protein via hybridoma technology. Among these compounds, 9F8 showed particularly high neutralizing activity against IMVs, with an IC50 below 0.1 μg/mL. Bispecific antibodies such as 9F8-7C9 target A29L and B6R at the same time, showing greater broad-spectrum neutralization activity in vitro and in vivo [76]. A28 protein was identified as a key neutralization target. Monoclonal antibodies against A28 (such as 10M2146 and 8M2110) effectively neutralize MPXV and VACV with the participation of complement by binding to their N-terminal domain [77]. Crystal structure analysis revealed the structural basis of its recognition epitope, and A28 immunized mice could induce complement independent neutralizing antibodies and provide complete protection against lethal VACV challenge. Two recent studies identified the extracellular virion protein A35R as a conserved target (orthologous to VACV A33) via single-cell sequencing approaches. Fully human anti-A35 mAbs bind a groove at the A35 dimer interface, restrict EEV-mediated spread in vitro, and act via direct neutralization plus complement- and Fc-dependent effector functions. Furthermore, they confer robust protective efficacy in animal models. These data support A35-specific mAbs as candidates for passive immunization and inform epitope-focused vaccine design [78,79].

Additionally, a hyperdiverse fully synthetic antibody library was utilized to isolate a neutralizing nanobody targeting the MPXV M1R membrane protein [71,84]. Due to the limited affinity of monovalent nanobodies (with KD values in the hundred-nM range), researchers used engineering strategies such as bivalent or trivalent tandem fusion, or Fc fusion, to increase affinity to the pM-to-nM range (an increase of over 400-fold) and significantly improve neutralizing activity against VACV and MPXV. The trivalent or Fc-fusion versions attained IC50 values of 2–3 μg/mL and neutralization effectiveness of above 90%. Additionally, several bivalent M1R-targeting nanobodies show cross-neutralizing action against CPXV, MPXV Clade I, and Clade II. Preclinical studies in a VACV (WR strain) mouse infection model demonstrated 100% survival, accompanied by reduced viral loads and mitigated tissue damage. The neutralization mechanism was elucidated via high-resolution crystallography of the antibody–antigen complex, providing a structural basis for drug design. The neutralizing nanobodies identify a conserved conformational epitope on M1R that is different from that of the conventional antibody 7D11, and this epitope is highly conserved among OPXV, according to epitope analysis. Owing to their low molecular weight and enhanced mucosal permeability, nanobodies such as Nb-M1R show promise for prophylaxis against respiratory exposure. The intranasal administration of Nb-M1R conferred complete protection in murine models, highlighting its potential for use as an inhalational countermeasure [85]. The engineered nanobodies thus represent potential drug candidates for the prevention and treatment of OPXV infections.

For the antibodies targeting another MPXV protein E8, a crucial attachment protein is on the surface of the IMV. E8 facilitates viral entry by attaching to host cell glycosaminoglycans, making it an ideal target for antiviral antibody development [86]. Researchers successfully isolated three fully human monoclonal antibodies targeting E8 from peripheral blood of recombinant vaccinia vaccination patients [83]. C5 and C9 showed ultra-high affinity for MPXV E8 and VACV D8, with C9 neutralizing MPXV (IC₅₀ = 3.0 μg/mL) by binding to 18 critical residues on E8. The neutralizing efficiency of C5, C9, and F8 against VACV was increased by dozens to almost a hundredfold in the presence of complement, according to a mechanism combining viral attachment blockage and complement-dependent cytotoxicity. While E8 antibodies reduced weight loss, expedited recovery, and decreased lung viral loads in animal models, their protective efficacy was limited. The antibody combination (C5 + C9 + F8) showed synergistic increase in in vitro neutralization experiments (IC₅₀ as low as 0.4 μg/mL), emphasizing the significance of a multi-epitope targeting strategy. Furthermore, the insensitivity of MPXV to complement-enhanced neutralization, possibly due to the absence of the MOPICE complement inhibitory protein, highlights the significance of viral immune evasion strategies on therapeutic efficacy [10].

Despite substantial progress, antibody drugs face challenges including immune escape and epitope competition. Future research should prioritize epitope complementarity, leveraging the small size and mucosal penetration of nanobodies alongside the high affinity of conventional mAbs to develop multitarget synergistic therapies. Concurrently, establishing dynamic primate infection models will be essential for verifying antibody neutralization against both IMV and EEV, while exploring novel delivery systems (e.g. inhalable nanobodies) to enhance mucosal immune protection. These advances not only offer immediate solutions for MPXV control but also reshape antiviral drug development paradigms through interdisciplinary innovations in structural and synthetic biology. With sustained international funding, antibody drugs are poised to become a cornerstone in ending the mpox pandemic.

Small-molecule inhibitors

Small-molecule antiviral agents exert antiviral effects by targeting critical stages of the viral life cycle through diverse mechanisms. Inhibition of viral replication is achieved by direct or indirect action on viral RNA polymerase, interfering with genomic RNA transcription; for instance, the nucleoside analogue prodrug remdesivir generates an active nucleoside triphosphate metabolite that competitively inhibits ATP incorporation, disrupting viral RNA synthesis. Inhibition of viral protease activity targets proteases essential for replication and assembly, such as SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro; the peptidomimetic inhibitor nirmatrelvir exemplifies this mechanism by blocking 3CLpro-mediated processing of viral polyproteins [87,88]. Interference with viral genome replication fidelity via mutagenesis induces error accumulation, as demonstrated by molnupiravir, whose active form incorporates into nascent RNA chains via viral polymerase to cause lethal mutagenesis. Blockade of viral cellular entry occurs through binding to viral surface proteins (e.g. influenza haemagglutinin), preventing membrane fusion; umifenovir (Arbidol) utilizes this approach to inhibit viral invasion [89,90]. Modulation of the host immune system enhances antiviral immunity alongside direct effects, illustrated by azvudine which combines direct antiviral activity with host immune activation [91].

Currently, the primary small-molecule agents utilized for mpox treatment lack formal approval for this specific indication; their application is predicated upon established antiviral activity against OPXV and historical clinical practice (as shown in Table 3). Tecovirimat, the most extensively utilized agent, functions as a specific inhibitor of the OPXV envelope protein VP37, blocking virion release. It offers advantages, including favourable oral and intravenous bioavailability and a relatively acceptable safety profile, with fatigue and headache being common adverse events. Animal studies have demonstrated significant viral load reduction and complete protection in nonhuman primates when the virus is administered early postinfection, whereas clinical observations in humans support its efficacy in ameliorating symptoms and promoting viral clearance. Although it was approved by the FDA for smallpox in 2018 and granted expanded access authorization for mpox in 2022, its use necessitates vigilance regarding potential resistance development (i.e. viral mutations with prolonged therapy), drug–drug interactions, and renal function monitoring. Cidofovir, a broad-spectrum anti-DNA viral agent that inhibits viral DNA polymerase, has demonstrated efficacy in preventing disease progression when it is administered within 48 h postinfection in animal models [92,93]. However, its clinical utility is significantly constrained by pronounced nephrotoxicity requiring rigorous renal monitoring and dose adjustment, coupled with poor oral bioavailability necessitating intravenous infusion, which limits practicality [94]. Although it was approved in 1996 for cytomegalovirus retinitis in AIDS patients, it remains unapproved for mpox [95]. To address the limitations of cidofovir, a lipid-conjugated prodrug, brincidofovir, which has superior oral bioavailability, markedly reduced nephrotoxicity, and enhanced in vitro antiviral potency against OPXV (evidenced by a lower half-maximal effective concentration, EC50), was developed [96]. Animal studies indicate robust prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy against mpox, even with delayed administration. Although it was approved by the FDA for smallpox in 2021, it similarly lacks specific approval for mpox [97] and its use still carries risks of gastrointestinal disturbances and hepatotoxicity, mandating hepatic function surveillance. Collectively, while these existing agents provide critical therapeutic options for mpox, their limitations, including the resistance potential of tecovirimat, the nephrotoxicity of cidofovir, and the hepatointestinal toxicity of brincidofovir, underscore the ongoing imperative to develop safer, more efficacious, and specifically targeted antiviral therapies for this purpose.

Several pharmaceuticals are now undergoing clinical trials. NIOCH-14, a tecovirimat prodrug, displayed acceptable safety and tolerability in a Phase I clinical study. Its mode of action is similar to that of tecovirimat, since it inhibits virus maturation and release by targeting the viral envelope protein VP37 [102]. ASC10, an oral double prodrug of molnupiravir, is transformed into its active metabolite in vivo and suppresses viral replication by causing mistakes in viral RNA transcription [104]. The clinical study for the mpox indication has been approved to begin in the United States. Additionally, the topically applied nucleoside analogue trifluridine acts as a protein function inhibitor and is utilized to treat ocular disorders such as keratitis caused by MPXV [105]. Several preclinical therapeutic candidates have demonstrated substantial promise in the field of novel target research. Inhibitors of the viral RNA capping system, such as the 7-deaza-S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) analogue TO507, which targets the VP39 2′-O methyltransferase, have shown substantial inhibitory effects at both the enzymatic and cellular levels [106]. Peptidomimetic covalent inhibitors (e.g. substance A3) developed using structural biology target the I7L core protease, blocking its activity at nanomolar doses and efficiently decreasing vaccinia and MPXV propagation in cells [107]. In the host-targeting approach ibrutinib, a Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor, greatly prolongs survival and decreases viral load in a VACV-infected mice model by reducing BTK phosphorylation and nuclear translocation [108].

Challenges and conclusions

Effective global control of mpox remains hindered by an interplay of virological imperatives and entrenched sociobiological realities. At the virological level, MPXV is a DNA virus and, although its mutation rate is lower than that of RNA viruses such as influenza and SARS-CoV-2, its genome undergoes gradual evolution through gene mutations, deletions, and recombination, which can facilitate immune evasion or accelerate transmission. Notably, sustained human-to-human transmission has been associated with a specific pattern of genomic change: the host antiviral factor APOBEC3 exerts continuous mutational pressure on the viral genome, producing a restricted but functionally relevant degree of genetic diversity that may facilitate adaptation to novel transmission networks. This observation suggests that host-driven genome editing can influence the evolutionary trajectory of poxviruses, indicating that molecular host–virus interactions may contribute to epidemiological dynamics even in DNA viruses with relatively low baseline mutation rates. Moreover, the co-circulation of two distinct clades, Clade II having driven the 2022 global epidemic and Clade I exhibiting heightened severity, underscores the need for nuanced genomic surveillance and a deeper appreciation of viral adaptability.

In addition to virological considerations, structural and societal factors present further obstacles to effective control. Endemic regions, particularly in Africa, are characterized by health system limitations and unequal resource distribution, reflected in shortages of trained healthcare personnel, diagnostic reagents, and laboratory infrastructure. These constraints are intensified by regional conflicts, which interfere with disease surveillance, compromise medical facilities, and contribute to the geographic expansion of outbreaks beyond established endemic areas. At the same time, incomplete knowledge of zoonotic reservoir species and transmission dynamics continues to hinder source control efforts. The situation is further complicated by societal stigmatization, which has been amplified by the disproportionate case burden among men who have sex with men (MSM). This has contributed to persistent misconceptions about risk, resulting in reduced symptom reporting, delayed healthcare seeking, and incomplete case ascertainment. Stigma also limits equitable access to preventive and therapeutic interventions, including vaccination, diagnostic testing, and treatment, thereby weakening the overall public health response. In addition, delays in genomic surveillance and epidemiological analysis constrain the timely refinement of containment measures.

Taken together, these factors indicate a persistent challenge in mpox control. Ongoing viral evolution includes genomic changes that may enhance transmissibility or facilitate immune evasion. Addressing this evolution requires a public health response capable of timely adaptation. However, such adaptation is constrained by existing sociocultural biases and structural disparities. A multisectoral approach is therefore needed to address stigma, strengthen public health engagement, and improve mechanisms for global data sharing and resource coordination

A coordinated strategy may help address these challenges. Such a strategy would integrate nucleic acid-based diagnostics with high sensitivity and specificity, deploy next-generation vaccines, including mRNA-based formulations, alongside monoclonal antibody therapies, and advance the development of novel antiviral agents. The distinct biology of intracellular mature virions (IMV) and extracellular enveloped virions (EEV) has implications for the design of next-generation vaccines. Current vaccine-induced immunity is directed primarily against IMV surface proteins. In contrast, EEV particles disseminate more rapidly and exhibit relative resistance to complement-mediated neutralization. This resistance is further amplified in MPXV by the absence of the MOPICE complement regulatory protein. These differences suggest that incorporating EEV-specific antigens may be necessary to broaden protective coverage.

Repurposed smallpox antivirals and novel antibody therapies provide potential treatment options. However, confirming their efficacy against evolving viral strains remains a challenge. The interaction between antibody-based interventions and viral adaptation also merits attention. MPXV shows limited sensitivity to complement-enhanced neutralization, and therapeutic pressure may favour epitope competition and immune escape. The long-term utility of monoclonal antibody therapies will therefore depend on strategies that account for viral structural plasticity. These include multi-epitope targeting and approaches that combine the high affinity of nanobodies with the effector function of conventional antibodies.

Future work may focus on optimizing vaccination protocols, including dose-sparing approaches and equitable distribution, and on accelerating clinical trials for emerging therapeutics. Additional areas of investigation include the basic mechanisms of MPXV pathogenesis, the identification of new antiviral targets, and the evaluation of small-molecule inhibitors. A broader strategy that integrates global surveillance, targeted public health guidance, and sustained international collaboration may contribute to more effective outbreak containment, reduce disease burden, and strengthen health system preparedness for the evolving mpox pandemic.
