Exploring the potential causal association between oral microbiota and heart failure using Mendelian randomization in an East Asian population
Authors: Shaoyi Peng, Kaiyuan Li, Peng Liu, Lei Wang, Yilong Man
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Growing evidence suggests that the gut microbiota is closely associated with the risk of developing heart failure (HF). However, the potential impact of oral microbiota on HF has received far less attention. Using instrumental variable from the latest genome-wide association study data in East Asian populations, we evaluated the genetic predisposition to HF associated with 455 salivary microbiota and 540 tongue microbiota. The HF dataset was derived from Biobank Japan, including 9413 HF cases and 203,040 controls. The inverse-variance weighted method was used to assess potential causal associations between oral microbiota and HF. Robustness of the results was confirmed through MR-Egger regression intercept test and MR-PRESSO global test. Inverse-variance weighted analysis identified 33 tongue microbiota, including Veillonella atypica 1903 (odds ratio [OR] = 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.74–0.97, P = .013), and 28 salivary microbiota, including Centipeda massiliensis 3300 (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.67–0.94, P = .007), that may be negatively associated with HF. Conversely, 25 tongue microbiota, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae 1744 (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.16–1.94, P = .001), and 30 salivary microbiota, including Fusobacterium massiliense 586 (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.31–1.63, P = .017), showed a potential positive association with HF. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of these results. This study has preliminarily identified multiple oral microbiota that may have a causal association with the risk of developing HF, providing a more reliable theoretical basis for exploring the microbiota-related mechanisms underlying HF.

1. Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome resulting from impaired ventricular pumping or filling function, leading to an insufficient cardiac output to meet the body’s metabolic demands. It represents the severe stage of various heart diseases and significantly impacts patients’ quality of life and life expectancy.[1,2] The global incidence of HF is rising yearly, particularly in aging societies, and East Asia is no exception, facing an increasing burden of HF.[3–5]

The oral microbiota refers to the complex community of microorganisms residing in the human mouth, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and more. The diversity of these microorganisms is closely linked to human health.[6,7] The oral microbiota helps maintain the ecological balance in the mouth, protecting against harmful pathogens and supporting digestive and immune functions. However, when the oral microbiota becomes imbalanced, it can lead to oral diseases, such as periodontitis and dental caries, and potentially affect systemic health.[8,9]

In recent years, research has identified a potential link between oral microbiota dysbiosis and the development of cardiovascular diseases.[10–12] Studies have shown that patients with periodontitis have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, and the migration of oral pathogens may affect cardiac function through mechanisms such as inflammatory responses, immune reactions, and endothelial dysfunction.[13,14] Despite these findings suggesting an association between oral microbiota and cardiovascular health, the causal association between oral microbiota and HF has yet to be clearly established.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a method that utilizes genetic variants to infer causal associations between exposures and diseases.[15,16] MR studies use single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to overcome confounding and reverse causality issues present in traditional observational studies. By employing MR, genetic variants associated with the oral microbiota can be used as IVs to more effectively validate whether oral microbiota truly impacts the risk of HF, providing stronger evidence for potential clinical interventions.

This study utilizes the most recent and largest dataset of oral microbiota in East Asian populations to identify bacterial species that may have a causal association with HF risk. The aim is to provide a more reliable theoretical foundation for exploring the mechanisms related to HF and to further promote the prevention, treatment, and early diagnosis of HF.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

This study examines the potential causal association between oral microbiota as the exposure variable and HF as the outcome variable. The MR analysis follows 3 core assumptions and is based on previously published research and publicly available summary data. Therefore, no additional ethical approval or informed consent from participants is required for this study. This study also complies with the STROBE-MR guidelines (Fig. 1A) (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q176).

2.2. Data sources

The oral microbiota data used in this study were derived from an East Asian population, representing the first large-scale genome-wide association study for this group. The study analyzed 2017 tongue dorsum samples and 1915 saliva samples using high-depth whole-genome sequencing. The dataset includes 309 tongue microbiota (n = 2017) and 285 saliva microbiota (n = 1915) (Table 1). All samples met strict inclusion criteria to ensure a variant call rate of at least 98%, with an average sequencing depth exceeding 20×. Principal component analysis was used to exclude population stratification, and related individuals were excluded based on pairwise identity-by-descent analysis. Additional stringent criteria were applied, such as a minimum mean sequencing depth of 8×, a Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium P-value >10–5, and a genotype call rate of no <98% for any variant. After thorough quality control, a final cohort of 2984 participants was established, comprising 2017 individuals with tongue dorsum samples and 1915 individuals with saliva samples. This resulted in a dataset containing ~10 million variants, covering both common and low-frequency variants with a minor allele frequency of no <0.5%. This study includes specific information on sample collection, sequencing techniques, microbiota feature construction, observational studies, and genotyping analyses.[17]

2.3. Selection of genetic IVs

In MR studies, SNPs are used as IVs to mitigate reverse causality and the inaccuracies inherent in observational epidemiological analyses. When the SNP selection threshold was set at P < 5 × 10−8, the results showed a limited number of SNPs associated with oral microbiota. Given the sparse availability of SNP loci in the oral microbiota at the P < 5 × 10−8 threshold, we instead selected SNP loci with a threshold of P < 5 × 10−6. Although P < 5 × 10−8 is considered the conventional genome-wide significance level, this threshold yielded very few SNPs associated with oral microbiota traits. Therefore, we adopted a threshold of P < 5 × 10−6, consistent with prior microbiome MR studies, to increase the number of available instruments. We further ensured robustness by removing SNPs in linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.001) and retaining only those with F-statistics >10. The F-statistic was calculated using the following formula: F = R2(N − K − 1)/[K(1 − R2)], where R2 represents the proportion of variance in the exposure variable explained by each IV (Fig. 1B).

2.4. MR analysis

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was employed as the primary analysis approach to evaluate the causal association between oral microbiota and HF. In this method, the causal effects of different genetic variants on traits are weighted by their inverse variance, making IVW the standard approach for MR analysis. When estimating causal effects using the IVW method, a random-effects model is applied if there is evidence of heterogeneity; otherwise, a fixed-effects model is used. The results are presented as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) to reflect the causal impact of oral microbiota on HF.

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

This study utilized the Cochran Q test to assess heterogeneity among the SNPs, and the MR-Egger intercept test was performed to detect the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. A significant deviation of the intercept from zero (Egger intercept <0.05) indicates potential pleiotropy in the IVs.

2.6. Statistical methods

MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The R packages “TwoSampleMR” (https://mrcieu.github.io/TwoSampleMR/). Differences were considered statistically significant only when the P-value was <.05.

3. Results

3.1. Genetic instrument variables

Based on the IVs selection criteria, this study identified 6236 SNPs from the tongue microbiota and 6587 SNPs from the salivary microbiota for subsequent analysis (Tables S2 and S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q177).

3.2. Causal association between tongue microbiota and HF

The IVW analysis results revealed that 58 species of the tongue microbiota might have a potential causal association with the risk of developing HF (Figs. 2A and 3A). Among them, 33 species, such as Veillonella atypica 1903 (OR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.74–0.97, P = .013), were potentially negatively associated with HF, while 25 species, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae 1744 (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.16–1.94, P = .001), were potentially positively associated with HF. The Cochran Q test indicated no heterogeneity (P > .05), and no horizontal pleiotropy was detected in the analysis of the 58 tongue microbiota (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q177).

3.3. Causal association between saliva microbiota and HF

The IVW analysis results indicated that 58 species of the saliva microbiota might have a potential causal association with the risk of developing HF (Figs. 2B and 3B). Among these, 28 species, such as Centipeda massiliensis 3300 (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.67–0.94, P = .007), were potentially negatively associated with HF, while 30 species, such as Fusobacterium massiliense 586 (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.31–1.63, P = .017), were potentially positively associated with HF. The Cochran Q test indicated no heterogeneity (P > .05), and no horizontal pleiotropy was detected in the analysis of the 58 saliva microbiota (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q177).

4. Discussion

This study is the first to utilize oral microbiota and HF datasets from an East Asian population to explore the potential causal associations between tongue and salivary microbiota and HF. Through the use of MR, we preliminarily identified several microbiota species that are significantly associated with congestive HF, providing a theoretical basis for their potential roles in the pathophysiology of HF. These findings offer new insights into the complex associations between tongue and salivary microbiota and HF.

Our results suggest that the oral microbiota comprises both protective and harmful bacteria, and their presence may influence systemic inflammation and cardiovascular health. One of the key observations in this study is the negative association of bacteria such as Prevotella loescheii (from the tongue) and Haemophilus (from saliva) with HF. These bacteria are likely exerting protective effects against the development of HF, primarily through anti-inflammatory pathways.[18] P loescheii, for instance, has been shown in previous research to produce short-chain fatty acids, which have potent anti-inflammatory properties. Short-chain fatty acids inhibit the production of key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which are involved in systemic inflammation, a critical factor in the development and progression of HF.[19–21] By reducing systemic inflammation, these bacteria may help maintain vascular integrity and protect against the atherosclerotic changes that often underlie HF.[22] Similarly, Haemophilus has been linked to immune modulation and the reduction of inflammatory responses, contributing to a more stable cardiovascular environment and potentially lowering the risk of HF.[23,24]

In contrast, this study also identified certain bacterial species that appear to exacerbate HF risk. Notably, Fusobacterium nucleatum and Prevotella unclassified, found in both the tongue and saliva microbiota, were positively associated with HF.[25,26] These bacteria are well known for their pro-inflammatory roles, often implicated in oral infections and systemic inflammation. F nucleatum has been shown to release extracellular vesicles and lipopolysaccharides, which trigger a robust immune response characterized by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α.[27,28] These cytokines contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation, which is a recognized driver of endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and subsequent HF.[29] Chronic inflammation can lead to damage to the endothelial lining of blood vessels, promoting the formation of atherosclerotic plaques and ultimately impairing cardiovascular function, which is a critical pathway in the development of HF.[30]

The involvement of pro-inflammatory cytokines in HF has been well established in prior research.[31] Elevated levels of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α are frequently observed in HF patients, and these molecules are thought to exacerbate disease progression through their effects on the heart and vasculature. In this study, the bacteria positively associated with HF may contribute directly to this inflammatory environment. For instance, F nucleatum, a known pathogen in periodontal disease, can migrate from the oral cavity into the systemic circulation, where it activates immune responses that promote vascular inflammation.[32] This persistent inflammatory state can further impair cardiac function, particularly in individuals already predisposed to cardiovascular disease.

The association between oral microbiota and cardiovascular health is likely bidirectional, with systemic inflammation affecting both the heart and the oral microbiota. It is possible that the presence of specific oral pathogens not only exacerbates systemic inflammation but also leads to immune dysregulation. Some bacteria, such as S pneumoniae, can evade host immune detection, resulting in chronic infections that perpetuate low-grade inflammation. This chronic inflammation can slowly degrade cardiovascular health, particularly in the presence of existing comorbidities such as hypertension or diabetes, both of which are common in HF patients.

Another critical mechanism through which oral microbiota might influence HF is endothelial dysfunction.[33,34] Endothelial cells, which line the blood vessels, play an essential role in maintaining vascular tone and integrity. Bacterial products such as lipopolysaccharide from F nucleatum can activate Toll-like receptor 4 on endothelial cells, initiating the NF-κB signaling pathway and resulting in increased production of pro-inflammatory mediators.[35,36] This inflammation can lead to endothelial cell apoptosis and reduced nitric oxide availability, impairing vasodilation and promoting vascular stiffness.[37] As vascular stiffness increases, the heart must work harder to pump blood, which can ultimately lead to left ventricular hypertrophy and HF. This process suggests a direct mechanistic link between oral microbiota-induced inflammation and the development of cardiovascular pathology that leads to HF.

Our findings also suggest potential avenues for clinical intervention. Maintaining oral hygiene, regular dental checkups, and the development of targeted probiotics or antimicrobial agents may help modulate the oral microbiota to reduce cardiovascular risk. For instance, probiotic strains that promote anti-inflammatory oral environments could potentially lower systemic inflammation and, thereby, HF risk. Future clinical trials are needed to test the efficacy and safety of these microbiota-based interventions in cardiovascular disease prevention.

While this study sheds light on the potential causal roles of tongue and salivary microbiota in HF, it also underscores the need for further research to validate these associations and clarify underlying biological mechanisms. One major limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the data, which precludes establishing temporal relationships between shifts in oral microbiota composition and HF development. Although MR helps mitigate confounding and reverse causality, unmeasured factors such as diet, oral hygiene, and environmental exposures may still influence the observed associations. Moreover, oral microbiota profiles and host genetic backgrounds can vary significantly across populations. Since our study was based on East Asian cohorts, the findings may be affected by population-specific characteristics. Therefore, longitudinal studies in diverse populations, particularly Western or multi-ethnic groups, are warranted to strengthen causal inference and assess the generalizability of our results.

5. Conclusion

This study contributes to a growing body of evidence that implicates oral microbiota in the pathogenesis of systemic diseases, particularly congestive HF. The findings highlight the dual role of oral bacteria, where some species may offer protective effects by reducing inflammation, while others promote systemic inflammation and vascular dysfunction, thereby increasing HF risk. These insights suggest that targeting the oral microbiota, potentially through improved oral hygiene or probiotic interventions, could represent a novel avenue for reducing cardiovascular disease risk and improving outcomes in HF patients. Future research should aim to elucidate the complex interactions between oral microbiota and systemic health, and to explore the potential for microbiota-based therapies in the management of cardiovascular diseases.
