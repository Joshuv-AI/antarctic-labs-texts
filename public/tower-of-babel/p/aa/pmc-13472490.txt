Parathyroid autotransplantation for inadvertently resected or devascularized glands during total thyroidectomy: large retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mingjie Jiang, Yongchao Yu, Zan Jiao, Tong Wu, Ke Jiang, Zulu Ye, Zhongyuan Yang, Weichao Chen, Ankui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough recent guidelines recommend parathyroid autotransplantation for inadvertent excision of ischaemic glands, its benefit for the recovery of parathyroid function after total thyroidectomy remains uncertain.MethodsThis retrospective study analysed patients with normal parathyroid hormone (PTH) concentrations who underwent primary total thyroidectomy at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center between September 2013 and November 2024. Postoperative hypoparathyroidism and PTH trajectories were compared across three groups: autotransplantation, inadvertent parathyroid resection, and all glands preserved in situ. Long-term recovery was modelled using generalized additive mixed models (GAMM).ResultsOf 7539 eligible patients, 441 (5.8%) underwent autotransplantation and 814 (10.8%) had inadvertent parathyroid resection. Higher proportions of parathyroid autotransplantation or inadvertent resection were observed among patients undergoing central neck dissection, particularly bilateral procedures. Postoperative (POD) 1 hypoparathyroidism (PTH < 15 pg/ml) occurred in 64.9% and 62.4% of patients in the autotransplantation and inadvertent resection groups, respectively, compared with 50.7% of patients with in situ gland preservation. At 1 month, hypoparathyroidism was present in 17.1% and 22.5% of patients in the autotransplantation and inadvertent resection groups, respectively, compared with 11.9% of patients with in situ gland preservation. Among patients with POD1 hypoparathyroidism, the recovery of parathyroid function at 1 month was similar in the autotransplantation and in situ preservation groups (∼72% and ∼74%, respectively), but lower in the group with inadvertent resection (∼62%). GAMMs showed poorest long-term recovery after inadvertent resection, whereas recovery in the autotransplantation group approached in situ preservation without clear superiority.ConclusionsParathyroid autotransplantation improved long-term recovery compared with inadvertent resection but did not outperform in situ preservation. These findings support autotransplantation for inadvertently resected parathyroid glands, but not for devascularized glands.Keywords: parathyroid function recovery, PTH dynamics

Introduction

Hypoparathyroidism is one of the most common complications of thyroidectomy, particularly after total thyroidectomy combined with central neck dissection. Although meticulous surgical technique is fundamental for preventing hypoparathyroidism, inadvertent parathyroid gland resection or devascularization often occurs. To mitigate the risk of inadvertent parathyroid gland resection or devascularization, autotransplantation of devascularized or inadvertently removed parathyroid glands has been proposed1. However, most early clinical studies investigated parathyroid autotransplantation in patients with primary hyperparathyroidism, where glands are pathological and characterized by high proliferative potential2–5. The efficacy of autotransplanting normal parathyroid tissue in the context of thyroidectomy remains less well known.

To date, no high-quality randomized clinical trials have assessed the effectiveness of parathyroid autotransplantation. Most available evidence for parathyroid autotransplantation comes from retrospective studies with relatively small sample sizes. Although the American Thyroid Association recommends autotransplantation for devascularized or inadvertently resected parathyroid glands during thyroidectomy1,6, the supporting evidence is limited and controversial7–11. Conflicting results have been reported across studies, and even systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reached inconsistent conclusions12–14. Moreover, most comparisons have been made between patients who underwent autotransplantation and those whose parathyroid glands were preserved in situ. Because autotransplantation is performed only when glands are inadvertently resected or devascularized, which does not apply to in situ preserved glands, such comparisons may not reflect the true efficacy of autotransplantation and could bias the results. For example, one early study8 reported that autotransplantation reduced permanent hypoparathyroidism, but, in the same cohort, there were significantly more resected glands in patients who did not undergo autotransplantation. In line with the inaccurate comparison, recent meta-analyses have suggested that parathyroid autotransplantation may be associated with a higher risk of transient or protracted hypoparathyroidism13, or may not reduce the incidence of permanent hypoparathyroidism14.

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the real-world efficacy of parathyroid autotransplantation in a large cohort of consecutive patients undergoing primary total thyroidectomy at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. Specifically, the rate of hypoparathyroidism was compared among patients with parathyroid glands preserved in situ, parathyroid glands identified in resected specimens (inadvertent resection), or autotransplanted parathyroid glands. In addition, dynamic changes in postoperative parathyroid hormone (PTH) concentrations were examined across these groups. To assess the relative efficacy of autotransplantation in the context of devascularized glands, the study focused on patients with hypoparathyroidism on postoperative day (POD) 1 (POD1 PTH < 15 pg/ml).

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study used data from a prospectively maintained database at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (B2023-455-01). Consecutive patients who underwent primary total thyroidectomy between September 2013 and November 2024 were identified. Parathyroid function was assessed before surgery, immediately after surgery (≤ 24 hours), approximately 1 month after surgery, and within 6 months after surgery. Recovery of parathyroid function was defined as recovery of the PTH concentration from < 15 to ≥ 15 pg/ml. Postoperative PTH recovery trajectories were modelled, and the area under the PTH–time curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the benefit of parathyroid autotransplantation.

Surgical procedure and intraoperative management

All procedures were performed by high-volume head and neck surgeons using standardized thyroidectomy techniques. Following total thyroidectomy and any indicated central neck dissection (unilateral or bilateral), the surgical team systematically inspected the resected specimen for inadvertently removed parathyroid glands and assessed the vascularity of parathyroid glands preserved in situ.

Parathyroid ischaemia/devascularization was judged intraoperatively based on macroscopic findings, including pallor, dusky discoloration, lack of capsular bleeding after needle prick, and the absence of an identifiable vascular pedicle. When a parathyroid gland was identified in the fresh specimen during surgery or when a preserved gland appeared ischaemic, autotransplantation could be performed at the discretion of the surgeon. The transplanted gland was minced into small fragments and implanted into the ipsilateral sternocleidomastoid muscle. Implantation sites were marked with a non-absorbable suture or clip to allow future identification. No cryopreservation was performed.

All patients underwent intact PTH and serum calcium testing within 30 days before surgery and within 24 hours after surgery. Patients with immediate postoperative hypoparathyroidism were scheduled for subsequent follow-up PTH measurements for at least 6 months. Patients with PTH < 15 pg/ml were considered to have hypoparathyroidism. The administration of calcium and vitamin D supplementation was prophylactic or symptom-driven, and calcium was reassessed at follow-up.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: newly diagnosed benign or malignant thyroid disease; no history of previous neck surgery, and receipt of primary total thyroidectomy at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. The exclusion criteria were: procedures other than total thyroidectomy; coexisting benign or malignant parathyroid disease; concurrent partial or total laryngectomy; missing preoperative or immediate postoperative PTH measurements; and preoperative PTH < 15 or > 65 pg/ml. Thirty-three patients with simultaneous parathyroid autotransplantation and inadvertently resected parathyroid glands were excluded from the study.

Longitudinal modelling using generalized additive mixed models

Postoperative PTH recovery trajectories were modelled with all available measurements using generalized additive mixed models (GAMM)15, which allow flexible characterization of non-linear longitudinal trends while accounting for within-patient correlation. Models incorporated group-specific smooth functions of time (0–180 days) and patient-level random intercepts to adjust for baseline heterogeneity and repeated measures. Non-linear time effects were modelled using cubic regression splines, with penalization applied to balance goodness-of-fit and model complexity.

From the fitted models, population-average predictions on a common 0–180-day grid were obtained in each group and the AUC was computed by the trapezoidal rule. The AUC of the GAMM reflects the cumulative postoperative parathyroid function over time, integrating both the magnitude and duration of PTH recovery. A lower AUC indicates a prolonged or insufficient recovery trajectory, which may reflect impaired parathyroid reserve or delayed functional restoration. Missing follow-up values were handled under a missing-at-random assumption inherent to mixed-effects estimations. Analyses were performed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the mgcv and nlme packages.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as the median with interquartile range (i.q.r.), and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Group comparisons were made using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the unpaired t test for continuous variables. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient inclusion

From September 2013 to November 2024, 21 519 patients underwent thyroid surgery with or without lymph node dissection at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. Of these patients, 20 807 were diagnosed with thyroid disease and underwent primary surgery. Because unilateral thyroid surgery rarely causes hypoparathyroidism due to preservation of the contralateral glands, only patients with primary total thyroidectomy, normal preoperative parathyroid function, and complete data were included in this study. Ultimately, 7539 patients met these criteria (Fig. 1). Of these patients, 441 (5.8%) underwent parathyroid autotransplantation and 814 (10.8%) had parathyroid tissue identified in surgical specimens (inadvertent resection). Among the 441 patients who underwent parathyroid autotransplantation, 402 underwent transplantation of one parathyroid gland, 37 underwent transplantation of two glands, and 2 underwent transplantation of three glands.

Patient characteristics

There were no significant differences in the sex distribution among patients with all parathyroid glands preserved in situ (In situ group), patients with parathyroid glands in surgical specimens (In specimen group), and patients who underwent parathyroid autotransplantation (Autotransplantation group). However, patients in Autotransplantation group were slightly but significantly younger than patients in the other two groups. Concerning surgical methods, most patients underwent total thyroidectomy with bilateral (5074, 67.3%) or unilateral (2016, 26.7%) lymph node dissection, reflecting the predominance of thyroid carcinoma in this cohort. In addition, most patients receiving parathyroid autotransplantation underwent bilateral lymph node dissection. Accordingly, the rate of parathyroid autotransplantation was significantly higher among patients with bilateral dissection (7.6%) than among those with unilateral (2.5%) or no (1.3%) dissection. Similarly, inadvertent parathyroid resection was more frequent in patients with bilateral dissection (11.7%), followed by unilateral dissection (10.0%) and no dissection (4.2%). Preoperative baseline PTH concentrations were similar among the three groups. However, postoperative PTH levels were significantly lower in In specimen group and Autotransplantation group (Table 1).

Long-term dynamics in PTH concentrations

To evaluate postoperative trends in parathyroid function, GAMMs were used, with AUC as a surrogate marker to present the overall recovery capacity of parathyroid function rather than a single time-point measurement. Across all patients, recovery of parathyroid function was best in patients with in situ parathyroid gland preservation (In situ group), followed by parathyroid autotransplantation (Autotransplantation group), and worst after inadvertent parathyroid gland resection (In specimen group; Fig. 2a). In the bilateral dissection subgroup, recovery of parathyroid function in Autotransplantation group was similar to that in In situ group whereas parathyroid function remained impaired in In specimen group (Fig. 2b).

Among patients with POD1 hypoparathyroidism, autotransplantation of parathyroid glands achieved long-term recovery comparable to that of in situ preservation, both in the overall cohort and in the bilateral dissection subgroup, whereas inadvertent resection was associated with persistently impaired recovery (Fig. 2c,d).

Discussion

In this large cohort of consecutive patients undergoing primary total thyroidectomy, parathyroid autotransplantation not reduce, but slightly but significantly increased, the rates of postoperative hypoparathyroidism compared with in situ preservation. However, when the analysis was restricted to patients with POD1 hypoparathyroidism, recovery rates achieved in the group with parathyroid autotransplantation were comparable to those in the group with in situ preservation.

This study is the largest cohort study to date systematically evaluating the efficacy of parathyroid autotransplantation, and showed that parathyroid autotransplantation compensates for the detrimental effects of incidental parathyroidectomy, particularly in the long term. Patients undergoing parathyroid autotransplantation exhibited superior recovery than those whose glands were inadvertently resected, particularly over the long term. These findings suggest that parathyroid autotransplantation should be considered for inadvertently resected parathyroid glands, but not for devascularized glands.

Parathyroid autotransplantation inherently involves surgical manipulation and begins with the removal of parathyroid glands, which may cause additional tissue injury, especially for in situ parathyroid glands. Consistent with previous studies, the present study data showed a higher incidence of immediate postoperative hypoparathyroidism following autotransplantation7,16,17. This impairment typically resolves within 1 month, aligning with experimental findings. Specifically, electron microscopy has shown ischaemic degeneration of transplanted parathyroid tissue within the first postoperative week, with regeneration starting during the second week18. Functional recovery begins at around 1 week after surgery and is usually complete by 1 month18–20. Prospective studies similarly identified parathyroid autotransplantation as an independent risk factor for transient hypocalcemia but not for permanent hypoparathyroidism21, with similar conclusions reached in a previous meta-analysis22. These data highlight the need for careful consideration of the indications for parathyroid autotransplantation.

Clinically, autotransplantation is performed in the event of one of two circumstances: inadvertent resection or intraoperative evidence of ischaemia. In the first scenario, even limited survival of the autotransplanted tissue is beneficial, because transplantation mitigates the otherwise irreversible loss of functional tissue. The viability of transplanted parathyroid cells has been well established18, and functional competence has been confirmed by elevated PTH gradients between grafted and non-grafted study arms23,24. Incidental parathyroidectomy is strongly associated with an increased risk of transient and permanent hypoparathyroidism25. Together with the dynamic PTH data reported in this study, these previous findings support parathyroid autotransplantation when parathyroid glands are inadvertently removed. In practice, careful inspection of central neck dissection specimens for inadvertently resected parathyroid glands is warranted, particularly when not all parathyroid glands are identified during surgery. Furthermore, the ability to identify parathyroid glands in resected tissue can be enhanced by autofluorescence-based modalities, such as near-infrared autofluorescence.

In contrast, when glands are devascularized but left in situ, there are risks that autotransplantation may provide no additional advantage and could worsen outcomes. Compared with selective parathyroid autotransplantation, routine parathyroid autotransplantation was associated with higher rate of postoperative hypocalcemia9, emphasizing the potential harmful effect of parathyroid autotransplantation. In the present study, parathyroid autotransplantation did not enhance parathyroid recovery after surgery, even in patients with immediate postoperative hypoparathyroidism, suggesting that leaving ischaemic glands in situ may allow for spontaneous recovery. This is supported by the findings that PTH secretion from autotransplanted normal glands is significantly lower than that from hyperplastic glands26. Several studies have shown that in situ preservation of glands yields higher postoperative PTH levels27,28. The number of glands preserved in situ is the strongest predictor of acute and permanent hypoparathyroidism: the more glands that are left intact in situ, the lower the risk of permanent hypoparathyroidism, regardless of whether the removed glands were autografted or found in the specimen29,30. Furthermore, prospective studies have shown that the function of discolored glands left in situ recovers spontaneously within a short period, often more rapidly than in patients who undergo autotransplantation7. These findings, together with those of the present study, argue against autotransplantation for ischaemic glands during thyroidectomy. Similar recommendations have been proposed by the European Society of Endocrine Surgeons31.

This study has several strengths, including its large cohort of over 7000 patients undergoing total thyroidectomy and systematic comparisons between clinically relevant subgroups. Nonetheless, limitations inherent to the retrospective study design should be acknowledged. First, because many patients came from different provinces across China, a small proportion of patients were lost to follow-up, leading to missing data. However, the proportion of missing data was low, and long-term recovery dynamics were modelled using GAMMs to mitigate this limitation. Second, some factors were unbalanced between the groups; specifically, patients with parathyroid autotransplantation were slightly but significantly younger. This may be because surgeons have a more liberal autotransplantation policy for borderline-viable glands in younger patients to minimize the lifetime risk of permanent hypoparathyroidism. Third, most patients routinely received prophylactic calcium supplementation1, and symptomatic hypocalcemia was promptly treated, making serum calcium levels an unreliable marker of hypoparathyroidism; therefore, serum calcium data were not systematically analysed in this study. In addition, biological and histopathological changes following autotransplantation could not be directly evaluated, and further studies using advanced imaging or molecular techniques are warranted. Finally, the surgeries were performed by many different surgeons, which may introduce variability in intraoperative decision-making and assessment of parathyroid gland viability. However, given the large sample size, such variability is likely to be mitigated at the population level, thereby enhancing the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Perioperative strategies such as improved medical management may enhance the recovery of parathyroid glands32. Further efforts to collect relevant data using novel technologies may be useful, because novel technologies, such as autofluorescence-based identification or nanocarbon staining, may enhance intraoperative parathyroid preservation and reduce the need for autotransplantation33–35.
