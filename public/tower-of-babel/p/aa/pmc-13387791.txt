Iliopsoas Tenotomy in Patients With Neurological Diseases. A Novel Closed Technique Using an Ultrasound-Guided Minimally Invasive Approach in Neuro-Orthopaedics
Authors: Paolo Zerbinati, Isabella Tristano, Davide Glorioso, Jonathan Bemporad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The iliopsoas tenotomy aims at correcting the impaired hip extension that compromises patients' ability during gait, creating discomfort during postural transfers and prolonged sitting. The standard, open approach adopted for neurological disorders requires general anesthesia and sutures, with considerable odds of adverse events, such as nerve injuries, pain, and infections. To date, percutaneous release interventions have been done only on cadavers or adult patients with orthopaedic conditions. In this article, we introduce the ultrasound-guided minimally invasive approach in neuro-orthopaedics procedure for the iliopsoas tenotomy, a percutaneous release combining ultrasound-guidance and mini-invasiveness. This technique offers several advantages: reduced length of surgery, no surgical incisions, use of local anesthesia, and the possibility of visualizing the surgical target during the entire process, thus minimizing complications. It is especially useful for patients where open surgery is not recommended, such as those with extensive scarring in the abdominal region caused by previous interventions or those with a high probability of additional surgeries during their lifetime. Moreover, rehabilitation can start on day 1 after surgery and may considerably reduce recovery time.

The iliopsoas muscle contracture, particularly the psoas component, can lead to impaired hip extension during terminal stance in patients with spastic diplegia or quadriplegia caused by cerebral palsy or other neurological conditions such as brain injuries, stroke, or genetic syndromes.1 Excessive hip flexion during gait hinders the ipsilateral limb's loading and propulsive ability, often resulting in compensatory mechanisms and affecting the patient's overall balance and safety.2 The anteromedial surgical approach is the most described and used technique to release the iliopsoas tendon, usually carried out by exposing the muscle tendon within an open surgery and performing the tenotomy.3-5 Although widely used, this procedure may lead to several complications, such as paresthesia due to the injury of the femoral or lateral femoral cutaneous nerves, hematomas, pain, wound infections, and hip dislocation, thus prolonging the rehabilitation and recovery periods.6

In the orthopaedic field, arthroscopic and endoscopic iliopsoas tenotomies have emerged as less-invasive alternatives to open surgery.7-11 More recently, ultrasound-guided percutaneous iliopsoas release has been explored as a minimally invasive option, but current evidence is restricted to adult cadaveric models or to orthopaedic indications (eg, hip impingement after hip arthroplasty).12-14 Minimally invasive percutaneous ultrasound-assisted interventions offer several advantages, including direct visualization of the target and adjacent structures before, during, and after the procedure. At the same time, no surgical incisions are made, therefore reducing the chances of adverse outcomes.15

Because limb deformities and the properties of muscles and tendons in neurological patients differ substantially from those observed in typical orthopaedic populations (eg, muscle overactivity, fibrosis, altered architecture, and fatty degeneration), a tailored, condition-specific approach is required.16 This article aims to describe, for the first time ever, an ultrasound-guided minimally invasive approach in neuro-orthopaedics (UMAN) for performing an iliopsoas percutaneous release in patients with neurological disorders of all ages. The integration of ultrasonography guidance with a minimally invasive approach constitutes the primary rationale for this technique and may contribute to reducing procedure-related complications.

Patient Selection—Indications

We developed the UMAN technique for iliopsoas release to address patients who would otherwise be candidates for an open procedure. Previous studies describe its advantages in patients with orthopaedic disorders.13 This article extends its use to subjects with neurological disorders, such as cerebral palsy, acquired brain injuries, or genetic syndromes. We have also explored its use in younger and underage subjects, in whom specific anatomical features—such as tendon depth relative to the skin—must be carefully considered. The UMAN procedure is also particularly recommended for patients with extensive scarring in the abdominal and pelvic regions caused by previous procedures. Finally, it should be considered for patients expected to undergo an unforeseen number of additional surgeries during their lives with unavoidable incisions, stitches, and scars.

Patient Selection—Contraindications

Potential risk factors or causes for ineligibility to the UMAN procedure are (1) an important deformity that requires an open and more aggressive procedure to be corrected or (2) a high amount of fat tissue that makes it difficult to reach the targeted tendons with a needle.

Procedure Performance

Preoperative Planning

Surgical planning should begin with a comprehensive assessment, including a clinical evaluation at the bedside, functional tests, a patient interview, and instrumental gait analysis with surface electromyography. The clinical information collected includes passive and active hip range of motion, the severity of the iliopsoas spasticity, questionnaires on the patient's level of independence from caregivers during daily activities, their ambulatory and transfer abilities, and their pain levels or hygiene issues caused by iliopsoas contracture. The instrumental gait analysis, when available, provides an objective overview of joint torques and muscle activity during the gait phases and allows a deeper understanding of any underlying phenomena.17 At our institution, a multidisciplinary team consisting of a neuro-orthopaedic surgeon, a physical medicine and rehabilitation (PMR) clinician with expertise in ultrasonography examination, a bioengineer, and a physiotherapist expert in gait analysis is responsible for the design of the intervention and postoperative rehabilitation program.

Setup

The UMAN procedure is performed using a Sonoscape device with a 14 to 7 MHz linear array probe. The iliopsoas tenotomy is performed using a beveled 18-gauge, 3.5-inch coaxial Chiba needle, with the stylet rotated 180° generating a V-shaped cutting tip at the end of the needle, as shown in Figure 1.

The procedure is the same as described by Johnson et al12 and Sampson et al,13 using both cadavers and orthopaedic patients, respectively. However, for younger and underage patients with a more superficial tendon positioning, we recommend a custom-made 9-cm Chiba needle instead of the 15-cm needle used in the original article by Sampson et al.13

The patient lies supine. The assistant operator maintains the hip and knee of the treated side in maximum extension and the contralateral hip maximally flexed (as in the Thomas test),18 to place the psoas tendon in maximum tightness. The tension of the psoas tendon ensures the effective use of the needle, which would not effectively cut a slack tendon. The lead operator performs the psoas release according to the UMAN technique while simultaneously visualizing underlying structures with ultrasonography, as shown in Figure 2.13

The UMAN procedure enables real-time intraprocedural control and has the potential to enhance safety, but potential risks cannot be entirely excluded. At our institution, it is performed by a neuro-orthopaedic surgeon with 30+ years of surgical experience treating neurological conditions, and by a PMR clinician with 20 years of experience treating neurological conditions and 10 years of experience treating spasticity using ultrasound-guided injections (botulinum toxin, phenol, etc …).

Approach and Technique

With the patient lying supine, the ultrasonography probe is initially placed with a transverse (short axis) view over the inguinal region to identify the iliopsoas muscle and adjacent structures (ie, coxofemoral joint, femoral nerve, and femoral artery). The probe is then rotated to the longitudinal (long axis) view to evaluate these structures in this alternative plane. Once identified the safest plane (ie, the iliopsoas tendon, vessels, and nerve structures are clearly visible), the leading operator chooses the insertion point for the needle. After insertion, the needle is guided in a lateral-to-medial direction, in-plane relative to the probe, toward the lateral border of the iliopsoas tendon until it reaches the anterior-superior border of the acetabulum just distal to the anterior inferior iliac spine (see Figure 3).

Once the needle is correctly positioned and optimal visualization of the iliopsoas tendon is obtained, the needle is moved following a lateral-to-medial and deep-to-superficial pattern throughout the iliopsoas tendon until the entire tendon is fully treated, as shown in Video 1. The leading operator can circumvent nerves, vessels, and other nontarget structures, thanks to the ultrasound-based images. Furthermore, the simultaneous ultrasonography makes it possible to identify any potential injury to the neurovascular structures of the femur or excoriations of the iliopsoas muscle tissue immediately superficial to the tendon above the acetabulum.

The operator monitors the procedure's success by looking at the incision on the tendon with the longitudinal (long axis) approach. This step allows the operator to decide whether the resection can be considered sufficient or whether the technique needs to be repeated to achieve a more notable number of released fibers. We recommend cutting most of the tendon fibers (approximately 80%) to consider the procedure complete. The procedure is successful when an increase in the hip extension is achieved, possibly between −10° and 0°, or when no more tension can be detected with the needle on the tendon fibers. Immediately after the procedure, the patient should undergo an ultrasonography assessment to exclude hematomas or other major complications.

The entire procedure takes approximately 15 minutes and is performed under spinal anesthesia. Nevertheless, in the case of minors or noncompliant adults, general anesthesia might be necessary to ensure safety during the intervention.

Finalizing the Surgery

Because the treatment is based on needle insertion, neither sutures nor stitches are required, and the patient has no surgical wounds. The insertion point is protected with a plaster that can be removed after a few hours. No drainage is needed, and no immobilizers are applied.

Postoperative Care and Management

Postoperatively, when necessary, pain is controlled with oral medications, such as first-level pain drugs like acetaminophen or ibuprofen.

Usually, the percutaneous psoas lengthening described in this article is performed as part of a multilevel surgery, which is typical in these patients. Based on the surgery performed, our inpatients undergo 3 to 4 weeks of intensive rehabilitation with 90-minute sessions, 6 days a week. The physiotherapy program at our institution aims to increase the hip range of motion, focusing on improving extension, pain management following the surgery, and controlling the potential onset of any surgical complications. Functional goals are established and shared with the patient and their caregivers, according to each subject's preoperative condition. These should include care of postural hygiene and improvement of bed and wheelchair postures, improvement of postural transfers (eg, from the supine position in bed to the seated position and finally to the wheelchair), and an increase in walking autonomy when possible. When the intervention only targets soft tissues, patients are allowed weight-bearing immediately after surgery, and those who, before the surgery, were able to walk can resume walking the following day. When the surgery involves bone and soft tissues, weight-bearing depends on bone consolidation.

The most common adverse event in the open procedure of psoas lengthening is a psoas hematoma and wound issues.7 At our institution, we routinely perform clinical and laboratory examinations during the 30-day hospitalization to control for any complications.

Pearls and Pitfalls Based on the Authors' Experience

This innovative procedure allows for the recovery of hip extension with a very minimally invasive approach. Because neither surgical incisions nor stitches are required, there is little postsurgical pain, enabling the patient to start rehabilitation as early as the first day after surgery. Considering that patients with neurological disorders may undergo several surgeries during their lifetime, the UMAN technique avoids yet another surgical incision and can be performed on patients who already have substantial scarring. For example, we performed this procedure on a young patient with cerebral palsy who had undergone two distinct implants of an intrathecal baclofen pump and whose extensive scarring in the abdominal and pelvic region would have made the open approach for the iliopsoas tenotomy quite challenging.

The iliopsoas lengthening represents a frequent intervention in patients with cerebral palsy, being addressed in approximately 19% of total procedures.19 Therefore, the UMAN technique may have implications for healthcare systems and associated costs that should be considered in future studies and evaluations. First, the entire procedure takes approximately 15 minutes and is usually performed under spinal anesthesia for adults and under general anesthesia for children. It requires cheaper instrumentation (ie, needles) than the open procedure (ie, scalpels). It can be performed by PMR clinicians qualified in ecoguided, minimally invasive interventions and injection techniques. Second, this minimally invasive technique may speed up the recovery time compared with an open procedure due to the lack of pain. Finally, ultrasonography real-time guidance may reduce the risk of adverse events. To confirm the magnitude of these hypotheses and the effectiveness of the UMAN technique, we will be conducting a targeted study in the near future. In the literature, when minimally invasive techniques were compared with standard ones, the former resulted in similar outcomes with lower adverse event rates and a shorter hospital stay.20

Finally, by replicating the technique illustrated in this article, we believe that many interventions done on neurological patients could be replaced by minimally invasive procedures. At our institution, we have successfully implemented similar adaptations for percutaneous fibrotomies of the gastrocnemius muscles and percutaneous tenotomies of the rectus femoris, gracilis, semitendinosus, and biceps femoris.

Summary of the Results

For the first time in the literature, we presented an ultrasound-guided minimally invasive approach in neuro-orthopaedic surgery, named UMAN, for the iliopsoas tendon lengthening to restore hip extension suitable for adults and children alike. The whole procedure lasts less than 15 minutes. It can be performed under spinal anesthesia, without surgical wounds, and may be associated with low postoperative pain and less frequent postsurgical complications.
