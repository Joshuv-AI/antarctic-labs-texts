Preoperative systemic immune inflammation index predicts early complications and 30-day mortality after heart valve surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: IMade Adi Parmana, Dudy Arman Hanafy, Cindy Elfira Boom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) is considered a promising inflammatory index for predicting adverse outcomes in cardiovascular diseases. However, its prognostic value in patients undergoing heart valve surgery remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the predictive significance of preoperative SII for early postoperative complications and 30-day mortality.MethodsThis was a retrospective, single-center cohort study conducted at the National Cardiovascular Center Harapan Kita in Jakarta, Indonesia, a tertiary cardiac surgery referral hospital. It included adults (> 18 years) who underwent elective aortic, mitral, or combined valve surgery between January 2020 and December 2024. After applying exclusion criteria, 1347 patients were stratified by SII into low (< 551 × 103/mm3, n = 927) and high (≥ 551 × 103/mm3, n = 420) groups.ResultsHigh preoperative SII was significantly associated with acute kidney injury (AKI) (p = 0.002), prolonged mechanical ventilation (p = 0.039), extended total hospital length of stay (LOS-H) (p = 0.004), and 30-day mortality (p = 0.026). Multivariate logistic regression identified SII (OR 2.169; 95% CI 1.600–2.941; p < 0.001) as independent predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes. When analyzed as a continuous variable, preoperative SII remained independently associated with adverse postoperative outcomes (adjusted OR per 100-unit increase 1.196; 95% CI 1.148–1.245; p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis revealed the highest predictive value in patients undergoing aortic valve surgery, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.797 (95% CI 0.728–0.866).ConclusionsElevated preoperative SII is independently associated with an increased risk of early postoperative complications and 30-day mortality in patients undergoing heart valve surgery. Introduction

Heart valve disease (HVD) constitutes a significant burden on global cardiovascular health, contributing to physical disability and diminished quality of life, and representing an important cause of morbidity and mortality [1]. Data from the 2015 Global Burden of Disease Study reported that approximately 33.4 million individuals worldwide were affected by rheumatic heart disease (RHD), with the greatest burden seen in low- and middle-income countries [2]. Operative interventions for aortic valve pathology—such as transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), surgical aortic valve replacement (AVR), and aortic valve repair (AVr)—as well as procedures for mitral valve disease, including mitral valve replacement (MVR) and repair (MVr), have demonstrated favorable long-term outcomes. These procedures are associated with low rates of valve-related complications and significant improvements in patients’ quality of life [3]. Despite advances in surgical management, heart valve surgery remains associated with substantial postoperative morbidity and mortality, highlighting the importance of accurate preoperative risk stratification.

Heart valve surgery induces a systemic inflammatory response involving neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, which is linked to higher risks of postoperative morbidity and mortality [4]. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), a simple and readily obtainable inflammatory index, has demonstrated potential in predicting postoperative complications and poor outcomes in cardiovascular patients [5–8]. As a composite inflammatory index, SII is calculated using routine hematological parameters and incorporates two established inflammatory ratios: the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR)—obtained by dividing the absolute neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count, and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR)—determined by dividing the platelet count by the lymphocyte count. The SII is calculated using the following formula: SII = (Platelet count × Neutrophil count)/Lymphocyte count [9].

A study by Shvartz et al. demonstrated that an elevated SII (≥ 423.8 × 103/mm3) was predictive of mortality, acute kidney injury (AKI), post-operative atrial fibrillation (PoAF), stroke, and bleeding in a cohort of 363 patients undergoing aortic valve surgery [10]. Similarly, Yoon et al. reported that a high SII (≥ 455.6 × 103/mm3) was significantly associated with an increased incidence of 30-day postoperative complications in patients undergoing isolated tricuspid valve surgery [11].

Although SII has emerged as a promising prognostic inflammatory index in cardiovascular surgery, evidence in patients undergoing heart valve surgery remains limited, particularly in heterogeneous valve procedures. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative SII and early postoperative complications and 30-day mortality in patients undergoing heart valve surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the National Cardiovascular Center Harapan Kita, Jakarta, Indonesia, between January 2020 and December 2024. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No: DP.04.03/KEP108/EC063/2025), and all procedures were conducted under the ethical standards outlined in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the institutional review board waived the requirement for informed consent. All patient data were anonymized before analysis to ensure confidentiality.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were adults (> 18 years) who underwent elective mitral, aortic, or combined valve surgery. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete clinical data, hemoglobin levels < 10 g/dL, end-stage renal disease requiring hemodialysis, elevated liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] or aspartate aminotransferase [AST] > 2 times the upper limit of normal), active infection (preoperative leukocyte count > 11,000/mm3), a history of stroke within the previous 3 months, or known cardiac conduction abnormalities including pacemaker implantation.

Perioperative procedures

All patients underwent standard preoperative evaluation as part of routine clinical care. Standard intraoperative monitoring protocols were implemented, including electrocardiography, echocardiography, non-invasive arterial pressure measurement, and advanced hemodynamic monitoring. Anesthetic induction and maintenance were administered in accordance with established institutional guidelines. All surgical procedures were performed by experienced cardiovascular surgeons. Upon completion of the operation, patients were transferred to the cardiovascular intensive care unit (ICU) for standardized postoperative care.

Patient grouping

A total of 1347 patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled. The optimal SII cut-off value of 551 × 103/mm3 was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis based on the Youden index, with poor postoperative outcomes as the endpoint. This method was selected in accordance with previously published studies. Patients were subsequently stratified into two groups: low SII (< 551 × 103/mm3) and high SII (≥ 551 × 103/mm3). Data collection

Clinical data, including demographic, preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables, were collected from medical records. The demographic information encompassed age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, smoking history, blood pressure, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. Preoperative laboratory parameters included total leukocyte, segmented neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet count. Intraoperative variables comprised the type of surgical procedure performed, total duration of surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time, and aortic cross-clamp (AOX) time. AOX time was defined as the interval from application to release of the AOX. Zero values reflected missing or unrecorded timestamps inherent to retrospective data collection rather than true absence of cross-clamping. In addition, sensitivity analyses excluding observations with implausible zero timestamps were performed to evaluate potential bias.

Outcomes

Perioperative outcomes assessed included the need for intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support, perioperative blood transfusion, vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS), duration of mechanical ventilation (DOMV), total intensive care unit length of stay (LOS-ICU), total hospital length of stay (LOS-H), and various postoperative complications, including PoAF, stroke, AKI, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), major bleeding, septic shock, cardiogenic shock, gastrointestinal complications, 30-day mortality, and the composite of adverse postoperative outcomes.

VIS was calculated as follows: dopamine (μg/kg/min) + dobutamine (μg/kg/min) + 10 × milrinone (μg/kg/min) + 100 × epinephrine (μg/kg/min) + 100 × norepinephrine (μg/kg/min) + 10,000 × vasopressin (μg/kg/min). Prolonged mechanical ventilation was defined as > 24 h, prolonged ICU stay as > 48 h, and prolonged LOS-H as > 7 days. AKI was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria as an increase in serum creatinine ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h, an increase to ≥ 1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for at least 6 h. Mortality was tracked through the 30th postoperative day. The composite of adverse postoperative outcomes included the occurrence of at least one of the following events: acute kidney injury, prolonged mechanical ventilation (> 24 h), prolonged hospital length of stay (> 7 days), or 30-day all-cause mortality. This composite endpoint was selected to capture clinically relevant early postoperative morbidity and mortality while acknowledging the heterogeneous mechanisms underlying individual components.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A two-tailed significance level of 0.05 was used. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed due to the retrospective design of the study. All eligible patients within the study period were included to maximize statistical power and minimize selection bias. Data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (min–max) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test. The discriminative performance of SII was assessed with ROC analysis, and the optimal cut-off was identified via the Youden index. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent predictors of adverse postoperative outcomes, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Given that the NLR is mathematically embedded within the SII, potential multicollinearity was considered. Accordingly, multicollinearity diagnostics were formally quantified. Variance inflation factor (VIF), tolerance, and condition index assessments for all covariates were performed in the multivariable models. Moreover, SII was retained as the primary inflammatory index in the main multivariable model. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative multivariable models including NLR instead of SII to assess the robustness and consistency of the observed associations. To address potential overadjustment due to intraoperative variables that may lie on the causal pathway, two multivariable logistic regression models were constructed: (1) a preoperative model including baseline covariates only, and (2) an extended perioperative model additionally including intraoperative variables.

Results

A total of 1347 patients were included in the analysis and stratified into two groups based on SII values: high SII (≥ 551 × 103/mm3, n = 420; 31.2%) and low SII (< 551 × 103/mm3, n = 927; 68.8%). Compared to the low SII group, patients with high SII had a significantly higher proportion of females (p = 0.007), a greater prevalence of diabetes mellitus (p = 0.021), lower LVEF (p = 0.019), and worse NYHA functional class (p = 0.003). Laboratory parameters also showed elevated total leukocyte and platelet counts, as well as increased NLR values in this group (all p < 0.001). A significant difference was also observed in the type of valve pathology between the groups. The proportion of patients with mitral stenosis (MS) was higher in the high SII group (p < 0.05), with rheumatic heart disease (RHD) identified as the predominant etiology. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified elevated preoperative SII as an independent predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes (adjusted odds ratio [OR] per 100-unit increase 1.196; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.148–1.245; p < 0.001). When analyzed as a dichotomous variable, SII remained independently associated with adverse outcomes (OR 2.169; 95% CI 1.600–2.941; p < 0.001). No significant deviation from linearity was observed when SII was modeled continuously. Other significant predictors included age, CPB time (p < 0.001), BMI (p = 0.001), LVEF (p = 0.023), and AOX time (p = 0.010). These findings highlight the prognostic value of SII in preoperative risk stratification for patients undergoing heart valve surgery. Discussion

This study demonstrates that elevated preoperative SII is independently associated with adverse postoperative outcomes, including AKI, prolonged mechanical ventilation, extended hospital length of stay, and 30-day mortality in patients undergoing heart valve surgery. These findings support the role of SII as a practical inflammatory index that may complement existing preoperative risk stratification.

The association between elevated SII and postoperative AKI is biologically plausible [12]. Cardiac surgery induces systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular injury, which contribute to renal hypoperfusion and ischemia–reperfusion injury [13]. Increased neutrophil activity, platelet activation, and relative lymphopenia—components of SII—may further amplify oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades, promoting renal injury [14–16]. This aligns with previous studies linking inflammatory indices with AKI after cardiac surgery.

In line with prior evidence, patients with high SII experienced prolonged mechanical ventilation [7]. Systemic inflammation contributes to pulmonary dysfunction through increased vascular permeability, oxidative stress, and ventilator-induced lung injury [5]. In addition, reperfusion injury following aortic cross-clamp removal may exacerbate inflammatory responses, delaying respiratory recovery [17]. These mechanisms likely explain the association between elevated SII and prolonged ventilatory support observed in our cohort.

Consistent with our findings, elevated SII was associated with prolonged hospital stay. This may reflect the cumulative burden of postoperative complications, particularly respiratory dysfunction and delayed recovery [18]. However, hospital length of stay is also influenced by institutional and healthcare system factors, and therefore should be interpreted with caution.

Importantly, elevated SII was independently associated with increased 30-day mortality. A heightened inflammatory state may promote endothelial injury, prothrombotic conditions, and multi-organ dysfunction, all of which contribute to worse postoperative outcomes [19, 20]. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the prognostic value of inflammatory indices in cardiovascular surgery [9].

SII demonstrated more consistent prognostic performance compared to NLR across analyses. As a composite index incorporating neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, SII may better reflect the interaction between inflammation and thrombosis [21]. Nevertheless, given that NLR is embedded within SII, these indices are not independent, and their interpretation should consider this overlap.

ROC analysis showed moderate discriminative ability of SII, with an AUC of 0.742. While statistically significant, this indicates that SII should not be used as a standalone predictor but rather as a complementary tool within a broader clinical assessment. This suggests that SII may provide clinically meaningful risk stratification, particularly when integrated into routine preoperative assessment in resource-limited settings. The optimal cut-off value identified in this study may reflect the characteristics of our population, which has a high prevalence of rheumatic heart disease and chronic inflammatory burden. In addition, the inclusion of SII improved the predictive performance of the baseline model, suggesting its incremental value in risk stratification. The predictive performance was particularly higher in patients with aortic valve disease.

Several baseline characteristics differed between groups. Patients with higher SII had worse functional status and lower LVEF, which may reflect more advanced disease and increased inflammatory activity [22]. The higher prevalence of rheumatic pathology in our cohort may also contribute to elevated SII, given the chronic inflammatory nature of this condition [23–25]. These differences may partly contribute to the observed association between elevated SII and adverse postoperative outcomes. However, these findings are descriptive and should not be interpreted as causal.

The association between SII and intraoperative variables such as CPB and AOX duration should be interpreted cautiously. These variables are closely related and may introduce collinearity in multivariable models. Sensitivity analyses were performed to address this issue, and the apparent inverse association of AOX duration likely reflects statistical interaction rather than a true protective effect.

Despite these findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability and introduce residual confounding. Important clinical variables, including standardized surgical risk scores and certain comorbidities, were not consistently available. In addition, dynamic changes in SII were not evaluated, and the cut-off value requires external validation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Taken together, these findings support the potential role of SII as an adjunctive tool rather than a replacement for established risk models.

In conclusion, preoperative SII is independently associated with early postoperative complications and 30-day mortality in patients undergoing heart valve surgery. As a simple and readily available inflammatory index, SII may enhance preoperative risk stratification when used alongside established clinical parameters. External validation in broader populations may help clarify the generalizability and clinical applicability of these findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and single-center nature may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations and settings. Important established predictors of postoperative outcomes in cardiac surgery, including renal function parameters, pulmonary hypertension severity, previous cardiac surgery, urgency of surgery, and formal surgical risk scores (e.g., EuroSCORE II or STS score), were not consistently available in the retrospective dataset and therefore could not be included in the multivariable models. In addition, active infection was assessed only using preoperative leukocyte count, as other inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) were not consistently available in the retrospective dataset. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded.

Moreover, perioperative dynamic changes in SII were not evaluated, which may provide additional insights into its prognostic value. The optimal SII cut-off was internally derived and requires validation in independent cohorts. Additionally, long-term outcomes beyond the 30-day postoperative period were not assessed. Another limitation is that some intraoperative time variables contained missing timestamps due to the retrospective nature of data collection, which may have introduced the potential for selection bias. Due to the retrospective design and data limitations, advanced metrics such as net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement were not performed. Finally, internal validation of the ROC-derived cut-off and statistical comparison between AUCs was not performed, and the identified threshold should therefore be considered exploratory.

Conclusion

Preoperative SII is an independent predictor of early postoperative complications and 30-day mortality in patients undergoing heart valve surgery. As SII can be easily calculated from routine complete blood count and differential leukocyte analysis, it represents a practical and cost-effective inflammatory index for short-term risk stratification. Integrating SII into established preoperative risk assessment models and perioperative decision-making frameworks may enhance risk discrimination, enable earlier identification of high-risk patients, and guide more individualized perioperative management strategies.
