Silicone elastomer films loaded with 5-fluorouracil for time-dependent drug release
Authors: Mari Atabekyan, Zoya Farmazyan, Nelly Avagyan, Vigen Topuzyan, Nelly Babayan, Kristine Harutyunyan, Natalya Sarkisyan, Ruzanna Grigoryan, Stepan Grigoryan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silicone elastomer composite films loaded with the antitumor drug 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) were developed for controlled release applications. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMSOH) films were crosslinked using tetraethoxysilane and room-temperature vulcanization catalysts, with glycerol incorporated as a hydrophilic modifier to regulate permeability of the hydrophobic silicone matrix. Dimethyl sulfoxide was used to solubilize 5-FU during film preparation. Film microstructure and drug distribution were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy and Raman spectroscopic imaging. Among the tested formulations, the film containing 30 wt.% glycerol (F-4) exhibited uniform drug distribution and the most favorable 5-FU release profile in 0.9% NaCl solution, consistent with its morphological characteristics. Cytotoxicity and cytocompatibility were assessed in A549 and HepG2 cells using an ISO 10993-5 extract test. Extracts from the blank F-4 film were minimal cytotoxic across all extraction times, whereas extracts from the 5-FU–loaded F-4 film produced a time-dependent reduction in cell viability in both cell lines. The extract-mediated cytotoxicity closely followed the UV–Vis release profile, indicating that the biological response was predominantly driven by time-dependent 5-FU release rather than film-derived leachables. Overall, glycerol-modified PDMSOH films provide a tunable platform for sustained 5-FU delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55744-x.

Introduction

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) is an antimetabolite drug widely used to treat solid tumors, particularly gastrointestinal malignancies. Despite its widespread clinical use, 5-FU therapy is limited by narrow therapeutic index, limited tissue selectivity, variable efficacy, and development of drug resistance. In addition, 5-FU is rapidly cleared from systemic circulation, often necessitating continuous or prolonged infusion regimens to maintain therapeutic exposure1–6.

5-FU is currently administered via injections, oral formulations, hydrogel-based systems7. Prolonged-release approaches have also been investigated, including mesoporous silica carriers loaded with 5-FU8 as well as transdermal film formulations. Transdermal delivery systems (TDS) such as transdermal patches, offer several clinical advantages, including ease of application, sustained drug delivery maximizing therapeutic drug efficacy while minimizing side effects due to the possibility of its prolonged action. Patches can deliver a therapeutic dose of drug over an extended period ranging from one to 7 days9–11. However, not all drugs are suitable for transdermal delivery, as this approach depends on the permeability of the drug through the outermost layer of the epidermis (stratum corneum), which is determined by multiple factors, such as the molecular weight of the drug (> 500 Da), its aqueous solubility, octanol–water partition coefficient (log Po/w≈ 1–3), and melting point. Although 5-FU has a low molecular weight (130.01 g/mol) and sufficient aqueous solubility, its high melting point and very low lipophilicity (log P = − 0.85) limit its partitioning into the stratum corneum. Consequently, several studies have reported poor passive skin permeation of 5-FU in the absence of enhancement strategies12,13․ Drug release from polymer-based TDS typically occurs via one or a combination of the three possible mechanisms: (1) diffusion-controlled release, (2) release governed by drug–carrier affinity, and (3) through matrix degradation. Although any of these mechanisms may dominate in therapeutic dose of the drug release, it usually involves all three mechanisms to varying extent14.

Significant research efforts have focused on the transdermal delivery of 5-FU using various polymeric carriers, including Eudragit and Sylgard 18415.

In the present study, 5-FU–loaded cured silicone elastomer films were prepared using linear polydimethylsiloxane with terminal hydroxyl groups (PDMSOH) as the base polymer crosslinked with a tetrafunctional crosslinking agent, tetraethoxylsilane (TEOS), and cured in the presence of tin- or diamine based vulcanization catalysts at room temperature. The resulting films with a simple composition may offer practical advantages for controlled drug delivery applications.

The aim of this work was to investigate the microstructure, drug distribution, release behavior and cytotoxicity of 5-FU–loaded silicone compositions, with the goal of producing homogeneous drug-containing films with optimized release kinetics and potential suitability for biomedical applications.

Results and discussion

Silicone composite films synthesis

Polysiloxanes are inherently hydrophobic materials. Therefore, in order to incorporate hydrophilic drugs into such structures, hydrophilization is required using low- or high-molecular polyols such as glycerol. 5-FU is a hydrophilic drug16, so it does not dissolve or disperse in hydrophobic silicone matrix. For this reason, glycerol was introduced as hydrophilic modifier to obtain composite silicone films. 5-FU is soluble in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which is also well-known chemical permeation enhancer widely used in transdermal patches17,18. In this study DMSO was employed as a solvent for 5-FU in 2.5–5 wt.%. Ultrasonic treatment was additionally applied during films preparation to ensure homogeneous mixing of all components. Two films (F-1 and F-2) were prepared without glycerol. Stannous octoate Sn(oct)2 was used as the curing catalyst for F-1, while PDMSNH2 served as a catalyst for F-2. F-1 film cured within 24 h at room temperature forming a homogeneous and elastic material. However, only 7.4% of the loaded drug was released after 72 h, and the swelling degree was 6.0%. (Fig. 2). In contrast, the F-2 film cured over 7 days, and a homogeneous film of uniform thickness was obtained, however, despite the complete absence of swelling during 72 h, the cumulative drug release reached 64.0%. To enhance the drug release, glycerol was incorporated into hydrophobic silicone matrix, and the F-3 and F-4 composite films cured using Sn(oct)2 as the catalyst were obtained. The F-3 film, containing 21 wt.% glycerol cured within 3 h yielded a smooth, uniform film. Although only 6.5% of 5-FU released from F-3 film during the first 0.5 h, the cumulative drug release significantly increased to 50.8% after 24 h, reaching 81.2% after 72 h. It should be noted that the films obtained using the tin based vulcanization catalyst had a stable appearance, and they retained their shape and did not change over time. Since F-3 film had the most stable appearance, short curing time and showing high degree of the drug release, the study was continued to further enhancing the early-stage drug release. The glycerol content was increased to 31 wt.%, resulting in the F-4 film, which cured within 1 h, did not adhere to the casting plastic surface, exhibiting good mechanical stability. Drug release increased significantly, reaching 72.6% after 24 h and 94.9% after 72 h. The F-3 film swelling was about 110% after 24 h, and then it decreased, reaching 26.5% after 72 h due to glycerol diffusion into the NaCl solution. The rate of glycerol diffusion at swelling probably largely depends both on network structure of the cured silicone matrix and on amount of hydrophilic glycerol loaded into the matrix. Thus, in case of F-4 film with higher glycerol content, the swelling degree reached 146% after 24 h due to the reverse (osmotic) transition of water into the film and further decreased to 76.2% after 72 h (Fig. 2). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

FT-IR spectra of 5-FU, glycerol and 5-FU-glycerol mixture were recorded in the 400–4000 cm−1 region. The FT-IR spectrum of 5-FU exhibited characteristic absorption band at 3125 cm−1 corresponding to NH stretching, 1726 cm−1 to CO stretching, and 1662 cm−1 and 1247 cm−1 associated with ring vibration19,20. In FT-IR spectra, strong and medium-intensity bands attributable to glycerol were observed in all samples including peaks at 3300 cm−1 (–OH stretching), 2934 cm−1 (CH2 asymmetric stretching), 2879 cm−1 (CH2 symmetry stretching), and 1413 cm−1 (CH2 bending). Two strong absorption bands in the 1300–1000 cm−1 region correspond to C–O stretching vibrations: 1108 cm−1 (secondary alcohol C–O stretching) and 1030 cm−1 (primary alcohol C–O stretching). Additional peaks at 993, 851, and 674 cm−1 are assigned to C–C stretching, C–C–O symmetric stretching, and –OH wagging vibration of glycerol, respectively21. In contrast, the spectrum of the mixture containing 1 g of glycerol and 5-FU showed a strong suppression of the characteristic vibrational bands of 5-FU, indicating reduced spectral visibility of the drug in the presence of glycerol.

Raman spectroscopy and SEM analysis

The aim of the Raman spectroscopic and mapping studies was to evaluate the spatial distribution of 5-FU within the films and to assess possible interactions between the components of the composite system. To this end, Raman spectra of pure crystalline 5-FU were first recorded. The dominant Raman bands were observed at 367, 411, 468, 548, 636, 768, 1226, 1349, 1425, 1655, and 1671 cm⁻1. They can be assigned to the following vibrational modes: one out-of-plane pyrimidine ring bending mode, four in-plane ring bending modes, ring breathing, combined ring and C–F stretching, ring and C–H wagging, ring and N–H wagging, the C=C stretching, and symmetric C=O stretching, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S2). These assignments are consistent with previously reported data22,23. Although the intense Raman bands are commonly used for its identification, it is important to note that peaks below 200 cm⁻1 are often neglected, despite containing valuable information related to the crystalline structure of the compound, which can significantly influence drug release behavior in the composite systems. The degree of crystallinity of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) is a critical quality attribute (CQA) for transdermal delivery systems (TDS) since crystalline API embedded in a polymer matrix is unable to permeate the skin effectively, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy22. Specifically, crystallization decreases the fraction of solubilized API, altering the concentration gradient between the adhesive matrix and the skin, which is a key driving force for transdermal drug delivery23. Furthermore, API crystallization can modify the rheological properties of the drug-in-adhesive (DIA) matrix, potentially leading to adhesion failure and a reduction in the effective contact area between the TDS and the skin, ultimately decreasing the delivered drug dose. Consequently, control of API crystallization is a critical requirement in TDS development, and failure to do so may compromise product quality and patient outcomes24,25.

To analyze the distribution of 5-FU within the composite films, the following characteristic Raman bands were selected: 367 cm⁻1 (out-of-plane ring bending), 1226 cm⁻1 (ring+C–F stretching), 1347 cm⁻1 (ring stretching), and 1671 cm⁻1 (symmetric C=O stretching). Raman spectra of DMSO and glycerol were also recorded as this solvents mixture is integral part of the composite films facilitating dissolution of crystalline 5-FU, enabling the drug even distribution within silicone matrix. Raman characteristic bands of pure DMSO, glycerol and 5-FU are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 pointing out that there is no spectral overlap between the selected 5-FU bands and those of the solvents. To investigate potential interactions between 5-FU, DMSO, and glycerol, Raman spectra were recorded for the following mixtures: 5-FU+DMSO, DMSO+glycerol, 5-FU+glycerol, 5-FU+DMSO+glycerol (Fig. 5). In the 5-FU+glycerol mixture, undissolved drug particles were clearly visible in glycerol, indicating limited solubility of 5-FU in glycerol. Nevertheless, a small fraction of drug was dissolved, since in the Raman spectra of the mixture the characteristic peaks of 5-FU were present, and only a shift of the 1226 cm⁻1 (ring+C–F stretching) band to 1254 cm⁻1 took place. In contrast, in the 5-FU+DMSO mixture the drug was completely dissolved, forming a cloudy-whitish solution. In this mixture, spectral shifts were observed: the DMSO S=O stretching band shifted from 1034 to 1026 cm⁻1, while the symmetric C=O stretching band of 5-FU shifted from 1671 to 1706 cm⁻1. These shifts are likely attributable to van der Waals interactions between 5-FU and DMSO molecules.Fig. 5Raman peaks assignments of components mixture.

The Raman spectrum of cured PDMSOH film—the base polymer matrix for the composite films was also recorded26,27 (Supplementary Fig. S3). The characteristic bands of the spectrum represented in Supplementary Table S2 do not overlap with the main 5-FU peaks. The amorphous nature of the cured PDMSOH film is confirmed by XRD analysis showing two broad halos at approximately 12.8° and 22.6° (2θ)28–30 typical for polysiloxanes, where the first, more intense halo corresponds to the average interchain spacing, and the second, broader feature is associated with shorter-range intermolecular correlations. In contrast, the diffraction pattern of the crystalline 5-FU is dominated by a very intense diffraction peak at 28.7° and a weaker one at 16.3° (2θ)31. Importantly, the XRD analysis of the F-4 composite film revealed the complete absence of the intense characteristic diffraction peak of 5-FU crystalline phase at 28.7°.The diffraction intensity in the critical range from 27.6° to 29.0° (2θ) remained flat and featureless (Supplementary Fig. S4). This absence confirms absence of a separate 5-FU crystalline phase, demonstrates successful incorporation and molecular dispersion of the drug within the amorphous PDMSOH-glycerol matrix, and effective suppression of the drug recrystallization.

Based on these findings, in order to clarify the drug distribution in the synthesized composite films the four above-mentioned main 5-FU bands were used, as these bands do not overlap with bands of other components of the system.

In Raman mapping of the F-2 film (Fig. 6), presence of all these 5-FU bands is observed in the film’s recorded spectrum. The maps are nearly identical indicating a relatively uniform distribution of the drug in the analyzed region. Raman spectra of other two regions (Supplementary Fig. S5 and S6, F-2 film, points 2 and 3) of the same film also show characteristic 5-FU bands, however, the corresponding maps revealed a more localized, point-like distribution of the drug. It can be assumed that the F-2 film contains both uniformly distributed regions and discrete microdomains of 5-FU. Cytotoxicity evaluation of 5-FU–loaded F-4 film

To assess the in vitro cytocompatibility of the selected F-4 film and to determine whether potentially harmful leachables are released over time, an extract-based cytotoxicity evaluation was performed in accordance with ISO 10993-5. In parallel, the time-dependent biological activity of extracts from the 5-FU–loaded F-4 film was evaluated to relate extract exposure to 5-FU–mediated cytotoxicity. It was shown that the blank F-4 silicone film extracts exhibited minimal cytotoxicity in both cell lines across extraction times. In A549 cells, viability remained close to baseline (~ 95–100%) from 0.5–24 h and showed only a modest decrease at later extraction times (≈85% at 48 h and ≈75% at 72 h). In HepG2 cells, the F-4 extracts maintained high viability (≈90–100%) at all time points. Although DMSO and curing-related leachables are recognized as potential contributors to cytotoxicity, the blank F-4 extract, containing the same matrix and curing components in the absence of 5-FU, exhibited only minimal cytotoxicity. In accordance with ISO 10993-5, cytotoxic potential is indicated when cell viability decreases below 70% of the control.

In contrast, extracts from the 5-FU–loaded F-4 film extracts induced a marked reduction in cell viability in a time-dependent manner. In A549 cells, viability decreased from ~ 80% at 0.5 h to ~ 55–60% at 2.5–4.5 h and reached ~ 40% by 24 h, remaining around this level through 72 h. In HepG2 cells, the reduction was more gradual, declining from ~ 85–90% at 0.5 h to ~ 70–75% at 4.5 h and ~ 50–60% from 24–72 h. For both cell lines, viability was significantly lower for the 5-FU–loaded F-4 film extracts compared with the corresponding F-4 silicone film extracts at each extraction time.

The cytotoxicity profile of the 5-FU–loaded F-4 film extracts was consistent with the UV–Vis release kinetics. Increasing extraction time resulted in progressively stronger reductions in MTT viability, with the response approaching a plateau from approximately 24 h onward. In parallel, UV–Vis analysis in 0.9% NaCl demonstrated a biphasic release behavior, characterized by an initial burst within the first hours, followed by a rapid increase up to ~ 24 h and a slower approach to a near-plateau by 48–72 h (≈90–95% cumulative release). The 5-FU dose–response experiment further supported this interpretation, showing a clear concentration-dependent decrease in viability in both A549 and HepG2 cells, indicating that the extract-mediated effects are compatible with pharmacologically active 5-FU levels. Collectively, these data suggest that the cytotoxicity observed for the drug-loaded extracts is predominantly driven by time-dependent 5-FU release, whereas extracts from the blank F-4 silicone film exhibit minimal cytotoxicity. Differences in the relative sensitivity of A549 and HepG2 between extract exposure and direct 5-FU treatment likely reflect cell line–specific responses; nevertheless, both cell models consistently distinguish the cytocompatibility of the blank F-4 silicone film from the expected cytotoxic activity of the 5-FU–loaded film.

Based on the direct 5-FU treatment experiment, the IC₅₀ values were estimated to be approximately 150 µM for HepG2 and 480 µM for A549, corresponding to ~ 19.5 and ~ 62.4 µg/mL, respectively. These values are lower than the nominal concentrations estimated from the early F-4 release time points. For instance, the nominal 5-FU concentration corresponding to the 0.5 h release time point was estimated to be ~ 127 µg/mL, which would be expected to produce a stronger cytotoxic effect than that observed experimentally. However, direct comparison between the release data and the cytotoxicity results should be made with caution, because the two experiments were performed in different media. Release kinetics were determined in 0.9% NaCl, whereas cytotoxicity extracts were generated in complete culture medium, whose composition differs substantially from saline and may influence the availability of 5-FU in the extract.

Although topical 5-FU is clinically relevant for skin lesions such as actinic keratosis, the present cytotoxicity experiments were designed as a general ISO 10993-5 extract-based evaluation of matrix cytocompatibility and release-associated biological activity rather than as a disease-specific skin model. Future studies should therefore include keratinocyte-based or reconstructed skin models to better assess the suitability of this system for dermatological applications. In addition, evaluation of whether therapeutically sufficient systemic exposure can be achieved by passive dermal delivery would require dedicated studies of skin permeation, transdermal flux, and pharmacokinetics. Such investigations will be necessary to determine whether this system is more appropriate for topical, local, or transdermal use.

Conclusions

Silicone elastomer composite films loaded with 5-fluorouracil were developed as a promising system for controlled time-dependent drug release. The films microstructure and the drug distribution within polymer matrix were investigated by scanning electron microscopy and Raman mapping analyses. The incorporation of approximately 30 wt.% of glycerol enabled uniform drug distribution, favorable microstructural features, and sustained release kinetics leading to the pronounced morphological changes. The F-4 composite film contains large droplets, with an average size of approximately 3.7 µm, corresponding to glycerol-rich domains containing dissolved 5-FU and DMSO uniformly distributed throughout the polymer matrix. XRD analysis of the F-4 composite film revealed the complete absence of the intense characteristic diffraction peak of 5-FU crystalline phase at 28.7°, demonstrating molecular dispersion of the drug within the amorphous PDMSOH-glycerol matrix, and suppression of drug recrystallization. The in vitro transdermal drug permeability of F-4 film was investigated using synthetic skin diffusion membrane. The collected samples were analyzed spectrophotometrically at 266 nm wavelength, and the drug permitted amount from this formulation reached 37.3% after 48 h.

Blank F-4 film extracts showed minimal cytotoxicity, whereas drug-loaded extracts induced time-dependent cytotoxicity in A549 and HepG2 cells, consistent with the UV–Vis 5-FU release profile. Overall, the F-4 composite film represents a promising and tunable system for sustained time-dependent delivery of 5-fluorouracil using cytocompatible silicone matrix.

Materials and methods

5-fluorouracil (5-FU, > 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), hydroxyl-terminated PDMS (PDMSOH, viscosity 2000 cSt, MW∼36,000 g/mol; Gelest), tetraethoxysilane (TEOS, Sigma-Aldrich), bis(3-aminopropyl)-terminated PDMS (PDMSNH2, MW 850–900 g/mol and 3000 g/mol; Gelest), stannous octoate (Sn(Oct)2, Sigma-Aldrich), glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich) were used as received without further purification.

5-FU is poorly soluble in water (12.2 g/L at 20 oC), soluble in 1N NH4OH (50 mg/ml) and DMSO (10–50 mg/ml).

Preparation 5-FU containing silicone films by solution casting method

Specific amounts of PDMSOH were mixed with the crosslinking agent TEOS (Table 1). DMSO and the drug were weighed in a separate Eppendorf tube, mixed for several minutes until the drug complete dissolution, and this solution was added to the prepared silicone mixture. The resulting emulsion was stirred thoroughly, and the curing catalyst was introduced. The mixture was manually stirred for 8–10 min and poured onto plastic Petri dish. For preparing glycerol containing composite films the specific amount of glycerol (Table 1) was added to the prepared silicone mixture and the resulting emulsion sonicated (40 kHz) for 30 min forming homogeneous dispersion, the curing catalyst was introduced, stirred manually for several minutes and poured onto plastic Petri dish. The reaction mixtures were left to cure at room temperature; the diameter of the films obtained was approximately 4.5 cm, and a thickness- 0.3–0.5 mm.

5-FU release and swelling studies

Calibration of 5-FU (UV–Vis) and release. A calibration curve for 5-FU was prepared in 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution and in phosphate buffer solution (pH 5.5). UV–Vis absorbance was measured at 266 nm using Cary 100 UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The calibration curves were linear over the concentration range of 5.5–0.374–5.5 mg/100 ml, with a regression coefficient 2.86 (R2 = 0.9993). For in vitro 5-FU release assessment film samples were placed in Eppendorf tubes containing 2.0 mL of 0.9% (w/v) NaCl or phosphate buffer solution (pH 5.5) and incubated at 37 ± 1 °C or 32 °C, respectively. At predetermined time points, the release medium was collected for analysis and replaced with fresh 0.9% NaCl or phosphate buffer solution (2.0 mL). The 5-FU released amount was quantified at 266 nm using the calibration curve. Cumulative drug release (%) was calculated relative to the total 5-FU content in each sample. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean values.

Swelling. The swelling studies were conducted using three small pieces (0.07–0.10 g) of each composite film. Samples were immersed in 0.9% NaCl or phosphate buffer solution at 37 °C or 32 °C, respectively. After 24 h, the samples were removed, gently blotted with filter paper and weighed. Then a new portion of fresh solution was added. In vitro testing of the F-4 film for transdermal 5-FU permeability was performed using a Franz Diffusion Cell Apparatus (Model 2351-6C) and a Strat-M synthetic skin diffusion membrane (Millipore, Merck). The film was fixed over the membrane. The receptor compartments were filled with 10.0 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 5.5). The temperature was maintained at 37 °C with constant stirring at 800 rpm using a magnetic stirrer. At time intervals of 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, and 48 h, 0.7 mL samples were collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh phosphate buffer (pH 5.5) to maintain sink conditions. The collected samples were analyzed spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 266 nm.

Raman spectroscopy, SEM, X-ray diffraction

Raman measurements were performed using Bruker SENTERRA II Raman spectroscope (Bruker, Germany) with OLYMPUS 50 × objective, the laser wavelength 785 nm, the power 50 mV, Raman spectra were recorded in the range of 50–3200 cm-1. The areas of mapping 90 × 60 μm2 with a step size of 2 µm, resulting in the collection of 2500 spectra per map. The mapping results were processed using the OPUS software. The characteristic 5-FU peak at 1350 cm⁻1 was selected for chemical mapping.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a Prisma E SEM instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

The XRD data on the phase composition of F-4 film were collected by a MiniFlex 600 Rigaku Smart Lab SE diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Japan) equipped with a D/teX Ultra 250 1D detector and Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) operated at 40 kV and 15 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 20–100° with a step size of 0.01° and a scanning rate of 2°·min⁻1. Phase identification was performed using the JCPDS–ICDD database.

FT-IR spectra were recorded on Nicolet Avatar 330 FT IR spectrometer.

Mass spectrometry was employed to determine whether residual TEOS partitioned into phosphate buffer solutions collected during the 5-FU release kinetics study of sample F-4. Sampling was conducted over a time range from 30 min to 72 h. Analysis was performed using a Waters Xevo G3 HRMS equipped with an Atmospheric Pressure Solid Analysis Probe (ASAP) ionization source. No traces of TEOS were detected, and screening for potential hydrolytic degradation products yielded negative results.

Cytotoxicity assessment

Cell culture

Human hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 (HB-8065, ATCC) and human lung cancer epithelial cells A549 (CCL-185, ATCC) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). Cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (A549) and DMEM (HepG2) (Gibco, UK), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Capricorn, South America), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and 1% L-Glutamine (Capricorn, Germany). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Sample preparation

Cytotoxicity was assessed using an extract-based approach in accordance with ISO 10993-5 [ISO2009]32. Test samples comprised (i) a film and (ii) an identical film loaded with 5-FU. Samples were sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation for 30 min, then immersed in complete cell culture medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum) at an extraction ratio of 0.1 g/mL (0.3 g sample in 3 mL medium). Extractions were performed at 37 °C for 0.5, 2.5, 4.5, 24, 48, and 72 h. At each time point, the resulting extracts were collected in triplicate and stored at 4 °C until use.

MTT test

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (Greiner CELLSTAR, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) at a density of 2.0 × 104 cells/well in 100 µL complete medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ prior to treatment. The medium was then replaced with the corresponding sample extracts (prepared as described above), and cells were exposed for 24 h. Following treatment, cell viability was assessed using the MTT colorimetric assay33,34. Briefly, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich; cat. no. M5655) was added to each well and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. The supernatant was then removed, and 150 µL DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (HumaReader HS, HUMAN, Germany) at 570 nm with a 630 nm reference wavelength. The viability of untreated cells was considered 100%.

All experiments were performed in triplicates. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (Boston, MA, USA). To evaluate differences between treatments, the Student’s t-test were applied. The p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
