Inter‐rater agreement of HER2‐low scores between expert breast pathologists and the Visiopharm digital image analysis application (HER2 APP, CE2797)
Authors: Suzanne Parry, Lila Zabaglo, Abeer M Shaaban, Andrew Dodson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInter‐observer concordance data for the HER2 category as assessed by a group of 16 specialist breast pathologists on 50 diagnostic core biopsies was compared with that produced by digital image analysis (DIA) using the HER2 APP, CE2797 (VP APP; Visiopharm, Hoersholm, Denmark). Comparing pathologists' consensus scores and DIA scores, 36 cases (73.5%) agreed. Fleiss' kappa statistic was 0.433 (indicative of moderate agreement). Cohen's weighted kappa was used to compare the scores of individual raters to consensus scores; for all 50 cases the kappa scores had a range between 0.412 and 0.854; the VP APP was ranked 12th of 17 raters (kappa score 0.638 indicating substantial agreement). Results for HER2‐low cases (N = 44) showed a kappa score range of 0.295 to 0.823; the VP APP ranked 12th of 17 (score 0.535 indicating moderate agreement). For high agreement cases the kappa score range was 0.664 to 1.000 for all HER2 scores (N = 24) and the VP APP scored 0.916 (indicating almost perfect agreement). For the HER2‐low scores (N = 20), the kappa score range was 0.506–1.000 and the VP APP scored 0.860 (almost perfect agreement). DIA of the proportions of tumour cells showing expression within each of the HER2 categories demonstrated that the majority of cases showing a low level of agreement between pathologists showed heterogeneity and/or a level of expression close to a cut‐point for decision making. This study demonstrates that the VP APP produces results that are extremely well‐aligned to those of expert pathologists in cases with good overall agreement, and in difficult cases its reproducibility will outperform that of the visual scorer. The results also suggest that use of the VP APP has the potential to reduce the proportion of cases referred for gene amplification testing by reducing the number of cases incorrectly classified as HER2 2+.Keywords: external quality assessment, EQA, proficiency testing, immunocytochemistry, ICC, immunohistochemistry, IHC, human epidermal growth factor‐2, HER2, HER2‐low, digital image analysis, DIA, concordance, inter‐rater agreement

Introduction

Following on from the publication of the DESTINY‐04 Breast trial [1] and subsequent approval of the antibody–drug conjugate trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‐DXd) as a therapeutic agent for the treatment of metastatic HER2‐low breast cancer, there has been a massive need for appropriate selection of patients eligible for this new antibody–drug conjugate therapy. In their paper published in 2023, an international group of experts in breast oncology working under the auspices of the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) produced a set of expert consensus statements on the definition, diagnosis and management of HER2‐low breast cancer. One of these concerned the use of new technologies to improve the quantitative detection of HER2‐low expression in breast cancer and stated that ‘a key unmet need remains the improvement of concordance in HER2 scoring among pathologists. In this setting, artificial intelligence (AI)‐powered HER2 analysis may help achieve consistent HER2 expression level evaluation in breast cancer by reducing interobserver variability in HER2‐low cases’ [2].

Being fully in agreement with this statement, the authors of this paper sought a collaboration with Visiopharm A/S (Hoersholm, Denmark), a world leader in the production of digital image analysis solutions in the field of cellular pathology [3], with the aim of producing early evidence on the performance of the company's updated CE‐IVD certified diagnostic HER2 APP (#90007) [4], in comparison to a group of expert breast pathologists.

We used inter‐observer concordance HER2 category data generated in a study where a group of 16 specialist breast pathologists undertook the assessment of breast cancer cases, the majority of which had previously been shown to express HER2 in the low or ultralow range. This data was compared with that produced by digital image analysis of the same sample set.

Materials and methods

The study cases comprised 50 breast cancer samples (1 resection and 49 core biopsies) that had been selected from the routine clinical caseload of a single, large UK NHS Trust Cellular Pathology Department (based in Queen Elizabeth Hospital, Birmingham, UK) to be enriched for tumours expressing HER2 in the HER2‐low range (0, 1+, and 2+) based on staining carried out as part of their routine clinical work‐up using the PATHWAY 4B5 assay (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA). The department is a reference centre for HER2 immunohistochemistry and HER2 gene fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) testing and performs well in the UK NEQAS ICC and ISH HER2 programmes. In brief, the original HER2 scores at diagnosis were as follows: 20/50 (40%) were HER2 0, 13/50 (26%) were HER2 1+, 12/50 (24%) were HER2 2+, of which 3/12 (25%) were HER2 gene‐amplified; the remaining 5/50 (10%) were HER2 3+. Supplementary material, Table S1 gives full details of the scores assigned at diagnosis.

Whole slide images (WSIs) were obtained at ×40 magnification using an Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems, Vista, CA, USA). These images were made available to 16 expert breast pathologists based in the UK and Republic of Ireland who assessed them for HER2 expression following the UK HER2 scoring guidelines (2023 update) [5] in a study aimed at assessing inter‐rater concordance in this area of pathology practice [6].

The study reported here used the same set of WSIs to compare the performance of a digital image analysis application designed to assess HER2 expression in this clinical setting (the RUO version, #10185) of the CE‐IVD certified diagnostic HER2 APP (#90007) (VP APP, Visiopharm, Hoersholm, Denmark) with that of the expert pathologist group. To facilitate this, WSIs and anonymised data from the original study [6] were made available by its chief reporting author (AMS).

The VP APP is an automated single‐cell approach software application for HER2 IHC quantification and 0, 1+, 2+, and 3+ categorisation using an algorithm aligned with ASCO/CAP HER2 IHC guidelines [7].

The level of inter‐rater agreement was assessed using Fleiss' multiple‐rater kappa statistic (κ).

Cohen's weighted kappa coefficient was calculated to assess the agreement between individual raters' scores (pathologists and VP APP) and the consensus scores. This was done for all cases, and for the sub‐set of cases where the level of agreement (LoA) was at least high (>75%). A second sub‐set of cases to be analysed comprised all HER2‐low cases, defined as those in which the consensus score was 1+ or 2+. The third sub‐set analysed consisted of HER2‐low cases in which the LoA was categorised as at least high. The HER2 0 score comprised both the HER2‐ultralow (weak/faint staining in >10% of cells) and the HER2 Null (no staining) group.

Data were collated and analysed using Excel (Office 365, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA), which was also used for the preparation of charts and tables. Additional analyses and chart production used the two software packages, SPSS (Version 29.0.2.0 [20], IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Prism (Version 10.3.0, GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA).

Specific ethical approval was not required for this study as it was conducted for the purposes of quality improvement on materials where patient consent had previously been obtained as part of the surgical procedure consent.

Comparison of pathologists' consensus score with VP APP score

In a comparison between the pathologists' consensus HER2 scores, which were previously published [6], and the scores produced by the Visiopharm HER2 APP (VP APP), 36/49 (73.5%) cases agreed and 13/49 (26.5%) disagreed. For one case (#31), the pathologists' scores were split equally between 1+ and 2+, and it was not possible to assign a consensus score; hence, the total number of comparisons was 49. LoA, level of agreement.

Of the 13 non‐concordant cases, 9 (69.2%) occurred between scores that were assigned to cases in the poor or low level of agreement (LoA) categories and four (30.8%) to cases in the high LoA category. None of the cases in the poor LoA category, and none of the cases where the pathologists had been unanimous in their HER2 score assignment (absolute LoA) showed disagreement between that score and the VP APP score. Of the discordant cases, the VP APP scored fewer cases as equivocal (2+) compared with the expert pathologists: three versus seven cases, respectively.

Of the seven cases that had a pathologist's consensus score of 2+, but were assessed as 1+ by the VP APP, five had a FISH result recorded at original diagnosis, all of which (100.0%) were non‐amplified, supporting the hypothesis that the cases were overscored by visual assessment (see also supplementary material, Table S2).

Table 2 shows the 13 non‐concordant cases, including data on the proportion of invasive tumour cells ascribed to each HER2 score category by the VP APP analyses. Detailed case‐wise examination of the VP APP proportion results for each HER2 category indicates that in the majority of cases (9/13, 69.2%), there was a wide disagreement between pathologists' consensus and VP APP data. For example, in Case 38, which was scored as a HER2 2+ case by 12 pathologists (High LoA category), the VP APP assessed only 1.1% of all invasive cells in that category; similarly, in Case 45, only 4.2% of cells were categorised as HER2 2+ by the VP APP, while 14 pathologists scored it in that category (again, this was a High LoA category case). In four cases (4/13, 30.8%), there was evidence that the proportion of cells assessed by the VP APP as being in the category that agreed with the pathologists' consensus score was close to the required cut‐point for that category. An example is Case 32, where the majority of cells (88.8%) showed no HER2 expression, and the case was scored as HER2 0 by 10 pathologists (Low LoA category) and therefore as HER2‐ultralow. However, the VP APP analysis indicated that 11.2% of the cells present were stained with an intensity that was classified as 1+; thus, the application returned a HER2 1+ score (HER2‐low) for the case. In a second similar case (Case 37), 14.9% of the invasive tumour cells were assessed as HER2 1+ by the VP APP and 83.8% as HER2 0. These cases indicate the difficulty visual assessors have when cases are close to cut‐points, that is, the 10% cut‐point that divides HER2 1+ from HER2 0.

In the contingency table shown in Table 3 it can be seen that, where the scores differed, none did so by more than one category. For the majority of discrepant cases (9/13, 69.2%) the VP APP returned a lower HER2 score than the pathologists' consensus score. Of these, the most common discrepancy which was seen in 7/13 (53.8%) was for cases scored by pathologists' consensus as 2+ and by the VP APP as 1+.

Effects of adding in the VP APP score to the pathologists' consensus

As previously noted, the VP APP score disagreed with the pathologists' consensus score in 13/49 (26.5%) of cases. The consensus score was not altered by the addition of the VP APP data in any of those cases. It did alter the LoA category into which cases were classified in 5/13 (38.5%); for three, it changed the category from Low to Poor, and for two from High to Low. When the VP APP score was added to that of the pathologists in the case in which it had not previously been possible to assign a consensus score, it was categorised as 1+ (see also supplementary material, Table S3).

Fleiss' multiple‐rater kappa statistic

Fleiss' kappa statistic gives a measure of the agreement between two or more raters assessed using a categorical scale. The overall agreement between 16 pathologists rating all 50 cases was 0.433 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.417–0.449). When the scores of the VP APP were included, the result was almost unchanged at 0.433 (95% CI: 0.418–0.448). A κ value in the range of >0.40 to 0.60 is indicative of moderate agreement.

The κ values for agreement within individual HER2 categories were closely similar when results for pathologists alone were compared with those for pathologists plus the VP APP. Paired results for the HER2 0 and the HER2 2+ categories were 0.437 and 0.444, and 0.431 and 0.424, respectively; both sets being close to the kappa values for the overall agreement and all indicating moderate agreement. However, the paired results for the HER2 1+ category were substantially lower (0.292 and 0.296) indicating only fair agreement (κ values in the range >0.20 to 0.40). In contrast, the results for agreement of scores in the HER2 3+ category were 0.803 and 0.808, both indicating agreement that was almost perfect (κ value range >0.80 to 1.00). HER2 category proportional scores

As well as assigning a HER2 category score, the VP APP also produces data on the proportion of invasive tumour cells characterised within each of the HER2 categories from 0 to 3+. The app's algorithm evaluates both membrane completeness and staining intensity in deriving the final category score for any given case.

The graphs shown in Figure 2 depict the proportions of cells assigned to each HER2 category by the VP APP for each case. Cases have been grouped according to their overall consensus HER2 category score.

HER2 0 consensus score cases

Seven cases were classified as HER2 0 by consensus; all were shown by the VP APP to comprise tumour cells lacking HER2 expression (HER2 0), with a range of 83.8% to 99.1%. The VP APP classified five of these as HER2 0 (concordant); in these cases, no other category of HER2 expression was demonstrated in >10% of the invasive tumour cell population. In the two discordant cases, both classified as HER2 1+ by the VP APP, 11.2% and 14.9% of cells, respectively, were assessed as expressing HER2 at a 1+ level. HER2 1+ consensus score cases

This was the largest group with 24 cases being classified as HER2 1+ by consensus. The VP APP HER2 category was concordant in 21 of these cases; in this group, the proportions of tumour cells categorised as HER2 1+ ranged from 20.5% to 85.4%. In the majority of these cases, the proportion of HER2 2+ cells present was very low (range: 0.1–3.0); the exceptions to this were two cases in which 5.2% and 8.7% of the tumour cell populations expressed HER2 at the 2+ level. No 3+ cells were seen in any of the cases.

Two cases were classified as HER2 2+ by the VP APP, both of which were shown to contain proportions of 2+ above the required 10% cut‐point (16.8% and 20.7%, respectively).

Conversely, one case was classified as HER2 0. In this case, the proportion of 1+ cells was 7.4%, thus not reaching the required 10% cut‐point. Cases are illustrated in Figure 4.

HER2 2+ consensus score cases

Fourteen cases were classified as HER2 2+ by consensus. The VP APP dichotomised these into seven cases in which the HER2 category score was concordant, and seven cases in which it was assessed as being 1+. The range of 2+ proportions in the concordant cases was 10.3% to 57.7%, and in the discordant cases it was 1.0% to 8.3%. Across all the cases, the proportion of 3+ cells ranged between 0.0% and 1.2%.

For 12 of the cases, HER2 gene amplification results were available, showing that three were amplified and nine were non‐amplified. In four of the nine non‐amplified cases, the VP APP returned a score of HER2 1+; all of these three cases showed a high LoA for a 2+ score among the pathologists. The app returned a HER2 2+ score for all three gene‐amplified cases. Thus, if the application had been employed to assist at primary assessment, the FISH workload would have been reduced by 33.3% (4/12 cases) without compromising the detection of cases which required confirmation of their HER2 status by FISH testing.

Discussion

Following on from the DESTINY 01–03 trials that establish the efficacy of T‐DXd in metastatic HER2‐positive (HER2 3+) breast cancer, further trials were conducted to examine its role in tumours with low levels of HER2 expression. The DESTINY 04 trial examined the efficacy in patients with advanced HER2‐low breast cancer and was the first to show clinically meaningful benefits for patients with HER2‐low tumours [9]. Among all patients, the median progression‐free survival was 9.9 months in the T‐DXd treated group and 5.1 months in the physician's choice group (hazard ratio for disease progression or death, 0.50; p < 0.001), and overall survival was 23.4 and 16.8 months, respectively (hazard ratio for death, 0.64; p = 0.001). The results have led to the approval of the use of T‐DXd in the treatment of metastatic HER2‐low breast cancer in the USA and Europe.

Recently reported results from the DESTINY‐Breast06 trial showed a similar meaningful clinical benefit in HER2 ultralow breast cancer and USA and European approval for use of the drug in these ultralow expressing tumours [10].

Therefore, selection of patients with HER2‐low and ultralow breast tumours has become critical to inform patient management. Guidelines have been developed to emphasise the current need to distinguish between IHC score 0 and 1+ (currently designated as negative) and to refine the criteria for identifying breast cancer with low levels of HER2 expression [5, 7].

Several studies have highlighted the issue of high inter‐observer variability in the scoring of HER2‐low immunohistochemistry [6, 11, 12, 13]. Various issues have been implicated, including heterogeneity of HER2 expression, background/cytoplasmic staining, and the difficulties in defining the precise percentage of HER2 staining falling around the cut‐off value of positivity (10%) by eyeballing [14, 15].

Other reports in the literature indicating the difficulties faced by pathologists include a Chinese national proficiency testing scheme which showed a few institutions with systematic bias in scoring HER2 0, 1+, and 2+/FISH negative with unsatisfactory accuracy levels (below 59%); additionally, the concordance rate for HER2 0 cases was only 78.1%, suggesting that this is a challenging area for pathologists [16]; a Japanese study of 20 invasive breast cancers that were scored by 144 pathologists showed a mean percentage agreement between the test group and a set of seven expert reference pathologists of only 63.4% [17]; an Australian study of 60 breast cancer core biopsies from three laboratories assessed by nine experienced breast pathologists showed that HER2 low cancers with scanty (<20%) of tumour cells expressing the protein had the lowest concordance levels [13]; and finally, a recent study of 50 cases scored by 6 pathologists compared with digital image analysis showed that the latter was highly concordant with pathologist scores in 96% of cases, suggesting it may enhance standardisation and quantification of HER2 expression, particularly for IHC scores 0 and 1+ [18].

Standardising reporting using digital pathology and artificial intelligence algorithms has therefore become a priority [2, 19]. This, however, requires establishing ground truth for concordance comparison.

In this report, we have compared the HER2 category assessment results produced by a commercially available digital image analysis algorithm (VP APP from Visiopharm) with those produced by a group of expert breast pathologists highly experienced in performing this task in the clinical setting.

The strength of the study principally lies in the robust nature of the pathologist‐generated HER2 assessment data as previously published in the original study [6]. It also lies in the fact that, being derived as it was from a consensus of 16 raters, it was possible to attribute a high measure of certainty to the score for each of the samples assessed.

Additionally, the case set from the same study referenced in the previous paragraph [6], which comprised 50 clinically sourced diagnostic biopsy specimens, was deliberately enriched for cases that fall into the newly defined HER2‐ultralow and low categories, namely HER2 0, 1+, and 2+/FISH‐negative cases. The set was further selected to include cases that represented staining patterns that have been highlighted as major contributors to assessment difficulty, that is, tumour heterogeneity and poor localisation. Therefore, it is not surprising that, for several of the cases assessed, the LoA was low. This was particularly seen when the proportion of the tumour expressing HER2 staining was close to a critical cut‐point. This is to be expected, since it has been repeatedly demonstrated for multiple biomarkers that estimation of tumour proportion falling into a given category is highly unreliable. As an exemplar, this has been very well evidenced in the papers of the International Ki‐67 in Breast Cancer Working Group (IKWG), which undertook a series of studies over the course of 10 years that aimed to characterise the main contributors to variability for this biomarker. They concluded that, in regard to the assessment of Ki‐67% labelling index, only very low (≤5%) and very high (≥30%) values can be reliably categorised as low or high by visual scoring of IHC‐stained samples, and that substantial inter‐observer/laboratory variability is observed in the range of between those two extremes unless some form of formal counting or image analysis is used [20].

If one takes agreement of the HER2 category scores produced by the VP APP with the consensus scores, the VP APP software performed moderately well with the performance of the software being ranked 12th out of the 17 raters, as indicated by the Cohen's weighted kappa score of 0.638. But a fairer comparison might be that where the VP APP's results were compared to the pathologists' scores in cases where they showed high agreement. Here the kappa score was 0.916.

Detailed examination of the cases where there was a discrepancy between the consensus and the app revealed some consistent interpretive patterns that pathologists appear to rely on when making diagnostic decisions on HER2 category. For example, in the assignment of a 2+ category, the 14 cases that were scored as such by consensus all contained populations of 2+ cells. But the proportion of 2+ cells present had a significant bearing on the level of agreement; in all cases where the percentage of 2+ cells present was above 13% there was a high level of agreement, and it only required the percentage of 2+ cells present to be 1–2% for a HER2 2+ score to be considered, albeit by a lower majority of scorers. It is probable that pathologists do not mistake such low percentage populations as being above the required 10% cut‐point but are swayed to give the ‘benefit of the doubt’ in the knowledge that reflex testing for gene amplification will ensure that patients whose tumours do not show gene amplification will not be wrongly assigned as eligible for targeted therapy.

It is of interest that the proportion of cases scored as equivocal (2+) by the VP APP is markedly lower than that by pathologists. It is plausible that the human eye is drawn to the positive areas within the slide, leading to overestimation of the percentage expression. The use of the algorithm will therefore result in less FISH testing and foster improved turnaround times.

This study clearly demonstrates the valuable role digital image analysis can play in improving the standardisation and reproducibility of HER2 category assessment, particularly in the HER2‐low setting. Its ability to accurately measure proportions of stained tumour cells within each of the HER2 categories is particularly important in cases that exhibit results close to the relevant cut‐points for decision making.
