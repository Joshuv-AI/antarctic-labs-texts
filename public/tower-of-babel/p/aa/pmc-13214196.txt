Heterogeneous grip strength trajectories and clinical determinants in middle-aged and older chinese patients with cardiovascular disease: a group-based trajectory modelling analysis of the CHARLS
Authors: Shuyan Su, Zexin Chen, Tongchi Xu, Hongchao Wu, Xilun Ma, Ziting Peng, Minping Lin, Xiaoqin Xu, Ruibin Huang, Chaosen Zhong, Haizhu Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrip strength (GS) is a potent biomarker in cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, evidence is scarce regarding the long-term dynamic changes of GS and their multifactorial determinants. This study aimed to identify distinct GS trajectories and their determinants among middle-aged and older Chinese adults with CVD.MethodsWe analyzed data from 2,189 patients with CVD across three waves (2011, 2013, 2015) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). GS was measured using a standardized protocol. Sex-stratified group-based trajectory model (GBTM) was employed to identify distinct GS trajectories. Multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with trajectory group membership using the high-level GS trajectory as the reference group.ResultsGBTM identified three distinct parallel declining GS trajectories for each sex, classified as low-, middle-, and high-level GS groups. In fully adjusted models, older age, lower BMI, and higher FI were significantly associated with membership in both low- and middle-level GS groups compared to the high-level GS group for both sexes (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study revealed three GS trajectories among patients with CVD, with older age, lower BMI, and higher FI emerging as robust determinants of the low GS trajectory. These findings highlight the potential value of longitudinal GS monitoring for identifying high-risk individuals. Whether interventions targeting modifiable factors such as nutritional status and frailty can influence GS trajectories and subsequent clinical outcomes warrants further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05803-y.Keywords: CHARLS, Cardiovascular disease, Grip strength, Trajectory analysis, Aging, Frailty

Introduction

Muscular strength, a key indicator of physiological resilience in aging, is increasingly recognized as a robust predictor of morbidity and mortality across a spectrum of many chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1–5]. Consequently, the monitoring of muscle strength has become a clinical imperative, for which grip strength (GS) has emerged as the most widely adopted surrogate measure due to its simplicity, reliability and cost-effectiveness [6].

CVD, a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, represents a spectrum of cardiac and vascular conditions that disproportionately affect older adults [7, 8]. The rising incidence of CVD in the aging population severely impacts quality of life and imposes a substantial economic burden on global healthcare systems. Recently, a substantial body of evidence now supports the utility of GS as a prognostic biomarker for both the incidence and mortality of CVD [9–12]. For example, lower GS has been consistently associated with an increased risk of CVD, and its inclusion has been shown to enhance the predictive accuracy of established CVD risk score [11, 12]. However, the majority of existing research has focused on a single, baseline measurement of pre-disease GS as a predictor of CVD [10–12].

Given that GS is a dynamic parameter, a single measurement cannot fully capture its clinical relevance. Recent longitudinal studies on GS have begun to explore the relationship between the GS trajectories and the occurrence and progression of diseases [13–15]. Group-based trajectory model (GBTM) is a widely used approach for capturing heterogeneity in developmental or change trajectories within a population [16]. Liu et al. applied GBTM to identify three long-term GS trajectories in men and women and found that a low GS trajectory was associated with a higher risk of CVD [17]. However, the longitudinal trajectory of GS following a CVD diagnosis, a dynamic measure reflecting the progression of functional decline remains poorly characterized. As declining GS is a powerful predictor of all-cause mortality, understanding the heterogeneous GS trajectories in CVD patients is of paramount importance.

Therefore, this study aims to identify heterogeneous trajectories of GS and their determinants among middle-aged and older Chinese adults with CVD, utilizing data from the CHARLS and applying GBTM. The characterization of distinct GS trajectories and their associated factors may facilitate early identification of high-risk patient subgroups, thereby informing the development of targeted therapeutic or rehabilitative interventions.

Methods

This study employed GBTM to identify GS trajectories among 2,189 CVD patients using longitudinal data from three waves (2011–2015) of the CHARLS. Subsequently, multinomial logistic regression was utilized to determine factors associated with trajectory group membership (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the analytical procedure

Study design and population

The present study was a secondary analysis of data from the CHARLS. The CHARLS cohort was established using a multistage stratified probability-proportional-to-size sampling method, enrolling individuals aged 45 years and above from 28 provinces across China. The CHARLS is an ongoing survey with exams performed every 2 years. Data were collected every 2 years using a standardized questionnaire covering demographics, health status, and other variables [18]. Further details can be found on the CHARLS website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).

For this analysis, we used data from the 2011 baseline survey data (wave1) including 17,708 participants, and two follow-up waves in 2013 (wave 2) and 2015 (wave 3). The presence of prevalent CVD at baseline was the primary inclusion criterion. According to the Disease Classification Codebook, Text Coding Technical Report for CFPS, and the American Heart Association, CVD in this study included the following conditions (based on participant self-reports of physician-diagnosed diseases): heart disease (including angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease, and other heart problems), hypertensive disease, and stroke [19, 20].

The original CHARLS study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (approval number: IRB00001052 − 11015), and all participants provided informed consent.

Participants selection

From the initial baseline cohort (N = 17,708), we first identified participants with a prevalent diagnosis of CVD, resulting in a sub-cohort of 5,705 participants. From this CVD sub-cohort, we then performed the following exclusion criteria sequentially: we first excluded 141 participants who were younger than 45 years old at baseline (N = 5,564), then 1,361 participants who were lost to follow-up in both 2013 and 2015, resulting in a loss-to-follow-up rate of 24.5% (1,361/5,564). Subsequently, we excluded 1,958 participants with missing GS data across three waves, and finally, 56 participants with biologically implausible values for BMI or GS. This process yielded a final analytical sample of 2,189 participants.

Measurement of GS

GS was measured in kilograms using a YuejianTM WL-1000 dynamometer. Prior to measurement, participants were screened for recent hand injuries or pain. Eligible participants reported their dominant hand and were measured while standing, holding the dynamometer with the arm at a 90° angle at the elbow. Following a standardized protocol, participants were instructed to hold the dynamometer at a 90° angle. Two measurements were taken for each hand, and the mean value of all four measurements was used for this analysis to represent overall GS.

Variables

A comprehensive set of baseline covariates were selected as potential determinants of GS trajectories. These were categorized as follows: (i) sociodemographic characteristics: age, gender, residence (urban/rural), education level (illiterate, primary school, middle school, high school/vocational high school, and junior college or above), marital status (married/other) and social events; (ii) behavioral habits: smoking(ever/never), alcohol consumption(ever/never) and sleep time; (iii) anthropometric and laboratory examinations at baseline (2011): body mass index (BMI), white blood cell count, creatinine, total cholesterol, uric acid and hemoglobin; (iv) Frailty index (FI): a 32-item FI based on the CHARLS database was constructed following established methodology [21]. The index quantifies the accumulation of health deficits across multiple domains (physical function, cognitive, and disease status). Each deficit was scored as 0 (absent) or 1 (present), and the FI was calculated as the proportion of deficits present in an individual, expressed as a percentage ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater frailty [22].

Statistical analysis

The proportion of missing data for the main covariates with missing at random ranged from 0% to 22.75% (see Table S1). Under the assumption that data were missing at random, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with five imputed datasets was performed, and results were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

GBTM analysis was applied using the lcmm package in R to identify distinct GS trajectories stratified by gender. Models specified a linear time trend with class-varying fixed effects for slopes and a subject-level random intercept to account for within-subject correlation. Models with 1 to 6 latent classes were estimated via maximum likelihood, using one-class linear mixed model solutions as starting values. The optimal number of trajectory groups was determined by iteratively comparing these models and evaluating the following criteria: (1) lower values of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC); (2) good classification accuracy, indicated by an average posterior probability (APP) > 0.7 for each group and a proportion of members assigned with high confidence (> 0.7 posterior probability); and (3) practical interpretability, requiring each trajectory group to comprise at least 5% of the gender-specific sample; (4) acceptable classification accuracy, reflected by entropy values preferably > 0.6. Linear mixed models with group-by-time interactions were used to compare annual decline rates across trajectories, with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Baseline characteristics were compared across the identified trajectory groups using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables. To identify the baseline determinants of trajectory group membership, we performed multinomial logistic regression. The trajectory group with the most favorable outcome (e.g., higher GS trajectory group) was set as the reference category. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To ensure the methodological robustness of our findings, two rigorous supplementary analyses were conducted. First, to address potential selection bias arising from participants excluded due to missing GS data or loss to follow-up, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded cohorts. Furthermore, an extreme-case sensitivity analysis was performed by hypothetically assigning all excluded participants to either the lowest (worst-case) or highest (best-case) GS trajectory to test the stability of identified predictors. Second, to methodologically justify the trajectory approach over a single time-point assessment, we compared the GBTM classifications with a simple baseline GS stratification. Participants were categorized into three baseline groups based on tertiles (33rd and 67th percentiles) of baseline GS. Cohen’s Kappa statistic and confusion matrices were used to assess classification agreement. Additionally, identical multinomial logistic regression models were fitted using the baseline-derived groups, and the results were compared side-by-side with the GBTM findings to evaluate the models’ capacity to filter out cross-sectional noise.

All statistical tests were two-sided with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. and Stata version 17.0.

Results

Study population and identification of grip strength trajectories

This study included 2,189 patients with CVD, consisting of 917 men and 1,272 women. Group-based trajectory modeling was employed to identify distinct patterns of GS change over time for men and women separately. The selection of the optimal trajectory model was based on smaller BIC, AIC values, acceptable entropy, and APP values (≥ 0.7) (see Table S2). For both sexes, a three-trajectory model was selected as the optimal solution.

Compared to the high-level GS group, participants in the low- and middle- level GS groups were significantly older, more likely to be unmarried and reside in rural areas, had lower education attainment, lower BMI, shorter sleep duration, less social activities, and higher FI scores (all P-value < 0.05). Additionally, sex-specific differences were noted. Women in the low- and middle- level GS groups had a higher prevalence of smoking history and higher hemoglobin levels. In men, lower cholesterol level was associated with low- and middle- level GS groups compared to the high-level GS group.

Description of GS trajectories

Three distinct GS trajectories were identified for each sex: “low-level”, “middle-level”, and “high-level”. Among women (n = 1,272), the groups comprised 24.76% (n = 315) in the low-level, 59.12% (n = 752) in the middle-level, and 16.12% (n = 205) in the high-level GS group. For men (n = 917), the distribution was 13.74% (n = 126), 52.13% (n = 478), and 34.13% (n = 313), respectively.

As shown in Fig. 2, all identified GS trajectories showed a parallel pattern of gradually decline in GS from 2011 to 2015. In women, the annual decline rates were similar across trajectory groups (the low-level group: -1.35 kg/year, 95%CI: -1.75 to -0.95; the middle-level group: -1.18 kg/year, 95%CI: -1.44 to -0.92; the high-level group: -1.51 kg/year, 95%CI: -2.00 to -1.01), with no statistically significant differences between groups (low-level group vs. middle-level group: P = 0.768; low-level group vs. high-level group: P = 0.873; middle-level group vs. high-level group: P = 0.480). In men, the annual decline rates were similar comparable (low-level group: -1.39 kg/year, 95%CI: -2.12 to -0.66; middle-level group: -1.77 kg/year, 95%CI: -2.14 to -1.39; high-level group: -1.18 kg/year, 95%CI: -1.65 to -0.72), with no statistically significant differences between groups (low-level group vs. middle-level group: P = 0.638; low-level group vs. high-level group: P = 0.885; middle-level group vs. high-level group: P = 0.131).

Older age was associated with both the low-level (women: aOR = 1.167, 95% CI: 1.139–1.199; men: aOR = 1.188, 95% CI: 1.144–1.233) and middle-level GS groups (women: aOR = 1.092, 95% CI: 1.068–1.116; men: aOR = 1.093, 95% CI: 1.067–1.120). Lower BMI was associated with both the low-level (aOR = 0.885, 95% CI: 0.841–0.932; men: aOR = 0.900, 95% CI: 0.838–0.967) and middle-level GS groups (women: aOR = 0.948, 95% CI: 0.911–0.985; men: aOR = 0.943, 95% CI: 0.905–0.984 ). Methodological validation and sensitivity analyses

The comparison between GBTM and baseline stratification highlighted substantial limitations of single-point assessments. Classification agreement was exceptionally low in women (Kappa = -0.023) and men (Kappa = -0.340) (see Table S3). Notably, the confusion matrix revealed that 231 women initially classified as having “high-level” GS group at baseline were reclassified into the “middle-level” GS trajectory by GBTM, and 164 men initially classified as having “low-level” GS group at baseline were also reclassified into the “middle-level” GS trajectory by GBTM. This indicated that longitudinal modeling corrected massive transient measurement errors. Furthermore, multinomial logistic regression comparisons (See Table S4) demonstrated that while both methods identified core determinants (age, BMI, FI), the baseline model falsely identified significant associations with transient factors (e.g., hemoglobin and cholesterol in men, educational level in women) that were not supported longitudinally.

Regarding potential selection bias, excluded participants were significantly older and a slightly lower FI than the analytical sample (See Table S5). Extreme-case sensitivity analyses (See Table S6) confirmed that older age and higher FI remained robustly associated with adverse GS trajectories across all extreme scenarios for both sexes. Lower BMI also showed consistent associations in most comparisons, though its significance was attenuated in some extreme scenarios (e.g., female middle versus high comparison in the best-case scenario). Conversely, associations regarding social activities exhibited directional reversal under extreme assumptions, shifting from protective factors in worst-case scenarios to risk factors in best-case scenarios, which indicates high susceptibility to selection bias.

Discussion

This study explored the heterogeneous trajectories of GS in middle-aged and older Chinese adults with CVD using a nationally representative sample. Three different levels of GS decline trajectories were identified in CVD patients, and our findings indicated that the low GS trajectory pattern among CVD patients was associated with older age, lower BMI and higher FI. Early identification of CVD patients with persistently low GS may help target those at high risk, and interventions addressing modifiable factors such as nutritional status and frailty could potentially mitigate muscle strength decline and contribute to better long-term outcomes.

Liu et al.’s study identified three declining GS trajectory patterns before CVD diagnosis and the low GS trajectory pattern was associated with a higher risk of CVD [17]. Similarly, through GBTM, we discovered three distinct GS trajectories in Chinese middle-aged and older patients with diagnosed CVD in both sexes. Importantly, while GS declined gradually in all groups, the rates of decline were similar across trajectories, indicating that patients in the low-level trajectory group maintained their disadvantage throughout the follow-up period. Early identification of these patients may be critical for implementing timely interventions to mitigate averse outcomes.

Given the well-established association between GS and the prognosis of CVD, this study further investigated factors influencing GS trajectories among middle-aged and elderly Chinese patients with CVD [6, 23, 24]. We found that older age, lower BMI and higher FI were associated with low GS. Age is a definite and non-modifiable risk factor for declining GS [25, 26]. Previous studies have shown that GS peaks in early adulthood, remains relatively stable through midlife and gradually declines thereafter [27]. Frailty is a complex geriatric syndrome involving the cumulative decline of multiple physiological systems. This syndrome manifests as increased fatigue, reduced physical activity, diminished muscle strength, and poorer exercise tolerance, all of which can directly contribute to lower GS [28]. BMI is commonly used indicator for clinical assessment of nutritional status and low BMI typically reflects a state of malnutrition. Prior researches have demonstrated that GS was negatively associated with malnutrition and patients with poor nutritional status have lower GS than those in better nutritional conditions, which was consistent with our findings [29]. Collectively, these findings suggest that older age, poorer nutritional status, and frailty are key factors contributing to low GS in CVD patients.

These factors do not operate in isolation but are deeply interconnected in a vicious cycle. Malnutrition plays a significant role in frailty and sarcopenia [30]. A malnourished state directly reduces the supply of substrates for muscle protein synthesis, promotes muscle breakdown, and leads to a decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength, i.e., the onset of sarcopenia [31]. Sarcopenia is key in the development of physical frailty [32]. Frail individuals often experience further suppression of appetite and nutrient utilization due to reduced physical activity and elevated inflammatory levels, thereby forming a vicious cycle of “malnutrition–sarcopenia–frailty” [33, 34]. Moreover, aging-related in body composition and metabolic function may be further exacerbated by malnutrition and frailty, accelerating the decline in muscle strength and function. Therefore, routine clinical screening for malnutrition and frailty in middle-aged and elderly CVD patients may be warranted.

Malnutrition and frailty are potentially reversible clinical states, suggesting that interventions targeting these factors might help maintain or improve GS in CVD patients. Previous studies have shown that well-planned diet and nutritional supplements are crucial for maintaining muscle mass and strength in elderly. Key components include adequate protein intake, as well as supplementation with creatine, leucine, and vitamin D [35, 36]. Exercise intervention also plays an important role in improving muscle strength and physical performance [37–39]. Several studies have shown that resistance training increases muscle mass in frail older adults and was considered to be a safe strategy for this population [40–42]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that combined exercise and nutritional therapy improve physical function better than exercise alone [43–45].

A major methodological strength of this study is the demonstration of GBTM’s analytical superiority over traditional baseline stratification. Our comparative analysis highlighted the “snapshot error” inherent in single-time-point assessments. Notably, the confusion matrix revealed that baseline measurements frequently overestimated GS status in women, as 231 “high-level” individuals were reclassified into the “middle-level” trajectory by GBTM. Conversely, baseline measurements underestimated GS status in men, as 164 “low-level” individuals were reclassified into the “middle-level” trajectory by GBTM. Furthermore, single-point models were easily misled by cross-sectional artifacts, such as temporary fluctuations in hemoglobin or cholesterol, generating false-positive risk factors. By incorporating multi-wave data, GBTM acts as a longitudinal physiological filter. It successfully smoothed out these short-term noises, successfully isolating the true, long-term drivers factors for the low GS in CVD patients (age, frailty and BMI).

Several limitations must also be acknowledged. Firstly and most notably, the exclusion of participants due to missing GS data or loss to follow-up introduced selection bias. Our baseline comparison confirmed that the excluded cohort was older and had lower FI. Nevertheless, we conducted a rigorous extreme-case sensitivity analysis to stress-test our findings. This analysis proved that our core conclusion—that older age, greater frailty, and lower BMI drive long-term GS deterioration—is mathematically robust and not an artifact of missing data. However, the sensitivity analysis also revealed that conclusions regarding secondary factors, particularly social activities, are highly contaminated by selection bias and should be interpreted with extreme caution in future clinical applications. Second, diagnosis of CVD and several deficits included in the FI calculation in this study were self-reported, there might exist inevitably a potential for information bias. Third, the CHARLS has a relatively short follow-up period, which prevented us from assessing longer-term GS changes in this cohort. Finally, the findings regarding GS trajectories and the determinants of decline in CVD patients were based on observational data and may not be causal.

Conclusions

Our study has uncovered a concerning fact that the GS trajectories of middle-aged and elderly Chinese CVD patients showed a continuous decline. Older age, lower BMI and higher FI were identified as influential factors associated with low GS trajectory in CVD patients. The regular and continuous GS assessment complements the single static baseline measurement, providing a simple and practical method to identify individuals with declining muscle strength. These findings emphasize the potential modifiable factors, including nutritional status and physical condition, which may provide information for future intervention studies aimed at maintaining the muscle strength in CVD patients.
