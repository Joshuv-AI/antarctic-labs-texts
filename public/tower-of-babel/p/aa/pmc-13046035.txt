Successfully treated delayed upward trans-tentorial herniation (UTTH) following ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement for an acute obstructive hydrocephalus secondary to midline posterior fossa mass in a low-resource setting: case report
Authors: Ermias Fikru Yesuf, Atiklet Zerihun Zewdie, Mestet Yibeltal Shiferaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:UTTH is an exceedingly rare, deadly complication of ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) placement for hydrocephalus secondary to a posterior fossa mass.Presentation of case:VPS was inserted for a 4-year-old patient with hydrocephalus secondary to a posterior fossa mass before tumor resection. Initially, the child had clinical improvement, but after 9 days, he developed deterioration. Brain computed tomography (CT) revealed a UTTH; therefore, ligation of the VPS, intubation, mechanical ventilator support, dexamethasone, mannitol, and urgent resection of the mass were performed. The patient was discharged in improved condition.Discussion:When patients present with hydrocephalus secondary to a posterior fossa mass, initial treatment can be inserting a VPS. Even though initial VPS insertion has advantages it can rarely lead to UTTH, usually within a few hours after surgery. Even if the prognosis is poor, UTTH can be treated by intubating and putting the patient on a mechanical ventilator, ligating the VPS, and resection of the posterior fossa mass.Conclusion:Although extremely rare, delayed UTTH can occur after VPS insertion for hydrocephalus secondary to a posterior fossa mass. When a patient for whom VPS placement is done shows clinical deterioration, an urgent CT scan must be done to rule out a UTTH, even if the patient was having clinical improvement for several days after the VPS insertion. Presence of slit ventricles, relatively open sylvian fissures despite decreased mentation, and effacement of the quadrigeminal cistern are findings indicative of a UTTH. Prompt treatment of UTTH following VPS insertion can result in a satisfactory outcome.

Introduction

An increase in localized intracranial pressure can cause brain herniation[1,2]. Early identification and treatment of the different types of cerebral herniation are essential since there is a very high risk of morbidity and mortality associated with brain herniation syndrome[3,4].

UTTH is one of the different types of brain herniation syndromes, and it can occur as a result of different types of intracranial pathologies[3]. It is an upward shift of the cerebellum and brain stem through the tentorial incisura that can cause the squeezing of the mesencephalon, third ventricle, and vein of Galen[4]. This type of brain herniation is not usually described in detail in the works of literature compared to other types of brain herniation syndromes, and it is exceedingly rare[2,4,5]. Many reports on posterior fossa masses describe compression and distortion of parts of the brain due to downward herniation through the foramen magnum[5].

CSF (cerebrospinal fluid) diversion has several advantages if done before craniotomy for tumor resection[4]. The advantages include improvement of symptoms of the hydrocephalus and increased intracranial pressure, and decreasing the difficulty of the tumor resection surgery[4]. Patients with infratentorial mass resulting in obstructive hydrocephalus (OHCP) pose a special clinical challenge because, while inserting a VPS can alleviate symptoms, its aftereffects must also be considered[6].

In this case report, we discuss the case of a successfully treated delayed UTTH after a VPS was inserted for OHCP secondary to a posterior fossa tumor in a 4-year-old male child.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[7].

Case presentation

A 4-year-old male patient came with a complaint of difficulty in keeping balance for 3 months, with associated headache and blurring of vision of the same duration. Two weeks prior, he developed decreased mentation and vomiting. The child has no past medical or surgical history; no drug intake history or known allergies. The family and social history and review of symptoms were unremarkable.

On physical examination, his vital signs were blood pressure (BP) 90/60, pulse rate (PR) 96, temperature 36.2, and respiratory rate (RR) 24 (regular). His Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was 9/15 (E:2, V:2 & M:5), he had no motor preference, and pupils were mid-size and reactive to light bilaterally. The CT image shows an acute OHCP secondary to a posterior fossa mass. Since intensive care unit (ICU) beds and a mechanical ventilator were not available on that day, a right-sided Keen’s point VPS was placed. The patient progressively improved, and on the second postoperative day, his GCS became 15/15. On his 10th postVPS insertion day, his GCS started to decrease, and progressively became 9/15 (E:2, V:2 & M:5), and vital signs became BP 100/60, PR 74, temperature 36.5, and RR 18 (irregular), pupils were mid-size and sluggishly reactive to light bilaterally. The patient was loaded with mannitol and dexamethasone, and a control CT scan was taken that showed an upward trans-tentorial herniation and slit ventricles (Figs. 1 and 2). An endotracheal tube was inserted and connected to a mechanical ventilator. The shunt was ligated in the ICU at the level of the thorax, and the patient’s GCS progressively improved. Surgery

On the second day of ICU admission, the patient was taken to the operating room, after general anesthesia, a three-point skull clamp was applied, and he was put in a prone position. A midline skin incision was made from the inion to the second cervical vertebra. Subperiosteal dissection was made, and a suboccipital craniotomy was done. Dura was opened in a “Y” fashion and tented, and then the arachnoid was opened. A heterogeneous mass with solid and cystic components was identified, which was soft. The tumor was debulked with suction, and a piecemeal resection through the transvermian approach was done. Dura was closed with periosteal graft, and there was no intraoperative vital sign derangement or major vessel injury.

Postoperative course

The patient was transferred back to the ICU. He was extubated on the first postoperative day. Control CT showed remnant tumor, but there was improvement of the upward herniation and an increase in the size of ventricles (Figs. 3 and 4). Then he was transferred to the ward on the third day. In the ward, his vital signs were within the normal range, the wound was clean, GCS was 15/15, cranial nerves were intact, power was 5/5, but cerebellar signs were positive. The patient was discharged on the eighth postoperative day after tumor resection was done. After 1 month on postop follow-up, his condition was similar to the condition on discharge, and the VPS is still ligated. Discussion

OHCP is very common, with an incidence close to 80% in patients with posterior fossa tumors[4]. In the case that we presented, the patient presented with symptoms attributable to the posterior fossa mass (that includes difficulty in keeping balance, headache, and vomiting) and acute obstructive hydrocephalus (that includes blurring of vision, headache, vomiting, and decreased mentation).

When patients present with acute obstructive hydrocephalus secondary to a posterior fossa mass, initial treatment can be given by inserting a VPS. Placement of VPS results in improvements of the patient’s condition, including the visual complaints and papilledema, and it also makes the dura lax during tumor resection surgery[4]. For our patient, VPS was inserted to alleviate the symptoms of the OHCP, and the patient had clinical improvement for the first 9 days.

Even though pretumor resection CSF diversion has advantages[4], patients can rarely develop clinical deterioration and even death[1,8–10]. The rare causes for this fatal consequence are intra-tumoral hemorrhage and UTTH[6,8–10]. Intra-tumoral hemorrhage after CSF diversion is relatively more commonly seen than UTTH[9]. Ventricular drainage for OHCP secondary to a posterior fossa mass before tumor resection can also result in intra-tumoral hemorrhage and upward herniation at the same time[11].

Different risk factors for upward trans-tentorial herniation include location of the lesion near the upper vermis, when the OHCP is treated by CSF diversion before posterior fossa mass is resected, and when the tentorial incisura is large[1,5]. In our patient, the tumor involves the upper vermis, and the development of the herniation after the placement of a VPS goes in line with the risk factors mentioned in the studies as mentioned earlier.

The possibility of patients developing a UTTH should be suspected if a patient develops any decrement of mentation, change in breathing pattern, or brainstem reflexes[2,4,5,12]. A brain CT scan must then be done; UTTH can be reliably diagnosed by the effacement of the quadrigeminal cistern at the level of the tentorial incisura on the CT scan[13]. UTTH usually occurs within a few hours after CSF diversion procedures[8]. On the contrary, our patient developed this complication 10 days after VPS placement. This makes this case very unique and indicates that the possibility of UTTH is possible even after days following VPS placement.

There are different reasons for patients’ deterioration and death associated with a UTTH. One reason is obliteration of the superior cerebellar arteries (SCA) with resultant infarction, cytotoxic edema and mass effect[3], the second possibility is the compression of the brain stem against clivus which results in loss of crucial functions of the brainstem[1,5] and the third reason is the shift in the position of the great cerebral vein of Galen and basal veins of Rosenthal with a possible impairment of venous drainage and infarction[1,5]. The CT scan of our patient that was done at the moment of deterioration shows obliteration of the pre-pontine cistern (Fig. 1). From this, it is apparent that there is a mass effect on the brainstem that can lead to its dysfunction. The impairment of venous drainage can also be suspected since the quadrigeminal cistern is effaced with resultant collapse of the basal veins. We did not attribute infarction in the SCA territory since there was no hypodensity, and the patient showed significant improvement after treatment.

Different possible treatments can be used for treating patients with UTTH. One treatment is intubating and putting the patient on a mechanical ventilator, which can decrease the risk of aspiration, hypoxic insult to the brain, and, with hyperventilation, the increased intracranial pressure can be addressed[12]. Although the removal of the VPS can be done[2], another less time-consuming procedure is ligating the ventriculoperitoneal shunt, usually at the level of the thorax[12]. Therefore, we did the ligation of the VPS.

The definitive treatment is doing a suboccipital craniotomy and resection of the posterior fossa mass[2,4,12]. For the patient in this case, we did shunt ligation, medical treatment for increased intracranial pressure, and surgical resection of the brain tumor. These treatments were done before permanent injury to the brain occurred, resulting in a very good outcome. Conclusion

In this case report, we demonstrated that a UTTH after VPS placement can occur in a delayed fashion, and whenever a patient for whom CSF diversion is done shows clinical deterioration emergency CT scan must be done to rule out a UTTH. Presence of slit ventricles, relatively open sylvian fissures despite GCS drop, and effacement of quadrigeminal cisterns are findings indicative of a UTTH. A UTTH can occur without the presence of intra-tumoral bleeding. Rapid detection and prompt treatment of post-CSF diversion UTTH can result in a satisfactory outcome.

Strengths and Limitations

This case illustrates a very rare delayed upward herniation following VPS placement. There was a close follow-up of the patient throughout the admission, and that was the foundation for early proper management and rewarding patient outcome. The main limitation was the inaccessibility of magnetic resonance imaging, which would have given a better detailed image. Moreover, it would have been better if the tumor resection surgery had been done using neuromonitoring.

Patient perspective

The family of the child stated that they are grateful for the follow-up and treatment the child received.
