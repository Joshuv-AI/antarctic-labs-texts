Proteomic Profiling Captures Residual Cardiovascular Risk Beyond the PREVENT Model in Individuals With Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic Syndrome Stages 2–3
Authors: Xuefei Han, Jiacheng Ding, Jingqian Li, Yiyin Gao, Yunqian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome reflects complex pathobiological interactions among metabolic disorders, kidney injury, and cardiovascular disease (CVD). Stages 2 and 3 represent critical phases of disease progression characterised by high pathological heterogeneity. This study aimed to develop a CVD protein risk score (PRS) for this population and evaluate its incremental predictive value over the PREVENT model.MethodsThis study included 24 017 participants with CKM Stages 2–3 from the UK Biobank. Using 2923 plasma proteins measured via the Olink platform, a PRS was developed in a training set (n = 19 218) using the LASSO method. In the validation set (n = 4799), the incremental predictive performance of this score over the PREVENT model was assessed using Harrell's C‐statistic, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsA risk score comprising 63 proteins was constructed, primarily reflecting inflammation, kidney injury and matrix remodelling. Key proteins included growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), hepatitis A virus cellular receptor 1 (HAVCR1), matrix metallopeptidase 12 (MMP12) and NT‐proBNP. In the validation set, after adjusting for PREVENT risk factors, individuals in the high PRS group had a 2.56‐fold higher risk of CVD compared to those in the low score group (HR: 2.56, 95% CI: 1.96–3.37). Integrating the score into the PREVENT model improved the C‐statistic by 0.034 (0.672–0.706) and achieved a 10‐year NRI of 15.8% (95% CI: 9.5%–20.9%) and an IDI of 2.2% (95% CI: 1.3%–3.3%).ConclusionCombining the PREVENT model with the PRS developed in this study enhances the prediction of future CVD events in the CKM Stages 2–3 population. This approach facilitates the capture of residual risk and supports precision risk stratification and management for this high‐risk group.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, cohort study, database research, diabetic nephropathy

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Driven by population aging, CVD has emerged as a critical public health challenge. In 2023, the American Heart Association (AHA) formally introduced the concept of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome to address this escalating burden [2]. CKM syndrome is a systemic pathological state that reflects the complex pathophysiological interplay between metabolic dysfunction, renal impairment, and CVD [3]. Specifically, Stages 2 (metabolic risk clustering) and 3 (subclinical organ damage) represent pivotal phases in disease progression, serving as the primary focus for early screening and precision intervention [4].

Developing risk assessment models is essential for achieving precision prevention. Recent years have seen continuous updates to cardiovascular risk prediction frameworks. In 2013, the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCEs) were introduced to assess atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) risk [5]. Compared to the Framingham Risk Score, PCEs incorporated stroke into the composite ASCVD endpoint, expanded the target population, and included diabetes as a key risk factor [6]. However, this model was limited by its race‐specific application and its failure to cover the total CVD burden. To address these gaps, the AHA launched the race‐free PREVENT equations in 2024 to predict total CVD risk (ASCVD and heart failure—HF). Beyond traditional risk factors—such as age, sex, smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes history—the PREVENT equations further incorporate estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) to better capture the specific risks inherent to CKM populations. Developed and validated using data from 46 population‐based cohorts, the PREVENT model demonstrates strong validity and generalisability [7]. While PREVENT has significantly improved risk prediction accuracy, clinical indicators alone may struggle to capture residual CVD risk and the intricate multi‐organ mechanisms present in CKM Stages 2–3 individuals, who exhibit high pathological heterogeneity. Therefore, there is a need to explore complementary approaches to enhance risk stratification for incident CVD in individuals with CKM Stages 2–3.

Advances in proteomic technologies offer new opportunities to bridge these gaps. Plasma proteomics integrates genetic influences with environmental factors, behaviours and comorbidities, playing a vital role in regulating biological processes [8]. The proximity extension assay (PEA) on the Olink platform enables the evaluation of thousands of proteins in large‐scale populations, providing a unique opportunity to construct optimal risk prediction equations. Previous studies have demonstrated the advantages of integrating proteomics with traditional risk factors to predict adverse health outcomes [9, 10, 11]. However, the potential for circulating plasma proteins to provide incremental value beyond the PREVENT model specifically for CVD prediction in CKM Stages 2–3 populations remains unconfirmed.

Utilising data on 2923 proteins from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB‐PPP), this study developed and validated a protein risk score (PRS) specifically for participants in CKM Stages 2–3. Our primary focus was to evaluate the incremental value of this score over the PREVENT model for CVD prediction. As a proof‐of‐concept study, we evaluated the incremental predictive value of this score for CVD beyond a refitted PREVENT‐variable model and explored the potential utility of proteomic profiling for precision risk stratification in this high‐risk population.

Methods

Study Populations

This study was conducted using the UKB, a large‐scale prospective cohort study that recruited over 500 000 participants aged 40–69 years from 22 assessment centres across England, Scotland and Wales between 2006 and 2010. Follow‐up is ongoing. The UKB has collected extensive phenotypic and genotypic information through touchscreen questionnaires, physical examinations and biological sample testing. Detailed information regarding the study design and data collection procedures is available online and in previous publications [12, 13, 14].

The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi‐centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. This specific study was approved by both the National Health Service National Research Service (ref: 11/NW/0382) and the Institutional Review Board of Tulane University (study number: 2018‐1872). The analysis was carried out under the UK Biobank approved application number 629227. Analysis methods and study materials supporting these findings are available from the corresponding author upon request. Due to the sensitive nature of the data, access requires training in human subject confidentiality agreements and an approved application to the UK Biobank.

Initially, 52 995 participants from the UKB‐PPP who underwent plasma proteomic measurements using the antibody‐based Olink Explore 3072 PEA were included. Participants were subsequently excluded based on the following criteria: (i) missing information on CKM syndrome (n = 8840); (ii) participants in CKM Stages 0–1 (n = 14 972); (iii) participants in CKM Stage 4 or those diagnosed with CVD via self‐report or clinical diagnosis prior to baseline (n = 4946) and (iv) individuals with a protein missingness ratio exceeding 80% (n = 42). A total of 24 017 participants in CKM Stages 2–3 were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). Of these, 19 218 (80%) and 4799 (20%) participants were randomly assigned to the training set and validation set, respectively [14].

Definition of CKM Syndrome

CKM syndrome demonstrated interconnected roles of metabolic dysfunction, kidney injury, and cardiovascular abnormalities and is divided into five Stages (0–4):

Stage 0: Individuals without CKM risk factors, including normal BMI, waist circumference, glucose, blood pressure, lipid levels and absence of CKD and CVD.

Stage 1: Presence of any of the following: (1) BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 for Asians or ≥ 25 kg/m2 for other ethnic groups; (2) waist circumference ≥ 80 cm (Asian women), ≥ 90 cm (Asian men), ≥ 88 cm (other women), ≥ 102 cm (other men) and (3) prediabetes defined as HbA1c 5.7%–6.5% or fasting glucose 100–126 mg/dL.

Stage 2: Moderate‐to‐high‐risk CKD (eGFR 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m2) plus metabolic abnormalities, including hypertension, diabetes, triglycerides ≥ 135 mg/dL, or metabolic syndrome (≥ 3 of the following: increased waist circumference, HDL‐C < 40 mg/dL for men/< 50 mg/dL for women, TG > 150 mg/dL, blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mmHg or antihypertensive therapy, or prediabetes).

Stage 3: Individuals with very‐high‐risk CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or predicted 10‐year CVD risk > 20% using the PREVENT model.

Stage 4: Individuals meeting criteria for Stages 0–3 and having established CVD, including HF, atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, stroke or peripheral artery disease. Detailed definitions are provided in Table S1.

Proteomics Measurements

Details regarding the Olink proteomic assay, data processing, and quality control have been previously described in detail. Briefly, the UKB‐PPP selected plasma samples collected from over 50 000 individuals during their baseline assessments. Each participant's sample was collected in an EDTA tube, centrifuged, and subsequently stored at −80°C. Between 2021 and 2022, these plasma samples were analysed at Olink Analytical Services in Sweden using the antibody‐based Olink Explore 3072 PEA.

This analysis identified 2923 distinct proteins across panels covering cardiometabolic, inflammatory, neurologic and oncologic pathways. To ensure high data quality, a rigorous quality control (QC) process was implemented. This process included sample selection, data import, the exclusion of participants who withdrew consent or had unprocessed data, and the detection and removal of outliers. Furthermore, data with QC or detection warnings were excluded, and potential sample swaps were identified and corrected. These strict protocols ensured that intra‐plate and inter‐plate coefficients of variation remained below 20% and 10%, respectively. Protein levels are reported as normalised protein expression (NPX) values, which represent Olink's relative protein quantification unit on a log 2 scale [15, 16].

Calculation of CVD PRS

Twelve proteins with a missingness rate ≥ 20% were excluded from the analysis. The remaining 2911 proteins exhibited varying degrees of missing data (Table S2). To minimise information leakage during the imputation process, we utilised a k‐nearest neighbours (k = 10) approach to fill in missing protein values separately within the training and validation sets. Data from 19 218 participants in the training set were used for feature selection and the estimation of β coefficients (weights), while data from 4799 participants in the validation set were used to evaluate the resulting PRS. To identify proteins associated with CVD, we employed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression models for feature selection. This supervised machine learning method selects variables with the highest predictive value by shrinking the coefficients of less relevant covariates to zero [17]. Within the training set, we constructed a LASSO Cox proportional hazards model encompassing 2911 proteins, with age and sex included as forced covariates. To ensure the stability and reproducibility of the selected biomarkers, we determined the optimal penalty intensity through 10‐fold cross‐validation, which also provided the coefficients for the candidate proteins. The optimal lambda was selected using the lambda.1se criterion, which corresponds to the largest regularisation parameter whose cross‐validated error is within one standard error of the minimum. These coefficients were then used to calculate individual risk scores, excluding the linear predictions for age and sex; the corresponding weights for each protein are provided in Table S3. The risk scores for each participant were calculated as the linear component of the Cox model using the following formula: Coef [Protein 1] × Protein 1 value + Coef [Protein 2] × Protein 2 value + Coef [Protein 3] × Protein 3 value + … + Coef [Protein n] × Protein n value.

Assessment of Study Outcome

The primary outcome of this study was total CVD events, defined as a composite of fatal and non‐fatal ASCVD and HF. Consistent with the PREVENT model, ASCVD included coronary heart disease (CHD; encompassing myocardial infarction and fatal CHD) and stroke. Mortality data, including the date and cause of death, were obtained from death certificates provided by the National Health Service (NHS) Information Centre (England and Wales) and the NHS Central Register Scotland (Scotland). Dates and causes of hospital admissions were identified through record linkage with the Hospital Episode Statistics (England and Wales) and the Scottish Morbidity Records (SMR01) (Scotland).

The specific ICD‐10 codes used for CVD diagnosis are detailed in Table S4. The follow‐up period for each participant was calculated from the date of recruitment until the occurrence of a CVD event, death, loss to follow‐up, or the end of the study follow‐up, whichever occurred first. Diagnostic follow‐up for clinical events was conducted through September 2023, and mortality data were available through July 2024. Details of the study design and data collection procedures for UKB are available online (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

Traditional Risk Factors and PREVENT Risk Model

Traditional risk factors were defined in accordance with the PREVENT model. The background and rationale for developing these modern risk prediction equations were reviewed in detail in the 2023 AHA scientific statement regarding absolute risk assessment for total CVD incorporating CKM health [4]. In the current analyses, we refitted the clinical risk factors from the PREVENT to best fit our cohort data, aiming to improve the performance of traditional risk factors. Variables utilised to calculate PREVENT risk included age, sex, current smoking status, BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, history of diabetes, eGFR, and the use of antihypertensive or statin medications. eGFR was derived using the race‐free 2021 CKD‐EPI equations (the specific equations are presented in Table S5).

Sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medication use—including age, sex, smoking status and antihypertensive therapy—were collected through touchscreen questionnaires and baseline verbal interviews. BMI was measured by trained professionals and calculated using the following formula: BMI = Weight (kg)/Height (m)2. SBP was measured after participants had rested in a seated position for at least 5 min, using digital or manual sphygmomanometers with appropriately sized cuffs. To minimise measurement error, the average of two SBP readings was recorded. A history of diabetes was identified through participant self‐reports and health‐related records linked to ICD‐10 codes. Smoking status was categorised as never smoked, ever smoked, or currently smoked.

Statistical Analysis

We described the baseline characteristics of the overall cohort of participants in CKM Stages 2–3, with additional stratification by training and validation sets to assess their comparability. Baseline characteristics of participants in CKM Stages 2–3 were summarised as means (standard deviations, SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages, %) for categorical variables. The PRS was categorised into tertiles: high risk (top tertile), intermediate risk (middle tertile) and low risk (bottom tertile). We utilised the Kaplan–Meier method to calculate the cumulative incidence of CVD and its subtypes—specifically CHD, stroke and HF—across both the training and validation sets. Differences in cumulative incidence between PRS groups were assessed using the log‐rank test.

To evaluate the association between the PRS and CVD events, including overall CVD, coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and HF, we employed the Fine and Grey subdistribution hazard model to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), treating non‐cardiovascular death as a competing risk. We implemented two levels of adjustment: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, while Model 2 further adjusted for PREVENT risk factors, including age, sex, current smoking, BMI, SBP, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, diabetes history, eGFR and the use of antihypertensive or statin medications. HRs were estimated both per SD increment of the continuous PRS and by tertiles, using the lowest tertile as the reference. Additionally, restricted cubic splines (RCS) nested within Cox models were used to examine the dose–response relationship between the PRS and CVD risk, using the same adjustments as Model 2.

Subsequently, three predictive models were developed: (1) the PREVENT model; (2) a protein‐only model (comprising age, sex and the PRS) and (3) a combined model (PREVENT plus the PRS). The incremental predictive value of the PRS was determined through systematic comparison with the established PREVENT model. Model discrimination was evaluated using Harrell's C‐statistic, and reclassification ability was assessed via the 10‐year net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) using the survIDINRI R package. Additionally, time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) at 10 years were estimated using the time ROC R package to assess model discrimination. Model calibration was evaluated using the 10‐year calibration slope, with values closer to 1 indicating better calibration. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the net clinical benefit of each model across a range of threshold probabilities, providing an estimate of the potential clinical utility of incorporating the PRS into the prediction framework. To evaluate the clinical feasibility of a more parsimonious approach, a simplified risk score was constructed using the five proteins with the largest absolute LASSO coefficients, and its incremental predictive performance over the PREVENT model was further assessed. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which participants classified into CKM Stage 3 solely based on a PREVENT‐predicted 10‐year CVD risk > 20% were excluded, and the incremental predictive value of the PRS was re‐evaluated in the remaining participants. To minimise the potential influence of reverse causation, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants who developed CVD events within the first 2 years of follow‐up. The association between the PRS and CVD outcomes and the incremental predictive value of the PRS over the refitted PREVENT‐variable model were re‐evaluated in the remaining participants using the same analytical approach as the primary analysis.

Finally, proteins identified via LASSO Cox regression as having predictive value for CVD were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using g: Profiler and Enrichr. This analysis was based on Gene Ontology (GO) terms (including Biological Processes, Cellular Components, and Molecular Functions) and KEGG pathways. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied, with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 considered statistically significant. We visualised the 10 most representative pathways for each GO category and Reactome. To explore the relationships between candidate proteins, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database with a confidence score threshold ≥ 0.7. The resulting networks were visualised using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.3).

Results

Participant Characteristics

This study included a total of 24 017 participants, comprising 12 338 (51.4%) males and 11 679 (48.6%) females, with a mean age of 57.6 years (SD = 8.0). Over a median follow‐up of 17.2 years, 3451 incident CVD events were recorded, with 2750 cases occurring in the training set and 701 in the validation set. The baseline characteristics of the study population according to CVD outcome are summarised in Table 1. Individuals who experienced CVD events were generally older and were more likely to be male and current smokers. They also exhibited higher BMI and SBP, lower levels of HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol (TC), and eGFR, and had a higher prevalence of diabetes and use of antihypertensive and statin medications.

Association Between PRS and CVD

Within the training cohort (n = 19 218), 63 proteins were identified from an initial pool of 2911 circulating plasma proteins using the LASSO method to construct the CVD PRS (Table S1). Among these selected biomarkers, 32 proteins were positively associated with CVD risk, while 31 showed a negative association. Of the 10 proteins with the largest absolute coefficients, seven were positively correlated with CVD risk: growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), hepatitis A virus cellular receptor 1 (HAVCR1), matrix metallopeptidase 12 (MMP12), NT‐proBNP, phosphocholine phosphatase 1 (PHOSPHO1), leukocyte immunoglobulin‐like receptor A5 (LILRA5), and RNA binding fox‐1 homologue 3 (RBFOX3). The remaining three proteins among the top 10 were negatively associated with CVD risk, namely brevican (BCAN), ADP‐ribosyl transferase 3 (ART3) and CD34 molecule (CD34).

Compared with the lowest tertile of the CVD PRS, participants in higher tertiles had significantly higher incidence rates of CVD, including CHD, stroke and HF (log‐rank p < 0.001). Consistent results were observed across both the training and validation sets (Figure 1). Table 2 presents the findings from the competing risk models for composite CVD, CHD, stroke and HF associated with the PRS. In the training set, after full adjustment for age, sex, and clinical risk factors, compared with the low‐score group, participants in the intermediate‐score group had a 52% increased risk of CVD (HR: 1.52, 95% CI: 1.33–1.74), while those in the high‐score group showed a 191% increased risk (HR: 2.91, 95% CI: 2.53–3.35). These findings were confirmed in the validation set, where intermediate and high scores were associated with a 32% (HR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02–1.71) and 156% (HR: 2.56, 95% CI: 1.96–3.37) higher risk of CVD, respectively. The PRS remained significantly and positively associated with CVD outcomes after adjusting for age, sex and clinical risk factors. In the training and validation sets, each one SD increment in the PRS was associated with a 73% (HR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.64–1.82) and 61% (HR: 1.61, 95% CI: 1.45–1.78) increase in the risk of CVD events, respectively (Figure 2).

The sensitivity analysis excluding participants who developed CVD events within the first 2 years of follow‐up yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. In the validation set, after full adjustment for risk factors, the high‐score group showed a 168% increased risk of CVD (HR: 2.68, 95% CI: 2.00–3.59), a 154% increased risk of CHD (HR: 2.54, 95% CI: 1.79–3.60), a 124% increased risk of stroke (HR: 2.24, 95% CI: 1.30–3.88), and a 371% increased risk of HF (HR: 4.71, 95% CI: 2.54–8.74) compared with the low‐score group. These findings confirm that the associations between the PRS and CVD outcomes are not materially influenced by reverse causation (Table S7).

PRS Significantly Enhances CVD Prediction Performance in Individuals With CKM Stages 2–3

Results indicated that the combined model, which integrated the PREVENT model and the PRS, exhibited the optimal predictive performance (Table 3). In the training and validation sets, the C‐statistics for the refitted PREVENT model were 0.688 (95% CI: 0.678–0.697) and 0.672 (95% CI: 0.642–0.701), respectively. Notably, the C‐statistic of the standalone PRS model was significantly stronger than that of the refitted PREVENT model, with increases of 0.027 and 0.022 in the training and validation sets, respectively. The addition of the PRS to the PREVENT model further enhanced the C‐statistic (+0.032 in the training set and +0.034 in the validation set). Furthermore, the inclusion of the PRS significantly improved the reclassification capability of the PREVENT model. In the training set, the NRI was 16.7% (95% CI: 14.1%–19.1%) and the IDI was 2.6% (95% CI: 2.1%–3.4%). These results were replicated in the validation set (NRI: 15.8% [95% CI: 9.5%–20.9%]; IDI: 2.2% [95% CI: 1.3%–3.3%]). Finally, although the C‐statistic of the standalone PRS model was nearly comparable to that of the combined model and demonstrated better reclassification in the training set, the NRI improvement in the validation set did not reach statistical significance (NRI: 6.0%, 95% CI: −3.6% to 12.1%). Time‐dependent ROC analysis demonstrated that incorporating the PRS improved the 10‐year AUC of the combined model compared with the refitted PREVENT‐variable model alone, increasing from 0.696 to 0.733 in the training set and from 0.676 to 0.718 in the validation set (Figure S2). Calibration slopes for both models were close to 1.0 (training set: 0.9912 vs. 0.9934; validation set: 0.9672 vs. 0.9677), indicating stable calibration after incorporating the PRS (Table S8). DCA demonstrated that the combined model achieved greater net benefit than the refitted PREVENT‐variable model alone across threshold probabilities of 0%–40%, suggesting the potential clinical decision‐making value of incorporating the PRS (Figure S3).

The simplified 5‐protein PRS also demonstrated meaningful incremental predictive value over the PREVENT model. In the training set, incorporating the simplified 5‐protein PRS into the PREVENT model improved the C‐statistic from 0.688 (95% CI: 0.678–0.697) to 0.708 (95% CI: 0.698–0.717), with an NRI of 12.4% (95% CI: 9.4%–15.2%) and an IDI of 1.7% (95% CI: 1.3%–2.2%). In the validation set, the C‐statistic improved from 0.672 (95% CI: 0.642–0.701) to 0.702 (95% CI: 0.683–0.720), with an NRI of 13.8% (95% CI: 7.1%–17.9%) and an IDI of 2.1% (95% CI: 1.2%–3.3%). Although the predictive performance of the simplified 5‐protein model was marginally lower than that of the full 63‐protein PRS, the results suggest that a substantially reduced protein panel retains clinically meaningful incremental predictive value over the PREVENT model, supporting the potential feasibility of a more streamlined proteomic approach in individuals with CKM Stages 2–3.

The sensitivity analysis results were consistent with the primary analysis. In the training set, the PREVENT model achieved a C‐statistic of 0.674 (95% CI: 0.664–0.684). Incorporating the PRS into the PREVENT model improved the C‐statistic to 0.710 (95% CI: 0.700–0.720), with NRI of 17.7% (95% CI: 14.7%–20.1%) and IDI of 2.5% (95% CI: 2.0%–3.2%). In the validation set, the PREVENT model achieved a C‐statistic of 0.681 (95% CI: 0.661–0.701), which improved to 0.704 (95% CI: 0.684–0.723) after incorporating the PRS, with NRI of 14.7% (95% CI: 8.6%–18.6%) and IDI of 1.9% (95% CI: 1.1%–3.1%). These findings confirm that the incremental predictive value of the PRS over the PREVENT model remains robust after excluding participants whose Stage 3 classification was determined solely by the PREVENT risk threshold (Table S9). Similarly, the sensitivity analysis excluding participants who developed CVD events within the first 2 years of follow‐up yielded consistent results. In the validation set, incorporating the PRS into the refitted PREVENT‐variable model improved the C‐statistic from 0.692 (95% CI: 0.672–0.711) to 0.713 (95% CI: 0.694–0.733), with an NRI of 17.1% (95% CI: 10.0%–21.2%) and an IDI of 2.1% (95% CI: 1.2%–3.1%), confirming that the incremental predictive value of the PRS is not materially affected by reverse causation (Table S10).

In summary, the PRS demonstrated incremental value in enhancing the CVD risk stratification capability of the refitted PREVENT‐variable model in individuals with CKM Stages 2–3, supporting the complementary role of proteomic information alongside conventional risk factor‐based assessment for incident CVD risk in this high‐risk population. The consistency of these proof‐of‐concept findings across both the training and validation sets supports the internal robustness of the results.

Pathway and Network Analysis of CVD PRS Components

The PRS demonstrated exceptional incremental value for CVD prediction in the CKM Stages 2–3 population. To elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying this score, we performed functional enrichment analysis on the 63 proteins constituting the score (Figure 3). GO analysis revealed distinct functional clusters. Molecular Function analysis showed that these proteins are highly enriched in the regulation of signal transduction, including signalling receptor activity, receptor ligand activity, signalling receptor activator/regulator activity, and glycosaminoglycan binding (Figure 3A). Furthermore, Biological Process analysis indicated that these proteins are extensively involved in cell adhesion, immune system processes, inflammatory responses, and cell surface receptor signalling pathways. Notably, the significant enrichment in the regulation of multicellular organismal processes suggests that the score captures the body's integrated coordination in response to metabolic stress (Figure 3B). Cellular Component analysis further confirmed that these proteins are primarily localised in the extracellular region, extracellular space, and extracellular matrix (ECM). Remarkably, the enrichment of extracellular exosomes and extracellular vesicles suggests that these proteins may participate in long‐distance organ crosstalk in CKM syndrome through exosome‐mediated systemic communication (Figure 3C).

Functional enrichment and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of the 63 proteins constituting the CVD protein score. KEGG (A); GO biological processes (B); GO cellular components (C); GO molecular functions (D) and PPI (E).

Most importantly, in‐depth analysis of KEGG pathways captured the hallmark pathological features of CKM syndrome (Figure 3D). First, the enrichment of ‘Regulation of IGF Transport and Uptake by Insulin‐like Growth Factor Binding Proteins (IGFBPs)’ and ‘Metabolism of Amine‐Derived Hormones’ suggests that the score may capture metabolic disturbances, including insulin resistance and hormonal dysregulation, potentially relevant to CKM pathophysiology. Second, the enrichment of the ‘Immune System’, ‘Innate Immune System’, ‘TNFR2 Non‐Canonical NF‐kB Pathway’ and ‘TNFs Bind Their Physiological Receptors’ pathways suggests that chronic low‐grade inflammation involving the TNF family may represent a potential biological mechanism underlying vascular endothelial injury in this population. Additionally, the enrichment of post‐translational protein phosphorylation and modification indicates that the score encompasses signalling proteins in highly active regulatory states rather than just structural proteins. Finally, the discovery of the ‘Reversible Hydration of Carbon Dioxide’ pathway may hint at potential pathological links involving renal acid–base regulation and vascular tone.

Finally, PPI network analysis further revealed the interactions between these proteins (Figure 3E). The results identified IL‐6 as a central hub, forming a radial linkage pattern with ANGPT2, CD34, CCL7, MMP12, PLAUR and TNFRSF. This network integrates three critical dimensions: systemic inflammation (mediated by CCL7 and TNFRSF), endothelial dysfunction and angiogenesis (ANGPT2 and CD34), and matrix remodelling and tissue damage (MMP12 and PLAUR).

In summary, these functional enrichment and PPI results suggest that the proteins comprising the PRS are enriched in pathways related to a putative ‘inflammation‐endothelial injury‐matrix remodelling’ molecular axis, which may be biologically relevant in the high‐risk context of CKM Stages 2–3.

This finding provides a mechanistic rationale for the ability of the PRS to capture residual CVD risk beyond the refitted PREVENT model in the complex high‐risk population with CKM Stages 2–3, further supporting the proof‐of‐concept value of proteomic information as a complement to conventional risk factor‐based models.

Discussion

Based on large‐scale proteomics, this study developed and validated a PRS composed of 63 plasma proteins in a high‐risk population of CKM Stages 2–3. Our primary findings demonstrate that the PRS exhibited significant and consistent incremental predictive value beyond conventional clinical risk factors. Compared with the conventional clinical risk factor model alone, the combined model achieved a C‐statistic improvement of 0.034 and an NRI of 15.8% in the validation set. These results highlight the complementary nature of protein biomarkers and traditional clinical risk factors, providing robust evidence that proteomics can capture residual risk beyond traditional clinical indicators—particularly during the critical period of pathological evolution in CKM Stages 2–3.

The concept of CKM syndrome, proposed by the AHA, reflects the complex pathophysiological interplay between metabolic disorders, kidney injury and CVD, emphasising the major role the CVD burden plays in the poor health outcomes, premature mortality and multi‐organ diseases associated with CKM [3]. To further define CKM staging and substantially reduce the risk of CVD progression in early‐stage CKM populations, the AHA developed the PREVENT equations [7]. These equations optimise CVD risk prediction performance for CKM populations by adding eGFR to previous traditional clinical risk equations. However, as previously mentioned, the pathogenesis of CVD is multifactorial. Traditional clinical risk factors alone cannot fully capture the complex biological mechanisms of CVD, especially in CKM Stages 2–3 populations characterised by high pathological heterogeneity. As direct executors of biological activity, plasma proteins not only reflect genetic susceptibility but also dynamically integrate the comprehensive effects of environmental exposure, lifestyle behaviours, and multi‐organ damage. This provides new insights for capturing subclinical mechanisms and strengthening CVD prediction.

Numerous prior studies have evaluated the predictive power of single or small sets of biomarkers for CVD or its component events [18, 19]. However, these individual biomarkers offer limited improvement in outcome discrimination. Recent research has demonstrated the potential of integrating large‐scale proteomics into cardiovascular risk prediction. For instance, studies based on the EPIC‐Norfolk cohort showed that integrating 50 proteins into clinical models improved the AUC for 3‐year MACE prediction by 0.071 [20]. Patrick et al. also integrated 114 CVD‐related proteins into the SCORE2 model alongside traditional risk factors, which significantly enhanced the C‐index (+0.031) and the NRI (+0.140) [9]. Additionally, a study also utilising UKB data reported that plasma proteomics can improve the prediction of various CVDs, including major MACE, ASCVD, myocardial infarction, aortic stenosis and HF [21]. While we acknowledge the prominent contributions of these studies to CVD risk prediction using plasma protein biomarkers, two major limitations remain. First, these studies identified a large number of proteins without constructing a composite PRS, lacking an integrated risk assessment framework for quantifying individual risk. Second, these studies were conducted in general populations, without specifically exploring the predictive value of CVD‐related proteins in the high‐risk subgroup of CKM Stages 2–3, which is characterised by high pathological heterogeneity. Given the significant cost of proteomic testing, applying it to the general population may increase the healthcare burden.

Research by Frederick's team showed that among the top 10 proteins with the greatest cardiovascular risk predictive value, ART3 was negatively correlated with outcomes and had the largest absolute coefficient. Our results similarly confirmed a negative correlation between ART3 and CVD risk, though it ranked eighth in predictive weight. More importantly, there was very little overlap between the proteins identified in the two studies. Of the 10 proteins with the highest predictive weights in their study, only two—ART3 and the widely recognised NT‐proBNP—overlapped with our results. This significant difference strongly suggests that the CVD risk in the CKM Stages 2–3 population is driven by a unique protein molecular network distinct from that of the general population. Protein biomarkers screened in the general population may fail to accurately capture the specific risk drivers of this group. This underscores the importance of identifying disease‐specific biomarkers in targeted clinical contexts, as general‐population‐derived risk scores may not accurately capture the specific risk signals driving CVD in this high‐risk subgroup. To develop a CVD risk prediction model more applicable to CKM populations, this proof‐of‐concept study constructed a comprehensive PRS in a CKM Stages 2–3 cohort, fitting multi‐dimensional molecular information into a single quantitative indicator. We quantified the model's discriminatory ability using the C‐statistic, NRI and IDI. This not only significantly improves the precision of risk stratification but also facilitates an intuitive clinical presentation of an individual's full risk profile, offering stronger translational potential.

Our results indicate that, compared to the low‐risk group (the lowest tertile of the PRS), the CVD risk for the high‐risk group (the highest tertile) increased by 191% and 156% in the training and validation sets, respectively. Furthermore, a model containing only age, sex and the PRS outperformed the refitted traditional clinical risk factors model and demonstrated a C‐statistic nearly equivalent to the model integrating both traditional clinical risk factors model and the PRS. Further incorporating conventional clinical risk factors significantly improved the NRI by 16.7% and 15.8% in the training and validation sets, respectively. These results show that both traditional risk factors and the PRS are indispensable for CVD risk prediction. However, obtaining traditional risk factors requires numerous professional medical staff, and the process of verbal interviews and recall is prone to bias and subjectivity. The advantages of proteomics—such as ease of sampling and high standardisation—can effectively correct errors in clinical risk factor collection, suggesting that proteomics alone has the potential to match or even replace traditional risk factors.

Functional enrichment and PPI network analyses provided biological plausibility for the ability of the PRS to capture residual CVD risk in individuals with CKM Stages 2–3. GO analysis suggested that the constituent proteins are predominantly localised in the extracellular region and matrix, with potential involvement in exosome‐mediated inter‐organ communication, which may reflect the systemic multi‐organ crosstalk characteristic of CKM syndrome. At the pathway level, KEGG analysis identified enrichment in IGF signalling and amine‐derived hormone metabolism pathways, suggesting that the PRS may capture metabolic disturbances including insulin resistance and hormonal dysregulation that are not reflected by conventional clinical risk factors. The enrichment of innate immune pathways, including the TNFR2 non‐canonical NF‐κB pathway and TNF receptor signalling, further supports the potential relevance of chronic low‐grade inflammation to vascular endothelial injury in this population. Notably, the enrichment of post‐translational protein phosphorylation pathways indicates that the score encompasses proteins in active signalling regulatory states, potentially reflecting dynamic pathological processes beyond static structural changes.

Specifically, the 10 proteins with the largest absolute coefficients in the PRS cover multiple stages of CKM progression. Among them, seven proteins were positively correlated with CVD outcomes, and three were negatively correlated. GDF15, the protein with the largest positive coefficient, along with NT‐proBNP, underscores the chronic inflammation and haemodynamic stress driven by metabolic disorders [22, 23, 24]. It should be noted that current evidence does not support a direct causal relationship between GDF15 or NT‐proBNP and CVD; their elevated levels more likely reflect the pathological state of cardiac dysfunction. Therefore, within the current PRS, GDF15 and NT‐proBNP primarily function as reflective biomarkers of disease state rather than direct causal targets. HAVCR1 (KIM‐1), as a sensitive marker of tubular injury, reflects subclinical CKD pathological changes when overexpressed. Since this early renal injury often precedes changes in eGFR, it highlights the central role of the cardio‐renal axis in CKM Stages 2–3 [25]. MMP12, a macrophage elastase, suggests plaque instability caused by extracellular matrix remodelling. Additionally [26], MMP12 promotes platelet aggregation and adhesion, further facilitating thrombus formation after plaque rupture [27]. As a phosphatase, PHOSPHO1 expression increases in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) under calcifying conditions, indicating its key role in the early stages of vascular mineral deposition [28, 29]. LILRA5 is an activating innate immune receptor primarily expressed on monocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages, emphasising the critical role of the immune system [30]. RBFOX1 is a key RNA‐binding protein that regulates calcium signalling and alternative splicing in cardiomyocytes, playing an essential role in maintaining normal cardiac function [31]. Notably, we identified a negative correlation between BCAN and CVD for the first time. Previous studies have linked BCAN to the brain's extracellular matrix [32, 33]. However, direct evidence for their causal role in CVD remains lacking. We speculate that BCAN reflects extracellular matrix stability, and its decreased expression may mark the destruction of vascular endothelial structures leading to decreased vascular compliance. However, this association warrants further mechanistic investigation. Furthermore, the negative correlations of CD34 and ART3 with CVD risk suggest that a decline in endothelial repair capacity and the disruption of matrix stability together form the pathological background for increased susceptibility to CVD [34, 35].

Finally, PPI analysis emphasises the key role of IL‐6. As a central cell cytokine of innate immunity, IL‐6 performs a wide range of physiological functions related to host defence, immune cell regulation, proliferation and differentiation [36]. As a network hub, IL‐6 may drive a cascade of downstream factors such as ANGPT2, MMP12 and CD34, suggesting a CVD risk driven by the ‘inflammation‐endothelial injury‐matrix remodelling’ core axis, which together constitutes the complex molecular regulatory network of CKM. These findings provide biological plausibility for the ability of the PRS to capture complex residual CVD risk in individuals with CKM Stages 2–3.

Although this study developed a CVD PRS for the high‐risk population with CKM Stages 2–3 and provided proof‐of‐concept evidence that this score may offer incremental discriminative value beyond a conventional clinical risk factor model, several limitations remain. First, although multiple covariates were adjusted for and conclusions were drawn from long‐term prospective follow‐up, a causal relationship between the constituent proteins and CVD cannot be established. Associative analyses of plasma protein concentrations alone cannot distinguish causal targets driving disease progression from bystander proteins accompanying disease development. The plasma proteins comprising the PRS in this study primarily function as reflective biomarkers of disease state rather than direct causal targets, although this does not compromise the predictive performance of the score. Secondly, the GO/KEGG enrichment analysis and PPI network construction conducted in this study were mainly used to verify the biological rationale of the screened proteins and belong to descriptive and correlational analyses. Second, missing data for certain proteins or traditional risk factors were inevitable, though we filled these values using k‐nearest neighbours and multiple imputation (except for those with high missingness ratios). Fourth, despite rigorous internal validation, the model's generalisability has not yet been validated in a completely independent external cohort due to the current lack of other public cohorts with large‐scale Olink proteomics data and high testing costs. This means that the current findings are primarily applicable to populations in the United Kingdom and other Western European populations with similar cardiovascular risk profiles. Future calibration and validation in more diverse ethnic groups and populations are warranted to evaluate the generalisability and clinical translation potential of this score. Finally, we screened proteins for the score based only on baseline measurements; further research is required to evaluate whether fluctuations in protein expression can further improve CVD risk stratification.

Conclusions

In summary, this proof‐of‐concept study developed a PRS in high‐risk individuals with CKM Stages 2–3 and demonstrated its incremental discriminative value over traditional clinical risk factor models. These findings support the potential value of proteomics as a supplement to traditional risk factor models, providing a new exploratory direction for CVD risk stratification in this high‐risk population.
