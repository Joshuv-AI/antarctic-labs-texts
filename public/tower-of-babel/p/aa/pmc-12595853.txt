Does ulnar variance relate with TFCC lesions?
Authors: Yusuf Can, Banu Karaalioğlu, Mehmet Kürşat Yılmaz, Kadir Uzel, Adnan Kara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to investigate triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) alterations and accompanying ligamentous pathologies in cases with positive (PUV) and negative ulnar variance (NUV) using MRI, and to compare findings with a control group presenting with ulnar-sided wrist pain but no trauma history.Materials and methodsA total of 124 patients were retrospectively evaluated: groups with radiographically confirmed PUV or NUV and a control group with ulnar-sided wrist pain but no trauma history. MRI examinations of the wrist were assessed by two musculoskeletal radiologists. Evaluated parameters included TFCC morphology and integrity, disc thickness and angle, presence and location of tears, integrity of associated ligamentous structures, degenerative findings in ulnar carpal bones, and distal radioulnar joint changes.ResultsTFCC tears were observed in 91% of PUV cases, 63% of NUV cases, and 40% of the control group. Central disc perforation rates were 40% in PUV, 10% in NUV, and 10% in controls (P < 0.001). Volar radioulnar ligament (VRUL) and dorsal radioulnar ligament (DRUL) tears were significantly more common in PUV cases (VRUL: 26%, DRUL: 44%) compared to NUV (VRUL: 5%, DRUL: 29%) and controls (VRUL: 0%, DRUL: 7%). Ulnar-sided attachment tears were more frequent in NUV, though not statistically significant (P = 0.062). Ulnocarpal chondromalacia (Palmer 2B) was present in 16% of PUV cases, and degenerative arthritis in ulnolunate and ulnotriquetral joints (Palmer 2E) in 42%, compared to only 5% in NUV and controls (P < 0.001). Ulnotriquetral ligament tears were significantly higher in PUV and frequently accompanied VRUL tears (P = 0.007). Chronic ECU tendon tears were more common in both PUV and NUV groups versus controls (P = 0.002).ConclusionMultiple compartment TFCC tears are frequently observed in PUV and NUV cases. PUV is significantly associated with disc thinning, central perforation, degeneration, and ulnocarpal joint changes. VRUL and DRUL tears, which are not part of the traditional Palmer classification, are notably common in PUV. Conversely, ulnar attachment tears are more frequently noted in NUV cases.

Does ulnar variance relate with TFCC tears?

Background

The term ulnar variance refers to the relative lengths of the distal articular surfaces of the radius and ulna [1]. Palmer and Werner described the best method how determine ulnar variance [2]. This variance is related with some ulnar pathologies [3]. There is studies reported the association of negative ulnar variance and Kienböck’s disease, avascular necrosis and scapholunate pathologies [4–7]. On the other hand Triangular fibrocartilaginous complex (TFCC) tears and ulnar impaction syndrome have a relationship with positive ulnar variance [1, 3, 5, 7, 8]. The pathologic changes that appear in ulnar impaction syndrome most commonly occur with positive ulnar variance but can occasionally occur with neutral or negative ulnar variance [9, 10]. Palmer designed a classification system for TFCC complex lesions based on mechanism, location, and involved structures (Table 1) [11]. This classification system is helpful in determining the mechanism of injury and directing clinical management.

TFCC is supposed to be main stabilizing structure of the distal radioulnar joint [12]. It is a fibrocartilage-ligamentous complex inserted between ulnar head, lunate and triquetrum bones. TFCC functions as a cushion for ulnar carpus and stabilize it [13]. Palmer and Werner showed positive ulnar variance results in an ulnocarpal compressive load, and it has been indicated with degenerative TFCC tears and chondromalacia of bones [11]. Conversely in negative ulnar variance most of the force converted to tension and stretch of disk proper [13]. Due to these anatomical features, TFCC injury is closely related to ulnar variance.

TFCC tear is one of the most common causes of ulnar-sided wrist pain. Plain radiographs are essential for initial evaluation and for measuring ulnar variance. However, MRI is frequently used to confirm the diagnosis of TFCC injury. High-resolution MRI provides clear visualization of the TFCC and carpal cartilage [13]. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between TFCC tear and ulnar variance in patients who underwent MRI due to ulnar-sided wrist pain.

Although previous studies have reported associations between ulnar variance and TFCC pathology, most investigations have focused on positive ulnar variance and its link with degenerative changes and ulnar impaction syndrome [14–18]. However, less is known about the specific spectrum of TFCC lesions in both positive and negative ulnar variance, particularly with respect to ligamentous injuries not fully captured by the Palmer classification. Clarifying these associations is clinically relevant, as patterns of TFCC injury may vary according to ulnar variance and could influence both diagnostic interpretation and therapeutic decision-making in patients presenting with ulnar-sided wrist pain. The present study therefore aimed to evaluate TFCC morphology, tear location, and associated ligamentous abnormalities on MRI in patients with positive and negative ulnar variance, compared with a control group.

Materials and methods

For the study, patient data were collected after obtaining approval from the institutional clinical research ethics committee. All patients were informed about the purpose of the study in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written/oral consent was obtained for their participation.

Between the years 2022 and 2025, 124 patients admitted with a history of ulnar sided wrist pain were enrolled in the study. Patients with a history of trauma or surgery, or lacking a wrist radiographic image, were excluded from the study. Ulnar variance was assessed on standard posteroanterior wrist radiographs obtained in a neutral forearm position. Patients categorized into three case groups consisting of 43 positive ulnar variance (PUV), 41 negative ulnar variance (NUV) and 40 controls (44 males and 80 females, with a mean age of 37.3 ± 10 years). Patients older than 55 years were not included, as degenerative TFCC tears become increasingly prevalent with age; therefore, degenerative changes were evaluated in relation to ulnar variance rather than age. The control group consisted of patients referred for MRI due to ulnar-sided wrist pain, with conditions such as ganglion cysts or carpal tunnel syndrome, but without any history of trauma. Their ulnar variance was measured and classified as neutral. Patient characteristics shown in Table 2.

The ulnar variance was quantified on plain radiographs. All MR images were obtained with a 3 T MAGNETOM Vida (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with manufacturer provided coils. Coronal T2 weighted images (T2 WI) (TR/TE/flip angle = 2500- 29–150, a 3 mm slice thickness, a 110 mm field of view, an imaging matrix of 230 × 288 using zero-fill interpolation, 252 kHz receiver bandwidth, and three or four excitations) were used for analysis. The MR images of the patients were independently evaluated by two radiologists in a blinded fashion. To ensure reliability within this framework, each radiologist conducted repeated assessments for intraobserver agreement, and interobserver agreement was quantified using established statistical methods. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. On imaging, the morphological changes of the TFCC complex, including the integrity of each component, were evaluated to determine the presence of degeneration and tear. The term “tear” was applied based on the following MRI criteria:

On MRI, ulnar variance was assessed by measuring the difference between the cartilage surface level of the lunate fossa and that of the ulnar head (Fig. 1a). The TFCC angle was defined as the angle formed between the long axis of the TFCC and these reference lines (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, the structural integrity and associated pathologies of nearby ligamentous structures were also examined in the same manner. Furthermore, degenerative changes in the ulnocarpal and radioulnar joints were examined, and the results were compared with those of a control group.

Statistical analysis

A chi-square test was conducted to compare the frequency differences among three independent groups (PUV, NUV, and normal variance) concerning various TFCC pathologies both generally and at specific sites. Cramer’s V test was employed to assess the magnitude of these. The mean TFCC angle and disc thickness, and their intergroup relationships, were analyzed using ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons.

Results

The mean ulnar variance on the MRIs of all subjects was + 0.24 mm with a range of −6.01 to + 6,31 mm. Mean variance in PUV was + 4.21 (± 1.52) mm and − 3.91 (± 1.38) mm in NUV. TFCC disc thickness was negative correlated with ulnar variance. Disc proper was thicker in NUV groups and thinner in PUV groups, in comparison to the control group (Fig. 1). The average TFCC angle was 26.4° ± 12.65 (ranging from − 3° to + 54°), and the mean TFCC thickness measured 1.22 ± 0.64 mm (range: 0.30 to 3.30 mm). A strong positive correlation was identified between ulnar variance and TFCC angle (r = 0.836) (Fig. 2), whereas a negative correlation was found between ulnar variance and TFCC thickness (r = − 0.742) (Fig. 3). TFCC thickness showed an almost linear decrease when ulnar variance was less than + 1.1 mm. Beyond this threshold, the thickness tended to plateau, likely due to the limited cartilage space between the lunate and the ulnar head. The mean disc thickness and angles were compared across groups, and significant differences were found for both parameters. The mean TFCC thickness in the NUV group was significantly greater than in both the PUV and control groups, and the control group showed a significantly higher mean thickness than the PUV group. Regarding the TFCC angle, the mean value in the PUV group was significantly higher than in both the NUV and control groups, while the control group had a significantly higher mean angle than the NUV group.

Ulnar variance and TFCC tears showed a strong positive correlation. In the PUV group, 91% of cases showed at least one compartment tear of the complex. This rate was 63% in the NUV and 40% in the control group (Fig. 6). Comparison of TFCC accompanied pathologies between the groups showed that disc perforation frequencies (Palmer 2 C) was significantly high in PUV cases than the NUV and control groups, the rates were 40%, 10% vs. 10%, subsequently (P < 0.001). In order to gain a comprehensive understanding of the related pathologies of TFFC complex, each individual component evaluated in isolation. The results demonstrated that volar radio-ulnar ligament (VRUL) and dorsal radio-ulnar ligament (DRUL) tears frequently co-exist with PUV, with a rate of 26% for VRUL tears and 44% for DRUL tears (Fig. 7). The prevalence of these injuries was significantly lower in the NUV and control groups (VRUL: 26%, 5% vs. 0%, respectively, P < 0.001; DRUL: 44%, 29% vs. 7%, P:0.001). In contrast, the frequency of ulnar-sided tears was higher in NUV cases than in the others, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P:0.062).

Variance associated degenerative changes were detected with greater ratio in PUV’s than in NUV and control group. The prevalence of ulnar carpal chondromalacia, classified as Palmer 2B, was observed in 16% of the PUV group, while degenerative arthritic changes of the Ulnolunate and Ulnotriquetral joints (Palmer 2E) were detected in 42%. The frequency of Palmer 2B and 2E accompaniment was reported at 5% for each and was found to be the same among the NUV and control groups. (P < 0.001).

A notable prevalence of ulnotriquetral ligament tears was observed among cases of PUV in comparison to other groups. It was assumed that these injuries probably occur in conjunction with VRUL tears (P:0.007). A substantial number of cases in the PUV and NUV groups demonstrated a chronic tear of the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon, in comparison to the controls (PUV 26% (11/43), NUV 27% (11/41), P: 0.002). Discussion

Evaluating ulnar variance is important in unexplained ulnar-sided wrist pains. Ulnar variance can be affected by daily activities that involve repetitive forearm rotation and gripping [13]. Published studies showed the mean ulnar variance ranges from − 0.46 mm to + 0.31 mm and differs by ethnicity, gender, and age [19]. In our study the mean ulnar variance was + 0.24 mm.

Ulnar variance and TFCC thickness showed a negative correlation as reported previous studies [13, 20]. It has been observed that degenerative thinning of the disc, central perforation and also degenerative changes in the ulno-carpal joint are more prevalent among individuals with PUV. Volar and dorsal radioulnar ligament tears are not included in the Palmer classification system. However, tears of these ligaments have been observed in an innegliable number of variances, especially in PUV. These ligaments strengthen the disc by aligning the anterior and posterior edges and also exhibits a stabilizing effect. A positive variance causes stretching of these ligaments, which increases their susceptibility to injury by exposing them to repeated trauma during movement. These findings support that positive ulnar variance is a predisposing factor for TFCC disc injury.

We acknowledge that the relationship between TFCC morphology (thickness and angle) and ulnar variance was initially described by Palmer and Werner [11, 21]. Also Zhan et al. investigated the relationships between age, gender, ulnar variance, and the triangular fibrocartilage disc using high-resolution MRI in asymptomatic adults [18]. Our intention was not to introduce these parameters as novel findings but rather to confirm their reproducibility using MRI in a contemporary cohort and to highlight their potential role in everyday imaging practice. Although these measurements may not directly alter the clinical management of TFCC injuries, they contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of TFCC anatomy and its variations. Moreover, by systematically assessing TFCC thickness and angle in relation to ulnar variance, our study reinforces prior observations and provides additional imaging-based evidence that may aid radiologists in recognizing predisposition factors for TFCC pathology.

The prevalence of disc injuries among the variances showed that patients with PUV have higher TFCC injury rates especially more advanced with involvement of more than one component comparing to control group and NUV. It has been postulated that TFCC abnormalities frequently accompanies positive and neutral variance [11, 13]. In this regard, our findings align with those of previous studies, which have highlighted the role of PUVs as a predisposing factor for TFCC disc injury [1, 6, 8, 9, 13]. However, in addition to previous knowledge that disc degeneration with thinning and central perforation are frequently observed in PUV cases, it is important to note that other components of the TFCC were also quite prone to injury in variances. Furthermore, our results revealed that more than one compartmental injury is seen in a significant proportion of NUV and PUV groups than in normal variances which should challenge the management further. Although it is acknowledged that TFFC injury patterns differ between variances, detailed analyses being revealed in this study has not been reported to date. It is apparent that sensitivity of the distinct compartments of the complex to injury exhibited notable difference among variances. Ulnar lengthening in PUV exposes a stretching force on VRUL and DRUL, hence it is anticipated that these ligaments exhibit a heightened vulnerability to injury even during physiological movements. Conversely, ulnar shortening exerts a stretching force on the ulnar attachments of the complex, thereby increasing their susceptibility to injury and accounting for the significantly higher frequency of ulnar-sided tears in NUV cases.

TFCC injuries have been less frequently observed in NUV cases [11]. In their study on NUV, Yoshioka et al. found no evidence of advanced disk injury, in contrast to the results of this study which showed that 63% of NUV cases exhibited at least one compartmental tear [13]. Furthermore, NUV patients were more susceptible to ulnar-sided tears (tears of the foveal and styloid attachment) than PUV patients. The significant finding of the study is the potential association between negative ulnar variance and ulnar-sided tears of the TFCC attachment. This relationship may provide useful diagnostic information and could influence therapeutic decision-making, particularly in patients presenting with ulnar-sided wrist pain.

A limitation of our study is that MR arthrography (MRA), which has been reported to provide higher sensitivity for detecting subtle or central TFCC tears, was not performed. We relied solely on conventional MRI, which, although non-invasive and widely available, may miss some small or equivocal lesions. It should also be noted that in clinical practice, MRA is generally reserved for selected cases or when detailed assessment is required at 1.5T, whereas high-resolution 3 T MRI has been considered comparable to MRA in most situations [22, 23]. This context should be kept in mind when interpreting our findings.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, a key limitation of this study is the absence of arthroscopic confirmation, which is considered the gold standard for evaluating triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) lesions. The potential for overlap between degeneration, partial tearing, and rupture, emphasizing this as a limitation of MRI-based assessment. As this was a retrospective MRI-based study, arthroscopic correlation was not feasible for all cases. Therefore, the diagnostic conclusions should be interpreted with caution, although MRI remains the most widely used non-invasive tool in clinical practice. Despite this limitation, we believe our study highlights the relation ulnar variance and TFCC abnormalities. Second limitation is the number of patients with TFCC injury and healthy volunteers was limited. Finally it was a retrospective study and there was no follow-up. Future studies with larger sample size and more balanced cohorts are warranted.

Conclusion

The incidence of TFCC complex multiple compartment tears is quite high in ulnar variance. Ulnar variance is an important determinant of disc thickness. In PUV, disc thinning, degeneration and central perforation in the disc and ulno-carpal degenerative changes usually accompanies. VRUL and DRUL tears which are not included in the Palmer classification frequently complicate the process. In NUV, ulnar attachment tears were relatively common. This finding has potential diagnostic value and may inform therapeutic strategies in ulnar-sided wrist pain.
