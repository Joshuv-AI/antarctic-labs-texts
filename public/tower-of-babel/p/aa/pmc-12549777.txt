Anaerobic bio-reduction ignites persistent photothermal conversion in misaligned porphyrin supramolecular assembly for infected wound regeneration
Authors: Xuejiao Wang, Qiyang Cai, Yingjie Dai, Jinxuan Wu, Zhi Su, Xiangyu Wei, Yaqing Zhao, Jianbin Ye, Wenbin Zhang, Yuelu Fan, Hanlin Huang, Shouan Wang, Zheyuan Liu, Hu Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To address the dual challenges of antibiotic resistance and microbial dysbiosis caused by non-selective photothermal therapy, we engineered an intelligent nanosystem (TPPS4-Ca) achieving specific recognition and localized photothermal eradication by pathogens. The innovative out-of-plane coordination between Ca2+ and anionic porphyrin (TPPS4) constructs redox-responsive self-assemblies, which not only suppresses the common self-quenching effect of porphyrin aggregation but also significantly enhances the electrostatic targeting toward negatively charged bacteria. Moreover, within the hypoxic microenvironment of facultative anaerobes, TPPS4-Ca undergoes selective reduction to phlorin/chlorin species with exceptional near-infrared (NIR) photothermal conversion efficiency and operational stability (negligible performance decay over 30 days). The system demonstrates strain selectivity, showing 99.9 % inhibition against facultative anaerobes while exhibiting negligible activity toward commensal aerobes. In rat-infected wound models, TPPS4-Ca enables precise clearance of Escherichia coli (E. coli) through localized photothermia therapy while accelerating wound closure. Comprehensive biosafety assessments confirm high biocompatibility at cellular and systemic levels. This molecular assembly strategy establishes a promising pathway for developing bacteria-responsive selective photothermal therapeutics with enhanced long-cycling performance.

Introduction

Global mortality directly attributable to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) reached 4.71 million in 2021, surpassing that of HIV/AIDS to become a leading public health threat [[1], [2], [3]]. This bacterial resistance crisis poses formidable challenges to conventional antibiotic therapies, driving the exploration of antibiotic-independent alternatives. Photothermal antibacterial therapy (PTT) has gained significant attention due to its physical mechanism of rapidly disrupting bacterial membrane structures through localized hyperthermia and low propensity for resistance induction [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. However, most existing photothermal materials lack strain selectivity. Their indiscriminate bactericidal mechanisms irreversibly disrupt commensal microbiota while eliminating pathogens, triggering microenvironmental dysregulation at infection sites [8,9]. This further leads to systemic pathologies including breakdown of immune homeostasis and cytokine storms, severely compromising patient prognosis [10,11]. Achieving precise pathogen recognition and controlled elimination has emerged as a critical scientific challenge for next-generation intelligent antimicrobial systems.

In recent years, intelligent antimicrobial strategies based on bacterial microenvironment-responsive materials have offered new insights for precise regulation of antimicrobial activity compared to externally triggered systems [[12], [13], [14]]. Specifically, conventional external triggering systems (e.g., light, ultrasound, or magnetic fields), while enabling spatiotemporal control, suffer from inherent limitations: (1)limited tissue penetration depth (e.g., UV/visible light), which severely restricts their therapeutic efficacy in deep-seated tissues [15]; (2) lack of intrinsic targeting, potentially causing non-specific damage (e.g., thermal damage) to healthy tissues along the activation path [16]; In contrast, functional materials engineered to be activated specifically by pathogen-derived metabolites (e.g., H2O2, H2S) [[17], [18], [19], [20]] or microenvironmental signatures (e.g., acidic pH, low redox potential) [[21], [22], [23]], offer the paramount advantage of intrinsic targeting and autonomy.These materials are activated only upon encountering the specific “signals” created by pathogens, thereby confining the antimicrobial effect strictly to the infection site. Redox-responsive systems targeting the low redox potential of facultative anaerobes have garnered significant interest. These bacteria are capable of directly transferring electrons to extracellular acceptors (such as iron or manganese oxides, or specific organic molecules) during the terminal step of their respiratory chain. This unique process, known as Extracellular Electron Transfer (EET), not only enables the bacteria to complete their energy metabolism but also generates a transmembrane redox potential difference (Eh), thereby exhibiting a reducibility [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Heterogeneity in electron transport chain composition and environmental adaptability across bacterial species results in marked differences in their reductive capacities [28]. Capitalizing on this differential, redox-responsive materials designed for targeted activation under anaerobic conditions establish a novel targeting mechanism for selective antimicrobial behavior.

Supramolecular host-guest recognition systems have attracted considerable attention for their programmable stimulus responsiveness [29], and have demonstrated substantial potential in reductive antibacterial therapies. For instance, perylene diimides, naphthalene diimides, and related compounds have been used to form supramolecular complexes with cucurbiturils (CB[n]). Facultative anaerobes can reduce these complexes to anionic radicals that absorb near-infrared (NIR) light, enabling photothermal therapy in situ within hypoxic niches [21,28,[30], [31], [32]]. Further research has revealed that porphyrins can be anaerobically reduced to phlorin and chlorin derivatives, which exhibit markedly enhanced NIR photothermal conversion efficiencies for localized hyperthermia [32,33]. Crucially, porphyrins retain aerobic photodynamic activity. Such dual-responsive properties provide a novel paradigm for environment-adaptive antimicrobials. However, current studies predominantly focus on small molecules or simple binary/ternary host-guest complexes, which still suffer from rapid metabolic clearance and limited targeting efficiency [34,35]. Constructing bacteria-responsive supramolecular assemblies with prolonged circulation properties thus emerges as a critical frontier for overcoming existing technological barriers [36]. In fact, the conjugated structure of porphyrin macrocycles and their peripheral functional groups (anionic/cationic derivatives), provide versatile self-assembly pathways [[37], [38], [39]]. While cationic porphyrins can target bacterial surfaces via electrostatic interactions, their inherent cytotoxicity necessitates complex structural optimization to balance antimicrobial activity and biosafety [32,33]. Conversely, anionic porphyrins exhibit intrinsically lower cytotoxicity but suffer from weak electrostatic repulsion with negatively charged bacterial membranes, fundamentally limiting their applicability [40]. Crucially, coordination of metal ions to pyrrolic nitrogen atoms enables charge modulation that enhances the interactions between materials and bacteria [41,42]. Moreover, metal coordinated assembly governs molecular packing patterns, circumventing aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) typical of conventional porphyrin aggregates [[43], [44], [45]]. Thus, the coordination approach facilitates rational design of stable antimicrobial architectures with enhanced activity and operational safety.

Herein, we propose a metal coordination-mediated self-assembly strategy for anionic porphyrins (Scheme 1), constructing intelligent nanoassemblies through specific Ca2+ coordination with tetrakis(4-sulfonatophenyl)porphyrin (TPPS4). This design confers dual capabilities of targeted adhesion enhancement and redox responsiveness. Within the hypoxic milieu of anaerobic pathogens (e.g., Escherichia coli, E. coli; Enterococcus faecalis, E. faecalis), TPPS4-Ca undergoes selective reduction to phlorin/chlorin derivatives with dramatically augmented NIR absorption, achieving 58.17 % photothermal conversion efficiency. Notably, Ca2+ serves dual roles in this design: (1) coordination assembly stabilizes TPPS4 into long-circulating nanostructures; (2) serving as an electrostatic bridge, it amplifies electrostatic interactions between the porphyrin assemblies and bacterial surfaces, enabling selective inactivation of anaerobic pathogens (>99.9 % efficacy). Pathogen-specific activation triggers localized photothermal effects exclusively at infection sites, preserving high biocompatibility. In vivo validation demonstrates potent antibacterial action (>99.9 % reduction in E. coli survival) and accelerated wound healing in rat models. Materials and methods

Materials

Meso-Tetra(4-sulfonatophenyl)porphyrin (TPPS4, AR) was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca(NO3)2·4H2O, 98.5 %), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, AR), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, AR) were obtained from Shanghai Hushi Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd. Yeast Extract, Tryptone, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Agar powder was supplied by Bioengineering (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium dithionite (Na2S2O4, 88.0 %), DMEM high-glucose medium (with glutamine and sodium pyruvate, 500 mL), trypsin digestion solution (0.25 %, containing EDTA and phenol red, 100 mL), CCK-8 Cell Counting Kit (500 tests), and physiological saline (0.9 %, sterile) were acquired from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd. Glucose, sodium chloride, bacterial culture, and Bacterial Viability/Toxicity Assay Kit (20 tests) were used. All aqueous solutions were prepared with deionized water. Bacterial strains, including Escherichia coli TOP10, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212, Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, were obtained from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC). L929 and 293T cell lines were procured from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Beijing, China).

Synthesis of TPPS4-Ca

An aqueous solution of meso-tetra(4-sulfonatophenyl)porphyrin (TPPS4) and calcium nitrate was mixed at a molar ratio of 1:2 (TPPS4: Ca2+), yielding a final TPPS4-Ca complex concentration of 1 mM. The pH was adjusted to 7.0 using a pH meter. The mixture was then incubated at 25 °C for 24 h under light-protected conditions to facilitate coordination-driven self-assembly.

Characterization

The morphology, dimensions, and spatial distribution of elements in the material were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HITACHI Regulus 8100, Japan). Elemental composition mapping was performed with the integrated energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) system. Dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern Nano ZSE, UK) was employed to measure the hydrodynamic radius in aqueous solution. UV–Vis absorption spectra were recorded on a spectrophotometer (UV-1900i, China) over a range of 200–800 nm with a scanning step of 0.5 nm, using ultrapure water as the solvent. Steady-state fluorescence spectra were acquired using a fluorescence spectrometer (FS5, UK) with both excitation and emission slits set to 4 nm and ultrapure water as the solvent. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained on a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (USA) in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 at a scan speed of 4 cm−1 with 64 accumulated scans. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific ESCALAB Xi+, USA) was used to determine elemental composition and chemical states. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker 400 MHz spectrometer (Germany) using D2O as the solvent, with 16 scans accumulated for signal enhancement. Zeta potential measurements were conducted on a Malvern Nano ZSE analyzer (UK). Changes in the surface potential of 0.2 mM Ca2+ solution and TPPS4 solutions before and after metal ion addition were analyzed, with 5 independent replicates (20 scans per replicate). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were acquired on a Bruker EMX nano spectrometer (Germany) under a magnetic field range of 3320–3540 Gauss (G) with a resolution of 4 μG and a scan rate of 0.1 G/s. The redox potential of bacterial suspensions was measured using a pH/mV meter (Seven Compact S210, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) results were obtained using a SHIMADZU Essentia LC-16 system. Laser irradiance was quantified with a power/energy meter (LELPM18PBS, Leiao Optoelectronics Co., China), which calculates radiant energy per unit area and time.

Cyclovoltammetric (CV) analysis

Electrochemical measurements were conducted at 298 K (25 °C) using a standard three-electrode configuration on a Gamry Interface 1010 electrochemical workstation. The system employed an Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl, saturated) reference electrode, a glassy carbon working electrode, and a platinum wire counter electrode. Experiments were performed in a 50 mM NaCl aqueous electrolyte solution at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

Theoretical calculations employed the B3LYP-D3/6-31G∗∗ level for structural optimizations and vibrational frequency analyses, while single-point energies were computed at the B3LYP-D3/6-311+G∗∗ level incorporating the SMD continuum solvation model (water) for implicit solvent effects.

Photothermal performance evaluation

TPPS4-Ca was reduced with Na2S2O4 and diluted to a 0.1 mM sample solution. The solution was irradiated with a 660 nm near-infrared (NIR) laser at a power density of 1 W/cm2 for 10 min. The temperature was recorded at 30-s intervals, and thermal images were captured every 2 min using an infrared thermal camera (FLIR E53). To assess photothermal stability, three heating-cooling cycles were performed under identical irradiation conditions (1 W/cm2, 660 nm), with each cycle consisting of 10 min laser exposure followed by 10 min natural cooling. Additionally, the sample solution at a defined concentration was irradiated for 10 min (1 W/cm2, 660 nm), after which the light source was blocked to monitor temperature decay with measurements taken every 30 s. Sterile water served as a blank control and was irradiated under the same conditions for 10 min, with its temperature recorded at the 10-min timepoint. Ambient temperature was monitored using a laboratory thermometer. The cooling regression line was derived from UV–vis spectrophotometric absorption intensity data, enabling calculation of the photothermal conversion efficiency (η) according to the following formula:(1)τs=mwaterCwaterhS(2)Q0=hS(Tmax,water‐Tsurr)(3)ηT=hS(Tmax‐Tsurr)‐Q0I(1‐10‐A660)In Equation (3), h is the heat transfer coefficient, S is the container surface area, Tmax corresponds to the maximum temperature of the sample during irradiation, Tsurr indicates the ambient temperature during testing, I is the near - infrared radiation power, Aλ is the sample's absorbance at 660 nm, and Q0 is the heat input rate from solvent - absorbed irradiation light (in mW). In Equation (1), τs is defined as the thermal time constant of the system, mwater and Cwater are the water mass (1 g) and specific heat capacity (4.2 J g−1 K−1). In Equation (2), Tmax, water signifies the maximum temperature attained by water during irradiation.

Bacterial culture

E. coli, E. faecalis, B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa were selected for in vitro antibacterial assays. Bacterial strains were cultured in 20 mL of Lysogeny broth (LB) medium at 37 °C under constant agitation (165 rpm) for 8–10 h. Bacteria in the exponential growth phase were harvested via centrifugation and subsequently resuspended in sterile water.

In vitro photothermal antibacterial assay

Bacterial cultures were harvested, washed twice with sterile water, and resuspended in sterile water. A 200 μL aliquot of the bacterial suspension was analyzed using a microplate reader to measure the optical density at 600 nm (OD600), and the suspension was diluted to OD600 = 0.5. For the experimental group, the OD600 = 0.5 bacterial suspension was mixed with TPPS4-Ca solution to a final concentration of 0.1 mM, while the control group was mixed with sterile water. Subsequently, the near-infrared (NIR)-irradiated group was exposed to an 660 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 10 min, and the dark control group was kept in darkness for 10 min. After treatment, the mixtures were serially diluted, and 100 μL of each dilution was spread onto agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h in an electric thermostatic incubator. Colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted, and the bacterial survival rate was calculated using the formula:Survivalrate(%)=A1A0×100%

A1 = Number of colonies in the experimental group, A2 = Number of colonies in the control group.

Bacterial cell membrane integrity testing via scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Following identical bacterial treatment procedures for six distinct experimental groups, the mixed suspensions were centrifuged (4100 rpm, 2 min) and washed once. The pellets were resuspended in 100 μL of sterile water to ensure homogeneous distribution. A 2 μL aliquot was deposited onto a silicon wafer and air-dried naturally to form a uniform film. Samples were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde solution. Subsequently, gradient dehydration was performed using 40 %, 70 %, 90 %, and 100 % ethanol. After complete ethanol evaporation, the samples were sputter-coated with gold for 120 s and subjected to SEM analysis.

Live/dead bacterial staining assay

Following the same bacterial treatment procedures as described above, mixed suspensions from six experimental groups were stained using a live/dead bacterial viability kit. Specifically, NucGreen stained both live and dead bacteria green, while EthD-Ⅲ selectively labeled dead bacteria red. The samples were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, followed by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 5 min) to collect bacterial pellets. The pellets were washed three times with sterile water and resuspended in sterile water, then maintained on ice until analysis. A 10 μL aliquot of the suspension was placed on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Leica TS2R-FL microscope. NucGreen fluorescence was detected at excitation/emission wavelengths of 503/530 nm, and EthD-Ⅲ fluorescence at 530/620 nm. Fluorescence intensities were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Zeta potential measurements

Under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow hood, pre-activated cultures of E. coli, E. faecalis, B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa were diluted to an optical density (OD600) of 0.5. The bacterial suspensions were then mixed with 0.1 mM TPPS4-Ca and incubated for 10 min. Subsequently, the mixtures were placed on ice until further analysis.

Quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM-D) measurements

The adsorption of microorganisms on substrates was monitored using a QCM-D instrument (Q-sense E4, Biolin Scientific, Sweden). Sterile water was initially injected into the flow module at a rate of 0.20 mL min−1 to stabilize the baseline. Subsequently, 100 μL of 0.1 mM TPPS4 and TPPS4-Ca solutions were spin-coated onto the QCM chips and dried. Microbial suspensions (OD600 = 1) of selected strains E. coli, E. faecalis, B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa were individually pumped into the flow module at a rate of 0.10 mL min−1 to allow equilibration.

Cytotoxicity assay in vitro

Human embryonic kidney cells (293T) and mouse fibroblast cells (L929) were retrieved from −80 °C storage and immediately thawed in a 45 °C water bath. After complete dissolution, the cell suspension was transferred to pre-prepared culture medium, followed by centrifugation (1500 rpm, 3 min) to remove supernatant. The pellet was resuspended in 3 mL fresh medium and evenly distributed into culture flasks containing 10 mL medium for adhesion monitoring.

Upon reaching confluence, adherent cells were transferred to a sterile biosafety cabinet. Culture medium was aspirated, and cells were detached using trypsin digestion solution (3–5 min). After centrifugation (1500 rpm, 3 min), the supernatant was discarded, and cells were resuspended in a minimal volume of medium. A 10 μL aliquot of homogenized cell suspension was loaded onto a hemocytometer for counting, followed by dilution to 50,000 cells/mL. A 100 μL aliquot of diluted cell suspension was seeded into 96-well plates, with 200 μL PBS added to peripheral wells to minimize evaporation. Plates were incubated at 37 °C under 5 % CO2 for 6–10 h to ensure cell adhesion. Supernatant was carefully removed, followed by addition of 100 μL test compound solutions (varying concentrations) to experimental groups or 100 μL fresh medium to control groups. After 24 h incubation (37 °C, 5 % CO2), 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured after 1 h incubation. Cell viability was calculated using the formula:Cellviability(%)=Bt‐B0‐BaB‐B0×100%

B represents the absorbance of the control group comprising untreated cells, B0 corresponds to the blank control wells containing culture medium alone (used for zero calibration), Bt denotes the absorbance of the experimental group with material-treated cells, and Ba indicates the inherent absorbance of the material itself measured at 450 nm.

Cell migration assay

L929 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C under 5 % CO2 until the monolayer reached approximately 90 % confluence. A straight scratch was made vertically through the center of each well using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. The wells were then gently washed with PBS to remove floating cells and loose debris, thereby clarifying the wound edges. Subsequently, the cells were either left untreated or treated with 0.1 mM TPPS4 or TPPS4-Ca in culture medium. The distance between the two edges of the scratch was observed at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope, and image data were analyzed with Image J software. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The relative migration percentage of cells treated was calculated using the following formula:Relativemigration(%)=S0−StS0

where S0 represents the initial scratch area, and St represents the scratch area after 24 h.

Zein experiment

TPPS4 and TPPS4-Ca solutions with a concentration of 0.1 mM were prepared using ultrapure water and allowed to stand for 24 h. Then, 5 mg of zein was weighed and added to 1 mL of each of the aforementioned solutions, followed by thorough mixing. The mixtures were incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 165 rpm for 2 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 7200 rpm for 2 min. The supernatant was collected, and ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectra were recorded from 200 to 300 nm using a 3 mm cuvette to observe changes in the absorption peak around 277 nm.

In vivo wound healing assay

Animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Research Council and approved by the Ethics Committee for Laboratory Animals of Fujian Medical University (No: IACUC FJMU 2023 - Y- 0552).

A total of 32 male Sprague-Dawley rats (SD rats were sourced from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd.) weighing 165 g were selected as experimental subjects. The animals underwent one week of acclimatization with free access to water and provided with a quantified diet, resulting in a body weight gain to approximately 200 g. The rats were randomly divided into four groups (eight rats per group) based on body weight: Control-NIR(−) group, TPPS4-Ca-NIR(−) group, Control-NIR(+) group, and TPPS4-Ca-NIR(+) group. Following anesthesia with 0.3 % pentobarbital sodium, the dorsal hair of each rat was depilated. A full-thickness circular wound (8 mm diameter) was created on the rat's dorsum. The wound was subsequently infected with 10 μL of 7.5 × 108 CFU/mL E. coli suspension for 2 h, thereby establishing a full-thickness infected cutaneous wound model. According to experimental grouping, different treatments were administered: For the TPPS4-Ca treatment groups, a topical application of 20 μL of 0.1 mM TPPS4-Ca solution was administered to the wound site. Control groups received an equal volume (20 μL) of sterile water. rats in the near-infrared (NIR) treatment groups received 660 nm NIR irradiation at 1.0 W/cm2 for 10 min. Thermal parameters were recorded every 30 s during irradiation, with thermal imaging captured every 2 min. Wound progression was monitored by photographic documentation on days 0, 3, 7, 11, and 14 post-modeling (day 0 denoting the modeling day). Wound area changes were quantified using image analysis software (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health, USA). Daily body weight measurements were conducted to assess growth status throughout the experimental period.

Wound healing efficacy was evaluated by calculating the wound area using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) based on photographic documentation of the rat wounds. Measurements were performed on days 0, 3, 7, 11, and 14 post-modeling to quantify the percentage of wound closure, which was determined using the formula:Relativewoundarea(%)=AnA0×100%where A0 is the initial wound area on day 0 and An is the wound area on day n of treatment (day n, n = 3, 7, 11, 14).

On days 1、days 3、days 5 and 11 post-treatment, three rats from each group were randomly selected for wound exudate collection using sterile swabs. The collected exudates were dispersed in 1 mL of normal saline through vigorous vortexing, followed by serial dilution. Subsequently, 100 μL of the diluted exudate was pipetted onto LB agar plates and evenly spread using glass beads. The plates were then inverted and incubated at 37 °C in an electric thermostatic incubator for 18 h. Bacterial colonies were enumerated after incubation, and the bacterial survival rate was calculated as the ratio of colony counts between the experimental and control groups.

Histological Analysis of Skin Tissues Following Different Treatments. On days 3 and 10 post-treatment, wound and adjacent tissues were harvested for histomorphological evaluation. Briefly, excised wound tissues were fixed in 10 % paraformaldehyde solution, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and paraffin-embedded. Serial sections were prepared and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson's trichrome staining and Immunohistochemical staining (TNF-α, IL-10, MMP9, and CD31). Histological parameters, including granulation tissue thickness, scar width, and collagen deposition, were quantitatively analyzed using these stained sections. In vivo biosafety evaluation. On days 3 and 10, major organs including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney from different treatment groups of rats were collected and subjected to H&E staining to observe their histological responses.

Statistical analysis

Data was presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.) from at least three independent experimental runs. Data was analyzed using GraphPad 8.01 software with either Student's t-test or Two-Way ANOVA. Significance was set at p < 0.05, with the following designations: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

The coordination self-assembly of TPPS4-Ca

Free TPPS4 in aqueous solution failed to form ordered aggregates due to non-directional electrostatic repulsion between molecules, resulting in negligible Tyndall scattering that confirmed the absence of colloidal structures (Figs. S1 and S2). Upon uniformly mixing Ca(NO3)2 and TPPS4 in aqueous solution, a pronounced Tyndall effect was observed (Fig. S2), indicating successful formation of coordination-driven TPPS4-Ca assemblies.Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed monodisperse quasi-spherical nanoparticles generated through this metal coordinated assembly (Fig. 1a). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 1b and S3) confirmed the co-localization of S (from TPPS4) and Ca within these architectures, verifying co-assembly. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1c) analysis corroborated the SEM observations, showing uniform assemblies with hydrodynamic diameters centered at approximately 500 nm (Fig. 1c).The coordination mode between metal ions and porphyrins critically governed the spatial arrangement of porphyrin molecules, thereby modulating the morphology and optoelectronic properties [46,47]. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis (Fig. 1d) revealed the disappearance of the absorption peak at 996 cm−1 and the intensification of the signal at 1396 cm−1, indicating coordination between Ca2+ and pyrrolic nitrogens. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further corroborated the coordination (Fig. 1e). The binding energy of the Ca2+ 2p3/2 orbital shifted negatively from 347.3 eV in free Ca2+ to 346.9 eV (ΔE = −0.4 eV) in the TPPS4-Ca. This negative shift reflected increased electron density on Ca2+, confirming its coordination with pyrrolic nitrogens of TPPS4. The ionic radius of metal ions critically governed whether coordination to pyrrolic nitrogens in porphyrins occurred through in-plane or out-of-plane modes [42,48]. It was noteworthy that Bi3+ coordinated to TPPS4 via out-of-plane coordination due to its large size with radius about 0.100 nm [42,49]. With Ca2+ (0.099 nm) and Bi3+ exhibiting comparable ionic radii, we proposed out-of-plane coordination for Ca2+-TPPS4. Chemical reduction behavior of TPPS4-Ca coordination self-assemblies

To probe the reduction behavior of TPPS4 and TPPS4-Ca assemblies, we performed chemical reduction using sodium dithionite (Na2S2O4) under anaerobic conditions (Fig. 2a). Addition of Na2S2O4 induced rapid reduction of TPPS4, evidenced by an instantaneous color shift from pink to dark green. UV–Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2b) revealed concomitant attenuation of the porphyrin Soret band at 415 nm, with emergence of new absorption peaks at 435 nm (visible) and 645 nm (NIR). The reduction process was further characterized via cyclic voltammetry (CV), electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), and 1H NMR. As reported in previous studies [32,50], electrochemical or chemical reduction of meso-substituted porphyrins can generate both phlorin and chlorin-type derivatives due to the presence of conjugated double bonds susceptible to stepwise reduction. CV profiles (Fig. 2c) exhibited two distinct redox couples at −0.13 V and −0.67 V, which is indicative of a concerted two-electron transfer process. EPR analysis (Fig. 2d) detected no radical signals pre- or post-reduction, confirming non-radical products. 1H NMR (Fig. 2e) demonstrated the disappearance of the pyrrolic proton signal at 7.7 ppm for TPPS4 after reduction, along with new signals at 2.5 ppm and 3.4 ppm, consistent with the formation of phlorin/chlorin species. FT-IR spectra (Fig. 2f) further corroborated reduction through significant attenuation of the 1600 cm−1 band (C=C/C=N stretching), indicating partial hydrogenation to C-N single bonds. XPS N1s analysis (Fig. S8) provided additional evidence, showing a positive shift in binding energy from 398.1 eV to 398.5 eV (ΔE = +0.4 eV) attributable to reduced electron delocalization at nitrogen atoms following protonation to -NH- moieties. Bacteria-triggered reduction and photothermal conversion in TPPS4-Ca coordination self-assemblies

Systematic NIR photothermal performance demonstrated that the chemically reduced products of both TPPS4 and TPPS4-Ca exhibited excellent photothermal responsiveness under 660 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm2). As shown in Fig. 3a, the temperature increase (ΔT) of both materials stabilized at 27 °C during 10 min irradiation, confirming the high photothermal conversion capability. Thermodynamic modeling of time-temperature curves (Fig. 3b) quantified the photothermal conversion efficiency (η) as 56.03 % for reduced TPPS4 and 58.17 % for TPPS4-Ca. Crucially, photothermal cycling stability tests (Fig. 3c) revealed ≈20 % efficiency decline in TPPS4 derivatives after multiple cycles, whereas TPPS4-Ca assemblies maintained near-invariant photothermal performance. This established that Ca2+ coordination enhanced photothermal stability. Photostability assays monitoring UV–Vis spectra pre-/post-irradiation (Fig. S11) further corroborated this finding. To assess bacterial in situ reduction of TPPS4-Ca assemblies, the material was incubated with E. coli, a representative facultative anaerobe with strong reducing capacity. Post-incubation, solution color shifted from pink to dark green. Corresponding UV–Vis spectra (Fig. 3d) revealed a redshift in the Soret band (435 nm) and enhanced Q-band absorption (645 nm), this spectral evolution closely matched that of chemically induced reduction and confirmed bacterial in situ activation. Quantification via 660 nm absorbance demonstrated E. coli-mediated reduction efficiency of 51.75 ± 2.3 %. We further evaluated reduction capacity across diverse bacteria (Fig. 3d and e), including E. faecalis, Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), correlating outcomes with redox potential quantification. Under identical conditions (Table S1), facultative anaerobes with strong reducing capacity (redox potentials below the TPPS4-Ca threshold, E. coli: 109 mV; E. faecalis: 167 mV) successfully reduced TPPS4-Ca (186 mV). In contrast, obligate aerobes (B. subtilis: 306 mV, P. aeruginosa: 344 mV) exhibited no reduction. This established facultative anaerobe-specific reducibility as an inherent property of the assembly system. To determine whether bacterial reduction produces the same products as chemical reduction, TPPS4-Ca was incubated with four test bacteria species and analyzed by HPLC. Only samples treated with anaerobic bacteria (E. coli, E. faecalis) exhibited the same two peaks as observed in the chemically reduced sample (Fig. S9), confirming identical reduction products and demonstrating the selective activation of TPPS4-Ca by bacteria with high reducing capacity.

To leverage the bacteria-selective in situ reduction of TPPS4-Ca to NIR absorbing phlorin/chlorin species, the microbe-responsive photothermal performance was investigated. Upon reduction by E. coli, TPPS4-Ca achieved rapid heating from 27.1 °C to 52.2 °C within 10 min of 660 nm NIR irradiation, significantly exceeding free TPPS4 (Fig. S12). This confirmed that Ca2+ coordination enhanced both reduction efficiency and energy conversion through strengthened electrostatic interactions between the assembly material and bacterial surfaces. Strain-comparative experiments (Fig. 3f) revealed distinct photothermal profiles. Effective reductant strains (E. coli and E. faecalis) enabled rapid heating to >50 °C, whereas ineffective strains (B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa) plateaued at 37.4 °C and 41.7 °C, which are below the bactericidal threshold [51]. To further demonstrate the crucial role of Ca2+, control experiments were carried out using Na+ and K+, which possess smaller ionic radii and weaker coordination tendencies. As shown in Fig. S13, TPPS4 coordinated with Na + or K+ showed negligible photothermal activity and were unable to achieve effective bacterial eradication, confirming that not all cations can facilitate the desired assembly and photothermal effect. Infrared thermography (Fig. 3g–S14) visualized spatial temperature distributions, confirming a strict correlation between photothermal output and bacterial reducing capacity. To rule out potential interference from culture media components, control experiments were performed using Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) and Brain-Heart Infusion (BHI) broth. As illustrated in Fig. S15, both E. coli and E. faecalis consistently induced strong photothermal conversion of TPPS4-Ca regardless of the medium used, whereas P. aeruginosa and B. subtilis failed to elicit a noticeable response. These results confirm that the reduction process is predominantly dependent on bacterial physiology rather than medium-derived factors. Critically, long-term co-culture studies (Fig. 3h and i) demonstrated robust stability of TPPS4-Ca assemblies. TPPS4-Ca maintained consistent photoresponsiveness after 1-week and 1-month incubations (post-irradiation ΔT <1.5 °C), contrasting sharply with TPPS4 monomers which lost >40 % of their response within 24 h (Fig. S16). This exceptional operational stability is attributed to the rigid coordination architecture formed by Ca2+-directed staggered stacking, which effectively suppresses molecular dissociation and π-aggregation quenching. This stability is consistent with previous cycling tests and meets the practical application requirements for persistent infection control.

To assess the selectivity of TPPS4-Ca in clinically relevant polymicrobial consortia, we co-cultured E. coli and P. aeruginosa at varying ratios. The photothermal response of TPPS4-Ca was strictly dependent on the ratio of the reductively competent E. coli (Fig. S17). Negligible heating was observed in P. aeruginosa-dominated cultures, while robust activation required a high E. coli abundance, demonstrating infection-dependent activation.

Bacteria-triggered photothermal disinfection via reductive activation of TPPS4-Ca coordination assemblies

To evaluate the photothermal antibacterial selectivity towards facultative anaerobes and aerobic bacteria, plate counting was performed to analyze bacterial viability. TPPS4-Ca assemblies under 660 nm photothermal irradiation achieved >99 % inhibition against E. coli and E. faecalis (Fig. 4a and b and S18), while exhibiting negligible activity for B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa. This starkly contrasted with free TPPS4, which showed limited selectivity (28.2 % and 41.8 % inhibition against E. coli and E. faecalis, respectively). This disparity aligned with prior photothermal conversion performance data. It confirms that the coordination assembly of TPPS4-Ca not only sustained high energy conversion efficiency but also exhibited superior durability, thereby fulfilling critical stability requirements for clinical photothermal applications. To elucidate material-bacteria interaction mechanisms, interfacial adsorption dynamics were investigated. Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 4c) revealed significantly greater positive shifts in bacterial surface charge following TPPS4-Ca treatment versus TPPS4 alone, confirming enhanced electrostatic attraction through Ca2+ coordination assembly. Real-time quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) monitoring tracked the dynamic adsorption processes of four bacterial strains onto unmodified, TPPS4-modified chips, and TPPS4-Ca-modified gold chips. Frequency shift attenuation (|ΔF|), which is proportional to adsorbed biomass, showed that TPPS4-Ca enhanced adsorption by 129.3 % (E. coli), 203.3 % (E. faecalis), 827.8 % (B. subtilis), and 315.1 % (P. aeruginosa) versus bare gold (Fig. 4d). Notably, adsorption capacity exceeded that of TPPS4 by 1.6-3.25 × across strains (Figs. S19 and S20), consistent with Zeta potential data and establishing that Ca2+ coordination amplified electrostatic interfacial forces to promote targeted bacterial adhesion. To investigate the bacterial death mechanisms, SEM and live/dead bacterial staining assays were conducted to analyze TPPS4-Ca-treated pathogens (Fig. 4e–h). Control groups maintained intact rod/spherical morphologies with smooth surfaces regardless of irradiation. Strikingly, the TPPS4-Ca exhibited distinct species-selective photothermal antibacterial effects.

Aerobic bacteria (B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa) remained undamaged, while facultative anaerobes (E. coli and E. faecalis) displayed collapsed morphology, wrinkling, and severe membrane disruption. Live/dead fluorescence assays (Fig. 4e–h, S21–S23) further elucidated bactericidal mechanisms. NuGreen (green) penetrates all bacteria regardless of membrane integrity, whereas EthD-III (red) exclusively penetrates membrane-compromised cells. Controls displayed homogeneous green fluorescence (viable cells) with near-zero red/green intensity ratios, confirming structural and metabolic integrity. TPPS4-Ca-treated groups exhibited dramatically enhanced red fluorescence, indicating irreversible membrane damage. Notably, membrane compromise in TPPS4-treated groups was significantly less severe than in TPPS4-Ca-treated samples as confirmed by both SEM imaging and fluorescence quantification analysis. The above results collectively confirmed that the antibacterial efficacy of TPPS4-Ca is strictly dependent on electrostatic adsorption to the bacterial surface and its reduction-activated photothermal conversion capacity.

Prior to in vivo infected wound treatment, the cytotoxicity of TPPS4-Ca was evaluated. Cationic porphyrins were deliberately excluded due to their bio-toxicity concerns. Two representative cell lines (mouse fibroblast L929 and human embryonic kidney cells 293T) were selected to assess the cytotoxicity of the system, which revealed significantly higher viability with TPPS4-Ca versus TPPS4 monomers (Fig. S24) Specifically, TPPS4 itself exhibited moderate cytotoxicity, with cell viability rates of 77.22 ± 4.68 % (L929) and 83.11 ± 8.17 % (293T). Ca2+ coordination substantially improved biocompatibility, elevating viability to 91.48 ± 5.40 % (L929) and 97.24 ± 1.88 % (293T) at tested concentrations. To assess the potential of TPPS4-Ca to promote cell migration, a scratch assay was performed using L929 fibroblasts. The treatment group (0.1 mM TPPS4-Ca) showed a migration closure rate of 83.5 ± 2.23 % within 24h, significantly higher than that of the control group (6.95 ± 3.90 %) and TPPS4 group (14.22 ± 1.71 %) (Fig. S25). Moreover, the cells exhibited enhanced pseudopodia formation and directional movement, indicating that TPPS4-Ca not only exhibits good biocompatibility but may also actively facilitate cell migration, thereby contributing to wound closure. This enhancement may stem from Ca2+ mimicking endogenous calcium signaling pathways in biological systems, thereby improving metabolic compatibility between the material and cells. Additionally, the potential for skin irritation was assessed using an in vitro Zein model (Fig. S26). The absence of a significant increase in absorbance at 227 nm following co-incubation with TPPS4-Ca suggests that the material does not induce substantial protein denaturation, supporting its preliminary dermal biocompatibility.

Conclusion

We successfully engineered a bacteria-triggered reduction system (TPPS4-Ca) for photothermal selective sterilization. Ca2+ directs out of plane coordination with TPPS4 to form quasi spherical nanoparticles, which are selectively reduced by facultative anaerobes in hypoxic microenvironments to generate phlorin/chlorin species with exceptional photothermal conversion efficiency (58.17 %). Additionaly, the coordination architecture concurrently enables enhanced electrostatic targeting via charge reversal and extended functional stability (<1 % efficiency decay after 30-day incubation in bacterial suspensions) by suppressing molecular dissociation and π-aggregation quenching. This dual functionality achieves pathogen specific eradication (>99 % inhibition) with preserved mammalian cell viability (>95 %). Moreover, the activation of TPPS4-Ca exhibited strong dependence on the abundance of reductively competent bacteria within mixed cultures, suggesting its potential to selectively target high-density infections while minimizing impact on commensal-dominated microflora. In E. coli infected wound models, TPPS4-Ca self-assemblies enabled 95.44 % wound closure by Day 14 through precision bactericidal action and scar suppression, demonstrating immediate clinical translation potential. This work integrates bacterial metabolic specificity with photothermal responsiveness, overcoming nonslective activation limitations of conventional agents. It establishes a new paradigm for developing integrated antibacterial and wound regenerative biomaterials.
