Post stress sensory contact with a male mouse is associated with altered social behavior in a female vicarious social defeat stress model
Authors: Yuhei Yajima, Aki Takahashi, Atsushi Toyoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression affects females more than males, with responses to antidepressants also exhibiting a distinct difference between sexes. Stress models in rodents have predominantly been developed for males, and their application in females remains challenging. This study investigated how post stress sensory contact with male mice affects the behavioral outcomes of female C57BL/6JJcl (B6) mice subjected to vicarious social defeat stress (VDS). We compared three types of VDS paradigms in female mice: the conventional paradigm and two adapted protocols that eliminated sensory contact with male ICR mice during the post-stress period under either single-housed (SH) or pair-housed (PH) conditions. In the conventional paradigm, female B6 mice did not exhibit a significant increase in social avoidance. In contrast, in the adapted paradigm, social avoidance behavior was observed in the PH group, indicating that post stress sensory contact with male ICR affects the stress susceptibility of female VDS model. Furthermore, the estrous cycle stage did not affect social avoidance behavior in each group. Next, we compared two different housing conditions to examine the appropriate method for eliminating sensory contact in female VDS model. SH mice showed reduced feed intake, increased water consumption, and attenuated body weight gain, regardless of VDS exposure. These findings suggest that post-stress sensory contact with a male mouse may influence social behavior in female mice exposed to VDS. The present study highlights the potential impacts of these experimental variables when interpreting behavioral outcomes in female VDS models.Keywords: depression, female model, sensory contact with male, mice, vicarious social defeat stress

Introduction

Depression affects more than 280 million individuals worldwide and remains a significant public health challenge owing to its complex and poorly understood pathophysiology [1]. Previous studies have consistently shown that the prevalence of depression is approximately twice as high in women as it is in men [2, 3], suggesting the involvement of sex-specific pathological mechanisms. Neurobiological factors, such as differences in brain structure and function [4], as well as hormonal fluctuations associated with the ovarian cycle [5], have been implicated in these sex differences. Furthermore, clinical studies have indicated that responses to antidepressant treatments may vary according to sex, underscoring the need for research that explicitly considers biological sex as a variable [6].

Social defeat stress (SDS) model is widely used to study the pathophysiological mechanisms of depression and has demonstrated strong construct, predictive, and face validity in male mice [7]. However, applying SDS to female mice remains challenging owing to the lack of naturally occurring aggression between females and males. Several strategies have been developed to overcome this limitation, including chemogenetic manipulation to enhance aggression in male mice [8] and the use of pheromones in male urine to promote male aggression towards females [9]. Despite these advances, defeat stress models that do not rely on experimental interventions and more closely replicate naturalistic conditions are required, particularly in female subjects [10].

Vicarious social defeat stress (VDS) is a promising alternative as it induces psychological stress through exposure to social defeat without physical confrontation [11,12,13]. While this model has been successfully applied to female mice, depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors induced by VDS may be sensitive to contextual variables such as housing conditions or baseline anxiety, potentially limiting its reproducibility across laboratories [14]. Although sensory contact with male mice and housing conditions during stress exposure have been shown to modulate stress responses in other paradigms [8], their influence on the development of VDS in female mice remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap hinders the establishment of a robust and reproducible VDS model in female mice.

In this study, we hypothesized that limiting sensory exposure during VDS would improve the reproducibility of stress-related behaviors in female mice. We first examined the impact of restricting sensory contact with male mice on social behavior in a female VDS model and then investigated optimal strategies for limiting such sensory contact to male mice. Specifically, we generated a female VDS model using either the conventional paradigm or a modified paradigm in which sensory contact with male mice was restricted by housing females individually (single-housed; SH) or in same-sex pairs (pair-housed; PH) following daily stress sessions. Behavioral outcomes were assessed using the social interaction (SI) test. Next, to investigate appropriate strategies for limiting sensory contact to male mice after VDS exposure, we compared physiological measures, including body weight gain, between the SH and PH conditions within the adapted VDS paradigm. Our findings aimed to refine the VDS model as a robust and sex-appropriate tool for investigating stress-induced behavioral phenotypes in female rodents.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Eight-week-old female and male C57BL/6JJcl (B6) mice were purchased from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan), and male Slc:ICR (ICR) mice were purchased from SLC Japan (Shizuoka, Japan). Female and male mice were housed in same-sex groups of 3–4 during the two-week habituation periods. All mice were maintained under a 12:12 h reversed light/dark cycle (lights on from 17:00 to 5:00) at a controlled temperature of 23 ± 2°C and 50–60% humidity. Throughout the experimental period, female and male B6 mice were fed a semi-purified diet (AIN-93G, Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan) and reverse osmosis water ad libitum. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Ibaraki University (approval numbers: 21040, 22051, and 24010).

Conventional VDS paradigm

A VDS model for male mice was established by Warren et al. [11], and this protocol was modified for female mice [13] (Fig. 1A). Aggressive ICR mouse were housed individually in cages divided into two compartments using a transparent Plexiglass barrier, facilitating visual, auditory, and olfactory contact (SD cage) (cage size 220 mm × 320 mm × 135 mm; Natsume Seisakusho, Tokyo, Japan). During each 10 min session, a male B6 mice was placed in the same compartment as the aggressive ICR mouse, this physical contact causing SDS. Simultaneously, a female B6 mice were placed in the adjacent compartment to observe this interaction. Subsequently, the male B6 mice were moved to the compartment where the female mice were, and the female mice were placed in a compartment adjacent to a different aggressive ICR mouse until the next stress session the following day. The VDS paradigm was performed in the light phase (19:30–22:30), and female mice were exposed to the vicarious defeat of different male mice for 10 days. Control B6 mice were kept in pairs in each compartment in SD cage with the divider for 10 days. Aggressive ICR mice were selected as previously described [15].

Experimental schematics of conventional (A) and adapted VDS paradigm (B).

Adapted VDS paradigm

An adapted VDS paradigm was employed, which modified a previously described procedure [13] (Fig. 1B). For each stress session (10 min/day), female B6 mice were returned to their home cage (cage size 143 mm × 293 mm × 148 mm; Charles River Laboratories Japan, Kanagawa, Japan), either in SH or PH, and housed for 24 h until the next stress session [8]. This approach avoided cohabitation between female B6 mouse and aggressive ICR mouse (Fig. 1B). Control B6 mice were housed either singly or in pairs in the cages for 10 days. PH cage mates were assigned within each experimental group; that is, pairs were formed either within the control group or within the VDS group. Under the PH conditions, to control prior social contact between paired mice, cage mates during the habituation period were selected as pairs.

Body weight gain, feed intake, and water intake

During the 10 days experimental period, body weight gain, feed intake, and water intake were measured using a protocol modified from previously reported methods [15, 16]. Body weight gain was calculated as the difference in body weight between day 1 and day 10. Feed and water intakes were measured daily by weighing the feed and water bottles simultaneously each day and subtracting the previous day’s values. For the PH group, individual estimates were obtained by dividing the total cage value by two.

Social interaction test

On day 11, the SI test was conducted to assess the effects of VDS on social behavior in the female B6 mice using previously described methods [15, 17] (Fig. 2). The test apparatus consisted of a square open-field arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) containing a wire-mesh enclosure (“interaction box”, 10 cm × 10 cm × 13 cm) with three windows, placed on one side of the arena (O’Hara & Co., Tokyo, Japan). The interaction zone was defined as the area within approximately 7 cm of the interaction box, whereas two corner zones were designated on opposite sides (8 cm × 8 cm each). The SI test comprised two 2.5 min sessions. In the first (nonsocial) session, the subject mouse was placed in the arena with an empty interaction box. In the second (social) session, an unfamiliar male ICR mouse was introduced into the interaction box. The total distances, the time spent in the interaction zone (sec) and in the corner zone (sec) were measured in each trial with Image SI software (O’Hara & Co., Tokyo, Japan). The data analysis was conducted after the two sessions. The SI ratio was calculated as follows:

Time course of VDS paradigm and SI test.

SI ratio = (interaction time with target present) / (interaction time with target absent).

Estimation of the estrus cycle stages

After the SI test, the estrous cycle stage of each female B6 mouse was determined using a modified version of a previously described method [18]. Briefly, approximately 15 µl of distilled water was gently pipetted into the vagina, and the resulting lavage was transferred onto a glass slide. The vaginal smears were air-dried and stained with Giemsa solution (Merck, Tokyo, Japan). The estrous cycle stages were identified by observing the types and proportions of epithelial cells using light microscopy (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as means ± SEMs. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to was used to examine the effects of the two factors. If an effect of interaction was observed, t-tests were performed to compare control and VDS groups within each group. ANOVA was performed to examine the effect of the estrous cycle stages on the SI ratio in control and VDS female mice. F-values are reported with two degrees of freedom, expressed as F (df1, df2), where df1 represents the degrees of freedom for the factor and df2 represents the degrees of freedom for the error term. All the analyses were performed using Python (version 3.8). ANOVA was performed using statsmodels [19]. t-tests were performed using SciPy stats [20]. Data pre-processing was performed using pandas and numpy [21, 22]. The significance threshold was set at P<0.05. The marginally significance threshold was set at P<0.1.

Results

Effect of post stress sensory contact on social behavior of VDS model of female mice

To investigate the effects of the presence or absence of post stress sensory contact with male ICR mice after psychological stress induced by VDS on social behavior in the female VDS model, the SI test was performed (Fig. 1). Iñiguez et al. employed a SI test were using unfamiliar male CD-1 mice as social targets to investigate the effects of VDS on social behavior in female mice [13]. Therefore, we used unfamiliar male ICR mice as social targets in our study. We prepared two additional housing conditions in addition to the conventional VDS paradigm. Specifically, after each 10 min witnessing stress session, female mice were housed either individually or together with another female (without a partition) in a separate cage until the next defeat episode. We calculated SI ratio to evaluate depressive-like behavior [7, 17]. ANOVA was performed with VDS (Control, VDS) and stress paradigm (Conventional, SH, PH) as fixed factors. ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of VDS (F1,40=6.29, P=0.016; Fig. 3). The interaction between VDS and the stress paradigm showed a marginal trend but did not reach statistical significance (F2,40=2.50, P=0.095; Fig. 3). No significant main effect of the stress paradigm on SI ratio was observed (F2,40=0.228, P=0.79; Fig. 3). These results suggested that the effects of VDS differ depending on the stress paradigm used. Furthermore, post hoc comparisons using t-test showed that the SI ratio of the VDS group were significantly lower than those of the control group under the PH condition (P=0.049; Fig. 3), whereas the VDS group under the SH condition showed a trend toward lower SI ratio compared with the control group ; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.065; Fig. 3). In contrast, the conventional VDS paradigm did not affect SI ratio of female mice (P=0.73; Fig. 3). We showed total distance traveled, time spent in interaction zone, time spent in corner zone measured during the social interaction test (Table 1). There were no significant effects of VDS in each stress paradigm.

Estrous cycle stage of the VDS model of female mice under conventional, single or pair housing conditions

Vaginal cytology was performed after the SI test to examine the potential influence of the estrous cycle stage on SI ratio in the VDS model ( Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). We observed no significant main effect of the estrous cycle stage on the SI ratio was observed under the conventional condition (F3,8=1.36, P=0.32), SH condition (F3,6=1.37, P=0.32), or PH condition (F3,11=1.46, P=0.28). These results suggested that under the present experimental conditions, the estrous cycle stage did not significantly influence VDS-induced social behavior.

Effect of housing condition on feed intake, water intake, and body weight gain of the adapted VDS model in female mice

We found that eliminating sensory contact with male ICR mice plays a crucial role in the reduction of SI ratio in female VDS model mice. To further examine the appropriate method for eliminating sensory contact after VDS exposure, we compared two different housing conditions. Specifically, food intake, water intake, and body weight gain were measured in female mice that were housed either individually or co-housed with another female until the next defeat episode following each witnessing stress session. Total feed intake, water intake, and body weight gain were analyzed over the 10-day experimental period (Supplementary Figs. 1A–F ). ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of housing condition on total feed intake (F1,19=11.5, P=0.0031), with PH mice consuming more feed than SH mice (Fig. 4A). On the other hand, no significant main effect of VDS treatment on total feed intake was observed (F1,19=0.0916, P=0.77; Fig. 4A). A significant main effect of housing condition was found for total water intake (F1,19=13.3, P=0.0017), with SH mice consuming more water than PH mice (Fig. 4B). VDS treatment had no significant effect on total water intake (F1,19=0.224, P=0.64; Fig. 4B). Housing condition showed a significant main effect on body weight gain (F1,19=6.65, P=0.015), with PH mice gaining more weight than SH mice (Fig. 4C). No significant main effect of VDS was observed (F1,19=0.476, P=0.50; Fig. 4C). Collectively, SH mice showed reduced feed intake, increased water consumption, and attenuated body weight gain compared PH mice, regardless of VDS exposure.

Discussion

SDS paradigms have been widely used to study depression-like behaviors in rodents; however, their application in female mice remains limited because of low aggression from conspecific males. The VDS was developed as a non-physical alternative; however, studies using female mice remain limited. Therefore, broader replication across research groups is essential to establish VDS as a rigorous and reliable stress model for females [14]. In the present study, we established a new protocol for the development of a VDS mouse model.

First, we hypothesized that limiting post stress sensory contact with male mice would improve the reproducibility of stress-induced behaviors in the female VDS model [8]. Therefore, in addition to the conventional VDS paradigm, we implemented an adapted VDS paradigm that restricted sensory contact with male aggressors during the post-stress period. Specifically, instead of co-housing female B6 mice with male ICR mice after VDS exposure, we applied either SH or PH conditions to examine whether limiting post stress sensory contact with a male mouse under specific housing conditions influences the social behavior in female mice subjected to VDS (Fig. 1B). This study was not designed to determine whether SH and PH conditions produce distinct behavioral outcomes. VDS significantly reduced the SI ratio in female mice under the PH condition. Under the SH condition, the VDS group showed a trend toward a lower SI ratio compared with the control group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3). Previous studies have reported that post stress sensory contact with male CD-1 mice decreases stress susceptibility in female repeated SDS model of mice, which has been interpreted as a confounding effect of exposure to mating-related sensory cues [8]. Therefore, our findings suggested that eliminating post stress sensory exposure to male ICR mice may help control potential confounding factors that could influence the establishment of the VDS model in female mice and may contribute to the induction of depression-like behaviors as well as improved reproducibility. Several studies have reported that male interactions affect stress responses in female rodents. Cohabitation with male rats suppresses the increase in locomotor activity observed in female rats subjected to chronic mild stress [23]. Furthermore, sexual experience confers resilience to restraint stress in female rats [24]. These findings suggest that stress responses in female stress models may be influenced by interactions with males, including sensory cues that can attenuate stress-induced behavioral responses. Therefore, sex-specific physiological and behavioral mechanisms should be carefully considered when adapting male-based stress paradigms in female models.

Krishnan et al. defined mice subjected to chronic SDS with an SI ratio below 1 as stress susceptible and, based on this criterion, elucidated molecular mechanisms underlying stress susceptibility in the brain reward system [17]. In addition, Takahashi et al. reported that restricting post stress sensory contact with male mice increased the proportion of stress susceptible individuals in a female repeated SDS model [8]. In contrast, in the present study, we observed only one stress susceptible individual among the ten mice subjected to VDS under the PH condition. These results suggest that the effects of restricting post stress sensory contact with male mice may vary depending on the intensity and nature of the stress paradigm applied.

In addition, the present study revealed that housing conditions affect physiological measures in female B6 mice. Individually housed female mice exhibited reduced feed intake, increased water consumption, and suppressed body weight gain compared with those housed in pairs, regardless of VDS exposure (Fig. 4). These alterations may reflect the effects of social isolation stress rather than the VDS itself. Psychosocial stressors, including social isolation, have been associated with activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and altered feeding behaviors [25]. Psychogenic polydipsia and appetite disturbances were also commonly reported in psychiatric disorders [26, 27]. Notably, female rodents are more sensitive to the effects of social isolation than male rodents [28], further supporting the notion that housing conditions can significantly influence behavioral and physiological phenotypes in female stress models.

Since the estrous cycle stage affects psychiatric symptoms in humans [29], we examined whether the estrous cycle stage influences social behavior in the female mouse model and found that the estrous cycle stage did not influence social avoidance in VDS female mice (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 ). These results were consistent with previous reports indicating that SDS-induced behaviors are not influenced by the estrous cycle stage in female mice [8, 9, 30]. However, the potential impact of VDS on ovarian hormone levels and, conversely, the influence of hormonal fluctuations across the estrous cycle on VDS-induced behavior remains unclear. Moreover, the number of mice per stage in the estrous cycle was highly variable per condition in this study. Therefore, this study provides preliminary evidence suggesting the minimal involvement of the estrous stage in modulating SI behavior in VDS-exposed females. Further studies are required to explore the roles of estradiol and progesterone. Notably, disruptions in the menstrual cycle have been reported in human patients with depression [31], and ovariectomies have been shown to attenuate depression-like behaviors in female rats with VDS [32]. These findings suggest that ovarian function, including cyclic hormonal changes, may play an important role in the pathophysiology of depression in females.

In previous studies, molecular and neuronal mechanisms within the ventral tegmental area − nucleus accumbens circuit, have been shown to regulate stress susceptibility in mouse models of SDS [17, 33]. Moreover, the ability to vicariously experience another individual’s aversive events, referred to as observational fear (OF), has been reported [34, 35], and neural networks involving the anterior cingulate cortex and basolateral amygdala are partially engaged in the behavioral outcomes observed in OF mouse models [36, 37]. Therefore, future studies targeting these brain regions in the female VDS model are needed.

This study has several limitations. First, the present study lacks control group exposed to sensory contact with male ICR for 10 min or 24 h without VDS. Future studies using such control groups will be necessary to clarify the effects of sensory contact with male mice on female social behavior. Second, measuring only the SI ratio may limit the sensitivity and interpretability of model [38]. Therefore, future studies should incorporate additional behavioral, physiological, and molecular indicators to more accurately assess stress responses and to further refinement of the construct validity of female adapted VDS models. Despite several limitations, the present findings offer descriptive evidence for the development of a female VDS model and may inform future investigations of stress paradigms in female rodents.
