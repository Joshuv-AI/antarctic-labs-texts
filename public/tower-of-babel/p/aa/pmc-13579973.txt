Classification of 24-h movement behaviour patterns among university students and their relationship with physical fitness: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Yunfeng Song, Ming Liu, Liquan Cao, Yang Liu, Chi Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUniversity students’ 24-h movement behaviours, including moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), light-intensity physical activity (LIPA), sedentary behaviour (SB), and sleep (SLP), are interdependent and may jointly shape physical fitness. However, their combined relationship with physical fitness remains unclear, as most previous studies have focused on single behaviours or variable-centred associations and have rarely identified latent subgroups based on multidimensional behavioural patterns. Therefore, this study aimed to identify latent categories of 24-h movement behaviour patterns among university students using latent profile analysis (LPA), characterise these categories, and further examine their associations with physical fitness.MethodsA total of 5,849 university students aged 18–23 years were recruited from 12 universities in Tianjin, China, including 2,267 males and 3,582 females. Time spent in MVPA, LIPA, SB, and SLP was assessed using the Chinese versions of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Physical fitness was evaluated according to the National Student Physical Fitness Standard (2014 Revised Edition), and total physical fitness score was used as the outcome variable. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted separately by sex to identify 24-h movement behaviour patterns, and the BCH approach was used to compare differences in total physical fitness scores across latent classes.ResultsA four-class model was identified as the optimal solution for both sexes. Among males, the four profiles were low-activity / long-sleep (9.88%), higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary (29.16%), low-activity / high-sedentary (52.89%), and high-activity / low-sedentary (8.07%). Among females, the four profiles were higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary (13.51%), low-activity / long-sleep (12.98%), low-activity / high-sedentary / short-sleep (68.48%), and high-activity / low-sedentary (5.03%). Significant overall differences in total physical fitness scores were observed across latent classes in both males (χ² = 30.435, P < 0.001) and females (χ² = 26.215, P < 0.001). In both sexes, profiles characterised by lower sedentary behaviour and greater daily movement tended to show more favourable physical fitness scores, whereas low-activity and high-sedentary profiles showed poorer performance.ConclusionUniversity students showed clear heterogeneity in 24-h movement behaviour patterns, and these patterns were significantly associated with physical fitness. These findings support the development of sex-specific and profile-based intervention strategies to improve physical fitness among university students.Keywords: 24-h movement behaviour, Compositional data analysis, Latent profile analysis, Physical fitness

Introduction

A healthy lifestyle is generally defined as a set of interrelated and relatively stable health-related behaviours and is an important determinant of health across the life course [1, 2]. For young people, the development of regular physical activity, limited sedentary behaviour (SB), and adequate sleep (SLP) is particularly important, because behavioural patterns established during youth may have long-term effects on subsequent health trajectories [3]. At the same time, non-communicable diseases remain one of the major contributors to the global burden of disease. According to the World Health Organization, non-communicable diseases caused at least 43 million deaths in 2021, accounting for approximately three quarters of all non-pandemic-related deaths worldwide [4]. Therefore, promoting healthy lifestyles among young people is of substantial public health importance.

The university period is a critical transition from family dependence to independent living. It is also an important window for consolidating health behaviours and accumulating health capital [5]. In 2020, 235.3 million students were enrolled in higher education worldwide, and in 75 national systems, more than 50% of the relevant age cohort entered higher education [6]. Evidence from China shows that total higher-education enrolment reached 47.63 million in 2023, with a gross enrolment ratio of 60.2% [7]. However, unhealthy behaviours remain common among university students, particularly insufficient physical activity, prolonged sedentary time, and poor SLP. These behaviours may adversely affect mental health and academic performance and may increase the risk of overweight, obesity, and future non-communicable diseases [8, 9]. Recent evidence from China between 2019 and 2023 further indicates an overall decline in physical fitness among Chinese university students, accompanied by a continued increase in overweight and obesity [10]. These findings highlight the persistence and urgency of physical fitness problems in this population. Meanwhile, recent World Health Organization data show that approximately 31% of adults worldwide did not meet the recommended level of physical activity in 2022, and insufficient physical activity appears to be even more pronounced among university students [11].

In this context, explaining health problems among university students from the perspective of a single health behaviour may be limited. The 24-h movement behaviour framework has therefore received increasing attention [12]. This framework emphasises that moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), light-intensity physical activity (LIPA), SB, and SLP are not independent of one another; rather, they jointly constitute the complete allocation of time within a 24-h day, with a co-dependent and mutually constraining relationship. Previous studies have shown that combinations characterised by higher physical activity, lower sedentary behaviour, and appropriate sleep duration are more favourable for health [13]. Research in university students has likewise demonstrated that 24-h movement behaviour is closely associated with physical fitness, and reallocating time to MVPA is generally beneficial for physical fitness performance [14]. Accordingly, focusing on a single behaviour alone may fail to fully capture the real behavioural characteristics of university students and their health implications.

Despite the growing body of research on healthy lifestyles and physical activity among university students, most existing studies have adopted a variable-centred approach. These studies typically examine linear associations between a single health behaviour, such as physical activity, and outcomes such as body mass index or physical fitness, or analyse correlations among multiple variables [15]. Although such approaches are useful for identifying general associations between variables, they are limited in their ability to identify latent heterogeneous subgroups based on multidimensional behavioural combinations within the population, thereby restricting a deeper understanding of subgroup-specific characteristics and influencing factors. As a person-centred statistical approach, latent profile analysis (LPA) can identify latent subgroups according to individuals’ performance patterns across multiple behavioural dimensions and is therefore better suited to revealing the heterogeneous structure within a population [16]. Accordingly, this study used LPA to explore latent categories of 24-h movement behaviour patterns among male and female university students and further examined their relationships with physical fitness, with the aim of providing evidence for improving physical fitness and developing targeted health promotion strategies among university students.

Methods

Participants

From April to June 2025, a stratified random cluster sampling method was used to recruit university students, defined in the present study as students aged 18–23 years enrolled in higher-education institutions in Tianjin, China. First, six districts were randomly selected from Tianjin using a random number table, and two higher-education institutions were then selected from each district, yielding 12 institutions in total, comprising 11 undergraduate universities and one junior college. Subsequently, classes were stratified by grade within each selected institution, and two classes were randomly selected from each grade. Recruitment was conducted through class-based announcements with the assistance of university teachers and trained research staff. All students in the selected classes were informed of the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality of data before being invited to participate. A total of 5,849 university students were included, comprising 2,267 males and 3,582 females.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18–23 years; (2) enrolled as a full-time student in one of the selected higher-education institutions; (3) self-reported good health, with no major diseases or exercise contraindications, and ability to complete all questionnaire and physical fitness assessments; and (4) voluntary participation with written informed consent. Health status was evaluated using self-reported health information collected before testing, including whether participants had major diseases, recent physical limitations, or contraindications to exercise. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) inability to engage in physical activity within the previous week; (2) severe physical disease, injury, or contraindications to exercise; and (3) incomplete questionnaire data or missing physical fitness indicators.

Measures

Assessment of daily behaviours

The Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and the Chinese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) were used to assess time spent in 24-h movement behaviour. The reliability and validity of these scales have been established in Chinese populations, and both have been widely used in related research [17, 18]. The assessed indicators included daily time spent in MVPA, LIPA, SB, and SLP. Based on the IPAQ scoring criteria and previous studies, participants were classified into low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600 to < 3,000 MET-min/week), and high (≥ 3,000 MET-min/week) physical activity levels [19].

Physical fitness assessment

Physical fitness was assessed using the equipment adopted in the Fifth National Physical Fitness Monitoring programme ( Beijing Xin Donghua Teng, Beijing, China) in accordance with the National Student Physical Fitness Standard (2014 Revised Edition) [20]. The specific test items and procedures were as follows:

Height test: Height was measured using a standardized height measuring instrument. During the test, participants were required to stand barefoot and upright on the base plate of the stadiometer, with the heels, sacrum, and scapular region close to the vertical column. The head was maintained in the horizontal position, with the upper margin of the tragus and the lowest point of the orbital margin aligned. Height was recorded in cm to one decimal place.

Quality control

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of data collection and testing, all survey and testing procedures were conducted by trained professionals from the Key Laboratory of Physical-Medical Integration of Tianjin University of Sport. Before the formal survey, designated staff provided detailed explanations of the procedures, operation methods, and precautions for completing the mobile phone-based online questionnaire and guided participants through the process. An online questionnaire was used to improve efficiency and facilitate data management. After completion, the questionnaire was checked and reviewed immediately by the on-site researchers and submitted only after confirmation of accuracy. The entire survey and testing process lasted approximately 30 min. Questionnaire data were double-entered using EpiData 3.1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 and Mplus version 8.3.

First, to characterise the time-use distribution of 24-h movement behaviour among university students, descriptive analyses were conducted for each compositional part. Geometric means were calculated to describe the time distribution of each behavioural component, and a variation matrix was used to quantify the degree of dispersion between components [21]. Given the constant-sum property of compositional data and the collinearity among components, an isometric log-ratio (ilr) transformation was applied [22]. In addition, weekly activity behaviour data were standardised to 24-h movement behaviour data [23].

Second, LPA was conducted in Mplus 8.3 separately for males and females using time spent in MVPA, LIPA, SB, and SLP within a 24-h day to identify latent profiles of 24-h movement behaviour. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), with lower values indicating better model fit. The Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (LMRT) and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used to determine the optimal number of classes; a significant result (P < 0.05) indicated that the current k-class model fit the data better than the k − 1-class model. Entropy was used to evaluate classification accuracy, with values closer to 1 indicating higher classification precision [24]. The final model was determined by considering overall fit indices together with substantive interpretability. The latent profiles of 24-h movement behaviour were then treated as the independent variable, total physical fitness score was treated as the outcome variable, and the Bolck–Croon–Hagenaars (BCH) approach was used for comparisons. A two-sided α level of 0.05 was adopted.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

A total of 5,849 university students were included, including 2,267 males and 3,582 females. The mean age of the participants was 19.2 ± 1.2 years. The mean height and weight were 166.1 ± 8.5 cm and 60.9 ± 10.7 kg, respectively. Overall, 62.3% of participants were from urban areas, 5.9% reported current smoking, and 22.4% reported alcohol drinking. First-year students accounted for the largest proportion of the sample. The mean total physical fitness score was 70.8 ± 9.7, with female students showing a higher mean score than male students (Table 1).

Latent classification of 24-h movement behaviour patterns

To explore the latent structure of the compositional data, latent profile models with two to five classes were fitted sequentially, and multiple fit indices were used to evaluate model performance (Table 3). Among male university students, AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased continuously as the number of classes increased, indicating progressive improvement in model fit. The LMRT and BLRT were significant for the 2-, 3-, 4-, and 5-class models (all P < 0.001), suggesting that each current model fit better than the preceding model. However, model selection also needed to take substantive interpretability and sample representativeness into account. In the 5-class model, the smallest class accounted for less than 5% of the total sample. Such a very small latent subgroup may reflect insufficient representativeness, reduced model stability, and limited practical value for subsequent analysis and application. Considering the fit indices and practical interpretability together, the 4-class model (C1–C4) was selected as the optimal model for male university students. Specifically, C1 was characterised by relatively low MVPA (M = 34.47 min) and the longest SLP duration (M = 784.56 min); C2 by the longest LIPA duration (M = 434.30 min) and relatively low SB (M = 394.82 min); C3 by relatively low MVPA (M = 33.24 min) and the longest SB duration (M = 608.28 min); and C4 by the longest MVPA duration (M = 130.19 min) and relatively low SB (M = 395.71 min). These four profiles were therefore labelled as low-activity / long-sleep, higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary, low-activity / high-sedentary, and high-activity / low-sedentary, accounting for 9.88%, 29.16%, 52.89%, and 8.07% of the male sample, respectively.

Among female university students, AIC, BIC, and aBIC also decreased continuously from the 2-class to the 5-class models, and both LMRT and BLRT indicated that models with more classes fit better than those with fewer classes. The 4-class model had an entropy value of 0.962, indicating high classification accuracy and clear separation between classes. Taking model fit, class separation, class size, and interpretability into account, the 4-class model (C1–C4) was selected as the optimal model for female university students. Specifically, C1 was characterised by the longest LIPA duration (M = 406.49 min) and relatively short SB duration (M = 357.78 min); C2 by relatively low MVPA (M = 35.25 min) and relatively long SLP duration (M = 503.25 min); C3 by relatively low MVPA (M = 21.18 min), the longest SB duration (M = 711.47 min), and the shortest SLP duration (M = 369.01 min); and C4 by the longest MVPA duration (M = 168.57 min) and relatively low SB (M = 306.26 min). Associations between 24-h movement behaviour patterns and physical fitness

The BCH approach was used to compare differences in total physical fitness scores across latent classes of activity behaviour in male and female university students (Table 4). Significant overall differences in total physical fitness scores were observed across latent classes among males (χ² = 30.435, P < 0.01). Pairwise comparisons showed that the higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary group had significantly higher total physical fitness scores than the low-activity / long-sleep group (χ² = 11.102, P < 0.01) and the low-activity / high-sedentary group (χ² = 14.158, P < 0.01). The high-activity / low-sedentary group also had significantly higher scores than the low-activity / long-sleep group (χ² = 15.824, P < 0.01) and the low-activity / high-sedentary group (χ² = 17.123, P < 0.01). No significant difference was observed between the low-activity / long-sleep group and the low-activity / high-sedentary group (P = 0.654), nor between the high-activity / low-sedentary group and the higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary group (P = 0.053).

Discussion

This study focused on 24-h movement behaviour among university students, used latent profile analysis to identify behavioural combination patterns, and further compared total physical fitness scores across different latent classes. The results showed that both male and female university students could be classified into four latent behavioural profiles and that significant differences in total physical fitness scores were observed across profiles. These findings suggest that 24-h movement behaviours among university students are not distributed as a homogeneous pattern; rather, they are organised into multiple heterogeneous time-allocation profiles jointly characterised by MVPA, LIPA, SB, and SLP, which supports the value of person-centred approaches for identifying behavioural heterogeneity in this population [25].

Compared with previous studies, the number of profiles identified in the present study was greater than the three-profile structure reported by Wang et al. for healthy lifestyles among university students, and this discrepancy may be attributable primarily to differences in the behavioural domains assessed and the analytical framework employed [15]. Although previous studies have provided important evidence on lifestyle patterns in this population, they have often used broad lifestyle constructs that combine heterogeneous health-related domains, including diet, sleep, smoking, alcohol consumption, and other dimensions. This broad construct-based approach may obscure the specific time-use composition of 24-h movement behaviours and may limit the ability to identify more refined behavioural subgroups defined by daily activity allocation. In contrast, the present study focused specifically on the 24-h movement behaviours, and further adopted sex-stratified latent profile analysis. This approach enabled a more detailed characterisation of latent heterogeneity in daily behavioural time allocation. In the present study, male students were mainly concentrated in the “higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary” and “low-activity / high-sedentary” profiles, whereas female students were predominantly classified into the “low-activity / high-sedentary / short-sleep” profile, suggesting that the clustering of unfavourable behaviours may be more pronounced among female students. This finding is broadly consistent with previous evidence showing marked sex differences in lifestyle behaviours and movement patterns among university students [26]. Existing studies have reported that male university students generally engage in higher levels of physical activity, whereas female students are more likely to report poorer sleep quality, more sleep problems, and less favourable health behaviours overall [26, 27]. Accordingly, the high prevalence of the “low-activity / high-sedentary / short-sleep” profile among female students in the present study may reflect the combined influence of academic pressure, emotional burden, and disrupted daily routines, which may predispose female students to an unfavourable behavioural pattern characterised by low activity, prolonged sedentary time, and insufficient sleep [28]. In addition, sedentary behaviour is already highly prevalent in university populations. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that university students spend, on average, 7.29 h per day in sedentary behaviour, and both self-reported and objectively measured data have consistently shown sedentary time to be high in this age group [29]. Therefore, the identification of high-sedentary profiles in both male and female students in the present study is not unexpected, but rather reflects a realistic and widespread behavioural feature of contemporary college life.

With regard to physical fitness outcomes, the present study found that the “high-activity / low-sedentary” and “higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary” profiles tended to show relatively higher total physical fitness scores in both sexes, whereas the “low-activity / high-sedentary” and “low-activity / high-sedentary / short-sleep” profiles performed worse. These findings are highly consistent with the results reported by Yang et al., who demonstrated, using compositional data analysis, that increasing MVPA and reducing sedentary behaviour were key behavioural characteristics associated with better physical fitness among university students [30]. Notably, the present study further showed that the “higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary” profile did not differ significantly from the “high-activity / low-sedentary” profile in some comparisons of total physical fitness. This finding suggests that, among university students, improvements in physical fitness may not depend solely on very high levels of MVPA; increasing LIPA while simultaneously reducing sedentary behaviour may also yield meaningful health benefits. This interpretation is supported by previous evidence indicating that LIPA may contribute positively to selected components of physical fitness, including cardiopulmonary and endurance-related indicators, particularly when it replaces prolonged sedentary time [14]. Therefore, campus-based interventions may need to move beyond a narrow focus on structured moderate-to-vigorous exercise and instead promote a broader strategy of increasing total daily movement and compressing sedentary time across the whole day [31].

The present study also found that among male students, the “low-activity / long-sleep” profile did not show a clear advantage in physical fitness over the “low-activity / high-sedentary” profile, suggesting that longer sleep duration alone may be insufficient to offset the adverse effects of inadequate physical activity [32]. In other words, although sleep is an essential restorative behaviour, adequate sleep duration cannot fully compensate for the absence of sufficient movement stimuli, particularly MVPA and accumulated daily activity, in relation to physical fitness development. This finding extends previous evidence by indicating that the health value of sleep should be interpreted within the overall 24-h behavioural composition, rather than in isolation [33]. By contrast, female students in the “low-activity / high-sedentary / short-sleep” profile showed the poorest physical fitness performance, indicating that the combination of short sleep and prolonged sedentary time may further amplify the negative effects of low activity [34]. Previous studies have shown that prolonged sedentary time is significantly associated with poorer sleep quality and shorter sleep duration, while insufficient sleep may increase fatigue, reduce motivation for physical activity, and impair behavioural self-regulation [35]. As a result, female students with this clustered behavioural pattern may enter a self-reinforcing cycle of prolonged sitting, insufficient sleep, and persistent inactivity, which may ultimately contribute to poorer physical fitness outcomes.

The present findings have important implications for public health practice in university settings. First, health promotion strategies should not rely solely on uniform recommendations to increase exercise, but should instead adopt profile-specific and sex-sensitive approaches [36]. For students in the “low-activity / high-sedentary” and “low-activity / high-sedentary / short-sleep” profiles, intervention priorities should include reducing prolonged sitting, improving daily sleep schedules, and gradually increasing routine movement across the day [37]. For students in the “higher light-intensity activity / low-sedentary” profile, intervention efforts may focus on facilitating progression towards higher levels of MVPA while maintaining relatively low sedentary exposure [38]. Such a differentiated intervention strategy may be more realistic and more effective than a one-size-fits-all approach for improving physical fitness among university students.

Limitations

Despite enriching the theoretical and practical understanding of 24-h movement behaviour patterns and their associations with physical fitness among university students from 12 universities in Tianjin, this study has several limitations. First, the sample was restricted to universities in Tianjin, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to students in other regions of China. Second, several behavioural variables were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which may have introduced recall and information bias. In particular, although the IPAQ is widely used, it may overestimate physical activity compared with objective measures such as accelerometers [39]. It also captures total sedentary time but does not assess the patterning of SB, including prolonged sitting bouts or breaks in sedentary time, which may be important for health outcomes. Similarly, sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI, which reflects subjective sleep quality and may be affected by recall bias and individual differences in sleep perception. The lack of objective measures, such as accelerometry, actigraphy, or wearable-device-based monitoring, means that findings related to physical activity, SB, and SLP should be interpreted with caution. Finally, although participants were drawn from multiple academic programmes, we did not classify students by degree course or academic discipline. Differences between health-related and non-health-related programmes may influence health behaviours and should be considered in future studies.

Conclusions

University students showed clear heterogeneity in 24-h movement behaviour patterns, and these patterns were significantly associated with physical fitness. Profiles characterised by lower sedentary behaviour and greater daily movement were linked to more favourable physical fitness, whereas low-activity and high-sedentary profiles were associated with poorer outcomes. These findings support the use of sex-sensitive, profile-based health promotion strategies to improve physical fitness among university students.
