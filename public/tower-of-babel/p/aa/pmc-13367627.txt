Impact of Postoperative Chronic Back Pain Syndrome on the Incidence of Psychiatric and Brain Disorders, and All-Cause Mortality [Letter]
Authors: JiaLe Li
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the nationwide retrospective cohort study by Park et al, which examined the association of postoperative chronic back pain syndrome (PCBPS) with psychiatric disorders, brain disorders, and all-cause mortality among patients undergoing lumbar spine surgery1 The authors should be commended for addressing an important postoperative condition using a large national claims database. However, several aspects of the study design and analysis may require clarification before these associations are interpreted as evidence of an independent long-term prognostic role of PCBPS.

First, the temporal alignment between cohort entry, exposure definition, and outcome follow-up is central to the interpretation of this study. Park et al defined PCBPS using three post-index criteria: a diagnosis code for post-lumbar surgery syndrome, gabapentinoid prescription for at least 12 weeks after surgery, or reoperation after the index lumbar surgeryThese criteria represent events or treatment patterns that occur after the initial operation rather than baseline characteristics available at cohort entry. If individuals were classified as exposed from the date of index surgery on the basis of information observed later during follow-up, immortal time bias may have been introduced.2,3 Patients must remain alive and under observation long enough to satisfy a PCBPS definition; assigning this pre-exposure person-time to the exposed group can distort hazard ratio estimates. It would therefore be important to clarify whether PCBPS status was handled as a time-varying exposure, with person-time allocated according to the date on which each PCBPS criterion was first fulfilled. Time-updated Cox models, landmark analyses, or a target-trial framework would help align exposure assignment with the start of follow-up and outcome ascertainment.

Second, competing risks deserve consideration when evaluating nonfatal outcomes. The authors assessed psychiatric disorders and brain disorders, including stroke, Parkinson’s disease, and dementia, while also analyzing all-cause mortality. In this postoperative population, death may preclude the subsequent occurrence or diagnosis of nonfatal neurologic and psychiatric outcomes. Standard Cox models that treat death as non-informative censoring may therefore estimate associations that do not directly correspond to clinically interpretable cumulative incidence. Complementary competing-risk analyses, including cause-specific hazard models, Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models, and cumulative incidence functions, would provide a more complete understanding of these outcome patterns.4

Third, selection related to health check-up data may influence both generalizability and internal validity. The study flowchart shows that many patients were excluded because health check-up records within two years were unavailable or because health check-up results were missing. Because lifestyle factors and body mass index were derived from health check-up data, the final analytical cohort may have overrepresented individuals with greater engagement in preventive healthcare or better access to routine medical assessment. This selection process could be related to both the likelihood of PCBPS classification and the subsequent detection of psychiatric or neurologic diagnoses. Comparing included and excluded patients, applying inverse probability weighting, or conducting sensitivity analyses for missing health check-up information would help assess the potential influence of this selection mechanism.5

Fourth, the clinical interpretation of modest relative effects would benefit from absolute risk estimates. Several reported associations, particularly those for dementia, overall brain disorders, and all-cause mortality, were statistically significant but relatively small in magnitude. In a cohort exceeding 600,000 patients, statistical significance alone does not necessarily indicate substantial clinical importance. Reporting absolute risk differences and excess events per 1000 patients would allow readers to better evaluate the practical relevance of the findings. This is particularly important because PCBPS is a heterogeneous postoperative pain condition, and its incidence and clinical presentation may vary according to surgical procedure, clinical setting, and operational definition.6

In summary, Park et al provide valuable population-level evidence regarding long-term outcomes after lumbar spine surgery Nevertheless, potential time-zero misalignment, immortal time bias, competing risks, health check-up-related selection, and limited contextualization of modest effect sizes should be addressed before PCBPS is regarded as an independent predictor of subsequent neuropsychiatric disorders and mortality. Additional analyses incorporating time-varying exposure modeling, competing-risk methods, and sensitivity analyses for health check-up-related selection would substantially strengthen the causal interpretation and clinical applicability of this important study.
