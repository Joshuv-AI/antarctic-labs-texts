Multi-omics analysis reveals the association of cesarean delivery with altered gut microbial profiles and a Th2-biased immune response in neonates
Authors: Chunhui Lai, Jiangang Zhang, Yu Xiong, Yijin Wang, Zhenxing Liu, Mingfang Shi, Shenglin Ye, Jianghui Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gut microbiota plays a crucial role in providing essential stimulatory signals for the development of the immune system during the early stages of life. Cesarean delivery is associated with altered vertical microbial transmission and may affect early immune priming. Currently, only a limited number of studies have investigated the interactions among gut microbiota, fecal metabolites, and immune function in neonates delivered by cesarean section, which underscores the necessity for further research.MethodsWe performed metagenomic sequencing and untargeted metabolomics to compare the gut microbiota and fecal metabolites of neonates born through cesarean delivery (n = 18) and vaginal delivery (n = 20). RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was utilized to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Immune profiling involved flow cytometry analysis to determine the proportions of Th1 and Th2 cells, ELISA-based quantification of plasma IFN-γ, IL-12p70, IL-4, and IL-10, and assessment of STAT4 and STAT6 expression via ELISA and Western blot. Multi-omics integration was applied to elucidate the systemic impact of cesarean delivery on the neonatal gut microbiome, metabolome, and immune system.ResultsThe composition and functional features of the gut microbiota, and fecal metabolite profile, were significantly altered in Cesarean group. PBMC gene expression also showed marked differences, presenting a Th2-biased immune response and enrichment of genes associated with systemic lupus erythematosus and primary immunodeficiency. Flow cytometry and ELISA confirmed a Th1/Th2 imbalance, while Western blot revealed decreased STAT4 and increased STAT6 expression in the Cesarean group. Multi-omics analysis indicates that Bacteroides sp. is associated with alterations in fecal metabolite in neonates delivered via cesarean section. The reduced abundance of Bacteroides sp. and Bacteroides fragilis correlated with Th1/Th2 dysregulation. Additionally, gut microbiota changes were correlated with variations in the host oxidative phosphorylation pathway via fecal phosphate levels.ConclusionsThis multi-omics study reveals an association between the mode of delivery and distinct gut microbiota structure, fecal metabolite profiles, and immune development during early life. This provides a framework for investigating the potential connection between early-life immune programming and mode of delivery.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07988-4.

Background

Microorganisms colonize the human body shortly after birth and engage in a lifelong symbiotic relationship with the host, contributing to mutual physiological benefits [1–4]. The gut microbiota and its metabolites play a vital role in the programming of essential bodily systems—such as the immune and central nervous systems—during critical developmental windows, potentially exerting structural and functional impacts throughout life [5–7]. Perturbations resulting from the absence of appropriate microbial signals during this sensitive period may lead to long-term alterations in immune function [8, 9]. Emerging evidence confirms that cesarean delivery disrupts the vertical transmission of specific microbial strains from mother to neonate, which is essential for proper immune priming [10–13]. Cesarean delivery has been shown to alter the neonatal gut microbiota, thereby influencing host interactions through mechanisms involving intestinal epithelial maturation, regulation of the gut barrier, and modulation of the innate immune system early in life, ultimately shaping phenotypic outcomes related to infant metabolism [14].

On a global scale, the rate of cesarean deliveries carried out without medical indication persists in increasing, and projections indicate that 28.5% of all births will be via cesarean delivery by 2030 [15]. This trend has raised concerns about potential long-term health implications for infants, including an elevated risk of immune-related disorders such as allergies and autoimmune diseases [11, 16, 17]. Currently, there are no well-established or standardized clinical treatments for correcting microbial imbalances associated with cesarean birth. Although interventions such as probiotics and dietary modifications have been explored [18, 19], the mechanistic links between cesarean delivery, gut microbiota, and immune development remain poorly understood. Moreover, available literature on the relationship between gut microbiota, fecal metabolites, and immune function in cesarean-delivered neonates is limited, underscoring the need for further research in this critical area [20, 21].

The gut microbiota provides essential stimuli for immune maturation [22]. Disruption of specific microbial communities due to cesarean delivery has been shown to interfere with maternal–neonatal transmission and subsequent immune activation [23]. The gut microbiota comprises a diverse assemblage of microorganisms that perform a range of biological functions, including immune regulation, metabolic activity, and pathogen defense [24]. For example, Bacteroides fragilis has been demonstrated to restore dysregulated Th1/Th2 balance in germ-free mice through TLR2-mediated NF-κB activation [25]. Recent studies emphasize the association between gut microbial composition and immune development, particularly among cesarean-born infants, who frequently exhibit altered microbial profiles compared to those delivered vaginally [11, 26, 27]. However, current understanding of the interactions between cesarean delivery, the gut microbiota, and immune function remains incomplete, and further investigation is required to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Unraveling how gut microbiota influence immune development and function is crucial for developing targeted strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of cesarean delivery on neonatal health.

In this study, we applied a multi-omics approach to investigate the effects of cesarean delivery on the neonatal gut microbiota, fecal metabolome, and immune function (Fig. 1). This provides a framework for investigating the potential connection between early-life immune programming and long-term health outcomes. We found that Bacteroides sp. is associated with alterations in fecal metabolite in neonates delivered via cesarean section. The reduced abundance of Bacteroides sp. and Bacteroides fragilis correlated with Th1/Th2 dysregulation. Additionally, gut microbiota changes were correlated with variations in the host oxidative phosphorylation pathway via fecal phosphate levels. In summary, cesarean delivery is association with a distinct developmental trajectory of the neonatal gut-immune axis during early life.

Methods

Study subjects

Participants were recruited between February and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) full-term singleton neonates; (2) birth weight > 2500 g; (3) no postnatal antibiotic exposure; and (4) no history of infection or neonatal asphyxia. Exclusion criteria included: (1) meconium-stained amniotic fluid, birth injury, asphyxia, or any Apgar score < 7 at birth; (2) maternal complications during pregnancy; and (3) prolonged use of probiotics or antibiotics by mothers during pregnancy or lactation. According to the mode of delivery, neonates were assigned to either a Vaginal group or a Cesarean group (all elective procedures). Research has elucidated that the influence of delivery mode on the composition of neonatal gut microbiota reaches its pinnacle approximately one week postpartum and gradually diminishes with advancing age [28]. Therefore, we chose to collect samples approximately one week after the childbirth as the designated time point. Clinical data—including sex, age, birth weight, gestational age, and feeding mode—were collected for all subjects.

Sample collection

The stool samples were freshly collected from each participant using sterile tubes and stored at − 80 °C until further processing. Peripheral blood was drawn into EDTA-coated tubes. A portion of each sample was centrifuged at 2000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min to separate plasma, which was then stored at − 80 °C. The remaining blood was used for PBMC isolation via human peripheral blood lymphocyte separation medium (P8610, Solarbio, China). The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanning Second People’s Hospital (approval no. Y2024126). Written informed consent was obtained from the parent or legal guardian of each enrolled neonate.

Metagenomic sequencing and data analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using the PF Mag-Bind Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA), and its quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The DNA was fragmented to approximately 350 bp using a focused ultrasonicator (M220, Covaris, USA). The fragmented DNA was ligated with Y-shaped adapters, and adapter dimers were removed using magnetic bead–based purification. The library template was enriched through PCR amplification. Single-stranded DNA fragments were generated through sodium hydroxide denaturation to finalize library construction. DNA clusters were generated via bridge PCR, and the resulting amplicons were linearized into single strands. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina, USA). Group differences in alpha diversity indices were assessed using the t-test. Beta diversity based on Bray–Curtis distance was visualized via nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), and significance was tested using analysis of similarity (ANOSIM). Differential species and functional features were identified with linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), using a logarithmic LDA score threshold of 3.5. Co-occurrence networks among the top 50 most abundant genera were constructed using Spearman correlation (|r| ≥ 0.5, p < 0.05) and visualized in Cytoscape.

Metabonomic analysis based on liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC/MS)

Fecal samples (25 mg) were homogenized with beads in 500 µL of extraction solvent. After homogenization, ultrasonication, and centrifugation, the supernatant was collected into injection vials. Chromatographic separation was performed on a ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, USA). MS1 and MS2 data were acquired using a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Exploris 120, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Metabolite Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) analysis was conducted to visualize discrimination between cesarean and vaginal delivery groups and to compute the variable importance in the projection (VIP) values for prioritizing differentially abundant metabolites. Variables with VIP scores > 1.0 were recognized as significant contributors to group separation. Differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were chosen through multivariate statistical analysis, where a P value < 0.05 from the Student’s t-test and a VIP score > 1 were regarded as significantly different [29]. Multiple testing correction was not applied to the metabolomics data across the full set of detected metabolites. A post hoc power analysis was carried out utilizing the pwr package in R.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was isolated from PBMCs using TRIzol reagent (Cat#15596026CN, Invitrogen Life Technologies, USA) through phase separation, precipitation, and washing steps. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and its integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB#E7770, New England Biolabs, USA) for. Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and randomly fragmented. The fragmented mRNA was reverse-transcribed to synthesize cDNA, which was then purified and ligated with sequencing adapters. cDNA fragments (approximately 400–500 bp) were size-selected, PCR-amplified, and purified to generate sequencing-ready libraries. Library quality was evaluated with an Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit (5067 − 4626) on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) to ensure quality standards. Expression levels were normalized using the FPKM method. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3), with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those showing |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and an adjusted P value < 0.05. The RNASeqPower (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/RNASeqPower.html) was employed to estimate the statistical power for the detection of DEGs in RNA-Seq experiments. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0). All analyses were performed on the GenesCloud platform (https://www.genescloud.cn). Multi-omics analysis, which is based on a correlation-based network, was derived from Spearman correlation analysis, integration of common pathways between the transcriptome and metabolome, and STRING protein-protein interaction analysis.

Flow cytometry

Isolated PBMCs were seeded in a 24-well plate and stimulated with 2 µL of leukocyte activation cocktail containing BD GolgiPlug™ (Cat# 550583, BD Biosciences, USA), followed by incubation for 4.5 h at 37 ℃ under 5% CO2. Cells were then collected, pelleted by centrifugation, and resuspended in PBS. To prevent nonspecific antibody binding, cells were treated with 1 µg of Fc receptor blocking reagent (Cat# 564219, BD Biosciences, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. The cell suspension was divided into two tubes: one served as a negative control, and the other as the experimental sample. The sample tube was stained with 0.5 µg of CD3-PC5.5 (Cat# 552852, BD Biosciences, USA) and 0.5 µg of CD4-FITC (Cat# 561005, BD Biosciences, USA), gently vortexed, and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Unbound antibodies were removed by washing with 1 mL PBS. Both tubes were subsequently fixed and permeabilized with 250 µL of Fixable/Perm Solution (Cat# 554722, BD Biosciences, USA) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by a wash with 1 mL Perm/Wash Buffer (Cat# 554723, BD Biosciences, USA). The sample tube was then intracellularly stained with 0.5 µg of IFN-γ-PE (Cat# 562016, BD Biosciences, USA) and 0.5 µg of IL-4-APC (Cat# 500811, BioLegend, USA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, cells were washed once with Perm/Wash Buffer and resuspended in 200–300 µL PBS. Samples were acquired within 2 h on a FACSCanto flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Plasma samples stored at − 80 °C were thawed at room temperature for 30 min prior to analysis. IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10, and IL-12p70 levels were measured using the following commercial ELISA kits: QuantiCyto® Human IFN-γ ELISA Kit, QuantiCyto® Human IL-4 ELISA Kit, QuantiCyto® Human IL-10 ELISA Kit, and QuantiCyto® Human IL-12p70 ELISA Kit (all from Neobioscience, Shenzhen, China). STAT4 and STAT6 concentrations were determined using human STAT4 and STAT6 ELISA kits (Quanzhou Ruixin Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Quanzhou, China), respectively. All absorbance readings were obtained using the microplate reader module (HB-900E, JiaXing CRED Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., China) of a fully automated ELISA system at wavelengths of 450–630 nm as specified by the respective kit protocols.

Western blot analysis

PBMCs were lysed in RIPA buffer (Cat# C05-01001, Bioss, USA) and incubated on ice for 20 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay kit (Cat# C05-02001, Bioss, USA). Samples were normalized to the same concentration with PBS. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5× loading buffer (Cat# C05-03001, Bioss, USA), denatured by heating at 95–100 °C for 5–10 min, and separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels (Cat# SPGT001, Biolight, China). After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a membrane, blocked, and probed with primary antibodies against STAT4 (Cat# 2653T, CST, USA) and STAT6 (Cat# 5397T, CST, USA), followed by incubation with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using ECL substrate (Cat# C05-07004, Bioss, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc™ XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH (Cat# 2118T, CST, USA) was used as the loading control. Band intensity was quantified using Image Lab software (v6.0), and relative expression of STAT4 and STAT6 was normalized to GAPDH.

Statistical analysis of clinical data

GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was applied for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean (standard deviation, SEM) or median (inter quartile range, IQR) for quantitative variables, and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Differences in clinical indices among groups were determined using pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and Student’s t test (for normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data) for quantitative variables. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Information on cohort neonates

Owing to missing data or the withdrawal of study subjects, 38 healthy neonates were successfully enrolled. Among them, 18 patients were in the Cesarean group, and 20 patients were in the Vaginal group. Both groups received mixed feeding and the proportion of breastfed milk in each group is summarized in Table 1. There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of general clinical data, including sex, age, gestational age, birth weight, Apgar score, and feeding mode (P > 0.05), indicating that the groups were well-matched at baseline. However, it is acknowledged that potential residual confounders, including unmeasured maternal factors (such as microbiota, diet, body mass index, stress levels, and lifestyle) and the hospital environment, cannot be entirely excluded.

The Th1/Th2 immune imbalance in neonates born via cesarean delivery

We have confirmed that alterations in the gut microbiota structure of neonates born via cesarean delivery are closely related to an imbalance in the neonatal IFN-γ/IL-4 ratio [26]. This finding was further validated by flow cytometry, which demonstrated a significant reduction in the proportion of Th1 cells and a concomitant increase in Th2 cells in the peripheral blood of neonates delivered via cesarean delivery compared to those delivered vaginally, resulting in a markedly altered Th1/Th2 ratio (Fig. 2A). This immunological imbalance was further supported by ELISA quantification of plasma cytokines, which revealed significantly decreased levels of IFN-γ and IL-12p70, as well as a reduced IFN-γ/IL-4 ratio, while levels of IL-4 and IL-10 were elevated (Fig. 2B-F). Collectively, these findings provide robust evidence of a Th1/Th2 immune imbalance in neonates born by cesarean delivery.

Cesarean delivery alters the composition and functional profile of the gut microbiota in neonates

There were 150 genera unique to the Cesarean group, 203 genera specific to the Vaginal delivery group, and 372 genera shared by both groups (Fig. 3A). There was no statistically significant difference in the total abundance of phyla-level gut microbiota between the two groups of neonates (Fig S1 A-B). However, the Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratio was significantly elevate in the Cesarean group (Fig. 3B), indicating a potential dysbiosis in the gut microbiota of cesarean-delivered neonates. The proportions of Escherichia and Bacteroides in the Cesarean group decreased, whereas the proportions of Klebsiella, Pantoea, and Streptococcus increased (Fig. 3C). Alpha diversity analysis revealed no significant differences in the Sobs, Chao, Ace, or Shannon indices of the neonatal gut microbiota between the two groups (Fig S2). However, there was a significant difference in the Simpson index, which was lower in the Cesarean group than in the Vaginal group (Fig. 3D). This finding indicates that the evenness of the neonate gut microbiota in the Cesarean group was lower than that in the Vaginal group. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis indicated that the structure of the gut microbial community in the Cesarean group changed significantly (Fig. 3E). LEfSe analysis revealed that the Cesarean group was enriched with five bacterial taxa, whereas the Vaginal group exhibited enrichment of ten bacterial taxa (Fig. 3F). Genera co-occurrence network revealed that the Cesarean group was absence of Bacteroidota and Verrucomicrobiota (Fig S3). To quantify the specific differences in the gut microbial networks of the two groups, we calculated the number of edges (connections) and nodes (genera) of the microbial networks. The results revealed 102 overlapping edges between the Cesarean and Vaginal groups, 134 edges unique to the Vaginal group, and 103 edges unique to the Cesarean group (Fig S4 A). Additionally, the closeness centrality at various nodes (genera) of the microbial networks differed between the two groups (Fig S4 B). To assess the functional contributions of the gut microbiota, we selected the top 10 most abundant bacterial genera for analysis. The results revealed that the functional architecture of the gut microbiota differed significantly between the two groups (Fig. 3G). Specifically, compared with the Vaginal group, the Cesarean group showed a reduced contribution of Escherichia, while the contribution of Klebsiella, Streptococcus, and Lacticaseibacillus were increased. Notably, the Cesarean group lacked detectable functional contribution from Bacteroides and was instead characterized by the Pantoea. In summary, the structure of the gut microbiota of neonates delivered by cesarean delivery has changed significantly, with reduced evenness and evident microecological dysbiosis.

Cesarean delivery alters the intestinal metabolic profile of neonates

We further employed untargeted metabolomics to investigate differences in fecal metabolites between the two groups of neonates. A total of 227 significantly DEMs were identified, with 22 upregulated and 205 downregulated in the Cesarean group relative to the Vaginal group (Fig. 4A and Table S1). Among these, 49 DEMs showed changes of more than 4-fold (Table S2). In the Cesarean group samples, the contents of amine compounds such as tetradecylamine, tetrabutylammonium cations, and pentadecylamine were greater, whereas the contents of amino acids and lipid compounds such as Lys-Val and barogenin were lower (Fig. S5). The results of a matchstick analysis of DEMs indicated that lipids and lipid-like molecules were significantly downregulated in the Cesarean group, whereas organic nitrogen compounds were significantly upregulated in the gut of the Cesarean group (Fig. 4B). These findings clearly and robustly demonstrate that cesarean delivery is associated with alterations in the intestinal metabolic profile of neonates. KEGG pathway annotation was performed for the DEMs, leading to the assignment of 14 DEMs to 45 KEGG pathways (Table S3). These DEMs were mapped onto different KEGG pathways (Fig. 4C). These 12 KEGG pathways, which were found to be responsive, spanned four functional categories: Environmental Information Processing, Metabolism, Organismal Systems, and Cellular Processes. Based on the observed effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.768) and actual sample size (n = 38), a post hoc power analysis was carried out. The analysis indicated that the achieved power was 63.3%.

Associations between the gut microbiome and fecal metabolites

Increasing evidence has demonstrated a strong association between gut microbiome and fecal metabolites [30, 31]. The interplay between fecal metabolites and the gut microbiome has significantly advanced the development and application of multi-omics approaches in biomedical research. To investigate the potential associations between alterations in the gut microbiome induce by different delivery modes and their corresponding metabolic products, a Spearman correlation matrix was constructed (Fig. 4D). The results indicated that the abundance of most species such as Escherichia coli, Escherichia sp., Bacteroides sp., Phocaeicola vulgatus and Bacteroides fragilis, were positively correlated with the level of myo-Inositol、Lactaldehyde、Cys-Gly、1-(1Z-Octadecenyl)-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine、Cholesteryl sulfate and Lithocholate_3-O-glucuronide. A regulatory network integrating the gut microbiome, DEMs, and signaling pathways was constructed to elucidate their interactive roles in the biological process (Fig. S6). The results revealed that Bacteroides sp. exhibited the highest number of interactions with DEMs, showing associations with nine DEMs. These metabolites are involved in 22 KEGG pathways, indicating that Bacteroides sp. may be a key microbial taxon strongly associated with metabolic variation. Collectively, these results indicate that gut microbiome dysbiosis associated with cesarean delivery is correlated with alterations in fecal metabolite profiles.

Transcriptome expression profile differences between vaginally and cesarean-delivered neonates

RNA-Seq was performed to identify DEGs in PBMCs from the two neonatal cohorts. The PCA results revealed a clear separation between the groups (Fig. 5A). The volcano plot illustrates the distribution of up-regulated and down-regulated genes in the Cesarean group (Fig. 5B, statistical power = 0.70). The top 10 up-regulated and down-regulated mRNAs in the Cesarean group are presented in Table 2. Gene expression clustering analysis further confirmed distinct transcriptomic profiles between the two groups (Fig. 5C). The biological functions of the DEGs were analyzed using GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. GO analysis results indicated that the significantly enriched biological process (BP) included positive regulation of phosphorylation, positive regulation of phosphorus metabolic process and positive regulation of protein phosphorylation, among others (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the DEGs were predominantly associated with Glycosphingolipid biosynthesis - lacto and neolacto series, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway and Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 5E). Focusing on immune differentiation, we observed that DEGs associated with Th1 cell differentiation (e.g., IFNGR1, STAT4) were upregulated in the Vaginal group, whereas genes related to Th2 cell differentiation (e.g., MAF, IL2RG, IL5RA, STAT6) were upregulated in the Cesarean group (Fig. 5F). GSEA showed significant positive enrichment for systemic lupus erythematosus and primary immunodeficiency in the Cesarean group (Fig. 5G-H). Given that STAT4 and STAT6 are critical transcription factors involved in Th1 and Th2 cell signaling and the regulation of associated gene expression [32], we further evaluated their protein expression. Western blot and ELISA analyses consistently showed that STAT4 expression was significantly downregulated, whereas STAT6 expression was upregulated in the Cesarean group (Fig. 5I-K). Collectively, these results suggest that cesarean delivery is associated with a distinct transcriptomic and immune phenotype in neonatal PBMCs, characterized by a preferential skewing toward Th2-like responses.

Association between Th1/Th2 cell imbalance and the gut microbiome in neonates born via cesarean delivery

Gut microbiota play an integral role in the development and regulation of host immunity, serving as a critical factor in neonatal immune system development [22]. We conducted multiomics analyses incorporating metagenomic, metabolomic, and transcriptomic data to construct a regulatory network integrating gut microbiome, DEMs, KEGG pathways, and DEGs (Fig. 6A). The network suggested that several gut microbiota, including Enterococcus sp., Escherichia coli, and Escherichia sp., were associated with variations in the host’s oxidative phosphorylation pathway, correlating with phosphate levels. Furthermore, Klebsiella aerogenes and Streptococcus sp. were linked to host pathways such as aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, dopaminergic synapse, melanogenesis, and prolactin signaling through tyrosine. To further investigate the correlations between Th1/Th2 cell imbalance in neonates born via cesarean delivery and the gut microbiome, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between gut microbial abundance and the levels of IL12p-70, IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10, STAT4, and STAT6. Notably, the gut microbiota, which were significantly more abundant in the Vaginal group, including Bacteroides sp., Bacteroides fragilis, Escherichia sp., and Escherichia coli (Fig. S7), exhibited strong positive correlations with a higher IFN-γ/IL-4 (Fig. 6B). Collectively, these findings provide robust evidence that mode of delivery influences the development of the host immune system through alterations in gut microbiota composition. Collectively, these findings support an association where the mode of delivery is linked to the development of the host immune system, potentially mediated by alterations in gut microbiota composition.

Discussion

The interactions among the gut microbiota, host metabolism, and immune function are complex. To explore whether cesarean delivery is associated with alterations in neonatal metabolism and immune system development, we conducted an integrated multi-omics study. Metagenomic sequencing and untargeted metabolomics were conducted on neonatal stool samples that were collected from different delivery groups within 7 to 10 days post-birth. RNA-Seq was used to compare transcriptomic profiles, and pairwise Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to integrate gut microbiome, DEMs, and immune parameters. The results revealed that the evenness of the gut microbiota in neonates delivered by cesarean delivery was lower than that in the vaginal delivery group, with significant structural changes and microecological dysbiosis. The fecal metabolome of neonates delivered by cesarean delivery showed significant changes, including weakened lipid metabolism and enhanced metabolism of nitrogen compounds. At the host level, cesarean delivery was associated with a distinct transcriptomic landscape and immune cell differentiation pattern in neonatal PBMCs, skewed toward a Th2-biased response. Furthermore, gut microbiota that were more abundant in vaginally delivered neonates showed positive correlations with a balanced Th1/Th2. This multi-omics study indicates that during early life, the mode of delivery is strongly correlated with alterations in the gut microbiota structure, altered metabolic profiles, and imbalanced Th1/Th2 immune responses. Our findings formulate a hypothesis suggesting that the gut microbiota potentially plays a role in mediating early-life immune programming related to cesarean birth.

The gut microbiome plays crucial roles in the regulation of human health [33]. Our findings demonstrate that cesarean delivery was associated with significant gut microbial dysbiosis, as evidenced by a reduced Simpson index, an elevated Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratio, and decreased abundance of the genus Bacteroides. These observations are consistent with previous studies [26, 34–36]. Notably, the functional architecture of the microbiome also differed, with cesarean-delivered neonates showing a lack of functional contribution from Bacteroides—a genus known for its role in immune modulation and metabolic health [37]. Co-occurrence network analysis further highlighted the structural fragility of the microbial community in these neonates, evidenced by the absence of key phyla such as Bacteroidota and Verrucomicrobiota. Collectively, these results underscore that cesarean delivery is associated with a distinct and potentially compromised gut microbial ecosystem in early life.

There is a strong correlation between the gut microbiome and fecal metabolites, and fecal metabolomics is a useful tool to supplement gut microbiome research [38]. The combined application of the metagenome and metabolome can provide complementary information on the same metabolic pathways of microorganisms and neonates, enabling a comprehensive understanding of the interactions between the host and microbiota. Among the 12 KEGG pathways associated with DEMs identified in this study, those most closely related to immune regulatory functions is ABC transporters. ABC transporters play a critical role in immune defense by modulating various processes in both innate and adaptive immune cells [39]. The gut microbiome and the ABCB1 in the intestinal epithelium have been shown to play a synergistic role in maintaining gastrointestinal homeostasis [40, 41]. An analysis of the correlation between the gut microbiome and differentially abundant metabolites revealed that DEMs were significantly correlated with gut microbiome. Furthermore, through the constructed regulatory network integrating bacterial and DEMs, we identified that Bacteroides sp. may be a key microbial taxon strongly associated with metabolic variation. The results revealed that significant changes in the fecal metabolome of neonates born via cesarean delivery are closely related to changes in the gut microbial community structure during early life.

The results of the transcriptomic analysis in this study demonstrate that the gene expression profiles of PBMCs from the two neonatal groups exhibit significant differences. The DEGs are primarily enriched in BP related to the regulation of phosphorylation and are closely associated with the mTOR signaling pathway and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions. Studies have shown that mTOR is intensively involving in the metabolic reprogramming of T cell differentiation and playing a critical role in driving T-cell differentiation and function [42, 43]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the mode of delivery is associated with the response of T-cell metabolic reprogramming. Our research findings further confirmed that the gene expression profiles associated with Th1 cell differentiation in neonates delivered by cesarean delivery were significantly down-regulated, while those linked to Th2 cell differentiation were markedly up-regulated. Flow cytometry results further confirmed a significantly increased proportion of Th2 cells and a significantly reduced proportion of Th1 cells in the peripheral blood of neonates in cesarean group, indicating an attenuated Th1 cell response. Evidence suggests that specific microbial taxa, particularly those belonging to the Bacteroidetes, play a pivotal role in promoting Th1-mediated immune responses and facilitating immune tolerance [44]. The results of correlation analysis showed that bacterial species enriched in the vaginal group—including Bacteroides sp. and Bacteroides fragilis—exhibited a significant positive correlation with the IFN-γ/IL-4 ratio. Bacteria produce unique metabolism derived-molecules that may influence immune system development during early life [9]. Polysaccharide A, a capsular polysaccharide derived from Bacteroides fragilis, has been shown to enhance the expression of genes associated with the IL-2 signaling pathway, as well as STAT1 and STAT4, which are key transcription factors involved in the Th1 response [45]. Jun Miyoshi [46] et al. identified a specific Bacteroides strain (Bacteroides sp. CL1-UC) capable of activating dendritic cells (DCs) to secrete IL-12p40, thereby promoting Th1 differentiation and subsequent IFN-γ secretion. Therefore, we hypothesize that the decreased abundance of Bacteroides sp. and Bacteroides fragilis colonizing the intestines of neonates delivered via cesarean delivery could potentially contribute to the development of a Th2-biased immune phenotype.

The GSEA results revealed significant enrichment of gene sets associated with systemic lupus erythematosus and primary immunodeficiency in the Cesarean group. While this does not signify the existence of these diseases in neonates, it implies that the transcriptional program in PBMCs of neonates delivered via cesarean section may resemble specific dysregulated immune states, potentially mirroring altered immune maturation or a predisposition to immune dysregulation. This transcriptional signature is in line with our observed Th2 bias, as both Th2 skewing and certain autoimmune susceptibilities have been associated in early immune programming [47]. Our observational design restricts causal interpretation; however, we identified molecular patterns indicating that cesarean delivery may be related to immune imbalance. This necessitates prospective validation. Through multi-omics analysis, we established associations between specific microbes with host pathways. For example, the correlation network indicated associations between Enterococcus, Escherichia, and phosphate-related regulation of oxidative phosphorylation. Most notably, vaginal-enriched taxa, such as Bacteroides sp., Bacteroides fragilis, and Escherichia coli, exhibited strong positive correlations with a higher IFN-γ/IL-4 ratio, which supports a potential role of these microbes in promoting Th1-like responses. These data formulate hypotheses for future experimental research.

There are several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the sample size is relatively small. According to the post hoc power analysis, 28 participants per group would be necessary to achieve 80% power. Some findings might be false positives or may lack validity in larger studies because the study employed a small sample size when analyzing multiple types of complex data. Second, despite careful matching, residual confounding stemming from unmeasured maternal factors cannot be entirely ruled out. These factors could encompass microbiota, diet, body mass index, stress levels, and lifestyle. Such factors are recognized to exert an influence on both the maternal microbiome and the in-utero environment [48–50], potentially having an impact on neonatal gut colonization and immune development independently of the delivery mode. Hence, although our analysis indicates an association between the mode of delivery and multi-omics features, the observed differences may be partially affected or confounded by unmeasured variables, a limitation frequently encountered in observational studies. Third, The absence of functional validation. Fourth, certain analysis methods (such as LEfSe) possess limitations, and correlation-based network analyses do not establish directionality. The multi-omics integration and network analyses were exploratory and intended to generate new hypotheses, not to prove a biological mechanisms. Finally, this study is constrained by its cross-sectional design. The solitary sampling time point hinders any deduction regarding the dynamic alterations, stability, or recuperation of the neonatal gut microbiota over time. Consequently, no definitive conclusions can be reached regarding the persistence or reversibility of the delivery mode - associated differences that have been observed. Future research should prioritize the following areas: first, a large-scale prospective cohort with comprehensive collection of maternal metadata and other major confounders is required to validate our findings; second, future studies should include longitudinal sampling at multiple postpartum time points to track the trajectory of microbial colonization, the maturation of metabolic networks, and their long-term interplay with immune development; third, targeted metabolites or microbiota for quantification or functional validation in vitro or in vivo models (e.g., fecal microbiota transplantation in animal models) are required.

Despite these limitations, our study offers valuable insights for the translational research. It identifies potential microbial and metabolic biomarkers—such as Bacteroides abundance, Th1/Th2 ratio, and specific lipid metabolites—that could be leveraged in future studies aimed at mitigating cesarean-associated dysbiosis. Interventions such as targeted probiotics or microbial consortia transplantation could be designed based on these findings to restore immune balance in affected neonates.

Conclusion

This multi-omics investigation reveals an association between cesarean delivery and a distinct developmental trajectory of the neonatal gut-immune axis during early life. It shows coordinated changes in microbiome structure, metabolic activity, host transcriptome, and immune function, marked by a Th2-biased immune response. While causal mechanisms remain unclear, these findings provide a basis for understanding how birth mode influences early immune programming. They also highlight the potential of targeting the gut microbiota to support immune development in cesarean-born infants and point to promising avenues for future translational research.
