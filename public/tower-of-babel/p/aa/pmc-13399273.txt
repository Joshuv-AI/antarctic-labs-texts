Subdeltoid ultrasound-guided brachial plexus block: A technical description with anatomical correlation and a preliminary case series
Authors: Revathi Nair, Ankita Goyal, Kajal Jain, Anjali Aggarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Regional anaesthesia in the emergency department requires reliable techniques with minimal complications. We describe a subdeltoid ultrasound-guided brachial plexus approach targeting a potential “nerve funnel” at the surgical neck of the humerus, bridging infraclavicular and axillary approaches. Cadaveric dissection explored its anatomical basis and correlated it with clinical sonographic findings. This retrospective case series included six adult patients with traumatic upper-limb injuries. Cadaveric observations suggested terminal nerve clustering and proximity of the musculocutaneous and intercostobrachial nerves at the subdeltoid level. All patients achieved adequate anaesthesia with a single 15 mL injection of 0.5% bupivacaine with 4 mg dexamethasone. The mean pain-free duration was 17.3 hours, with consistent tourniquet tolerance and no procedural complications. The subdeltoid approach appears feasible as a single-injection technique for upper-limb procedures. Using a superficial acoustic window and favourable anatomical clustering, it may provide effective coverage with technical simplicity. These preliminary findings require validation in larger prospective studies.

INTRODUCTION

Effective upper limb anaesthesia in the emergency department requires reliable, technically simple techniques suitable for trauma patients. Although infraclavicular block (ICB) and axillary block are commonly used, both have limitations. The ICB often requires deep needle trajectories and carries a risk of pneumothorax,[1] while the traditional axillary block targets nerves that have already begun to diverge, often necessitating multiple needle passes and supplemental blocks for the musculocutaneous and intercostobrachial nerves.[2]

The subdeltoid approach offers an alternative ultrasound window to address these limitations. By positioning the ultrasound probe proximal to the standard axillary crease at the transition zone of the surgical neck of the humerus, the clinician can utilise the subdeltoid space. At this level, the terminal branches of the brachial plexus (radial, median, and ulnar) appear to lie in close proximity, forming a “nerve funnel” posteromedial to the axillary artery along with the musculocutaneous and intercostobrachial nerves.

This report describes the technique and its initial clinical application in a small retrospective case series. In addition, cadaveric dissection was performed to explore the anatomical basis corresponding to the observed sonographic findings.

CASE SERIES

This retrospective case series included six consecutive adults managed at a Level 1 trauma emergency department between October and December 2025 using the subdeltoid block as routine care. The objective was to describe the technique and anatomical basis, rather than establish comparative efficacy. Written informed consent for procedural anaesthesia and for publication of anonymised clinical data/images was obtained from all patients, and no patient-identifiable information is included.

Six adult patients (>18 years) with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II were included. Indications included distal forearm fractures and complex hand or forearm injuries requiring surgery. Patients with coagulopathy, pre-existing neuropathy, or infection at the injection site were excluded. All blocks were performed by a single experienced anaesthesiologist using a portable ultrasound system (GE Logiq, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with a 5–11 MHz linear transducer.

Block technique

All blocks were performed in the operating room, following initial evaluation in the emergency department. Standard monitoring was applied, and intravenous access was secured before the procedure.

With the patient supine and head turned contralaterally, the affected limb was abducted to approximately 90°. A high-frequency linear ultrasound transducer (5–11 MHz) was placed transversely over the anterior axillary fold and then moved proximally to identify the subdeltoid region at the level of the surgical neck of the humerus. The operator stood beside the affected limb, with the ultrasound screen facing the procedural field to allow ergonomic in-plane craniocaudal needle advancement and continuous real-time needle-tip visualisation.

The long head of the biceps and distal deltoid fibres were identified as sonographic landmarks. The axillary vessels and adjacent neural structures were visualised. The target site for local anaesthetic deposition was the posteromedial aspect of the axillary artery (approximately the 6 o’clock position), where the terminal branches of the brachial plexus appeared clustered [Figure 1a and b].

Under aseptic precautions, a 22G 50-mm echogenic needle was advanced in-plane craniocaudally towards the target. After negative aspiration, 15 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine with 4 mg dexamethasone was injected incrementally under ultrasound visualisation.

Adequate spread of local anaesthetic around the nerve cluster was confirmed sonographically. No immediate complications such as vascular puncture or paraesthesia during needle advancement were observed.

Block assessment and outcomes

Patients were monitored for sensory and motor block onset. Sensory blockade was assessed by loss of pinprick sensation in the distributions of the radial, median, ulnar, musculocutaneous, and intercostobrachial nerves. Motor blockade was assessed clinically using gross motor function, including elbow flexion (musculocutaneous nerve), wrist and finger extension (radial nerve), and wrist and finger flexion (median and ulnar nerves). Successful block was defined as adequate surgical anaesthesia without the need for supplemental regional techniques, additional local infiltration, or conversion to general anaesthesia. Tourniquet tolerance during surgery was recorded in all cases. Postoperative pain was assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS) at 1, 6, 12, and 24 hours.

Sensory blockade was achieved in all assessed nerve distributions. Motor blockade corresponded to the expected distal nerve distributions assessed clinically. All patients tolerated the tourniquet without additional analgesia or sedation. The mean duration of the postoperative pain-free period was 17.3 hours. No immediate complications such as vascular puncture, local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), pneumothorax, or neurological paraesthesia were observed.

Cadaveric correlation

Cadaveric dissection was performed to correlate anatomical findings with the sonographic “subdeltoid window”. Dissections were carried out on formalin-fixed adult upper limb specimens in the Department of Anatomy, in accordance with standard institutional ethical and biosafety protocols. A stepwise dissection approach was used to identify the relationship between the axillary artery and the distal cords and proximal terminal branches of the brachial plexus at the level of the surgical neck of the humerus [Figure 1c and d]. Cadaveric observations illustrated neural clustering at this level and complemented the sonographic findings. No dye injection study was undertaken because the aim was anatomical correlation, not spread analysis.

DISCUSSION

This case series suggests that the ultrasound-guided subdeltoid approach may be feasible for selected upper-limb procedures. By targeting the surgical neck of the humerus, it may combine advantages of infraclavicular and axillary approaches.

The ICB is favoured for its proximal coverage but can be technically demanding, especially in obese patients or those with significant pectoral bulk.[34] In the present series, the subdeltoid target was consistently identified at a superficial depth of 1.5–2.5 cm, providing a favourable acoustic window through the relatively thin distal fibres of the deltoid and the long head of the biceps. This superficial location and in-plane craniocaudal needle trajectory may improve procedural ease compared with the steeper ICB approach.[4] Although the present series was not designed to specifically evaluate obese individuals, the relatively superficial target and favourable acoustic window may facilitate image acquisition and needle visualisation even in patients with increased soft-tissue bulk. However, the ease of performing this technique and its success in obese individuals require further evaluation.

Unlike the traditional axillary block, where the radial, median, and ulnar nerves are often separated by fascial septa,[1] the subdeltoid approach utilises a more proximal ultrasound window at the level of the surgical neck of the humerus. At this level, cadaveric observations in the present report suggest that the terminal branches remain in closer proximity around the axillary artery, forming a “funnel-like” arrangement. This may allow single-injection coverage and reduce multiple needle passes compared with conventional axillary block. A recognised limitation of the axillary block is inconsistent coverage of the musculocutaneous and intercostobrachial (T2) nerves.[2] Because the subdeltoid approach is more proximal, local anaesthetic may spread before these nerves diverge. The consistent tourniquet tolerance observed in this series supports this possibility, although this requires further evaluation.

The anatomical correlation may explain the observed clinical performance. Cadaveric dissection showed close approximation of distal cords and terminal branches around the axillary artery, corresponding to the sonographic “subdeltoid window”. This clustering may explain the sensory blockade and tourniquet tolerance after a single injection.

Practically, this technique may offer a simplified single-injection option in trauma settings, where rapid execution and minimal patient repositioning are desirable. Its use in crush and blast injuries highlights potential utility in high-acuity settings.

These findings are limited by the small sample size, retrospective design, and absence of a comparator group. In addition, the cadaveric component was limited to structural anatomical correlation without assessment of injectate spread. All blocks were performed by a single experienced operator; multicentre studies are needed to assess reproducibility and the learning curve. Prospective comparative studies are required to validate efficacy, safety, and reproducibility.

CONCLUSION

The subdeltoid ultrasound-guided brachial plexus block appears feasible for distal upper-limb anaesthesia. Its superficial anatomical window may provide effective coverage with a simplified needle trajectory. These preliminary findings suggest a potential role in selected trauma settings, pending further validation.
