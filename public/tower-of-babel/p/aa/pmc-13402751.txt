Toward scalable and sustainable CO₂ capture: RSM-Guided optimization of Mg(OH)₂ slurry bubble column reactor
Authors: Masoomeh Jamal Livani, Leila Vafajoo, Mohammad Kazemeini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing concentration of atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂), driven by industrial activities and fossil fuel consumption, necessitates the development of efficient and scalable carbon capture technologies. In this study, the chemical absorption of CO₂ using a magnesium hydroxide [Mg(OH)₂] slurry was investigated in a semi-batch slurry bubble column reactor. The process was optimized using a Box-Behnken Design (BBD) within the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) framework, evaluating the effects of four key parameters: slurry initial concentration (0.001–0.003 mol/L), liquid volume (15–25 L), increase in liquid height within the column due to gas retention (1–4 cm), and temperature (30–50 °C). A total of 23 experimental runs were conducted, and CO₂ removal efficiency was selected as the response variable. The regression model developed showed excellent agreement with experimental data (R² = 0.9923), and the maximum CO₂ removal efficiency achieved was 82.10% under optimal conditions (25 L liquid volume, 0.001 mol/L Mg(OH)2 initial concentration, 40 °C, 1 cm increasing the height of the liquid inside the column). The findings emphasize the critical roles of gas-liquid interfacial area, slurry alkalinity, and operating temperature in enhancing CO₂ absorption. This work contributes to the advancement of sustainable CO₂ capture technologies and provides a robust foundation for future industrial-scale implementations of Mg(OH)₂-based absorption systems.Keywords: CO2, Mg(OH)₂ solution, Slurry absorption, Bubble column, Box-Behnken Design, Gas-liquid interfacial areaSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Rising atmospheric concentrations of carbon dioxide (CO₂), largely attributed to anthropogenic activities such as fossil fuel combustion and industrial processes, remain a critical driver of global climate change1,2. Over the past decades, numerous studies have highlighted the pressing need for effective strategies to mitigate CO₂ emissions, with global emissions surpassing 36 Gigatons annually in recent years3. Among the various carbon mitigation approaches, post-combustion CO₂ capture technologies have gained prominence due to their compatibility with existing energy infrastructure and their potential for immediate deployment4.

Chemical absorption using amine-based solvents, such as MEA, is widely adopted for its efficiency in dilute CO₂ streams5,6. However, MEA systems face major barriers to large-scale use due to high regeneration energy, oxidative and thermal degradation, volatility, corrosion, and formation of stable byproducts7–9. These issues have prompted interest in alternative absorbents. Ammonia-based systems offer higher capacity but suffer from volatility and difficult product separation10,11. while potassium carbonate solutions are environmentally benign yet limited by slow kinetics, even when promoted12.

In response to these limitations, recent research has focused on mineral-based and hybrid sorbents, which promise enhanced sustainability and stability. Of particular interest are magnesium-based materials, such as MgO and Mg(OH)₂, which not only serve as efficient CO₂ sorbents but also enable permanent carbon fixation via mineralization. Wu et al. demonstrated that polarization of CO₂ molecules significantly enhances their interaction with Mg-based surfaces, thereby improving adsorption efficiency13. These findings build upon earlier work suggesting that Mg(OH)₂ can spontaneously react with CO₂ under ambient conditions, forming stable carbonates with minimal energy input14.

Further innovation in CO₂ capture involves the integration of reactive capture with in situ regeneration and mineralization15. For instance, Liu and coworkers proposed a novel approach that simultaneously captures CO₂ using aqueous solvents and subsequently converts it into stable carbonates, inherently regenerating the solvent and reducing operational complexity. This integrated methodology not only minimizes waste generation but also improves the overall thermodynamic efficiency of the capture process16.

In parallel with absorption-based techniques, methods such as solid-phase adsorption using porous materials and emerging microfluidic platforms have shown promise for compact, modular CO₂ capture systems, offering enhanced surface area contact and fine control over reaction environments17,18. These technologies, although still maturing, may complement conventional systems such as adsorption19–22, extraction23–26, aqueous two-phase systems27–29, membrane technology30, and photocatalysis31,32, and play a critical role in decentralized or niche applications.

Given the promising characteristics of Mg(OH)₂ and its byproduct compatibility with flue gas desulfurization(FGD) systems, this study investigates the potential of using Mg(OH)₂ slurries for CO₂ absorption under typical FGD outlet conditions (~ 52 °C). The system is designed to operate in a regenerable configuration, facilitating CO₂ sequestration and reagent recovery. Particular attention is given to optimizing operational parameters such as slurry concentration, gas–liquid contact efficiency, and regeneration dynamics, with a view to establishing a scalable, environmentally benign capture strategy.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) underscores the urgency of limiting global warming to below 1.5 °C, a goal that necessitates drastic reductions in CO₂ emissions. This mounting challenge has stimulated intensive research into carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technologies aimed at intercepting CO₂ before its release into the atmosphere.

In light of these limitations, extensive efforts have been directed toward identifying alternative solvents and capture agents with improved stability, lower regeneration energy demands, and reduced environmental impact. Aqueous ammonia has been proposed as a viable alternative, exhibiting higher CO₂ loading capacity under comparable conditions. Nonetheless, challenges associated with its volatility and the instability of intermediate products—such as ammonium bicarbonate—continue to hinder its practical application33. Similarly, potassium carbonate solutions, which offer a less corrosive and more environmentally benign profile, suffer from low reaction kinetics, necessitating the use of promoters like piperazine. Despite enhancing the rate of CO₂ absorption, piperazine introduces concerns regarding toxicity and environmental persistence12,34–39.

In response to the need for more sustainable and robust capture materials, research has increasingly focused on mineral-based systems and hybrid materials. Magnesium-based compounds, particularly MgO and Mg(OH)₂, have garnered significant interest due to their thermodynamic favorability for CO₂ binding and their abundance in natural and industrial sources. These materials offer the potential for both temporary and permanent CO₂ sequestration via mineralization pathways, wherein gaseous CO₂ is converted into stable solid carbonates. Recent studies have demonstrated that the polarization of CO₂ molecules enhances adsorption behavior on Mg-based surfaces, enabling more efficient capture under ambient conditions40. Moreover, Mg(OH)₂, a byproduct of the magnesium-enhanced lime (MEL) flue gas desulfurization process, offers a low-cost, environmentally benign, and readily available absorbent that can be seamlessly integrated into existing infrastructure14,41.

Beyond conventional absorption systems, other techniques such as adsorption using porous solid sorbents (e.g., metal-organic frameworks, zeolites) and microfluidic-based gas–liquid reactors are gaining traction42. Adsorption processes offer modularity, low thermal requirements, and high selectivity, while microfluidic platforms provide enhanced control over interfacial transport and reaction kinetics in a compact format. These alternative approaches, though still emerging, are promising complements to absorption technologies, especially in applications requiring mobility, high-throughput screening, or localized capture17,18.

Despite the demonstrated promise of magnesium hydroxide as a carbon dioxide absorbent, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding the systematic optimization of its performance in practical reactor configurations, particularly under the dynamic conditions encountered in industrial applications. By employing a rigorous statistical approach— BBD within RSM—this study provides novel insights into the interplay of key process variables influencing CO₂ absorption efficiency in a slurry bubble column reactor. The outcomes address both the operational limits and the underlying mechanisms governing gas–liquid interactions, enabling the identification of optimal operating windows for maximum carbon capture. Ultimately, the findings not only advance the fundamental understanding of mineral-based capture technologies but also lay the groundwork for environmentally benign, cost-effective alternatives to conventional amine systems, expanding the toolkit for scalable and sustainable climate solutions.

Materials and methods

The investigation focused on determining the optimal conditions for CO₂ removal within a semi-batch bubble column reactor by systematically analyzing the effects of the process variables, including solution temperature, liquid volume, Mg(OH)2 initial concentration, and increase in liquid height within the column due to gas retention. The efficiency of CO₂ capture was employed as the key metric to assess the reactor’s performance under varying operational scenarios.

Chemicals

All chemicals utilized in this investigation were procured from commercial suppliers and employed as received, without further purification. Magnesium hydroxide was obtained from Merck Chemicals (Germany), and high-purity gases, analytical grade CO₂ and N₂ (≥ 99.9%), were supplied by Zagros Chemical Co., serving to replicate flue gas conditions. The absorbent medium for CO₂ capture consisted of an aqueous Mg(OH)2 solution, initially adjusted to a pH of 10, and prepared by dispersing Mg(OH)₂ pellets in deionized water. To simulate emissions representative of flue gas, the composition of the synthetic gas mixture was regulated by mixing CO₂ with nitrogen.

Experimental procedure

A slurry bubble column three-phase reactor was employed in this experiment as a mass transfer apparatus as shown in Figure-1. Although gas–liquid mass transfer is a key mechanism in bubble column absorption, the present study focuses on experimentally measured CO₂ removal efficiency and its statistical modeling; independent determination of kL​, a, or kLa was not performed and is beyond the scope of this work. The reactor consisted of a cylindrical plexiglass column with a height of 2.5 m and an internal diameter of 15 cm, equipped with a gas distributor located at the bottom of the column, which determines the bubble rise velocity. The pore size of the distributor ranged from 400 to 500 micrometers. The reactor was also fitted with a temperature controller capable of maintaining temperatures within the range of 30 to 50 °C.

Flue gas absorption was optimized using a CO₂ and N₂ gas mixture balanced to achieve concentrations between 2000 and 3000 ppm, with a purity of 99.99%. For simplification, mass transfer was assumed to be uniform throughout the entire column. The inlet flow rate of carbon dioxide was adjusted using a flowmeter, ranging from 1 to 3 L per minute.

For the preparation of the liquid phase, the concentration range of Mg(OH)₂ was set between 0.001 and 0.003 mol/L. (Mg(OH)₂ molarity; equivalent OH⁻ normality = 2 C = 0.002–0.006 N; 0.06–0.17 g·L⁻¹ Mg(OH)₂). Each desired concentration was homogenized in distilled water using a magnetic stirrer with a heater, bringing the liquid phase temperature close to the experimental temperature. Once prepared, the bubble column was filled and made ready for experimentation.

Carbon dioxide gas was introduced into the column at a specific and constant flow rate. Due to the use of a mesh-type gas distributor and a constant superficial gas velocity, the bubbles ascended at a uniform rate. From the beginning of the experiment, CO₂ absorption took place between the gas and liquid phases, with major chemical reactions occurring during the absorption process. The concentration of CO₂ in the outlet gas was then measured using a detector.

Theory

In the gas phase, CO₂ is transferred from the bulk gas stream toward the gas–liquid interface. Within the liquid phase, this transferred CO₂ undergoes further mass transfer coupled with chemical reactions. The liquid in the absorption column is assumed to be ideally mixed. Depending on the relative size of suspended solid particles compared to the thickness of the liquid film, chemical absorption and solid dissolution processes may occur either sequentially or simultaneously45. CO₂ absorption proceeds through multiple steps, involving a series of key reactions, denoted in Eq. (3) through (10).

The dissolution of solid Mg(OH)₂ within the liquid film significantly enhances the overall absorption rate. This effect is further amplified by the interaction between the dissolved CO₂ and the dissociated Mg(OH)₂ species in the film45. As Mg(OH)₂ dissolves, it liberates hydroxide ions (OH⁻), which facilitate CO₂ absorption by reacting with carbon species in the bulk liquid, as represented in Eq. (3). During this absorption process, intermediate carbonate species such as HCO₃⁻ and CO₃²⁻ are formed (Eqs. (3)–(7)), which subsequently react with magnesium ions to produce Mg(HCO₃)₂ and MgCO₃ (Eq. (8))40.

The distribution and speciation of these products are highly dependent on the pH within the absorption medium. At higher pH values (9 < pH < initial), CO₃²⁻ ions predominate; in the intermediate pH range (7 < pH < 9), HCO₃⁻ ions are dominant; and under more acidic conditions, dissolved CO₂ and H₂CO₃ become the major species. While Mg(HCO₃)₂ remains soluble in the liquid, MgCO₃ tends to precipitate out, thus influencing the solid–liquid equilibrium in the reactor40.

Design of experiments

In the present investigation, the process of carbon dioxide (CO₂) capture, specifically targeting the efficient removal of CO₂ from gas streams, was systematically optimized using the BBD, a variant of the broader RSM. RSM serves as a robust statistical framework that facilitates process optimization by enabling the identification of key influencing variables, uncovering potential process inefficiencies, and improving overall process resilience in the face of external disturbances43.

The BBD approach, recognized for its practical efficiency, was chosen for this study due to its capability to reduce the total number of required experimental trials without compromising the quality of information about variable interactions. Unlike full factorial designs, BBD is a fractional factorial design that operates at three levels for each factor but does not include experimental runs at the extreme corners of the design space, thereby ensuring safety and cost-effectiveness during experimentation.

To construct the experimental matrix, Design Expert software (version 11.0) was employed. The model considered four independent process parameters that are known to significantly affect the CO₂ capture efficiency: (i) the volume of liquid used in the system, ranging from 15 to 25 L; (ii) the initial concentration of the absorbent, varying between 0.001 and 0.003 mol/L; (iii) the liquid height variation due to the gas hold-up in the column, which was adjusted between 1 and 4 cm; and (iv) the operational temperature, set within the range of 30 to 50 °C. These variables were selected based on their established impact on the absorption kinetics and thermodynamic efficiency of CO₂ removal.

This experimental design framework enabled a comprehensive investigation of both individual factor effects and their interactions across multiple levels. At the same time, it minimized resource consumption by reducing the number of experiments needed compared to other multivariate approaches. The primary response variable under analysis was the effectiveness of the CO₂ capture process, with particular emphasis on maximizing removal efficiency and ensuring minimal system defects.

In the RSM design, the “liquid head increase due to gas holdup” (ΔH)—a direct index of gas holdup (())) —was chosen as the main hydrodynamic variable to estimate the net effect of holdup on mass transfer and CO₂ removal efficiency in isolation. Since ΔH and gas flow rate (or superficial gas velocity, (Ug)) are intrinsically correlated, and changes in (Ug) affect the phenomenon mainly through changes in () and bubble geometry, simultaneously introducing both factors as independent factors will exacerbate multicollinearity in the design matrix, increase the variance of the regression coefficients, and weaken the discriminatory power of the ANOVA; thus, the interpretation of main effects and interactions will be distorted. Therefore, in this study, (Ug) was used solely as a manipulated variable to achieve predetermined ΔH setpoints and was kept constant after ΔH was stabilized; and ΔH/itself was modeled and analyzed as an independent factor in the RSM design.

Subsequent statistical analyses were conducted to derive a second-order polynomial regression model that accurately describes the relationship between the input parameters and the system’s response. The resulting model provided critical insights into the optimal operational conditions necessary to achieve the highest possible CO₂ removal efficiency within the tested parameter range.

Among various absorbents, magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂) has gained attention for its alkaline nature and capability to react with CO₂ under mild operating conditions. In this study, a three-phase slurry bubble column reactor was employed to evaluate the performance of CO₂ removal using Mg(OH)₂ as the reactive medium.

Effect of liquid volume

Figure-6a illustrates the effect of liquid volume (V) on the efficiency of CO₂ removal, while maintaining all other process parameters at their respective constant values. The graph presents a clear positive correlation between the liquid volume and the percentage of CO₂ captured, indicating that increasing the liquid volume enhances the absorption capacity of the system. The solid black line represents the model prediction, while the blue dashed lines define the 95% confidence interval, showing the expected variation range of the system’s behavior.

As observed, CO₂ removal efficiency increases from approximately 48% at a liquid volume of 15 L to nearly 65% at 25 L. This behavior can be attributed to the enhanced availability of the alkaline slurry (Mg(OH)₂), which provides more reactive surface area and ion exchange sites for the absorption of CO₂. A larger liquid phase volume likely facilitates improved mass transfer conditions by reducing the gas–liquid interfacial resistance and enhancing CO₂ solubilization in the aqueous medium, thereby promoting a more favorable reaction environment for bicarbonate and carbonate formation.

Furthermore, the narrow width of the confidence band across the volume range suggests low variability in the model’s prediction and high reliability of the experimental data, reinforcing the statistical validity of the observed trend.

In summary, increasing the liquid volume positively affects the chemical absorption efficiency of CO₂ in a slurry bubble column reactor using Mg(OH)₂. This trend highlights the importance of sufficient liquid phase availability in designing and scaling up CO₂ capture systems to achieve higher removal efficiencies under optimized operating conditions.

Effect of initial slurry concentration

Figure-6b illustrates the influence of the initial concentration of the alkaline Mg(OH)₂ slurry on CO₂ removal efficiency, with all other process variables held constant. As indicated in the figure, an increase in the initial concentration from 0.001 mol/L to 0.003 mol/L leads to a gradual yet measurable improvement in the removal percentage, rising from approximately 53% to over 60%. The black solid curve represents the predicted values, while the dashed blue lines denote the 95% confidence interval of the model, reflecting a statistically valid prediction range.

This enhancement in CO₂ removal efficiency with increased initial concentration is consistent with the expected behavior of gas–liquid absorption systems46. A higher concentration of the absorbent (Mg(OH)₂) introduces more reactive hydroxide ions into the liquid phase, facilitating a more effective neutralization of CO₂ via the formation of bicarbonate and carbonate species40. As the molarity increases, the driving force for the chemical reaction also intensifies, promoting greater conversion of dissolved CO₂.

However, it is important to note that the increase in removal efficiency appears to be nonlinear and slightly flattened in the mid-range concentrations. This behavior suggests that beyond a certain concentration threshold, the system may begin to approach saturation or diffusion limitations, where additional Mg(OH)₂ contributes less significantly to performance gains.

Its influence may vary depending on interactions with other variables such as temperature, gas height in the column, and liquid volume. Therefore, a full-factorial or response surface-based analysis is essential to uncover potential synergistic or antagonistic effects among the parameters.

Overall, increasing the initial concentration of Mg(OH)₂ in the slurry enhances the CO₂ capture capacity of the system, though optimization must consider both the economic cost of higher reagent usage and potential diminishing returns at elevated concentrations.

Effect of gas holdup index (ΔH)

In gas–liquid absorption processes, gas hold-up is a fundamental hydrodynamic parameter representing the volumetric fraction of gas retained in the reactor. Variations in liquid height within the column are a direct consequence of changes in gas hold-up. Therefore, analyzing variations in liquid height provides valuable indirect insight into the dynamic behavior of gas distribution within the system.

As shown in Figure-6c, increasing variations in gas height—corresponding to higher gas hold-up—are associated with a progressive decline in CO₂ removal efficiency. Specifically, when gas height increases from approximately 1 cm to 4 cm, removal efficiency drops from about 70% to below 60%. This inverse correlation underscores a critical point: while moderate increases in gas retention may initially enhance gas–liquid interfacial area and contact time, excessive gas accumulation leads to adverse hydrodynamic effects that counteract absorption.

Given that gas height variations reflect shifts in gas hold-up, the observed nonlinear response suggests that the system approaches a saturation regime, beyond which the benefits of increased gas retention diminish or even reverse. The concave profile of the efficiency curve indicates that initial increases in gas height variation (from low ε values) negatively impact performance more significantly than further increases at higher ε.

This analysis highlights the importance of distinguishing between observable parameters (e.g., gas height) and their underlying causes (e.g., gas hold-up). It is misleading to interpret increased gas height variation as beneficial without recognizing the hydrodynamic penalties associated with excessive gas hold-up. Therefore, process optimization should target an optimal range of gas hold-up, rather than focusing solely on reactor geometry.

To further clarify this relationship, future studies should couple quantitative measurements of gas hold-up with real-time monitoring of gas height variations and other dynamic parameters such as bubble size distribution, slurry rheology, and gas superficial velocity.

In summary, variations in gas height, as a hydrodynamic response to increasing gas hold-up, play a critical role in determining the efficiency of CO₂ absorption. Understanding and controlling these variations, rather than simply maximizing gas volume, are essential for achieving robust and scalable reactor performance.

Effect of temperature

The operating temperature emerged as a pivotal parameter governing CO₂ removal efficiency in chemical absorption systems utilizing magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂) alkaline slurry47. Experimental investigations revealed a non-monotonic relationship between temperature and removal efficiency, driven by competing thermodynamic and kinetic mechanisms. At lower temperatures (0–40 °C), CO₂ removal efficiency exhibited a steady increase, rising from 40% to a peak of ~ 85% at 50 °C. This enhancement is attributed to accelerated reaction kinetics and improved CO₂ solubility within the slurry, consistent with the Arrhenius dependency of reaction rates on temperature. However, beyond the critical threshold of 50 °C, as shown Figure-6d, a gradual decline in efficiency was observed, dropping to ~ 70% at 60 °C. This reversal stems from two primary factors: (1) thermal degradation of Mg(OH)₂ into MgO and H₂O, which reduces slurry alkalinity and active sites for CO₂ absorption, and (2) diminished CO₂ solubility at elevated temperatures due to Le Chatelier’s principle48. The interplay of these effects underscores the delicate balance between kinetic advantages and thermodynamic limitations in high-temperature regimes.

The nonlinear behavior aligns with prior studies on alkaline slurries, where excessive temperatures destabilize the gas-liquid interface through bubble coalescence and reduced contact time. Furthermore, interactions between temperature and secondary parameters, such as slurry alkalinity and gas height in the column, amplify system complexity. For instance, elevated temperatures exacerbate slurry deactivation and foam formation, counteracting kinetic gains. A comparative evaluation with amine-based solvents reveals material-specific limitations, as Mg(OH)2 systems demonstrate peak removal efficiency at lower operating temperatures (40–50 °C) than amine counterparts (60–80 °C), attributable to the intrinsic thermal sensitivity of alkaline slurries. Scalability challenges further complicate industrial adoption, as industrial reactors often operate at higher temperatures, necessitating additives (e.g., stabilizers or anti-foaming agents) or process modifications to mitigate slurry degradation. These findings emphasize the need for advanced formulations or hybrid systems to enhance thermal resilience without compromising absorption capacity49.

Overall, Fig. 6(a–d) demonstrates that all four operating parameters have a positive impact on CO₂ removal efficiency within the investigated ranges, with gas holdup and liquid volume showing particularly strong effects. The combined presentation of these main effects allows direct comparison of parameter sensitivity and provides a clear basis for subsequent optimization and interaction analysis.

Investigation of the interactive Effects of Process Parameters on CO₂ Capture Efficiency

The 3D response surface plots presented in Figure-7 (a–d) offer valuable insights into the complex interactions among the key operational parameters affecting CO₂ absorption efficiency in a semi-batch slurry bubble column reactor using magnesium hydroxide as the absorbent.

Figure 7 (a) depicts the interactive effect of liquid volume and initial slurry concentration. The response surface shows a pronounced increase in CO₂ removal with rising liquid volume, reaching a maximum at 25 L. This trend can be attributed to the increased gas–liquid contact area and longer gas residence times resulting from higher liquid holdup. The interaction analysis (Fig. 7a) reveals that this trend can be reversed when other parameters—such as gas height and liquid volume—are at specific levels. This apparent contradiction arises because the main effect analysis isolates concentration while holding other factors constant, whereas the interaction plot accounts for simultaneous variations in multiple parameters, capturing viscosity-related mass transfer limitations and particle aggregation at higher concentrations. Accordingly, the efficiency drop observed in Fig. 7a reflects complex multi-factor dynamics rather than an inconsistency in experimental outcomes.

Response surface plots of CO₂ removal efficiency showing the interaction effects of: (a) liquid volume (V) and initial slurry concentration (C₀) at h = 2.5 cm and T = 40 °C; (b) V and h at C₀ = 0.002 mol/L and T = 40 °C; (c) h and T at V = 20 L and C₀ = 0.002 mol/L; and (d) h and C₀ at V = 20 L and T = 40 °C.

While intuitively higher alkalinity should favor absorption, excessive concentrations may lead to higher viscosity and potential particle aggregation, which hinders bubble dispersion and mass transfer. Furthermore, at higher molarities, saturation effects may limit the solubility-driven driving force for CO₂ absorption, and the slurry’s surface may become more prone to fouling, thereby reducing interfacial reactivity50.

Figure 7 (b) illustrates the combined influence of liquid volume (V) and gas variation height (h) on CO₂ removal. The surface reveals a sharp enhancement in removal efficiency with increasing V and decreasing h. A smaller gas dispersion height (e.g., 1 cm) indicates minimal stratification of the gas phase, enhancing gas–liquid interfacial area and improving hydrodynamics through increased bubble–slurry contact frequency. The increase in V further augments these effects by increasing the reactor’s effective absorption zone. This interaction is particularly important from a hydrodynamic perspective, as gas hold-up (a key driver of mass transfer) is inversely correlated with superficial gas velocity when the variation height is low. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining a thin gas distribution layer to reduce bubble coalescence and enhance interfacial renewal rates51.

Figure 7 (c) evaluates the synergistic influence of gas variation height and operating temperature. The surface exhibits a saddle-like curvature, where the maximum CO₂ removal is achieved at intermediate temperatures (40–45 °C) and lower gas variation heights (~ 1 cm). This optimal thermal regime reflects a balance between the thermodynamic driving force for gas dissolution and the kinetic limitations of the Mg(OH)₂–CO₂ reaction system. While higher temperatures enhance molecular diffusivity and promote rapid neutralization reactions, they also reduce CO₂ solubility due to exothermic dissolution behavior. The decreasing efficiency at 50 °C likely reflects this thermodynamic trade-off. Moreover, lower gas dispersion heights, as discussed previously, favor finer bubble distribution and minimize back-mixing, leading to enhanced absorption kinetics under elevated temperatures52.

Figure 7 (d) explores the interaction between initial slurry concentration and gas variation height. The surface shows that both lower C₀ and smaller h lead to greater removal efficiencies. The deleterious effect of increased C₀, consistent with Plot (a), is again likely attributed to viscosity-induced mass transfer resistance and potential solid–solid aggregation phenomena. The inverse relationship with gas height reinforces the need to control gas introduction depth to manage bubble behavior, especially in semi-batch slurry systems where residence time and gas holdup are closely coupled. Importantly, the combined optimization of C₀ and h reveals a non-linear interaction, suggesting that suboptimal settings in either parameter can significantly compromise overall reactor performance despite favorable values in the other53.

Taken together, these results confirm the statistical robustness of the quadratic regression model (R² = 0.9923), validating its use for predictive optimization of the CO₂ absorption process. The maximum experimentally observed removal efficiency of 82.10% under optimal conditions (V = 25 L, C₀ = 0.001 mol/L, T = 40 °C, h = 1 cm) demonstrates the potential of Mg(OH)₂ slurry as a viable absorbent in sustainable carbon capture applications. From a process engineering perspective, the observed trends highlight the critical interplay between hydrodynamic conditions, thermochemical parameters, and mass transfer constraints.

Conclusion

This study introduces a systematic and data-driven approach to optimizing carbon dioxide (CO₂) capture using magnesium hydroxide [Mg(OH)₂] slurry in a bubble column reactor, leveraging the BBD within the RSM framework. Distinct from prior research, the work elucidates complex, nonlinear interactions among critical operational variables—including slurry concentration, liquid volume, gas hold-up, and operating temperature—providing a holistic understanding of their synergistic effects on absorption efficiency. The research reports a notably high removal efficiency (up to 82.1%) under optimized conditions, and, importantly, highlights a counterintuitive finding: higher Mg(OH)₂ concentrations can negatively impact performance due to increased viscosity and aggregation effects.

Understanding and optimizing the behavior of Mg(OH)₂ slurries in bubble column reactors offers a unique avenue to overcome several limitations of traditional carbon capture strategies-addressing issues of solvent degradation, operational cost, and regeneration efficiency. By targeting key operational variables through advanced statistical design, this work not only enhances the fundamental understanding of gas–liquid–solid interactions in such systems but also charts a path toward scalable, industrially viable carbon capture solutions. The insights gained herein have the potential to significantly influence the future landscape of post-combustion CO₂ mitigation, supporting the transition to cleaner energy systems and aligning with global climate targets. As such, this study directly responds to pressing environmental imperatives while laying the groundwork for the adoption of mineral-based, sustainable capture technologies at scale.
