Performance of hydroxy-aluminum–stabilized expansive soil as a covering layer
Authors: Jianbo She, Shuai Li, Yongwei Zhou, Shaohua Xian, Yousheng Tang, Zheng Lu, Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpansive soil, a problematic clay soil susceptible to significant volume changes with moisture variation, is typically mitigated using cohesive non-swelling soil (CNS) covering technology. This study evaluates the comprehensive performance and feasibility of using chemically stabilized expansive soil (CSS) treated with hydroxy-aluminum solution as a replacement for natural CNS as a covering layer for expansive soil (ES). Through a series of physical-mechanical tests, chemical analyses, and laboratory model tests, the inhibition effect of different covering layers (CSS, CNS, and gravel) on expansive soil was investigated, along with the relationship between electrical resistivity and swelling deformation. The results indicate that: (1) After modification with hydroxy-aluminum solution, the engineering properties of expansive soil were substantially improved, with shear strength enhanced and swelling percentage significantly reduced. Overall, CSS exhibited performance comparable to that of CNS. (2) The CSS layer provided the most effective inhibition of expansive soil, reducing swelling deformation by approximately 67%--a clear improvement over both gravel and CNS layers, thereby confirming the viability of CSS as an alternative covering material. (3) A strong correlation was observed between the resistivity ratio and the swelling rate of the expansive soil layer. Specifically, this relationship followed a linear function under the gravel and CNS layers, whereas it exhibited a power function under the CSS layer. These findings offer valuable insights for the engineering application of CSS covering technology and for the use of electrical resistivity as a practical evaluation method.Keywords: Expansive soil, Hydroxy-aluminum solution, Chemically stabilized expansive soil, Cohesive non-swelling soil, Electrical resistivitySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Expansive soil (ES) is a special type of clay soil characterized by significant swelling-shrinkage behavior, which poses hazards to various types of shallow, lightweight engineering structures. The in-situ treatment of expansive soil remains a challenging problem for both the engineering and academic communities. Over the years, the cohesive non-swelling soil (CNS) covering technology has been widely applied in subgrade and slope improvement in expansive soil regions, due to its advantages of simplicity, safety, and environmental friendliness1–3. The effectiveness of this method is closely related to the ionic composition of the CNS material. Specifically, the migration and accumulation of cations from the CNS layer into the expansive soil are considered the primary mechanism inhibiting swelling deformation, an effect found to be more significant than the self-weight effect of the CNS layer4,5. However, in practice, suitable natural CNS materials are often scarce. As an alternative, traditional additives such as lime6,7, fly ash6,7, and fibers8–10 have been extensively investigated for enhancing the properties of expansive soils11. Nevertheless, these conventional stabilizers face significant limitations. For instance, studies have shown that lime treatment can be effective but may suffer from durability issues under cyclic wetting-drying conditions12,13. Due to the agglomerated structure of natural expansive soil, achieving uniform mixing on-site with these additives is difficult, often resulting in low construction efficiency and poor performance. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop a new type of stabilizer and in-situ treatment technology.

Recent advances in chemical stabilization have explored various ionic and polymer-based modifiers. Hydroxy-aluminum solution, as a novel ionic stabilizer, has gained attention due to its unique properties. It contains abundant Al13 polymer ([AlO4Al12(OH)24(H2O)12]7+, [Al13]7+), which is considered an ideal intercalator and chemical stabilizer for swelling clay minerals14,15. When crosslinked with montmorillonite, the Al13 polymer can be adsorbed in the crystal layer, forming a strong and irreversible thermally stable pillared structure16,17. Compared to traditional additives, hydroxy-aluminum solution exhibits high charge density, water solubility, and electrical conductivity. Previous studies have demonstrated that it can significantly improve the properties of swelling clay soils through mixing and blending methods18–20. Furthermore, some studies indicate that it can be combined with electrochemical methods such as electroosmosis to achieve in-situ modification of expansive soil, with notable improvements in both basic physical properties and swelling behavior21. Studies have also highlighted the potential of aluminum-based stabilizers for enhancing clay durability and reducing hydraulic conductivity. Notably, Yazdandoust et al22. reported that polymer-stabilized expansive soils maintain long-term stability in swelling inhibition and do not fail after wet-dry cycles. As a typical cationic polymer, hydroxy-aluminum solution contains abundant Al13 polymer that can be adsorbed onto clay particle surfaces, forming stable complexes23,24 that are firmly fixed within the soil solid phase and difficult to release25. Therefore, combining hydroxy-aluminum solution with in-situ treatment technology to modify shallow expansive soil for use as a covering layer–replacing the natural CNS layer for deeper soil–holds promise for achieving a synergistic effect: stabilizing the shallow layer while inhibiting the swelling of deeper soil layer. Such a strategy could overcome the limitations of conventional CNS technology and shows strong potential for practical engineering applications.

Despite these advances, research on the stabilization of expansive soil using hydroxy-aluminum solution remains limited. To date, no studies have reported on the use of such stabilized expansive soil as a covering layer, nor has its performance been systematically compared with that of natural CNS material. Whether chemically stabilized expansive soil (CSS) can perform equivalently to CNS requires further validation. This study aims to investigate the feasibility of using CSS as an alternative covering material. Specifically, it attempts to: (1) evaluate the performance of CSS as a covering layer rather than merely as a mixed stabilizer, through systematic comparison with natural CNS under various compaction conditions; and (2) explore the quantitative relationship between swelling inhibition and electrical resistivity evolution, which has rarely been investigated in previous studies. For this purpose, a series of laboratory tests were conducted to examine the permeability, shear strength, swelling characteristics, and cation concentration of ES, CSS, and CNS, followed by laboratory model tests to assess the inhibition effect of different covering layers (CSS, CNS, and gravel) on expansive soil under unidirectional water infiltration. The findings are expected to validate the feasibility of replacing CNS with CSS as a covering layer, and provide a foundation for further application and evaluation of in-situ CSS treatment technology.

Materials and methods

Materials

The hydroxy-aluminum solution was prepared using the alkali neutralization titration method26. In accordance with the procedures described by Yao et al15. and She et al18., the following parameters were adopted to obtain a solution with a relatively high content of Al13: the total Al concentration was 0.1 mol/L, the OH/Al molar ratio was controlled at 2.5 with a reaction temperature of 60 °C, and an alkali neutralization rate of 50 mL/min. The resulting solution was aged at room temperature for 1 week.

Based on the previous studies18, CSS in this study was prepared by directly adding hydroxy-aluminum solution to expansive soil, and the Al/soil ratio of CSS was set at 0.18 mmol/g. Naturally air-dried expansive soil was first oven-dried, ground, and passed through a 0.5 mm sieve. It was then thoroughly mixed with the hydroxy-aluminum solution at the specified ratio and aged at room temperature for 4 weeks. After removing the supernatant from the crosslinked expansive soil, the remaining soil was air-dried, ground, and sieved to obtain the CSS sample. The expansive soil sample was collected from a publicly accessible highway cut slope in Nanning City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China. The CNS sample was obtained from an authorized borrow pit in Wuhan City, Hubei Province, China. These are the same sources as those used in our previous research4,5,18. As indicated in Table 1, the CNS is classified as low liquid limit clay (CL), while the expansive soil is identified as high liquid limit clay (CH) with a free swelling ratio of 65.5%. The hydroxy-aluminum solution significantly improved the physical properties of expansive soil: the silt content increased and the clay content decreased, resulting in a significant reduction in the plasticity index from 38.06 to 21.49, corresponding to a decrease of 43.5%. Experimental program

The experimental program was designed to evaluate the feasibility of using CSS as an alternative covering layer for expansive soil. The program consisted of two phases: basic property tests and laboratory model tests.

Basic property tests. This phase compared the basic physical-mechanical and chemical properties of expansive soil, CSS, and natural CNS. For each material, permeability, shear strength, swelling percentage and cation concentration tests were conducted. It should be noted that the inclusion of the shear strength tests is based on the requirement that a covering layer must not only inhibit swelling but also maintain mechanical stability under external loads.

Laboratory model tests. This phase evaluated the inhibition effect of different covering layers (CSS, CNS, and gravel) on expansive soil using a custom-designed model apparatus. Four laboratory model tests were designed, as outlined in Table 2.

In all tests, the thickness of the expansive soil layer was kept constant at 6 cm. Tests 1 and 2 were designed to examine the effect of CSS layers with different thicknesses on expansive soil. Electrical resistivity, which is a recognized indicator for characterizing soil structure and performance28–31, has been employed in the in-situ testing and evaluation of frozen soil32,33, saline soil34, and expansive soil35,36. Our previous research also demonstrated its utility in analyzing the effectiveness of CNS layers in treating expansive soil5. Therefore, electrical resistivity monitoring was incorporated in this study.

Following the method proposed by She et al37. and Yao et al4., the tests were conducted using a transparent acrylic model box, as shown in Fig. 2. Previous studies using this setup demonstrated consistent correlations between ion migration and swelling inhibition across both small and large scales, validating its use for comparative evaluation. The dimensions of the model box are 30 cm × 30 cm × 30 cm, with scale markings attached to the external surfaces of all four sides, providing an accuracy of 1 mm. The experiment utilized a unidirectional water injection method for saturation, focusing on monitoring the electrical resistivity of each soil layer and the swelling deformation of the expansive soil layer during the saturation process.

Test procedures

Basic property tests

Basic physical-mechanical tests. The experimental program included permeability tests, direct shear tests, and swelling percentage tests, all conducted in accordance with the Chinese standard JTG 3430 − 202027. The permeability tests were performed using the variable head method. Direct shear tests were carried out with a strain-controlled apparatus under vertical pressures of 50, 100, 200, and 400 kPa, at a shearing rate of 0.8 mm/min. The swelling percentage tests were conducted using a consolidation device. Naturally air-dried expansive soil, CNS, and CSS were ground and passed through a 2 mm sieve. All soil specimens were prepared at the optimum moisture content with four compaction degrees (100%, 95%, 90%, and 85%). Prior to testing, specimens for the permeability and direct shear tests were placed in a vacuum container for de-airing and water saturation.

Cation concentration tests. As suggested in the Chinese standard JTG 3430 − 202027, all soil samples were oven-dried, ground, and passed through a 1 mm sieve. A measured amount of soil was then weighed and transferred into a centrifuge bottle. De-ionized water was added at a soil/water mass ratio of 1:1, followed by vigorous shaking for 3 min to ensure thorough mixing. The mixture was then centrifuged to separate the leachate. The concentrations of K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺ in the leachate were determined.

Laboratory model tests

Throughout the testing period, the model box was maintained in an indoor environment with a controlled temperature of 20 °C to eliminate temperature effects on electrical resistivity measurements.

As shown, the permeability coefficients of the three soil samples ranged from 10− 7 to 10− 9 cm/s, and were inversely correlated with the degree of compaction. The expansive soil exhibited the lowest permeability, ranging from 10− 8 to 10− 9 cm/s. After modification with hydroxy-aluminum solution, the permeability coefficient of CSS reached 10− 7 cm/s, representing an increase of 1 ~ 2 orders of magnitude compared to expansive soil. This increase is attributed to the flocculation and aggregation of clay particles induced by the hydroxy-aluminum treatment, which reduces soil dispersibility and swelling potential while enlarging inter-particle pores18. Importantly, although CSS exhibits higher permeability than untreated expansive soil, its absolute value remains within the range of 10− 7 cm/s–characteristic of low-permeability clay soil. Furthermore, the permeability coefficient of CNS ranged from 10− 7 to 10− 8 cm/s, closely matching that of CSS. This indicates that CSS achieves permeability performance comparable to CNS, suggesting that the modest increase in permeability does not compromise its suitability as a covering material.

Shear strength

Figures 4 and 5 present the variations in shear strength and strength parameters of ES, CNS, and CSS under different compaction degrees.

Variations in shear strength of soil samples under various compaction degrees. (a) K = 85%, (b) K = 90%, (c) K = 95%, (d) K = 100%.

Variations in shear strength parameters of soil samples under various compaction degrees. (a) cohesion, (b) internal friction angle.

It can be seen from Fig. 4 that the shear strength of all soil samples increased with the degree of compaction. Higher compaction led to greater dry density, tighter particle bonding, and enhanced resistance to shear failure, which was macroscopically reflected in an increased shear strength. Notably, the shear strength of expansive soil was relatively low, while those of CNS and CSS were comparatively higher.

As shown in Fig. 5, CNS exhibited the lowest cohesion but the highest internal friction angle at the same degree of compaction. This can be attributed to its low clay content and its high silt content (Table 1). After modification with hydroxy-aluminum solution, both cohesion and internal friction angle of expansive soil increased. This improvement is due to the adsorption of Al13 and other polymers from the hydroxy-aluminum solution onto the clay particle surfaces, leading to ion exchange and flocculation-agglomeration effects18. These processes alter the particle size distribution, increasing the content of coarse particles such as silt, which in turn enhances both cohesion and internal friction angle in CSS. Therefore, hydroxy-aluminum solution significantly improves the shear strength of expansive soil. At a high degree of compaction (K = 100%), the strength of CSS even exceeded that of CNS (Fig. 4(d)).

Swelling percentage

The swelling percentages of the soil samples under different degrees of compaction are presented in Fig. 6.

Swelling percentage of soil samples under various compaction degrees. (a) ES, (b) CSS, (c) CNS.

It can be observed that the swelling percentage of all soil samples exhibited a nonlinear increase over time, with a rapid rise in the initial phase followed by a gradual stabilization in later stages. Notable differences were observed in the swelling behavior of expansive soil under various compaction degrees (Fig. 6(a)). At lower compaction levels (K = 85%~90%), larger internal pore spaces within the expansive soil allowed for rapid water infiltration, causing swelling deformation to increase quickly in the initial stage and nearly stabilize within 50 ~ 100 min. As compaction increased, the number of permeable pores decreased. Consequently, the initial swelling rate slowed, and the time required for deformation stabilization was extended accordingly38. In contrast, the swelling percentage curves of CSS and CNS were similar across compaction degrees, reaching near-stability at around 400 min and 300 min, respectively. This indicates that modification effect reduces the dispersibility and hydrophilicity of expansive soil, and inhibits its swelling potential. As discussed in Sect. 3.1.1, the comparable permeability coefficients of CSS and CNS also contributed to their similar swelling development trends.

As shown in Fig. 7, the swelling percentage was positively correlated with the degree of compaction. Expansive soil showed the highest swelling percentage, ranging from 16% to 23%, while that of CNS was the lowest, ranging from 5% to 12%. The swelling percentage of CSS ranged from 8% to 15%, approximately 3% higher than that of CNS but representing a reduction of 37.5%~50% compared to expansive soil. These results demonstrate that hydroxy-aluminum solution significantly reduces the swelling potential of expansive soil, bringing its values close to those of CNS.

Stable swelling percentage of soil samples under various compaction degrees.

In summary, expansive soil stabilized with hydroxy-aluminum solution shows notable improvements in permeability, shear strength, and swelling characteristics. The comprehensive performance of CSS is comparable to that of CNS, confirming its potential for use as a covering layer for expansive soil.

The predominant cations in the leachate of CNS and expansive soil were Ca2+, followed by Mg2+, while the concentrations of K+ and Na+ were relatively low. Compared to expansive soil, the CSS showed a significant increase in the concentrations of K+, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺. This increase is attributed to ion exchange resulting from the adsorption of Al13and other polymers from the hydroxy-aluminum solution into the interlayers of montmorillonite and onto clay particle surfaces. This process displaces lower-valence cations such as K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺, leading to their increased release into the leachate. The notably high concentration of Na⁺ in CSS may also be partly due to residual sodium from the alkaline solution used in preparing the hydroxy-aluminum solution, consistent with findings reported by She et al18. Additionally, the concentrations of K+, Na+ and Ca2+ in CSS were significantly higher than those in CNS, while Mg2+ concentration remained similar. These results suggest that CSS used as a covering layer may be more effective than a CNS layer based on previous studies4,5.

Laboratory model tests

Swelling deformation

Figure 8 shows the swelling deformation curves of the expansive soil layer under saturated condition in each model.

Swelling deformation curves of the expansive soil layer in each model.

It can be observed that after the injection of de-ionized water, the swelling deformation of the expansive soil layer initially increased rapidly and then gradually stabilized over time. Specifically, in the absence of a covering layer (Model ES 6 + CSS 0), the swelling deformation increased most quickly, reaching 6 mm on 1 st day and 10.5 mm on 2nd day, stabilizing around 8th day. In contrast, the swelling of expansive soil was inhibited when covered with a gravel or CNS layer. This inhibition effect was more pronounced under the CNS layer, consistent with previous studies4,5. In Model ES 6 + CSS 9, noticeable swelling deformation of the expansive soil layer occurred only after 6th day, and the final stable deformation was lower than that in Model ES 6 + CNS 9. This indicates that the CSS layer provides more effective inhibition during both the initial and later-stage swelling compared to the CNS layer.

Figure 9 illustrates the stable swelling deformation of the expansive soil layer for each model. The greatest deformation occurred in the model without a covering layer, reaching 12 mm, corresponding to a swelling rate of 20% (the ratio of swelling deformation to soil layer thickness). This value is nearly equal to the swelling percentage of expansive soil at 19% (Fig. 7). With the gravel and CNS layers applied, the swelling deformation was reduced to 10 mm and 5 mm, corresponding to reductions of 16.67% and 58.33%, respectively. The results indicate that the self-weight of the gravel layer partially mitigates the swelling of expansive soil, although its inhibition effect is less pronounced compared to that of the CNS layer. When a CSS layer was applied, the swelling deformation was further reduced to only 4 mm, a 66.67% reduction, representing improvement of 50% and 8.34% over the gravel and CNS layers, respectively. Clearly, under similar self-weight conditions, the CSS layer provides the most effective inhibition. This superior performance is likely attributed to the presence of Al13 polymer and the higher concentration of cations in the CSS layer compared to the CNS layer (Table 5).

Electrical resistivity

This study monitored the electrical resistivity of both the expansive soil layer and the covering layers, denoted as X1-X2 and X2-X3 (where X represents the letters A, B, C, D, corresponding to measurement points at the four corners of the respective soil layers, see Fig. 2). Figure 10 presents the variation in electrical resistivity for Models ES 6 + CSS 9 and ES 6 + CNS 9 during the saturation process.

Variations in electrical resistivity of soil layers in Models ES 6 + CNS 9 and ES 6 + CSS 9.

The variation patterns of electrical resistivity in both models were similar. After water injection, the electrical resistivity of the expansive soil layer decreased significantly, dropping from approximately 21Ω.m and 27Ω.m to around 3.6Ω.m and 0.6Ω.m, respectively. In contrast, that of the CNS and CSS layers changed only slightly, decreasing from about 4.7Ω.m and 0.8Ω.m to around 3.6Ω.m and 0.6Ω.m, respectively. A notable difference in resistivity variation was observed between the expansive soil layer and the covering layers, although the stable resistivity values of both layers eventually converged to around 3.6Ω.m and 0.6Ω.m, respectively.

Specifically, the low initial resistivity of the CNS and CSS layers can be attributed to their high initial moisture content (14.17% and 16.36%, respectively, exceeding their optimum moisture content of 13.8% and 15.96%, as shown in Tables 1 and 3). Under this condition, pore water connectivity was enhanced. Furthermore, both layers were rich in ionic components such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and Al13 polymer (see Table 5), which significantly improved the conductivity of soil particles and pore water, leading to lower initial resistivity. The consistently lower resistivity of the CSS layer compared to the CNS layer can be explained by its higher initial moisture content and greater ion concentration. During saturation, although increased moisture content further enhanced the overall conductivity of the covering layers, the low initial resistivity meant that this change did not cause a significant further reduction. As a result, the electrical resistivity of both covering layers remained largely stable throughout the saturation process.

For the expansive soil layer, the difference in initial resistivity (approximately 20Ω.m and 27Ω.m, respectively) can also be attributed to variations in initial moisture content (16.57% and 15.83%, respectively, see Table 3). After water injection, the resistivity decreased significantly due to both the increased moisture content and the downward migration and accumulation of cations from the covering layers, which collectively enhanced electrical conductivity. Once the soil reached saturation, continuous and stable pore water pathways had formed between the expansive soil and covering layers, the resistivity of the soil became primarily governed by the resistivity of the pore water39. Since the types and concentrations of cations in the pores of expansive soil were largely influenced by migration from the covering layers, the stable resistivity values of the two layers became essentially consistent.

Figure 11 shows the variation in electrical resistivity of the expansive soil layer in different models. The resistivity in Model ES 6 + gravel 9 was significantly higher than that in Models ES 6 + CNS 9 and ES 6 + CSS 9. This difference is not only related to its lower initial moisture content (w = 15.83%) but also to the fact that the pore water from the covering layers seeps into the expansive soil layer5. Additionally, during the saturation process, the downward migration and accumulation of cations from the covering layers further reduced the resistivity of the expansive soil. Consequently, the electrical resistivity in Model ES6 + Gravel 9 remained relatively higher overall.

The variation of the resistivity ratio and swelling rate over time for the expansive soil layer in each model is presented in Fig. 12.

Variations of the resistivity ratio and swelling rate over time for the expansive soil layer.

As shown in Fig. 12, a strong correlation was observed between the resistivity ratio and swelling rate. In Model ES 6 + gravel 9, the swelling rate increased rapidly during the first 4 days, with the resistivity ratio decreasing sharply, and both stabilized after 6th day. In Model ES 6 + CNS 9, the resistivity ratio and swelling rate changed significantly during the first 6 days, respectively, and then began to stabilize. In contrast, for Model ES 6 + CSS 9, the resistivity ratio rapidly decreased during the first 8 days, but swelling deformation was only observed after 6th day, and both parameters then stabilized after 14th day. This phenomenon is attributed to the inhibition effect caused by downward-migrating Al13 polymer and cations such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ from the CSS layer migrating into the expansive soil4,5, which prevented swelling during the first 6 days. Furthermore, Model ES 6+ CSS 9 exhibited the lowest swelling rate and resistivity ratio, Model ES 6 + gravel 9 the highest, and Model ES 6 + CNS 9 intermediate values. Clearly, a higher ionic content in the covering layer leads to greater accumulation of migrated cations in the expansive soil, resulting in a more significant inhibition effect. Macroscopically, this is reflected in slower development of swelling deformation, a more gradual decrease in resistivity, and a longer time to reach stability. As a result, the final stable values of both swelling rate and resistivity ratio are lower.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that CSS achieves performance comparable to natural CNS, validating its potential as an alternative covering material. The superior swelling inhibition of CSS (66.67% reduction vs. 58.33% for CNS and 16.67% for gravel) is attributed to its abundant Al13 polymer and higher concentrations of K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺, which migrate into the underlying expansive soil. This ion migration mechanism is consistent with our team’s previous findings on CNS-based swelling inhibition4,5, which demonstrated that cation migration from covering layers is the primary factor inhibiting swelling deformation. Compared to conventional stabilizers (e.g., lime, cement, silica fume)41-43 and physical reinforcement methods (e.g., micropiles)44, CSS offers distinct advantages. Due to its high charge density, water solubility, and electrical conductivity, hydroxy-aluminum solution enables not only conventional mixing stabilization but also in-situ electrochemical treatment of shallow expansive soil for use as a covering layer, achieving efficient field modification. This dual applicability addresses the construction challenges associated with traditional additives while expanding the potential for practical implementation.

While this study provides encouraging evidence for the feasibility of CSS as an alternative to CNS, several aspects warrant further investigation. First, although the ion migration mechanism is supported by higher cation concentrations in the CSS pore solution (Table 5) and consistent with observed macroscopic behavior (Figs. 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12), direct depth-dependent chemical profiling would provide more definitive evidence of cation accumulation in the underlying expansive soil. Second, the laboratory model tests have demonstrated the feasibility of CSS as an alternative to CNS, but the long-term durability of CSS under wet-dry cycles has not been addressed. While literature indicates that polymer-stabilized soils maintain stability after cycles22, systematic durability experiments are needed. Third, although hydroxy-aluminum solution (polyaluminum chloride, PAC) benefits from mature industrial production processes and has been widely applied in water treatment45, commercially available PAC typically contains lower Al13 content than the laboratory-prepared solution used in this study. Further process optimization to enhance Al13 yield while maintaining cost-effectiveness would be valuable for meeting the technical requirements of expansive soil modification. Additionally, a comprehensive cost-benefit analysis comparing CSS with alternatives will be essential to fully establish its economic viability for large-scale applications.

Despite these considerations, this study makes two primary contributions: demonstrating CSS as a technically viable alternative to CNS for covering layer applications, and establishing the quantitative relationships between electrical resistivity evolution and swelling deformation for non-destructive monitoring. Future research addressing durability, ion migration quantification, Al13 content optimization, and economic analysis will further support the transition of CSS technology to engineering practice.

Conclusions

This study investigated changes in permeability, shear strength, swelling characteristics and cation concentration of expansive soil, CNS, and CSS. Laboratory model tests were further conducted to evaluate the inhibition effect of various covering layers (CSS, CNS, and gravel) on expansive soil. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) Treatment with hydroxy-aluminum solution substantially improved the engineering properties of expansive soil. Enhanced aggregation and cementation between clay particles led to increased permeability and shear strength, while the swelling percentage decreased by 37.5%−50%. Although the swelling percentage of CSS remained slightly higher than that of CNS, it still fell within the range typical of non-swelling soils, indicating that CSS achieves performance comparable to CNS.

(2) The inhibition effect on expansive soil varied markedly with covering layer type. The gravel layer, which relies primarily on self-weight stress, provided only a 16.67% reduction in swelling deformation. In contrast, both the CNS and CSS layers demonstrated superior performance, reducing swelling deformation from 12 mm to 5 mm and 4 mm, respectively, corresponding to decreases of 58.33% and 66.67%. The superior performance of CSS is attributed to its abundant Al13 polymer and higher concentrations of cations (e.g., K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) compared to CNS, which enhance ionic migration into the underlying expansive soil and inhibit swelling more effectively.

(3) A strong correlation was observed between the resistivity ratio and the swelling rate of the expansive soil layer, with the relationship varying with covering type. Under the gravel and CNS layers, this relationship followed a linear function, whereas under the CSS layer, it exhibited a power function. Higher cation concentrations in the covering layer promoted greater cation accumulation in the expansive soil, which macroscopically manifested in slower swelling deformation, a more gradual resistivity decrease, and ultimately lower stable values of both swelling rate and resistivity ratio.

(4) These findings demonstrate that CSS can serve as an effective alternative to natural CNS for expansive soil covering applications, offering equivalent or even superior performance. The established relationship between electrical resistivity and swelling behavior provides a practical, non-destructive method for monitoring the in-situ performance of covering layers. This approach holds promise for advancing CSS-based in-situ treatment technology in expansive soil regions, potentially overcoming the limitations associated with scarce natural CNS materials.
