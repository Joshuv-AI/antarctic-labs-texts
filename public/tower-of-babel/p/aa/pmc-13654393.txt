Giant Aneurysm Involving the Sinus of Valsalva and the Right Coronary Artery with a Massive Intramural Thrombus
Authors: Jie Liu, Yuji Xie, Yifan Liu, Ying Zhong, Yuman Li, He Li, Mingxing Xie
Published: 2026

A 44-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with dyspnea and wheezing. Her history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia raised the possibility of underlying coronary artery disease; however, given her age, gender, and clinical presentation, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, pulmonary disease, and other non-atherosclerotic etiologies also were considered. An electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with non-specific ST changes and counterclockwise rotation. Initial transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large sac-like structure (25 × 31 mm) arising from the right sinus of Valsalva, raising suspicion of a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SoVA; Fig.1, A and B; Videos 1 and 2 , view videos online). The aortic valve was tricuspid with no stenosis and mild regurgitation. The left ventricular ejection fraction was normal. Computed tomography (CT) performed after the echocardiogram reported a giant right coronary artery aneurysm (CAA; 39 × 48 mm) with calcified arterial wall and thrombi (Fig. 1, C and D; Video 3 , view video online). The distal segment of the left anterior descending coronary artery was dilated (3.6 mm). CT 3-dimensional imaging of the entire aorta demonstrates dilation of the ascending aorta and aortic arch (Supplemental Fig. S1). Subsequently, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was obtained. The TEE revealed an elongated aneurysm involving the right sinus of Valsalva and the proximal right coronary artery (RCA; 33 × 50 mm), which had calcified wall and intra-aneurysmal thrombosis (Fig. 1, E-I; Videos 4-7 , view videos online). The aneurysm involved the right coronary sinus and extended contiguously into the proximal RCA, forming a single continuous aneurysmal lesion. In our case, the aneurysm was continuous, with no normal segment intervening between the sinus and the proximal RCA. Intraoperative findings confirmed the large aneurysm involving the proximal RCA and the right sinus of Valsalva (Fig. 1J). The gross pathology showed a true aneurysm with abundant thrombi and calcifications (Fig. 1, K and L). This patient underwent aneurysm resection with distal coronary artery bypass grafting and thrombectomy. The patient achieved an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on the 10th day after surgery.

The incidence of both SoVA and CAA is low,1,2 and their co-occurrence is exceedingly rare. The reported incidence of SoVA ranges from 1.2% to 4.94% in Asian populations, compared to 0.5% to 1.5% in Western populations.3 Similarly, CAAs are infrequent, with giant CAA being particularly rare, with an approximate incidence of 0.02%.2 Although no uniform diagnostic criterion exists for giant CAA, the most frequently applied threshold in adults is > 20 mm, with alternative definitions including > 8 mm and > 4 times the reference vessel diameter.2 SoVA and CAA are caused by congenital or acquired etiologies. Here, we report a case of an aneurysm with high-risk morphology involving the right sinus of Valsalva and the proximal RCA, which posed diagnostic challenges. The high-risk features included the large size, along with thrombosis and calcification.4 Given the patient’s hypertension and hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis is the most plausible cause of the coronary aneurysms. The giant continuous aneurysm is likely congenital, with secondary dystrophic calcification and thrombus formation exacerbated by the same risk factors. The presence of multiple aneurysms suggests a generalized arterial wall vulnerability. Connective tissue disorders and vasculitis were considered, but they lacked clinical or imaging support; we acknowledge the absence of genetic testing as a limitation. Initial transthoracic echocardiography was insufficient for definitive diagnosis. CT was essential for anatomic characterization of this aneurysm, providing detailed information on its size, extent, and relation to adjacent structures. However, intraoperative TEE offers unique value by displaying the origin and extent of complex aneurysms in real time, assessing hemodynamics and cardiac function, guiding surgical strategy, and enabling immediate post-repair assessment. Regular postoperative transthoracic echocardiography follow-up revealed no significant peripatch residual shunt (Supplemental Fig. S2); however, aortic valve regurgitation progressed from mild to moderate at 16 months (Supplemental Fig. S3). This case highlights the importance of considering a combination of multiple imaging modalities to obtain the correct diagnosis and aid preoperative planning.
