Aortic Coarctation Assessed With a Multimodality Approach
Authors: Zinya H. Talukder, Prashilkumar Patel, Muneeba Ali, Asha Marwaha, Ross G. Biggs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraumatic aortic injury is the second most common cause of death after blunt trauma in motor vehicle accidents. Aortic coarctation leading to resistant hypertension is a rare late complication of aortic repair.Case SummaryWe present a case of a 50-year-old male with previous history of aortic repair presenting with resistant hypertension. Multimodal imaging with computed tomography angiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed focal narrowing of the proximal descending thoracic aorta requiring surgical repair.DiscussionThis case highlights the importance of a multimodal diagnostic approach to diagnose clinically significant aortic coarctation and underscores the need for vigilance for secondary causes of resistant hypertension.Take-Home MessagesIn the workup of resistant hypertension in patients with prior aortic surgery, the use of multimodal imaging to assess stenosis, graft status, and collaterals is essential. Anatomical intervention leads to improved blood pressure control; however, postrepair surveillance for hypertension and vascular is critical.

History of Presentation

A 50-year-old male presented with lower-extremity claudication and exertional fatigue for 2 to 4 years. The physical examination was grossly unremarkable with chest clear to auscultation and normal heart sounds.Take-Home Messages•In the workup of resistant hypertension in patients with prior aortic surgery, the use of multimodal imaging (CTA + MRI/MRA with phase-contrast) to assess stenosis, graft status, and collaterals is essential.•Anatomical intervention leads to improved blood pressure control; however, postrepair surveillance for hypertension and vascular is critical.

Past Medical History

He has past medical history of traumatic proximal descending aortic and left subclavian artery transection after a motor vehicle accident at age 17 requiring open surgical repair, resistant hypertension (on 4 antihypertensive agents), aortic valve insufficiency, and hyperlipidemia.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included peripheral artery disease (PAD), coronary artery disease, aortoiliac disease, vasculitis, and myopathies.

Investigations

Initial electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm. Resting ankle-brachial index (ABI) testing was consistent with mild PAD in lower extremities (Table 1). Aorto-iliac duplex testing confirmed the absence of PAD. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) chest revealed a flap-like filling defect within the aortic lumen near the distal aspect of the prior aortic repair, with a minimal aortic lumen diameter of 1.2 × 0.8 cm (Figure 1). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) demonstrated focal narrowing of the proximal descending thoracic aorta measuring 1.3 × 1.5 cm. Phase-contrast imaging revealed a maximum systolic velocity through this region of 2.7 m/s. Management

The patient underwent repair of his aortic coarctation and transposition of his left vertebral artery from the aortic arch to his left subclavian artery. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed graft material from the origin of the left subclavian artery to the proximal descending thoracic aorta; there was calcification within the graft causing luminal narrowing with turbulent flow on color-flow Doppler.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's postoperative course was uncomplicated. He required fewer antihypertensive agents and experienced complete resolution of his lower-extremity claudication. Discussion

Resistant hypertension is defined as blood pressure (BP) that remains above the goal despite the concurrent use of 3 antihypertensive agents of different classes.1 Secondary causes such as congenital and acquired narrowing of the aorta (coarctation) must be considered. The congenital form of coarctation of the aorta (CoA) presents in childhood, but adult presentation is increasingly recognized and remains associated with long-term morbidity despite repair.2,3 In patients with prior aortic repair, acquired coarctation may manifest as resistant hypertension. The American Heart Association scientific statement defines resistant hypertension as BP that remains above target levels despite optimal therapy with at least 3 antihypertensive agents of different classes, including a diuretic, after exclusion of pseudo-resistance. American Heart Association and European Society of Cardiology estimate a prevalence of 12% and 10% to 20% of treated hypertensive patients, respectively.1,4

Although the pathogenesis of congenital and acquired CoA is not fully understood, proposed mechanisms include reduced anterograde intrauterine flow to the fetal arch and migration of ductal tissue into the fetal aortic wall.5 In acquired forms, abnormal hemodynamics, endothelial dysfunction, and ductal tissue deposition contribute to aortic narrowing.6

In adults with prior aortic repair, late-detected or recurrent narrowing may emerge as a cause of secondary hypertension.3 A systematic review revealed development of hypertension of 47% even after a successful CoA repair.3,7 Our patient underwent aortic repair in adolescence and developed resistant hypertension and lower-extremity claudication decades later, which was consistent with acquired coarctation.

The pathophysiology of resistant hypertension involves increased sympathetic nervous system activity, volume overload, and altered vascular structure and function.1 European Society of Cardiology guidelines emphasize first excluding pseudo-resistance such as white-coat effect, measurement error, and nonadherence.4 In CoA, hypertension results from the narrowing of the aorta which leads to an increase in proximal aortic pressures, left ventricular afterload, left ventricular hypertrophy, and elevated systemic pressures.3 Reduced perfusion distal to the coarctation triggers activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, further exacerbating hypertension.8 Even after surgical repair, patients with CoA often exhibit residual aortic stiffness, impaired endothelial function, and abnormal arch geometry, contributing to persistent hypertension and cardiovascular risk.3,6 Our patient's MRI revealed a peak velocity of 2.7 m/s at the site of the narrowing and the absence of prominent collaterals supporting the hemodynamic significance of the lesion.

Risk factors for resistant hypertension include obesity, high salt intake, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol use, obstructive sleep apnea, chronic kidney disease, older age, and polypharmacy or medication nonadherence.1,4 After repair of CoA, risk factors for persistent or recurrent hypertension include older age at repair, residual stenosis or re-coarctation, complex anatomy, concomitant congenital lesions, such as bicuspid aortic valve, and posttraumatic or postsurgical etiologies which may predispose to graft failure or intimal hyperplasia.2,3 In our patient, the presence of graft calcification, luminal narrowing several decades after repair, and long-standing hypertension suggest that multiple risk factors contributed to his presentation.

Evaluation of resistant hypertension includes excluding pseudo-resistance via adherence assessment and home/ambulatory BP, identifying modifiable lifestyle/medication factors, then evaluating for secondary causes.1,4 For CoA, clinical examination to assess extremity BPs, pulse delays, and signs of extremity claudication are fundamental.3 Imaging such as Doppler ultrasound of aortic vessels, CTA, MRI/MRA, and phase-contrast flow assessment are also essential. Peak systolic velocities >2 m/s or minimal lumen diameters below certain thresholds raise suspicion of hemodynamic significance.2,3 Invasive angiography remains reserved for when intervention is planned or noninvasive imaging is inconclusive. Our patient's resting ABI and aorto-iliac duplex ruled out primary PAD. His CTA identified a focal aortic narrowing, and MRI/MRA revealed a minimal increase in phase-contrast velocity without any significant collaterals (Table 2). After confirming adherence and optimizing lifestyle, management of resistant hypertension includes escalating pharmacologic therapy using a thiazide-like diuretic, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist followed by additional agents as required.1,7 In CoA, anatomical correction via surgical or percutaneous stenting is critical. Guidelines recommend percutaneous stenting when feasible or surgical repair when anatomy is complex.2,3 In our patient, the decision for redo thoracotomy with graft revision and subclavian/vertebral artery transposition resulted in a reduction in antihypertensive usage and resolution of claudication. Postrepair, antihypertensive therapy is continued as hypertension persists in 20% to 70% of patients. Monitoring with ambulatory BP monitoring, exercise testing, and imaging for residual arch abnormality or recoarctation is essential.3

Conclusions

Resistant hypertension in a patient with a history of traumatic aortic repair warrants careful consideration of acquired aortic coarctation. Multimodality imaging, including CTA and MRI/MRA with phase-contrast velocity assessment, can characterize the lesion and guide intervention.
