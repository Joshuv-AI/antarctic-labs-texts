Patient flow and safety after implementing a community paramedicine service: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Odd Eirik Elden, Emma Brulin, Oddvar Uleberg, Bodil J Landstad, Havard Dalen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveCommunity paramedicine services (CPSs) may alleviate the increasing pressure on emergency medical services (EMSs) but lack the capacity for patient transport. The study aims to determine whether a municipality implementing the CPS between periods (CPSREGION) compared with a control municipality (CTRREGION) served by EMS only affected patient flow and safety in a rural Norwegian setting.DesignA quasi-experimental study evaluating patient flow and safety before and after the introduction of CPS in one of two rural municipalities.SettingTwo rural Norwegian municipalities that were served by EMS from nearby municipalities before the study started.ParticipantsBefore and after the introduction of CPS, a total of 604 and 650 patients, respectively, were included in CPSREGION, and 367 and 408 patients, respectively, in CTRREGION.InterventionsCPS was introduced in CPSREGION between the two data collection periods, whereas CTRREGION continued to be served by EMS.Outcome measuresThe outcome of patient flow was assessed by the number of admissions to nearby hospitals, the number of patient contacts and the location for delivery and treatment. The outcome for safety was assessed as the need for medical recontact within 7 days and 30-day mortality.ResultsHospital admission rates increased over the two study periods, being insignificant in CPSREGION (+4.7%, p=0.373) and significant in CTRREGION (+23.2%, p<0.001). EMS recontacts within 7 days and 30-day mortality rates were both unchanged (p≥0.119).ConclusionsIntroducing CPS resulted in fewer hospital admissions, with more patients being treated locally. Introduction

Over recent decades, emergency medical services (EMSs)1 have experienced increased workload and strain due to the increasing number of missions, greater disease complexity and rising public expectations for the quality of the services provided.1 2 Underlying causes are, for example, an ageing population, urbanisation, unnecessary use of EMS and more specialised treatment, while access to EMS clinicians and economic resources is becoming more restricted.3,7 Thus, there is a mismatch between public expectations and resources available.1 8 These challenges are tangible beyond rural areas. Thus, there is a need to use available resources more wisely and thereby also improve employee retention.

Community paramedicine services (CPSs) can provide person-centred care in a diverse range of settings that focus on the community needs.9 10 Through close interdisciplinary interaction with other healthcare teams, it may support both EMS and other prehospital healthcare systems.11 CPS focuses on two main areas: (1) preventive and follow-up care in collaboration with other healthcare services to prevent illness through chronic disease management, educational programmes and early interventions, and (2) responses to EMS calls with paramedics and equipment early on-site, still allowing for treatment of the patients in their own homes.12 Importantly, CPS differs between countries and communities to adapt to the local needs.13 CPS can therefore be one possible solution to alleviate the increased strain on both prehospital and in-hospital services.1 13 14 CPS has been shown to reduce emergency department (ED) transports by 14%–78%, leading to decreased ED crowding and freeing up EMS resources for other tasks.15 CPS has also been shown to reduce healthcare costs,13 14 although some concerns remain regarding funding of the service, integration with allied health personnel and the level of training needed for personnel.7 16 17 Moreover, previous studies have shown that CPS improves participants’ health through better management of chronic conditions and improved quality of life.7 18 19

The beneficial effects observed with CPS, including reduced hospital admissions, ED visits and healthcare costs, are mostly observed in urban studies.12 13 15 The evidence outside urban areas is scarce.13 Thus, the aim was to evaluate how CPS influenced patient flow and safety after EMS dispatch in a municipality implementing CPS between periods (CPSREGION) compared with a control municipality (CTRREGION), without CPS, in rural Norway. We hypothesised that CPS would alleviate pressure on the hospitals without causing harm to patients.

Methods

Study design

The study employed a quasi-experimental design to evaluate the impact of implementing CPS on patient flow and safety. The CPS was introduced in CPSREGION in Central Norway on 1 November 2021, and CTRREGION served as the comparator. Data were collected over two 2-year periods, where the first period (2018–2019) took place before CPS intervention in CPSREGION and the second period (2022–2023) took place after CPS was implemented in CPSREGION in November 2021 (online supplemental file 1). The CPS intervention covered weekdays between 08:00 and 16:00 and was based on the Rural Expanded Scope of Practice (RESP) model from Australia, consisting of rural community engagement, emergency response, situated practice and primary healthcare.20 Data were reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology recommendations.21

Overview of the study locations, population and prehospital service chain

Central Norway is characterised by agricultural lowlands with scarce population and smaller cities. Three hospital trusts cover 56 556 km2, serving 746 835 inhabitants (as of 1 January 2023).22 Nord-Trøndelag Hospital Trust, with two local hospitals, covers the northern part of Central Norway, where both the municipalities of Inderøy (CPSREGION) and Overhalla (CTRREGION) are located. Both municipalities are defined as rural areas with <10 000 inhabitants according to the Norwegian Directorate of Health.23 Neither CPSREGION nor CTRREGION had local ambulance stations throughout the study period, and they were similar with respect to geography, demographics (mean age 43 years and 41 years, respectively) and the distances from the municipality centre to the nearest ambulance station (23 km in both regions) and hospital (31 km and 24 km, respectively).

For the best possible comparison across CPSREGION and CTRREGION, we extracted socioeconomic and health data from the Trøndelag Health Study’s (HUNT) third (HUNT3, 2006–2008) and fourth (HUNT4, 2017–2019) waves.24 Coupling of HUNT data with the EMS and CPS patient data was conducted by the HUNT study centre using the 11-digit national identification number, before de-identified data were made available for the research group. Employment status, education level, income, sources of income, body mass index, self-reported health status in somatic and psychiatric domains, frequency of sick leaves, general practitioner (GP) visits and outpatient somatic and psychiatric visits were assessed.

One common Emergency Medical Communication Centre (EMCC) directs all Nord-Trøndelag Hospital Trust EMS and CPS activities in the participating municipalities. EMS or CPS is dispatched by the EMCC after a phone call to ‘1-1-3’, the national medical emergency number in Norway, or at a request from in-hospital or prehospital healthcare personnel. The EMCC operators used the Norwegian Index for medical emergency assistance to decide the level of urgency for all EMS and CPS missions.25 Three levels of acuity are used to categorise urgencies: red, yellow and green. The red category (acute response) indicates an immediate need for EMS/CPS due to a life-threatening situation, while yellow (urgent response) is without lights and sirens for patients with a condition that can evolve to a life-threatening situation, and lastly, green indicates non-urgent or planned missions.25 All Norwegian EMS are quite uniformly staffed with two paramedics educated at upper secondary school for 2 years, with an additional 2 years as apprentices before certification.26 CPS is a new concept in Norway, first used in 2018.27 The CPS in this study consisted of one paramedic using an emergency vehicle equipped as a standard ambulance except for the stretcher, meaning the CPS could not offer patient transport. Data collection

Data were collected from CPSREGION and CTRREGION during the two study periods. Patient flow data were collected from the regional Acute Medical Information System (AMIS) (CSAM Health AS, Oslo, Norway),28 which documents comprehensive details for all EMS and CPS contacts in Nord-Trøndelag Hospital Trust. Data included were (a) date, time points and locations of EMS/CPS mission and delivery, (b) personal identification number, age and sex of the patient, (c) urgency level, (d) services used and number of ambulances and (e) prehospital treatment. Qlik Sense (QlikTech International AB, King of Prussia, Pennsylvania, USA) was used for storing and processing AMIS data.29 Hospital data were extracted for the occurrence and time points for admissions to hospital, outpatient visits and discharge information. Norway has a National Cause of Death Registry linked to the national personal identification number.30 The date of death was extracted from the hospital administrative system, which is continuously updated for deaths from the central Norwegian registry for Norwegian citizens.

We only included EMS missions with patient contact in the two municipalities. A mission with more than one EMS unit per patient was counted as one patient contact. Similarly, CPS and EMS together for the same patient were counted as one patient contact. Missions were excluded from safety analyses, concerning mortality, if the patients did not have a Norwegian personal identification number.

Data were collected before the introduction of CPS, from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2019, and after the introduction of CPS (1 November 2021) from 1 January 2022 to 31 December 2023. We excluded the period from 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2021 to avoid any potential impact from the COVID-19 pandemic.

Outcomes

Outcomes were equally assessed across regions and time intervals using the national personal identification number to ensure complete coverage. Patient flow was assessed by admissions to any Central Norway Health Authority hospital, number of patient contacts and location for delivery/treatment (eg, hospital, GP, nursing home, home or on scene). Safety was assessed as the need for medical recontact within 7 days for patients left at home or on the scene after examination and treatment by EMS and 30-day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed by histograms and Q-Q plots. Normally distributed data are presented as means (SD). Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages. The Mann-Whitney U test and Student’s t-test were used as appropriate. Proportions were assessed as appropriate by the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier plots and log-rank tests. Patient flow analyses were analysed on the level of patient contacts, while survival was analysed on the patient level using the last EMS contact (corresponding to the shortest time interval). In cases not experiencing death, data were censored at 30 days. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.29 and V.30; IBM, Armonk, New York, USA).31 The codes used for analyses are shown in online supplemental file 2.

Public and patient involvement

The Health User Organisation, Nord-Trøndelag Hospital Trust, represented the users in the project. The users and paramedics within Nord-Trøndelag Hospital Trust were involved in planning, approval and performance of the CPS and research activities throughout the entire study period.

Safety evaluation concerning the need for recontact and mortality

Figure 4 shows the 30-day mortality of patients. There were no significant differences across regions and study periods (all p≥0.119). In more detail, during the first and second study periods, 43 (7.1%) and 42 (6.5%) of the study participants died within 30 days in CPSREGION and 27 (7.4%) and 37 (9.1%) in CTRREGION, respectively. There were no significant differences with respect to mortality in patients not transported to the hospital between regions or study periods (p≥0.427). According to Statistics Norway, the annual number of deaths per 1000 inhabitants, as assessed in the mid-point of each period, was 16.2 and 19.2 in the first and second study periods in CPSREGION and 21.0 and 22.9 in CTRREGION. Recontacts with EMS or CPS within 7 days were very rare in non-hospitalised patients (with the highest number of six patient recontacts found in CPSREGION in the first period). No significant differences were found for recontacts across regions or study periods (all p≥0.245).

Supportive data for the comparison between study regions

940 (47.3%) of study participants had available socioeconomic and health data from the HUNT3 or HUNT4 studies. There were no significant differences across CPSREGION and CTRREGION for any of the available parameters (employment status, educational level, income, source of income, body mass index, self-reported health status within somatic or psychiatric domains, frequency of sick leaves, GP visits and outpatient hospital visits), all p>0.05 (data not shown). However, for body mass index (HUNT3: 28.0 kg/m2 in CPSREGION and 27.4 kg/m2 in CTRREGION) and the proportion attending out-of-hospital somatic help last year (HUNT4: 31.8% in CPSREGION and 38.5% in CTRREGION), there were some signs of possible regional differences with p-values of 0.10.

Discussion

In this quasi-experimental study evaluating the changes in patient flow and safety after introducing CPS in one of two rural regions served by EMS from nearby municipalities, the main findings were: implementing CPS changed the patient flow towards more local treatment, with a significantly lower hospital admission rate in CPSREGION (+4.7%) after CPS implementation compared with CTRREGION (+23.2%), with a difference of p<0.001. Moreover, the need for medical recontacts and 30-day mortality among patients served by EMS service was unchanged after introducing CPS. No significant differences in 30-day mortality were found in the subgroups of patients not transported to the hospital between regions or study periods.

Patient flow

The increase in EMS missions and hospital admissions challenging prehospital and hospital healthcare services is a worldwide phenomenon and well-known from previous studies.2 32 Even though there is a risk that expanding prehospital services, with CPS, who work closer to patients, could lead to an even larger increase in hospital admissions, previous studies have shown that CPS may reduce the number of hospital admissions.15 33 34 Thus, the finding of lower hospital admissions rates in CPSREGION after CPS was introduced was well aligned with expectations as well as previous studies.15 33 34

The CPS introduced to the CPSREGION followed the Australian RESP model.20 Thus, the CPS was also involved in several prehospital healthcare activities not related to medical emergencies, for example, blood sampling, venous line insertion, injection, vaccination, assisting the GP or home visits (online supplemental file 3). The increase in the total number of patient contacts with the prehospital services from the first to the second period was therefore highest in CPSREGION. Even though the CPS requested help from EMS for hospital admissions in 63 of the home visits requested from allied health services during the second period, closer collaboration with the local healthcare system may be the main reason for the lower rate of hospital admissions. Offering follow-up visits and building relationships with patients, families and other healthcare providers may have contributed to leaving more patients at home or on the scene. The opposite was found in CTRREGION, where hospital admissions increased significantly more over time, accompanied by a lower proportion of patients delivered to the GP. The presented results are strengthened by similar findings, as seen for CTRREGION for the whole region of Central Norway, with a population of almost 750 000 inhabitants, with an annual number of approximately 50 000 EMS transports to the hospital. Also, the lack of differences in prospective data from the HUNT study across the regions supports the conclusion that the differences in study regions over time are likely related to the CPS intervention and not to other known factors.

As data from period 1 and period 2 were collected either prior to or after most COVID-19 pandemic restrictions on public life and healthcare providers, the pandemic had no significant impact on the study. Importantly, the rate of hospital admissions related to COVID-19 was low in period 2 and does not explain the increased proportion of patients transported to the hospital in CTRREGION during the last study period.

Safety concerns related to CPS

Very few studies have assessed the safety of CPS in rural settings,13 while more data are available from urban areas.18 35 36 This contrasts with the fact that the Institute of Medicine (USA) considers safety evaluations as one of six quality dimensions important to incorporate into healthcare services.37 However, the complexity of studying safety within healthcare services may be the main reason for the scarcity of studies, since the interaction with confounding effects, as well as the isolation of the intervention, may be challenging. In the current study, we used 30-day mortality and the need for recontact with the EMS and CPS as safety endpoints and compared data over two periods in two similar municipalities. The only acknowledged change between the two study periods was the CPS intervention in CPSREGION, while no recognised organisational changes were introduced in the CTRREGION. As shown by the presented data, we found no signs of safety concerns related to CPS, with a numerically reduced non-significant mortality in EMS patients in the CPSREGION after CPS was introduced compared with the CTRREGION (p=0.119). This finding may indicate that the local healthcare system in the CPSREGION provided better end-of-life care without assistance from EMS and hospitals after CPS implementation. Also, recontacts with EMS were rare, and it is important that even in missions where CPS were not supported by EMS, there were no signs of reduced safety. The finding of low recall rates within 7 days for patients not transported to the hospital in the CPSREGION in period 2 is well aligned with the findings from studies evaluating aspects of non-conveyance (ie, patients not transported to hospital by regular EMS) showing recall rates of 4.2%–4.9% at 2–3 days.38 39

It may be argued that mortality is not an optimal outcome measure for assessing safety in the prehospital healthcare service, due to a heterogeneous patient population, with few patient contacts being lifesaving and a significant proportion in palliative settings, where the goal is rather to provide good end-of-life care than lifesaving interventions. Through a consensus process, Turner et al proposed a set of potential EMS quality measures, subdivided into ambulance service operations, clinical management of patients, impact of care on patients and time measures that reflect preferences and other potential quality measures of services and its users.40 The most important measures were (1) mean change in pain score, (2) correct management of call to EMCC, (3) response time, (4) proportion of correct decisions to leave patient at scene, (5) proportion of unnecessary transport to hospital and (6) outcome/survival.40 These quality measures can be used to compare EMS, regions or measure performance over time.40

Populations

The study populations across the CPSREGION and CTRREGION were similar with respect to demography, travel distances and healthcare infrastructure. To the best of our knowledge, the only significant change between regions and study periods was the implementation of CPS in CPSREGION after the first study period. Both study regions were rural municipalities, even though the number of inhabitants was higher in CPSREGION. Previous studies from rural areas overlap with the presented data, and we believe that the results may be generalisable to other areas with similar characteristics.1 39

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of the study are: first, the design, including two similar municipalities, where the CPS intervention in one of them was the only acknowledged intervention to the prehospital healthcare service. Also, data from both the overarching regional level, from Central Norway Hospital Trust and Statistics Norway, and complete data from the EMS databases support the presented results related to the CPS interventions. Second, all patient contacts and endpoints within the prehospital and hospital services could be identified and tracked using the personal national identification number, ensuring complete data. Also, the finding of no difference in premorbid socioeconomic and health data across the two study cohorts from previous participation in the HUNT population study adds strength to the study findings relating to the CPS intervention.

The study also has some limitations. first, the quasi-experimental design indicates that participants were not randomised to the intervention. Second, the patients’ and the paramedics’ experiences could provide valuable information regarding the impact of the service. However, the inclusion of the comparable region adds validity to the study. Also, the study periods before and after the CPS intervention were well balanced, and although the numbers in some subcategories were quite low, most analyses had sufficient power to address the research questions. Lastly, due to the design and the study regions being rural municipalities without their own EMS, generalisation to more urban regions or municipalities with different characteristics may be limited.

Conclusions

The CPS implementation in a rural municipality served by EMS from a nearby region changed the patient flow towards more local treatment and reduced hospital admissions. Importantly, there were no safety concerns, as shown by no change in 30-day mortality or recontacts in patients served by CPS compared with the region without a change of prehospital service. We conclude that CPS may be a safe way to alleviate the strain on the healthcare system.
