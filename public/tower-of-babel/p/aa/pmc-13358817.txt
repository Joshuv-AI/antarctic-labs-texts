Metastasectomy versus stereotactic body radiotherapy for lung metastases: a systematic review and meta-analysis of reconstructed time-to-event data on overall and progression-free survival
Authors: Angelique Runkel, Tulio Caldonazo, Hristo Kirov, Lukas Mayer, Felipe S Passos, Murat Mukharyamov, Johannes Fischer, Aryan Dadashzadeh K, Tim Sandhaus, Torsten Doenst, Patrick von Samson-Himmelstjerna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide, with the majority of cancer-related deaths attributable to metastatic disease. Particularly in patients with pulmonary oligometastases, local ablative treatment strategies such as metastasectomy and stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) are important therapeutic options. However, to date, no randomized controlled trials and only limited direct comparative analyses are available evaluating those treatment options. Therefore, the present study aims to perform a meta-analysis based on reconstructed time-to-event data to compare long-term overall survival (LOS) and progression-free survival (PFS) between these treatment strategies based on currently available evidence.MethodsFirst, we defined our research question using the PICOTS framework. A systematic literature search of three databases (Ovid MEDLINE, the Cochrane Library, and ScienceDirect) was performed in March 2024 and updated in April 2026. We applied the same predefined search strategy across all databases, which was fully documented. Study selection was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Kaplan-Meier curves were processed, time-to-event data were reconstructed, and overall event curves were generated. A Cox regression model was performed. The risk of bias was addressed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), and sensitivity analyses were conducted. The risk of publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for the estimates.ResultsOf 5,480 screened studies, nine met the inclusion criteria. They were published between 2014 and 2025. A total of 1,535 patients were included in the final analysis, of whom 949 underwent surgical metastasectomy and 586 were assigned to SBRT. Compared with SBRT, metastasectomy was associated with improved LOS (HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90, P=0.002) and long-term PFS (HR: 0.774, 95% CI: 0.66–0.91, P=0.002) in the overall analysis. All included studies were retrospective, with limited sample sizes and variable follow-up. In some cases, systematic differences in patient characteristics, as well as variability in the availability and implementation of treatment protocols, may have introduced additional bias. To address these limitations, a subgroup analysis including only risk-adjusted studies was performed. Within this analysis, there was no significant difference in LOS (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.45–1.10) or long-term PFS (HR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.39–1.15).ConclusionsThis analysis suggests that surgical treatment of lung metastases may be associated with better LOS and PFS compared with SBRT in non-risk-adjusted real-world data, reflecting current treatment allocation and clinical practice. However, when using matched cohorts, both treatment strategies show no difference in LOS or PFS, suggesting that, when applied to the same patient population, they may represent equivalent treatment alternatives. Due to inherent limitations and potential selection bias, further investigation is necessary to confirm these findings.Keywords: Metastasectomy, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), lung metastases, cancer

Highlight box

Key findings

• The results may suggest that in non-risk-adjusted observational real-world data, metastasectomy is associated with improved long-term overall survival and progression-free survival compared with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT); however, in matched cohorts, no significant differences were observed, suggesting comparable effectiveness in similar patient populations.

What is known and what is new?

• Approximately 90% of cancer-related deaths occur after metastatic spread, and up to one third of cancer patients develop pulmonary metastases. Metastatic disease requires a multidisciplinary treatment approach, with local therapies such as surgical resection and stereotactic radiotherapy being available options. While surgical metastasectomy has long been known to be associated with a survival benefit in patients with metastases from various primary tumors, stereotactic radiotherapy for lung metastases has emerged in recent years due to technical advances and has demonstrated favorable outcomes compared with medical therapy alone.

• This manuscript provides the first systematic comparison of metastasectomy and SBRT in patients with lung metastases of extrapulmonary origin.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Large, randomized trials stratified by primary tumor origin are needed to provide robust treatment recommendations for this potentially large patient population, in which this analysis suggests that both surgery and SBRT may be associated with favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Background

From a worldwide perspective, cancer remains the second leading cause of death worldwide, with 90% of cancer-related deaths attributed to metastatic disease (1-3). While the global burden of metastatic disease has remained largely static, the last decade has seen a paradigm shift in the management of oligometastatic states through local ablative interventions (2,4,5).

These treatments are especially relevant for lung metastases, as the lungs are one of the organs most commonly affected by metastatic—and more specifically, oligometastatic—spread (4,6). Despite increasing use of local ablative therapies for pulmonary metastases, the optimal treatment modality remains controversial. Locally highly effective treatment methods include surgical metastasectomy and stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) (4). Surgical resection of pulmonary metastases has long been established as a beneficial treatment option in selected patients, for example in those with colorectal cancer, renal cell carcinoma, or sarcoma (7-9). SBRT on the other hand has emerged as an effective local ablative treatment during the last years with randomized trials demonstrating a survival benefit over medical treatment alone in cases of pulmonary metastases from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (10,11).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Randomized controlled trials directly comparing these two treatment modalities are lacking. One major reason for this is the historical development of SBRT, which was initially applied primarily in inoperable patients as a less invasive treatment alternative (12). Only in recent years has SBRT increasingly been used in operable patient populations (13). Additionally, certain tumor entities—such as pulmonary metastases from colorectal cancer—have traditionally been considered relatively radioresistant, further limiting the use of radiotherapy in this setting (14).

Currently available head-to-head comparisons are limited to small, predominantly unicentric and retrospective, tumor-specific observational studies. As a result, it remains unclear whether SBRT can be considered an equivalent alternative to surgical metastasectomy for the treatment of pulmonary metastases from extrapulmonary primary tumors.

To comprehensively assess the current state of evidence, a systematic review and meta-analysis is warranted. By pooling data, this investigation may increase statistical power, reduce center-specific bias related to local expertise, treatment protocols, and patient selection. At the same time, it cannot eliminate bias in patient selection and treatment allocation; however, it allows these limitations to be explicitly acknowledged and highlights the need for further research.

Since the primary endpoint of interest in the context of local ablative treatments for oligometastatic disease is long-term overall survival (LOS) and long-term progression-free survival (PFS), reconstruction of individual patient-level time-to-event data is necessary to adequately address this outcome and was therefore performed in this meta-analysis.

Objective

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to compare surgical metastasectomy and SBRT for the treatment of lung metastases from any primary extrapulmonary tumor by summarizing the currently available observational evidence. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2674/rc).

Methods

This analysis did not require ethical approval since it was based exclusively on previously published data without human or animal involvement. Registration was completed through the National Institute for Health Research International Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD42024532141).

Search strategy

To identify contemporary studies on long-term outcomes of metastasectomy or SBRT for lung metastases, we performed a systematic search of three databases (Ovid MEDLINE, the Cochrane Library, and ScienceDirect) in March 2024. The systematic research was updated in April 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text terms related to stereotactic radiation therapy (including stereotactic radiotherapy, stereotactic radiation therapy and radiosurgery), lung metastases (including pulmonary metastases, lung metastases, secondary lung cancer, and lung- and pulmonary oligometastases), and surgical treatment (including metastasectomy, surgery, and resection). Boolean operators (“AND”, “OR”) were used to appropriately combine the search terms. We used the exact same search strategy to perform systematic literature research in all databases mentioned above. Table S1 provides the complete search strategy for Ovid MEDLINE. No filters or limits were used.

Study selection

After removing duplicates, two independent reviewers (L.M. and A.R.) screened all records. Any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (T.C.). Titles and abstracts were reviewed against pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, full texts were retrieved for a second eligibility screening. This was again done by two independent reviewers (L.M. and A.R.) and verified by a third reviewer (T.C.). After that, reference lists of these studies were screened for important literature not captured by the original search.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were published in English and directly compared outcomes between patients undergoing metastasectomy or SBRT for lung metastases of any extrapulmonary origin. Included studies were also required to report the outcomes of interest.

Studies were excluded if they were not published in English, lacked relevant endpoints, were conference abstracts or proceedings, were case reports, or employed non-comparative designs. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to evaluate the quality of included studies (Table S2).

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (L.M. and A.R.), with a third author (T.C.) verifying accuracy. Extracted variables comprised study characteristics (publication year, country, sample size, study design, and risk adjustment strategy) as well as patient demographics (age, sex, number of metastases, synchronous or metachronous presentation, primary tumor, and metastasis size at intervention).

Study outcomes

Primary outcome was LOS. Secondary outcome was long-term PFS.

Statistical analysis

We applied a reconstructed time-to-event approach for both primary and secondary endpoints. Individual patient data (IPD) were reconstructed from Kaplan-Meier curves of all eligible studies using the methods described by Wei et al. (15,16). Since the primary endpoint of interest in the context of local ablative treatments for oligometastatic disease is LOS and long-term PFS, reconstruction of individual patient-level time-to-event data is necessary to adequately address this outcome and was therefore performed in this meta-analysis. In order to address, discuss, and minimize additional bias arising from the limited comparability of cohorts in some of the included studies, a subgroup analysis including only risk-adjusted studies was performed.

Raster and vector images of the survival curves were pre-processed and digitized to extract survival or mortality values corresponding to specific time points. When available, additional information such as number-at-risk tables or total event counts were used to improve the accuracy of the reconstructed time-to-event data. Deviations from monotonicity were identified using isotonic regression and corrected with a pool-adjacent-violators algorithm (15,16). The timing of failure events was carefully checked against the original publications to ensure consistency with reported survival or mortality data.

Meta-analysis of reconstructed data

LOS and long-term PFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Between-group differences were assessed with Cox proportional hazards regression models. The proportional hazards assumption was checked by examining scaled Schoenfeld residuals, log-log survival plots and predicted versus observed survival functions. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier product-limit method, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each group. An HR below 1 indicated a lower event rate in the surgical arm. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses included a two-stage meta-analysis and funnel plot to evaluate the robustness of the primary endpoint results. The two-stage meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects DerSimonian-Laird model, as previously described (17). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q statistic and the I2 statistic, with high heterogeneity defined as P<0.10 and I2≥50%. The risk of bias of the individual included studies was assessed using the NOS. Publication bias was assessed by creating a funnel plot. All analyses were carried out using R (version 4.3.1, R Project for Statistical Computing) in RStudio and STATA IC17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas).

Results

Study characteristics

From 5,480 records identified in the systematic search, nine studies met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. Figure 1 shows the PRISMA flow diagram. The studies were published between 2014 and 2025 and conducted in the United States, China, Italy, Japan, Spain, South Korea and the Netherlands. Table 1 provides study details. A total of 1,535 patients were included in the final overall analysis, of whom 949 underwent surgical metastasectomy and 586 were assigned to SBRT. Five studies involved risk-adjusted populations. Addressing only risk-adjusted studies, the cohort included 1,222 patients, with 769 receiving surgical treatment and 453 receiving SBRT.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias of the individual included studies was assessed using NOS. A detailed study-level overview is provided in Table S2.

In the selection domain, all studies fulfilled between three and four out of four criteria, indicating generally good methodological quality. However, limitations were observed regarding the representativeness of the SBRT groups, as patients were not always derived from the same underlying population. In some studies, patients deemed inoperable were more likely to be assigned to SBRT, introducing a potential selection bias. In two studies, the treatment allocation process was not described, and in one study it was explicitly stated that no specific criteria were used for the decision between SBRT and surgery. Nevertheless, it is important to note that four studies explicitly included only patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1 or 0–2 in both groups.

In the comparability domain, five out of nine studies performed adequate risk adjustment and achieved the maximum score of two out of two points. The remaining studies received one point, reflecting limited adjustment for potential confounders.

Regarding the outcome/exposure domain, overall ratings were moderate. Five out of nine studies received a full score of three points. The remaining studies showed limitations related to the limited follow-up duration as well as limited availability and heterogeneous application of SBRT, which may have affected outcome assessment and follow-up consistency.

Patient characteristics

The demographic characteristics of the patient populations in each study are summarized in Table S3. The age of included patients ranged from 50.3 to 71.7 years. Between 15.3% and 50.4% of patients were female, most common number of metastases was one with a percentage between 37.0% and 77.5%, most diagnoses were metachronous in 77.0% to 92.9% of cases. Primary tumor entities included colorectal cancer, gynecological cancer, squamous cells carcinoma of head or neck, renal cell- and urothelial carcinoma, salivary gland tumors, esophageal cancer, sarcoma, melanoma and hepatocellular carcinoma. The median size of treated metastases ranged from 1.2 to 2.0 cm.

Long-term survival

A total of five Kaplan-Meier curves were processed, digitized, and reconstructed. Figure 2 displays the pooled Kaplan-Meier curves for long-term survival across the entire observation period. In this analysis, metastasectomy was associated with superior long-term survival compared with those treated with SBRT (HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90, P=0.002). After restricting the analysis to propensity score-matched studies, patients undergoing metastasectomy non-significant difference compared with those treated with SBRT in case of LOS (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.45–1.10) (Figure 3). Seven out of nine studies provided reliable number-at-risk tables.

Discussion

Key findings

We conducted a meta-analysis of comparative studies evaluating treatment effects of surgical metastasectomy versus stereotactic radiotherapy for lung metastases of any extrapulmonary origin. Our results suggest that in non-risk-adjusted real-world data reflecting current treatment allocation and clinical practice, metastasectomy appears to be associated with improved LOS and PFS compared with SBRT. However, this advantage is not observed in risk-adjusted or matched analyses, where both treatment strategies demonstrate comparable outcomes, suggesting potential equivalence when applied to similar patient populations.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first quantitative analysis addressing this topic by reconstructing time-to-event data, with a specific focus on long-term outcomes. According to the latest cancer report from the American Cancer Society, in 2023, 2,001,140 patients in the US were diagnosed with malignant diseases (18). Given that, approximately 30% of these patients experience pulmonary metastases during the course of their disease (4,6), millions of people worldwide require effective treatments for this specific stage of cancer. While distant metastases remain to be managed with palliative systemic therapy or best supportive care in many cases, there are new treatment options for oligometastatic disease that offer significant survival benefits, and even the potential for cure (2,5). This emphasizes the importance and relevance of research in this area.

However, it is important to mention that studies included within this work showed retrospective design and were conducted over a time frame of eleven years. Due to the fast development in SBRT, protocols and technical equipment used within the included trials differed and may not be of current clinical standard anymore. Besides this, it is important to note that four out of nine study populations were not risk-adjusted, and patients receiving SBRT tended to be older. Due to incomplete reporting of performance status and comorbidities in some of the included studies, it is possible that baseline characteristics differed between treatment groups. Participants were referred to surgery primarily when their general condition and respiratory function made them suitable for metastasectomy. Additionally, patients presenting with more superficial lesions preferentially received surgery, although, current evidence indicates that also the efficacy of SBRT diminishes as the lesion location becomes more central (19). These aspects may have limited the treatment effect of SBRT observed in the overall cohort. To address this limitation of the study, a separate analysis including only risk-adjusted studies was performed. Pooling only matched HRs, there was no apparent benefit of surgery compared to SBRT in case of LOS or long-term progression-free survival, suggesting potential equivalence of these treatment options when applied to similar patient populations.

A further limitation of this study is the restriction to English-language publications, which may introduce language and geographic bias, particularly considering the substantial body of thoracic oncology research conducted in non-English-speaking regions such as Asia.

Comparison with similar research

In the context of surgical metastasectomy, multicentral trials report 5-year survival rates of 46% for patients undergoing resection of pulmonary metastases from any origin (20). Notably, survival rates are significantly higher for certain types of cancer, such as colorectal carcinoma. Surgical treatment of lung metastases from colorectal carcinoma has been associated with an overall 5-year survival rate of up to 53.5% (21), increasing to 67.8% following R0 resection (22,23). Comparable outcomes were already reported in studies conducted between the 1990s and early 2000s, positioning surgical metastasectomy as the oldest and most well-established approach for the treatment of oligometastatic disease (22). Moreover, advancements in surgical techniques have made the procedures safer and more effective. The optimization of preoperative staging using ultra-thin sliced single-breath computed tomography (CT) (<1 mm) (24,25) and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), for example, aids in the detection of metastases in case of epithelial tumors, such as colorectal, breast cancer, and melanoma, as well as involved lymph nodes, with high sensitivity (25,26). This allows a more precise indication for surgery and personalization of surgical planning.

This personalization includes decisions regarding the choice between video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) or thoracotomy, anatomical versus atypical resection, necessary margins and lymph node management (27). At the same time, in case of atypical resection new techniques such as laser-assisted surgery could lead to further reduction of parenchyma loss (28).

On the other hand, SBRT for lung tumors have been strongly driven by advances in imaging, motion management, and dose calculation technologies since the 1990s (29). Key technological advancements include the widespread implementation slice thickness <2 mm in planning CTs (30) as well as the combination with PET/CT images (31), the of daily image guidance with cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) (32), and the introduction of 4D-CBCT for patients with highly mobile tumors (33). Respiratory motion management techniques such as 4D-CT, deep inspiration breath hold (DIBH), gating, and tracking (e.g., via CyberKnife) have significantly enhanced targeting accuracy, although patient compliance remains important for their successful use (34-36). In addition, the evolution of dose calculation algorithms has led to more accurate dose delivery (37). Furthermore, modern delivery techniques like volumetric modulated arc therapy combined with flattening filter free beams enable faster and more efficient treatment while maintaining high precision (38).

Initially developed for patients with inoperable early-stage lung cancer, SBRT is now increasingly applied to operable patients as well, owing to improved treatment precision and clinical outcomes in case of NSCLC (39). While lobectomy with lymphadenectomy remains standard care for operable patients (40), recent evidence suggests that SBRT can offer comparable or even better 3-year estimated overall survival (SBRT: 95%, lobectomy: 79%) and recurrence-free survival (SBRT: 86%, lobectomy: 80%) while being associated with fewer treatment-related complications (41). However, we must keep in mind that previously mentioned data comparing outcomes for NSCLC come from two randomized controlled trials closed early due to poor recruitment. Chang et al. analyzed the data of a total of 58 patients with a comparably short follow-up time. Furthermore, the use of modern surgical techniques was limited, with only five patients undergoing VATS (41). Moreover, these data describe outcomes after treatment of NSCLC, not lung-metastases from extrapulmonary origin, as analyzed within this work. The analysis of risk-adjusted cohorts in this meta-analysis—within which a substantial proportion of patients in the SBRT group are likely to have had high and comparable performance status and may have been potentially operable—further supports previously mentioned findings, meanwhile the overall cohort showed better LOS und PFS in case of surgery. This may be attributable to several factors.

Explanations of findings

First, surgeons may be able to remove the entire malignant mass in a short period of time. In contrast, radiation biology uses the mechanism of fractionated detriment of malignant and faster repair of healthy cells (42). However not all malignant cells might be eradicated during the sessions, potentially allowing residual cells to survive and potentially spread (42). Moreover, in the case of SBRT there is no lymph node sampling performed which is often considered another cause of lower locoregional control (43). Although this might be also valid in some surgical cases, the lack of pathology-controlled therapeutical outcome in case of SBRT might also explain why the survival curves in the overall cohort remain relatively close to each other, while the PFS curves show a more pronounced divergence. It is possible that these effects are more common in the context of metastases than in NSCLC, particularly given that the overall 3-year survival in the cohort analyzed here was substantially lower than the 95% reported by Chang et al. (41). Differences in radio sensitivity of metastases and underlying tumor biology may contribute to this observation as well (44,45). Nevertheless, this mechanistic approach does not appear to fully and exclusively explain the findings of the present analysis, as the statistically significant advantage of metastasectomy was no longer detectable in the analysis of risk-adjusted populations alone.

The observed difference in the overall cohort is likely influenced by patient selection bias and systematic differences between the cohorts, such that, in particular, inoperable patients were more frequently allocated to SBRT since metastasectomy is known to be contraindicated in certain patients, such as those with significant respiratory compromise, a history of prior radiation therapy or inadequate performance status (46). Even though this analysis suggests worse survival with SBRT in the overall cohort reflecting current clinical treatment allocation and patient selection, the analysis of only risk-adjusted cohorts suggests that, in similar patient populations, both treatments may be considered equivalent options.

Implications and actions needed

Guidelines for SBRT in case of lung metastases recommend both, surgical metastasectomy as well as SBRT as evidence based treatment methods after discussion in an interdisciplinary team (47). Basis for these recommendations are randomized trials comparing SBRT to medical therapy showing a significant survival benefit as well as favorable PFS (10,11,48,49). Notably, all studies except the SABR-COMET II trial observe long-term survival and PFS only in cases of pulmonary metastases of NSCLC (48). Moreover, the number of patients included in all trials evaluated within the context of guideline recommendations remains relatively small. Treatment protocols were not standardized and studies showed high heterogeneity of 87% for local control rates (47). Therefore, the present analysis provides valuable evidence for pulmonary metastases originating from extrapulmonary tumors, suggesting that in everyday clinical practice patients receiving metastasectomy still show improved LOS and long-term PFS. Nevertheless, applied in cohorts with similar baseline characteristics both treatment modalities may be associated with similar long-term outcomes. Large, randomized trials are needed to compare these treatment options not only against medical standard therapy but also through head-to-head trials stratified by malignant origin. Importantly, there is a need to systematically assess adverse events associated with both treatment modalities, particularly in light of the possibility that SBRT may represent a less invasive and potentially better-tolerated treatment option.

Moreover, we also want to emphasize that both SBRT and surgical treatment are important and rapidly advancing approaches. As such, treatment decisions should always be individualized and discussed within an interdisciplinary tumor board.

Conclusions

Our results suggest that surgical treatment may be associated with improved LOS and long-term PFS compared with SBRT within the limits of the currently available observational evidence. However, when considering only risk-adjusted studies and therefore more comparable patient populations, both approaches may represent equivalent treatment options. This analysis underscores the need for further research in this clinically highly relevant field affecting a large patient population.
