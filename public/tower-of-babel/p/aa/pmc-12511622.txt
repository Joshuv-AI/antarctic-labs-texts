Examining intimate partner violence as a barrier to childhood immunization in India with focus on maternal and child factors
Authors: Priyanka Kumari, Rahul Kumar, Rangasamy Nagarajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intimate partner violence (IPV) can negatively impact the use of child health services, in addition to causing direct harm and even death to women. IPV is defined as physical, sexual, or emotional abuse and controlling behaviors by a current or former partner or spouse. Evidence points to a connection between the children of violent intimate partners and the children’s vaccination status. This study aims to investigate the relationship between women’s exposure to intimate partner violence (IPV) and childhood immunization in India. The National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) 2019–21 is used, and information from 5106 mothers of children selected from the violence module is utilized in this study. Bivariate and multivariate multilevel logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between women’s exposure to IPV and the full immunization of children. To assess any moderating effect of the education of the woman, the sex of the child, and the birth order on the association between exposure to spousal IPV and full immunization, interactions were checked. The crude analysis revealed that children of mothers who experienced sexual violence (COR: 0.75; 95% CI 0.61–0.83), emotional violence (COR: 0.91, 95% CI 0.81–0.99) and any forms of violence (COR: 0.83, 95% CI 0.70–0.95) were less likely to be fully immunized than no abused women were. Moderation analysis revealed that sexual, emotional, and any form of IPV are also moderated by a secondary level of education. A significant association between any form of IPV and full immunization was found for children in second or third birth order (AOR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.52–0.84; p = 0.001). Intimate partner violence (IPV) severely impacts maternal and child health, influencing decision-making and access to healthcare services. This leads to poorer health outcomes, including lower immunization rates for children. Addressing IPV through targeted interventions is crucial for improving maternal and child well-being. Increased efforts are needed to reduce IPV prevalence and raise awareness about the importance of reproductive and child health initiatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-05474-3.

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as any behavior within an intimate relationship that causes physical, sexual, or emotional harm to a partner1. IPV manifests in various forms, including physical violence, which involves acts such as hitting, slapping, kicking, choking, or the use of weapons, all of which can lead to physical injury or death. Sexual violence includes coerced or forced sexual acts, such as rape, forced intercourse, and reproductive control, including forced pregnancy or denial of contraception. Emotional or psychological violence encompasses verbal abuse, humiliation, intimidation, threats, and manipulation, all of which can undermine an individual’s sense of self-worth and mental well-being. Additionally, controlling behaviors such as isolating a partner from family and friends or restricting access to healthcare, education, employment, and financial resources are considered a form of IPV. These forms of abuse often overlap and have both immediate and long-term consequences for a woman’s physical, mental, and reproductive health.

IPV is widely recognized as a serious public health concern and a violation of human rights. Globally, IPV affects one in four women during their lifetime2,3. Its effects extend beyond physical injuries to include chronic health conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, respiratory illnesses, depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, and substance misuse3–5. Due to underreporting and social stigma, many women do not seek medical help, making the full extent of IPV difficult to measure.

In South Asia, particularly in India, IPV is highly prevalent. Over one-third of Indian women have reported experiencing some form of partner violence6. A multicountry WHO study revealed that 69% of women had experienced at least one episode of IPV, 42% reported physical violence in their lifetime, and 12% experienced physical abuse during pregnancy7. According to the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), 29.3% of married women aged 18–49 have experienced physical or sexual violence by their spouse8. This translates to millions of women annually suffering from intimate partner violence (IPV). Despite the widespread nature and severe health consequences of IPV, the majority of affected women do not seek help. NFHS-5 data show that 77% of women who have experienced violence never sought assistance or disclosed the abuse8. At the same time, India accounts for one-quarter of global child deaths, highlighting a critical intersection between maternal well-being and child survival7.

Children exposed to IPV are also at risk. Even if not directly abused, children may experience emotional trauma, behavioral issues, developmental delays, and poor academic performance9,10. IPV in the home increases the likelihood of harsh parenting practices, including physical punishment and neglect, particularly for children under the age of three11. The Family Violence Prevention Fund (FVPF) reports that child maltreatment co-occurs with IPV in 30% to 60% of cases, with potentially fatal consequences for young children12.

Mothers who disclosed IPV were significantly less likely to report having a regular site or a designated pediatric provider for their child’s healthcare. Children in these mothers were also less likely to receive the recommended five well-child visits in their first year and were significantly less likely to be up-to-date on immunizations by age two. Moreover, mothers with IPV histories reported lower trust and poorer communication with their child’s primary care provider13.

Abusive co-parents may employ a range of coercive tactics that directly or indirectly hinder children’s access to healthcare. These behaviors include obstructing or delaying medical treatment, stalking or intimidating the caregiving parent, engaging in legal harassment (often referred to as “legal abuse”), neglecting children’s health and safety needs, and exercising economic control to limit healthcare-related expenses14,15. In addition to individual-level abuse, structural barriers within systems such as family courts and healthcare institutions further exacerbate these challenges. For example, custody arrangements and legal decision-making disputes may delay or prevent timely medical care, while healthcare systems may lack protocols to identify and respond to coercive control or parental interference16,17.

In addition to the direct harms, IPV can limit women’s ability to access health services, including essential preventive care for children. Fear, emotional distress, and lack of autonomy can prevent mothers from seeking timely immunizations for their children18. Immunization is one of the most cost-effective interventions to reduce under-five mortality. Despite efforts by the Universal Immunization Programme and WHO guidelines, many children in India remain under-vaccinated or receive vaccines late19.

IPV had negatively affected mother’s health, with mental health concerns being the most frequently reported unmet need. For their children, the most common unmet needs were immunizations and preventive care. Transportation, cost, and fear of encountering the abuser were primary barriers to accessing healthcare. Notably, more than 60% of participants reported never having been screened for IPV by healthcare providers, though most supported routine screening and expressed a strong preference for integrated, trauma-informed, and accessible healthcare services20.

While some studies have explored the health consequences of IPV on women and children, there is limited research on how maternal exposure to IPV affects child immunization especially in low- and middle-income settings. Evidence from both high- and low-income countries suggests a consistent association between IPV and lower rates of complete childhood immunization11,21. Similar findings have emerged from studies conducted in India18,22.

Given India’s high rates of IPV and child mortality, it is crucial to understand how IPV affects preventive child health services. This study investigates the association between maternal experiences of IPV across physical, emotional, and sexual dimensions and the immunization status of children aged 12–23 months. By addressing this gap, the research aims to inform targeted health interventions and policies that support the well-being of both mothers and children.

Methods

Data source

The study used data from the fifth round of the India Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) conducted in 2019–21, known as the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5). The survey was carried out across all the states and union territories of India. The NFHS-5 sample is a stratified two-stage sample. The 2011 census served as the sampling frame for the selection of PSUs. The PSUs were villages in rural areas and census enumeration blocks (CEBs) in urban areas. In the second stage, 22 households were randomly selected with systematic sampling in every selected rural and urban cluster. The information was gathered from 636,699 households, 724,115 women aged 15–49 years, and 101,839 men aged 15–54 years.

Study participants

The study is based on 232,920 children born to women aged 15–49 years in the past five years preceding the survey. Among these children, 43,181 aged 12–23 months were selected. Approximately 28,299 women of aged 18–49 49 years were selected and interviewed in the domestic violence module of the NFHS-5 survey. A total of 5,106 mothers aged 18–49 of children aged 12–23 months has been selected. The final analytic sample was restricted to these samples only (Fig. 1). We restricted the analytic sample to children aged 12–23 months to align the measurement of child immunization with maternal exposure to intimate partner violence (IPV) reported in the past 12 months. This age range corresponds to the period during which children are expected to complete their basic vaccination schedule, allowing for a meaningful assessment of full immunization status. Limiting the sample in this way ensures temporal consistency between the IPV exposure and immunization outcome, reducing recall bias and enhancing the internal validity of the analysis.Fig. 1Schematic representation of participant selection procedure.

Measures

Outcome variable

The outcome variable of this study was full immunization among children aged 12–23 months. According to the guidelines developed by the WHO, children are considered fully immunized when they receive vaccination against tuberculosis (BCG), three doses of diphtheria, whooping cough (pertussis), and tetanus (DPT) vaccine; three doses of poliomyelitis (polio) vaccine; and one dose of the measles vaccine by the age of 12 months. BCG should be given at birth or at first clinical contact, DPT and polio require three vaccinations at approximately 4, 8, and 12 weeks of age, and the measles vaccine should be given at age 12 months or soon after reaching 9 months. Information on child immunization was collected from the immunization cards shown by household members during the survey, or in the absence of the immunization card, the information gathered from the mother of the respective child was recorded. For this study, a full immunization variable was constructed from all eight basic vaccinations and categorized as fully immunized and not fully immunized.

Predictor variable

The predictor variable in this study is women’s exposure to violence perpetrated by their current husband or most recent husband. To measure IPV, the NFHS 2019–21 collected information from ever-married women on whether they had ever experienced violent acts committed by their husbands. A woman was considered to have experienced physical violence by her intimate partner if she replied affirmatively to any of the following queries: if her partner pushed, shook, or threw something at her; slapped; twisted her arm or pulled her hair; punched her with his fist or with something that could hurt her; kicked, dragged, or beat; and tried to choke or burn her on purpose, threatened or attacked with a knife, gun, or any other weapon. Sexual abuse was similarly determined by an affirmative reply when asked if her partner ever physically forced her to have sexual intercourse with him even when she did not want to do so. Similarly, emotional violence was captured by three questions concerning whether the husband ever (a) humiliated her in front of others, (b) threatened to hurt or harm herself or (c) insulted or made her feel bad about herself. To measure women’s exposure to intimate partner violence (IPV) in the past 12 months, we created four separate yes/no (binary) variables. These included: (a) physical violence, (b) sexual violence and (c) emotional violence. We also created a fourth variable (d) any IPV, which was marked “yes” if the woman experienced at least one of the three types of violence mentioned above.

Covariates

The study used several socioeconomic and demographic variables to explain the differentials and determinants of immunization coverage as explanatory variables. The sex of the child was categorized as male or female, and the birth order was one, two or three or four or more, respectively. The ages of the women were divided into three subgroups (18–24, 25–34 and 35–49), and the mothers’ and fathers’ education subgroups were not educated, primary, secondary and higher. The categories for women’s occupation are not in the workforce and working, and media exposure, such as no and yes. Two categories exist for place of residence: rural and urban. The wealth index (poorest, poor, middle, rich, richest) was used. The caste of the household head was used as a covariate with four categories to categorize individuals according to these marginalized classes: scheduled caste (SC), scheduled tribe (ST), other backward classes (OBC) and others. The other class is the category containing those individuals who are not members of any marginalized classes.

Statistical analysis

Univariate and bivariate analyses were used to describe the characteristics of the women in relation to the outcome of interest and each type of IPV. Finally, bivariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between women’s exposure to IPV and full immunization of their children. Overall, three models have been included in the analysis. Model 1 presents unadjusted associations between IPV and child immunization outcomes. Model 2 includes adjustments for covariates and examines the effects of each type of IPV (physical, sexual, and emotional) separately. Model 3 includes the composite variable "any form of IPV" to assess the overall impact of IPV exposure.

The regression results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We conducted separate fully adjusted models for each type of IPV (emotional, physical, sexual, or any violence) for each outcome variable while controlling for confounders to identify the associations between spousal IPV and the full immunization of children.

To assess any moderating effects of education of mother, sex of the child, and birth order on the association between exposure to spousal IPV and full immunization, interactions were checked (interaction between IPV and education, sex of the child and birth order). Finally, analyses of the associations between exposure to spousal IPV and full immunization were stratified by level of education, sex and birth order of the child. Before the multivariate regression analysis, multicollinearity was checked via variance inflation factors (VIFs). This indicates no multicollinearity since all the variables have a VIF < 1.98, and all the variables were considered for the analysis.

Result

Half of the children (50.1%) were born in the second or third birth order. A total of 22%, 5% and 10% of the women had experienced physical, emotional and sexual IPV, respectively, in the last 12 months. Almost 26% of the respondents had experienced any violence from their partner. The rates of no education and primary, secondary and higher education for women are 20.6%, 12.3%, 52.1% and 14.8%, respectively; these rates are 15.7%, 12.6%, 55.5% and 16%, respectively, for their partners. Regarding the occupation of women, 77.2% are not indulged in any workforce, and 70.6% of women are exposed to media. The majority of the respondents lived in rural areas (79.7%) and were OBCs (37.3%). Nearly one quarter of the respondents (27.6%) belonged to the poorest household wealth quintile (Table 1).

Approximately 76%, 70% and 74% of children whose mothers experienced physical, emotional and sexual assault by their partner, respectively, in the past 12 months are fully immunized. Significant variation in complete immunization coverage according to sociodemographic factors has been reported. Full vaccination coverage was lower among children with four or more birth orders (69.1%). Children of mothers aged 18–19 years and those who are illiterate and not exposed to any media have a lower percentage of full immunization. Similarly, children with the father’s highest educational status have a higher rate of full immunization (83.6%). Children whose mothers belong to the poorest wealth quintile have a lower status of full immunization (70.5%) (Table 1).

In contrast, partial immunization was reported for 18.0% of children, with the highest rates among children of mothers with no education (24.4%), those with no media exposure (24.2%), and higher birth order children (24.7%). Children whose mothers were exposed to emotional (22.2%) and sexual violence (22.8%) also had elevated partial immunization rates. Children of mothers with primary education (21.9%) and those from the poorest households (23.1%) showed higher rates of partial immunization.

No immunization was observed in 3.8% of children overall. The highest rates were among children whose mothers had no education (7.2%), belonged to the poorest wealth quintile (6.5%), and were aged 35–49 years (8.6%). Similarly, children of mothers who had experienced sexual violence had a higher no immunization rate (6.4%) compared to those who had not (3.7%). Children with mother of no media exposure (6.3%) and those from scheduled tribes (5.1%) had higher prevalence of no immunization (Table S1).

After adjusting for sociodemographic factors in Model 2, only sexual and emotional violence retained a significant relationship with childhood immunization (AOR: 0.68, 95% CI 0.54–0.71; AOR: 0.93, 95% CI 0.82–0.99). The odds of full immunization were lower among children with higher birth orders (four or more) (AOR: 0.67, 95% CI 0.52–0.85) than among those with first- or second-order births. Children of mothers aged 35–49 years were more likely to be immunized (AOR: 1.34, 95% CI 1.21–1.51) than women aged 18–24 years. Mothers’ education has a significant effect on child immunization status. The children of mothers with higher education are more likely to be fully immunized than the children of illiterate mothers are (AOR: 1.30, 95% CI 1.19–1.53). However, mothers’ occupation and fathers’ education are not statistically associated with the immunization status of children. Mothers exposed to mass media were significantly more likely to fully immunize their children (AOR: 1.42, 95% CI 1.20–1.67), highlighting the role of information access in promoting health behaviors. Household wealth also had a strong association with the vaccination coverage of children. Children from the richer wealth quintile are more likely to be fully immunized (AOR: 1.51, 95% CI 1.14–1.96) than those from the poorest quintile. Additionally, children of mothers of others category are less likely (AOR: 0.58, 95% CI 0.38–0.88) to be vaccinated than are those of mothers of scheduled caste (Table 2).

Model 3 also confirms that maternal exposure to any form of IPV is significantly associated with lower child immunization coverage, and this association remains consistent with Model 2 even after adjusting sociodemographic characteristics. Mothers who reported experiencing any form of IPV had significantly lower odds of fully immunizing their children compared to those who did not (AOR: 0.89, 95% CI 0.74–0.99). This suggests that IPV negatively influences maternal health-seeking behaviors related to child preventive care. Female children had slightly lower odds of being fully immunized than male children (AOR: 0.90, 95% CI 0.75–0.99), indicating persistent gender-based disparities in healthcare utilization.

Children of birth order 4 or more had significantly lower odds of full immunization (AOR: 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.87) compared to first-born children, suggesting that immunization coverage declines with higher birth order. Maternal age was positively associated with immunization. Mothers aged 25–34 and 35–49 had higher odds of fully immunizing their children (AORs: 1.12 and 1.32, respectively), compared to those aged 18–24. Higher maternal education was associated with increased immunization coverage. Mothers with secondary (AOR: 1.37, 95% CI 1.12–1.68) and higher education (AOR: 1.41, 95% CI 1.07–1.72) had significantly greater odds of immunizing their children compared to those with no education.

Interestingly, rural mothers had higher odds of immunizing their children (AOR: 1.40, 95% CI 1.16–1.69) than urban counterparts, possibly reflecting the effectiveness of rural outreach programs or differing healthcare-seeking patterns. There was a clear socioeconomic gradient; children from richer and richest households had significantly higher odds of being immunized (AOR: 1.50, 95% CI 1.16:1.96) compared to those from the poorest households. Belonging to the “Other” caste category was associated with significantly lower odds of full immunization (AOR: 0.57, 95% CI 0.36–0.85) compared to Scheduled Castes, indicating social disparities in immunization coverage.

This finding indicates that IPV has a significant detrimental effect on childhood immunization.

Moderation effects

To assess whether women’s education, the sex of the child, and birth order moderated the effects of spousal IPV on the immunization status of children, the education level of the mother, the sex of the child, the birth order and the IPV interaction terms were analyzed (Table 3). Overall, several moderation effects were identified in the multivariate logistic moderation analysis. The first effect was the association between exposure to physical IPV and full immunization, moderated by secondary-level education (AOR = 1.87, 95% CI 1.50–1.98; p < 0.001). Sexual, emotional, and any form of IPV are also moderated by a secondary level of education. The sex of the child positively moderates the relationship between any form of IPV and full immunization (AOR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.59–0.90; p = 0.003). There was an association between exposure to sexual IPV and full immunization for those with female children (AOR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.38–0.83; p = 0.004). Discussion

In this paper, we examine the impact of different types of intimate partner violence (IPV) on children’s immunization status. Our findings show that children of women who experience IPV are significantly more likely to be under-immunized, a pattern also observed in previous studies from India18,22. IPV contributes to reduced maternal healthcare utilization23, which can compromise child health. This growing body of evidence highlights the link between maternal IPV and an increased risk of child illness and death, both in India and globally18,24–27.

While approximately 80% of children whose mothers experienced intimate partner violence (IPV) are fully immunized, it is important to examine the remaining 20% who are not. Although IPV is a significant risk factor for under-immunization, it does not act alone. The children in this group likely face compounded vulnerabilities. Maternal mental health issues such as depression and anxiety, are common among IPV survivors and may hinder their ability to access preventive care like immunization13,23. Economic barriers, including financial dependency and poverty, further limit healthcare access18,26, while rural residence and systemic health service gaps add logistical obstacles. IPV can also lead to reduced autonomy and social isolation, undermining women’s decision-making power for child healthcare2,28,29. Additional household-level stressors such as food insecurity and lack of documentation may also affect immunization30.

Women who endure IPV often face mental trauma, lifelong physical disabilities, chronic health conditions, and other morbidities. Children of women who have been exposed to IPV tend to have lower immunization rates, more frequent visits to emergency rooms, and greater interaction with their pediatricians. These children also face heightened risks of obesity, nutritional deficiencies, and poor lung function, in addition to reporting more general health problems compared to children without IPV exposure31.

There are several pathways through which IPV may affect children’s health. In India, approximately 30% of women have reported experiencing domestic violence, affecting millions annually8. Women who had experienced physical abuse by their husband or live-in partner were much more likely to describe their health as poor, be diagnosed with an STD, and experience other gynecological issues than those who had not been abused32,33,35. Furthermore, women who experienced severe abuse spent nearly twice as many days in bed due to illness compared to women who did not experience abuse34,36.

Emotional and sexual violence significantly undermines women’s physical, psychological, and social well-being, with direct consequences for maternal and child health. Emotional IPV, through verbal abuse, threats, humiliation, and coercive control, often leads to depression, anxiety, and low self-efficacy, which reduce a woman’s ability to seek timely and appropriate healthcare, including child immunization18,35. These mental health challenges are further compounded in patriarchal households where women have limited autonomy, making it difficult to make independent health decisions or travel for medical appointments35,36.

Similarly, sexual violence often accompanied by stigma, trauma, and fear can lead to social withdrawal and reduced trust in healthcare systems, thereby lowering the likelihood of seeking antenatal care or following up on immunization schedules29,37. In South Asian contexts, women subjected to sexual IPV have been shown to be less likely to deliver in health facilities and access preventive child healthcare. Moreover, both emotional and sexual IPV frequently co-occur with economic abuse and controlling behaviors, which further constrain women’s mobility and financial ability to access healthcare services. This multi-dimensional restriction psychological, social, and economic forms a powerful barrier to child immunization, particularly in low-resource settings where systemic support is often limited38,39.

IPV is also associated with increased controlling behaviour by the partner. A dominant partner may restrict a woman’s access to financial resources, education, employment, and healthcare, all of which can impact the health of the children, including their access to immunization services. Studies indicate that Indian women, who are primarily responsible for their children’s health, often face low levels of autonomy. Despite their responsibility, these women may have little influence over household finances or decisions related to childcare33–36.

Interestingly, our finding that physical IPV was not significantly associated with child immunization, unlike emotional and sexual IPV, aligns with some emerging research suggesting that physical abuse while more visible may sometimes prompt outside intervention or healthcare contact, partially offsetting its negative effects37. In contrast, emotional and sexual violence often remain hidden, are associated with prolonged psychological distress and social isolation, and may more deeply impair a mother’s ability to access timely child healthcare38. These distinctions underscore the importance of differentiating between types of IPV in both research and intervention design.

Mothers with limited mobility often face delays in obtaining medical care for their children, as they must wait for a family member to accompany them or take their child to a healthcare facility. Many women also feel they cannot seek medical care for their sick child without first obtaining approval from their husband or parent-in-law. On the other hand, women with greater freedom of movement are more confident in navigating the healthcare system independently. As a result, these women are more likely to adhere to their child’s vaccination schedule, which requires regular visits to medical facilities at designated intervals23,36,38–41.

To date, no studies have examined the moderating effects of women’s educational status, the sex of the child, and the child’s birth order on the adverse impact of IPV on children’s immunization status. Our research indicates that women’s education moderates the relationship between IPV and child immunization.

Maternal education is a pivotal determinant of child health, particularly in the context of immunization. Educated mothers are more likely to possess enhanced health literacy, enabling them to comprehend vaccination schedules, understand the importance of immunizations, and navigate healthcare systems effectively. A study analyzing data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-4) highlighted those children of mothers with higher education levels had significantly better immunization coverage compared to those whose mothers had no formal education42. Furthermore, maternal health literacy, distinct from formal education, has been independently associated with improved child vaccination coverage in both rural and urban settings in India40. In the context of IPV, educated mothers may have greater autonomy and decision-making power, potentially mitigating the adverse effects of IPV on child immunization43.

Specifically, children of women with secondary education levels were more likely to be fully immunized than those of women with no education. Education serves as a protective factor when women are exposed to IPV, increasing the likelihood of their children receiving immunizations. Studies have also highlighted gender disparities in child immunization18,36,44. Our study further explored the differences in immunization rates between male and female children, revealing a lower likelihood of full immunization in female children compared to male children. Gender disparities in child immunization persist in India, often influenced by deep-rooted cultural norms and son preference. Studies have shown that girls are less likely to receive complete vaccinations compared to boys45,46. In households experiencing IPV, a mother’s diminished decision-making capacity may further exacerbate these disparities, leading to lower immunization rates among female children. The intersection of IPV and gender bias underscores the need for targeted interventions that address both issues concurrently46.

Additionally, we found that children born later in the birth order were less likely to receive full immunization compared to first-born children. Evidence indicates that higher birth order is associated with a decreased likelihood of complete immunization, possibly due to resource dilution and increased caregiving burdens in larger families47,48. In the presence of IPV, these challenges may be magnified, as financial strain and maternal stress can further impede healthcare-seeking behaviors. Additionally, mothers facing IPV may prioritize the needs of younger or male children in resource-limited settings, leading to uneven immunization across siblings48.

While immunization services can be leveraged to promote awareness about intimate partner violence (IPV), they also present a crucial opportunity to identify women experiencing abuse. This reflects a bi-directional relationship, just as IPV undermines maternal and child health service utilization, routine immunization visits can serve as discreet and non-stigmatizing entry points for IPV screening and support. However, this opportunity is often missed due to limited training and awareness among healthcare providers49,50. Evidence suggests that when frontline health workers are adequately trained to recognize and respond to IPV, identification and referral rates improve significantly51. Integrating IPV screening protocols and brief counseling interventions into child health service encounters can offer timely support for survivors and enhance the overall health system’s response to violence against women52,53.

Moreover, our analysis revealed that women who lacked exposure to mass media were significantly less likely to complete their child’s immunization schedule. This finding highlights the critical role of mass communication in influencing health-seeking behavior, particularly in resource-limited contexts54,55. Health facilities especially immunization clinics offer valuable platforms for targeted health messaging. We recommend that posters, pamphlets, and audiovisual materials addressing both childhood immunization and intimate partner violence (IPV) be displayed in clinic waiting areas. These materials can help educate women about IPV, inform them of available support services, and promote awareness of their health rights, while also encouraging adherence to childhood immunization schedules56. Such communication interventions have been shown to enhance awareness and shift health behaviors related to maternal and child health and IPV across various low- and middle-income countries57,58.

Conclusion

The intersection of intimate partner violence (IPV) and maternal and child health is a pressing public health issue with far-reaching implications. IPV compromises maternal health, disrupts caregiving practices, and limits access to essential healthcare services, including childhood immunizations. Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that IPV erodes women’s autonomy and decision-making power, thereby contributing to underutilization of critical health services for both mothers and children.

To address these intersecting vulnerabilities, programmatic interventions must be both integrated and targeted. One actionable recommendation is the incorporation of child immunization status screening during the intake process for women entering shelters or safe houses. This would help identify and promptly address immunization gaps among children exposed to IPV. Further, training community health workers and social service providers to routinely assess and refer children for immunization can ensure timely and equitable healthcare access.

Establishing strong referral networks between IPV support services and public health systems is essential to providing continuity of care. In parallel, shelter-based health education programs can raise awareness among survivors about the importance of immunization and other child health services.

Additionally, integrating targeted media campaigns within health facilities can raise awareness about IPV and promote timely child immunization, especially among women with limited access to traditional information channels.

Finally, fostering collaboration between public health agencies, non-governmental organizations, and women’s rights groups is critical to developing a holistic response that addresses the dual burden of IPV and poor child health outcomes. Such a multisectoral approach can help safeguard the health and well-being of both women and children, promote gender equity in healthcare access, and contribute to the broader goals of public health and social justice.
