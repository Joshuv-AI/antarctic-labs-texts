Learning mimetic cuckoo call innovations from neighbors in a Chinese songbird
Authors: Changjian Fu, Xiaochun Wang, Fiona Backhouse, Zhongqiu Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSome oscine passerines incorporate heterospecific sounds into their repertoires, including vocalizations of other bird species, sounds of other fauna, and even anthropogenic sounds, through vocal mimicry. However, few studies have investigated whether mimics learn heterospecific sounds from model species or from conspecific tutors. Here, we investigate mimicry acquisition using innovation in Cuculidae calls imitated by the Chinese blackbird (Turdus mandarinus). If the mimicry innovation arises and spreads among several neighbors and is not produced by model species, the mimicry must be acquired partially from conspecifics. We found that: (1) Cuculidae calls imitated by blackbirds were reasonably accurate, but with some differences between mimetic and real calls in acoustic structures. (2) We identified four unique mimetic units (mimicry innovation or copy errors), and these units only occurred at certain sites and were shared by several neighbors. In aggregate, frequency parameters (the first principal component) of unique mimetic units were higher than usual mimetic units (p < 0.001). Our findings provide further evidence that mimetic units can be partially learnt from conspecifics based on four cases of unique mimetic units. Introduction

Prior studies have estimated that 8.9% of oscine species worldwide exhibit some level of vocal mimicry1. Most of these songbirds incorporate various mimetic vocal or non-vocal sounds into their song repertoires, combining vocal mimicry with species-specific songs2-4. Vocal mimicry can include: heterospecific songs and calls of other bird species (e.g. ‘recital’ song of male superb lyrebirds, Menura novaehollandiae)3, mobbing alarm calls (e.g. D-song of male superb lyrebirds during ‘dance’ displays)5, sounds of other fauna such as anuran species (e.g. vocal mimicry in northern mockingbirds, Mimus polyglottos)6, and even environmental sounds or artificial noise7. Vocal mimics can acquire these heterospecific and non-vocal sounds through three pathways, including: (1) learning directly from model species, (2) learning from conspecific tutors (social transmission), and (3) the evolution of vocal mimicry among innate bird calls, such as the cuckoo nestlings that mimic the begging calls of the host species nestlings7,8. Oscine mimics acquire mimetic sounds mainly by the first two ways, but only a few studies have verified these hypotheses9,10. We still know little about mimicry acquisition in most vocal mimics.

One method to understand the acquisition of mimicry is to investigate the mimetic repertoires9,11. When individuals learn directly from the models, neighboring individuals will share more similar mimetic repertoires than distant individuals, because mimetic repertoires might be tied to the local species assemblage, or may resemble the local dialects of model species9,12. Under this scenario, differences between individuals or populations in mimetic repertoires are consistent with differences in model species occurrences13. However, learning by social transmission can also cause mimetic repertoire sharing and model species matching9, if the mimics only choose the models that can be heard in the surrounding environment. Comparisons of mimetic repertoires cannot necessarily distinguish whether notes are acquired from heterospecific or conspecific tutors. More subtle evidence is then needed for further investigation, such as analyzing the song sequence or acoustic structure of mimetic vocalizations shared by mimics from different sites9,10. An alternative method is to find mimetic units with innovation or copy errors and investigate the extent to which they arise and spread. Song innovation has been found in some non-mimicking songbird species14. For example, over 37% of the Rottnest Island population of western gerygones (Gerygone fusca) shared a novel rhythmic song that was not found in their mainland populations in Australia14. To our best knowledge, few studies have investigated whether mimetic song innovation exists and is socially transmitted among conspecific neighbors. Some mimics such as Acrocephalus warblers have been observed to extract song elements from different model species and recombine these elements into a new song not produced by models7,15. Theoretically, if a mimetic song innovation (or copy error) is shared by at least two conspecific individuals, then the mimics must at least partially acquire mimicry from conspecifics because the innovation cannot be produced by the models.

In this study, we focus on mimicry innovation or copy errors to investigate the acquisition of mimetic units in a common songbird in China: the Chinese blackbird (Turdus mandarinus)16,17. This songbird is a versatile mimic that is often observed to have elaborate mimetic song repertoires. However, no published evidence illustrates their vocal mimicry and its acquisition. Chinese blackbirds have often been observed mimicking the mating calls of cuckoos, which provide us with ideal candidates for investigating mimicry acquisition. We chose Cuculidae species as models for two reasons: first, cuckoo calls are usually simple and stereotyped18,19, therefore it is easy to identify mimetic calls within blackbird song repertoires. In addition, if some learning mistakes or innovations occur in mimetic cuckoo calls, it’s easy to identify. Second, song dialects exist in many songbirds, and this can generate dialect matching between songbird models and mimics12,20,21. This dialect matching will conceal social transmission of mimetic units: it will be difficult to ensure that the mimics learn dialects from conspecific neighbors or model species if the imitations are accurate. On the contrary, Cuculidae calls are innate22, whereas songs of oscine passerines are acquired by learning from tutors23,24. Geographical variation of cuckoo calls is only present among subspecies across a very large scale25, consequently, dialect matching in mimicry of Cuculidae model species should not be apparent on a microgeographic scale.

To investigate the acquisition of mimicry in Chinese blackbirds, we used mimicry of cuckoo calls to answer two questions. First, we investigated whether the cuckoo call imitations by male Chinese blackbirds are accurate in acoustic structures. To test this, we compared acoustic structures between model calls and mimetic units. Second, we investigated whether mimetic song innovation exists in cuckoo call imitation and is shared by several individuals or not. If so, we also tested the differences in acoustic structures between unique (caused by innovation or copy errors) and usual mimetic units. If several males within a population share a unique mimetic unit, we can determine that mimetic units are at least partially acquired from conspecific tutors.

Methods

Study species and sampling sites

The Chinese blackbird is a large, all-black thrush with an orange to bright yellow bill17. It is one of the most common urban bird species in China, inhabiting nearly all types of urban ecosystems17,26. Individual males are territorial and largely solitary during the breeding season, and produce very large song repertoires containing mimetic units. Our study object, subspecies T. m. mandarinus, is sedentary in our study area—Nanjing, eastern China16. Blackbirds start chasing each other, claiming territory and singing in mid-February and continue until the courtship period ends in late June (F. C. and W. X. 2023, personal observation). We studied blackbirds at seven sampling sites (Fig. 1a), including urban parks, university campuses, greenbelts, and scenic areas in the urban area of Nanjing (32.0351-32.1311°N, 118.7541-118.9550°E). The main tree species at these sites are Platanus orientalis, Magnolia Grandiflora, Cedrus deodara, Metasequoia glyptostroboides, and Ginkgo biloba, and these provide perches for singing blackbirds.

Recording song repertoires

We recorded the song repertoires of male blackbirds in the daytime (08:00-19:00 h) during breeding season (February-June, 2023). Weather without rain or strong wind was required for field work. Recordings were made by following focal individuals as closely as possible without disturbing them, usually 10-20 m from the focal individuals10. Male territories were found by route survey or random walking and listening for vocal cues (song or call)17. We distinguished focal birds and their neighbors by three criteria. First, distance was at least 100 m between two territories we chose. Second, male blackbirds usually produced songs in several fixed perches surrounding their territories, and they would expel neighbors who strayed into their territory (F. C. and W. X. 2023, personal observation). Third, we needed to observe focal birds for at least one hour to record over 10 min high-quality songs (high signal to noise ratio), and their neighbors usually sing during this time; in most cases, this is enough to discriminate focal birds and neighbors.

We recorded repertoires of 57 males in total. Of these males, we recorded at least 10 min of singing (duration: Mean ± SD = 23.9 ± 10.4 min, range 10.9-63.1 min) from 52 individuals to cover their entire repertoires. From our own field observations of Chinese blackbirds and studies of a related species, common blackbirds (T. merula)27,28, in most cases, 10 min of continuous song bouts contain over 100 songs that cover all or most of the blackbirds’ repertoires. For a few individuals that produced extremely large repertoires (N = 9), we recorded their songs over 20 min or as appropriate. Although the recording durations of the remaining five males were too short (less than 10 min) to cover entire repertoires, at least two focal mimetic units (mimetic Cuculidae calls) were contained. Consequently, song recordings of these males were also used for subsequent analysis. Recordings were made using a handheld RODE NTG4 + directional microphone and a TASCAM Portacapture X8 portable recorder (TEAC Co., Ltd, Tokyo) with 48 kHz sample rate and 16-bit or 24-bit resolution. All recording files were saved as wav format.

Putative models and call recording collection

A total of nine Cuculidae species occur at our sampling sites according to China Bird Report (http://www.birdreport.cn/), field experience of researchers (F. C. and W. X. 2023, personal observation), and anecdotal evidence provided by birdwatchers. These Cuculidae species include the lesser coucal (Centropus bengalensis), chestnut-winged cuckoo (Clamator coromandus), Asian koel (Eudynamys scolopaceus), large hawk-cuckoo (Hierococcyx sparverioides), Indian cuckoo (Cuculus micropterus), Himalayan cuckoo (C. saturates), oriental cuckoo (C. optatus), common cuckoo (C. canorus), and Asian lesser cuckoo (C. poliocephalus). Most of these species are summer visitors in Nanjing and produce stereotyped mating calls during the breeding season16,18,29. We collected mating call recordings of these cuckoos through our own field recordings (May-July, 2023), recordings provided by birdwatchers in Nanjing, and recordings from near our sampling sites downloaded from Xeno-canto (https://www.xeno-canto.org). Recordings for each species were collected from at least two individuals, and each recording contained at least three high-quality calls.

Identifying usual and unique mimetic units

Avisoft-SASLab Lite (https://www.avisoft.com/) was used to create spectrograms for each recording with settings: Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) 256, FlatTop window, frame size 100%, bandwidth 235 Hz, resolution 63 Hz, overlap 50%, temporal resolution 8 ms. We looked throughout all recordings of blackbird songs (nearly 22 h in total) to find mimetic cuckoo calls. Mimetic units were identified by listening to recordings and comparing spectrograms by eye between blackbird songs and the calls of putative models. We also consulted professional experts and senior bird-watchers with abundant natural history tips in China to confirm that the units we identified were cuckoo call mimicry rather than species-specific songs. Blackbirds usually incorporate mimetic units into songs (Figure S1, F. C. and W. X. 2023, personal observation), thus if a song contained mimetic cuckoo calls, we cut this song into a single recording.

In our study, we defined ‘usual’ mimetic units as entire vocalizations or one or more elements from the model species’ calls that are mimicked without obvious changes. We defined ‘unique’ mimetic units as mimicked vocal elements with an acoustic structure that is altered from that found in the models (i.e., innovation or copy errors)7. For example, an element may be only produced once within the original model call, but when it is extracted by the mimic and repeated two or three times, this forms a new song type or a series of ‘unique’ mimetic units. We checked all recordings we cut to find unique mimetic units based on our definition. We generated maps using geographic coordinates of individuals that produced unique mimetic units to determine if they are neighbors (Fig. 1a). Mimetic element 2 of large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 in site 5 was usually repeated one to three times, and so these were defined as unique repeated mimetic units (Figs. 1b and 2). In addition, some individuals in site 5 also produced mimetic call type 2 (or element 2 only) with an obvious lower frequency in the spectrogram compared with the model calls, usual mimetic units, and unique repeated mimetic units (Figs. 1b-c and 2e-f). These low-pitched mimetic call type 2 were not repeated, so we defined these units as unique low-pitched mimetic units.

Acoustic parameter measurement

For the Cuculidae species that we verified were imitated by blackbirds, we collected recordings from at least seven individuals, with each recording containing at least four high-quality calls. Acoustic parameters were measured using Raven Pro 64 bit 1.6.5 30 with settings: Hann display type, FFT 512, overlap 50%. We selected a total of 217 cuckoo calls from recordings of the models, and 225 mimetic cuckoo calls from 310 songs of blackbirds. We then drew selection boxes around each unit or call on the spectrogram, using Raven to automatically measure the following seven parameters for all mimetic units and model calls: (1) high frequency (fhigh, kHz), (2) low frequency (flow, kHz), (3) frequency range (frange, kHz), (4) peak frequency (fpeak, kHz), (5) bandwidth 90% (BW90, kHz), (6) duration (D, s), (7) duration 90% (D90, s, Table S1, Figure S2). For large hawk-cuckoo call type 2, we measured these seven parameters for both element 1 and 2 (Figure S2), as well as the (8) time interval (TI, s) between the two elements (Table S1, Figure S2).

Imitation accuracy

Only two blackbirds mimicked Indian cuckoo calls, thus these mimetic units (N = 2, n = 5, Table S2, N indicates the number of individuals; n indicates the number of mimetic units) were removed from statistical analysis. To evaluate mimicry accuracy, we first conducted principal component analysis based on acoustic parameters (1)-(8) (Table S1) to assess the similarity between blackbird mimicry and model calls. Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) was also performed using hclust function (package ‘stats’) based on acoustic parameters (1)-(7) (Table S1) to assess the mimicry accuracy5. For large hawk-cuckoo call type 2, we only took the acoustic parameters of element 2 into hierarchical clustering analysis, because some blackbird individuals only mimicked this element. Dendrograms were drawn using ggtree function (packages ‘ggplot2’, ‘ape’, ‘ggtree’, ‘treeio’, and ‘ggdendro’) in R31-34. Shapiro-Wilk tests and Levene’s tests were then conducted for each acoustic parameter. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then used to compare differences between model calls and mimetic units for parameters that obeyed both normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. Kruskal-Wallis rank sum tests were used for the remaining parameters. Some blackbird individuals mimicked only element 2 of large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 (Fig. 2d-e), so we also conducted ANOVAs or Kruskal-Wallis rank sum tests on the same parameters for element 2 specifically.

Mimetic unit analysis

To compare acoustic differences among unique, usual mimetic units, and model calls, we first performed principal component analyses to condense acoustic parameters. PC1 explained 46.5% and 62.1% of the variation for mimetic element 2 of large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 and Asian koel call respectively (Table S3). Frequency parameters contributed mostly to PC1 for both mimetic types (Table S3). Unique, usual mimetic units, and model calls appeared to mainly differ in frequencies on the spectrograms, thus PC1 was extracted for comparing differences. Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was then used to compare differences among model calls, usual and unique mimetic units. For large hawk-cuckoo call type 2, we only analyzed the acoustic parameters of element 2, because some individuals only mimicked this element.

Ethical note

The study was conducted and approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Committee of Nanjing University (IACUC-2003001). This study was purely observational and did not involve any animal capture or sampling of blood or tissue. In recording song repertoires, we maintained an adequate distance (10-20 m) to avoid disturbing focal birds’ spontaneous activity.

Results

Imitation accuracy

Chinese blackbirds sang with immediate variety, switching to a new song type immediately after a previous song. Nearly 44% (25/57) of Chinese blackbirds mimicked vocalizations from Cuculidae species. Four mating calls from three model species were imitated by the blackbirds in total, including the large hawk-cuckoo (call type 1: N = 7, n = 56; call type 2: N = 13, n = 114), Asian koel (N = 10, n = 55), and Indian cuckoo (N = 2, n = 5, Fig. 2, Table S2).

Principal component analysis (PCAs) showed that mimetic units and model calls were classified clearly for all cuckoo calls (Fig. 3 ). PC1 (mostly frequency parameters including fhigh, frange, fpeak, and BW90, Table S1, Table S4), PC2 (mostly temporal parameters including D and D90, Table S4), and PC3 together explained over 85% of the total variation of acoustic parameters in all PCAs except large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 (72.7%, Table S4). Hierarchical cluster analysis partitioned model and mimic cuckoo calls into five main groups: two sets of Asian koel calls (groups 1 and 2), two sets of element 2 of large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 (groups 3 and 5), and one set of large hawk-cuckoo call type 1 (group 4, Fig. 4). Most imitations by blackbirds were clustered together and could be distinguished from model calls.

Most parameters between mimetic units and model calls were significantly different (p < 0.05, Table S5). Durations of mimetic units were shorter than models for all cuckoo call types (Duration: p < 0.01; Duration 90%: p < 0.01, Table S5). Mimetic units had a broader frequency range and bandwidth 90% than both model large hawk-cuckoo call type 1 (frequency range: H = 62.841, df = 1, p < 0.001; bandwidth 90%: H = 52.177, df = 1, p < 0.001) and Asian koel call (frequency range: H = 23.286, df = 1, p < 0.001; bandwidth 90%: H = 21.739, df = 1, p < 0.001, Table S5). Mimetic units had a narrower frequency range than model large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 for both the first (H = 25.863, df = 1, p < 0.001) and the second elements (H = 13.205, df = 1, p < 0.001; F1, 127 = 10.707, p = 0.001), but had no significant difference in bandwidth 90% (Table S5).

Unique mimetic unit

Four types of unique mimetic units were identified in total. First, unique mimetic Indian cuckoo calls were only produced with the first three elements (Fig. 2k), but the original model calls contained four elements (Fig. 2j). These units were only found in two neighboring males in site 6 (individual 6-10 and 6-11, Table S2, Fig. 1). Second, unique mimetic Asian koel calls dramatically increased in frequency in the latter half of the call (Fig. 2i), whereas usual mimetic units had only a mild increase in frequency (Fig. 2h). These unique units were shared by four individuals in site 3 (Table S2, Fig. 1), comprising two sets of neighbors. Third, unique repeated mimetic large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 was a repetition of the second element in the call, usually repeated two or three times (Fig. 2d-e), and was shared by four individuals in site 5 (Table S2, Fig. 1), three of them neighbors. Fourth, unique low-pitched mimetic large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 was an altered unit with lower pitch than repeated, usual mimetic units, and model calls (H = 94.319, df = 3, p < 0.001, Fig. 5a), and was shared by four individuals in site 5 (Table S2, Fig. 1). Three males in site 5 produced both unique repeated and low-pitched mimetic element 2 of large hawk-cuckoo call type 2 (Fig. 2f); among these individuals, two males (individual 5-1 and 5-5, Table S2, Fig. 1) often incorporated a low-pitched mimetic element 2 and one to three repeated mimetic element 2 into a song (i.e., hybrid mimetic song, Table S2, Fig. 2e). Another individual (5-2, Table S2) in site 5 produced both usual and unique low-pitched mimetic element 2.

Discussion

In this study, we identified four unique mimetic units that were shared by several male blackbirds, suggesting social transmission occurs in vocal mimicry. In addition, each unique mimetic unit only occurred at one site and was shared by several neighbors, indicating social learning of mimetic units. Our results also suggest that all cuckoo calls imitated by Chinese blackbirds were reasonably accurate but with some differences in acoustic structure, as the PCAs and HCA classified both mimetic units and model calls clearly, but with some overlap between model and mimetic calls (Figs. 3 and 4). For mimicry accuracy, the results are consistent with studies on other species that found some differences between mimetic and model vocalizations, with one mimic even producing mimicry from different models with different accuracy35,36. In blackbirds, mimetic accuracy also appears to differ between models: the mimetic Asian koel calls are more easily distinguished from real calls with less overlap than other cuckoo calls according to Fig. 3d, and all parameters were significantly different (Table S5).We also found that all mimetic call types had shorter durations than model calls. A shorter duration in mimetic units has also been found in other mimicking species, such as superb lyrebirds, which mimic fewer repetitions of song elements of grey shrike-thrush (Colluricincla harmonica) songs36. We present three possible hypotheses for the acoustic differences between model and mimetic calls. First, we speculate that blackbirds alter acoustic features, such as song duration, to save energy when producing mimetic cuckoo calls. Cuckoos usually produce continuous loud calls with high energy; for example, the common cuckoo calls can be heard from a distance of approximately 2-3 km37,38. Shorter mimetic cuckoo calls may be easier for blackbirds to produce to some degree. Second, low mimetic accuracy may be a result of learning mimetic units from conspecifics. For example, mimetic songs by Albert’s lyrebirds (Menura alberti) show less variation in acoustic structures within sites than the real songs of the satin bowerbird (Ptilonorhynchus violaceus), suggesting social transmission of mimetic songs12. In theory, inaccurate mimicry or copy errors may happen in males that acquire heterospecific sounds directly from other species, and these inaccurate mimetic units may then be socially transmitted among several neighbors. Third, mimetic accuracy may be limited by physical constraints, as the syrinx of the mimicking species is best suited to producing species-specific songs rather than heterospecific vocalizations7.

To date, little is known about how oscine passerine mimics learn and develop their mimetic song repertoires7. A few studies suggest that some mimics learn directly from heterospecific species7,9, whereas some other studies support learning mimicry from both conspecifics and model species12. One recent study demonstrated that the sequence of mimetic units is socially transmitted within populations of male Albert’s lyrebirds10, though mimetic units may still be at least partially learnt from heterospecific models10,12. Our findings suggest that some unique mimetic units are acquired from conspecifics. Mimetic song innovation (or copy errors) could happen when one male learns a call from the model species or from conspecifics and then sings an altered version of this call, which the close neighbors then also acquire. However, we argue that while blackbirds acquire unique mimetic units from conspecifics, there must still be some learning from heterospecifics. First, to resemble the model calls, the mimetic units were most likely initially learnt from the model species. Second, the fact that each site with mimicry contained some ‘usual’ mimicry suggests at least some learning from heterospecifics. We found that each unique mimetic unit type only occurs in one sampling site (Fig. 1), indicating that social transmission of certain units may occur only within the neighborhood. However, individuals in most sites produced cuckoo calls despite distances of up to more than 20 km between sites (Fig. 1). Chinese blackbirds have limited dispersal during the breeding season (F. C. and W. X. 2023, personal observation), and so it is unlikely that the usual mimetic units spread across sites through learning from conspecifics.

Learning mimetic units from conspecifics may function in song-type matching of mimetic song types7. Vocal mimicry may function in both mate attraction and resource/territory defense in a similar way to non-mimetic birdsong7, and song-type matching usually signals aggressively to rivals and territory intruders (i.e. neighbors or other males)39,40. Mimetic song-type matching may occur in mimics such as superb lyrebirds (F. B. 2023, personal observation)7 and chorister robins (Cossypba dichroa)41. Male chorister robins reproduced mimetic songs to match the mimicry of vocalizations of black-headed orioles (Oriolus larvatrrs) and emerald cuckoos (Cbrysococcyx cupreus) broadcast by the playback experiments41. The sharing of mimetic units consequently may function in territorial defense through mimetic song-type matching during encounters between close neighbors7. This is also supported by our finding that every unique unit was found among several close neighbors. Another alternative explanation of the social transmission can be facilitating female attraction. In non-mimicking songbirds, females prefer familiar songs from their natal regions to the unfamiliar songs from distant regions42,43. For instance, female rufous-collared sparrows (Zonotrichia capensis) prefer the song dialect of males from their own population to the songs of a population 25 km away42. However, we still have limited knowledge about if or how vocal mimicry functions in intra-/intersexual interactions, thus mimetic repertoire analyses and interactive playback experiments should be conducted on both males and females in the future.

Our findings also report that individuals can mimic two variants in acoustic structure of the same original call type from one model species. We speculate that individuals acquired unique repeated and low-pitched mimetic units from multiple tutors that could be both conspecific and model species, or only model species, and then passed these units on to other conspecifics, resulting in shared unique mimetic units. This finding provides further evidence that blackbirds acquire mimicry from conspecifics and that the process of mimicry learning can be more complex than previously expected. Similarly, a hybrid mimetic song type also occurred in two neighbors (individuals 5-1 and 5-5, Fig. 1), who incorporated both repeated and low-pitched units into one song type (Fig. 1b). Hybrid songs (or mixed songs) have been found in non-mimicking passerines, both within species (such as the ovenbird [Seiurus aurocapilla])44, or even between two cryptic species (such as between the Sichuan leaf warbler [Phylloscopus forresti] and the Gansu leaf warbler [P. kansuensis])45. For instance, the ovenbird is a single-song species, and a few individuals can produce hybrid songs composed of phrases from two different song types44, but the jury is still out on why hybrid songs exist. Unlike ovenbirds, blackbirds produce elaborate song repertoires and are considered open-ended learners46; the arising of hybrid mimetic song may simply reflect the versatile learning ability of blackbird individuals. Other mimicking species also produce complex hybrid units, for example, northern mockingbirds combine both red-eyed vireo (Vireo olivaceus) ‘myaah’ call and American robin (Turdus migratorius) alarm call into one single mimetic song47. Further studies of ontogeny in certain individuals could provide deep insights into the development of mimicry learning and mimetic units rearrangement in the future.

In this study, we leveraged mimicry innovation (or copy errors) of cuckoo calls mimicked by Chinese blackbirds to investigate the acquisition of mimetic units. We found that Chinese blackbirds mimicked model calls accurately, but with some differences in acoustic structures, indicating the trade-off between accuracy and versatility. Four types of unique mimetic units were sung by blackbirds at three sites respectively, providing evidence that blackbirds obtain heterospecific vocalizations at least partially from conspecific neighbors. Moreover, frequency parameters of unique units were higher than usual mimetic units and model calls in general. Our study provides an approach to investigating mimicry acquisition in other mimicking species. More similar studies on other avian mimics should be conducted to investigate if mimicry innovation is a widespread phenomenon among a variety of oscine mimics in the future. In addition, it remains to understand the mechanisms behind mimicry innovation or learning mistake and the pathway of their sharing, which may be based on laboratory approaches. These studies will help us to better understand how the mimicry is acquired based on ontogeny and cultural transmission.
