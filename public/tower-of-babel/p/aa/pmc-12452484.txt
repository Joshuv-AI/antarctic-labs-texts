Temporal dynamics of macrophage activation during Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi infection: An integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis
Authors: Fanfan Yang, Yuhui Liu, Ying Zhang, Dandan Wei, Zhongyi Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSalmonella enterica serovar Typhi (S. Typhi) is a major bacteria responsible for foodborne illnesses. Understanding the temporal dynamics of the host immune response and associated metabolic reprogramming is crucial for identifying potential therapeutic targets. This study employed an integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic approach to investigate the response of macrophages infected with S. Typhi over a defined stage (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was used to profile gene expression changes, while untargeted metabolomics characterized metabolic shifts. Gene set enrichment and pathway analyses were conducted to identify key immune and metabolic pathways. Additionally, Convergent Cross-Mapping (CCM) analysis was applied to uncover causal relationships between gene expression and metabolite fluctuations. Transcriptomic revealed distinct temporal phases of immune activation. Early responses were dominated by interferon signaling, cytokine production, and inflammatory responses, whereas later stages involved sustained immune signaling and metabolic adaptation. Key pathways, including NF-κB, TNF, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, were significantly enriched. Metabolomic profiling demonstrated a transition from glycolysis and amino acid metabolism in early infection stages to fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function in later stages, with glutathione metabolism playing a central role in oxidative stress regulation. CCM analysis identified critical genes (e.g. RGS4, KIF18A, RSPO1) exhibiting strong causal relationships with metabolic alterations, underscoring the tight integration between immune and metabolic responses. This study provides a comprehensive, time-resolved view of S. Typhi infection, highlighting dynamic immune activation and metabolic reprogramming in macrophages. These findings advance our understanding of host-pathogen interactions.KEYWORDS: Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, THP-1 macrophage, infection, transcriptomic, metabolomic

Introduction

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi (S. Typhi), a formidable Gram-negative pathogen and uniquely adapted to human hosts, stands as the primary causative agent of typhoid fever, a life-threatening systemic infection that afflicts approximately 11–21 million individuals globally each year, with a mortality rate of 10% in untreated cases [1–3]. This pathogen is also a major culprit behind severe foodborne illnesses, leveraging its sophisticated type III and VI secretion systems as molecular syringes to inject a repertoire of effector proteins directly into host cells [4]. These effector proteins act as molecular manipulators, intricately altering the host cytoskeleton dynamics and hijacking membrane trafficking pathways [5]. By doing so, S. Typhi not only gains efficient entry into host cells but also ensures its intracellular persistence, allowing it to evade the initial onslaught of the host immune system.

The journey of S. Typhi within the human body commences with fecal-oral transmission, a common route facilitated by contaminated food and water [6]. Once ingested, the pathogen targets and penetrates the intestinal epithelium, breaching the first line of defense [7]. It then disseminates through the lymphatic and hematogenous routes, ultimately reaching the mononuclear phagocyte system, with macrophages being its primary target. Macrophages, as key sentinels of the innate immune system, play a pivotal and complex role in the host defense against S. Typhi [8,9]. The interaction between S. Typhi and macrophages is a dynamic and intricate battle, where immune activation and metabolic rewiring are tightly coupled [10]. Upon encountering S. Typhi, macrophages immediately initiate a rapid and comprehensive transcriptional reprogramming. This reprogramming triggers the activation of interferon signaling pathways, which are crucial for antiviral and antibacterial responses [11,12]. Simultaneously, macrophages ramp up the production of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), orchestrating a powerful inflammatory response aimed at eliminating the invading pathogen [13,14].

Macrophages exhibit significant metabolic reprogramming in response to S. Typhi infection. During the early stages of infection, macrophages enhance glycolysis and amino acid metabolism to meet the demands of rapid activation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [15–17]. As the infection progresses, they shift toward reliance on fatty acid β-oxidation and enhanced mitochondrial function to obtain the energy support required for sustained defense [18,19]. This dynamic metabolic transition works synergistically with glutathione metabolism to collectively maintain the functional integrity of macrophages in an oxidative stress environment [20–22]. Key genes, such as G-protein signaling 4 (RGS4) and kinesin family member 18A (KIF18A) serve as central hubs linking immune and metabolic regulation: RGS4 modulates both immune responses and metabolic processes by regulating G protein-coupled receptor signaling [22–24], while KIF18A participates in the metabolic adaptation of immune cells through microtubule dynamics regulation [25]. These molecular mechanisms fully demonstrate the precise synergistic integration between immune defense and metabolic regulatory networks during host-pathogen interactions [26,27].

To comprehensively investigate the dynamic host-pathogen interactions, we adopt an integrated approach combining transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses at 2, 4, 8, and 24 hours post-infection. For transcriptomic analysis, we employ next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology, which allows for the comprehensive profiling of the entire transcriptome. NGS provides high-resolution data on gene expression levels, enabling us to detect even subtle changes in gene expression during the infection process. On the metabolomic front, we use advanced mass spectrometry-based techniques to identify and quantify a wide range of metabolites, providing insights into the metabolic changes occurring within the cells. Building upon this multi-omics framework, we systematically examine the immune response timeline: at 2 hours post-infection we capture early interferon signaling and inflammatory initiation; by 4 hours we observe intensified cytokine production and immune cells recruitment; at 8 hours we document the adaptive transition marked by chemokine-driven migration and enzyme activation; while 24 hours reveals sustained responses and macrophage metabolic adaptations for prolonged anti-S. Typhi defense. This carefully designed time-course study aligns with the sequential phases of S. Typhi infection, spanning from the initial pathogen recognition and acute inflammation to the later stages of metabolic reprogramming for persistent immunity. By integrating multi-omics data across these critical time points, this study aims to uncover the spatiotemporal mechanisms underlying the interactions between S. Typhi and macrophages. The insights gained from this research have the potential to identify novel immune-metabolic checkpoints, which could serve as promising targets for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies against typhoid fever and related foodborne illnesses caused by S. Typhi.

Methods

Strains and cell culture conditions

S. Typhi strain used in this study was wild-type S. Typhi GIFU10007 provided by Prof. Ying-Zhang at Department of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology of Jiangsu University School. Bacteria were grown overnight in 2 mL LB liquid medium shaking at 250 rpm 37°C. The bacteria reached logarithmic growth phase (OD600 nm = 0.4) after 1:100 dilution in fresh LB, centrifuged at 1700 × g, supernatant was discarded, and suspended in RPMI 1640 medium (11875093, Gibco) for extracellular infection assays. THP-1 human monocytes were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (TCHu 57, CAS) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099141C, Gibco) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 cell incubator.

Sample preparation and RNA extraction

THP-1 cells were induced by PMA (final concentration of 150 ng/mL)(16561–29–8, Sigma) for 48 h to be mature macrophage cells, which were infected with S. Typhi at a multiplicity of infection = 20:1. Equal volume of fresh LB culture medium was added at 0 h for the control group, cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 1 h, and then gentamicin was added to a final concentration of 100 μg/mL for 1 h to kill extracellular bacteria. One hour after addition of gentamicin was considered as the starting time point, medium was discarded, and cellular RNA was extracted after rinsing twice with 1 × PBS at 4°C. For the remaining infections, gentamicin concentration was decreased by 50 μg/mL and PMA-THP-1 macrophage RNA was extracted after culturing for the appropriate time (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h), with three biological replicates per time point. RNA extraction was performed using the TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted RNA was then quantified using ND-100 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop), ensuring an RNA integrity number (RIN) of at least 7.0 for each sample. Total RNA was extracted from PMA-THP-1 macrophages infected with S. Typhi at different time points.

Library preparation and transcriptomics RNA sequencing

For RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), it was performed by Shenzhen BGI Co., Ltd. The sequencing produced 6.64 Gb of raw data per sample on average. The total number of 16,965 genes was identified across all samples, ensuring comprehensive transcriptome coverage.

Data processing and quality control

The raw sequencing data underwent thorough quality control: Low-quality reads (those with phred scores below 20) were filtered out [28]. Sequences containing adapter contamination or poly-A tails were removed. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using the HISAT2 aligner, with the mapping efficiency calculated at 95.31% to the reference genome and 75.47% to genes. Raw read counts were processed using DESeq2 to normalize for library size and perform differential expression analysis. Gene expression was filtered for genes with at least one read in all replicates. Quality Control results are in Table S2 and Table S3.

Differential gene expression analysis

The DESeq2 pipeline was implemented using the R package to identify genes significantly differentially expressed at each time point compared to the control (0 h). The data were normalized using the “DESeqDataSetFromMatrix()” function, followed by the execution of the DE analysis via the DESeq() function. A significance threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.01 and log2 fold change (log2FC) > 2 was used to classify genes as significantly upregulated or downregulated at each time point (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h). To visualize the global changes in gene expression, volcano plots were generated for each time point comparing the gene expression between infected and control conditions. The volcano plots were constructed using the “ggplot2” package, where the x-axis represents the “log2 fold change” and the y-axis represents the “log10 adjusted p-value.” Genes with significant changes in expression (upregulated or downregulated) were highlighted with labels for the top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes. The “ggrepel” package was used to prevent overlapping labels for significant genes. The function “create_volcano_plot()” processed the DESeq2 results to categorize genes into “Upregulated,” “Downregulated,” or “Not Significant” groups.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG Pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the upregulated and downregulated gene sets at each time point using the clusterProfiler package. Gene sets were analyzed for their biological processes (GO terms) and enriched pathways (KEGG pathways). Enrichment was performed separately for upregulated and downregulated genes, and the top 20 pathways for each analysis were plotted using bar plots. Gene Ontology Enrichment was conducted using the “enrichGO()” function with the Biological Process (BP) ontology. Significant GO terms were selected with an adjusted p-value threshold of “p < 0.05.” KEGG pathway enrichment was assessed using the “enrichKEGG()” function. Similar to GO, a p-value threshold of “p < 0.05” was applied to select significant pathways. These results were visualized with bar plots showing the top 20 enriched terms for both GO and KEGG using the `ggplot2` package.

Clustering of temporal gene expression

To identify temporal expression patterns, genes were clustered based on their expression profiles across the five time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h). Clustering was performed using the “Mfuzz” algorithm, which identifies groups of genes with similar temporal expression profiles. Genes were classified into 9 clusters, each representing a distinct temporal expression pattern. Clusters were analyzed for significant enrichment in specific GO terms and KEGG pathways, using the same enrichment procedures described above. The clusterProfiler package was used for the enrichment analysis of the cluster-specific gene sets, and the results were visualized with bar plots for the top pathways and biological processes in each cluster.

Metabolomic sample collection and preparation

For the untargeted metabolomics and LC-MS/MS analysis by Shenzhen BGI Co., Ltd., cellular samples were collected at five different time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h) following infection. Each time point consisted of six biological replicates, yielding a total of 30 samples. The samples were assigned specific identifiers for tracking and organization during the experimental procedure.

Metabolomic data processing and quality control

Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [29]. This included peak extraction, alignment, and metabolite identification using the BMDB, mzCloud, and ChemSpider databases. Metabolite identification was based on accurate mass and retention time, with a precursor mass tolerance of < 5 ppm, fragment mass tolerance of < 10 ppm, and retention time tolerance of < 0.2 min.

Following compound identification, data were imported into the MetaX software for preprocessing, including normalization using Probabilistic Quotient Normalization (PQN) and batch effect correction using QC-RLSC. To assess data quality, the repeatability of quality control (QC) samples was examined by comparing the base peak chromatograms (BPC), principal component analysis (PCA), and coefficient of variation (CV) for each compound (supplementary Metabolomic_QC.zip).

Metabolites were identified using high-resolution MS/MS data and were annotated against the BMDB, mzCloud, and ChemSpider databases. Metabolite identification credibility was assessed according to the standards outlined by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI), with confidence levels ranging from Level 1 (high confidence, matching standards) to Level 5 (low confidence, no match).

Statistical analysis

For univariate analysis, differential metabolites were selected based on a combination of VIP > 1, fold change (FC) ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 0.83, and a q-value < 0.05. Differential metabolites were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps to display significant changes across experimental time points.

Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG database. Pathways with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The analysis identified key metabolic pathways associated with differential metabolites, which were visualized using bubble plots and pathway maps.

qRT-PCR and metabolites detected by colorimetry

For the validation experiments, cellular samples were collected at five time points following infection (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h). Each time point comprised three biological replicates, with each replicate subjected three replicate wells.

RNA extraction was performed using the TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen). The extracted RNA was then quantified using ND-100 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop). The total RNA(2.6 μg) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcriptase (R323–01, Vazyme). SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q312,Vazyme) and specific primers (Supplementary Table S1) were used for qRT-PCR on a fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument.

Infected cells were collected and assayed for pyruvate (A081–1–1, NJJCBIO) and ATP (A095–1–1, NJJCBIO)concentrations using kits.

Convergent Cross-Mapping (CCM) analysis for causality assessment

To investigate potential causal relationships between gene expression and metabolite concentrations, we employed Convergent Cross-Mapping (CCM) using the CAT Bridge tool, a powerful platform for analyzing causal dynamics in complex systems. CCM evaluates how well the historical data of one variable (e.g. gene expression) can predict the future state of another variable (e.g. metabolite concentration). In this study, the CAT Bridge tool was used to apply CCM, examining whether temporal changes in gene expression could accurately predict fluctuations in metabolite levels at multiple time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h post-infection). For each gene, a CCM score was calculated, representing the strength of the relationship between gene expression and metabolite concentration. A high CCM score indicates a strong causal influence of gene expression on metabolite levels at the corresponding time points. This analysis allowed for the identification of key genes whose expression patterns significantly impact metabolite profiles during S. Typhi infection, providing insights into the metabolic pathways and molecular mechanisms involved in the host’s immune response.

Results

Early transcriptional response to S. Typhi infection: immune activation and inflammatory pathways at 2 hours

To investigate the transcriptional dynamics of S. Typhi infection, differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was performed on THP-1 macrophages at 2 hours post-infection compared to uninfected controls (0 hours). The results are shown in Figure 1.

Differential gene expression and gene set enrichment analysis in THP1 macrophages following infection with S. Typhi enterica at different time points (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h post-infection). (A) volcano plots for each time point (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 24 h) comparing gene expression between infected and control conditions. The x-axis represents the log2 Fold change (log2FC), and the y-axis represents the -log10 of the adjusted p-value. Differentially expressed genes are highlighted in red for upregulated and blue for downregulated. (B) gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showing the biological processes enriched in the upregulated and downregulated genes. The most significant biological processes are displayed as bar plots, with red bars indicating upregulated processes and blue bars indicating downregulated processes. (C) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis displaying the most significantly enriched pathways for each time point. Red bars represent upregulated pathways, while blue bars represent downregulated pathways.

The analysis identified 166 upregulated and 43 downregulated genes, indicating a rapid cellular response to bacterial invasion. Upregulated genes were significantly enriched in immune response and inflammatory pathways, while downregulated genes reflected potential suppression of specific cellular processes.

Among the upregulated genes, IFNB1 (Interferon Beta 1) showed a strong increase in expression (log2 fold change ≈ 11.19), indicating robust activation of interferon-mediated immune responses. IFIT2 (Interferon Induced Transmembrane Protein 2) was also significantly upregulated, supporting activation of antiviral defense mechanisms. Other upregulated genes included NR4A1 and NR4A2, which are involved in immune cell transcriptional regulation and inflammatory responses.

Conversely, several genes were significantly downregulated, indicating potential pathogen-induced suppression of host pathways. RIMBP3 (RIM Binding Protein 3), associated with cellular membrane stability and synaptic function, exhibited downregulation, suggesting possible dysregulation of cell signaling during infection. GPR82 (G Protein-Coupled Receptor 82), a GPCR involved in cellular signaling, was also downregulated, which may reflect altered host responses to external stimuli. Similarly, ELF3 (E74 Like ETS Transcription Factor 3), a transcription factor involved in immune pathway regulation, was suppressed, potentially modulating the host immune response.

To further understand the biological impact of these gene expression changes, Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses were performed. Upregulated biological processes were predominantly associated with immune activation, including response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), cytokine production, and leukocyte chemotaxis. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis confirmed significant activation of cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, NF-κB signaling, and the S. Typhi infection pathway, highlighting early immune recognition and response to bacterial invasion.

These findings demonstrate that at 2 hours post-infection, THP-1 macrophages initiate a strong immune response characterized by interferon signaling, cytokine production, and inflammation. Simultaneously, the suppression of metabolic pathways suggests a pathogen-induced modulation of host cellular processes, possibly to favor bacterial survival.

Immune signaling intensification and metabolic adaptation at 4 hours post-infection

At 4 hours post-infection, transcriptomic changes became more pronounced, with 547 genes upregulated and 169 genes downregulated. The host immune response appeared more coordinated, with increased expression of immune-related genes such as IFNB1, CXCL9, IFNL1, and IFIT2, which are involved in cytokine production, chemotaxis, and antiviral defense.

GO analysis revealed an enrichment of processes related to immune response, including cytokine production, response to interleukin-1, and leukocyte chemotaxis, reflecting increased immune cell recruitment and inflammatory signaling. The KEGG pathway analysis showed significant activation of cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NF-κB signaling, TNF signaling, and NOD-like receptor pathways, further supporting a heightened inflammatory and immune response.

Interestingly, several genes involved in metabolism and immune regulation were downregulated at this time point, including P2RY11, IGFALS, and TCF15. This suggests a shift in cellular priorities, with host metabolism adapting to sustain the ongoing immune response. Pathway analysis also revealed downregulation of autophagy, a crucial process for cellular homeostasis and pathogen degradation. The suppression of this pathway suggests that S. Typhi may actively interfere with host defense mechanisms to evade immune responses and prolong its intracellular survival.

Overall, at 4 hours post-infection, the immune response is more established, with greater cytokine signaling, immune cell migration, and inflammatory responses. However, the concurrent downregulation of metabolic and autophagy pathways suggests that S. Typhi may be manipulating host cellular functions to maintain infection.

Enhanced cytokine production and immune cell migration at 8 hours post-infection

By 8 hours post-infection, the immune response intensified further, with 771 upregulated and 234 downregulated genes. The upregulation of CXCL9, CXCL10, CCR7, and IDO1 indicated a shift toward adaptive immune responses and immune regulation. CXCL9 and CXCL10 play key roles in immune cell recruitment, while CCR7 is essential for antigen-presenting cell migration and lymphoid tissue homing. IDO1, an immunoregulatory enzyme, suggests a more complex immune adaptation occurring at this stage.

GO enrichment analysis revealed continued activation of cytokine production, leukocyte adhesion, and response to bacterial components (LPS response). KEGG pathway analysis further confirmed the upregulation of cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, TNF signaling, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways, indicating a strong immune and inflammatory response.

Notably, autophagy and other metabolic pathways were significantly downregulated, suggesting that S. Typhi may actively suppress these host defense mechanisms to maintain infection. The downregulation of genes such as MPL and SP7, involved in cellular growth and differentiation, suggests that host cellular proliferation is suppressed in favor of immune activation.

Compared to 2-hour and 4-hour post-infection time points, the 8-hour response represents a more refined immune activation phase, with increased cytokine signaling and immune cell migration. However, the downregulation of autophagy indicates potential bacterial evasion mechanisms, enabling prolonged intracellular persistence.

Sustained immune activation and pathogen evasion mechanisms at 24 hours post-infection

At 24 hours post-infection, S. Typhi induced a sustained immune response, with 1370 genes upregulated and 728 genes downregulated. The upregulated genes included CXCL11, CCL8, CCR7, CXCL10, and IL6, suggesting continued recruitment of immune cells to the infection site. IDO1 remained highly expressed, further supporting immune regulation and potential pathogen persistence mechanisms.

GO analysis confirmed the enrichment of defense responses to viral-like infections, cytokine production, and leukocyte adhesion, reflecting ongoing immune activity. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the sustained activation of cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling, and NOD-like receptor pathways, demonstrating prolonged inflammatory signaling.

In contrast, several metabolic and cellular homeostasis pathways were significantly downregulated, including protein localization, RNA processing, and autophagy, suggesting that S. Typhi may continue to manipulate host cellular processes to evade immune clearance. Interestingly, the S. Typhi infection pathway itself was downregulated, possibly indicating an adaptive response by the host to counteract bacterial persistence or bacterial modulation of host gene expression.

Compared to earlier time points, the 24-hour response represents a late-phase immune response, with a combination of inflammation, immune regulation, and potential immune exhaustion. The continued downregulation of autophagy and host metabolic pathways suggests that S. Typhi is actively interfering with host defenses, prolonging its survival within macrophages.

Long-term immune surveillance and pathogen clearance (Clusters 4, 7, and 9) ensure continued host defense, while transient activation of Cluster 5 genes supports short-lived but necessary immune responses.

This analysis provides a comprehensive view of immune gene regulation over time, identifying potential therapeutic targets for modulating immune responses and enhancing host defense mechanisms against S. Typhi infection.

Metabolomics pathway enrichment reveals distinct metabolic reprogramming over time

To complement gene expression analyses, a metabolomics pathway enrichment study was conducted on THP-1 macrophages infected with S. Typhi at multiple time points (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h). Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using both positive (pos) and negative (neg) ion modes, identifying metabolic pathways significantly impacted by infection (Figure 4).

Pathway enrichment analysis of metabolomics data at different time points post- S. Typhi infection. The pathway enrichment analysis shows the significantly enriched metabolic pathways at 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h post-infection vs 0 h, based on metabolite data from positive (pos) and negative (neg) ion modes. Each bubble represents a metabolic pathway, with the size of the bubble indicating the number of metabolites associated with that pathway and the color gradient representing the p-value (from red: most significant, to blue: least significant).

At 2 hours post-infection, key enriched pathways in positive ion mode included oxytocin signaling, glutathione metabolism, and purine metabolism, suggesting early immune signaling and antioxidant defense activation. Enrichment of pyrimidine metabolism and proline metabolism indicates increased nucleotide synthesis and amino acid metabolism as part of the host’s rapid response. In negative ion mode, cyclic GMP-AMP signaling, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and glycolysis were significantly enriched, reflecting early energy metabolism shifts and sulfur amino acid pathways critical for immune cell activation. Enrichment of AMPK signaling and 2-deoxy-sugar acid metabolism further indicates a transition toward energy conservation mechanisms.

At 4 hours post-infection, metabolic adaptation continued. In positive ion mode, enrichment of vitamin digestion and absorption pathways suggested a response to oxidative stress, while glutathione metabolism remained active, reinforcing antioxidant defenses. Enrichment of tryptophan and tyrosine metabolism indicated immune signaling and inflammatory response modulation. In negative ion mode, insulin resistance pathways were enriched, indicating shifts in cellular metabolism in response to infection. Additional enrichment of cysteine, methionine, and pyruvate metabolism suggested sustained inflammatory and immune responses.

At 8 hours post-infection, metabolic reprogramming showed a transition toward cellular repair and immune regulation. Pyrimidine metabolism, oxyntic signaling, and neurotrophin signaling pathways were enriched in positive ion mode, highlighting potential shifts toward immune regulation and tissue remodeling. Leishmaniasis metabolism and glutathione metabolism remained active, supporting host defense mechanisms. In negative ion mode, pathways such as the TCA cycle and glycolysis were enriched, reflecting enhanced energy production for immune function and cellular repair. Enrichment of AMPK signaling and ascorbate metabolism suggested adaptations to oxidative stress and energy balance.

At 24 hours post-infection, the metabolic profile indicated a transition toward immune resolution and homeostasis. In positive ion mode, vitamin B6 metabolism, taste transduction, and glutathione metabolism were enriched, suggesting a gradual return to cellular homeostasis. The ferritin metabolism pathway was also enriched, indicating a role in oxidative stress regulation. In negative ion mode, enrichment of TCA cycle, insulin signaling, and AMPK signaling suggested ongoing metabolic reprogramming for tissue repair and immune function. Cysteine and methionine metabolism, along with fatty acid oxidation, were significantly enriched, suggesting an increased reliance on fatty acid catabolism for immune support.

In order to further validate the metabolomics results, we selected the following metabolite: glycolytic intermediate pyruvate and energy provider ATP, detected by colorimetric. The results were generally consistent with the metabolomics data trends (Supplementary Figure S1B). These findings illustrate a distinct temporal metabolic shift during infection, with early stages (2 h–4 h) marked by glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, and glutathione metabolism, followed by a transition to fatty acid oxidation, mitochondrial function, and immune regulation at later stages (8 h–24 h). These results emphasize the host’s adaptive metabolic response to infection and identify potential targets for therapeutic interventions to modulate host metabolism during bacterial infections.

Temporal metabolic reprogramming during S. Typhi infection

Heatmap of metabolomics data across time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h) provided further insights into temporal metabolic reprogramming in THP-1 macrophages infected with S. Typhi (Figure 5).

Heatmap of metabolite profiles across different time points post-S. Typhi infection. The heatmap shows the temporal changes in metabolite levels of THP-1 macrophages infected with S. Typhi enterica at 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h post-infection. Each row represents a different metabolite, and the color scale (from blue to red) reflects the intensity of metabolite concentration, where red indicates high concentrations and blue represents lower concentrations. The heatmap was generated using hierarchical clustering to group metabolites based on their temporal expression patterns. Notable patterns include a peak in metabolic activity at 2 h post-infection, followed by shifts in metabolic pathways toward lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function at later time points (8 h and 24 h).

At early infection (0 h–2 h), significant shifts were observed in metabolites involved in amino acid metabolism, purine metabolism, and glutathione metabolism. Notably, adenosine monophosphate, guanosine, and palmitoleic acid showed increased concentrations at 2 h, indicating immune activation and oxidative stress.

During mid-infection (2 h–8 h), the heatmap revealed a transition from glycolytic intermediates and amino acids (2 h–4 h) to lipid metabolism (8 h). Metabolites such as L-tryptophan and glutamine decreased, while pyruvic acid and cytidine increased, reflecting a shift in energy utilization strategies. Dynamic fluctuations in cyclic ADP-ribose and sphingosine suggested ongoing immune signaling and stress adaptation.

At late infection (8 h–24 h), recovery of metabolites involved in cellular repair and mitochondrial function was evident, including adenosine, folic acid, and arachidonic acid, suggesting a transition toward immune resolution and inflammation control. Some metabolites, such as spermine, maltooligosaccharides, and N-acetylglutamic acid, continued increasing over time, indicating persistent metabolic adaptation.

These results highlight dynamic metabolic regulation during infection, revealing key shifts from glycolysis (early infection) to oxidative phosphorylation and lipid metabolism (later infection). The heatmap findings support the pathway analysis results, reinforcing the critical role of metabolic adaptation in immune response and pathogen clearance.

Gene-metabolite interactions reveal coordinated immune and metabolic regulation during S. Typhi infection

Our CCM analysis revealed significant causal relationships between gene expression and metabolite concentrations across multiple time points post- S. Typhi infection (Table 1 presents a summary of the top 20 genes, while the complete gene list is provided in Table S4). Genes such as RGS4, KIF18A, and RSPO1, which exhibited strong CCM scores (close to 1.0), were identified as having a predictive influence on metabolite levels, suggesting that these genes may play a pivotal role in regulating metabolic processes during infection.

RGS4 (Regulator of G-protein signaling 4) plays a crucial role in immune cell signaling by modulating G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling pathways. RGS4 has been shown to regulate cytokine release and immune responses, particularly by controlling the activation of NF-kB signaling, which is essential for the inflammatory response during S. Typhi infection. The upregulation of RGS4 at 2 hours post-infection indicates its pivotal role in the initial immune activation phase, potentially by enhancing macrophage responsiveness to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). KIF18A (Kinesin family member 18A), involved in microtubule dynamics, has been implicated in various cellular processes, including cell division and trafficking. Our results suggest KIF18A’s involvement in regulating cytokine production and immune cell movement. The high expression of KIF18A in our dataset may indicate its role in facilitating macrophage polarization and migration, critical steps for effective immune response during the early phase of S. Typhi infection. RSPO1 (R-spondin 1), a member of the R-spondin family of proteins, is known for its role in activating the Wnt signaling pathway. RSPO1 plays an important role in promoting cell proliferation and differentiation, particularly in immune cells. It can enhance macrophage survival during infection and regulate cytokine production. The elevated levels of RSPO1 observed in our study, especially at later time points, highlight its potential role in modulating the immune response and maintaining tissue integrity during prolonged infection.

The integration of these key genes (RGS4, KIF18A, RSPO1) with the metabolic changes, particularly in glutathione metabolism and amino acid metabolism, reveals a complex and coordinated regulation of immune activation and metabolic reprogramming in response to S. Typhi infection. These genes not only contribute to immune signal transduction but also help reprogram macrophage metabolism, ensuring adequate energy supply and cellular protection during the immune response. Additionally, cluster-based analysis further supported these findings, as genes exhibiting high CCM scores were enriched in pathways related to immune system modulation and metabolic reprogramming. This suggests that these genes may not only contribute to the direct immune response but also influence broader metabolic shifts, potentially through modulation of nutrient sensing and energy metabolism pathways.

Discussion

This study unveils dynamic metabolic reprogramming in THP-1 macrophages during S. Typhi infection, with profound implications for host cell fate and pathogen persistence. During the early phase of infection (2–4 h), the glycolytic pathway was significantly upregulated, reflecting early energy metabolism changes. S. Typhi infects macrophages early, where type III secretion systems are expressed to resist host immune responses [30]. S. Typhimurium suppressed serine synthesis in the host via the type III secretion system (T3SS) effector, SopE2, promoting the accumulation of glycolytic intermediates, especially 3-phosphoglycerate (3PG), pyruvate, and lactate [31]. In addition, sseK3 enhanced the level of glycolysis in macrophages infected by S. Typhimurium [32]. Although this relationship has not been explored in the context of S. Typhi infection, the heightened expression of genes linked to glycolysis and lactate production could potentially contribute to similar processes involved in intracellular replication. Metabolic shifts from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation (FAO) at late infection stages (8–24 h) coincide with sustained immune activation, suggesting a critical role in macrophage survival [33]. Elevated FAO intermediates (e.g. palmitoleic acid, arachidonic acid) and mitochondrial metabolites (adenosine, folic acid) at 24 h post-infection indicate enhanced energy production via oxidative phosphorylation, which may support macrophage viability during prolonged inflammation [34,35]. This metabolic adaptation could represent a host strategy to balance immune effector function with cellular homeostasis, as mitochondrial dysfunction or excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production – common in glycolysis-dominant responses – often leads to macrophage apoptosis or necrosis [15,36].

Mitochondrial function emerges as a pivotal regulator of macrophage fate during S. Typhi infection. Transcriptomic analysis reveals upregulation of mitochondrial metabolism genes in Cluster 6 (e.g. CYB5D1) and Cluster 9 (e.g. MSMO1), while metabolomic profiling shows increased TCA cycle intermediates (citrate, succinate) at 8–24 h. These changes may maintain mitochondrial membrane potential and reduce ROS-mediated damage, promoting macrophage survival rather than pyroptosis or apoptosis [15,37–39]. Concurrently, glutathione metabolism, a key antioxidant pathway, is persistently activated throughout infection, further protecting mitochondria from oxidative stress. This mitochondrial – metabolic axis likely dictates whether macrophages undergo functional polarization (e.g. M1-like pro-inflammatory or M2-like reparative states) or commit to cell death, influencing the balance between pathogen clearance and tissue damage [22,40].

The interplay between metabolic reprogramming and host – pathogen interactions reveals adaptive strategies for both parties. Enhanced FAO in late infection may serve a dual role: while providing energy for macrophage survival, it could also facilitate S. Typhi persistence by limiting pro-inflammatory responses [41,42]. FAO is known to promote an anti-inflammatory phenotype in macrophages, potentially allowing the pathogen to evade immune surveillance [43]. This is supported by downregulation of pro-apoptotic genes (e.g. PRF1) and upregulation of immunoregulatory factors (e.g. IDO1) at 24 h, which together may create a tolerogenic microenvironment favoring bacterial survival.

Early induction of chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10 (2–8 h) drives immune cell recruitment, a critical step in orchestrating acute inflammation [44,45]. Their expression peaks coincide with the transition from glycolysis to FAO, suggesting a metabolic – cytokine crosstalk [43,46,47]. CXCL9/CXCL10 signaling via the CXCR3 receptor promotes T cell and natural killer cell migration, while also enhancing macrophage phagocytic activity [48–50]. However, prolonged chemokine expression (e.g. at 24 h) may contribute to chronic inflammation, potentially exacerbating tissue injury [51,52]. The causal link between RGS4 (a G-protein signaling regulator) and glutathione metabolism – identified via CCM analysis – highlights how immune signaling networks directly modulate metabolic pathways to shape inflammatory outcomes [53–55].

These findings carry significant immunoregulatory implications. The metabolic switch to FAO in late infection represents a potential checkpoint for balancing host defense and immunopathology [33,56]. Targeting FAO enzymes (e.g. CPT1A) or mitochondrial biogenesis pathways could either enhance macrophage effector function (to clear S. Typhi) or reduce excessive inflammation (to prevent tissue damage) [56–58]. Similarly, modulating CXCL9/CXCL10 signaling may optimize early immune recruitment while mitigating late-stage immunopathology [45,59,60].

Future studies should validate these mechanisms in macrophages and in vivo models of typhoid fever. Key directions include: 1) metabolic tracing of fatty acid oxidation flux during infection to define its contribution to ATP production and immune signaling; 2) investigation of how S. Typhi manipulates glycolysis (e.g. via type III secretion systems or type VI secretion systems) to subvert host defenses; and 3) preclinical testing of metabolic modulators (e.g. FAO inhibitors or activators) in combination with antibiotics to improve treatment outcomes. By dissecting the metabolic – immune interface in typhoid infection, this study paves the way for developing precision therapies that target both pathogen virulence and host metabolic checkpoints.
