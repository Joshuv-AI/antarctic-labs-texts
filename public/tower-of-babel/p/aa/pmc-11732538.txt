Advances in lysosomal escape mechanisms for gynecological cancer nano-therapeutics
Authors: Heng Wei, Yingying Hao, Jin Zhang, Yue Qi, Chong Feng, Chen Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGynecological cancers present significant treatment challenges due to drug resistance and adverse side effects. This review explores advancements in lysosomal escape mechanisms, essential for enhancing nano-therapeutic efficacy. Strategies such as pH-sensitive linkers and membrane fusion are examined, showcasing their potential to improve therapeutic outcomes in ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers. We delve into novel materials and strategies developed to bypass the lysosomal barrier, including pH-sensitive linkers, fusogenic lipids, and nanoparticles (NPs) engineered for endosomal disruption. Mechanisms such as the proton sponge effect, where NPs induce osmotic swelling and rupture of the lysosomal membrane, and membrane fusion, which facilitates the release of therapeutic agents directly into the cytoplasm, are explored in detail. These innovations not only promise to improve therapeutic outcomes but also minimize side effects, marking a significant step forward in the treatment of ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers. By providing a comprehensive analysis of current advancements and their implications for clinical applications, this review sheds light on the potential of lysosomal escape strategies to revolutionize gynecological cancer treatment, setting the stage for future research and development in this vital area.Keywords: Gynecological cancers, Nano-therapeutics, Lysosomal escape, Endosomal escape, pH-sensitive nanoparticles, Cancer nanotechnology, Drug delivery systems

Introduction

Gynecological cancers, including ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers, represent an important area of concern in the field of oncology due to their prevalence and mortality associated with late diagnosis [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite advances in screening and treatment modalities, these cancers still pose a huge challenge to global healthcare systems. Traditional treatment options, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation, have been the cornerstone of gynecological cancer treatment [4,5]. However, the effectiveness of these treatments is often limited by factors such as tumor heterogeneity, the development of drug resistance, and the inability to selectively target cancer cells without damaging healthy tissue [6,7]. These limitations highlight the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies that can provide specificity against tumors, overcome resistance mechanisms, and reduce adverse side effects. Gynecological cancers, including ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers, represent a significant challenge in oncology due to their complex biological mechanisms and high resistance to traditional treatments. These cancers often exhibit tumor heterogeneity, where different cells within the same tumor may respond differently to treatments, complicating effective therapy. Additionally, the development of drug resistance, particularly in ovarian cancer, limits the efficacy of chemotherapy. Cervical cancer, often linked to persistent human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, presents unique therapeutic challenges that necessitate innovative approaches.

The emergence of nano-therapies represents a paradigm shift in cancer treatment. Nanotechnology-based therapies utilize the unique properties of nanoparticles (NPs) to enhance drug delivery, improve the bioavailability of therapeutic drugs, and be able to target cancer cells [8,9]. These nanoscale carriers can be designed to preferentially accumulate in tumor tissue through enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects, a phenomenon in which a tumor's leaky vasculature allows for passive targeting of NPs [10,11]. Despite these advantages, the clinical success of nano-therapy is hampered by various biological barriers, the most important of which is the lysosomal degradation of therapeutic agents after cellular uptake [12,13].

Lysosomes are the digestive system of cells and play a vital role in degrading and recycling cellular waste, including foreign bodies such as NPs. Lysosomes are not only involved in the degradation and recycling of cellular waste but also play a crucial role in cellular trafficking. Their ability to penetrate cell membranes is facilitated by specialized transport proteins and membrane-associated enzymes that regulate the movement of materials in and out of the lysosome. This dynamic process ensures that lysosomes can interact effectively with other cellular compartments, aiding in the delivery and processing of therapeutic agents encapsulated within nanomaterials. After nano-therapeutic drugs are internalized in cells, they are often trapped in endosomal and lysosomal compartments, where acidic conditions and hydrolases degrade the therapeutic agent, reducing its efficacy [14,15]. The mechanism of lysosomal escape is critical for the efficacy of nano-therapeutics. One common strategy involves the use of the proton sponge effect, where NPs induce an influx of ions into the lysosome, causing it to swell and eventually rupture. Another approach uses pH-sensitive linkers that degrade under acidic conditions, releasing the therapeutic payload directly into the cytoplasm. Additionally, some nanocarriers are designed with fusion peptides that mimic viral entry mechanisms, promoting fusion with the lysosomal membrane and subsequent release of the drug into the cell's interior. Therefore, the ability of NPs to escape lysosomes is a key determinant of their therapeutic success. This concept, known as lysosome escape, is central to the design of effective nano-therapy [16]. Strategies that promote the release of NPs from lysosomes can prevent the degradation of encapsulated drugs, ensure their complete delivery to the cytoplasm, and enhance their therapeutic effects [17]. Maintaining lysosome stability is essential for the successful delivery of therapeutic agents. This involves regulating the pH and enzyme activity within the lysosome to prevent premature degradation of the nanocarriers. Techniques such as using stabilizing agents that reinforce the lysosomal membrane and employing pH-buffering materials can help maintain an optimal environment for lysosome function and stability. The synthesis of nanocarrier materials designed for lysosomal escape involves several innovative strategies. These include the use of polymers that are stable at physiological pH but degrade in acidic environments, such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) or chitosan (CS)-coated NPs. Additionally, incorporating fusogenic lipids into the NP design can enhance their ability to fuse with lysosomal membranes and release their payloads directly into the cytoplasm. Such materials are engineered to respond to the specific conditions within lysosomes, ensuring efficient drug delivery and minimizing degradation.

Basics of nano-therapeutics in cancer treatment

Nanotechnology-enhanced therapeutics in cancer treatment

Nanotechnology-based drugs represent a revolutionary advance in cancer treatment, offering a multifaceted approach to overcoming the limitations of traditional therapies [18,19]. These nanoscale therapeutics typically range in size from 1 to 100 nm and take advantage of unique physical, chemical, and biological properties not found in their bulk counterparts [20,21]. One of the most significant benefits of nanotechnology in cancer treatment is the potential for targeted drug delivery. This precise targeting minimizes the impact on healthy cells, thereby reducing the adverse side effects typically associated with conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy. In addition, nano-therapeutic drugs can improve the solubility and stability of insoluble drugs, thereby improving their bioavailability and efficacy [22,23]. This is critical for many effective anti-cancer drugs that are limited in clinical use due to poor solubility. Nanocarriers can also protect therapeutic drugs from premature degradation in the bloodstream, ensuring that higher concentrations of drugs reach the tumor site [24,25].

The design of nano-therapies allow for controlled drug release, which can be triggered by specific stimuli within the tumor microenvironment, such as pH changes or enzyme activity [26,27]. This controlled release mechanism ensures that the drug is released at the right time and place, maximizing its therapeutic effect while further reducing toxicity.

Mechanisms of action of nano-therapeutics: targeted delivery and controlled release

Nano-therapeutic drugs use a complex mechanism of action, mainly through targeted delivery and controlled release to distinguish from traditional therapeutic agents. These mechanisms are the basis for improving the efficacy and reducing toxicity of nanomedicine in cancer treatment. Targeted delivery is achieved by designing NPs that can specifically recognize and bind to cancer cells, thereby protecting healthy tissue (Fig. 1). Targeting early-stage ovarian cancer through nanotechnology and “theranostic” systems could significantly improve patient outcomes by integrating molecular targets for diagnosis, imaging, and therapy [28,29]. Current nanotechnology-based frameworks, like nano micelles and pnanoliposomes, offer promising strategies for precise tumor targeting, treatment, and overcoming drug resistance [30]. This specificity is typically mediated by the binding of targeted ligands to the surface of NPs, which can recognize and bind to biomarkers overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells [31,32]. For gynecological cancers, where certain receptors or proteins may be overexpressed, NPs can be engineered to target these specific markers, ensuring that therapeutic drugs are delivered directly to tumor cells. This targeted approach can minimize systemic side effects and increase the concentration of the drug at the tumor site, thereby enhancing the therapeutic effect.

Controlled release, another hallmark of nano-therapeutics, refers to the ability of NPs to release their therapeutic payload in a controlled manner over time or in response to specific physiological triggers within the tumor microenvironment, such as pH differences, temperature changes, or enzyme activity. The study introduces multifunctional FA-Bi2S3-perfluoropentane (PFP)-doxorubicin (Dox)-PLGA NPs referred as FBPD NPs, which combine therapy and diagnostic imaging for targeted ovarian cancer treatment. These NPs, synthesized using a double emulsification method, encapsulate Bi2S3, PFP, and Dox, and respond to near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation by increasing temperature, converting PFP from liquid to gas, and efficiently releasing Dox [33]. They enable precise computed tomography (CT) and photoacoustic imaging for therapy guidance and monitoring, demonstrating significant tumor accumulation and effective photothermal and chemotherapy in vitro and in vivo. This advancement represents a promising approach to precision therapy in ovarian cancer [34]. This property ensures that the drug is released when it is most needed and can work most effectively, further enhancing the effectiveness of treatment. The controlled release mechanism also helps to maintain therapeutic drug levels within the required range, reducing the frequency of dosing and improving patient compliance. Targeted delivery and controlled release mechanisms together underpin the effectiveness of nano-therapy, providing an improved cancer treatment with higher efficacy and lower toxicity than conventional chemotherapy. Critical role of NP design in overcoming cellular barriers

The design of NPs plays a key role in the ability to overcome cell barriers, a fundamental challenge in effectively delivering cancer treatments [38,39]. Cellular barriers, such as cell membranes, endosomal compartments, and lysosomal degradation pathways, can significantly impede the efficacy of drug delivery systems [40]. Thoughtful engineering of NP characteristics, including size, shape, surface charge, and hydrophobicity, is critical to facilitating their passage through these barriers to deliver therapeutic agents directly to sites of action within cancer cells. The size of the NPs was carefully optimized to enhance their cellular uptake and take advantage of the EPR effects in the tumor vasculature. This allows the NPs to preferentially accumulate in the tumor tissue. In addition, the NPs' surface charge and hydrophobicity are customized to improve their interactions with cell membranes, promote endocytosis, and reduce nonspecific binding to healthy cells [41,42]. Biomimetic magnetic NPs, Fe3O4-sulfasalazine (SAS)@platelet (PLT), offer a novel approach to enhance cancer immunotherapy by inducing mild immunogenicity through ferroptosis, effectively improving the response rate in non-inflamed tumors, repolarizing macrophages, and synergistically increasing the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapy, showing promising potential for clinical tumor metastasis treatment [43]. The EPR effect in tumor cells facilitates the accumulation of NPs in tumor tissues due to the leaky vasculature and poor lymphatic drainage characteristic of tumors. This phenomenon allows for enhanced targeting of therapeutic agents. Fe3O4-SAS@PLT, which stands for Fe3O4 NPs coated with a biomimetic PLT membrane, is designed to enhance immunogenicity and improve cancer immunotherapy. These NPs leverage the body's natural mechanisms to evade immune detection and deliver therapeutic agents directly to cancer cells.

Surface modification of NPs with specific ligands or antibodies can further refine their targeting capabilities, ensuring that they bind to specific receptors that are overexpressed on cancer cells. This specificity not only enhances the therapeutic effect, but also reduces the off-target effect. In addition, NPs designed for lysosomal escape ensure that therapeutic agents are released within the cytoplasm rather than being degraded, either by incorporating materials that can destroy lysosomal membranes or by designing pH-sensitive coatings that trigger drug release in lysosomal acidic environments. Therefore, the complex design of NPs is essential to overcome a complex set of cellular barriers faced during drug delivery.

The importance of lysosome escape in the treatment of gynecological cancer cannot be overemphasized. Given the complexity of these cancers and the challenges associated with existing treatments, nano-therapies that effectively bypass the lysosomal barrier have the potential to significantly improve patient outcomes. By delivering therapeutic drugs directly into the cytoplasm, these advanced nano systems can increase the concentration of active drugs at the site of action, reduce systemic toxicity, and mitigate the development of drug resistance. In addition, the integration of targeted ligands into NP designs can enhance the specificity of drug delivery, minimizing the impact on healthy cells and tissues.

In conclusion, the introduction of nanotechnology into the field of gynecological cancer treatment offers a promising avenue to address the limitations of traditional therapies. The development of nano-therapies capable of lysosomal escape represents a cutting-edge approach that could revolutionize the treatment landscape for ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers.

Lysosomal barrier to nano-therapeutics

Lysosomal functions and their role in degrading therapeutics

Lysosomes are crucial organelles within cells, often referred to as the “waste disposal system” of cells. They play key roles in degrading macromolecules, recycling cellular components, and breaking down foreign substances internalized by cells (Fig. 2). The acidic environment in the lysosome (pH maintained around 4.5 to 5.0) and many hydrolases capable of degrading proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids (NAs) facilitate this function. When therapeutic agents, including nano-therapeutic drugs, are internalized by cells, they are usually encapsulated in endosomes that subsequently fuse with lysosomes. In the harsh acidic environment of the lysosome, the therapeutic agent may be degraded before it has a chance to exert its intended effect. This degradation process poses a significant obstacle to the effectiveness of many drugs, particularly those designed to function within the cytoplasm or modulate intracellular targets.

Active targeting of nanoparticles (NPs) to cancer cells. Active targeting of NPs involves engineering them to specifically recognize and bind to cancer cells, enhancing treatment efficacy. By attaching ligands or antibodies to the NP surface, these targeted systems can home in on specific markers overexpressed on cancer cells, ensuring precise drug delivery and minimizing impact on healthy tissues.

The role of lysosomes in degrading foreign substances, including therapeutic drugs, underscores the need to design drug delivery systems that can evade lysosomal capture and degradation. Therefore, lysosome escape strategies are a key aspect of developing effective nano-therapeutics. By preventing the degradation of therapeutic drugs in the lysosome, these strategies ensure that the drugs reach their intended intracellular targets, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy and improving outcomes for diseases, including gynecological cancers.

The lysosomal barrier challenge to nano-therapeutic efficacy and strategies for escape

The lysosomal barrier significantly challenges the efficacy of nano-therapy in treating gynecological cancers and other diseases. This barrier contains various cellular mechanisms designed to protect cells from foreign substances, including therapeutic agents. Once nano-therapeutic drugs are internalized by cells, they are typically sequestered into endosomal compartments that mature into lysosomes, where encapsulated drugs can be degraded by acid hydrolases. This process not only reduces the concentration of the therapeutic agent available to exert its intended effect but also changes the drug's structure, rendering it inactive.

To address these challenges, researchers have explored various strategies for nano-therapeutic drugs [44,45]. The aim is to prevent the nanocarriers from being trafficked into lysosomes or to facilitate their escape once internalized. One approach involves designing NPs with a pH-sensitive coating that is unstable under the acidic conditions of the lysosome, resulting in the release of the therapeutic agent directly into the cytoplasm. Another strategy uses materials that can destroy the lysosomal membrane, promoting the release of the NP's contents into the cytoplasm before degradation occurs.

The use of fusion lipids is another innovative approach to lysosome escape. These lipids undergo conformational changes in the acidic environment of the lysosome, causing the lipid bilayer of the NP to fuse with the lysosomal membrane, thereby releasing the encapsulated drug. Similarly, the incorporation of peptides that can destroy endosomal membranes was explored using the concept of the “proton sponge effect”. This involves designing NPs that can cause ions to flow into the endosome, leading to osmotic expansion and rupture of the endosome membrane, thus facilitating escape into the cytoplasm. Polymer NPs that degrade in response to acidic lysosomal environments to release their payloads have also been developed. These materials are designed to be stable at physiological pH but will degrade or change conformation in response to the lower pH in the lysosome, triggering the release of therapeutic agents.

Advances in lysosomal escape mechanisms

Recent advancements in lysosomal escape techniques for nano-therapeutics

To improve the efficacy of nano-therapy, significant progress has been made in developing lysosome escape techniques. These advances aim to address the key challenge of lysosomal degradation, a major obstacle limiting the therapeutic potential of nanomedicine, particularly in the treatment of gynecological cancers (Table 1) [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53]]. The latest innovations in this field not only expand the scope of nano-therapeutics, but also provide new pathways for their successful intracellular delivery [46]. The study introduces a shell-stacked NP (SNP) capable of remarkable size reduction and surface charge reversal in acidic tumor environments, enhancing tumor penetration and cellular uptake. This dual-transformable nanomedicine design, featuring a disulfide-cross-linked core for stable drug encapsulation and controlled release, significantly improves the depth of tumor penetration and exhibits potent antitumor efficacy in lung carcinoma models, demonstrating the potential for improved cancer treatment outcomes [46,47].

One of the most promising advances involves the design of pH-sensitive NPs. Inspired by electronic transistors, researchers have developed “proton transistors” using ultra-pH-sensitive (UPS) NPs to digitize biological pH signals, enabling precise biosensing and diagnostic imaging. These NPs exhibit a binary response to pH changes, transitioning from non-fluorescent aggregated micelles to brightly fluorescing individual unimers across narrow pH ranges. A broad library of these NPs covers the physiological pH spectrum, allowing for high-resolution pH mapping in live cells and identifying cellular processes like organelle acidification associated with cancer. The technology's clinical potential is highlighted by its successful use in image-guided surgery for solid tumors, showing significant improvements in patient outcomes and demonstrating its promise for future medical applications [48,49]. They are designed to remain stable in the blood, but undergo structural changes or break down when they encounter the acidic environment of lysosomes [48,50]. This responsiveness helps to release the therapeutic payload directly into the cytoplasm, bypassing the degradation of lysosomal enzymes [51]. The use of a polymer that undergoes the protonation process under acidic conditions exemplifies this approach, as it causes the NPs to swell and destroy the lysosomal membrane, enabling escape.

Another innovative technique focuses on incorporating fusion peptides into NP designs [52]. These peptides promote the fusion of the NP containing vesicles with the lysosomal membrane, resulting in the release of the active ingredient of the nano-therapeutic drug directly into the cytoplasm of the cell. This approach mimics the viral escape mechanism and provides a biomimetic way to overcome the cellular barrier. In addition, considerable progress has been made in the development of NPs capable of inducing the proton sponge effect. When the material used in this method degrades or expands in the lysosomal body, it causes the inflow of ions and water, resulting in the osmotic expansion and rupture of the lysosomal membrane. This not only helps release the payload of the NPs, but also minimizes the possibility of enzymatic degradation.

These advances represent a leap forward in nano-therapeutic design, providing new strategies for overcoming lysosomal barriers (Fig. 3). By enhancing lysosomal escape, these technologies improve the delivery and efficacy of nanomedicine, holding great promise for treating gynecological cancers and other challenging diseases.

Innovations in materials and methods for lysosomal escape

The development of materials and methods that facilitate lysosome escape is a rapidly growing area in the field of nano-therapeutics, driven by the need to improve the delivery and efficacy of treatments for diseases such as gynecological cancer. Researchers aim to create innovative solutions that enable therapeutic agents to bypass or disrupt the lysosomal pathway, thereby enhancing their intracellular delivery and therapeutic potential. Among these advances, pH-sensitive connectors, fusion lipids, and NPs specifically designed for endosomal destruction stand out for their effectiveness and innovative approaches.

pH-sensitive linkers have become a key tool in nano-therapeutic drug design. These joints are stable at the neutral pH of the blood flow but are cleaved in the acidic environment of the lysosome. This specificity allows the therapeutic payload to be released in a targeted manner within the cell, minimizing lysosomal enzyme degradation. The use of pH-sensitive linkers in drug conjugates and NP coatings represents a sophisticated strategy for precisely controlling drug release when and where it is needed. CS-coated Fe3O4 NPs (CS-Fe3O4) demonstrate enhanced water dispersion and cytocompatibility, showing no adverse effects on the viability of gynecological cells in vitro. Their improved performance in acidic environments suggests promising potential for safe and effective drug delivery applications in gynecological medicine [53,54]. The folic acid (FA)-FCP nanoplatform, featuring pH-sensitive ferritin nanocages loaded with curcumin and perfluorohexane, and coated with FA, offers a promising theranostic solution for cancer treatment. Demonstrating high biocompatibility, stability, and targeted drug release in acidic conditions enhanced by low-intensity focused ultrasound, FA-FCP enables efficient cell uptake and lysosomal relocation, providing significant tumor treatment effects and contrast-enhanced ultrasound imaging for non-invasive cancer theranostics [[55], [56], [57]].

Fusion lipids are another breakthrough development. These lipids are incorporated into the NP membrane and promote the fusion of the NP with the lysosome membrane during acidification. This fusion process allows the contents of the NPs to be released directly into the cytoplasm, effectively bypassing lysosomal degradation. The mechanism, inspired by the viral entry process, provides a biomimetic strategy for enhancing nano-therapeutic delivery [58]. This study explored the cellular interactions of lyotropic liquid crystalline NPs (LLCNPs) with different internal structures, finding that non-lamellar LLCNPs, particularly cubosomes, had significantly higher cellular uptake compared to liposomes [59]. Despite similar physicochemical properties, cubosomes, hexosomes, and micellar cubosomes showed comparable levels of uptake, suggesting an energy-independent mechanism of cellular entry. This indicates the potential of specifically structured LLCNPs, like cubosomes, for efficient drug delivery by possibly bypassing endosomal escape challenges [60].

In addition, NPs specifically designed to destroy endosomes have shown great promise. These NPs employ various mechanisms, such as the proton sponge effect, to induce osmotic expansion and rupture of endosomal membranes, thereby facilitating the release of their payloads into the cytoplasm [61]. This study demonstrates that cetrimonium bromide (CTAB)-coated gold nanorods, through the lysosomal proton sponge effect, can cause lysosomal swelling and rupture, leading to the release of Cl− ions into the cytoplasm. This event triggers transient receptor potential channels melastatin 2 (TRPM2) channels, inducing a significant influx of Ca2+ ions and enhancing cancer cell apoptosis. This finding elucidates the connection between the proton sponge hypothesis and calcium ion dynamics, contributing to a better understanding of NP-induced cytotoxicity in cancer cells [62,63]. Materials that can buffer or change conformation in response to lysosomal acidic environments are particularly effective in this regard, as they can facilitate endosome escape without relying on traditional fusion or infiltration mechanisms.

Case studies on successful lysosomal escape in gynecological cancer nano-therapeutics

Recent research in the field of nanomedicine provides compelling case studies highlighting the successful application of lysosome escape mechanisms in the treatment of gynecological cancers. These studies demonstrate the potential of innovative nano-therapeutic strategies to enhance drug delivery and efficacy, overcoming one of the major barriers to effective cancer treatment. pH-sensitive NPs are engineered to respond to the acidic environment of lysosomes, enabling them to release their therapeutic payload effectively within the targeted cancer cells. These NPs can swell or degrade under acidic conditions, leading to the rupture of lysosomal membranes and the release of therapeutic agents directly into the cytoplasm. This mechanism ensures that the drugs exert their intended effects within the cancer cells, thereby enhancing the overall efficacy of the treatment.

One well-known case study involved the targeted delivery of chemotherapy drugs to ovarian cancer cells using pH-sensitive NPs [64]. The study demonstrated that pH-sensitive poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO)-modified poly(beta-amino ester) (PbAE) NPs encapsulating paclitaxel significantly inhibited tumor growth in mice with human ovarian adenocarcinoma xenografts, offering a safer and more effective alternative to traditional aqueous solution formulations [64,65]. These findings highlight the potential of using pH-sensitive NP systems for improved solid tumor therapy with reduced systemic toxicity. The researchers developed NPs that are stable in the blood but sensitive to the acidic environment of cancer cell lysosomes. Triggered by acidic pH, these NPs undergo structural changes that promote the escape of chemotherapy drugs from the lysosome into the cytoplasm, significantly enhancing their cytotoxic effects on cancer cells. This approach not only improves the efficacy of chemotherapy drugs, but also reduces their toxicity to healthy cells, demonstrating the potential of pH-sensitive materials in lysosome escape strategies. A co-delivery system using hollow mesoporous silica NPs was developed to enhance the anti-tumor effect of chemotherapy drugs Dox and NVP-AEW 541 (NVP) on CD117+CD44+A2780 ovarian cancer stem-like cells [66,67]. This system, targeted at insulin growth factor receptor 1 (IGF-1R) showed significantly higher apoptosis rates and altered expression of key apoptotic markers compared to treatments with free drugs, indicating improved efficacy in inducing cancer cell apoptosis through targeted delivery [68].

Another breakthrough study focuses on the development of fused lipid NPs for the treatment of cervical cancer. These NPs are designed to fuse with lysosomal membranes during acidification, releasing their payloads directly into the cytoplasm of cancer cells [69]. The method shows a significant increase in therapeutic effectiveness because it bypasses the lysosomal degradation process, thus allowing the therapeutic agent to reach a higher concentration within the cell [70]. Silica-coated gold NPs coupled with scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI) antibodies (Au@SiO2-SR-BI) were synthesized for targeted cervical cancer therapy and imaging. These NPs, uniform in size and efficiently binding to SR-BI receptors on cancer cells, demonstrated enhanced cell targeting and induced significant apoptosis in treated cells. Photothermal therapy using these particles effectively ablated solid tumors in vivo, with observed changes in apoptosis-related protein expressions. This approach highlights the potential of antibody-conjugated Au@SiO2 NPs for targeted cervical cancer treatment and diagnostic imaging [71].

These case studies highlight the importance of lysosome escape in enhancing the delivery and efficacy of nano-therapeutics for gynecological cancer. By utilizing innovative materials and methods such as pH-sensitive NPs and fusion lipids, researchers have made significant progress in overcoming the challenges posed by cell barriers.

NP-mediated targeted drug delivery offers a promising strategy for eradicating cancer stem cells (CSCs) (Fig. 4), the elusive cells that drive tumor initiation, metastasis, and recurrence. By specifically targeting unique markers on CSCs, NPs can deliver therapeutic agents directly to these critical cells. This precision targeting could significantly improve the effectiveness of gynecological cancer treatments by addressing the root cause of the disease and preventing its progression and recurrence. CSCs play a crucial role in cancer progression and resistance to therapy, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy. CSCs are known to have enhanced lysosomal activity, which can contribute to the sequestration and degradation of therapeutic agents. Therefore, targeting lysosomal escape mechanisms in CSCs is essential for improving the efficacy of nano-therapeutics. By facilitating the escape of therapeutic agents from lysosomes within CSCs, we can ensure that these agents reach their intracellular targets and exert their intended effects, thereby reducing the likelihood of recurrence and metastasis. This makes lysosomal escape strategies particularly relevant in the context of CSC-targeted therapies for gynecological cancers.

Application to gynecological cancers

Implications of lysosomal escape advancements for treating gynecological cancers

Advances in nano-therapeutic lysosome escape mechanisms have far-reaching implications for the treatment of gynecological cancers, including ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers. These cancers are often characterized by late diagnosis and resistance to conventional treatments, posing major challenges for oncology. Innovative strategies developed for lysosome escape could revolutionize the treatment of these cancers by enhancing the efficacy and specificity of nano-therapeutic delivery.

For ovarian cancer, which is notorious for its poor prognosis and high recurrence rate, the ability of nano-therapeutic drugs to bypass lysosomal degradation means that higher concentrations of therapeutic drugs can be delivered directly to cancer cells [72,73]. By effectively targeting and killing cancer cells while minimizing damage to healthy tissue, this can improve outcomes, especially in advanced disease [74,75]. The study developed a hybrid biomimetic coating combining murine ovarian cancer cell membrane and red blood cell membrane on indocyanine green-loaded magnetic NPs for targeted ovarian cancer therapy, exhibiting specific tumor targeting, prolonged circulation, and enhanced immune response, demonstrating a promising synergistic photothermal and immunotherapy approach [8,76].

For cervical cancer with significant viral etiology, lysosome escape technology could facilitate the implementation of gene therapy or RNA-based therapies aimed at silencing or suppressing viral oncogenes [77,78]. By ensuring that these therapeutic agents reach intracellular targets without degradation, therapeutic effectiveness against HPV-induced malignancies can be significantly improved [79]. Dihydroartemisinin (DHA)-loaded poly(ethylene glycol)methyl (MPEG)-ether-poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) micelle NPs were developed to overcome DHA's solubility and bioavailability challenges, showing enhanced therapeutic efficacy and lower toxicity in cancer treatment. These NPs, which release DHA in a sustained manner, demonstrated significant anti-cancer effects both in vitro and in vivo, including cell cycle arrest, apoptosis induction, tumor growth inhibition, and prolonged survival in tumor-bearing mice, marking them as a promising system for targeted cancer therapy [80,81].

Uterine cancer, the most common type of which is endometrial cancer, could also benefit from these advances in nanotechnology. The targeted delivery and controlled release capabilities of nano-therapeutic drugs could improve the treatment of this cancer by precisely targeting tumor cells with chemotherapy drugs or novel therapies, thereby reducing the side effects associated with systemic therapy [79,82]. The study explores the use of peptide-based NPs modified with iRGD for efficient DNA delivery and suicide gene therapy in treating uterine leiomyoma and pancreatic cancer cells. These NPs demonstrated enhanced cell uptake, gene transfection efficiency, and significant therapeutic potential in uterine leiomyoma cells, highlighting their promise as a novel, fertility-preserving treatment option for this common benign reproductive system tumor [83].

Overall, the development of the nano-therapeutic lysosome escape mechanism represents a critical step in the treatment of gynecological cancer. By overcoming one of the key barriers to effective drug delivery, these advances hold the promise of more effective, targeted, and less toxic treatments for ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers, potentially improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

Enhanced lysosomal escape: impact on therapeutic efficacy, specificity, and side effects in gynecological cancer treatments

Enhancing the progress of nano-therapeutic lysosome escape has a key impact on improving the efficacy, specificity, and reducing side effects of gynecological cancer treatment. This strategic breakthrough addresses one of the most significant challenges in nanomedicine by ensuring that therapeutic drugs can reach intracellular targets without being degraded by the lysosomal pathway. The impact of these advances is profound and has the potential to significantly improve outcomes for patients with ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers.

Enhanced lysosomal escape ensures that a higher concentration of the drug reaches the cytoplasm, thereby enhancing its anti-tumor activity and thus directly contributing to improved therapeutic effectiveness. This is particularly important for gynecological cancers, where the need for targeted and effective treatment is high due to the aggressive nature and late diagnosis of these diseases [84]. By bypassing the lysosomal degradation environment, nano-therapy can more fully exert its intended effects, thereby improving tumor regression and patient survival. The study introduced multifunctional PLGA NPs modified with cyclic Arg-Gly-Asp (cRGD) (NPs-cRGD) for dual-mode imaging and overcoming cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer. These NPs ensured controlled drug and gene release, enhanced ultrasound and MRI visibility, and facilitated efficient cellular uptake and lysosomal escape. NPs-cRGD effectively combated cisplatin resistance through multiple mechanisms, including inhibiting antiapoptotic effects, depleting glutathione to increase cisplatin accumulation, and inducing cell apoptosis via upregulation of p53 and increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. In vivo, NPs-cRGD combined with ultrasound showed promising tumor targeting, effective tumor growth inhibition, and low systemic toxicity, offering a novel strategy for monitoring and overcoming drug resistance in ovarian cancer therapy [85].

In addition, the specificity of nano-therapeutic drugs is significantly improved by the lysosome escape mechanism. Targeted delivery systems, such as those utilizing pH-sensitive coatings or ligands that bind to specific tumor markers, ensure that the release of therapeutic drugs is highly targeted to cancer cells. This specificity minimizes the impact on healthy cells, thereby reducing collateral damage from common side effects of conventional cancer treatments. A novel photoactivatable polymeric NP system (CNPPtCP/Si (c-fos)) was developed for the combined delivery of chemotherapy and gene therapy, specifically targeting platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. Upon blue-light irradiation, this system enables efficient endosomal/lysosomal escape and controlled release of a Pt(IV) prodrug and small interfering RNA (siRNA) (c-fos), leading to synergistic therapeutic effects of photoactivated chemotherapy (PACT) and RNA interference (RNAi) with low toxicity. This approach demonstrates a promising strategy for enhancing treatment efficacy against challenging cancers by leveraging light-controlled drug release and gene unpacking [86,87].

Reducing side effects is another important benefit of enhanced lysosomal escape. By preventing the degradation of therapeutic drugs in the lysosome, these strategies reduce the need for high systemic dose chemotherapy, which is often associated with severe side effects, such as myelosuppression, gastrointestinal toxicity, and neurotoxicity. The ability to deliver drugs directly to cancer cells means that lower total doses can be used, resulting in less systemic toxicity and improved quality of life for patients receiving treatment. This study expanded the potential of small NA therapeutics by demonstrating that cationic amphiphilic drugs (CADs) can significantly enhance the delivery and functional efficacy of siRNA and antisense oligonucleotides from endolysosomal compartments into the cytosol. A drug repurposing screen identified 56 CAD adjuvants that facilitate this process, particularly with NPs designed to release decomplexed siRNA within the endolysosomal compartment. The efficacy of this approach was confirmed in human ovarian cancer cells, providing crucial insights into the use of CAD adjuvants for improving NA therapeutic delivery and action [88].

Ongoing clinical trials and preclinical studies are actively exploring these concepts, and several promising pathways are being investigated. For example, trials using NPs specifically designed for lysosomal escape in ovarian cancer treatment are evaluating their ability to enhance the delivery of chemotherapy drugs and siRNA molecules. Similarly, preclinical studies for cervical and uterine cancer are evaluating the efficacy of targeted nanocarriers that can bypass the cell barrier, aiming to establish new standards of treatment specificity and tolerability.

Challenges and future directions in lysosomal escape for gynecological cancer treatment

While advances in lysosomal escape mechanisms have opened new horizons for the treatment of gynecological cancers, several challenges must be addressed to realize the full potential of these innovative strategies. These challenges include ensuring the safety and specificity of nano-therapies, achieving production scalability, and overcoming potential resistance mechanisms specific to lysosomal escape.

One of the major issues in developing nano-therapies with lysosomal escape capabilities is ensuring their safety. These strategies can improve treatment effectiveness, but they must also ensure minimal toxicity and side effects. The design of NPs requires careful consideration of their composition, size, surface charge, and degradation products to avoid adverse immune reactions or toxicity to healthy tissues. It is critical to specifically target cancer cells without affecting normal cells, which requires the development of highly sophisticated targeting mechanisms capable of distinguishing between cancer cells and healthy cells based on molecular markers or tumor-specific microenvironmental features.

Scaling up the production of nano-therapeutic drugs with enhanced lysosomal escape features poses another challenge. The complex design and precision manufacturing processes required for these NPs can hinder mass production and distribution, affecting their availability and cost-effectiveness. Furthermore, cancer cells may develop resistance mechanisms against lysosomal escape strategies. Just as tumors can develop resistance to chemotherapy drugs, they also have the potential to adapt to evade the effects of nano-therapeutic drugs designed to bypass lysosomal degradation.

Addressing these challenges will require joint efforts in several future research directions. The design of the next generation of nano-therapeutic drugs should focus on biocompatible and biodegradable materials to minimize toxicity while maximizing therapeutic effectiveness. Innovations in targeting mechanisms, such as the use of biomimetic approaches or the utilization of tumor-specific markers, can enhance the specificity of drug delivery. The exploration of alternative lysosomal escape mechanisms, such as light-activated or magnetic field-responsive NPs, offers another promising avenue for research. These can provide a more controlled and precise release of the drug, further reducing side effects.

In addition, understanding the tumor microenvironment and its interactions with nano-therapies may lead to more efficient designs that take advantage of the unique characteristics of cancer cells. This includes studies of tumor vascular, immune, and stromal components, which may reveal new targets for lysosomal escape strategies. Finally, addressing scalability and manufacturing challenges will require innovation in NP synthesis and assembly, possibly through automation and the use of novel materials to streamline the production process without compromising the function of nano-therapeutic drugs.

Conclusion

The introduction of nanotechnology into gynecological cancer treatment represents a significant leap forward. By overcoming lysosomal degradation, these advanced nano-therapeutics promise to deliver more effective, targeted, and less toxic treatments. Continued research and clinical integration of these strategies hold the potential to revolutionize cancer therapy, offering new hope for improved patient outcomes. The exploration of lysosomal escape mechanisms in the context of nano-therapy represents a significant advancement in the fight against gynecological cancers. This innovative approach addresses one of the biggest obstacles to effective cancer treatment: the degradation of therapeutic agents within the lysosomal environment. By developing strategies that enable therapeutic NPs to bypass or evade lysosomal degradation, researchers have opened new ways to deliver drugs to cancer cells more efficiently and specifically. These advances promise to significantly improve treatment outcomes for ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers, which continue to pose significant challenges in terms of diagnosis, treatment resistance, and patient outcomes. The importance of lysosomal escape mechanisms extends beyond improving the efficiency of drug delivery. It embodies a broader shift in oncology toward precision medicine, where treatments are tailored to the unique characteristics of tumors and their microenvironments. By ensuring that nano-therapeutic drugs can reach their targets within cancer cells without being degraded, these strategies enhance the specificity of drug action, thereby minimizing collateral damage to healthy cells and tissues. The potential for these advances to transform the cancer treatment landscape is profound. Lysosomal escape mechanisms represent a key innovation in the development of the next generation of nano-therapies, offering new hope for overcoming the limitations of existing therapies. These strategies lay the foundation for designing efficient, targeted treatments that can significantly improve patient outcomes for gynecological cancers and potentially other cancers as well. The ability to bypass cellular barriers and deliver therapeutic agents directly to sites of action within cancer cells could revolutionize our approach to cancer treatment, making treatments more effective, less toxic, and more tailored to the needs of individual patients. In conclusion, advances in the nano-therapeutic lysosomal escape mechanism highlight a critical moment for cancer research. Integrating these strategies into clinical practice will require a multidisciplinary approach, leveraging insights from nanotechnology, molecular biology, and clinical oncology. The development of lysosomal escape mechanisms in nano-therapy provides a promising avenue for enhancing the treatment of gynecological cancers. By overcoming one of the key barriers to effective drug delivery, these advances pave the way for more effective, less toxic, and highly specific cancer treatments. As research in this area continues to evolve, the potential to transform the cancer treatment landscape is enormous, offering new hope to patients and heralding a new era in the fight against cancer. Future research should focus on further refining lysosomal escape strategies and exploring their clinical applications. Advances in personalized medicine and targeted therapies hold promise for more effective treatments with reduced side effects. By integrating these strategies into clinical practice, we can hope to see significant improvements in patient outcomes for ovarian, cervical, and uterine cancers.
