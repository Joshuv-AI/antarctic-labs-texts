Educational support programs for individuals with end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis: a scoping review
Authors: Priscille Musabirema, Johanna Elizabeth Maree, Amme Mardulate Tshabalala, Maryellen S. Kelly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global prevalence of End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) is increasing, with estimates suggesting that more than 5 million people worldwide require renal replacement therapy (RRT), and this burden falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), highlighting the urgent need for effective strategies to support patient self-management. This scoping review synthesizes evidence on the types, delivery methods, and reported outcomes of educational support programs for individuals with ESRD undergoing hemodialysis (HD).MethodsThis review followed the six-stage methodological framework by Arksey and O’Malley and adhered to the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines. Studies examining educational interventions for ESRD patients on HD were included. A comprehensive search of MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science was conducted for English-language articles published between 2011 and 2021. Search terms included combinations of “end-stage renal disease,” “ESRD,” “hemodialysis,” and “haemodialysis” with “educational program,” “support program,” “patient education,” and related terms. Pairs of reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and assessed full-text articles, resolving discrepancies through group discussion. Data were extracted using a standardized form capturing key study characteristics and synthesized using descriptive statistics and content analysis.ResultsEighteen studies (n = 18) from ten countries met the inclusion criteria. All studies reported that educational interventions, regardless of delivery mode or design, positively influenced quality of life (QOL) and treatment adherence among ESRD patients. Specifically, improvements were documented in serum phosphate control, intradialytic weight gain, psychological well-being (anxiety, depression, stress), self-esteem, dietary knowledge, and physical functioning. Structured self-management programs, particularly those incorporating nutritional education and psychological support, demonstrated the greatest breadth of benefit across clinical and psychosocial domains.ConclusionThis review maps the evidence on educational support programs for HD patients, revealing variability in intervention types, delivery methods, study designs, and outcome measures. These inconsistencies highlight the need for more rigorous, standardized research particularly in LMICs to determine the most effective educational strategies. The findings provide a foundation for developing evidence-based, patient-centered educational programs adaptable to diverse global populations.

Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) is a progressive condition in which the kidneys can no longer maintain normal physiological functions, necessitating renal replacement therapy (RRT) such as hemodialysis (HD) or renal transplantation.

Globally, ESRD represents a significant and growing health burden. Current estimates indicate that over 5 million people require RRT worldwide, with incidence rates rising by approximately 4–7% annually in many regions [1]. The condition contributes substantially to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs, accounting for a disproportionate share of chronic disease expenditure globally. The global prevalence of ESRD is rising primarily due to increasing rates of non-communicable diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, as well as aging populations [2]. This rising trend is most pronounced in LMICs, where limited access to RRT and healthcare resources exacerbate the challenges faced by individuals with ESRD [3].

HD remains the most common form of RRT for ESRD, particularly in LMICs where renal transplantation is less accessible due to organ donor shortages, high costs, and limited healthcare infrastructure [4]. However, adherence to the strict regimen required for HD including regular dialysis sessions, fluid and dietary restrictions, medication adherence, and lifestyle changes is often suboptimal [5, 6]. Non-adherence is associated with poorer health outcomes, including increased hospitalization rates, cardiovascular events, metabolic disturbances, and premature death.

Educational support is widely recognized as a cornerstone of effective patient care for individuals with chronic conditions such as ESRD. Providing individuals with the knowledge and skills needed to manage their condition and make informed decisions about their health can improve both adherence and QOL [7]. Research has shown that educational interventions can improve treatment adherence, reduce complications, and improve QOL for ESRD patients undergoing HD [8, 9]. Patient education can address barriers to adherence by enhancing understanding of the condition, the benefits of treatment, and the consequences of non-compliance [10].

Despite the growing recognition of the importance of education in managing ESRD, there is limited understanding of the most effective types of educational support programs, especially in low-resource settings. Several reviews have noted that such programs are inconsistently developed and implemented across regions, with Africa and other LMICs being particularly underserved [11]. The lack of structured educational support programs, alongside inadequate healthcare infrastructure, poverty, and social stigma, compounds the challenges faced by individuals with ESRD in these regions.

This scoping review was conducted to map the evidence on educational support programs for individuals with ESRD undergoing HD, with the aim of synthesizing evidence on: (i) the types and characteristics of educational interventions used; (ii) the delivery methods employed; and (iii) the outcomes reported, specifically in relation to QOL and treatment adherence. By identifying patterns across the literature and highlighting gaps, this review provides a foundation for developing and implementing evidence-based educational strategies for this population.

Methods

A scoping review was conducted following the six-stage methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [12]. The PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews checklist guided the summarization and reporting of the evidence (Fig. 1) [13]. No formal protocol was registered for this review; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Search strategy

Studies examining educational programs for individuals with ESRD undergoing HD were eligible for inclusion. A comprehensive search was conducted across five electronic databases: MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science, covering articles published from 2011 to 2021. These databases were selected to ensure broad and diverse coverage of relevant literature across nursing, medicine, and health sciences.

The search was developed by the research team in consultation with a multidisciplinary clinical panel (nephrologists, nurse specialists, nephrology technicians, and nutritionists). Both MeSH terms and free-text keywords were used. The search combined ESRD-related terms—including “end-stage renal disease,” “ESRD,” “chronic kidney disease,” “hemodialysis,” “haemodialysis,” and “renal dialysis”—using the Boolean operator OR, and these were then combined with education-related terms—including “educational program,” “educational intervention,” “patient education,” “support program,” and “educational support program”—using AND. Searches were applied to title, abstract, and keyword fields. No research librarian was formally involved; this represents a limitation. The search was conducted between December 2020 and December 2021. An example of the search strategy used in PubMed is provided in Table 1.

The time restriction (2011–2021) was chosen to capture contemporary HD educational practices aligned with current HD care guidelines; the review was not updated after 2021, which is a further limitation.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they: (i) examined educational interventions or programs for individuals with ESRD on HD; (ii) included adult participants aged 18 years or older; (iii) were published in English between 2011 and 2021; and (iv) reported outcomes related to QOL, treatment adherence, or self-management. Studies involving populations other than adults with ESRD on HD, or those not reporting educational program outcomes, were excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

Pairs of reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, and any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion among all authors. Two different reviewers conducted full-text screening independently; disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer. As only pairs rather than all four reviewers screened each record, the risk of inter-rater inconsistency is acknowledged as a limitation.

Data were extracted using a standardized extraction sheet capturing: study identification (year, title, authors, country); study design and sample size; sampling method; educational program description, type, duration, and delivery mode; data collection methods and instruments; data analysis techniques; and key outcomes (QOL and adherence measures).

The extracted data were synthesized descriptively to identify patterns, similarities, and differences across studies. Findings were analyzed using descriptive statistics and content analysis, and presented in both narrative and tabular formats. Since this is a scoping review, formal quality appraisal was not conducted. The column labeled “measures to minimize bias” in Table 2 reflects information reported by authors in the original studies, not a formal quality assessment; studies not reporting such measures were recorded as “not reported.”

Characteristics of Included Studies

The search identified 18 eligible studies (n = 18) from ten countries. Four studies (n = 4; 22.2%) were published in 2016, three (n = 3; 16.5%) in 2019, and two (n = 2; 11.1%) each in 2013, 2014, and 2020. Iran published the highest number of studies (n = 6; 33.3%), followed by Taiwan (n = 2; 11.1%) and the USA (n = 2; 11.1%); all other countries contributed one study each (n = 1; 5.6%).

The 18 included studies represented 1,999 individuals with ESRD on HD, with an average sample size of 63 participants per study. Sampling methods included random sampling (n = 6; 33.3%), purposive sampling (n = 4; 22.2%), and convenience sampling (n = 4; 22.2%); the remaining four studies did not report their sampling method.

50% of studies employed randomized controlled trial (RCT) designs (n = 9; 50%), followed by quasi-experimental (n = 6; 33.2%) and other designs. Of the studies including both intervention and control groups, only six reported using randomization [14–19]. Two studies focused on developing and piloting educational interventions and did not report participant allocation [20, 21]. Duration of interventions ranged from 2 weeks to 5 months, with most lasting approximately 2 months.

Educational interventions (Table 2)

Some studies incorporated educational interventions with more than one component. Two main intervention types were most frequently used across the studies (n = 10 studies): self-care interventions (n = 5) and nutrition/diet-related interventions (n = 5). An additional two studies focused exclusively on educational programs targeting treatment adherence. Interventions were delivered through various methods, including peer support networks, educational pamphlets, individual face-to-face counseling, video-based education, and group educational workshops.

Types of available interventions

Five studies focused on self-care education [14, 17, 23, 24]. Two studies included components on medication use [14, 25], while three provided direct education about ESRD pathophysiology [23, 25, 26]. Four studies addressed fluid restrictions [14, 15, 25, 27]. Five studies covered nutrition and dietary restrictions [20, 25, 27, 28, 29]. Four studies emphasized the importance of adherence to HD [16, 18, 25, 30]. Three studies addressed exercise and energy management [21, 22, 31], and one study included a session on intradialytic yoga [32].

Study outcomes

All 18 included studies reported that educational interventions regardless of mode or design positively influenced QOL and treatment adherence for patients with ESRD. These interventions ranged from structured self-management programs to targeted nutritional education and psychological support, demonstrating effectiveness across clinical and psychosocial domains. Quality of life outcomes

Across the included studies, educational interventions contributed to improvements in multiple QOL domains. The following outcomes were most consistently reported:

Reduction in hyperphosphatemia-related symptoms

Multiple studies [17, 18] reported that education on dietary phosphate control led to significant reductions in serum phosphate levels, accompanied by a decrease in associated symptoms such as pruritus, thereby enhancing patient comfort and skin health.

Controlled intradialytic weight gain

Studies focusing on self-efficacy training and fluid restriction education [15, 27, 31] reported that patients progressively reduced intradialytic weight gain, reflecting improved adherence to fluid intake guidelines and contributing to cardiovascular stability.

Psychological well-being

Several interventions resulted in reductions in stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms among patients undergoing HD [15, 17, 25]. These findings highlight the importance of integrating cognitive-behavioral approaches and stress management within ESRD educational programs.

Enhanced self-esteem

Interventions that included psychosocial support and coping strategy education were associated with increased self-esteem among HD patients [22, 32, 33], suggesting that educational programs can foster emotional resilience.

Improved dietary knowledge

A significant number of studies demonstrated that patients who received tailored nutritional education [16, 20, 24, 34] gained improved understanding of dietary restrictions, which is essential for controlling disease progression and minimizing complications.

Physical well-being

Studies such as Afrasiabifar et al. (2013) and Lazarus (2019) reported improved physical functioning and reduced fatigue following lifestyle and exercise-focused education, indicating that structured interventions can enhance physical capacity and daily living.

Across multiple QOL domains

Several studies demonstrated broad improvements across physical, emotional, and social well-being dimensions following educational interventions [35], consistent with a holistic benefit beyond isolated clinical parameters.

Treatment adherence outcomes

Educational interventions also showed a consistent positive relationship with improved treatment adherence across the included studies:

Improved dietary and fluid management knowledge

Four studies (n = 4; 22.2%) documented that targeted education led to greater awareness of dietary and fluid restrictions, enabling more informed decision-making [16].

Adherence to fluid restrictions

Seven studies (n = 7; 38.8%) specifically reported improvements in patients’ adherence to fluid intake guidelines, with implications for reducing complications such as hypertension and fluid overload [14].

Enhanced self-management

Two studies (n = 2; 11.1%) demonstrated improved self-management skills among patients [29], including goal-setting, symptom monitoring, and communication with healthcare providers.

Overall treatment adherence

Five studies (n = 5; 27.7%) reported greater consistency in following prescribed treatment regimens, including medication schedules, dietary plans, and dialysis attendance [14].

Discussion

This scoping review mapped evidence on educational support programs for individuals with ESRD undergoing HD, synthesizing findings from 18 studies across 10 countries. All included studies reported that educational interventions delivered through diverse methods and designs were associated with improvements in QOL and treatment adherence. The most consistently reported outcomes included reduced intradialytic weight gain, lower serum phosphate levels, improved psychological well-being, enhanced dietary knowledge, and better adherence to fluid and dietary restrictions. These findings underscore the broad value of educational support in ESRD care, while also revealing important gaps in the literature regarding intervention standardization, methodological rigor, and geographic representation.

The geographic distribution of included studies is noteworthy. Most studies (n = 11; 61.1%) were conducted in Asia, with Iran accounting for the highest proportion (n = 6; 33.3%). This concentration may be partly explained by structural factors: in many Asian countries, HD is government-funded, which may facilitate greater institutional interest in educational programs aimed at improving adherence and reducing downstream costs [36]. The global ESRD burden is heavily concentrated in Asia, with Asian patients representing approximately 60% of the global ESRD population and 90% of those undergoing HD [35]. Future research and publication efforts should aim for greater geographic diversity to strengthen the generalizability of findings.

Notably, no studies from Africa were identified. In many African settings, most individuals with ESRD discontinue HD and die prematurely, due to a combination of financial barriers, limited dialysis infrastructure, and low health literacy regarding HD [37]. A well-developed, culturally adapted educational program could improve understanding of HD, help reconcile traditional healing practices with biomedical approaches, and potentially increase the duration of treatment adherence. This represents an urgent priority for future research, and is directly relevant to the stated aims of improving educational programs for underserved populations in LMICs.

From a methodological perspective, this review identified a diverse range of study designs, including RCTs and quasi-experimental approaches. However, many studies provided insufficient detail on randomization procedures, blinding, and bias minimization, only three of the 18 studies reported specific bias reduction measures [15, 16, 22]. This limits confidence in the certainty of reported outcomes. Future educational intervention studies should adhere to higher standards of reporting, particularly those employing experimental designs, and should follow established reporting guidelines such as CONSORT.

The review identified a variety of educational delivery approaches, including peer support networks, printed educational materials, individual face-to-face sessions, video-based instruction, and group seminars [38, 39]. All delivery modalities were associated with positive outcomes, suggesting flexibility in implementation. However, this review was unable to directly compare the effectiveness of individual programs due to heterogeneity in educational content, delivery frequency, and study design. An important gap therefore remains: identifying which educational program configurations are most effective from clinical, QOL, and cost perspectives including for use in resource-limited settings.

Patient engagement in the design of educational programs is a recognized element of patient-centered care [40]. Involving patients in curriculum development enables the creation of content tailored to their specific needs and priorities [41–43]. Notably, 10 of the 18 educational programs included patient engagement in their development [14, 15, 17, 20, 23–26, 28, 29], which may have contributed to their reported effectiveness. It is recommended that future programs systematically incorporate patient engagement in both development and evaluation phases.

Across all included studies, improvement in QOL was reported; however, standardized QOL measurement instruments were not consistently used. The variety of QOL instruments employed including disease-specific tools (e.g., KDQOL) and generic measures (e.g., SF-36) limits the comparability and generalizability of findings. Future research should adopt standardized QOL measurement frameworks, ideally using instruments validated for HD populations across diverse cultural contexts, to allow more meaningful comparisons and meta-analyses.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, no formal scoping review protocol was registered prior to commencement. Second, the search was limited to English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages. Third, the search was conducted up to December 2021 and has not been updated; evidence published since then is not captured. Fourth, as pairs rather than all four reviewers screened each record, there is a risk of residual inter-rater inconsistency. Fifth, the small sample sizes of most included studies (average n = 63) limit the generalizability of findings. Sixth, the concentration of included studies from Iran warrants caution in applying findings to other contexts. Seventh, this review was unable to make direct comparisons between the effectiveness of individual programs due to heterogeneity in intervention design, outcomes, and measurement. These limitations should be considered when interpreting findings.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that educational interventions have broad value in ESRD care, with consistent benefits reported across clinical, psychological, and behavioral outcomes. These programs empower patients with the knowledge and skills needed to manage their condition, improve their QOL, and enhance adherence to complex treatment protocols. However, the evidence base is limited by variability in intervention types, study designs, outcome measures, and geographic representation. Future research should prioritize the development of rigorously designed, culturally adapted, and patient-centered educational programs, particularly in LMICs, including African settings using standardized outcome measures to enable meaningful cross-study comparisons. Identifying the most effective program configurations from clinical, QOL, and cost perspectives remains a critical next step.
