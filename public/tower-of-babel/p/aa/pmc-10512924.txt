Calcineurin inhibitors or cyclophosphamide in the treatment of membranous nephropathy superimposed with FSGS lesions: a retrospective study from China
Authors: Hong-Guang He, Yi-Yun Huang, Qin-Qing Liang, Qiu-Rong Ye, An-Dong Li, Kun Ye, Qiu-Xia Wu, Yan-Wu You
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCyclophosphamide (CTX) and calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) based regimens are recommended as immunosuppressive therapies for patients with idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN). Focal and segmental glomerular sclerosis (FSGS) lesions, which are common in membranous nephropathy (MN), are poor predictors of outcome. This study compared the differences of prognosis between two regimens in patients with IMN combined with FSGS lesions.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 108 patients with biopsy-proven IMN, accompanied with FSGS lesions, nephrotic syndrome and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 who were treated with CTX or CNIs. We used propensity score matching (PSM) for balancing the confounding variables.ResultsDuring follow-up, 10 patients (10/55 [18.2%]; nine males) in the CNIs group showed a 50% decline in eGFR; eight had a not otherwise specified variant. Patients initially treated with CNIs had a significantly higher risk of progression to the primary outcome and a lower probability of complete or total remission. The relapse rate was higher in patients who initially received CNIs- than in those who received CTX-based treatment. Before PSM, age and 24-h urine protein level differed significantly between the groups. The PSM model included data from 72 patients. Worse outcomes were also noted among patients who initially received CNIs than those who received CTX-based treatments after matching.ConclusionsPatients with MN combined with FSGS lesions have a higher risk of renal functional decline and a higher rate of relapse after CNIs than after CTX therapy.Keywords: Membranous nephropathy, cyclophosphamide, calcineurin inhibitors, focal and segmental glomerular sclerosis, prognosis

Introduction

In China, membranous nephropathy (MN) is one of the most common causes of nephrotic syndrome, the incidence of MN increased dramatically, whereas that of other glomerular diseases remained stable [1]. MN represents a spectrum of diseases that share a common pathological pattern characterized by glomerular capillary wall alterations resulting from the formation of subepithelial immune deposits [2]. The identification of the target autoantigen M-type phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) and its antibody in MN [3] was a milestone in our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanism that subsequently led to novel diagnostic and monitoring methods. PLA2R antibodies are specific for MN in up to 99% of cases [4], and a positive serum PLA2R antibody test result is a practical and noninvasive method for diagnosing MN [5]. Unlike the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 guidelines [6], renal biopsy is no longer required to confirm the diagnosis of MN in patients with nephrotic syndrome and positive PLA2R antibody test results. Regarding the prognosis of MN, the risk classification for the progressive loss of renal function is based on clinical and laboratory criteria without pathological support [7].

Nevertheless, a renal biopsy can provide crucial and credible information, particularly regarding focal and segmental glomerular sclerosis (FSGS) lesions [8–10], chronic tubulointerstitial injury [11], and vascular lesions [9], which could provide more pathological information for clinical decision-making. FSGS lesions are important negative prognostic predictors in patients [12]. MN accompanied by FSGS lesions is often accompanied by a decreased glomerular filtration rate. It is controversial whether FSGS lesions can predict prognosis independently of clinical data [13,14]; as in immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy [15] and lupus nephritis [16], FSGS lesions are not uncommon in MN[17], thus, the prognosis among the different treatment regimens in patients with MN combined with FSGS lesions should be investigated.

Based on clinical and laboratory criteria, the immunosuppressant regimens recommended by the KDIGO guidelines for moderate-risk MN included rituximab or calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) [7], whereas CNIs and cyclophosphamide (CTX) are recommended for patients with steroid-dependent or steroid-resistant primary FSGS [18]. However, no previous studies have investigated treatment protocols for patients with MN based on pathological lesion status.

The present study enrolled IMN patients with FSGS lesions, presenting with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2, nephrotic syndrome and treated with CTX or CNIs. We aimed to compare the intergroup differences in prognosis. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to reduce the influence of confounding variables.

Materials and methods

Clinical parameters

MN was confirmed using renal biopsy in all patients at our center between 2007and 2020, 58 patients underwent electron microscopy. Patients with ‘full house’ pattern in immunofluorescence, autoimmune diseases, malignant tumors, human immunodeficiency virus infection, hepatitis B or C virus infection, exposure to certain drugs associated with MN, diabetes mellitus, or accompanied with other glomerular diseases, inconformity to nephrotic syndrome, and a baseline eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 were excluded (Figure 1). All enrolled patients received persistent CTX or CNIs-based treatment unless the occurrence of adverse events prevented treatment from continuing, and followed up for no less than 6 months.

Study cohort flowchart. CTX: cyclophosphamide; CNIs: calcineurin inhibitors; TAC: tacrolimus; CSA: cyclosporin; ESRD: end-stage renal disease; CKD: chronic kidney disease.

For the enrolled patients, baseline clinical and laboratory criteria were collected, including sex, duration before biopsy, age, mean aterial pressure, serum creatinine, serum albumin, cholesterol, triglyceride, and 24-h urinary protein. The calculation of eGFR was based on the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation[19], and CKD was classified according to KDIGO 2012 [6]. Hypertension was defined as blood pressure over 140/90 mmHg or currently receiving antihypertensive treatment.

Histopathologic parameters

The collected pathological features included pathological stage, percentages of focal and segmental sclerosis, global sclerosis, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis. FSGS lesions were classified using the Columbia FSGS classification system [20]. Late stage FSGS (lesions >50% area of glomeruli), especially those that could not be explained by the disease course, were not be considered; FSGS lesions associated with severe ischemic glomeruli were excluded. PLA2R in renal tissue was examined by immunohistochemical staining. Tubulointerstitial fibrosis is defined as an increase of the extracellular matrix that separates the tubules in the renal cortex. The grading of arterial intimal thickening and arteriolar hyalinosis was assessed on a semi-quantitative scale of 0–3 [13].

Treatment

Only patients initially treated with CTX or CNIs were enrolled in this study. The CNIs were administered either alone or in combination with glucocorticoids. Cyclosporine (CsA) was administered at a mean initial dose of (2.7 ± 0.6) mg/(kg·d) in 46 patients. Tacrolimus (TAC) was gived at a mean initial dose of (0.055 ± 0.011) mg/(kg·d) in 9 patients. The CNIs were orally administered at 12-h intervals. After 12 months, tapering to a lower level was considered. If complete remission (CR) was achieved in less than 6 months, the dose was tapered off after obtaining CR for 6 months. Glucocorticoids were administered concomitantly at a initial dose of (0.35 ± 0.10) mg/(kg·d) (converted to prednisone) for one month in 52 patients of CNIs group and gradually reduced to 5–10 mg per day at 6 months.

The CTX group patients were treated with oral cyclophosphamide for 6 -9 months at a mean initial dose of (1.5 ± 0.4) mg/(kg·d), while intravenous cyclophosphamide was administered at (0.8–1.0) g per month for 6 months. Glucocorticoids were orally started at (0.9 ± 0.1) mg/(kg·d) (as prednisone) for two months, reduced 5 mg/day every two weeks, gradually tapered to10 mg/day.

Study definitions and outcomes

Complete remission (CR) was defined as proteinuria <300mg per 24 h at least twice, with a normalized serum albumin level and stable eGFR. Partial remission (PR) was defined as 24-h urinary protein >300 mg but <3.5 g and a decrease no less than 50% from the baseline value, confirmed twice, with improvement or normalization of the level of serum albumin and a stable eGFR. No remission of urine protein was defined as failure to achieve CR, PR, and/or reduction of eGFR by >30%. The primary outcome was a 50% decline in eGFR during the follow-up period. Secondary outcomes included initial CR or PR. The follow-up period ended on December 31, 2022.

Statistical analysis

Categorical parameters were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Normally distributed continuous data were analyzed using the t-test, while abnormally distributed data were analyzed using Mann-Whitney non-parametric test. Survival curves among groups were determined by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank tests. Propensity score matching with a 1:1 ratio was used for balancing the differences between the CTX and CNIs groups. Age and 24-h urine protein levels were chosen for the PSM model, and the caliper width was set at 0.3 [21]. A two-sided p-values <0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS 22.0 software was used for the data analysis.

Results

Baseline clinical and laboratory parameters

One hundred and eight patients were enrolled. Mean age at baseline was 49 ± 13 years, with a median follow-up of 30 (IQR, 15–51) months and a male predominance (male vs. female, 74:34). The mean duration before biopsy was 6 ± 8 months, and the mean serum creatinine level was 79 ± 19 μmol/L, mean 24-h proteinuria content level was 6.6 (IQR, 4.9–9.8) g/24 h, and mean serum albumin level was 20.9 (IQR, 17.8–24.0) g/L.

The CNIs group, including nine TAC and 46 CsA as initial treatment, had a baseline older age and higher level of 24-h proteinuria content, while there were no differences between the two groups in other laboratory and pathological parameters (Table 1).

Renal outcome

Progression in the unmatched cohort

Eleven patients (10.2%) progressed to a 50% decline in eGFR, with more CNIs patients (10/55 [18.2%]) than CTX patients (1/53 [1.7%]) at the end of the observation. Among the 10 patients (two with TAC and eight with CsA as initial treatment) with worsening renal function in the CNIs group, six patients had persistent nephrotic syndrome, of which three patients reached the primary outcome within the first 6 months during follow-up, and the other three patients had elevated albumin levels and decreased proteinuria levels, but did not meet the standard of remission. Of the 10 patients, four achieved remission (PR in three, CR in one) in the first induction treatment period but progressed to the primary outcome after relapse. One patient of the CTX group reached the primary outcome after adjusting for CsA treatment due to the failure of the CTX-initial treatment.

Patients treated with CNIs had a higher risk of progression to primary outcomes (χ2=8.326; p = 0.004) (Figure 2(A)). In the CNIs group, of the 10 patients progressing to the primary outcome, nine were male, eight had the not otherwise specified (NOS) type, one had a tip lesion, and one had a perihilar type. The baseline serum albumin of the patients with deteriorated renal function was lower than that in patients with stable renal function (p = 0.048).

Both in the unmatched cohort (p = 0.004) (A) and the matched cohort (p = 0.008) (B),the event-free survival for primary outcome was significantly lower in CNIs group than CTX group; Both in the unmatched cohort (p = 0.041) (C) and in the matched cohort (p = 0.034) (D), the event survival for initial partial remission was significantly lower in CNIs group than CTX group; Both in the unmatched cohort (p = 0.047) (E) and in the matched cohort (p = 0.012)(F),the event survival for initial complete remission was significantly lower in CNIs group than CTX group.

Eight patients in the CNIs group achieved cumulative CR at the 6-month follow-up versus only three patients in the CTX group. However, there was no significant difference in cumulative CR at any given time point between the CTX and CNIs groups. Significant differences in cumulative total remission between the two groups were observed at 6 (p = 0.043) and 9 (p = 0.042) months (Table 2). Patients in the CTX group were more likely to achieve both partial (χ2=4.185, p = 0.041) and complete (χ2=3.957, p = 0.047) remission than patients in the CNIs group (Figures 2(C,E)).

Regarding the patients who achieved remission during the initial treatment, the relapse rate in the CNIs group (15/43 [34.9%]) was higher than that in the CTX group (5/48 [10.4%]) (p = 0.006); the relapse time was 36 (19, 48) months in the CTX group and 30 (16, 39) months in CNIs group (p = 0.612).

The relapse rate in the CNIs group (9/21 [42.9%]) was higher than that in CTX group (2/24 [8.3%]) (p = 0.013) among the patients. Progression in the matched cohort

Before matching, there were significant differences in age and 24h urine protein level between the two groups. To balance the differences in age and 24h urine protein levels, the patients in the two groups were matched using PSM in a 1:1 ratio. The caliper width was set to 0.3 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score.

After matching, 72 patients were included in the model. There were no significant differences in all covariates (Table 1). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis performed on patients in the two groups after matching showed a higher risk of progression to the primary outcome (χ2=7.012; p = 0.008), lower likelihood of PR (χ2=4.510; p = 0.034), and lower likelihood of CR (χ2=6.340; p = 0.012) in the CNIs group than in the CTX group (Figure 2(B,D,F)).

Adverse events

Worsening of renal function was the most common serious adverse event in the CNIs group(18.2%); of the ten patients, five got further deterioration to end-stage renal disease. One patient in each group died. The patient in the CTX group did not achieve remission and died of an intracranial infection at 8 months after treatment. Patients in the CNIs group died of pneumonia after 4 years and achieving CR. There were no significant differences in the rates of infection, elevation of liver enzymes, leukopenia, thrombosis, and hyperglycemia between the two groups (Table 4).

Discussion

The risk of progressive renal function loss in MN, assessed using clinical and laboratory examinations, is widely accepted in clinical practice [7]. In earlier studies, the predictive value of the FSGS lesion in MN was limited; it could not be used as an independent predictor for renal function deterioration [13,14]. Pathological information provided by kidney biopsy has not been widely used to assess the progression of renal function and efficacy of immunosuppressants, especially in MN patients with PLA2R antibody positivity. Nevertheless, in some previous studies, FSGS lesion [8–10] was used to predict renal function deterioration in patients with MN and might provide more pathological information for clinical decision-making.

Moderate-risk treatments for MN include wait-and-see, rituximab, and CNIs with or without glucocorticoids [7]. Cyclophosphamide was not recommended for moderate-risk MN due to its side effects, although cyclophosphamide + glucocorticoid has been used in MN for decades [22]. Considering the high risk of renal function decline in patients with MN combined with FSGS lesions, immunosuppressive agents could be used to prevent renal function deterioration. Patients treated with CsA tended to achieve remission sooner, and was caught up later by the patient in the rituximab group [23]. In moderate-risk patients, the use of CNIs shortens the period of proteinuria [7]; however, with comorbid FSGS lesions, the prognosis should be reevaluated. The present study was intended to compare the prognosis of CTX- and CNIs-based treatments and explore the necessity and importance of pathological parameters.

FSGS is a histological pattern of glomerular lesion defined by focal and segmental obliteration or collapse of glomerular capillary tufts with an increase of extracellular matrix [24]. In the Columbia Classification, FSGS includes five variants [20]. These lesions may be caused by circulating factors, genetic risk alleles, drugs, infection, or maladaptive responses that share the initiating and defining features of podocyte injury [24–26]. It was suggested that FSGS should be distinguished from segmental scarring originating from the post-inflammatory necrotic or proliferative lesion [26]. However, in addition to nonspecific scar formation, a considerable proportion of segmental sclerotic lesions in IgA nephropathy shared similar mechanisms of podocyte damage with primary FSGS [27]. In another study, all biopsies of patients with IgA nephropathy had lesions basically similar to those described in primary FSGS [28].

In MN, subepithelial immune deposits may tamper with podocyte attachment to the glomerular basement membrane [10], and promote the formation of FSGS lesions. The hydraulic permeability of the glomerular capillary walls may be reduced by the epithelial immune complex [29], basement membrane thickening, and foot process broadening [30]. Subsequently, increases in the filtration surface area and net pressure for ultrafiltration are observed. The enlarged glomeruli in IMN patients with FSGS lesions suggest that glomerular hyperperfusion might contribute to the formation of FSGS lesion [31].

Podocyte damage in FSGS can induce the transforming growth factor–β (TGF-β)/Smad signaling pathway, which could lead to overexpression of extracellular matrix[32], and the upregulation of TGF-β is one of the important mechanisms in CNI nephrotoxicity [33], independent of the hemodynamic effects of CNI [34]. Therefore, in MN patients superimposed with FSGS lesions, CNIs may exacerbate the proliferation of extracellular matrix.

Wang et al. found that non-glomerular tip lesion was a negative predictor of IMN [17]. In this study, the NOS variant was most common seen in the CNIs group with deteriorated renal function, whereas only one patient with the tip lesion had progressive renal function. A previous study found that the NOS type may be a later progressive FSGS lesion from the tip lesion [35]. This may indicate two different populations or two different periods of FSGS lesions in MN, some of which are manifested by cytoskeletal injury of podocytes, which may benefit from CNIs [36], while another group presenting with the proliferation in extracellular matrix at a progressive stage (may manifest with the NOS type) were at a high risk of progressing to renal dysfunction after CNIs treatment.

In children with minimal change disease (MCD), the duration of treatment and massive proteinuria during treatment are risk predictors of CsA-induced tubulointerstitial lesions [37]. Unlike patients with MCD, patients with MN often achieve remission later [38,39], the duration of nephrotic proteinuria during CNIs treatment may be much longer, and FSGS lesions might further extend the disease duration in patients with MN, thus increasing the probability of renal function deterioration. However, long-term and low-dose of CsA is safe for maintaining CR in the adult responders with MN [40]. Glucocorticoids monotherapy has shown benefit in Chinese population with MN [41], for their podocyte-protective, immunosuppressive, and anti-inflammatory effects. High dose oral glucocorticoids are recommended as the first-line immunosuppressive treatment for primary FSGS [7]. The patients with MN superimposed with FSGS lesions might benefit from the higher dose of glucocorticoids in CTX group.

There were some limitations in this study. First, it was a retrospective study conducted at a single center. Second, this study did not establish conservative treatment or rituximab groups because of insufficient data. Third, the study spanned more than a decade, there were intragroup differences in the medicine dosage protocol, and few patients had the data on baseline serum levels of the PLA2R antibody. Patients lost to follow-up may have affected study outcomes.

In summary, the findings of the present study suggest that patients with MN superimposed with FSGS lesions treated with CNIs are more likely to develop a progressive loss of renal function. We also found that the probability of remission was lower in the CNIs versus CTX group, whereas the relapse rate was higher. Most cases with renal function deterioration are of the NOS type. Renal biopsies may provide specific information for treatment decisions.
