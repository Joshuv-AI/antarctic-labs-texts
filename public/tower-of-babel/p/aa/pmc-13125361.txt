Port‐site hernia following laparoscopic gynecological surgery: A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Franciszek Ługowski, Zofia Włodarczyk, Anna Owczarek, Łucja Zaborowska, Artur Ludwin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPort‐site hernia (PSH) is a rare but clinically relevant complication of laparoscopic gynecologic surgery. Despite the widespread adoption of minimally invasive techniques, no prior systematic review has comprehensively evaluated PSH incidence and risk factors in this population.Material and MethodsA systematic review and meta‐analysis were performed following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025649280). PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase were searched without language or date restrictions. Eligible studies included randomized or cohort designs reporting PSH incidence after laparoscopic gynecologic procedures. Pooled incidence was calculated using a random‐effects model.ResultsTwenty‐two studies comprising 70 346 patients were included. The overall pooled PSH incidence was 0.29% (95% CI: 0.00 to 0.02), with significant heterogeneity (I 2 = 99.3%). Sensitivity analysis excluding outliers reduced heterogeneity (I 2 = 83.1%) without altering direction or significance. Elevated body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus, and advanced age were consistent patient‐related risk factors, while larger trocar size (≥10 mm), single‐port access, and inadequate fascial closure were major surgical contributors.ConclusionsPSH following laparoscopic gynecologic surgery remains a rare but clinically significant occurrence. Surgical technique, particularly trocar size selection and fascial closure, plays a decisive role in risk reduction. Attention to modifiable intraoperative factors is crucial for optimizing patient safety and preventing postoperative hernia formation.

Key message

Port‐site hernia is a rare yet serious event in gynecologic laparoscopy. This first systematic review highlights obesity, single‐port access, and trocars ≥10 mm as major risk factors. Fascial closure reduces the risk; however, no specific technique is superior.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past decades, advances in laparoscopic instrumentation, combined with the growing surgical expertise of gynecologists, have substantially expanded the role of laparoscopy in clinical practice. As a result, procedures that were once performed exclusively by laparotomy are now routinely carried out laparoscopically across a wide spectrum of benign and malignant gynecologic indications. 1 , 2 The laparoscopic approach offers numerous well‐documented advantages, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, and improved cosmetic outcomes. 3 , 4 , 5 Despite these benefits, the procedure is not without risk, and procedure‐specific complications, such as port‐site hernia (PSH), remain clinically significant and warrant careful consideration. 6

Early‐onset PSH represents the most frequently documented subtype, typically manifesting within the first 14 days following surgery. 7 Clinically, these hernias most often present as small bowel obstruction, a potentially life‐threatening condition that frequently requires urgent reoperation. Furthermore, late‐onset hernias, although less common, typically occur as a localized bulge several months after surgery. 8 The reoperation rate in this subgroup remains comparatively low, as these hernias are frequently managed conservatively. 9

Several risk factors for PSH have been described, relating both to patient characteristics and surgical technique. The most frequently studied include elevated body mass index (BMI), age over 60 years, the size and location of trocar placement, as well as the method of fascial closure. 7 , 10 , 11 , 12 The estimated incidence of PSH following laparoscopic surgery ranges from 0% to 5.2%. 13 Therefore, effective prevention, early detection, and safe management of PSH are essential components of a safe and efficient laparoscopic approach in gynecological surgery. 14

To date, no systematic review regarding PSH in gynecologic surgery has been published. Hence, the objective of this study was to systematically evaluate the available data on the incidence of PSH undergoing laparoscopic gynecological surgery for both benign and malignant indications, as well as to identify significant risk factors and predictors of hernia development.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also screened to identify additional eligible reports.

No restrictions were applied regarding language, publication status, or date. The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and the protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025649280). 15 For the purposes of this review, PSH was defined as the development of an abdominal wall hernia at the site of trocar insertion, diagnosed clinically (palpable bulge/pain) or radiologically (computed tomography/Ultrasound), or confirmed during reoperation. This definition encompasses hernias occurring at standard multiport trocar sites as well as umbilical incisions used for single‐port access (SPA) surgery. SPA hernias were included because, despite the larger incision length, the mechanism involves the insertion of a multi‐channel port device, distinct from a standard laparotomy incision.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree) and free‐text terms related to port‐site hernia, laparoscopy, and gynecology. An example PubMed search string was:

(“Hernia, Ventral”[Mesh] OR “Hernia”[Mesh] OR hernia*[tiab] OR “incisional hernia”[tiab] OR “trocar‐site hernia”[tiab] OR “port‐site hernia”[tiab] OR “laparoscopic port‐site hernia”[tiab] OR “laparoscopic trocar‐site hernia”[tiab] OR “Richter's hernia”[tiab] OR “Richters hernia”[tiab]) AND (“Laparoscopy”[Mesh] OR laparoscop*[tiab] OR “minimally invasive surgery”[tiab] OR MIS[tiab]) AND (“Gynecology”[Mesh] OR gynecol*[tiab] OR hysterectom*[tiab] OR oophorectom*[tiab] OR adnex*[tiab] OR myomectom*[tiab] OR salpingectom*[tiab] OR “pelvic surgery”[tiab]).

Database‐specific adaptations of this strategy were applied across all platforms.

Selection criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) design: randomized controlled trials and prospective or retrospective cohort studies, (2) population: patients undergoing laparoscopic gynecological surgery for any benign or malignant indication, and (3) outcomes: incidence of PSH as the primary outcome, whereas secondary outcomes included patient‐related risk factors (BMI, age, comorbidities), surgical risk factors (trocar size, location, single‐port vs. multiport approach), time to hernia diagnosis, and the influence of fascial closure techniques.

Exclusion criteria included (1) non‐original articles (reviews, editorials, case reports, and case series), studies lacking extractable data on PSH incidence, studies involving non‐gynecological laparoscopic procedures, and overlapping datasets (in which case the most complete or recent study was included).

Three reviewers (F.Ł., Z.W., and A.O.) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full‐text assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or arbitration by a fourth reviewer (Ł.Z.).

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed independently by three reviewers using the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool. 16 The tool evaluates seven domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result.

Each study was judged as having low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias in each domain, with an overall judgment derived accordingly. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by discussion or arbitration with the fourth author (Ł.Z.).

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted using a standardized form. Extracted information included study characteristics (design, country, setting, recruitment period, sample size),patient demographics, surgical procedures, trocar characteristics, follow‐up duration, andoutcome data on PSH incidence (number of cases and denominator) and secondary outcomes.

Statistical analysis

A single‐arm meta‐analysis of PSH incidence was performed using JASP (version 0.95.1, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). The effect size was expressed as a pooled incidence proportion with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A random‐effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML) was applied to account for between‐study variability.

Between‐study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q test, the I 2 statistic, and τ 2 (between‐study variance). Forest plots were generated to display study‐specific and pooled estimates.

Sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding studies with extreme effect sizes (outliers) and by stratifying results according to study size, design, and surgical indication.

Other reported outcomes (e.g., predisposing factors, timing of hernia occurrence, management strategies) were synthesized narratively, as the data were too heterogeneous or insufficient for meta‐analysis.

Following the removal of 563 duplicate entries, 1281 titles and abstracts were further screened to evaluate eligibility. A total of 169 publications underwent an in‐depth full‐text analysis, resulting in 147 studies being excluded from further assessment (Figure 2). Incidence of port‐site hernia

A meta‐analysis of 22 studies reporting the incidence of PSH following laparoscopic gynecologic surgery was conducted, including a total of 70 346 patients. PSH occurred in 202 cases, and the overall pooled incidence was 0.29% (95% CI: 0.00 to 0.02). Between‐study heterogeneity was considerable (Q (21) = 199.71, p < 0.001; I 2 = 99.29% [98.71, 99.69]), reflecting variability in surgical procedures, study designs, and follow‐up duration across the included studies.

A visual inspection of the forest plot (Figure 3) suggested potential small‐study effects, with several studies [e.g., Tewari et al., 35 Demirayak et al., 21 Moulton et al., 2017, 25 and Salamah et al. 33 ] deviating from the expected distribution, possibly contributing to the observed asymmetry.

To assess the influence of outlier studies on the pooled estimate, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding four studies with markedly higher effect sizes [Tewari et al., 35 Demirayak et al., 21 Moulton et al. 2017, 25 and Salamah et al. 33 ]. These studies reported incidence rates substantially above the mean and contributed disproportionately to the observed heterogeneity. After their exclusion, the pooled incidence of PSH was 0.10% (n = 66) but the heterogeneity was markedly reduced (Q (17) = 57.54, p < 0.001; I 2 = 83.14% vs. 99.29% in the full model). The between‐study variance (τ 2) decreased to 0.00, indicating a more homogeneous set of studies (Figure 5).

BMI

Multiple studies identified an elevated BMI as a significant risk factor for the development of PSH. 20 , 25 , 26 , 35 , 36 The study by Cybulska et al. 20 reported that the median BMI for patients who developed a hernia was significantly higher than for those who did not (36.9 kg/m2 vs. 30.8 kg/m2, respectively; p = 0.004). The same study also found that patients with a BMI ≥40 kg/m2 were five times more likely to develop a hernia compared with those with a BMI <40 kg/m2 (6.9% vs. 1.4%, p = 0.001). 20 This association was further supported by two large cohort studies, which, through multivariable analysis, found that increasing BMI was an independent predictor for PSH development. 25 , 35 Importantly, the study by Yoo et al. 36 found that median BMI was significantly higher in the hernia group in univariate analysis (26.5 kg/m2 vs. 23.7 kg/m2, p = 0.04), although this did not remain an independent risk factor in the multivariable model. Conversely, some studies with smaller patient numbers did not find this association. One reported no significant difference in BMI for the two patients who developed hernias compared with the rest of their single‐port hysterectomy cohort. 23 Another large study from an Asian population reported a very low hernia incidence (3 cases in 2498 surgeries) and suggested that the relatively lower mean BMI of their cohort (22.6 kg/m2) may have contributed to this low rate. 28 The three patients in that series who developed hernias had BMIs of 24.3, 25.7, and 26.6 kg/m2. 28 Other studies provided descriptive BMI data for their hernia cases, such as 19.9, 28.1, and 31.5 kg/m2, without a statistical comparison to a control group. 19 , 21 , 37

Age

The evidence regarding age as a predisposing factor for PSH was mixed. A large study of 898 single‐port surgeries identified increasing age as an independent predictor for PSH development (Hazard Ratio [HR] 1.03, p = 0.017). 25 Similarly, a 20‐year retrospective cohort analysis found that the rate of PSH was significantly higher in patients aged ≥60 years compared with younger patients (0.112% vs. 0.011%, p = 0.008). 37 However, no other studies have found a significant association between age and the incidence of PSH. A retrospective analysis reported no difference in the median age of patients who developed a hernia versus those who did not (63.5 vs. 61.2 years, p = 0.2). 20 In addition, this evidence was further supported by two other studies where age was found to be significant on univariate analysis, but not an independent risk factor on multivariable analysis. 35 , 36 Other descriptive reports provided the ages of the hernia patients (e.g., cases at 42 and 67 years old) or noted that their single hernia case occurred in a patient younger than 50 years, without finding a statistical link to risk. 21 , 23

Type of trocars

Six studies provided data indicating that a larger trocar size is a significant surgical risk factor. 17 , 20 , 22 , 29 , 32 , 37 First, Kadar et al. 22 demonstrated that the frequency of PSH at extraumbilical sites was significantly greater with 12‐mm trocars compared with 10‐mm trocars (3.1% vs. 0.23%, p = 0.01). This is strongly supported by numerous subsequent reports where hernias were documented at larger port sites of 10 and 15 mm in diameter. 17 , 30 , 32 Importantly, an analysis of 760 cases reported that nearly all hernias (15 of 16) developed at the 10–12‐mm camera port site. 20 While hernias are most common at sites ≥10 mm, the study by Zhu et al. 37 also demonstrated two cases of hernia at 5 mm port sites. In this study, the largest fascial defects and subsequent hernias were associated with single‐port surgery, which uses incisions of 25–40 mm. 37

Closure technique

The method for fascial closure at trocar sites >5 mm was discussed in 10 studies, revealing varied findings. The importance of closure was highlighted in one study that showed leaving the fascia open at 12‐mm sites significantly increased the hernia rate (p = 0.021). 22 However, hernias can still occur despite meticulous closure. The same study noted that three of five hernias at 12 mm sites occurred after an “attempted” closure. 22 This observation was echoed in several other reports where hernias developed despite routine or prophylactic fascial closure. 17 , 27 , 29 , 30 , 32 Additionally, in the study by Park et al., 31 both hernias occurred in patients whose fascia was closed with an interrupted technique.

When comparing specific techniques, one large study found no significant difference in hernia rates between a standardized protocol using an interrupted non‐absorbable/delayed‐absorbable suture and a historical technique using a running absorbable suture (9.7% vs. 6.5%, p = 0.095). 35 Another study, while having few hernia cases, described changing their practice from a running to an interrupted suture technique, believing the latter provided better visualization of the fascia; in their series, two hernias occurred with running sutures and one with an interrupted suture. 28 Furthermore, the study by Su et al. 2 evaluating a Foley catheter‐assisted closure technique reported no hernias in either group, precluding any conclusion on prevention. Lastly, Cybulska et al. 20 described their institutional practice of routinely closing all 12‐mm midline ports without providing any comparative data.

Hernia site

Of the 19 studies that described hernia location, the most frequently reported site was the umbilical or camera port. 20 , 23 , 25 , 26 , 28 , 31 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 This is typically the largest fascial defect in both multiport and single‐port procedures. However, hernias at lateral or extraumbilical port sites were also common, particularly if the trocar used was 10 mm or larger. The study by Kadar et al. 22 reported that all six of its hernias occurred at extraumbilical sites. Furthermore, Zhu et al. 37 documented five of nine hernias at right lateral ports. Other publications delineated locations for PSH, such as suprapubic, right lower quadrant, and other unspecified or lateral sites. 17 , 27 , 29 , 30 , 32

Time to hernia

The time from surgery to the diagnosis of a PSH demonstrated a wide range, with both early‐onset (<30 days) and late‐onset (>30 days) presentations being common. Several studies documented early‐onset hernias presenting within days of the initial procedure, with diagnoses made on postoperative days 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 9. 19 , 22 , 29 , 32 , 37 A 20‐year cohort analysis classified seven of nine hernias as early‐onset, occurring within 2 weeks of surgery. 37 Late‐onset hernias were equally common. Median or mean times to diagnosis in large cohorts ranged from 10.6 months to approximately 18.8 months (570.2 days). 25 , 35 Specific late‐onset cases were reported at 6 and 8 months, 6, 8, and 11 months, and 6 and 9 months. 21 , 28 , 31 Some studies noted diagnoses occurring as late as 1–2 years postoperatively. 20 , 22 , 25 , 37 Other reports described the hernia as a “late complication” occurring “several months after surgery”. 23 , 28 , 31

Surgical approach (single‐port vs. multiport)

Six studies compared hernia rates between different surgical approaches, with a consistent finding that single‐port laparoscopic surgery (SPLS) is associated with a higher risk of PSH than conventional multiport laparoscopy. The retrospective analysis by Zhu et al. 37 found the PSH rate to be more than 27 times higher after single‐incision surgery than after conventional laparoscopy (0.357% vs. 0.013%, p = 0.003). This conclusion was further supported by three other comparative studies, all of which reported PSH cases exclusively in their single‐port cohorts, with zero hernias in the multiport groups. 19 , 21 , 23

Patient comorbidities

Six studies evaluated the impact of patient comorbidities on PSH risk. 20 , 21 , 25 , 26 , 35 , 36 Diabetes mellitus was the most consistently reported comorbidity associated with an increased risk of hernia development. Three large studies identified diabetes as an independent predictor in multivariable analysis. 25 , 26 , 35 Additionally, the evidence for hypertension was less consistent; one study found it to be an independent predictor, 25 while three others did not find a significant association. 20 , 35 , 36 Other conditions noted in univariate analyses to be associated with PSH included dyslipidemia. 36 One study provided a descriptive case of a patient with both hypertension and diabetes who developed a PSH. 21 In contrast, the study by Cybulska et al. found no significant association between hernia development and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, or smoking status. 20

DISCUSSION

This study represents the first systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluating PSH in gynecologic surgery, identifying 6 prospective and 16 retrospective cohort studies.

The evaluation of the results confirms that PSH is a recognized, albeit rare, complication with an overall incidence of 0.29%. This finding aligns with previous reports describing PSH as clinically significant, yet of occasional occurrence. 9 Clinicians should remain vigilant, especially in patients with risk factors or after the use of trocars larger than 10 mm. However, diagnostic imaging (such as CT or ultrasound) is indicated primarily for patients presenting with symptoms (e.g., abdominal pain or a palpable bulge). 38 Significant heterogeneity reflects methodological variability; however, the stability of pooled incidence in the sensitivity analysis confirms the reliability of this study's results. The observed asymmetrical pattern in the funnel plot may reflect small‐study effects and the inherent behavior of rare‐event data rather than true publication bias. To address potential skewing by outliers, sensitivity analyses were performed. The pooled incidence of 0.29% must be interpreted as descriptive rather than predictive for individual risk. The substantial heterogeneity (I 2 > 99%) observed across studies reflects fundamental differences in surgical indications (benign vs. malignant), follow‐up duration (ranging from days to years), and diagnostic rigor. Consequently, this estimate provides a broad epidemiological summary rather than a precise risk calculation for a specific patient or procedure.

Another important observation is that elevated BMI and older age emerged as notable patient‐related risk factors of PSH. While some studies did not demonstrate a clear interplay between elevated BMI and PSH incidence, others described a significant association. Larger cohort analyses found higher BMI among patients who developed PSH, with one study by Cybulska et al. showing a fivefold increased risk in patients with BMI over 40 kg/m2. 20 , 25 , 26 , 35 , 36 This association may be explained by the presence of increased visceral fat, which contributes to impaired fascial healing due to increased intra‐abdominal pressure. 36 Additionally, the presence of larger preperitoneal spaces in obese patients was found to be significant in PSH development. 39

Likewise, evidence referring to older age as a PSH development risk factor was inconsistent, as some studies showed the linkage, while others did not. 21 , 24 Older age may play an indirect role, as reduced tissue elasticity, impaired collagen production, and age‐related comorbidities may weaken the fascia and compromise wound healing, even if the age itself is not always an independent factor. Regarding comorbidities, diabetes mellitus was the most often reported condition associated with PSH risk, whereas findings concerning hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking were more ambiguous. 20 , 21 , 25 , 26 , 35 , 36 Overall, these findings suggest that patient‐related factors predispose to PSH mainly through mechanisms disrupting tissue healing and fascia integrity.

Patient‐related risk factors such as BMI and age represent an inconsistent linkage with PSH development. BMI is often an inaccurate measure, as it does not differentiate between higher adipose and muscle mass; thus, it might misclassify the metabolic state of patients. Additionally, age does not distinctly reveal the patients' health status, their tissue quality and healing potential, as patients of the same age may differ vastly in lifestyle, diet, comorbidities, and collagen integrity. Overall, patient‐related factors appear to have less impact on PSH development compared with surgical and technical components.

From a surgical standpoint, the trocar size is a surgical factor that is most commonly reported as a PSH risk factor. Multiple studies confirm that ports ≥10 mm are more likely to develop PSH, and the risk multiplies when 12–15 mm trocars are used. 9 , 40 The reason behind it is very clear, as larger ports create a more substantial fascial defect, which promotes peritoneal protrusion. Nevertheless, case reports of PSH in ports of 5 mm highlight that even small fascial deficits might be clinically relevant. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44

Fascial closure after laparoscopic procedures remains a subject of debate. It is commonly acknowledged that ports larger than 10–12 mm should be sutured. 22 However, it is not established whether all fascia defects should be closed. On the one hand, suturing all fascia defects could enhance the prevention of PSH by restoring the integrity of the abdominal wall. However, it is associated with longer operative time and greater traction and manipulation of the defect, which may lead to additional tissue trauma, ischemia, or non‐precise fascial planes alignment, all of which could lead to increased PSH risk. Moreover, PSH can still occur even after meticulous closure, underscoring its multifactorial character and leaving the issue without a definite conclusion.

Regarding hernia location, the umbilical port was the most common site, which coincides with being the largest fascia defect, as it usually is the camera port both in single and multiport operations. However, lateral and suprapubic port sites were also affected, especially at 10‐mm trocar sites, which emphasizes that port size, rather than its location, is of greater significance. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

The timing of hernia presentation varied widely, with both early‐onset cases and late‐onset cases being reported. 19 , 21 , 22 , 24 , 29 Hernia development after only a few days highlights the importance of surgical technicalities such as trocar insertion, used tension and traction, and fascia closure. Nevertheless, the frequent diagnosis of late‐onset hernias suggests that tissue remodeling, wound healing, and fascia weakening may also contribute to PSH. These findings underscore the complexity of PSH pathogenesis.

Concerning the surgical approach, the results consistently show higher PSH incidence in the single‐port rather than multiport approach. 18 , 19 , 21 , 25 , 26 , 37 This is mainly related to the urgency of creating a larger incision, which becomes a vulnerable site. Additionally, repeated instrument changes through the same port result in greater manipulation and tissue stretching, further promoting hernia development.

Unfortunately, none of the included studies specifically assessed the impact of tissue morcellation on the incidence of PSH. This factor could be clinically relevant, as morcellation, particularly when performed through enlarged trocar or suprapubic incisions, may increase fascial stretching and manipulation, potentially predisposing to subsequent hernia formation. 45 Moreover, repeated instrument exchanges and the extraction of large tissue fragments may compromise the integrity of the port‐site closure. 45 Future studies should investigate whether the use of morcellation, its technique, and the site of tissue extraction contribute to PSH risk in laparoscopic gynecologic surgery. While prolonged operative time is a plausible risk factor, limited data prevented a pooled analysis; however, one large cohort study found no significant association between the length of surgery and hernia development. 35

Overall, surgical and technical determinants are generally more decisive in PSH development because they directly influence the integrity of the abdominal wall at the trocar site. Factors such as trocar size, location, closure technique, and surgical approach have a direct mechanical effect on the fascia and peritoneum, determining whether a defect is created and how effectively it heals. In contrast, patient‐related factors like BMI or age only modulate the background healing capacity, but without a surgical defect or inadequate closure, a hernia would not occur. This makes surgical technique the primary determinant of PSH risk, while patient‐related variables act more as modifiers.

In our view, surgeons should prioritize the use of ports <10 mm where feasible, as these sites rarely require closure. For incisions of at least 10 mm and SPA, rigorous fascial closure is recommended, though it does not eliminate risk entirely. In high‐risk populations (BMI >40 kg/m2, diabetes), surgeons might consider using non‐midline ports or enhanced closure techniques, although superior evidence for specific closure methods is currently lacking.

To summarize, the presented study represents a synthesis of current knowledge on PSH following laparoscopic gynecological operations, a rare but serious complication. One of the key strengths of the systematic review and meta‐analysis is its transparent methodology, including its adherence to PRISMA guidelines and a registered PROSPERO protocol. Additionally, the current study incorporates the most recent studies, thus reflecting the up‐to‐date state of evidence. Nevertheless, certain limitations need to be addressed. Firstly, most of the studies have been categorized as “serious” in the risk of bias assessment, even though the overall classification primarily reflects serious risk of bias in just one of the evaluated categories. Moreover, meta‐analysis was only conducted for the incidence of PSH due to significant heterogeneity of outcome reporting in the analyzed papers. Furthermore, the absence of RCTs constitutes another significant limitation; as no such studies were identified through the literature search, the conclusions drawn are based solely on observational data, which may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of the findings.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrate that PSH represents a rare but clinically relevant complication of laparoscopic gynecologic surgery, with a pooled incidence of 0.29%. While inadequate closure is a risk factor, PSH can occur despite technical diligence. Current evidence does not demonstrate the superiority of any single closure technique.
