Theoretical and experimental study into laser-induced gold nanoparticles for superior surface-enhanced raman spectroscopy applications
Authors: Nader Javanmard, Mahdi Sovizi, Maryam Aliannezhadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comprehensive theoretical and experimental investigation into the fabrication of three-dimensional gold nanostructures with tailored optical properties using a laser-induced method, aimed at enhancing applications based on localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) and Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS). The effects of the second harmonic (without filtering the fundamental harmonic) of Nd:YAG laser pulses, along with key parameters such as laser fluence and pulse count, are analyzed to achieve precise control over nanoparticle size, shape, and arrangement. Theoretical modeling using Fourier heat equations and the novel Generalized Finite Difference Time Domain (G-FDTD) method provides initial insights into the optical fluences necessary for the formation of surface gold nanoparticles. Experimentally, 100 nm gold thin films are irradiated with different fluences (0.48, 0.12, and 0.053 J/cm2) and pulse numbers (ranging from 1 to 50 pulses) of Nd:YAG laser with a time width of 60 ns. Results demonstrate that manipulating laser parameters significantly influences the localized surface plasmon resonance, offering a scalable and reproducible platform for designing stable, biocompatible, and high-performance nanostructures for applications based on LSPR, like SERS and enhanced fluorescence. Also, the formed gold elliptical NPs are simulated to understand the obtained results and the efficiency of the samples in SERS applications.Keywords: Interaction of laser pulses with thin films, Surface plasmonic nanoparticles, Localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), Generalized finite difference time domain method (G-FDTD)Subject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

The optical properties of metallic nanostructures—particularly localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR)—are crucial in sensors such as LSPR, fluorescence, and SERS devices. When plasmonic nanoparticles are illuminated, they can induce collective electron oscillations, resulting in wavelength-dependent absorption that enhances local electric fields and nonlinear effects, such as Raman scattering. Various nanostructures, including spherical gold and silver nanoparticles, nanowires, gold elliptical NPs and disks, and coupled gold nanorods within silver nanoshells, etc., have been reported, highlighting the importance of tuning LSPR wavelengths and also developing plasmonic SERS substrates1–5.

Gold nanoparticles are valued for their physical and chemical stability, high malleability, and oxidation resistance, which is especially prominent in optics, electronics, medicine, and catalysis6. Its nanoparticles exhibit strong LSPR, enabling efficient light absorption, scattering, and energy conversion, which contribute to their sensing applications7,8. Gold nanoparticles are produced through physical methods like laser ablation or chemical routes such as co-precipitation and green synthesis9–12. While chemical methods pose environmental concerns, green synthesis offers sustainability, and it aligns with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) theory12, but may face stability issues like aggregation in liquid media. Laser ablation is an effective and biocompatible method for synthesizing plasmonic nanoparticles with the ability to avoid chemical solutions and precise size control by adjusting laser parameters13,14. Therefore, researchers focused on pulsed laser ablation in liquid as a top-down approach, which is related to the irradiation of laser pulses to solid bulk metal targets due to its special advantage15,16. A review of the method, the critical role of process parameters in controlling nanoparticle characteristics, parameter dependencies to optimize nanoparticle morphology, size, and structure, current challenges, and prospects in the field can be found in the scientific literature17. Furthermore, the synthesis of gold nanoparticles using pulsed nanosecond lasers in various liquid environments has been studied, along with the investigation of their morphological, chemical, optical, and structural microstructural characteristics, as well as the evaluation of their anticancer effects and cellular eradication18. Another approach to laser ablation involves irradiating a thin metal layer with laser pulses to induce nanoparticle formation directly on the substrate surface19,20.

Gold nanoparticles produced directly on surfaces using pulsed laser ablation demonstrate greater stability and possess unique properties relative to their dispersed counterparts in liquids, offering significant advantages for applications such as photochemistry and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS). Techniques such as nanosecond and resonant ablation allow fine-tuning of nanoparticle characteristics for specific uses13,21. Therefore, metal ablation using short and ultrashort pulsed lasers is of interest in forming gold NPs on the surface of dielectric, due to its advantages, and also its low ablation threshold and its high process efficiency. The pulse characteristics (wavelength, width, energy, repetition rate), along with the optical and mechanical properties of the metal (reflectivity, absorption, thickness, thermal conductivity), and the nature of the surrounding medium, are all critical factors in laser-metal interactions22,23. Many surface plasmon resonance-based spectrometers have been developed using the interaction of short and ultrashort lasers on thin films to produce small chips. Also, this area remains an active and promising research area at the edge of science. Due to the mentioned reason, numerous studies have investigated the interaction of nanoseconds, picoseconds, and femtoseconds laser pulses with metals24–27. For example, the transient response of a thin metal film to nanosecond laser pulses has been studied in thermoelastic and ablation regimes by a 3D finite element model24,28. In another research, the mechanisms and processes of forming micro and nanostructures by irradiation of short and ultrashort pulsed lasers on metallic layers have been explored, demonstrating the ability of the method to control the characteristics of nanostructures—such as size, shape, and structure—by adjusting laser parameters (energy, wavelength, and pulse duration), the properties of the metallic thin film (material and thickness), and the substrate characteristics13,19. Additionally, laser irradiation of bimetallic films can tune LSPR wavelengths and enable the fabrication of diverse bimetallic structures. Surface-structured bimetallic silver-copper nanoparticles have been fabricated via excimer laser pulse irradiation of layered films, as studied by Naranji and colleagues14. Additionally, irradiating Ag films on polycarbonate and BK7 substrates with excimer (ArF) laser pulses, both with and without external fields, produced 3D silver nanoparticles for SERS application13. Various micro/nanostructures fabricated by laser techniques, such as laser ablation, laser-induced periodic surface structures (LIPSS), in optoelectronic devices with the aim of inspiring future research directions in the design and application of laser-fabricated micro/nanostructures in the field of optoelectronics, have been investigated29.

The high stability, biocompatibility, and reproducibility of gold nanoparticles produced by irradiating laser pulses to gold film are important in applications based on LSPR and SERS, like in bioanalysis, environmental monitoring, materials science, etc.30,31. Therefore, this study presents a synergistic theoretical and experimental study of laser-processed gold nanostructures as an approach to tailoring 3D gold nanostructures through laser-induced fabrication, emphasizing the impact of unfiltered laser harmonics, fluence, and pulse number on the formation of nanoparticles with specific optical properties. The theoretical component develops a model to predict the laser irradiation, which can provide melting and ablation thresholds through Nd: YAG laser irradiation on a gold film. The experimental component details the deposition of gold thin films and their subsequent irradiation, with particular emphasis on the role of laser fluence and pulse number in nanoparticle formation on the film. It also examines how unfiltered laser harmonics, together with controlled irradiation conditions, enable precise manipulation of nanoparticle size and shape, thereby tailoring the LSPR properties. The Results section reports both the experimental measurements of melting/ablation thresholds and the optical characterization of the laser-produced nanoparticles; anisotropic nanoparticles are approximated by elliptical gold nanoparticles, and their optical response is assessed through numerical analysis. The agreement between experimental observations and theoretical predictions clarifies the underlying mechanisms and highlights the robustness of our approach. Finally, the application of these nanostructures as SERS substrates is demonstrated by detecting low concentration Rhodamine B (10−4 M), evaluating the efficiencies of the laser-introduced gold NPs in SERS. Therefore, the research integrates theory, synthesis, optical characterization, and sensing performance to provide a coherent, novel, and liquid- and chemical-free route for customizing laser-produced gold nanostructures using unfiltered fundamental harmonic of Nd:YAG laser pulses and advancing their use in analytical photonics.

Materials and methods

This section is divided into two parts: a theoretical part and an experimental part. The theoretical part discusses the mechanism of nanoparticle formation by Nd:YAG laser irradiation and provides a threshold for using the pure irradiation of first and second harmonics of a Nd:YAG laser pulse, and also a combination of them. It also details the methods used to calculate the spectrum and optical resonance of gold nanoparticles formed on glass substrates. The experimental part describes the procedures and conditions used for synthesizing gold nanoparticles through laser pulse irradiation, including the different fluences and the number of laser pulses.

Experimental

For the experimental studies, the polished borosilicate glass was purchased from the Iranian Organization for Science and Technology and used as a substrate for producing gold nanoparticles. To clean the substrate, the substrate was washed with deionized water twice to remove loose particulates. Then, it is submerged in an ethanol bath and sonicated for 5 min to remove organic contaminants. The substrate was dried in a clean, dust-free environment. Then, gold film with a thickness of 100 nm was initially deposited onto the substrate using the sputtering method by a DSR1 sputter coater model at a rate of 1 nm/sec in dimensions of 5 × 5 cm. Subsequently, the samples were exposed to the first and second harmonics of a homemade pulsed Nd:YAG laser, characterized by a pulse linewidth of 60 ns and a repetition rate of 1 Hz. A lens with a focal length of 15 cm was employed to ensure precise adjustment and control of the laser spot size on the samples. The study evaluated the effects of fluences and the number of laser pulses on the nanoparticles produced, as illustrated in the schematic diagram in Fig. 1. Also, the characteristics and properties of the applied laser radiation, and the labels of the samples are collected in Table 1. The samples were labeled based on the thin film material (Au), irradiated laser spot radius (1, 2, and 3 mm), and the number of irradiated Nd:YAG laser pulses.

To obtain preliminary radiation data and minimize trial and error, the equations governing the interaction of laser radiation with a gold thin film deposited on a glass substrate (100 nm thickness) were solved numerically for a single pulse, enabling the selection of an appropriate fluence. Accordingly, different below, near, and above the ablation threshold were selected for irradiation, and both single and multiple pulse irradiations of the Nd:YAG laser at a repetition rate of 1 Hz were employed for the experimental studies.

The laser-induced gold samples were characterized to find information regarding the morphologies and optical properties of the produced nanostructures by the pulsed Nd:YAG laser irradiations. The surface morphologies of the produced nanostructures were determined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM), model MIRA3, and the height of the nanostructures was measured with an atomic force microscope (AFM), model BIRISK. The optical properties of the laser-induced gold samples were analyzed using a diffuse reflectance spectrometer (DRS), model S-4100 SCINCO.

The potentials of the laser-induced samples for surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) were evaluated by preparing a low-concentration Rhodamine B solution (10−4 M) and recording its Raman spectrum using conventional Raman spectroscopy, as well as Raman spectroscopy employing the samples as SERS substrates. Subsequently, the efficiencies of these samples in SERS were evaluated, revealing a significant capability of certain produced samples for SERS applications.

Theoretical

The simulations consist of two main parts: the first part is related to the interaction of Nd:YAG laser beam with a thin gold layer, leading to the calculation of the melting and evaporation thresholds; and the second part focuses on investigating the optical response of the formed gold nanoparticles (elliptical gold nanoparticles) and calculating the local surface plasmon resonance wavelengths.

The written Fortran code by the G-FDTD method was employed for the simulations due to its robust capability in modeling complex geometries and also materials with intricate properties. The simulation time steps were established at 10 femtoseconds, and the spatial discretization was variable. The execution time for the Fortran code on a home computer equipped with a 4-core Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-10300H CPU operating at 2.50 GHz and 16 GB of RAM was approximately 110 min. In contrast, utilizing the supercomputer at Semnan University, which features 10 Intel Xeon processors running at 2.2 GHz and 18 GB of RAM, reduced the computation time to about 20 min.

To ensure the accuracy of the developed Fortran code, conditions consistent with those described in the reference24 were employed, and the obtained simulation results were compared with the experimental and computational findings reported in the earlier-mentioned study. The experimentally and theoretically reported threshold fluences required for vaporizing a 6 μm gold layer under second-harmonic radiation were 0.3 J/cm2 and 0.24 J/cm2, respectively. The melting threshold fluence obtained by the current Fortran code using the G-FDTD method is 0.268 J/cm2, suggesting a higher degree of concordance between the simulation results of the current study and the experimental findings from the reference24. Thereby, the validity and efficacy of our developed code are affirmed.

Optical properties of the elliptical gold nanoparticles

The localized surface plasmon resonance is an interesting optical response of plasmonic nanoparticles to a specific incident wavelength controlled by the shape, type, defect, and other properties of the NPs. These oscillations are generated on the surfaces of metal nanoparticles and are not capable of propagating. Additionally, resonance conditions are among the most important parameters for these nanoparticles. Since the resonance wavelengths of these localized surface plasmon oscillations in gold nanoparticles typically fall within the visible and NIR regions, they can be excited by directly irradiating light onto the nanoparticles.

To theoretically study the nanoparticles’ optical response, the elliptical gold nanoparticles are considered due to the shape of gold nanoparticles in the current study, by irradiating the nanosecond Nd:YAG laser pulses to the gold film. As depicted in Fig. 2, the direction of light propagation is along the z-axis. The particle cross-sections are circular in the plane perpendicular to the z-axis and elliptical in the plane containing the z-axis, respectively. The polarization of the light is assumed to be transverse (perpendicular to the z-axis). Due to the azimuthal symmetry of the nanoparticle shape around the propagation axis (z-axis), changing the light polarization direction in the x–y plane (perpendicular to the propagation axis) does not affect the behavior of the localized surface plasmon resonance.

Results and discussion

Initially, the threshold fluences of every Nd:YAG laser pulse at 532 nm, which can provide melting and ablation of a 100 nm gold film, were calculated using the Fortran code. It was done by solving the heat equation (Eq. 1) using a laser heat source (Eq. 2), and considering boundary conditions (Eq. 3). Absorption and reflection coefficients of the deposited gold film with a thickness of 100 nm obtained through the DRS of the gold film before laser irradiation.

The utilization of the fundamental harmonic of the Nd:YAG laser at 1064 nm has been previously reported for producing gold nanoparticles11. Additionally, the generation of gold nanoparticles using the second harmonic of this laser with filtration of the fundamental harmonic has been documented in the scientific literature12. While previous reports have demonstrated sequential dual-wavelength processing for bulk targets in liquid media36,37, there are no reports on using two harmonics of Nd:YAG laser together to form gold nanoparticles by irradiation of gold thin films deposited on a substrate under ambient conditions to provide plasmonic nanoparticles.

In this study, the threshold fluences for melting and evaporation are defined as the fluence at which the first point on the surface of the gold film undergoes melting and evaporation, respectively. Knowledge of these threshold values is essential for selecting appropriate fluences for irradiating the thin film. The threshold fluences of the Nd:YAG laser’s fundamental harmonic for melting and evaporation of gold film with a thickness of 100 nm are 0.14 J/cm2 and 0.38 J/cm2, respectively. Also, the threshold fluences of the second harmonic for melting and evaporation of this gold film are 0.05 J/cm2 and 0.125 J/cm2, respectively. As observed, the threshold fluence for the second harmonic (532 nm) is notably lower than that of the fundamental harmonic (1064 nm). This difference is attributed to the higher optical absorption and the lower reflectance of gold film at 532 nm compared with 1064 nm, making the second harmonic more effective for nanoparticle formation on glass substrates.

It is important to note that in typical Nd:YAG laser systems, the power ratio between the fundamental harmonic (1064 nm) and the second harmonic (532 nm) typically ranges from approximately 2:1 to 5:1. This indicates that the power output of the fundamental harmonic significantly exceeds that of the second harmonic, reflecting the efficiency limitations of the frequency conversion process. Therefore, in this study, the melting and evaporation threshold fluences at 532 nm are calculated under various conditions, including the filtered and unfiltered operation. Figure 4 illustrates the application of the second harmonic of the Nd:YAG laser under two conditions: (1) when the fundamental harmonic is filtered and (2) when the system operates without filtering, allowing different ratios of the fundamental harmonic to be present in the radiation, as is typical in standard Nd:YAG laser operation. The results demonstrate that the absence of filtering the fundamental harmonic reduces the threshold power for melting and evaporation, thereby implying may reduce the required fluence at 532 nm and enhance efficiency in producing gold nanoparticles. Therefore, producing gold nanoparticles by the Nd:YAG laser in an unfiltered operation is investigated, and the reported fluences are related to the Nd:YAG fluence at 532 nm.

Fluence of 0.48 J/cm2

In this section, the fluence of the second harmonic of Nd:YAG laser pulse is set to 0.48 J/cm2, and the effects of different numbers of laser pulses (1–50) on the morphology and optical properties of the formed surface gold nanoparticles are investigated. Field emission electron microscope (FESEM) images of the laser-induced gold nanoparticles on the gold surface with different pulse numbers ranging from 1 to 50 pulses are presented in Fig. 5. The results show that curved nanoparticles with circular cross-sections are formed by the applied Nd:YAG laser pulses with the fluence of 0.48 J/cm2. However, the dimensions and density of the formed gold nanoparticles are different. It can be observed that nanoparticles with circular cross-sections are formed using laser pulse irradiation on the glass, and no change in the morphology can be observed by changing the investigated parameters. Also, the results indicate that irradiating 35 and 50 pulses of second harmonic Nd:YAG laser to a 100 nm gold film causes the formation of large and small particles with diameters equal to (685,42) nm and (930,30) nm, respectively. The FESEM images of Au1-35 and Au1-50 samples at lower magnification are also shown in Fig. 5e,f, confirming the creation of large gold nanoparticles by 35 and 50 laser pulses on a 100 nm gold film. Additionally, the lower FESEM images of the samples, shown in the insets of Fig. 5e,f, indicate that most of the gold films were removed, and the substrates were significantly damaged. Therefore, it can be estimated that the laser-induced gold NPs are not desirable for SERS application, but it should be considered to offer a definitive opinion.

Observing the initial decreases in the average diameters of the produced gold NPs and increasing the number of formed gold NPs by increasing the pulse numbers from 1 to 20 can be explained by competition between two primary phenomena: nucleation and growth versus coalescence and ripening. At lower pulse numbers, the energy provided by each laser pulse promotes the nucleation of gold atoms into nanoparticles. Therefore, more nucleation sites are generated by increasing the pulse number, leading to the production of a greater number of smaller nanoparticles, which typically cause a decrease in the average diameter of the gold NPs. It means that they are formed rapidly before they can significantly grow in the mentioned conditions. Once the number of laser pulses reaches a special threshold value (a number between 20 and 35 pulses), the input energy can provide continuous nucleation along with coalescence and ripening, leading to the merging of smaller nanoparticles to form larger ones and growing larger particles at the expense of smaller ones due to surface energy considerations. It means that the second process is significant, ensuring the formation of gold NPs using 35 and 50 laser pulses, leading to the observed trend after the initial decrease.

To investigate the height of curvative gold nanoparticles created with a laser fluence of 0.48 J/cm2 on a thin gold layer, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was utilized, and these AFM images are presented in Fig. 6. The results indicate that an increase in the number of pulses leads to an increase in the height of the generated gold nanoparticles. However, the height of the nanoparticles is always less than their radius, which suggests that the created gold nanoparticles are not spherical but rather elliptical nanostructures or semispherical nanoparticles with a circular cross-section formed using the laser irradiation method. Furthermore, it can be observed that the most uniform height of Au NPs is in the Au1-10 sample, suggesting it as a candidate for SERS applications.

Differential reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) analysis is a real-time monitoring and non-destructive analysis that can be applied to plasmonic nanoparticles to determine the optical properties, and it is sensitive to localized plasmon resonance. Therefore, the DRS analysis was applied to the gold curvative nanoparticles created by a laser fluence of 0.48 J/cm2, and the results are presented in Fig. 7a. The results indicated that all samples have several LSPR peaks, demonstrating the formation of gold NPs by the selected method, and used fluence with the given pulse numbers of the Nd:YAG laser. Our observations are therefore consistent with anisotropic plasmonic behavior rather than with purely isotropic, spherical particles. The fluorescence enhancement and appearance of the characteristic Raman peaks of dye solution using the produced curvative gold nanoparticles during the Raman spectroscopy can be related to the increase in the electric field amplitude around gold nanoparticles, where dye molecules are present. When light interacts with gold nanoparticles, they exhibit strong plasmonic effects, leading to a significant increase in the electric field intensity near sensing molecules and enhancing the Raman signal. This enhancement allows for the detection of low concentrations of analytes compared to traditional Raman spectroscopy.

Comparing the results presented in Figs. 7, 11, and 16 shows the increasing prominence of the LSPR peak at ~ 280 nm with decreasing fluence. It can be interpreted in terms of the contribution and coupling of anisotropic plasmon modes. Our analysis indicates that the laser-processing conditions yield gold nanoparticles with approximately ellipsoidal (ellipse-like) geometry. Such anisotropic geometries engender multiple principal plasmon resonances, corresponding to the major and minor axes, which interact with the ambient medium and with each other. Under lower fluence, the relative excitation and coupling of these anisotropic plasmon modes lead to a stronger UV peak at ~ 280 nm, which is related to very small nanoparticles formed in the samples. In contrast, higher fluence tends to promote different modes, reducing the relative intensity of this particular UV peak. This interpretation aligns with established literature showing plasmonic peaks in the UV for anisotropic Au and Ag nanoparticles formed by ion implantation43, as well as for thin metal films irradiated with excimer (ArF) laser pulses13,40.

Theoretical investigation of gold ellipsoidal nanoparticles

Experimental results demonstrated the formation of curved gold nanoparticles with circular cross-sections and heights smaller than their diameters across all samples. These samples were prepared through irradiation of a gold film (thickness of 100 nm) with varying numbers of Nd:YAG laser pulses and fluences (0.053, 0.12, and 0.48 J/cm2). To gain further insight into the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) wavelengths, simulations were conducted assuming elliptical structures based on Eqs. (5) and (6), with the major axis corresponding to twice the nanoparticle radius and the other axis matching the nanoparticle height as measured by AFM analysis.

To validate our modeling approach, the results obtained for elliptical nanoparticles were compared with those corresponding to spherical nanoparticles, where the ellipsoid is transformed into a sphere (r = h/2), with the spherical gold nanoparticles reported in1. The comparison shows good agreement between the two sets of results, demonstrating the accuracy of our simulations.

Figure 18 presents the absorbed power spectra of elliptical gold nanoparticles with varying heights (c value) and diameters (d = 2r). As observed, multiple localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) peaks appear in the spectra of the ellipsoid gold NPs. The position and intensity of these peaks depend on the nanoparticle dimensions; specifically, increasing the height of elliptical nanoparticles results in higher LSPR peaks at lower wavelengths (for heights of 30 and 40 nm). Conversely, for nanoparticles with smaller heights (10 and 20 nm), the LSPR peaks shift toward longer wavelengths. Therefore, it can be concluded that each LSPR absorption peak of the samples obtained experimentally can be related to different LSPR peaks of the formed nanoparticles. The LSPR wavelengths for resonance modes 1 to 4 are approximately 380 nm, 450 nm, 560 nm, and 651 nm, respectively. These values are consistent with the experimental observations, with slight wavelength shifts attributed to variations in nanoparticle diameter. Additionally, the intensity and position of the resonance peaks are influenced by the nanoparticle size.

The focused spectrum of the elliptical gold nanoparticles with h = 10 nm is also plotted in the inset of (a) for more clarity.

Conclusion

Gold nanoparticles have unique optical properties that make them invaluable tools in analytical techniques such as Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) and plasmon-enhanced fluorescence. These properties are widely applicable in bioanalysis, environmental monitoring, and materials science, as they result from localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPR) that enhance excitation rates and improve signal-to-noise ratios. In this study, the laser-induced fabrication of gold nanostructures using Nd:YAG laser pulses was explored, focusing on the effects of laser fluence and pulse number on the morphology and optical characteristics of the formed gold nanoparticles. The results demonstrated that these parameters significantly influence the surface morphology and optical behavior of the nanostructures. Furthermore, the fabricated gold nanoparticles were employed in SERS for the detection of low-concentration RhB dye solution. Notably, the formed 3D gold NPs on the substrate with a fluence of 0.48 J/cm2 and 10 pulses, and also a fluence of 0.053 with 10, 35, and 50 pulses, facilitated the detection of characteristic Raman peaks of RhB, indicating their potential as effective substrates for SERS. Also, the formed 3D gold NPs on the substrate with fluence of 0.48 J/cm2 and 35 and 50 pulses, fluence of 0.12 J/cm2 with 20 pulses, and fluence of 0.053 J/cm2 with 35 pulses, induced high fluorescence enhancement, indicating their potential as effective substrates for fluorescence enhancement applications. These findings highlight the versatility and effectiveness of laser-fabricated gold nanostructures for advanced optical sensing and analytical applications.

The advancements demonstrated in this study open promising avenues for the development of highly tailored plasmonic nanostructures with broad applicability in nanophotonics and biosensing. Future research can explore further optimization of laser parameters, such as pulse duration, wavelength, and repetition rate, to fine-tune nanostructure morphology and enhance their optical responses. Integrating real-time feedback mechanisms during laser fabrication could improve precision and reproducibility, paving the way for scalable manufacturing processes.

Additionally, expanding the investigation to include a wider variety of substrate materials and multilayered configurations may unlock new plasmonic phenomena and increase the versatility of the fabricated nanostructures for applications like surface-enhanced spectroscopies, bioimaging, and optoelectronic devices. The combined use of advanced simulation tools, such as the G-FDTD method, with machine learning algorithms could accelerate the design cycle, enabling rapid prediction of optimal fabrication conditions for specific optical functionalities.

In terms of application, future work could focus on the integration of these nanostructures into portable sensing platforms and lab-on-a-chip devices, facilitating real-world deployment in medical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and food safety. Moreover, exploring the biocompatibility and stability of these structures under physiological conditions will be critical to transition from laboratory prototypes to commercial products.
