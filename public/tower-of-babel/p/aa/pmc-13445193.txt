Tobacco Use by Urbanization Level Among U.S. Adults, 2024
Authors: Juhan Lee, Maria A Parker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of this study was to examine rural–urban differences in the current use of tobacco products among U.S. adults using 2024 National Health Interview Survey data and the updated 2023 National Center for Health Statistics urban–rural classification scheme.MethodsThis study analyzed data from U.S. adults aged ≥18 years in the 2024 National Health Interview Survey (N=32,629). Weighted logistic regression models estimated the associations between rurality and the current use of any tobacco, cigarettes, E-cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco. A Poisson regression model assessed the number of tobacco products used, with a sensitivity analysis among adults with any tobacco use. The models adjusted for age; sex; race and ethnicity; sexual identity; region; family income-to-poverty ratio; past-year alcohol use; and, in sensitivity analyses, chronic and psychiatric conditions.ResultsRural residents had higher adjusted odds of current use of any tobacco (AOR=1.42; 95% CI=1.29, 1.56), cigarettes (AOR=1.31; 95% CI=1.17, 1.48), and smokeless tobacco (AOR=2.28; 95% CI=1.85, 2.80) than urban residents. Cigar use did not differ by urbanization level, and the association for E-cigarette use did not meet the multiple-comparison threshold. Rural residence was associated with a higher number of tobacco products used overall (adjusted prevalence rate=1.28; 95% CI=1.19, 1.39), but among adults who used any tobacco product, urbanization level was not associated with the number of products used. Among adults who used smokeless tobacco, rural residents had higher odds of daily use than urban residents (AOR=1.82; 95% CI= 1.26, 2.64).ConclusionsIn 2024, rural–urban disparities in tobacco use among U.S. adults remained concentrated in cigarettes and smokeless tobacco, supporting a focus on these products in rural surveillance, prevention, and cessation efforts.Keywords: Tobacco, nicotine, rural health, epidemiology

INTRODUCTION

Although overall tobacco use has declined in the U.S., adults living in rural areas continue to experience a disproportionate burden of tobacco-related morbidity and mortality.1 Rural adults have consistently shown a higher cigarette smoking prevalence and lower smoking quit ratios than urban adults, contributing to persistent geographic disparities in tobacco-related outcomes despite overall declines.2, 3, 4 Using the 2023 National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Urban/Rural Classification Scheme, 13.8% of U.S. residents lived in nonmetropolitan (referred to as rural in the remaining parts of this paper) areas in 2022, underscoring the importance of understanding how tobacco use continues to vary across urbanization levels in a sizable segment of the population.5

Prior national studies have shown that rural–urban differences in tobacco use are product specific rather than uniform across all tobacco products. Rural adults consistently use more cigarettes and smokeless tobacco than urban adults,2,4,6 whereas findings show little or no rural–urban difference in E-cigarette or cigar use.4,7, 8, 9 Several factors may contribute to these rural–urban differences. Rural communities may face greater exposure to procigarette and smokeless tobacco use social norms; lower tobacco control funds and associated resources; higher tobacco retailer density; and limited access to healthcare, prevention, and cessation services.3,4,7,8,10,11 These conditions may reinforce the continued use of products with long-standing cultural and commercial footholds in rural areas, particularly cigarettes and smokeless tobacco.

Recent analyses of 2023–2024 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) data have described national tobacco use prevalence in the U.S.,9 and a new NCHS report has described cigarette and E-cigarette use by urbanization level using the updated 2023 NCHS Urban-Rural Classification Scheme.12 Unadjusted analyses showed that cigarette and E-cigarette use increased with decreasing urbanization. However, less is known about whether these rural–urban differences in 2024 extend to any tobacco use, smokeless tobacco, cigars, daily use, and the number and patterns of products used, particularly in adjusted analyses with potential confounders. Because the 2024 NHIS was the first survey year to apply this updated classification, it provides an opportunity to reassess whether rural–urban disparities persist in the current tobacco product landscape. Furthermore, gaps remain in understanding rural–urban differences in polytobacco use and daily use of specific tobacco products, which might help clarify how rural–urban disparities are distributed across products and use patterns.

This study used 2024 NHIS data to examine rural–urban differences in the current use of any tobacco product, cigarettes, E-cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco as well as the number and patterns of products used and daily use of each tobacco product. This study examined whether observed rural–urban disparities were reflected in overall tobacco use prevalence, specific product use and daily use, or differences in the number and combination of products used in the 2024 tobacco product landscape. It is hypothesized that adults living in rural areas would have a higher prevalence of overall tobacco use than adults living in urban areas, with product-specific differences by tobacco type. If rural–urban inequities are driven primarily by a higher prevalence of specific products rather than broader differences across all products, public health priorities may need to be more product specific.

METHODS

Study Sample

This study analyzed the 2024 NHIS,13 an annual nationally representative survey of the U.S. civilian, non-institutionalized population conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to assess health behaviors, health conditions, and sociodemographic factors. The NHIS uses a stratified, multistage probability sampling design with weights and design variables that enable nationally representative estimation. The 2024 NHIS oversampled rural households12 and was the first survey year to apply the 2023 NCHS Urban/Rural Classification Scheme, which cannot be directly pooled with earlier NHIS data and necessitates renewed examination of rural–urban disparities. This study included all adult respondents aged ≥18 years in the 2024 sample public use file (N=32,629, response rate=47.9%).

Measures

The primary exposure was the urbanization level. Consistent with prior national surveillance reports and to facilitate interpretation, the NHIS urbanization variable was dichotomized as metropolitan versus nonmetropolitan, referring to metropolitan areas as urban and nonmetropolitan as rural throughout.6 The 2024 NHIS public-use file includes a 4-level NCHS rural–urban classification variable: (1) large central metropolitan (metropolitan statistical areas [MSAs] of 1 million or more population that contain the entire population of the largest principal city of the MSA or are completely contained in the largest principal city of the MSA or contain at least 250,000 residents of any principal city of the MSA), (2) large fringe metropolitan (MSAs of 1 million or more population that do not qualify as large central), (3) medium and small metropolitan (MSAs of 250,000 to 999,999 population and MSAs of less than 250,000 population), and (4) nonmetropolitan (micropolitan statistical areas and noncore counties).

Primary outcomes were current use of (1) any tobacco product, (2) cigarettes, (3) E-cigarettes, (4) cigars (including regular cigars, cigarillos, or filtered cigars), and (5) smokeless tobacco. Current use was defined using the NHIS item asking whether respondents now smoke or use each product every day, some days, or not at all; respondents reporting every day or some days were classified as current users. Among respondents reporting current use of each product, daily use (every day versus some days) of cigarettes, E-cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco by urbanization level was examined. In addition, the number of tobacco product categories currently used as a count outcome in the full sample (range=0–4) was examined. Among adults reporting any current tobacco use, mutually exclusive product-use patterns by urbanization level were described, and the 10 most prevalent patterns were presented.

On the basis of the literature related to tobacco use,14 covariates included age (continuous, range of 18–85 years), sex (female, male), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic other), sexual identity (straight, gay/lesbian/bisexual/something else), U.S. region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West), family income-to-poverty ratio (continuous, range=0–11), past-year alcohol use (yes, no), chronic diseases (yes, no), and psychiatric disorders (yes, no). The income-to-poverty ratio was based on U.S. Census Bureau poverty thresholds, which are updated annually for inflation and vary by family size and household composition but not by geography.15 Past-year alcohol use was used because past-30-day alcohol use was only assessed among those meeting past-year heavy drinking criteria. Chronic disease was defined as any self-reported lifetime diagnosis of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, coronary heart disease, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, cancer, or diabetes. Psychiatric disorder was defined as any self-reported lifetime diagnosis of depression or anxiety disorders.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics of study variables overall and by urbanization level were calculated, presenting unweighted counts and survey-weighted percentages. The Rao–Scott adjusted chi-square test for categorical variables and the Wald-adjusted t-test for continuous variables were used for bivariate associations between each study variable and urbanization level. Five survey-weighted logistic regression models were conducted to estimate urban–rural differences in each binary tobacco outcome and 1 survey-weighted Poisson regression model for the number of tobacco products currently used in the full sample (0–4). Poisson regression was selected owing to the limited range of the count outcome and a large proportion of zeros; as a robustness check, a negative binomial model for this outcome was conducted. Survey-weighted adjusted binomial logistic regression models were also conducted to examine daily use (versus nondaily use) by urbanization level among adult respondents who reported current use of each tobacco product. Among adult respondents who reported any current tobacco use, mutually exclusive product-use categories were created, and the 10 most prevalent patterns by urbanization level were presented.

To address concerns about overadjustment, 2 additional sets of models were conducted sequentially: (1) unadjusted models and (2) minimally adjusted models including age, sex, race/ethnicity, sexual identity, region, and family poverty. In sensitivity analyses, the adjusted models using the original 4-level urbanization variable were repeated to assess whether the findings were robust to alternative treatments of urbanization. In addition, the product-count model among adults who currently used any tobacco products (range=1–4) was repeated to determine whether rural–urban differences reflected greater use of multiple product categories among adults with existing tobacco use rather than differences in prevalences overall. Given that cigarette and E-cigarette use vary by age and urbanization level,12 interactions between urbanization level and age for each tobacco use outcome were tested by including main effects (urbanization and age) and an interaction term (urbanization × age) in 1 model, adjusted for covariates. To reduce the likelihood of Type I error across multiple prespecified outcomes, statistical significance was set at p<0.008 (0.05/6) using a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold.

All analyses incorporated NHIS sample weights and complex survey design variables, with variance estimated using Taylor series linearization,13 to generate nationally representative estimates for the 2024 U.S. adult civilian, non-institutionalized population. This study followed the STROBE guidelines. This secondary data analysis of deidentified, publicly available data was deemed exempt from IRB review.

RESULTS

Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics of the study variables by urbanization level. Among total adults in the 2024 NHIS (N=32,629; average age=48.3 years; 51.3% women; 11.8% non-Hispanic Black; 17.9% Hispanic; 5.7% sexual minority adults), 14.1% were living in rural areas. Overall, 18.9% reported any tobacco use, 9.9% reported current cigarette smoking, 7.0% reported current E-cigarette use, 3.6% reported current cigar use, and 2.5% reported current smokeless tobacco use. Among adults with any current tobacco use (n=5,884), the weighted mean number of tobacco products used was 1.2 (SD=0.5). In bivariate analyses, adults living in rural areas had a higher prevalence of any tobacco use, cigarette smoking, E-cigarette use, and smokeless tobacco use than adults living in urban areas, whereas cigar use did not differ by urbanization level.

Table 2 shows the results of the survey-weighted adjusted binomial logistic regression models. In unadjusted models, rural residence (versus urban) was associated with higher odds of current use of any tobacco product (OR=1.74, 95% CI=1.58, 1.92), cigarettes (OR=1.83, 95% CI=1.62, 2.06), E-cigarettes (OR=1.41, 95% CI=1.20, 1.65), and smokeless tobacco (OR=2.67, 95% CI=2.18, 3.27) as well as a higher number of tobacco products (prevalence rate=1.56, 95% CI=1.43, 1.70); cigar use did not differ by urbanization level (Table A.1, available online). In minimally adjusted models (i.e., adjusted only for sociodemographic factors), rural residence remained associated with current use of any tobacco product (AOR=1.35, 95% CI=1.23, 1.49), cigarettes (AOR=1.27, 95% CI=1.13, 1.43), smokeless tobacco (AOR=2.22, 95% CI=1.81, 2.73), and the number of tobacco products used (adjusted prevalence rate [aPR]=1.24, 95% CI=1.15, 1.35) (Table A.2 available online).

In the fully adjusted models adjusting for sociodemographics, alcohol use, and lifetime diagnosis of chronic disease and psychiatric disorders, rural residence (versus urban) was associated with higher odds of any tobacco use (AOR=1.42, 95% CI=1.29, 1.56), cigarette smoking (AOR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.17, 1.48), and smokeless tobacco use (AOR = 2.28, 95% CI = 1.85, 2.80) as well as a higher number of tobacco products used overall (range=0–4; aPR=1.28, 95% CI = 1.19, 1.39). The estimate for E-cigarette use was elevated (AOR=1.24, 95% CI=1.05, 1.46) but did not meet the prespecified multiple-comparison threshold, and cigar use did not differ by urbanization level (AOR=0.98, 95% CI=0.79, 1.21).

Table 3 shows adjusted binomial logistic regression results for the association between urbanization level and daily use of each tobacco product among adults with current use of that product. The analysis found rural–urban differences only in smokeless tobacco product use, indicating that among adult respondents who reported current smokeless tobacco use, rural residents were more likely to report daily smokeless tobacco use than urban residents (AOR=1.82, 95% CI=1.26, 2.64).

Findings were consistent in the sensitivity analyses. Models using the original 4-level urbanization variable showed a similar pattern, with the clearest disadvantages concentrated in rural areas (Table A.3, available online). When the analyses were restricted to adults who used any tobacco product (n=5,884), urbanization level was not associated with the number of products used (p=0.612) (Table A.4, available online), suggesting that the observed disparities were driven primarily by differences in prevalence. The negative binomial regression model for the product-count outcome yielded results nearly identical to those of the Poisson model (aPR=1.29, 95% CI=1.19, 1.39) (Table A.5, available online). The analysis observed a significant interaction between urbanization level and age only for current smokeless tobacco use (p=0.0052), indicating that the rural–urban difference in smokeless tobacco use varied by age (Table A.6, available online).

DISCUSSION

In this nationally representative study using the 2024 NHIS, adults living in rural areas had higher odds of current use of any tobacco product, cigarettes, and smokeless tobacco than those in urban areas. Rural residence was also associated with a higher number of tobacco product categories used overall. Among adults with current smokeless tobacco use, rural adults had higher odds of daily use than urban adults. The rural–urban association for smokeless tobacco use also appeared to vary by age in sensitivity analyses. In contrast, rural–urban differences in E-cigarette use did not meet the prespecified multiple-comparison threshold, and cigar use did not differ by urbanization level. The most frequently used tobacco products were similar across urban and rural adults with any tobacco product use, although cigarette use, E-cigarette use, and smokeless tobacco use were higher among rural residents. These findings indicate that rural–urban tobacco disparities in 2024 were concentrated in cigarettes and smokeless tobacco rather than being observed consistently across all tobacco products.

This study adds to recent national NHIS analyses by focusing specifically on rural–urban differences and by examining common product-use patterns and daily tobacco use. Newly published National Center for Health Statistics5 estimates using the updated 2023 NCHS urban–rural classification scheme similarly show that cigarette and E-cigarette use increases with decreasing urbanization level.12 Although recent national reports describe overall tobacco use prevalence in 2023–2024,9,12 the findings show that geographic disparities remain product specific in the current tobacco landscape. In particular, cigarettes and smokeless tobacco continue to account for the clearest rural disadvantage in the adjusted analyses, whereas E-cigarette differences do not meet the prespecified multiple-comparison threshold, and cigar use appears more similar across urbanization levels.

The persistence of higher cigarette and smokeless tobacco use in rural areas likely reflects structural and social factors that differ by geography, including weaker tobacco retail regulation, greater exposure to price promotions,16 lower cigarette taxes,17 and targeted marketing that normalizes smokeless tobacco in rural communities.18,19 Although these factors were not measured directly in NHIS, prior literature suggests that they may help explain why cigarettes and smokeless tobacco continue to show the clearest rural disadvantage. Findings indicate that smokeless tobacco remains a key contributor to rural tobacco disparities. This point is reinforced by the mutually exclusive pattern analysis, in which exclusive smokeless tobacco use ranked substantially higher in rural than in urban areas. Given the negative health risks of smokeless tobacco use, such as cancer and cardiovascular disease,20,21 this age pattern may warrant further study to clarify whether smokeless tobacco use is especially concentrated among older rural adults.

This study also observed that rural residence was associated with a higher number of products used overall but not among adults with current use, which is consistent with previous findings showing comparable multiple tobacco use among urban and rural adults.4 These findings suggest that geographic disparities might be driven more by differences in prevalence than by product-use patterns once tobacco use is established. This interpretation is also consistent with recent NCHS estimates showing an increase in cigarette use and a similar increase in E-cigarette use with decreasing urbanization level.12 The product-pattern rankings support this interpretation: in both rural and urban areas, the most common patterns were exclusive cigarette use and exclusive E-cigarette use, but exclusive smokeless tobacco use was more prominent in rural areas, whereas exclusive cigar use was more prominent in urban areas. This study also found higher odds of daily smokeless tobacco use among rural residents who currently used smokeless tobacco than among urban residents, suggesting greater persistence or intensity of smokeless tobacco use. This might reflect sustained use of certain products over time rather than broader differences in polytobacco use complexity among rural populations.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Tobacco use was self-reported and was subject to reporting bias. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. NHIS excludes institutionalized populations, which may limit generalizability. The urbanization variable is county based and may not fully capture within-county heterogeneity, so some individuals living in nominally metropolitan counties may reside in highly rural settings and vice versa. In addition, newer tobacco products, such as nicotine pouches, and unmeasured confounders not available in NHIS data, such as exposure to tobacco marketing, tobacco harm perceptions, cannabis use, and other substance use, could not be examined. In addition, some covariates were measured on different time scales than the current tobacco use outcomes. Finally, because these analyses used 2024 data, the tobacco product use and cessation (e.g., telehealth) and regulatory (e.g., introduction of nicotine pouches) landscape may have changed since the survey period.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides a timely portrait of rural–urban tobacco disparities in the first NHIS year using the updated 2023 NCHS urban–rural classification scheme. Future research should examine the motivations, access, and social contexts that may underlie persistent rural disparities, particularly for cigarettes and smokeless tobacco use. Continued surveillance of emerging products, including nicotine pouches, will also be important as the tobacco landscape evolves.22, 23, 24, 25 Expanding the coverage of smoking cessation treatments and medications for rural residents by leveraging telehealth and mobile health might be helpful in reducing rural tobacco-related inequities.26 Efforts to reduce rural tobacco-related inequities may be most effective when focused on the products for which rural disparities remain the largest, such as product-specific communication strategies (e.g., educating people about the risks of cigarette and smokeless tobacco use, providing tailored campaigns for rural areas).
