Prevalence and risk factors of Mycoplasma genitalium, Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Trichomonas vaginalis infections in pregnant women in seven hospitals in N’Djamena, Chad: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mahamat Mustapha Anouar, Boris Emmanuel Djoumsie Gomseu, Nandita Sharma, Sana Afreen, Tenzin Tsephel, Djamal Hachim, Ngam Daita, Annour Bechir Ahmat, Mahamat Mouctar Abdelkerim, Aboubakar Hassan Adam, Adam Mahamat Dahabaye, Chatté Adawaye, Foumsou Lhagadang, Jules-Roger Kuiate, Sunil Sethi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesMycoplasma genitalium, Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Trichomonas vaginalis are sexually transmitted pathogens that are highly prevalent in developing countries and are strongly associated with pregnancy complications. In Chad, screening for these sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in pregnant women is based solely on patient-reported symptoms, even though these infections are frequently asymptomatic. This study aims to determine the prevalence of M. genitalium, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis infections, as well as their associated risk factors.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we recruited pregnant women attending antenatal clinics at seven hospitals in N’Djamena. Endocervical swabs were collected, and DNA was extracted. Infections were diagnosed using PCR. Risk factors were identified using a structured questionnaire, and associations were assessed using logistic regression.ResultsA total of 525 pregnant women were enrolled, of whom 78.5% resided in urban areas, with a mean age of 25.16±5.54 years. Overall, 23.99% of the study population were diagnosed with at least one STI. The individual prevalence of M. genitalium, N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis and T. vaginalis infections was 13.33%, 5.14%, 0.95% and 4.57%, respectively. Coinfections were low, with M. genitalium-T. vaginalis at 0.95%, M. genitalium-N. gonorrhoeae at 0.38% and other combinations at 0.19% each. Women residing in rural areas had nearly two times the odds of M. genitalium infection compared with urban residents (OR=1.98), indicating a higher risk. Age<25 years (OR=1.76), employment outside the home (OR=1.70), lack of STI awareness (OR=6.74) and no prior history of M. genitalium infection (OR=1.71) were also associated with significantly increased risk.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates a high prevalence of STIs among pregnant women in Chad, underscoring the need for systematic screening rather than solely relying on syndromic management.

Introduction

Pregnancy induces significant immunological changes that increase susceptibility to infections in both the expectant mother and the fetus.1 Therefore, it is essential for pregnant women to adopt and maintain a healthy lifestyle to minimise the risk of contracting sexually transmitted infections (STIs) during this critical period. Pregnancy involves substantial hormonal, anatomical, physiological and immunological changes, with alterations in cervical immunity identified as potential pathways that increase a woman’s susceptibility to STIs.2 3 Pregnant women who contract STIs are at risk of miscarriage, stillbirth and preterm birth.14 These infections can also pass from mother to child, producing significant neonatal issues, including low birth weight, blindness, death, prematurity, sepsis, neonatal conjunctivitis and ongoing disabilities.46

The pathogens commonly involved in STIs in pregnant women in low-income countries include Chlamydia trachomatis, Mycoplasma genitalium, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Trichomonas vaginalis.4 6 7 In low-income countries, STI diagnosis and management are typically syndromic due to financial constraints and inadequate infrastructure, leading to low specificity and sensitivity, which often results in inappropriate and ineffective treatments.4 6 In Chad, M. genitalium, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis are not included in the antenatal care (ANC) protocol for pregnant women. Although screening for C. trachomatis during the third trimester is recommended, it is not routinely or systematically implemented in clinical practice, despite the presence of significant risk factors for STIs, such as low education, taboos and stigma, limited access to health services, low condom use, poverty, social inequalities and prostitution, in a context of high reproductive activity. With a population of around 20 million, Chad is indeed characterised by the youth of its population, with a median age at marriage of 15.9 years and a total fertility rate of 6.3 children per woman.8 Furthermore, of the 52% of girls who are married by the age of 16 years, 71% already have a child by the age of 19.810 The failure to take account of these STIs in pregnancy monitoring has led to a lack of awareness of the epidemiological status of these infections in pregnant women, although a few studies on the prevalence of STIs have been published in recent years.1012 This study aims to determine the prevalence and associated risk factors of M. genitalium, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis infections among pregnant women in N’Djamena.

Material and methods

Study area

N’Djamena, the capital of Chad, is located in the centre-west of the country (12°8′N, 15°2′E), covering an area of 395 km² and home to approximately 1 418 854 inhabitants across 10 districts.13 14 It is a port city at the confluence of the Logone and Chari rivers, forming a cross-border urban area with Kousséri (Cameroon), the capital of the Logone-et-Chari subdivision.11 14 Healthcare in N’Djamena comprises modern private clinics and under-resourced public facilities, facing challenges in quality, accessibility, infrastructure and staffing. Overcrowding and high out-of-pocket costs remain major concerns,15 with roughly 630 000 live births annually and only 158 hospitals nationwide providing neonatal care. In the country, only 18% (29/158) of hospitals offer Emergency Obstetric and Neonatal Care (EmONC) services, of which 25 provide comprehensive EmONC and 4 provide basic EmONC. Prenatal care in Chad follows the WHO model, with a minimum of four recommended ANC visits.16 The ANC protocol implemented in Chad largely adheres to the TORCH panel, which encompasses screening for toxoplasmosis, other infections (including HIV and hepatitis B), rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex virus and syphilis.17 Additionally, screening for C. trachomatis, particularly during the third trimester, is recommended due to its potential adverse effects on maternal and neonatal health. Nonetheless, the availability and quality of comprehensive ANC services may differ significantly between healthcare facilities, primarily due to disparities in resource allocation and infrastructural limitations.15 16

Study design

Selection of hospital

This cross-sectional study was conducted from January to December 2022 in seven hospitals across N’Djamena, Chad, including the University Hospital Mother and Child Centre, Union Hospital, Chad-China Friendship Hospital, King Faisal Hospital, Sultan Kachallah Kasser Hospital, Notre Dame des Apôtres Hospital and Alkher Health Centre. The selection of these facilities was driven by two key criteria: first, the high ANC attendance rates exhibited by these facilities, and second, their capacity to serve as a representative sample of the diversity of maternal health service providers within the city. The selection encompassed national public hospitals (eg, the University Hospital Mother and Child Centre and Chad-China Friendship Hospital), provincial hospitals (eg, the Union Hospital and Sultan Kachallah Kasser Hospital), faith-based and private institutions (eg, Notre Dame des Apôtres Hospital and King Faisal Hospital) and a primary health centre (Alkher Health Centre). This ensured the inclusion of women from a wide range of socioeconomic backgrounds and coverage of all 10 districts of N’Djamena. This inclusive approach enhanced the contextual relevance of the study findings and provided a strong foundation for understanding maternal health dynamics in N’Djamena.

Sample size calculation

In the absence of prior data on the prevalence of STIs in pregnant women in Chad, the expected prevalence was estimated based on studies from Nigeria,18 a neighbouring country sharing a border with Chad and having comparable health and demographic characteristics. One such study reported a prevalence of 18.2%, which was adopted for the calculation. Using Cochran’s formula for cross-sectional studies: n=Z2×p×(1−p)/d2.

With a 95% confidence level (Z=1.96), an expected prevalence of p=0.182 and a margin of error d=0.05, the minimum required sample size was estimated at approximately 229 participants. However, to strengthen statistical power and improve the representativeness of the findings, a total of 525 pregnant women were ultimately enrolled from seven hospitals across N’Djamena.

Participant inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were pregnant women, as confirmed by their physician, aged 15–40 years at the time of recruitment. All participants were required to provide written, signed informed consent (or fingerprints if unable to sign); for minors, assent was also obtained in addition to parental or guardian consent. Participants had to be in good general health at the initial examination, defined as the absence of serious acute medical conditions.

Exclusion criteria included current antibiotic treatment or exposure to antibiotics within the previous 15 days, cervical dilation or amniotic fluid leakage at the time of sampling. Participants were also excluded if the sample quality for PCR analysis was deemed inadequate by laboratory staff due to insufficient volume, visible blood contamination or concerns about collection accuracy (eg, incorrect swab insertion). Finally, participants with incomplete questionnaires or missing responses to sociodemographic or behavioural items were excluded.

Recruitment of participants

From January to December 2022, 785 pregnant women were initially approached to participate in the study. Of these, 17 declined to participate, citing religious (Muslim) restrictions or purely personal reasons. Ultimately, 768 women provided their consent. To ensure the validity and safety of the study, exclusion criteria were applied to the 768 women who consented to participate. Of these, 25 were excluded due to incomplete sociodemographic and sexual behaviour information. Of the remaining 743 participants, 80 with cervical dilation and 71 receiving antibiotic treatment were excluded, reducing the sample to 592 participants. Finally, the exclusion of 67 samples that were inappropriate for PCR analysis (owing to insufficient volume and doubt about the quality of the sample due to an incorrectly inserted swab to ensure the accuracy of PCR analyses) led to a final number of 525 participants included in the study. Collection of sociodemographic information

Data were collected using a standardised, anonymous questionnaire (see online supplemental material), and each participant was assigned a unique identification code. Data entry was performed daily into an Excel database, with strict cross-checking to ensure that no participant was recruited more than once. Care was taken to verify that the same individual was not enrolled again during a subsequent antenatal consultation.

Endocervical samples collection and PCR analysis

Endocervical samples were collected using sterile swabs with an unlubricated speculum, stored in saline at −80°C and processed for DNA extraction using the Chelex protocol. Infections with M. genitalium, C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae were detected by real-time PCR, using previously published primers, with a cycle threshold value of ≤37 considered positive.1921 T. vaginalis was identified through conventional PCR, with TVK3/TVK7 (a pair of oligonucleotides targeting a repetitive genomic sequence specific to T. vaginalis) which produced a 300 bp fragment visible on agarose gel.22 We included both positive and negative controls (full methodology is available in the online supplemental material).

Statistical analysis

To assess the relationship between sociodemographic variables and STI status, univariate analyses were first conducted. Variables with a p value<0.20 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. Model selection was performed using a backward stepwise approach based on the Akaike Information Criterion to identify the most parsimonious model. Interaction terms and multicollinearity were assessed, and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. ORs with 95% CIs were used to determine the degree of association between the infection and sociodemographic parameters (p≤0.05). The sociodemographic data and sexual behaviours were collected using a structured questionnaire administered by trained midwives during prenatal consultations in N’Djamena. The questionnaire included closed-ended questions on area of residence (urban: >10 000 inhabitants and rural: <10 000 inhabitants), age (in years, categorised as <25 and ≥25 years), occupation outside the home (yes/no), marital regime (monogamy, polygamy or not applicable), gravidity (primigravida: first pregnancy and multigravida: ≥2 pregnancies), number of sexual partners (one/more than one), use of hormonal contraceptives (yes/no) and knowledge of STIs (general and specific to M. genitalium, T. vaginalis and N. gonorrhoeae). The history of M. genitalium infection was self-reported or obtained from medical records. Infections with M. genitalium, C. trachomatis, T. vaginalis and N. gonorrhoeae were diagnosed by PCR on cervicovaginal samples. The questionnaire was designed to be clear and adapted to the local context, with predefined categories to facilitate analysis. Covariates were selected for their epidemiological relevance in studying STIs in pregnant women. All these analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) V.19 software.

Ethical considerations

Patients and the public were not involved in the design or conduct of this study. The questionnaire was developed with input from local midwives to ensure cultural appropriateness for pregnant women in N’Djamena. There are no specific plans to disseminate results directly to study participants; however, findings have been shared with healthcare providers at the participating hospitals to inform ANC practices. All patients with positive PCR test results were directed to their respective ANC professionals for suitable management and treatment as per national standards. Participants in the study were not paid for their contribution.

Patient and public involvement statement

While patients were not directly engaged with developing the study aim or objectives, their perspectives informed critical points in the study. The questionnaire used to collect parts of the sociodemographic and behavioural data was adjusted following discussions with local midwives and other health staff to address cultural concerns and as part of the analysis. The study team ensured throughout the study that data collection was acceptable and respectful of the participants’ needs. Furthermore, it is our intention to share the study findings with study participants and other stakeholders through antenatal clinics, community health centres and local meetings. Layperson summary reports will also be published in layperson’s language and local dialects to engage audiences.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 525 pregnant women were included in the study. Of these, 78.5% resided in urban areas, and the mean age was 25.16±5.54 years. Most participants were married (90.1%), with 70.1% living in monogamous households. Detailed sociodemographic data, including education level, occupation and STI knowledge, are presented in table 1. Most of them were aged >25 years (50.6%), and 65.7% of them were Muslims. Regarding education, 15.4% were illiterate, while 34.4% had secondary education. Unemployed participants were up to 72.8% with housewives being the predominant group. Almost all women were married (90.1%) and in monogamous households (70.1%). A large majority of the women (84.1%) did not use condoms. Up to 57.9% had general knowledge about STIs, with only a small percentage aware of specific infections like C. trachomatis (3.6%) and M. genitalium (1.1%) (table 1).

Prevalence of C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, M. genitalium and T. vaginalis infections in the study population

The overall prevalence of single or coinfections was 23.99%. Specifically, the prevalence rates were 13.33% for M. genitalium, 5.14% for N. gonorrhoeae, 0.95% for C. trachomatis and 4.57% for T. vaginalis. In general, coinfection rates were rare, with only 11 cases identified (2.09%). The combinations were as follows: M. genitalium-N. gonorrhoeae (2 cases, 0.38%), M. genitalium-C. trachomatis (1 case, 0.19%), M. genitalium-T. vaginalis (5 cases, 0.95%), N. gonorrhoeae-C. trachomatis (1 case, 0.19%), N. gonorrhoeae-T. vaginalis (1 case, 0.19%) and C. trachomatis-T. vaginalis (1 case, 0.19%).

Sociodemographic parameters associated with M. genitalium, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed a statistically significant positive association between M. genitalium infection and certain sociodemographic characteristics. Women residing in rural areas had nearly two times the odds of M. genitalium infection compared with urban residents (OR=1.98), indicating a higher risk. Age<25 years (OR=1.76), employment outside the home (OR=1.70), lack of STI awareness (OR=6.74) and no prior history of M. genitalium infection (OR=1.71) were also associated with significantly increased risk. No significant associations were observed with other sociodemographic variables (table 2).

Discussion

STIs during pregnancy pose significant risks, including miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth and various neonatal complications.14 The prevalence of M. genitalium (13.33%) observed in this study is comparable to rates reported in Zambia (12.6%) and other sub-Saharan regions (approximately 13.5%). This high prevalence likely reflects socioeconomic and healthcare disparities, as many infections are asymptomatic and thus remain undetected.23 24 These findings reflect the high STI burden in sub-Saharan Africa, where risk factors such as multiple sexual partnerships, inconsistent condom use and limited access to sexual health education drive ongoing transmission of M. genitalium and other STIs.25 Mahlangu et al26 found that M. genitalium prevalence is significantly lower in developed countries (1%) than in developing countries (3.9%), suggesting that socioeconomic factors and healthcare access influence disease distribution. Moreover, Ngobese and Abbai27 noted that pregnant women are more susceptible to STIs than non-pregnant women. This increased susceptibility may explain the higher prevalence of M. genitalium and N. gonorrhoeae in this study compared with Nodjikouambaye et al11, who studied non-pregnant adult female volunteers in N’Djamena. The high prevalence in this study may also stem from the asymptomatic nature of these infections in pregnant women, as physiological changes, such as altered vaginal discharge and urinary patterns, often mask symptoms, making infections harder to detect.1 2 28 This higher prevalence could also be attributed to the lack of routine monitoring of these STIs in pregnant women, even though these infections are associated with pregnancy complications.29 30 It is worth noting that routine screening for M. genitalium is not currently recommended for the general population or for asymptomatic individuals, including pregnant women. This is reflected in several national and international guidelines, including those from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and WHO, primarily due to insufficient evidence that treatment improves clinical outcomes and the significant challenges in management.31 32 These challenges include the high prevalence of azithromycin resistance and the limited therapeutic options available during pregnancy, as fluoroquinolones and doxycycline are contraindicated.33 34 This cautious approach is further justified by the limited and inconsistent evidence regarding the clinical significance of asymptomatic M. genitalium infections. While some observational studies have reported associations with adverse reproductive outcomes, such findings have not been consistently confirmed by prospective studies.3335 Therefore, current recommendations restrict testing to specific clinical contexts, such as persistent or recurrent non-gonococcal urethritis, suspected pelvic inflammatory disease or a confirmed infection in a sexual partner.32 34 35 Given the evolving understanding of this pathogen and its potential implications for maternal and neonatal health, there is a clear need for more robust evidence to inform future screening strategies and guidelines, particularly in high-risk or vulnerable populations. It is also important to note that reliable detection of M. genitalium relies primarily on nucleic acid amplification tests, which pose a significant challenge for screening, especially in Chad, where antenatal consultation coverage is around 50%.36 37

Residence in a rural area, age<25 years, professional occupation outside the home and lack of knowledge about M. genitalium infection were significantly associated with M. genitalium infection. The vulnerability of pregnant women in rural areas may be linked to low socioeconomic conditions, limited healthcare access, inadequate sexual education, risky practices and cultural stigma, along with healthcare facilities often lacking equipment and staff, further worsened by taboos, traditional care and customary beliefs.37 38 The exposure of young women could be explained by the fact that certain STIs prefer the specialised epithelial cells of the cervix, which are more exposed in young women.2 39 It can also be argued that young women are inexperienced and often more likely to explore their sexuality and have multiple sexual partners. Furthermore, younger women may have had limited access to reproductive health services in their first pregnancy, while employed women might experience greater mobility, which can increase their risk of acquiring STIs.40 Furthermore, the finding that ‘no previous infection by M. genitalium’ was a significant risk factor, derived from self-reported data in a context of non-routine screening, suggests that affected individuals may have been experiencing a primary infection or highlights the inherent difficulty in accurately recalling past asymptomatic infections.

The coinfection rate in this study was found to be low, with the most prevalent coinfection being that of M. genitalium and T. vaginalis. Coinfections of STIs are the result of mucosal inflammation, immune disruption, risky sexual behaviours and social factors.41 42 The low rate in this area may be explained by the syndromic STI management in N’Djamena, which provides extensive treatment for symptoms and has been shown to reduce coinfections.41 Moreover, the majority of participants were married (90.1%), a factor that is likely to have reduced their exposure to multiple partners and lowered the risk of coinfection.42 T. vaginalis has been observed to promote M. genitalium coinfection by causing inflammation that weakens mucosal defences and may protect mycoplasmas from immune attacks.43 44

The prevalence of N. gonorrhoeae (5.14%) observed in this study among pregnant women exceeds both the global average (1.85%) and the regional estimate for Africa (3.53%), placing it at the higher end of the sub-Saharan range (2%–7%).45 This elevated rate is probably driven by contextual factors, such as young maternal age, socioeconomic vulnerability, gender inequality and limited access to healthcare conditions, commonly encountered in low-resource settings like Chad.46

T. vaginalis is moderately prevalent among pregnant women in N’Djamena, with an infection rate of 4.57%. This falls within the low-to-mid range reported across sub-Saharan Africa, where rates vary from 3% to over 17%, depending on the region, population and diagnostic methods used.47 This prevalence is likely driven by factors such as young maternal age, low socioeconomic status, coinfections (eg, HIV) and poor hygiene.4749

The STIs identified in this study were predominantly asymptomatic. The most significant of these were T. vaginalis and M. genitalium, accounting for 91.6% and 80% of asymptomatic cases, respectively. These figures are consistent with those reported by Van Gerwen et al50, who found that T. vaginalis remains asymptomatic in 70%–85% of pregnant women. The asymptomatic nature of T. vaginalis infection during pregnancy is attributed to a combination of pregnancy-induced immune modulation, minimal local inflammation and diagnostic limitations.51 During pregnancy, immune responses are naturally downregulated to promote foetal tolerance, which reduces the inflammatory response typically triggered by T. vaginalis. Consequently, many infected women do not exhibit classical symptoms, making routine detection difficult without molecular screening.52 In contrast, while M. genitalium also demonstrated a high asymptomatic rate in this cohort (80%), this figure is slightly lower than the 90.6% reported by Schröder et al23 among pregnant women in Zimbabwe. M. genitalium is known to cause chronic, mild infections due to its ability to induce a limited inflammatory response.52 53 In pregnant women, the pathogen can persist in the reproductive tract by evading immune detection and initiating subtle cellular changes, such as swelling of the fallopian tube cilia and reduced ciliary motility, primarily mediated by elevated interleukin-6 secretion.54 Furthermore, M. genitalium causes minimal epithelial damage, contributing to the lack of significant clinical manifestations.55 These characteristics mean that, if present, symptoms are often misattributed to normal physiological changes in pregnancy, delaying diagnosis and treatment.

Limitations

The present study is not without its limitations. The data on sexual behaviour and risk factors were self-reported, which renders them susceptible to social desirability bias and recall bias. Notwithstanding the implementation of suitable precautions, it is possible that some infections may have remained undetected. This is due to a low bacterial load or DNA degradation, which is associated with delays between sample collection and PCR analysis. Participants were recruited exclusively from urban hospital settings in N’Djamena. Consequently, this may limit the generalisability of the findings to rural populations or other regions of Chad.

Conclusions

Common curable STIs such as M. genitalium, N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis and T. vaginalis infections were prevalent among pregnant women in N’Djamena and are associated with risk factors such as rural residence, age<25 years and occupation outside the home. Although further studies with larger samples are needed to allow better assessment, these results suggest that special attention should be paid to STI surveillance in pregnant women. This monitoring should be integrated into routine monitoring, rather than being limited to the syndromic management of patients.
