A comparative study of clonogenic potential of primary cells from dental pulp and gingiva in an individual
Authors: Raina Basu Roy, Emmanuel D. S. Azariah, C Deepak, Alan M. Punnoose, Viswanathan Paramesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Regenerative medicine is an emerging field that combines biotechnology with aspects of medicine, cell and molecular biology, materials science and bioengineering to regenerate, repair or replace tissues. Since 1990, growth factors and platelet concentrates have been considered novel and have been widely used in oral and maxillofacial surgery. In the following years, biomaterials, as well as various types of scaffolds and autologous tissues, were increasingly used. Stem cells are defined as special cells that have the capacity to both self-renew and give rise to a cell population that can be differentiated.Aim:The aim of this study was to investigate and compare the yield of primary cells from dental pulp and gingival tissue. This study was designed to investigate the growth potential of dental pulp tissue and gingival tissue by assessing the colony-forming units.Materials and Methods:Primary cells were isolated from dental pulp and gingival tissues collected from patients undergoing third molar extraction. Colony-forming unit (CFU) assay and scratch wound assay were performed to evaluate clonogenicity and proliferative potential.Results:Gingival stem cells demonstrated faster proliferation and higher colony-forming efficiency compared to dental pulp stem cells. Gingival stem cells also showed quicker wound closure in the scratch wound assay.Conclusion:Gingival tissue may serve as a more accessible and efficient source of stem cells for regenerative applications due to its rapid growth and enhanced clonogenic potential.

INTRODUCTION

Mesenchymal stem cells are a typical example of multipotent cells.[1] They are loosely packed, unspecialized mesodermal cells set in a gelatinous stroma that can give rise to connective tissue, bone, cartilage and the circulatory and lymphatic systems. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were first isolated and characterized from bone marrow (BMSCs) by Friedenstein et al. in 1974.[2] Mesenchymal cells are pluripotent cells that can differentiate into different types of cells (osteogenic, chondrogenic and adipogenic). Recently, orofacial and dental tissues have acquired interest as a further accessible source of mesenchymal stem cells since the oral area is rich in MSCs.[3]

Currently, five different human dental stem cells have been isolated and characterized: dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), stem cells from exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED), periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), stem cells from apical papilla (SCAP) and dental follicle progenitor cells.[4567] Human gingiva helps in the maintenance of oral health and shows a unique foetal-like scarless healing process after wounding.[891011] The gingival tissue is an accessible source from the oral cavity; it is often resected during general dental treatments and is treated as a biomedical waste. Gingival connective tissue is a reservoir of MSCs that could be used in regenerative procedures based on tissue engineering. Gingival primary cells exhibit clonogenicity, self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capacity, and possess stem cell-like properties.[12131415]

This study will help health professionals in selecting the optimal dental MSC source for regenerative medicine. Compared to all the different dental tissues, gingival tissue is abundant, easily accessible, and associated with minimal donor site morbidity. The aim of this study was to investigate and compare the yield of primary cells from dental pulp and gingival tissue. The objective of this study is to isolate primary cells from dental pulp and gingiva of the same individual and to identify clonogenic potential of primary cells from dental pulp versus gingiva by assessing CFU and scratch wound assay.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

An observational study was conducted in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Faculty of Dental Sciences at Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (SRIHER) between July 2018 and July 2019. The study cohort comprised patients aged 18-40 years undergoing elective tooth extraction surgeries. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all individuals. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee for student projects of Sri Ramachandra Medical College and Research Institute (Reference Number: CSP/18/APR/68/109).

Standard operating protocol for sample collection

Healthy individuals aged 18 to 35 years with no clinical evidence of dental caries were recruited for tissue harvesting. Donor patients performed a preoperative oral rinse with 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthwash, followed by the administration of local anaesthesia tailored to the specific tooth or gingival site targeted for removal. Third molar teeth and the attached gingival tissues were harvested at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, SRIHER.

Tissue transport protocol

The extracted teeth and attached gingival tissues were immediately immersed in a specialized transport medium and transferred to the laboratory for downstream processing.

Monolayer culture and expansion

The seeded flasks were incubated undisturbed under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2, 100% humidity) for 7 to 10 days to allow the cells to adhere to the substratum. Non-adherent cells were removed by washing the monolayers twice with 2 ml of PBS, and fresh growth medium was replenished. These primary adherent cells were designated as passage 0 (P0) cells. The culture medium was replenished every third day until the monolayers achieved 70–80% confluency [Note: the percentage of the flask surface area covered by the growing cells].

Cell trypsinization and passaging

Upon reaching a minimum of 70% confluency, the cell monolayers were washed twice with 2 ml of PBS. The adherent cells were enzymatically detached by adding 1 ml of trypsin (trypsinization). The enzymatic activity of trypsin was neutralized by adding 3 ml of complete growth medium. The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 minutes. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 2 ml of complete growth medium. This 2 ml cell suspension was divided into four equal aliquots of 500 μl each:

Scratch wound assay

Cell migration capacity was assessed using an in vitro scratch assay. Cells were seeded into 2-cm culture plates containing proliferation growth medium and incubated at 37°C under 5% CO2 [Figure 4]. After 24 hours of incubation to allow monolayer formation, a linear wound (scratch) was mechanically introduced into the monolayer using a sterile 10 μl pipette tip. To monitor cell migration and track the kinetics of gap closure, microscopic images were captured at defined time intervals (0, 3 and 6 hours) using an inverted microscope until complete closure of the wound area was achieved.

RESULTS

Out of the total patient pool presenting to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at SRIHER, 17 individuals met the specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, among whom 12 patients provided informed consent and were enrolled in the study. The study population exhibited an equal sex distribution, comprising 50% males and 50% females, where six participants (50%) were under 20 years of age and the remaining six participants (50%) were over 25 years of age, establishing an overall mean age of 23.45 years for the study cohort. The mean yield of harvested dental pulp tissue was 25.03 mg, spanning a range of 7.00 to 102.50 mg. Out of the 12 primary samples collected, variable culture success was observed due to contamination and attachment limitations; specifically, one sample failed to exhibit initial proliferation due to unsuccessful cell attachment, three samples successfully reached passage 1 (P1) before subsequent expansion was arrested due to microbial contamination, and another three samples successfully progressed to passage 2 (P2) before expansion was terminated by contamination. Ultimately, exactly five paired samples of dental pulp and gingival tissue harvested concurrently from the same individual demonstrated successful, uncontaminated growth up to passage 3 (P3), and these five paired donor samples were utilized for all downstream functional evaluations, including the colony-forming unit (CFU) assay and the scratch wound assay.

Distinct variations in proliferation kinetics were observed between the two stem cell populations, as the mean duration required to expand dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) from passage 0 (P0) to passage 3 (P3) was 17 days, with a range of 15 to 20 days, whereas gingival stem cells (GSCs) required a mean of only 8 days, with a range of 6 to 10 days, to reach P3. Furthermore, the GSC populations exhibited rapid proliferation, consistently achieving approximately 80% confluency [Note: surface area coverage] every 2 days. To evaluate self-renewal capacity in the colony-forming unit assay, 100 individual cells were seeded per culture plate and monitored over a 14-day incubation period, revealing that DPSCs formed a mean of 11 colonies per plate, with individual values ranging from 4 to 16 colonies, while GSCs exhibited a higher clonogenic potential, yielding a mean of 21 colonies per plate, with a narrow range of 20 to 25 colonies. Finally, the scratch wound assay evaluating in vitro collective cell migration revealed notable differences in the migratory rates required to achieve complete boundary closure, where DPSCs required a mean of 11 hours to achieve complete wound closure, with a documented range of 10 to 13 hours, whereas GSCs demonstrated accelerated migratory kinetics, achieving complete wound closure in a mean duration of 8 hours, with a range of 7 to 9 hours [Table 1].

Statistical analysis

All experimental procedures were performed in duplicate (n = 2), with quantitative data expressed as the mean + standard deviation, and intergroup statistical comparisons were evaluated via a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using OpenEpi software (Version 3.01, updated 2013/04/06), where a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Morphologically, both dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) and gingival stem cells (GSCs) demonstrated similar fibroblast-like characteristics. To evaluate the fibroblastic colony-forming ability of the isolated mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), colony-forming units (CFUs) were calculated based on 100-cell dilutions, revealing that the overall colony-forming efficiency of CFU-F was greater in gingival tissue than in dental pulp tissue after a 14-day observation period [Table 2], with the data demonstrating a statistically significant increase in CFU formation within the GSC cohort (P < 0.05). Additionally, the comparative proliferation and migratory dynamics between DPSCs and GSCs investigated using an in vitro scratch wound assay corroborated these findings, demonstrating a clear increase in proliferative and migratory activity in the GSC population relative to the DPSC population [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were first isolated from human dental pulp (DPSCs) by Gronthos et al.[8] in the year 2000.[67] Among the various oral tissue-derived stem cell populations, human dental pulp primary cells have been extensively studied due to their profound clinical potential. Prior literature has robustly demonstrated the regenerative efficacy and multipotent characteristics of MSCs derived from oral tissues.[123456789101112131415] Specifically, dental pulp stem cells and gingival-derived mesenchymal stem cells (GSCs) have been widely investigated owing to their ease of accessibility, robust proliferative capacity and prospective utility in regenerative medicine. The findings of the present study align with earlier reports documenting enhanced proliferative and clonogenic properties in gingival-derived cells relative to other oral tissue-derived stem cell subsets.[111213] These specific cells offer the distinct advantages of convenient anatomical accessibility and minimal donor-site harvesting morbidity. Nonetheless, donor variability and the precise anatomical site of stem cell extraction remain critical determinants of stem cell quality, which is essential for ensuring successful therapeutic outcomes.[16171819] Consequently, isolated cell lines can be systematically compared across various biological activities, including proliferation kinetics and colony-forming unit (CFU) efficiency.[202122232425] Recent comprehensive reviews and updated harvesting methodologies continue to emphasize the significant translational potential of these oral stem cell subpopulations.[26272829]

The current study was designed to investigate the feasibility of isolating primary cells from the dental pulp and gingiva anatomical regions that are readily accessible during routine oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures. To eliminate potential microbial contamination from the oral cavity, the protocol strategically selected fully impacted, completely bone-buried teeth that had no prior exposure to the oral environment. Splitting the teeth along their long axis (mesiodistally) proved to be technically demanding; however, this approach facilitated the harvesting of a significantly greater volume of pulp tissue compared to conventional cross-sectioning at the cementoenamel junction (CEJ). The study cohort comprised 12 paired gingival tissue samples and 12 dental pulp tissue samples harvested from healthy individuals. Healthy patients undergoing non-carious third molar extractions for orthodontic indications or the prophylactic removal of impacted teeth were included. Conversely, patients presenting with or without systemic diseases or infections, or those undergoing extractions due to dental caries, crown or root fractures, severe attrition, chronic infections (such as pulpitis, periapical granuloma or periodontal infections) or prior root canal therapy were strictly excluded.

Both the third molar and the attached gingival tissue were obtained from the same donor during a single clinical appointment under standard surgical protocols and informed consent. Following extraction, the tissues were immediately transferred to the laboratory in a sterile transport medium, allowing the simultaneous extraction of primary cells possessing mesenchymal stem cell potential from both niches. While the enzymatic digestion and culture media formulations employed in this study were identical to the protocol described by Ioannis Angelopoulos,[25303132] the traditional method of sectioning transversely at the CEJ level was replaced with a longitudinal, mesiodistal split. This modification successfully minimized the wastage of vital pulp tissue, resulting in a higher quantifiable tissue weight. Interestingly, the yield of pulp tissue harvested from patients under 20 years of age was 2.5 times greater than that obtained from older counterparts, although this descriptive finding did not reach statistical significance.[3132]

The primary experimental complication encountered across certain DPSC and GSC samples was microbial contamination at various stages of in vitro processing. From these occurrences, it can be inferred that laboratories utilizing shared experimental resources such as multi-user biological safety laboratories (BSLs), communal growth/culture media stocks and shared incubators face a substantially elevated risk of cross-contamination.

In terms of growth kinetics, the mean duration required for DPSCs to progress from passage 0 (P0) to passage 3 (P3) was 17 days (range: 15-20 days), whereas GSCs required a mean of only 8 days (range: 6-10 days). GSCs exhibited accelerated proliferation, consistently achieving approximately 80% confluency every 2 days. This confirms that the primary cells obtained via our modified longitudinal splitting method sustained less mechanical damage. Consequently, they remained sufficiently viable and healthy to propagate more effectively in vitro than cells harvested via conventional techniques, which may induce cellular trauma or inadvertently disrupt critical structural elements of the endogenous tissue niche.

CONCLUSION

Gingival tissue represents an abundant and highly accessible source of mesenchymal stem cells. In the present study, gingival-derived stem cells demonstrated accelerated proliferation kinetics, superior clonogenic capacity and enhanced in vitro wound healing potential relative to dental pulp stem cells. Taken together, these findings suggest that gingival tissue may serve as a highly promising alternative stem cell source for applications in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering.
