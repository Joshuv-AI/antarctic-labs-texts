Similar Outcomes after Cyst Decompression with Biceps Tenodesis Versus Cyst Decompression with Repair of Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior Lesions in Patients with Paralabral Cysts
Authors: Chul-Hyun Cho, Ji-Hoon Kim, Du-Han Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuperior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) lesions with paralabral cysts are uncommon. Although various procedures have been suggested, there is no consensus on the optimal choice. The purpose of this study was to compare clinical and radiological results between 2 procedures: SLAP repair with cyst decompression (RD group) and subpectoral biceps tenodesis with cyst decompression (TD group).MethodsArthroscopic surgery for management of a paralabral cyst with a SLAP lesion was performed from January 2009 to January 2023 in 42 patients (RD group: 17, TD group: 25), who were available for a minimum 12-month follow-up. The visual analog scale (VAS) pain score, University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) score, activities of daily living (ADL) score, and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV) were evaluated. Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and manual muscle testing (MMT) were performed 6 months after surgery.ResultsThe mean age of patients in the RD group was 40.0 years and that of the TD group was 46.4 years (p = 0.014). Both groups showed significant improvement in the VAS pain score, UCLA score, ADL score, SSV score, and MMT after surgery. However, no significant difference was observed between the 2 groups. All but 1 patient in the RD group were able to return to work and sports at preoperative levels. This patient was diagnosed with brachial plexopathy after surgery and showed spontaneous improvement. Twenty-five of 42 patients (59.5%) underwent follow-up MRI 6 months after surgery, and there was no recurrence of the ganglion cyst with complete resorption.ConclusionsCompared with cyst decompression and SLAP repair, cyst decompression combined with subpectoral biceps tenodesis demonstrated no statistically significant differences in clinical and radiographic outcomes in this retrospective cohort of patients with SLAP lesions and concomitant paralabral cysts. The findings of our study suggest that biceps tenodesis may be an effective surgical alternative for patients with compromised biceps integrity.

Although rare, suprascapular nerve entrapment syndrome has importance in the effort to understand lesions located around the shoulder.123) This syndrome is mainly caused by a superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) tear with a concomitant paralabral cyst.45) Although nonoperative treatment (medication, injection, or needle aspiration) can be administered, surgical treatment is recommended in cases involving neurologic symptoms such as muscle atrophy or weakness.678)

SLAP repair with cyst decompression is the most popular surgical technique for reducing pressure caused by the cyst and for closing the labrum’s one-way check-valve system.9101112) With advances in arthroscopic instrumentation, techniques, and educational systems, several authors have reported good results from use of arthroscopic SLAP repair with cyst decompression with minimal damage to soft tissue.9101112) In the effort to prevent formation of a paralabral cyst, several authors have recommended labral repair over cyst decompression.313) Schroder et al.13) treated patients with a SLAP tear with a spinoglenoid cyst using arthroscopic labral fixation without cyst decompression to avoid the potential risk of endangering the suprascapular nerve and reported good results. According to a systematic review of the management of SLAP lesions with a cyst, satisfactory results were obtained with use of SLAP repair with or without use of decompression techniques with no superiority.3)

However, SLAP repair is not always possible depending on labral conditions, and poor results have been reported when compared to biceps tenodesis.14151617) Katz et al.15) reported that 75% of patients with SLAP repair presented with pain and decreased shoulder motion. To date, studies on the retrospective outcomes of biceps tenodesis with cyst decompression in patients with a SLAP tear and a paralabral cyst have been reported.18) Perry et al.18) reported good results of biceps tenodesis with cyst decompression in 7 active-duty soldiers. However, no study comparing the results with SLAP repair and cyst decompression has been reported.

The purpose of this study was to compare clinical and radiological results between 2 procedures: SLAP repair with cyst decompression (RD group) and subpectoral biceps tenodesis with cyst decompression (TD group). We hypothesized that the TD group would not be inferior to the RD group in terms of clinical and radiological outcomes.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Keimyung University Dongsan Hospital (IRB No. 2024-06-013). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients.

This is a single-center retrospective cohort study comparing 2 surgical strategies for SLAP lesions with concomitant paralabral cysts, with an inclusion period from January 2009 to January 2023 and a minimum 12-month follow-up. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a paralabral cyst located around the glenohumeral joint, demonstrated on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (2) symptoms lasting more than 3 months despite suitable nonoperative treatments such as medication or injection; and (3) availability for a minimum of 12 months of follow-up after surgery. Patients who had undergone a previous operation or had a history of fracture were excluded. SLAP repair was usually performed before 2016. Subpectoral biceps tenodesis has been applied depending on the patient’s condition since 2016. Biceps tenodesis was performed in patients who exhibited poor biceps or labral pathology, including significant biceps tendon tears (> 20%) or SLAP type III or IV lesions.

Surgical Procedure

Arthroscopic surgery was performed by 1 experienced shoulder surgeon (CHC). After an examination under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a lateral decubitus position. A diagnostic arthroscopy was performed using standard posterior and anterior portals. Cysts were decompressed using a blunt probe and a suction shaver for dilating and debriding the foramen to decompress the cyst fluid. The entrance to the cyst was opened using a soft-tissue elevator, and the cyst was drained into the joint by application of manual pressure. Visible extrusion of gelatinous liquid was observed during this procedure. Depending on the conditions of the biceps tendon and labrum, we decided on SLAP repair or biceps tenodesis. Patients with intact biceps tendon and labrum underwent cyst decompression and SLAP repair. After cyst decompression, the mobility of the labrum was checked using an arthroscopic probe. A blunt shaver was used for debriding the underlying cartilage down to bleeding bone to promote healing. Two or 3 single-loaded all-soft suture anchors were placed according to the size and position of the labral tear (RD group) (Fig. 1). Biceps tenodesis was performed using the subpectoral mini-open technique for patients with a tear of the biceps tendon or poor condition with a labral tear. Following cyst decompression, tendon fixation was performed with the placement of a single 3.5-mm metal anchor on the anterior cortex in the bicipital groove (TD group) (Fig. 2). The long head of the biceps tendon was sutured using simple stitches with the elbow maintained at approximately 30° of flexion and the forearm in a neutral rotational position. Two patients had concomitant small rotator cuff tears and underwent arthroscopic repair. In addition, 1 patient demonstrated minimal calcific tendinitis of the infraspinatus tendon, for which simultaneous decompression was performed.

After surgery, a similar postoperative rehabilitation protocol, consisting of arm sling use for approximately 4 weeks, was followed. Strengthening exercises started 12 weeks after surgery as patients obtained a nearly full active range of motion, which was then continued for 3 to 6 months. Active work and sports activity were allowed 6 months after surgery.

Clinical and Radiologic Assessments

All preoperative and postoperative assessments were performed by the coordinator, who did not participate in the treatment. Assessment of pain intensity was performed using the visual analog scale (VAS) score, University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) score, activities of daily living (ADL) score, and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV) before and after surgery.

MRI scans of patients’ shoulders were obtained from a consecutive cohort of scans acquired using a 1.5-T MRI system (Siemens Magnetom Avanto System; Siemens Medical). The size of the cysts was measured by hand using Picture Archiving and Communication System software and qualitative assessment of fatty degeneration was performed according to the method reported by Goutallier et al.19) The location of the cyst was divided into posterosuperior and superior according to its location in the MRI sagittal view. Follow-up MRI was performed 6 months after surgery to confirm cyst resorption (Fig. 3).

Manual muscle testing (MMT) for external rotation and abduction strength was performed by a blinded assistant nurse using a digitally calibrated dynamometer with the arm held at the side of the body and with the elbow at 90°. The patient was instructed to externally rotate and abduct the shoulder against the calibrated dynamometer, and the greatest force generated was recorded in pounds. This test was performed preoperatively and again 6 months after surgery. Muscle strength was calculated by dividing the involved arm value by the uninvolved arm value and multiplying the result by 100.

Statistical Methods

IBM SPSS ver. 26.0 software (IBM Corp.) was used for data analyses. The nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for comparison of the pre- and postoperative results within each group, and the Mann-Whitney test was used to compare the results between the 2 groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

RESULTS

During the study period, 49 patients who underwent arthroscopic surgery for management of a paralabral cyst with a SLAP lesion met our inclusion criteria. Five patients were excluded because they were not followed up for at least 12 months, and 2 additional patients were excluded due to incomplete clinical score assessments. Finally, 42 patients (RD group: 17, TD group: 25) were included in this study. Comparison of the demographic data between the 2 groups showed significant differences in age, mean follow-up period, and preoperative cyst size. The mean age of patients in the RD group was 40.0 years and that for the TD group was 46.4 years (p = 0.014). The mean follow-up period was 77.2 months in the RD group, and 32.7 months in the TD group (p = 0.024). The mean size of the cyst was 17.2 × 24.9 × 21.7 mm in the RD group and 12.0 × 18.4 × 16.1 mm in the TD group (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Significant improvement in the VAS pain score, UCLA score, ADL score, and SSV score after surgery was observed in both groups (all p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between the 2 groups (all p > 0.05). All but 1 patient in the RD group were able to return to work and sports at preoperative levels. This patient felt a tingling pain in the forearm on the operated side after surgery. He underwent a nerve conduction velocity-electromyography (NCV-EMG) test 3 months after surgery and was diagnosed with brachial plexopathy. His symptoms showed spontaneous improvement 4 months after surgery, and normal findings were observed on the follow-up NCV-EMG test 1 year after surgery. No complications related to the surgical procedure, such as SLAP retear or Popeye deformity, were observed.

Twenty-five of 42 patients (59.5%) underwent follow-up MRI 6 months after surgery, and there was no recurrence of the ganglion cyst and complete resorption was observed in all cases. Additionally, the repaired labrum was well-maintained. Twenty-six of 42 patients (61.9%) underwent MMT 6 months after surgery. According to the MMT for external rotation and abduction strength, both groups showed significant improvement in external rotation strength after surgery (all p < 0.05), and abduction strength showed significant improvement in the TD group (p = 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the 2 groups (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This comparative study using 2 different surgical techniques was conducted to determine whether the outcomes of cyst decompression and biceps tenodesis differed from those for cyst decompression and SLAP repair, which is a popular method used for management of patients with a SLAP lesion with a concomitant paralabral cyst. Based on the findings of our study, satisfactory clinical and radiological outcomes after surgery were obtained in groups. In particular, complete resorption of the cyst was observed on follow-up MRI findings after both procedures.

SLAP repair, a commonly performed surgery for management of SLAP lesions with cysts, involves closing the one-way check-valve system.91112) Shon et al.12) evaluated the efficacy of all-intra-articular arthroscopic cyst decompression and SLAP repair in patients with symptomatic paralabral cysts and reported significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes and complete cyst resolution based on 6-month follow-up MRI. Other researchers have recommended SLAP repair alone. Schroder et al.,13) who performed arthroscopic SLAP repair without direct cyst decompression in patients with labral tears and spinoglenoid cysts, found that 88% of the cysts resolved completely and overall patient satisfaction with shoulder function was high.

However, recent studies have reported on complications or poor outcomes of SLAP repair surgery. Denard et al.,20) who assessed the long-term functional outcomes of arthroscopic SLAP repair in 55 patients over an average follow-up of 77 months, reported that 87% of patients showed good or excellent results; however, the percentage of patients aged 40 years or older was only 81%. In particular, more negative results have been reported in athletes.21) The overall return to play after SLAP repair ranged from 60% to 64% in baseball players2223) and return to the previous performance rate was 57% to 76%.242526) As a result, surgical treatment of SLAP tears has shifted with an increase in the frequency of biceps tenodesis and a decline in the frequency of SLAP repair, particularly in patients older than 40 years.27)

Owing to the reported unsatisfactory outcomes of SLAP repair, many studies on biceps tenodesis have recently been reported. Civan et al.14) reviewed 6 studies (287 patients) to determine which surgery, SLAP repair or biceps tenodesis, was effective and safe, and concluded that biceps tenodesis generally resulted in higher rates of satisfaction and lower reoperation rates, although similar functional scores were observed across both groups. Shin et al.28) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis for comparison of outcomes of SLAP repair versus biceps tenodesis in overhead athletes and reported that no significant differences in postoperative American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores or rates of return to sport and preinjury levels were observed between the 2 methods. However, a slightly lower complication rate was observed in the biceps tenodesis group, and the differences were not statistically significant, suggesting the viability of both treatments.28) In a large data study, Khazai et al.16) using a health-related database reviewed 25,142 patients to compare rates of additional shoulder surgery within 3 years after SLAP repair or biceps tenodesis. They reported that additional shoulder surgery was required in 11.5% of patients who underwent SLAP repair and in 13.0% of patients who underwent tenodesis, with no statistically significant difference between the groups. However, they also reported that younger patients (38.3 ± 14.0 years) were more likely to undergo SLAP repair, while older patients (49.3 ± 10.4 years) typically underwent tenodesis.

Few studies have confirmed the outcomes of cyst decompression and biceps tenodesis. Perry et al.18) performed combined subpectoral biceps tenodesis and arthroscopic indirect cyst decompression in 7 active-duty military patients with SLAP tears and associated paralabral cysts. The results showed significant improvements in external rotation strength, pain reduction, and overall shoulder function postoperatively, and all patients returned to full duty within a median of 20 weeks.18) Perry’s study18) was conducted on young people, but SLAP repair might be difficult in middle-aged and older patients due to the poor condition of the labrum or biceps tendon. We believe that these cases can be considered an appropriate indication for cyst decompression and biceps tenodesis.

Our study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective study with a relatively short follow-up and a small sample size. Only 26 patients underwent MMT and 25 patients had follow-up MRI evaluations. However, because SLAP tears combined with paralabral cysts are uncommon, most research related to paralabral cysts has a limited sample size. Second, NCV-EMG was not performed in all included patients. Third, the follow-up period was different between the 2 groups, as TD group had only been performed since May 2016. Fourth, the baseline demographic characteristics differed between the 2 groups, as biceps tenodesis was performed in patients with concomitant severe biceps or labral pathology.

Despite these limitations, our study is the first study to compare SLAP repair and biceps tenodesis in patients with suprascapular nerve entrapment syndrome. Well-designed multicenter randomized studies comparing these procedures are needed to obtain Level I or II evidence. Compared with cyst decompression and SLAP repair, cyst decompression with subpectoral biceps tenodesis demonstrated no statistically significant differences in the clinical and radiographic outcomes in this retrospective cohort of patients with SLAP lesions and concomitant paralabral cysts. The findings of our study suggest that biceps tenodesis may be an effective surgical alternative for patients with compromised biceps integrity.
