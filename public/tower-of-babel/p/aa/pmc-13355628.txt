The Efficacy and Safety of Telitacicept versus the Recommended Regimens in IgA Nephropathy: A Single-Center Experience
Authors: Xueting Li, Yinghui Zhang, Chenyun Xu, Ben Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIgA nephropathy (IgAN) is one of the leading causes of end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Telitacicept has been demonstrated significant efficacy in IgAN, but its comparative efficacy versus the recommended regimens in the 2025 KDIGO guideline remains unclear. The aim of this study was to investigate the clinical efficacy and safety of glucocorticoids, telitacicept, and nefecon in patients with IgAN, in order to determine the optimal treatment regimen.MethodsWe recruited 101 patients with IgAN confirmed by renal biopsy. The patients were categorized into three groups according to the treatment they received: the GC group, the Telitacicept group, and the Nefecon group. Treatment responses, including complete remission (CR) and partial remission (PR), and adverse events were compared among the three groups after 9 months of follow-up.ResultsAt the 9-month follow-up, the CR rates were 93.5%, 86.2%, and 70.7% for the GC group, Telitacicept group, and Nefecon group, respectively (p=0.035). During the follow-up, proteinuria in all three groups decreased significantly from baseline (p < 0.05), while eGFR remained stable At the 3rd, 6th, and 9th months, the median percentage reduction in proteinuria in the GC group was significantly greater than that in the Nefecon group (p<0.001; p=0.001; p<0.001). The GC group also showed greater reductions compared to the Telitacicept group (p=0.057; p=0.101; p=0.103), and the Telitacicept group also demonstrated higher proteinuria reduction rates than the Nefecon group, although neither difference was statistically significant. Furthermore, the incidence of adverse events, particularly infections, was significantly lower in the Nefecon group than in the GC group (p < 0.05).ConclusionOur study confirmed that glucocorticoids have superior efficacy to nefecon, but are associated with a higher incidence of adverse events. Telitacicept offered both efficacy and safety, and can be recommended for high-risk IgAN patients who are intolerant to glucocorticoids.Keywords: IgA nephropathy, glucocorticoids, telitacicept, nefecon, proteinuria reduction rate

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis and one of the leading causes of end-stage renal disease (ESRD).1,2 Previous studies have shown that 30% to 40% of IgAN patients progress to ESRD within 20 to 30 years of onset.3–5 In the past, the management focus of IgAN was on optimizing supportive treatments, including strict blood pressure control and the administration of the maximum tolerated dose of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors. The 2021 KIDGO clinical practice guideline recommends that patients with IgAN who are at high risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression may be considered for a 6-month course of corticosteroid therapy.6 However, the increased incidence of serious adverse events, particularly infections, raises concerns about the safety of corticosteroid treatment for IgAN.

Since 2021, the development of some specific intervention drugs, such as the oral targeted-release budesonide formulation (Nefecon), which can reduce pathogenic IgA in the circulation, has provided more treatment options for patients with IgAN. Furthermore, the 2025 KDIGO guideline includes nefecon and glucocorticoids as treatment options for IgA nephropathy. Meanwhile, accumulating evidence supports telitacicept as a new treatment option for patients with high-risk progressive IgAN or refractory disease after adequate supportive therapy and conventional immunosuppressive treatment, given its favorable efficacy and safety in IgA nephropathy.7 Nevertheless, there was limited data on the clinical use of glucocorticoids, telitacicept, and nefecon in IgAN, and there was no head-to-head comparison data evaluating their efficacy and safety. In this retrospective study, 101 patients with biopsy-confirmed IgAN were enrolled and divided into three groups based on their treatment regimens to investigate the clinical efficacy and safety of telitacicept and nefecon.

Methods

Study Patients

At the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, this retrospective study recruited 348 patients with kidney biopsy-confirmed IgA nephropathy from January 2018 to March 2025 (Figure 1). This research was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No.: EFY20250111). Informed consent was secured in writing from every participant.

Flowchart of the study.

Eligibility Requirements

(i) primary IgAN confirmed by renal biopsy; (ii) age between 18 and 75 years; (iii) the proteinuria was at least 0.5 g/d and ≤3.5 g/d, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥30 mL/min/1.73m2; (iv) the follow-up period should be at least 9 months.

Criteria for Non-participation

(i) secondary IgAN of any etiology; (ii) patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; (iii) active infection, pregnancy, or lactation; (iv) complications of severe hepatic insufficiency or significant cardiovascular disorders; (v) lack of baseline or follow-up data. (vi) treatment with other immunosuppressants; (vii) proteinuria > 3.5 g/d.

Intervention

Biopsy-proven IgAN patients were recruited in this study. Patients treated with oral glucocorticoids (0.5–0.6 mg/kg per day) were in the GC group. Patients treated with a subcutaneous injection of 240 mg telitacicept once a week were in the Telitacicept group, and those treated with nefecon 16 mg/day were in the Nefecon group. RAAS inhibitor therapy, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) (eg, irbesartan, valsartan), and sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2is) (eg, dapagliflozin), was also administered to the three groups, with the dosage being the maximum or tolerated dose.

Data Collection and Follow-Up

The clinical information of age, biologic sex, blood pressure, proteinuria, serum albumin (ALB), serum creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was collected through the electronic medical record at the following time points: baseline, 1, 3, 6, and 9 months, respectively. eGFR was calculated using the CKD-EPI equation.8 All baseline assessments were completed before immunosuppressive therapy. All severe adverse events (SAEs) were addressed and documented throughout both the inpatient and follow-up periods. Notably, every enrolled patient completed a follow-up period of no less than 9 months.

Outcomes

The primary clinical outcome was composite remission, which was defined as complete remission (CR) or partial remission (PR) within 9 months. CR: urinary protein excretion ≤ 0.3 g/d, a serum albumin level of at least 35 g/L, and a stable or improved eGFR. PR: urinary protein excretion between 0.3 and 3.5 g/d, or a reduction of ≥50% from baseline, and a serum albumin level ≥ 30 g/L. Composite remission comprises CR or PR. A decline in renal function was defined as either a ≥50% reduction in the eGFR from baseline or progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), which was characterized by a persistently low eGFR (<15 mL/min/1.73 m2) or the need to initiate long-term dialysis.

Severe adverse events, including infection, abnormal liver function, and diabetes, were documented as secondary clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± SD or median (Q25; Q75), depending on the distribution of the variable. Categorical variables are described using counts and percentages. For continuous variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among three groups if the data were normally distributed with homogeneity of variance; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis H-test was applied. For categorical variables, the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test was used. If significant differences were detected among the three groups, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with a Bonferroni correction to control for multiple testing errors. Cumulative complete remission rates and composite remission rates among the three groups were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and assessed using the Log rank test. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. All statistical analyses were completed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

Study Participants

101 patients with IgAN were recruited in this study, which included 31 patients who were treated with oral glucocorticoids (0.5–0.6 mg/kg per day), 29 patients who received a subcutaneous injection of 240 mg telitacicept once a week, and 41 patients who received nefecon 16 mg/day.

Baseline clinical indices, including age, sex, blood pressure, serum creatinine, and eGFR. These data were similar among the included patients (age: p = 0.061; sex: p = 0.186; SBP: p = 0.078; DBP: p = 0.108; Scr: p = 0.330; eGFR: p = 0.090). There were differences in the proteinuria and ALB among the three groups (p = 0.004, p = 0.001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons indicated no significant differences in either parameter between the Telitacicept and Nefecon groups (p = 0.434, p = 0.356). Conversely, patients in the GC group exhibited significantly higher baseline proteinuria and lower baseline ALB compared with those in the Nefecon group (p = 0.008, p = 0.006) (Table 1). In this study, for IgAN patients with proteinuria ≥2 g/d, we were more inclined to administer glucocorticoid therapy, which resulted in intergroup differences in baseline proteinuria and ALB among the three groups.

Primary Outcomes Comparison Among Different Groups

The CR rates of the three groups at different follow-up time points are presented in Table 2. There were no significant differences among the three groups in CR at different follow-up time points. After 3 months of therapy, 6 (19.4%), 5 (17.2%), and 13 (31.7%) cases achieved CR in the GC group, Telitacicept, and Nefecon groups, respectively, and the intergroup difference remained not statistically significant (p = 0.295). With the extension of treatment duration, the CR rates in all three groups showed an overall increasing trend. At the end of the study, the CR rates for the GC group, Telitacicept group, and Nefecon group were 58.1% (18), 44.8% (13), and 51.2% (21), respectively. There was still no statistically significant difference among the three groups. There was no statistically significant difference in the Kaplan-Meier curves for CR among the three groups (Figure 2).

Proteinuria and proteinuria reduction rates at different time points in each group are shown in Figure 4A and B. Overall, proteinuria in all three groups decreased significantly from baseline, and there was no statistically significant difference in proteinuria levels among the three groups at any time point (p > 0.05). However, the proteinuria reduction rate showed statistically significant differences among the groups at 3, 6, and 9 months (p < 0.05).

Specifically, at the 3rd month, proteinuria in the GC group decreased from 2.52 (1.23, 5.28) g/day to 0.56 (0.35, 0.92) g/day, with a reduction rate of 77.73 (57.47, 86.04)%; in the Telitacicept group, it decreased from 1.45 (1.14, 2.29) g/day to 0.73 (0.42, 0.89) g/day, with a reduction rate of 48.44 (30.32, 64.30)%; and in the Nefecon group, it decreased from 1.37 (0.70, 2.27) g/day to 0.73 (0.38, 1.37) g/day, with a reduction rate of 37.50 (18.21, 67.00) %. The differences in reduction rates among the three groups were statistically significant (p < 0.001). At the 6th month, proteinuria in the three groups decreased to 0.45 (0.18, 1.04) g/day, 0.47 (0.32, 0.96) g/day, and 0.61 (0.27, 1.12) g/day, respectively, with reduction rates of 79.85 (68.07, 90.18) %, 58.30 (39.45, 76.04) %, and 44.94 (19.46, 77.48) %. The differences among the groups were statistically significant (p = 0.001). At 9 months, proteinuria in the three groups further decreased to 0.27 (0.12, 0.52) g/day, 0.37 (0.24, 0.97) g/day, and 0.44 (0.24, 1.13) g/day, respectively, with reduction rates of 85.20 (71.12, 94.42) %, 66.43 (53.45, 82.05) %, and 46.70 (25.15, 81.93) %. The differences among the groups were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Given that there were statistically significant overall differences among the three groups, further post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that at the 3-month mark, the rate of proteinuria reduction in the Telitacicept group and the Nefecon group was significantly lower than that in the GC group. At the 6th and 9th months, only the Nefecon group had significantly lower proteinuria reduction rates than the GC group, but there was no significant difference between the Telitacicept group and the GC group.

Serum Albumin Level

In Figure 4C, the level of serum albumin increased from 36.20 (34.72, 41.22) g/L to 43.14 (40.88, 45.40) g/L in the GC group, from 39.82 (35.89, 44.20) g/L to 43.62 (41.13, 45.06) g/L in the Telitacicept group, and from 42.04 (38.30, 45.90) g/L to 42.20 (40.29, 45.10) g/L in the Nefecon group. There was no statistically significant difference among these three groups in the level of serum albumin level throughout all observation times (p > 0.05).

Renal Function

The initial serum creatinine level was 110.60 (78.02, 128.06) μmol/L, 129.44 (98.62,157.75) μmol/L, and 97.30 (79.80, 121.49) μmol/L, and the final level was 69.70 (60.41, 106.35) μmol/L, 51.91 (37.43,73.57) μmol/L, and 65.03 (58.01, 79.29) μmol/L, respectively, in patients who were treated with glucocorticoids, Telitacicept, and Nefecon (Figure 4D). There was no statistically significant difference among these three groups in the level of serum creatinine level throughout all observation times (p > 0.05).

During the follow-up period, the eGFR reduction rate in the Nefecon group was overall higher than that in the GC group and the Telitacicept group. A statistically significant between-group difference was observed only at month 3, whereas no significant differences were detected at other time points. At month 9 of follow-up, the eGFR reduction rates were −6.55 (−27.93, 3.50) %, −0.45 (−11.05, 11.08) %, and 1.64 (−6.57, 7.43) % in the Gc group, Telitacicept group, and Nefecon group, respectively (Figure 4E and F). Furthermore, throughout the follow-up, the magnitude of eGFR reduction was within a controllable range in all groups, and no rapid deterioration of renal function was observed.

Adverse Events

Adverse events were recorded during the follow-up period. Table 5 shows the incidence of adverse events among the three groups during the 9-month follow-up. The total incidence of adverse events was 41.9% (13/31) in the GC group, 0% (0/29) in the Telitacicept group, and 7.3% (3/41) in the Nefecon group (p < 0.001). Infection was the predominant adverse event. Compared with the Nefecon group, the GC group had significantly higher total infection rates (35.5% vs. 4.9%, p = 0.001) and pulmonary infection rates (19.4% vs. 2.4%, p = 0.038), but the difference in urinary tract infection rates (16.1% vs. 2.4%) was not statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (p = 0.078). No infections occurred in the Telitacicept group, and the differences between the Telitacicept group and the GC group were significant (p < 0.05). One case of abnormal liver function occurred only in the Nefecon group, while two cases (6.5%) of steroid-induced diabetes were observed only in the GC group, with no statistically significant differences among the three groups (p = 0.478, p = 0.172). In summary, the incidence of adverse events, especially infection-related events, was markedly lower in the Telitacicept and the Nefecon groups than in the GC group during the 9-month follow-up. All adverse events that occurred were not serious and were relieved after effective treatment.

Discussion

IgAN is the most common primary glomerulonephritis and one of the leading causes of ESRD.1,2 The prognosis for patients with IgA nephropathy, particularly those at high risk of progression, remains poor. Current treatment options, including glucocorticoid therapy, are limited. However, the recent development and application of a large number of new therapeutic drugs are expected to improve the situation.

Two pivotal studies, STOP-IgAN and TESTING, have provided apparently conflicting yet mutually supportive findings on the efficacy of glucocorticoids in treating IgA nephropathy (IgAN). STOP-IgAN revealed that supplementing optimized supportive care with systemic glucocorticoids led to decreased proteinuria but did not enhance long-term renal outcomes and raised the likelihood of adverse events.9 Conversely, the TESTING trial, primarily enrolling Asian cohorts, indicated that both full-dose and reduced-dose glucocorticoid protocols markedly lowered the incidence of end-stage renal disease in high-risk IgAN individuals.10 Based on the two studies, for IgAN at high risk of progression, the 2021 KDIGO guidelines recommend that glucocorticoid therapy may be considered on the basis of optimized supportive care.6 Currently, many studies have also reported the efficacy and safety of nefecon and telitacicept in the treatment of IgAN. In a randomized Phase 2 multicenter trial, telitacicept reduced 24-hour proteinuria by 49% from baseline at the end of the 24-week treatment, with stable eGFR.11 Recently, data presented at the 2025 ASN Annual Meeting demonstrated that at week 39, the 24-hour urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) had decreased by 58.9% from baseline in the telitacicept group, versus 8.8% in the placebo group. The ratio of UPCR at week 39 to baseline in the telitacicept group was 55% lower than that in the placebo group. For renal function preservation, the geometric mean percentage change in eGFR remained stable in the telitacicept group (−1.0%) but declined significantly in the placebo group (−7.7%).12 The NefIgArd study demonstrated that oral administration of nefecon at 16 mg/day for 9 months significantly reduced proteinuria and slowed the decline in eGFR, and that this efficacy was not affected by baseline eGFR levels.13

Our study showed that the glucocorticoids regimen for treating IgAN was superior to the telitacicept and nefecon regimen in terms of composite remission, proteinuria reduction, and renal protection. Specifically, the proteinuria reduction rate associated with telitacicept regimen was 66.43%, which was slightly lower than that of the glucocorticoids regimen’s rate of 85.2%; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Among the three treatment regimens, the nefecon regimen exhibited the least efficacy in reducing proteinuria, achieving only 46.7%, which was significantly lower than that of the glucocorticoids regimen. The results of our study were generally consistent with the data reported in previous studies related to telitacicept and nefecon.14,15 All three treatment regimens exhibited effective kidney-protective effects, with no deterioration in renal function observed. Additionally, telitacicept displayed the most favorable safety profile, whereas the incidence of adverse events associated with the hormone regimen was significantly higher than that of the nefecon regimen. Our study indicated that telitacicept was more effective than nefecon in reducing proteinuria and facilitated earlier clinical remission in patients with IgAN. The therapeutic advantage of telitacicept may be attributed to its dual-target blockade mechanism, which more comprehensively inhibits the production of pathogenic IgA1 upstream.16 In contrast, as an oral targeted formulation of budesonide, Nefecon exhibits variable bioavailability due to interindividual variability and the gastrointestinal environment, which may partially limit its full therapeutic efficacy.17,18

Based on new developments in IgAN assessment and therapy, the KDIGO 2025 IgAN guideline now encourages a more liberal kidney biopsy policy and suggests aiming for stricter proteinuria.19 Central to contemporary IgAN management is the equilibrium between therapeutic efficacy and safety. In this context, nefecon has been recognized in the KDIGO 2025 IgAN guideline because it can effectively decrease proteinuria and maintain renal function. In clinical practice, patients with high-risk progressive IgAN frequently face therapeutic dilemmas, with clinicians often recommending enrollment in clinical trials or the initiation of immunosuppressive therapies, including novel agents like telitacicept. And the latest Chinese clinical practice guidelines (2025) have suggested that it can be considered as a new treatment option for high-risk IgAN.

Therefore, for high-risk IgAN patients with contraindications to or requiring cautious use of glucocorticoids, treatment with either telitacicept or nefecon may be considered. Both regimens can ensure therapeutic efficacy, maintain stable renal function, and reduce the occurrence of adverse events. Thus, high-risk IgAN patients requiring cautious use of glucocorticoids may consider treatment with either telitacicept or nefecon. Both approaches can ensure therapeutic efficacy, preserve stable renal function, and decrease the incidence of adverse events.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, as a single-center retrospective study, the potential for selection bias and confounding factors cannot be fully excluded. Second, because we did not measure changes in serum biomarkers such as Gd-IgA1, the assessment of mechanisms underlying treatment benefit and prognosis was limited. Finally, the follow-up period of this study was 9 months, which was relatively short. To address these limitations, future research should include: (1) a prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a larger sample size and longer follow-up; (2) serial measurements of biomarkers such as Gd-IgA1 to elucidate mechanisms; and (3) exploration of large language models (LLMs)20 to reduce non-randomized bias via digital twin simulation, predict individual treatment response, and extract safety signals from unstructured clinical notes.

Conclusions

Our study confirmed that glucocorticoids have superior efficacy to nefecon, but are associated with a higher incidence of adverse events. Telitacicept exhibited favorable efficacy and safety profiles compared with nefecon in high-risk IgAN patients. Despite the lack of statistical significance, it represents a potential therapeutic alternative.
