New percentiles of gamma-glutamyl transferase serum levels in infants and the role of breastfeeding
Authors: Fabiola Di Dato, Valeria Delle Cave, Rosilenia Carandente, Alba Nappi, Giovanna Montesano, Pasquale Dolce, Serena Salomè, Francesco Raimondi, Raffaele Iorio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNormal gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) values in infant serum remain poorly defined. Establishing accurate reference ranges during infancy is essential for diagnosing hepatobiliary disorders such as biliary atresia and progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis. To establish reference percentiles for GGT, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in infants, and assess influence of breastfeeding.MethodsA retrospective, single-center, longitudinal study including 469 healthy infants was conducted. Liver function was tested from birth to 18 months, and the effects of breastfeeding were analyzed.ResultsGGT levels were higher in the first months of life and progressively declined over time. Breastfeeding in the first month of life was associated with higher levels of GGT, total bilirubin (TB), and AST compared to infants who were mixed-fed or exclusively formula-fed. Specifically, breastfed infants had a median GGT of 79 IU/L, while mixed-fed and formula-fed infants had GGT levels of 61 IU/L and 46.5 IU/L, respectively. Similarly, TB was 3.3 mg/dL in breastfed infants, 1.7 mg/dL in mixed-fed, and 1.1 mg/dL in formula-fed. Median AST levels were also highest in breastfed infants (36 IU/L), followed by mixed-fed (33.5 IU/L) and formula-fed (31 IU/L). A positive correlation was observed between GGT and TB (P < 0.001).ConclusionsAge- and sex-specific reference percentiles are provided for GGT, AST, and ALT in infants younger than 18 months. GGT, TB, and AST are physiologically elevated, particularly among breastfed infants.Keywords: Neonatal cholestasis, Liver enzymes, Aspartate aminotransferase, Alanine aminotransferase, Percentiles

Background

Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) is a membrane-bound enzyme involved in glutathione metabolism and antioxidant defense. It is widely expressed in several tissues, including the kidney, pancreas and intestine, with the highest activity in the liver, and is commonly used as a marker of hepatobiliary disease. However, GGT is not a specific marker of cholestasis, rather reflecting overall hepatobiliary function and oxidative stress [1].

In infants, normal serum levels of GGT, a marker of cholestasis, are not well defined. In the first months of life, diagnosing cholestasis is particularly challenging because multiple disorders can present with similar symptoms. Thus, the integration of clinical observations, laboratory tests, and imaging studies is needed for a comprehensive diagnostic approach [2]. Serum GGT level is included in the diagnostic score for biliary atresia [3], which is the leading cause of liver transplantation in children and should ideally be treated with portoenterostomy within 45–60 days after birth to obtain a favorable outcome [4]. In biliary atresia, GGT levels are usually higher than 300 IU/L, although lower values do not exclude this diagnosis [5]. Beyond biliary atresia, many other disorders, including genetic-metabolic conditions, may be responsible for infantile cholestasis [6]. Specific genetic and metabolic tests for these conditions have become available, but are usually expensive, time-consuming, and available in limited referral centers. Following the identification of several types of genetic cholestasis, GGT serum levels are increasingly used to classify infantile cholestasis, with low or normal levels often pointing towards bile acid synthesis disorders and progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC) [7]. Moreover, in recent years, PFIC has received significant and increasing attention with the emergence of targeted therapies such as ileal bile acid transporter inhibitors. These new agents have revolutionized the therapeutic approach to PFIC, offering an effective treatment for the management of severe pruritus and further highlighting the importance of serum GGT levels in identifying these conditions [8].

In children, GGT is a better indicator of cholestasis than alkaline phosphatase (ALP), because ALP levels can be affected by bone growth. Thus, ALP is preferred for diagnosing cholestasis in adults but has a limited diagnostic role in childhood [9]. GGT serum levels may also be influenced by drugs such as anticonvulsants, which can increase levels through an enzyme induction mechanism [10–12]. In infants, serum GGT levels seem to be influenced by various factors including age, sex, prematurity, parenteral nutrition, route of delivery, and perinatal stress, as demonstrated by elevated cord blood GGT activity in preterm neonates and in term male neonates with perinatal stress and vaginal delivery [13]. Currently, the reference range for infant serum GGT values is primarily derived from studies involving unhealthy full-term or preterm neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit [14]. On the other hand, reference ranges based on age- and sex-specific data have been established for aminotransferase serum levels (aspartate aminotransferase [AST] and alanine aminotransferase [ALT]) and GGT only in children and adolescents aged 11 months to 16 years [15]. Large initiatives such as the CALIPER study have significantly contributed to the establishment of pediatric reference intervals for biochemical markers; however, data specifically addressing infants, particularly for GGT, remain limited, reinforcing the need for age-specific reference values in early life [16].

For infants younger than 12 months, the only percentiles available are for ALT [17]. Accurate reference values for GGT are needed in infants to effectively identify those with hepatobiliary disease. This would also avoid over-investigation, taking into account that blood sampling in infants can be challenging, invasive, and stressful for patients, their parents, and healthcare professionals. In this context, the primary objective of this study was to establish age- and sex-specific first percentiles for GGT derived from a cohort of healthy, normal-weight infants aged 1 day to 18 months. The secondary aim was to assess the potential effects of breastfeeding on GGT serum levels during the first months of life and any correlations between GGT serum levels and other liver function tests. Furthermore, age- and sex-related percentiles for AST and ALT in healthy infants and the impact of breastfeeding on their serum levels were defined.

Methods

Study design

This was a single-center, retrospective, longitudinal study conducted at the Perinatal Infection Unit of the University of Naples Federico II (Campania, Italy). Infants aged 1 day to 18 months who were exposed prenatally to Toxoplasma gondii, Herpesvirus, Varicella-zoster virus, or Treponema pallidum were enrolled between January 2016 and June 2023. The study included only infants exposed to infection in utero, who were confirmed to be uninfected after 12 or 18 months of follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: Confirmed perinatal infection; acute or chronic disease at the time of enrollment or during observations; underlying liver disease; use of any medication during observations; Apgar score < 7 at 5 min; low birth weight; and gestational age < 37 weeks.

The main aims were to define age- and sex-related percentiles for GGT, AST, and ALT serum levels in healthy infants. Moreover, the potential effects of breastfeeding on liver enzymes were evaluated. For these purposes, the following data were collected: Birth date; sex; gestational age; delivery method; birth weight; Apgar score; perinatal events (e.g., need for phototherapy and/or medications); feeding type; and anthropometric parameters.

Liver biochemical tests (AST, ALT, GGT, total bilirubin [TB], direct bilirubin, albumin), renal function tests, and complete blood count were recorded at specific intervals: T0 (1–30 days); T1 (1–3 months); T2 (3–6 months); T3 (6–12 months); and T4 (12–18 months). This follow-up protocol was established for infants who had been exposed to infectious agents in utero. Laboratory measurements of AST, ALT, and GGT were performed using standardized automated enzymatic assays.

To assess the influence of breastfeeding, enrolled infants were divided into three groups, namely breastfed, formula-fed, and mixed-fed. The possible role of breastfeeding and delivery type was evaluated at T0 and T1, and T0, respectively. The study was conducted according to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with Good Clinical Practice standards. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee “Carlo Romano” of the University Federico II of Naples (Protocol Number 381/20).

Statistical analysis

Considering the primary objective of the study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was conducted. The maximum number of infants who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study, following recommendations for descriptive studies aimed at constructing reference centile curves. Furthermore, our study followed the World Health Organization Multicentre Growth Reference Study [18] recommendation of including at least 200 children per sex in longitudinal studies to ensure adequate precision in centile estimation during early childhood. Our study included 469 children (235 males and 234 females), with repeated measurements of liver enzyme levels. Given the balanced sex distribution, restricted age range (0–18 months), and use of smoothing techniques via the Lambda-Mu-Sigma (LMS) method [19], the available sample may be considered appropriate to estimate plausible reference centiles.

For comparisons of demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables between male and female infants at T0 (as reported in Table 1), continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Age-specific percentile curves for GGT, AST, and ALT were estimated using the LMS method, which models the median (M), coefficient of variation (S), and skewness (L) of the distribution as smooth functions of age. The model was fit separately for males and females. Several distribution families suitable for positive quantitative variables with potential skewness and/or kurtosis were tested, including Box-Cox Cole and Green original distribution, Box-Cox Power Exponential original distribution, and Box-Cox transformation original distribution. The best-fitting distribution was selected based on the generalized Akaike Information Criterion. The fitted models provided estimates of the 5th, 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 90th, and 95th percentiles.

To compare GGT, AST, and ALT levels across the three feeding groups (breastfed, formula-fed, mixed) at both timepoints (T0 and T1), the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. When statistically significant differences were found, Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons were used to identify pairwise differences between groups.

In addition, multivariable analysis was conducted for GGT levels using mixed-effects models. GGT values were log-transformed to approximate a bell-shaped distribution; therefore, exponentiated coefficients are interpreted as ratios of geometric means. These models included TB, feeding type, and their interaction term, to assess whether the effect of feeding on GGT varied by bilirubin levels. A random intercept was included for each subject to account for within-subject correlation due to repeated measurements.

For all statistical tests, the significance level was set to 0.05. All analyses were performed using R statistical software. Centile curves were obtained using the “LMS” function from the “GAMLSS” package in R (R Core Team).

GGT serum levels

GGT levels were higher in the first months of life, compared to later ages, with a decreasing trend over time. In the first 3 months, GGT levels were similar between sexes, with males averaging 68IU/L (IQR: 65.5) and females 64 IU/L (IQR: 58.2) at the start (T0), and dropping significantly by 3 months (T1) to about 36 IU/L (IQR: 30) for males and 33.5 IU/L (IQR: 25) for females. TB levels followed a similar pattern, starting at 2.1 mg/dL (IQR: 3.3) for males and 2.1 mg/dL (IQR: 3.78) for females at T0, and dropping to 0.63 mg/dL (IQR: 0.7) for males and 0.645 mg/dL (IQR: 0.628) for females at T1. At T0, there were no significant differences based on type of delivery in GGT (vaginal 70 IU/L [IQR: 73]; C-Sect. 62 IU/L [IQR: 51.8]) or TB (natural 1.97 mg/dL [IQR: 3.33]; C-Sect. 2.11 mg/dL [IQR: 3.48]) levels. Mixed-effects analysis showed that the interaction between feeding type and bilirubin levels was not significant, indicating that the relationship between bilirubin levels and serum GGT does not differ across the three feeding groups. The correlation between bilirubin and GGT levels was found to be significant over time (P < 0.001). An estimated exp(β) = 1.07 indicated that for each 1-unit increase in bilirubin levels, the geometric mean of serum GGT increased by a factor of 1.07, corresponding to a 7% increase.

AST and ALT serum levels

Median AST and ALT values did not significantly differ between males and females at T0 (male AST 33 IU/L [IQR: 11], female AST 34 IU/L [IQR: 16]; male ALT 23 IU/L [IQR: 11], female ALT 22 IU/L [IQR: 14]), or T1 (male AST 37.5 IU/L [IQR: 15.2], female AST 37 IU/L [IQR: 16.5]; male ALT 28 IU/L [IQR: 16], female ALT 26 IU/L [IQR: 16]). There was a significant difference in the median AST and ALT values of neonates based on the delivery method; vaginally delivered infants had higher levels than those born by Caesarean section (AST 36 IU/L [IQR: 15] vs. 32 IU/L [IQR: 13], P = 0.004; ALT 24 IU/L [IQR: 14.2] vs. 20 IU/L [IQR: 11], P = 0.012).

At T0, AST levels were higher in breastfed infants (36 IU/L [IQR: 14]) compared to formula-fed (31 IU/L [IQR: 10]) and mixed-fed groups (33.5 IU/L [IQR: 17]), with a significant difference between breastfed and formula-fed infants (P = 0.029). This pattern was confirmed at T1, with higher AST levels in breastfed infants (40 IU/L [IQR: 19]) compared to both mixed-fed (37 IU/L [IQR: 15], P = 0.014) and formula-fed infants (35 IU/L [IQR: 14], P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C).

In contrast, ALT levels were comparable across feeding groups at T0 (23 IU/L [IQR: 11], 22.5 IU/L [IQR: 13.8], and 21 IU/L [IQR: 8.25] for breastfed, mixed-fed, and formula-fed, respectively), except for a modest difference between breastfed (30 IU/L [IQR: 18.5]) and formula-fed infants (26 IU/L [IQR: 11]) at T1 (P = 0.021) (Fig. 3D).

Mixed-effects analysis showed that the relationship between serum bilirubin and AST or ALT level did not differ across the three feeding groups. Bilirubin levels showed a significant correlation with both AST and ALT levels over time (P < 0.001). The estimated exp(β) = 0.89 and 0.84 indicated that compared to breastfeeding, formula feeding reduced the geometric mean of serum AST and ALT by 11% and 16%, respectively.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to establish age-specific reference percentiles for GGT serum levels during infancy and to assess the effect of breastfeeding on GGT levels during the first months of life. The availability of percentiles of GGT serum levels is useful for the early diagnosis of infantile cholestasis, which is especially crucial for identifying specific forms such as biliary atresia rapidly and accurately.

The early diagnosis of biliary atresia is essential, as the success rate of Kasai portoenterostomy is inversely related to the age at which the procedure is performed [20]. The present study demonstrated that serum GGT levels are physiologically elevated up to six to seven times the adult upper limit in the first 6 months of life compared with infants older than 6 months to 12 months and adults. This is likely consistent with the physiological immaturity of the biliary excretory system in the first weeks of life [21]. Then GGT activity declines, reaching adult levels in the second 6 months of life. As previously reported in animal models, several phases of enterohepatic circulation are impaired in early infancy, including bile acid transport mechanisms, reduction in the size of the bile acid pool, and impaired bile acid synthesis [22]. Bile formation is regulated during development and remains functionally immature at birth, contributing to a transient state of “physiological cholestasis” in the perinatal liver [23]. Misinterpretation of these physiological elevations may lead to the overdiagnosis of cholestasis in otherwise healthy infants or in infants with prolonged jaundice. This condition is observed in up to 15% of all newborns and should raise suspicion for cholestatic liver disease. The current guidelines recommend measuring serum fractionated bilirubin after 14 days of life if bottle-fed and after 21 days if breastfed. It is important to be cautious when interpreting GGT levels in infants, as physiological elevations do not necessarily suggest the presence of cholestasis, which should instead be suspected if conjugated bilirubin is greater than 20% of the TB [24].

To date, reference percentiles for serum GGT in healthy infants have not been established, mainly because it is unethical to collect blood from healthy children. To overcome this reasonable limitation, we retrospectively sampled a cohort of infants who had undergone blood tests according to standardized protocols for suspected congenital infections and selected only those who were free of infection at the end of follow-up. This strategy allowed the inclusion of a large and representative sample of healthy full-term newborns, in whom we could evaluate serum levels of both liver enzymes and bilirubin, and how these levels might be affected by breastfeeding and delivery. Using this cohort, we demonstrated that during the first months of life, mean GGT levels are higher in breastfed infants compared to formula-fed and mixed-fed infants, suggesting that breastfeeding may directly affect GGT levels in early infancy.

High levels of GGT in breast milk may be a factor contributing to the observed phenomenon. Breast milk contains relatively high GGT activity, which may contribute to elevated serum levels in breastfed infants. In addition, the physiological immaturity of the neonatal intestinal barrier in the early postnatal period may facilitate the absorption of macromolecules such as GGT, allowing their passage from the intestinal lumen into the bloodstream. At the same time, bioactive components present in breast milk may stimulate hepatobiliary function. These mechanisms, as also suggested in the literature, may explain the higher GGT levels observed in breastfed infants compared to formula-fed infants [25].

Our analysis did not reveal a significant association between mode of delivery and postnatal serum GGT levels. This finding is in contrast to previous studies conducted on term neonates, which reported higher GGT activity in umbilical cord blood following vaginal delivery compared to Cesarean section, particularly among male infants [13]. The discrepancy may be attributed to the timing and source of sample collection (postnatal serum vs. cord blood), as well as variations in study populations and clinical settings. Additionally, it is possible that perinatal physiological adaptations and the dynamic maturation of hepatic function during the early neonatal period might mitigate the impact of how a baby is delivered on their serum GGT levels after the immediate peripartum period. The high rate of Caesarean section deliveries in this study, despite the healthy status of the infants, can be attributed to two main factors: The infants’ risk of perinatal infection (observed at a tertiary center) and the generally higher rate of Caesarean sections in our region (Campania, Italy). However, this higher rate of Caesarean sections did not impact the results of our study.

As expected, our study confirmed that breastfed infants have higher bilirubin levels in the first weeks of life with a progressive decline in values in the following months. This decline likely reflects liver maturation and improved intestinal function. Importantly, we observed a positive correlation between TB and GGT levels over time, regardless of feeding type. This association may indicate that GGT elevations can occur, even in the context of predominantly unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, and may be explained by shared mechanisms such as hepatic immaturity and increased enterohepatic circulation. Given that GGT is a marker of hepatobiliary function and membrane transport activity, its elevation in the absence of conjugated hyperbilirubinemia or other signs of cholestasis supports the hypothesis that higher levels of GGT in early life may, at least in part, be physiological.

Previous studies have established reference ranges for liver enzymes in pediatric populations aged 11 months to 16 years, noting that serum GGT levels vary by age and sex, and are higher in those with increased body mass index [15]. By contrast, our study addresses a critical gap by providing normative data for infants from birth to 18 months, a period previously uncharacterized in the literature. We observed that GGT concentrations were highest during the first months of life and declined with age, with no significant sex-based differences observed in the first 3 months of life. Regarding aminotransferases, prior research has demonstrated higher ALT and AST levels in males, particularly after the first year of life [15]. In our cohort, however, sex differences were minimal during early infancy. Additionally, ALT and AST percentiles in our population were consistently lower than those reported in older pediatric cohorts, underscoring the importance of age-specific interpretation. Notably, the 5th percentile for ALT in the first month of life ranged from 10 IU/L to 13 IU/L in our study, whereas previous studies had found upper reference limits of 60 IU/L for males and 55 IU/L for females under 18 months of age [17].

Moreover, we found that AST levels remained above adult normal ranges for up to 18 months, while ALT levels exceeded adult norms primarily during the first 6 months. In addition, we demonstrated that serum transaminases (ALT and AST) were also significantly higher in breastfed infants. This elevation could be related to the hepatotropic effects of growth factors and other bioactive compounds present in human milk, which promote hepatocyte maturation and metabolic function during early development [26].

A limitation of this study is the lack of data on ethnicity, which has been suggested to influence liver enzyme levels in previous research. Future studies should also examine this aspect, as differences in population characteristics may partly explain discrepancies with earlier reports.

Conclusions

Through serial measurements in a well-defined cohort of healthy term infants, we generated sex- and age-specific percentiles for GGT and aminotransferases levels up to 18 months of life. These reference values represent a useful clinical tool for evaluating liver function in neonates and infants. In addition, our data emphasize the impact of breastfeeding not only on bilirubin metabolism but also on liver enzyme expression, particularly GGT, during early infancy. Together, these findings highlight the importance of interpreting liver enzyme levels in the context of age and feeding practices to help identify low-GGT cholestasis and avoid unnecessary tests.
