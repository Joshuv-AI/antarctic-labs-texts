Uniportal robotic-assisted left upper lobectomy with pulmonary artery reconstruction for a central lung cancer: a step-by-step surgical technique
Authors: Chang Li, Yifan Shi, Cheng Ding, Jun Chen, Diego Gonzalez-Rivas, Jun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (uRATS) has emerged as an advanced minimally invasive platform for complex pulmonary resections. However, its application to centrally located lung cancers requiring vascular reconstruction following neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy remains technically demanding. We present a detailed surgical technique of uniportal robotic-assisted left upper lobectomy with intrapericardial venous division, bronchial transection for exposure, and pulmonary artery (PA) resection and reconstruction in a 70-year-old male with centrally located squamous cell carcinoma. The initial clinical stage was cT4N2M0. Following four cycles of neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, restaging demonstrated residual soft-tissue density closely abutting the PA, with positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 2.71, consistent with residual viable tumor. Through a 4 cm uniportal incision, intrapericardial control of the left upper pulmonary vein was achieved. Due to obliteration of the PA-bronchus plane, early bronchial transection was performed to facilitate safe arterial exposure. The apicoanterior PA trunk was resected under temporary occlusion (total clamp time 26 minutes) and reconstructed using a continuous 5-0 Prolene suture. Estimated blood loss was 100 mL, and no transfusion was required. The postoperative course was uneventful. Final pathology demonstrated a complete pathological response (ycT0N0M0). This case illustrates that, in experienced hands, uRATS can be successfully applied to complex central tumors requiring bronchovascular reconstruction after neoadjuvant therapy.Keywords: Uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (uRATS), bronchovascular reconstruction, left upper lobectomy, robotic pulmonary artery repair (robotic PA repair), neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy

Highlight box

Surgical highlights

• This case illustrates the use of uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (uRATS) for a complex central left upper lobectomy requiring intrapericardial venous division, early bronchial transection, and pulmonary artery (PA) resection with reconstruction after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy. Despite severely altered post-induction anatomy, the entire procedure was performed through a single 4-cm incision, demonstrating the feasibility of applying uRATS to advanced bronchovascular procedures traditionally managed through thoracotomy.

What is conventional and what is novel/modified?

• Conventional surgical principles—including fissure-based exposure, sequential vascular control, and intrapericardial access—were followed. However, several modifications were necessary due to dense fibrosis and the disappearance of normal hilar planes.

• The most notable innovations included deliberate early bronchial transection used as an exposure strategy when the bronchovascular plane was obliterated, and the completion of PA resection and reconstruction entirely through a uniportal robotic platform using wristed instruments and a running 5-0 Prolene suture.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This case highlights how uRATS can safely extend minimally invasive surgery to anatomically demanding central tumors requiring vascular reconstruction, particularly in post-induction settings. The technical refinements demonstrated—such as early bronchial transection and uniportal robotic vascular repair—may serve as useful strategies for similar cases. Wider adoption will require structured training, accumulation of experience in high-volume centers, and further prospective evaluation to define appropriate indications and ensure safe dissemination.

Introduction

Background

Minimally invasive thoracic surgery has advanced considerably over the past decade, particularly in lung cancer management. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) remains a widely used approach, but its limitations—especially restricted instrument articulation and reduced stability during complex hilar dissection—become evident in central tumors requiring vascular or bronchial reconstruction. Robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) was introduced to address these challenges, offering three-dimensional (3D) visualization and enhanced dexterity, with studies demonstrating comparable long-term oncologic outcomes to VATS and potential advantages in lymph node dissection and intraoperative bleeding control (1,2).

Uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (uRATS), first described by González-Rivas and colleagues, further integrates the benefits of robotic technology with the reduced access trauma of a single-incision approach (3-5). Early reports confirm its feasibility for major pulmonary resections, including sleeve and vascular procedures, and suggest potential ergonomic advantages in managing complex central lesions.

Rationale

Central-type lung cancers that closely abut or involve the pulmonary vessels or bronchial structures present significant technical challenges for minimally invasive surgery. VATS may be limited during delicate hilar maneuvers, while multiport RATS, though effective, still necessitates multiple incisions that increase intercostal trauma. Uniportal RATS offers a single-incision alternative that preserves robotic articulation and visualization while minimizing access-related morbidity. Despite increasing experience, there remain few detailed descriptions of uRATS applied to central tumors requiring both intrapericardial venous division and pulmonary artery (PA) resection with reconstruction. Documenting such cases is essential to refine technique and support broader, safe adoption.

Objective

This manuscript describes a step-by-step uniportal Da Vinci robotic left upper lobectomy for a centrally located squamous cell carcinoma following neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy. The procedure required intrapericardial division of the left upper pulmonary vein (LUPV), bronchial transection for exposure, resection of the apicoanterior PA trunk, and PA reconstruction using a running 5-0 Prolene technique. The aim is to illustrate the technical feasibility of performing complex vascular and bronchial procedures through a single robotic incision and to provide operative insights that may inform wider application of uniportal robotic techniques. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jovs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jovs-2025-1-58/rc).

Surgical setting and equipment

All procedures were performed in a certified thoracic surgery operating theatre at a high-volume academic medical center equipped for advanced minimally invasive surgery. A dedicated Da Vinci robotic system (model: Xi) was used, providing a stable platform for single-incision RATS. In this uniportal robotic approach, artificial capnothorax was not employed; therefore, airtight sealing was not required. A non-sealed trocar configuration was used to reduce the external profile of the trocars and minimize arm interference within the single incision. A 3D high-definition camera was employed through the uniportal access, and standard robotic instruments—including a Maryland bipolar dissector, Cadiere forceps, robotic scissors, and a robotic stapler—were prepared. Additional equipment included vascular bulldog clamps for temporary PA occlusion and 5-0 Prolene sutures (HS6856H, Ethicon, USA) for vascular reconstruction. Conventional VATS instruments were available for backup, and blood products were prepared preoperatively due to the anticipated complexity of vessel dissection and reconstruction.

Surgical team and roles

The operation was led by an experienced thoracic surgeon trained in uniportal robotic techniques. A dedicated bedside assistant helped maintain optimal exposure of the operative field, provided timely suction, and assisted with specimen handling. The scrub nurse synchronized robotic instrument exchange, and an anesthesiologist with thoracic expertise maintained single-lung ventilation and hemodynamic stability, particularly during PA clamping.

Patient selection, indications, and contraindications

The patient was a 70-year-old male with a centrally located left upper lobe squamous cell carcinoma diagnosed four months earlier. The initial clinical stage was cT4N2M0. He received four cycles of neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy consisting of albumin-bound paclitaxel, carboplatin, and pembrolizumab, which resulted in substantial tumor shrinkage. However, post-treatment contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed residual soft-tissue density surrounding the root of the LUPV, the left upper lobe bronchial orifice, and the surface of the proximal PA trunk. Positron emission tomography (PET)-CT demonstrated persistent focal fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in these regions, suggesting the presence of residual viable tumor.

Given the anticipated need for precise hilar dissection, possible intrapericardial venous control, bronchial transection, and potential PA resection with reconstruction, the patient was considered an appropriate candidate for uRATS. Contraindications such as inadequate pulmonary function, intolerance to single-lung ventilation, mediastinal invasion, or distant metastasis were excluded.

Preoperative evaluation and preparation

A comprehensive preoperative evaluation was undertaken to assess operative risk and define the extent of residual disease. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated adequate respiratory reserve for single-lung ventilation. Echocardiography and electrocardiography revealed no contraindications to prolonged anesthesia or PA manipulation.

High-resolution contrast-enhanced CT and 3D reconstructions were used to delineate the anatomical relationships between the tumor and hilar structures. Post-neoadjuvant imaging demonstrated persistent soft-tissue density closely abutting the root of the posterior ascending PA branch and the main PA trunk over an estimated length of approximately 1.5 cm, without a clear intervening fat plane. Accurate measurement of residual tumor size was limited due to adjacent atelectasis obscuring tumor boundaries. Representative CT images are shown in Figure 1. PET-CT showed residual metabolic activity with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 2.71, supporting the presence of residual viable tumor. Preoperative bronchoscopy performed after neoadjuvant therapy revealed narrowing of the left upper lobe bronchial orifice with mucosal congestion, without visible endobronchial tumor involvement.

Contrast-enhanced CT images before and after neoadjuvant therapy. (A) Pre-neoadjuvant contrast-enhanced chest CT demonstrating a centrally located left upper lobe tumor. (B) Post-neoadjuvant contrast-enhanced chest CT showing residual soft-tissue density adjacent to the PA. CT, computed tomography; PA, pulmonary artery.

Standard laboratory tests, coagulation profiles, and tumor markers were obtained. Given the anticipated complexity, blood products were cross-matched, and vascular reconstruction instruments—including bulldog clamps for temporary PA occlusion and 5-0 Prolene sutures—were prepared in advance.

Anesthetic preparation included double-lumen endotracheal intubation for left lung collapse and arterial line placement for continuous hemodynamic monitoring. Optional central venous access was considered due to potential PA clamping. The patient was positioned in the right lateral decubitus position with slight table flexion to facilitate intercostal space opening. Skin preparation extended widely across the hemithorax, and a urinary catheter and warming measures were applied.

Prior to incision, the robotic arms were aligned to optimize reach and minimize external collision during uniportal access. All equipment and emergency conversion tools were verified before docking.

Step-by-step description

Patient positioning, incision, and robotic setup

The patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus position. A 4 cm incision was created in the 6th intercostal space at the anterior axillary line, and a wound protector was inserted. The 30° camera was positioned at the dorsal aspect of the incision, with two robotic working arms arranged ventrally in sequence. A small ventral portion of the incision was intentionally preserved for the bedside assistant to introduce a suction device. The Da Vinci Xi system was then docked in a caudal-to-cranial direction, ensuring optimal triangulation through the single-port approach. A step-by-step demonstration of the operative workflow is provided in Video 1. The key operative steps of the procedure are illustrated in Figure 2.

Key intraoperative steps of the uniportal robotic procedure. (A) Intrapericardial division of the LUPV. (B) Bronchial transection to expose the PA. (C) Resection of the PA wall under temporary occlusion. (D) Continuous running suture reconstruction of the PA. LUPV, left upper pulmonary vein; PA, pulmonary artery.

Adhesiolysis and fissure-guided hilar dissection

Upon entering the thoracic cavity, fibrotic adhesions attributed to prior neoadjuvant therapy were encountered and carefully released with an electrocautery hook to restore exposure of the hilum. The oblique fissure was partially fused, and it was developed and divided using a combination of the Maryland bipolar dissector and a stapler, creating a clear operative plane toward the hilar vessels. This approach enabled prompt identification of the lingular artery, which was divided with a stapler, followed by meticulous dissection, ligation, and division of the C-segmental artery. These steps improved upper-lobe mobility and facilitated subsequent central hilar dissection.

Intrapericardial dissection and division of the LUPV

Hilar exposure revealed residual tumor involvement around the root of the LUPV, making extrapericardial dissection hazardous.

A pericardial window was created anterior to the phrenic nerve. The intrapericardial segment of the LUPV was mobilized and divided using a robotic stapler, improving access to the PA and bronchial structures. Care was taken to identify and preserve the phrenic nerve during pericardial opening. Dissection was performed anterior to the nerve, and energy devices were used cautiously with a safe distance to avoid traction or thermal injury.

Bronchial transection due to obliterated PA-bronchus plane

The left upper lobe bronchus was densely adherent to the PA trunk, with complete obliteration of the normal dissection plane due to fibrosis and residual tumor. Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT showed no bronchial root involvement, and intraoperative fiberoptic bronchoscopy confirmed a clear proximal airway prior to transection. To facilitate safe exposure of the central PA, the bronchus was sharply transected using robotic scissors at the planned resection level. Endobronchial suction was performed before division, and single-lung ventilation was maintained throughout the maneuver. The distal bronchial segment was then gently retracted to improve visualization of the PA trunk.

Temporary occlusion of the PA and resection of the apicoanterior trunk (A1+3)

The PA was temporarily occluded using bulldog clamps placed proximally at the root of the left PA and distally on the PA trunk, just distal to the origin of the lingular artery, after confirming hemodynamic stability. The apicoanterior arterial trunk (A1+3), partially infiltrated by tumor, was sharply resected using robotic scissors. The arterial defect was assessed to ensure clear margins.

PA reconstruction with continuous running sutures

After resection of the involved apicoanterior trunk, the PA defect measured approximately 2.5 cm in length, involving nearly one-third of the arterial circumference. Temporary occlusion of the PA was maintained for a total of 26 minutes. A brief loosening of the clamp was performed intraoperatively to assess the suture line, followed by immediate reapplication for reinforcement. Hemodynamic parameters remained stable throughout the occlusion period.

Given the limited circumferential involvement, primary closure was performed without patch augmentation. Reconstruction was completed using a continuous running 5-0 Prolene suture with a taper needle. Suturing was performed in a back-and-forth running fashion with evenly spaced bites of approximately 1.5 mm, and the arterial edges were gently everted to ensure precise approximation and maintain luminal patency. After completion of the initial suture line, the needle tip was inserted into the vessel lumen to inject heparinized saline, allowing evacuation of residual air before final tightening.

Upon gradual release of the bulldog clamps, minor oozing was observed and controlled with additional reinforcing stitches. Mild luminal narrowing was noted after closure; however, visual assessment demonstrated adequate flow to the remaining left lower lobe. At the completion of lung re-expansion, the PA exhibited satisfactory pulsation without evidence of compromised perfusion. Systemic heparinization was not routinely administered.

Completion of bronchial stump division and specimen retrieval

After completion of PA reconstruction and confirmation of vascular integrity, attention was returned to the bronchial stump. The proximal bronchial margin was submitted for frozen section analysis and confirmed negative. Definitive closure of the bronchial stump was then achieved using a robotic stapler inserted through the working arm, ensuring adequate stump length and alignment. The bronchial stump was checked for air leakage under saline irrigation during lung inflation. A single chest tube was placed through the same incision. The robotic system was undocked, and the incision was closed in layers. The total operative time was 7 hours and 14 minutes. The total estimated blood loss was 100 mL, and no intraoperative or postoperative transfusion was required.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored for hemodynamic stability, oxygenation, and chest tube output, with particular attention to bleeding from the reconstructed PA and air leak from the bronchial stump. A chest radiograph was obtained to confirm satisfactory lung expansion. Multimodal analgesia was administered to facilitate effective coughing and early mobilization, and the patient engaged in incentive spirometry and ambulation beginning on postoperative day 1 to support pulmonary recovery. Surveillance focused on chest tube drainage characteristics and on clinical signs suggestive of delayed vascular or bronchial complications, while lung expansion was assessed through routine postoperative evaluation rather than repeated imaging. The chest tube was removed once the lung remained fully expanded with minimal drainage and no air leak.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4. Final pathology demonstrated a complete pathological response, with no residual viable tumor in the lung specimen and no metastatic involvement in hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes, consistent with a post-neoadjuvant clinical stage of ycT0N0M0. The patient subsequently continued maintenance immunotherapy as planned. During two years of follow-up, no evidence of recurrence was observed.

Tips and pearls

Successful uniportal robotic resection for centrally located tumors relies on precise preoperative planning and meticulous intraoperative execution. Proper alignment of the 6th intercostal incision allows the camera to establish a stable posterior viewing axis, while the sequential arrangement of robotic arms maintains instrument triangulation within the limited uniportal corridor. This configuration minimizes external arm conflict and provides the surgeon with an in-line approach to the hilum, which is critical for vascular dissection and suturing.

During hilar exposure, gradual fissure development with a Maryland dissector provides a controlled pathway toward the arterial branches while reducing the risk of parenchymal tearing. Early division of the anterior fissure and lingular or segmental arteries improves mobility of the upper lobe and opens the working space for subsequent central dissection. When the LUPV root is indistinguishable due to tumor involvement or dense fibrosis, intrapericardial exposure offers a safer and more reproducible means of gaining circumferential control, avoiding the risk of venous avulsion in a narrow hilum.

When the bronchus and PA are inseparable due to fibrosis or tumor adherence, early bronchial transection may be employed as an exposure strategy. This maneuver should be considered only when proximal bronchial involvement has been excluded and oncologic margins can be reliably defined preoperatively and intraoperatively. Adequate residual bronchial length must be preserved to permit controlled traction without tension, and traction sutures may be used if direct grasping is insufficient. Definitive stump closure is performed after completion of vascular reconstruction, with margin confirmation prior to final stapled division and routine air-leak testing to ensure secure bronchial integrity. This approach should be avoided when proximal margin status is uncertain or when extensive airway involvement is suspected.

For arterial reconstruction, the uniportal robotic platform offers significant advantages through enhanced articulation and stable magnified visualization. A running back-and-forth 5-0 Prolene suture is effective for reconstructing the arterial wall even within a restricted hilar space. Before final tightening, saline injection via the needle tip ensures adequate de-airing of the lumen. Controlled release of the bulldog clamps allows assessment of the repair under physiologic pressure; minor oozing can be readily addressed with additional reinforcing stitches. The combination of wristed instrument dexterity, intuitive motion scaling, and direct visual access makes the robotic system particularly well suited for complex vascular reconstructions through a single incision.

In addition to technical considerations, awareness of potential intraoperative pitfalls and predefined bailout strategies is essential during complex bronchovascular procedures. Major vascular injury represents the most critical intraoperative risk during uniportal robotic bronchovascular procedures. In the event of acute hemorrhage, immediate compression of the bleeding site is performed using a robotic instrument (typically the Maryland dissector or Cadiere forceps), while the bedside assistant simultaneously introduces suction through the uniportal incision to maintain visualization. The robotic camera is repositioned to obtain direct exposure of the bleeding source, and non-essential robotic arms may be temporarily withdrawn to reduce external instrument crowding.

If direct repair cannot be safely performed under initial exposure, prompt proximal control of the main PA should be obtained. Intrapericardial clamping is considered when hilar exposure is limited due to tumor infiltration or fibrosis. Temporary compression is maintained until secure proximal control is achieved.

The decision to add an additional port versus converting directly to thoracotomy is based on hemodynamic stability and the ability to obtain proximal control within a short time frame. An additional port may be considered if visualization is suboptimal but bleeding remains controlled. Immediate conversion to thoracotomy is undertaken in cases of uncontrolled hemorrhage, inability to secure proximal vascular control, rapid hemodynamic deterioration, or suspicion of extensive arterial injury.

All emergency conversion instruments and blood products were verified and prepared prior to docking. Establishing a predefined bailout strategy is essential to maintain patient safety while preserving minimally invasive intent whenever feasible.

Discussion

Surgical highlights

This case demonstrates that uRATS can be extended to complex central lung cancers requiring combined intrapericardial venous division, bronchial transection for exposure, and PA resection with reconstruction after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy. Neoadjuvant treatment is known to induce dense fibrosis, inflammatory adhesions, and distortion of normal hilar planes, which may increase the technical complexity of minimally invasive resection, particularly when vascular control and reconstruction are required. In the present case, the PA-bronchus interface was completely obliterated, necessitating strategic exposure and controlled vascular repair in a fibrotic operative field.

Our experience aligns with growing evidence that uRATS can be successfully applied to advanced bronchovascular procedures, including sleeve resections and complex reconstructions, as demonstrated in subsequent multi-institutional studies (6). The uniportal configuration provided reduced access trauma while preserving a direct, coaxial approach to the hilum, enabling safe dissection in a heavily fibrotic post-treatment field. The robotic system offered the necessary dexterity and visual precision to perform vascular resection and reconstruction through a single incision, underscoring the potential of uRATS as a platform for advanced bronchovascular procedures. Rather than functioning merely as a minimally invasive alternative to multiport robotics or VATS, uRATS in this setting demonstrated its capability to accommodate surgically demanding central tumors that traditionally necessitate thoracotomy.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of the uniportal robotic approach lies in combining minimal access with high technical precision. Compared with uniportal VATS, uRATS offers greater stability and articulation, particularly valuable when tissue planes are distorted by tumor infiltration or post-treatment fibrosis. This allowed safe intrapericardial dissection and vascular reconstruction through a single incision. However, the technique has limitations: it requires extensive surgeon experience in both multiport robotics and complex open vascular procedures, and its safety cannot be generalized until larger datasets become available. As most published uRATS series come from high-volume centers, outcomes may not be reproducible in lower-volume settings, and long-term oncologic data for central tumors requiring vascular reconstruction remain limited.

Comparison with other surgical techniques and research

Existing evidence comparing RATS and VATS shows that robotic surgery achieves comparable oncologic outcomes with potential advantages in lymph node dissection, bleeding control, and conversion rates, although at higher cost and longer operative time (7,8). These data, however, primarily address standard lobectomy. Reports of uRATS demonstrate feasibility for sleeve resections, bronchovascular reconstructions, and even carinal procedures, suggesting enhanced capability for complex hilar operations compared with uniportal VATS due to superior ergonomics and suturing control (9,10). Nevertheless, vascular reconstruction—particularly PA repair—remains rarely reported in the uRATS literature, making the present case a meaningful addition. It aligns with emerging reports that uRATS may serve as an intermediate platform between multiport robotics and advanced uniportal thoracic surgery, offering enhanced precision in settings where conventional uniportal VATS would be technically prohibitive.

Implications and actions recommended

The experience from this case suggests that uniportal robotic surgery can expand minimally invasive options for selected patients with central lung cancers involving major vessels, provided that preoperative planning is meticulous and the surgical team has established expertise in both robotic surgery and vascular reconstruction. Intrapericardial venous control, bronchial transection for exposure, and robotic vascular suturing represent transferable strategies that may benefit similar cases. Nonetheless, broader adoption should be cautious: complex vascular reconstructions should be limited to experienced, high-volume teams, and further prospective studies and multicenter registries are necessary to define safety profiles, oncologic outcomes, and indications for uRATS in central tumors requiring reconstruction.

Conclusions

This case shows that uRATS can be successfully used for selected central lung cancers requiring intrapericardial venous division and PA reconstruction. The robotic platform provided stable visualization and precise instrument control, allowing these complex steps to be completed through a single incision. While the procedure proved technically feasible and safe in this patient, further studies with larger cohorts are needed to determine its broader applicability and long-term oncologic outcomes.
