Algae extract supplementation boosts humoral immunity induced by attenuated PRRSV-1 vaccination
Authors: Caroline Hervet, Frédérick Bussy, Anne Morvan, Francisco da Rocha Pinto, Catherine Belloc, Olivier Bourry, Pauline Maisonnasse, Pi Nyvall, Nicolas Bertho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPorcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS) remains a major concern for the swine industry worldwide due to its significant economic impact and its association with respiratory co-infections. Vaccination is a key strategy for PRRS control; however, the most effective commercial anti-PRRSV vaccines to date—live attenuated vaccines, also named modified live virus (MLV) vaccines—provide only partial protection. This study investigates whether oral supplementation with formulated Ulva-derived seaweed extracts can enhance immune responses to PRRSV-1 MLV vaccination.MethodsTwo supplementation protocols using commercial seaweed extracts formulated with vitamin A and D (Searup®) were tested: one starting one day post-vaccination for five consecutive days (post-vaccination), and another beginning one day before vaccination and continuing for two days post-vaccination (pre-vaccination).In this exploratory farm-based experimental setting, pigs were divided into six groups: three vaccinated groups (supplemented with seaweed either pre- or post-vaccination or not supplemented), and three non-vaccinated control groups receiving the same supplementation regimes. Serum cytokine levels, PRRSV viral loads, and anti-PRRSV antibody responses were measured to assess immune modulation.ResultsPost-vaccination supplementation had no significant effect on vaccine viral load or humoral response, though a modest reduction in viral persistence was noted at 12 days post-vaccination (dpv). In contrast, pre-vaccination supplementation significantly increased vaccine virus levels at 4 dpv (p = 0.048) and enhanced anti-PRRSV IgG levels (p = 0.029). This protocol also induced an early and sustained decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting an immunomodulatory effect of algal extract supplemented with Vitamins A and D.We present here for the first time, in field conditions and in the target species, significant immune modifications upon realistic oral supplementation with Searup®. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the potential of pre-vaccination seaweed supplementation as a cost-effective, practical strategy to enhance humoral responses to PRRSV vaccination. Further studies are needed to evaluate its impact on neutralizing antibody production, cellular responses and protection against heterologous field PRRSV strains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05085-0.

Implications

Vaccines are the most effective tool against infectious diseases; however, their efficacy can still be improved. Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) is a major contributor to the porcine respiratory disease complex, significantly impacting both animal well-being and the swine industry. In this study, we demonstrate that oral supplementation with commercial seaweed extract formulated with vitamins A and D around the time of PRRSV vaccination enhances the anti-PRRSV antibody response. This approach could serve as a cost-effective and easily implementable strategy to improve protection against porcine viral diseases.

Background

Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS) is a swine-specific viral disease that is enzootic in most pig-producing countries and remains one of the most economically impactful diseases affecting the swine industry [1, 2]. First reported in North America and in Europe [3] in the 1990 s, the virus subsequently spreads to Asia [4]. PRRS virus (PRRSV) is sub-divided into two species: PRRSV-1, mainly found in Europe, and PRRSV-2, predominantly present in North America and Asia [4, 5]. PRRSV causes reproductive disorders in pregnant sows and respiratory problems and growth delays in finishing pigs [3, 6]. Furthermore, PRRSV often contributes to the porcine respiratory complex (PRC) and thus, exacerbates respiratory disorders in association with other infectious agents [7], which leads to increased antibiotics use [8].

Several strategies have been implemented to control PRRS, combining biosecurity measures, diagnostic surveillance and vaccination [9, 10]. Among these, vaccination remains the most widespread strategy. Two types of commercial vaccines are available: inactivated vaccines are preferred in pregnant sows, and Modified Live Virus (MLV) vaccines are authorized for use in both sows and growing pigs [9, 11]. Although MLV vaccines offer better protection than inactivated ones, their efficacy remains limited. They reduce clinical signs and viral replication but do not prevent infection. Additionally, they carry risks of viral shedding, recombination with wild strains, and potential reversion to virulence. Protection also varies depending on the genetic similarity between circulating field strains and the vaccine strain. Inactivated vaccines are considered safer but provide insufficient protection and are mainly used as booster vaccines during gestation [12].

In parallel, to reduce antibiotic usage, the development of prophylactic alternatives such as probiotics, plant extracts and bacteriophages are on the rise. Numerous studies have demonstrated the antiviral properties of polysaccharides extracted from plants, algae, microorganisms, and fungi [13, 14]. In this context, the use of algae in veterinary medicine is generating increasing interest due to their multiple biological activities. Algae are employed in various areas of livestock farming from animal nutrition [13] to disease prevention [15] and treatment. Seaweeds contain bioactive molecules such as marine sulphated polysaccharides (MSP), carotenoids, and phenolic compounds with antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiviral [13, 16], and antitumoral [15] activities.

Some in vitro studies have shown an inhibitory effect of algae on PRRSV replication [17]. In a previous in vitro study we demonstrated that Ulva extract induces transcriptomic upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes (IL-8, IL-6, IFN-β) in immune cells and inhibits PRRSV replication in alveolar macrophages, but not in monocytes, following short-term exposure [18]. Additionally, we observed increased transfer of anti–Bordetella immunoglobulins in colostrum following oral supplementation with seaweed, suggesting a potential immunomodulatory effect [19].

In the present exploratory study, we aimed to investigate the in vivo effects of oral supplementation with algae commercial formulation on immune responses to PRRSV vaccination and viral replication. We used a PRRSV-1 MLV vaccine, commonly administered in French farms (Ingelvac PRRSFLEX® EU), both as a standard vaccination model and as proxy for acute infection, as previously done in studies using the Yellow Fever vaccine in humans [20]. Two supplementation protocols using the commercial Searup® product, consisting in an Ulva fraction formulated with vitamins A and D, following OLMIX recommendations for farm application, were evaluated: (1) an “anti-infectious” protocol, where pigs were supplemented for five consecutive days following ‘infection’ (i.e. MLV injection), which mirrors the typical administration method during PRRSV outbreaks on farms; (2) a “vaccination enhancement” protocol, with supplementation one day before and two days after vaccination, with no supplementation on the vaccination day. For both approaches, we monitored vaccine viral load, PRRSV-specific (Ab) responses, and serum levels of cytokines involved in the innate and adaptive immune responses. In this exploratory study, the immune response was assessed over a 12-day period — the minimum duration required to monitor serum viral load and cytokine levels, as well as to evaluate anti-PRRSV IgG antibodies.

Discussion

Despite decades of research, the development of broadly effective anti-PRRSV vaccines remains a major challenge [11]. Current MLV vaccines confer only partial clinical protection, particularly when pigs are exposed to heterologous field strains [9]. Within the One Health framework, there is a growing interest in the use of natural products – such as seaweed – as immunomodulatory agents to support or enhance host immune responses [19, 21]. While several in vitro studies have reported antiviral effects of algae [17], in vivo data remain scarce. The present study aimed to assess the effects of oral seaweed supplementation on both PRRS MLV replication and the associated innate and adaptive immune responses under field-like conditions.

Conducted in a commercial pig farm, the study reflected realistic animal management and exposure to environmental immune stimuli. However, this setting also introduced substantial inter-individual variability, which limited statistical power. This variability likely contributed to the modest differences observed in immune parameters and underlines the importance of replication in controlled conditions.

Standalone oral seaweed supplementation

In unvaccinated animals, the two Searup® supplementation regimens were first tested to assess the standalone immunomodulatory effect of seaweed: a continuous five-day post-vaccination regimen (CtSwPost) and a discontinuous “pre/post” regiment (CtSwPre), reflecting common on-farm practices. Neither protocol resulted in significant changes in systemic inflammatory cytokines and IL-10 levels, suggesting that seaweed extract alone does not induce strong innate immune activation in the absence of vaccination.

Post-vaccination Searup® supplementation

In vaccinated animals, oral seaweed supplementation after vaccination (VacSwPost) was set to recapitulate the anti-infectious protocol proposed to farmers, by treating animals for five consecutive days upon PRRSV outbreak. Herein, the PRRSV ‘infectious’ outbreak was mimicked by attenuated PRRSV vaccine injection. This protocol has minimal impact on vaccine viral replication or immune responses compared with the vaccinated control (Vac). However, at 12 dpv, a trend toward an earlier decline in viremia was observed in the VacSwPost group. This is in line with previous studies describing polysaccharide-mediated antiviral effects [22]. It is important to consider here that the attenuated vaccine strain used in this study does not induce clinical disease, which may limit the observable impact of immune modulation. Moreover, the vaccine strain was administered intramuscularly, while natural PRRSV infection typically occurs via mucosal surfaces, particularly the respiratory tract. Further studies using wild-type PRRSV challenge models via the intranasal route would provide a more physiologically relevant context to assess the immunomodulatory potential of seaweed.

Pre-vaccination Searup® supplementation

In vaccinated animals, oral seaweed supplementation one day before and two days after vaccination (VacSwPre) was set to recapitulate the pro-vaccination protocol proposed to farmers. In this group, a significant rise in vaccine virus replication was accompanied by early and sustained increases in anti-PRRSV IgG levels. This was somewhat unexpected, as algae-derived compounds have been associated with in vitro antiviral activity [18]. Indeed, sulfated polysaccharides extracts from Ulva sp, present in Searup®, can present a direct antiviral activity effective against human metapneumovirus [23], human T-cell lymphotropic virus type 1 [24], avian influenza viruses [25] and Japanese Encephalitis Virus [22]. This activity would mostly be due to the capacity of the MSP to block viral receptors and inhibit their entry into their target cells [26]. This discrepancy with our data could be explained by differences in seaweed species, supplementation timing and duration, extraction methods, purity, or specific bioactive compound composition – factors known to influence biological effects. To note these studies were mostly in vitro except one. Concerning PRRSV and porcine cells, in a previous study [18] we find no inhibitory effect of neither MSP extracts nor Searup® formulation on monocytes, in agreement with the in vivo data presented here.

In parallel, a reduction in serum IL-10 and IL-1α was observed. IL-10 is an anti-inflammatory cytokine involved in modulating Th1 responses. Its downregulation, together with reduced IL-1α and IL-6, may have fostered a more permissive immune environment, facilitating both viral replication and enhanced Ab production. These findings suggest that, under certain conditions, Searup® may act as an immunostimulant that promotes humoral immunity. The algae supplementation was formulated as the commercial product Searup®, with the addition of low concentrations of vitamins A and D3 (850,000 and 200,000 IU/kg, respectively). These vitamins are known to stimulate immunity [27]. In pigs, Vitamins A and D3 have been reported to increase local intestinal IgA responses with no [28] or negative impact on serum IgG [29]. While we cannot exclude the possibility that these vitamins may directly influence the effect of ulvan extracts upon viral infection, Bussy’s study [30] on Marek's disease in poultry reported no significant effect with vitamins A and D3 alone. Overall, we conclude that the algae extract formulated as Searup® enhances the humoral response to MLV PRRS vaccination.

Sulfated polysaccharides extracts have also been shown to act directly on immune cells through porcine TLR4 receptor (Berri, Algal Research 2017), a receptor mainly expressed on immune cells among them monocytes and macrophages, but also on endothelial and epithelial cells. TLR4 activation promotes the activation of cells and their release of inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα, IL-6 and IL-1. An in vivo effect on B cells upon oral feeding, either direct or indirect, has also been described [19]. Our results are also consistent with studies in murine models of influenza, where seaweed-derived compounds prior to infection enhanced Ab levels [31, 32] and neutralizing Ab titers, along with a significant protection against influenza [33].

Additionally, the short 12-day follow-up window did not allow assessment of neutralizing Ab responses, which generally appear 4 to 6 weeks post-PRRSV vaccination [34]. Prolonged monitoring would be essential to assess the long-term effects of seaweed supplementation on neutralizing Ab production and its potential to enhance durable immunity to PRRSV. The increase of antibodies response without neutralizing antibodies production might exposed to antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) [35]. Concerning PRRSV, ADE occurrence remain debated [36, 37]. Indeed, enhanced disease after experimental vaccination with live attenuated vaccines has not been observed, although the challenge infections were frequently performed in absence of detectable neutralizing antibodies [38–40]. Another safety concern involves the potential increase in vaccine viral load, which may facilitate transmission of the vaccine virus between animals as well as recombination of viral strain with circulating wild type virus. Although this issue requires further investigation, it is important to note that the vaccine used in this study (Ingelvac PRRSFLEX® EU) is among the least transmissible (personal data) and least symptomatic attenuated vaccines available, thereby substantially reducing potential risks.

Interleukin-10 upregulation is a feature of PRRSV infections [41, 42] and is thought to contribute to the induction of regulatory T cells [43–45]. Thus, the decrease of serum IL-10 might diminish the PRRSV-mediated regulatory T cells induction, and thus increase the T helper activation, and their positive impact on B cells differentiation and immunoglobulin production. Conversely, IL10 is also involved in the control of inflammation and its downregulation may facilitate pathologic inflammation. However, IL-10 decrease is concomitant with the downregulation of IL-1α and IL-6, two well-known cytokines involved in vivo in inflammation, and frequently associated with higher pathogeny of PRRSV infection [46–49], what might equilibrate the pro and anti-inflammatory stimuli.

Finally, this study focused primarily on humoral and cytokine responses. A more comprehensive analysis, including the cellular arms of adaptive immunity, such as T-cell subsets and cytokine-producing lymphocytes, would help clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed effects.

Comparison of post-vaccination and pre-vaccination oral Searup® supplementation

Our experimental settings were chosen to explore separately two different field protocols: the ‘anti-infectious treatment’, with Searup® given post- ‘infection’ and the pro-vaccination treatment, with Searup® given before and after vaccination, including a one day interruption the day of vaccination. The different design of these two protocols complicate their comparison, hence our choice to display them separately in the result section. However, taken together, they suggest that the timing of seaweed supplementation relative to vaccination plays a critical role in shaping the immune response, and that supplementation around the time of vaccination may enhance PRRSV-specific humoral responses while modulating inflammatory cytokine production. In a previous work we observed that in vitro Searup® stimulation had no impact on wild type PRRSV replication on monocytes or alveolar macrophages [18]. However, the attenuated PRRSV vaccine used here: Ingelvac PRRSFLEX®EU, like most of the MLV vaccines, has been attenuated by amplification on MARC145 green monkey kidney epithelial cell line, and does note replicate on primary monocytes and macrophages. Unfortunately, neither its porcine in vivo replication targets nor the receptor used by attenuated virus for infection is known. This make the vaccine’s increase viremia upon Searup® supplementation difficult to interpret. Two hypothesis can be proposed: an increase in receptor expression on target cells (epithelial or endothelial cells?) and/or a decrease in molecules involved in PRRSV replication control such as, among others, type I IFN [50], or host microRNA [51].

Conclusion

This initial in vivo immunological study suggests that oral Searup® supplementation administered around the time of vaccination could influence both vaccine virus replication and the host immune response against PRRSV. These findings pave the way for more comprehensive studies assessing the induction of neutralizing Ab, cellular adaptive immunity, and clinical protection following challenge with wild-type PRRSV strains. While these preliminary results require further validation, seaweed supplementation appears to be a promising, cost-effective, and easily implementable strategy to enhance vaccine responses against PRRSV-1 – and possibly PRRSV-2 – in commercial pig farming. Moreover, oral seaweed supplementation represents a practical approach that could be integrated into broader vaccination programs targeting other swine pathogens, with the aim of improving herd immunity and reducing the economic impact of endemic viral diseases in the pig industry.

Materials and methods

Vaccine

The PRRS MLV vaccine, Ingelvac PRRSFLEX® EU, derived from a PRRSV-1 strain, was obtained from Boehringer IngelHeim®. Pigs were vaccinated intramuscularly behind the ear at day 0 with a 1 ml dose of the vaccine.

Algae extract

Preliminary in vitro assays were carried out to select the most effective batch of Algae extract, following the protocol described in [18]. The selected extract was formulated as Searup® (Olmix SA, Bréhan, France) and administered orally at a dose of 3 ml per pig using a drencher. Searup® is a commercial product composed of an Ulva fraction formulated with vitamins A (850,000 IU/kg) and D3 (200,000 IU/kg), in accordance with European regulations (EU 2015/724 and EU 2017/1492).

Animals and experimental design

The experiment took place on a commercial fattening farm free of PRRSV. A multi-group experiment was designed to assess the effect of oral seaweed supplementation administered at different time points in pigs vaccinated or not with a single dose of Ingelvac® PRRSFLEX EU MLV vaccine. The administration of Searup supplementation, either concurrently with or following vaccination, reflects common practices in pig farming management. Using fifty-eight 8-week-old Large White-Landrace pigs (Sus scrofa), equally distributed between sexes and an average body weight of 25 kg. Animals were randomly assigned to six groups. All groups were housed within the same building, with control and vaccinated groups placed in separate rooms to prevent cross-contamination. Animals were distributed across six pens according to their treatment. Seronegativity was confirmed individually two days prior to vaccination using an anti-PRRSV IgG enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (pigtype® PRRSV Ab; PT272753, Indical Bioscience, Leipzig, Germany). At the beginning of the experiment, the farm was confirmed free of PRRSV genotype 1 and 2 field strains via RT-qPCR (Adiavet PRRSV kit—ADI133-100—Bio-X Diagnostics).

Three pigs from the control group were excluded from the analysis due to unexpected detection of the vaccine strain in their serum; the source of contamination remained unknown. All remaining pigs in the control groups were confirmed free of the vaccine strain up to 12 days post-vaccination. Animals in the vaccinated groups were confirmed free of the vaccine strain before vaccination. Blood samples were collected via the vena jugulars using sterile tubes, with pigs restrained by lasso. Protocols were approved by the institutional ethical committee “CERVO” under the statement number CERVO-2021–19-V.

At 12 days post-vaccination, animals were moved to the fattening unit.

The study included six experimental groups (Fig. 1):(i)Two groups received no seaweed supplementation and were either vaccinated (Vac – n = 10) or unvaccinated controls (Ct – n = 7);(ii)Two groups received oral seaweed supplementation from 1 to 5 days post-vaccination (dpv), and were either vaccinated (VacSwPost – n = 11) or unvaccinated (CtSwPost – n = 10);(iii)Two groups received oral seaweed supplementation at −1, 1 and 2 dpv, and were either vaccinated (VacSwPre – n = 10) or unvaccinated (CtSwPre – n = 10).

Sample preparation

Blood samples were collected at −2, 0, 2, 4, 8 and 12 dpv in dry tubes (Fig. 1). Tubes were kept at 4 °C for 2 h to allow clot formation, then centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Serum was aliquoted (2 × 200 µl were stored at −20 °C for short-term use and 2 × 500 µl were stored at −80 °C for long-term biobanking).

Virus quantification

Viral and total RNA extraction and reverse transcription

Viral RNA was extracted from 500 µl of serum collected at each time point using the Nucleospin Virus kit (Macherey–Nagel, ref.740983.250), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was eluted in 30 µl of RNAse-free water. Quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoPhotometer (Implen, Munich, Germany). Extracted RNA was stored at −80 °C. Reverse-transcription (RT) was performed using 10 µl of RNA and the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-rad, Hercules, CA, USA), as per the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative PCR for PRRSV-MLV

PRRSV genomic RNA was amplified using probes and primers targeting the ORF7 region of the PRRSV-1 MLV strain (forward: 5’-AACGYTCCCTCTGCTTGC-3’; reverse: 5’-CTCAACCTGAAAACTGACCTTCC-3’; probe: 6FAM-CGATCCAGACGGCTTTYAATCAAGGCG-TAM). A duplex qPCR was performed including porcine β-actin as an internal control (forward: 5’-CTCGATCATGAAGTGCGACGT-3’; reverse: 5’-GTGATCTCCTTCTGCATCCTGTC-3’; probe: TET-ATCAGGAAGGACCTCTACGCCAACACGG-BHQ1) according to [52]. Viral RNA amplification was conducted on a CFX Opus Real-Time PCR System (Biorad), using the Eurogentec Taqman Master Mix, 2 µl of cDNA and 10 µM of each primer in a final volume of 10 µl.

To establish a standard curve for absolute quantification, serial dilutions of the PRRSV-MLV vaccine strain (106.2 TCID50/ml) were processed the same way as serum samples. Results were expressed as “equivalent TCID50/ml”. Ct values above 36 were not analyzed.

The following qPCR program was used: enzyme activation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 6 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s.

Detection of anti-PRRSV IgG

Serum samples from −2, 8 and 12 dpv were analyzed for anti-PRRSV IgG using a commercial kit (pigtype® PRRSV Ab; PT272753, IndicalBioscience, Leipzig, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Results were expressed as Sample-to-Positive (S/P) ratios. Samples with S/P ≥ 0.4 were considered seropositive.

Luminex assay

Cytokine levels in serum were assessed at −2, 2 and 6 dpv using a multiplex Luminex assay (Millipore Porcine Cytokine kit, ref. PCYTMAG-23 K-08) for IFN-γ, interleukins IL-1β, IL-1α, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8 (CXCL-8), IL-10, IL-12, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and Granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and a Bio-plex 200 analyzer (Bio-Rad) as previously described [53]. Upper and Lower Limits of Quantifications (ULQ and LLQ) were: 24.41–10,000 pg/ml for GM-CSF, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, IL-18, TNFα; 122.07–500,000 pg/ml for IFN-γ, IL-1β; 4.88–20,000 pg/ml for IL-1α and 12,21–500,000 pg/ml for IL-8.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed using R software (v 4.3.1). Viral loads and Ab levels were compared between Vac vs VacSwPre or Vac vs VacSwPost at each time point using non-parametric Mann–Whitney U tests. Cytokine concentrations were compared between control groups at each time point using Kruskal–Wallis tests. Cytokine concentrations were compared between Vac vs VacSwPost or Vac vs VacSwPre non-parametric Mann–Whitney U tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to reduce dataset dimensionality and identify patterns of variation across variables and experimental groups.
