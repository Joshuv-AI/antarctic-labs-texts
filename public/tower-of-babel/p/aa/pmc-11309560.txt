CD19-Directed CAR T-Cells in a Patient With Refractory MOGAD
Authors: Jose Maria Cabrera-Maqueda, Maria Sepulveda, Raquel Ruiz García, Guillermo Muñoz-Sánchez, Nuria Martínez-Cibrian, Valentín Ortíz-Maldonado, Daniel Lorca-Arce, Mar Guasp, Sara Llufriu, Eugenia Martinez-Hernandez, Thais Armangue, Elianet G. Fonseca, María Teresa Alba-Isasi, Julio Delgado, Josep Dalmau, Manel Juan, Albert Saiz, Yolanda Blanco
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), relapse prevention and the treatment approach to refractory symptoms are unknown. We report a patient with refractory MOGAD treated with CD19-directed CAR T-cells.MethodsCD19-directed CAR T-cells (ARI-0001) were produced in-house by lentiviral transduction of autologous fresh leukapheresis and infused after a conventional lymphodepleting regimen.ResultsA 18-year-old man developed 2 episodes of myelitis associated with serum MOG-IgG, which were followed by 6 episodes of left optic neuritis (ON) and sustained the presence of MOG-IgG over 6 years despite multiple immunotherapies. After the sixth episode of ON, accompanied by severe residual visual deficits, CAR T-cell treatment was provided without complications. Follow-up of cell counts showed complete depletion of CD19+ B cells at day +7; reconstituted B cells at day +141 showing a naïve B-cell phenotype, and low or absent memory B cells and plasmablasts for 1 year. MOG-IgG titers have remained undetectable since CAR T-cell infusion. The patient had an early episode of left ON at day +29, when MOG-IgG was already negative, and since then he has remained free of relapses without immunotherapy for 1 year.DiscussionThis clinical case shows that CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy is well-tolerated and is a potential treatment for patients with refractory MOGAD.Classification of EvidenceThis provides Class IV evidence. It is a single observational study without controls.

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD)1 is an autoimmune demyelinating disease driven by attack-related clinical deficits and pathologically characterized by perivenous demyelination, predominant CD4+ T-cell infiltration, and complement deposition in active lesions.2,3 Owing to the lack of clinical trials, the best treatment approach is still unclear, and immunosuppressors, such as B-cell depleting therapies, are used for relapse prevention.2 Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cells genetically engineered to recognize CD19 and other B-cell surface proteins have started been used in several autoimmune diseases,4 such as systemic lupus erythematosus,5 myasthenia gravis,6 among other.7,8 Here, we report the use of autologous CD19-directed CAR T-cells in a patient with refractory MOGAD.

Case Report

A 25-year-old man developed when he was 18 years old sensory deficits in the lower limbs followed by severe paraparesis and urinary retention (Expanded Disability Status Scale, EDSS, score 7.5) associated with a spinal cord lesion that extended from the thoracic level (T4) to conus and serum MOG-IgG (titer 1:160) detected by live cell-based assay.9 He did not have medical history of interest, and the brain MRI, AQP4-IgG, CSF MOG-IgG, and oligoclonal bands were normal or negative. After IV methylprednisolone (IVMTP: 1 gr/d x 3) and slow prednisone tapering, the motor function recovered, but he remained with mild sphincter and erectile dysfunction (EDSS score 1.0). Five months later, he developed relapsing sensory deficits in the left leg and MRI findings of a new spinal cord lesion at T6, with persistent serum MOG-IgG positivity (Figure 1A), and he was started on long-term treatment with rituximab (EDSS score 1.5). Over the next 6 years, he had 6 episodes of left optic neuritis (ON), all of them treated with IVMTP accompanied by plasma exchange in 2 of the episodes. These episodes occurred despite continuous B-cell depletion with rituximab plus mycophenolate mofetil (500 mg/12 hs, 24 months), and overlapping treatment with IV immunoglobulins (IVIG; 2 g/kg, 6 months) and prednisone (30 mg/d and slow tapering schedule for 13 months) (Figure 1A). After the sixth episode of ON, optic coherence tomography (OCT) showed a substantial reduction in the thickness of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (35 µm left eye; 91 µm right eye) and the macular ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (37 µm left eye; 70 right eye) along with a reduction of visual acuity (VA) in the left eye (0.3 vs 1.0 in the right eye) (Figure 1B). Orbital and brain MRI studies showed a hyperintense and atrophic left optic nerve without gadolinium enhancement (Figure 1C).

(A) Clinical course since the onset of the disease until 1 year after anti-CD19 CAR T-cell therapy, including the episodes of myelitis and optic neuritis. Treatments during the 6.9 years preceding CAR T-cell therapy included rituximab, IV immunoglobulins (IVIg), prednisone (PD), and mycophenolate mofetil. Due to the relapse on day +29, the patient received IV methylprednisolone (IVMP: 1 gr/day x 3) and plasma exchange (PE). Since disease onset, serum MOG-IgG was tested 13 times by live cell-based assay and all the tests showed seropositivity (median titer 1:160; range, 80–160); they became negative (<1:80) for the first time at day +29. The blue line shows the CD19+ B-cell count, and the orange line represents CAR copy number by PCR. (B) Optical coherence tomography obtained 7 days before CAR T-cell infusion shows substantial reduction in peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in the left eye compared with the right eye (normal range), associated with (C) severe atrophy of the left optic nerve through its entire length (arrow) demonstrated by T2-FLAIR orbital MRI.

Owing to the aggressive course of the disease and severe visual impairment despite several immunotherapies, CAR T-cell therapy was approved for compassionate use. Mycophenolate and prednisone were discontinued 10 and 30 days before leukapheresis, and CAR T-cells were infused 1 month later when the percentage of CD19 in relation to the last dose of rituximab was 4.8%. CD19-directed CAR T-cells (ARI-0001)10 were produced by lentiviral transduction of autologous fresh leukapheresis in a closed automated CliniMACS Prodigy system. Lymphodepletion with cyclophosphamide (300 mg/m2 of body surface area/day) and fludarabine (30 mg/m2 of body surface area/day) on days −6, −5, and −4 was followed by the infusion of in-house developed ARI-0001 cells at a dose of 1 × 106 cells/kg divided in 3 fractions (10%, 30%, and 60% of the total dose) in 3 consecutive days. Infusions were well tolerated, and the patient did not develop adverse events related to CAR T-cell therapy, such as cytokine release syndrome, immune effector-cell associated neurotoxicity syndrome, insufficient hematopoietic reconstitution, or worsening of the previously detected IgG hypogammaglobulinemia (4 g/dL) (Figure 2; see eFigure 1 for additional data).

Complete depletion of CD19+ B cells was observed in the first post-CAR T-cell determination at day +7 (Figure 2). qPCR assays showed an expansion of CAR T-cells from 0.51 copies/genome at day +7 to 7.4 copies/genome at day + 90 (Figure 1A). At day +22, MOG-IgG was detected at titer 1:80 in serum. At day +29, the patient had a new episode of left ON: the VA was 0.1, and OCT and orbital MRI did not show changes. A peak in serum proinflammatory cytokines, mainly TNFα (10.7-fold increase compared with day +22) was observed (Figure 3; additional data are listed in eTable 1), while MOG-IgG tested negative for the first time (Figure 1A). Treatment with IVMTP followed by plasma exchange did not result in improvement. By then, T-cell phenotyping revealed a marked reduction of CD4+ naïve cells (CD4+ CCR7+CD45RA+) accompanied by an increase of CD4+ effector memory (CD4+CCR7+/−CD45RA−) and Tregs (CD4+CD25hiCD127low). CD19+ B-cell reconstitution occurred by day 141 postinfusion (3%; 20.00 cells/mm3), and the reconstituted B cells were 99% CD19+CD27−IgD+ naïve cells. At 1-year follow-up, the naïve T-cells remained below normal range, and the CD19+CD27+−IgD− switched memory B cells and CD19+CD38+IgM+ plasmablasts remained low (<0.5%) or absent (Figure 2). CAR T qPCR was negative at 190-day postinfusion. No reduction of anti-tetanus toxoid IgG was found at day +259. After the early relapse, the patient has remained free of relapses and MOG-IgG seronegative without immunotherapy for almost 1 year. At 1-year follow-up, brain and spinal cord MRI and OCT studies showed no changes.

Discussion

The experience with this clinical case suggests that CD19 CAR T-cell therapy is a potential treatment for patients with MOGAD refractory to other current immunotherapy approaches. In our patient, ARI-0001 was well tolerated and induced a fast depletion of B cells, disappearance of memory B cells and plasmablasts for more than 6 months, undetectable levels of MOG-IgG, and except for an early relapse that occurred a few weeks after CAR T-cell infusion, no new clinical attacks have occurred for 1 year without immunotherapy. Whether lymphodepletion with cyclophosphamide has contributed to the overall effect is presently unknown.

In MOGAD, the temporal dynamics of MOG-IgG are unclear, but conversion to negative MOG-IgG is associated with a significant reduction of relapses.9,10 Although seronegative conversion can occur spontaneously,11,12 this is unlikely in our patient who was consistently MOG-IgG positive for almost 7 years, despite multiple immunosuppressive therapies. MOG-IgG seronegative conversion in our patient and in patients with other antibody-associated diseases treated with CD19 CAR T–cell therapy4-8 suggests that these antibodies stem from the memory and plasmablast compartment.4 By contrast, the observation that vaccination-induced immunity was not affected, suggests that these long-standing antibodies arise from the long-lived CD19-negative plasma cells.4

The early relapse on day +29 when MOG-IgG had become undetectable is intriguing. Considering that 7 days before this relapse, MOG-IgG was still positive, we cannot rule out a delayed effect of the treatment. Relapsing symptoms occurred by the time that there was an increase in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, although the levels of TNF-alpha could have been related to the CAR T-cell expansion. Alternatively, autoreactive T-cells could have been expanded during the CAR T-cell manufacturing and subsequently infused.4 However, in this setting, one would have expected a more severe relapse.

The good tolerability of the therapy in our patient is consistent with the previously reported favorable toxicity profile of ARI-0001,11 which depends on the CAR construct and the fractionated administration of the cells.13

A limitation of study is that we did not assess whether the MOG-specific T-cell response was abrogated. Although our experience does not clarify the pathogenic role of MOG-IgG, the findings support the concept that MOG-IgG participates in the pathogenic mechanisms.

Overall, our findings suggest that CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy is a potential treatment approach for patients with refractory MOGAD. Nevertheless, since this is a single case, further studies are necessary to confirm these data.
