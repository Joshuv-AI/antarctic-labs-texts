Background

Chondroma is a rare benign bone tumor, accounting for less than 5% of all primary spinal tumors, with LSC being particularly uncommon. These tumors predominantly arise in the epidural space with a lateral predilection, often extending into the neural foramen and paravertebral region [1]. Their clinical presentation, typically low back pain and radicular symptoms, closely mimics sequestrated lumbar disc herniation, frequently leading to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis. While imaging may reveal lobulated masses with peripheral enhancement, definitive diagnosis and differentiation from malignancy rely on histology [2].

Surgical resection is the definitive treatment for symptomatic LSC. The conventional approach involves hemilaminectomy for adequate intraspinal exposure, decompression, and tumor removal [3]. Although effective, this open technique necessitates significant tissue disruption, prolongs recovery, and may compromise long-term stability. Recent advancements in minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS) have popularized endoscopic techniques due to their minimal tissue damage and accelerated recovery. However, to our knowledge, no reports exist on the endoscopic resection of LSC, making a full-endoscopic approach for benign spinal epidural tumors a challenging and pioneering endeavor.

This study reports a case of LSC successfully managed with AUSS via a far-lateral extraforaminal approach. To our knowledge, this is the first report utilizing AUSS as the primary technique for this rare tumor. We describe the technical nuances, assess the safety and feasibility, and discuss the clinical value of this novel minimally invasive alternative for extraforaminal LSC.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old male presented with a one-year history of recurrent left lower limb radicular pain. Preoperative contrast-enhanced lumbar MRI revealed a space-occupying lesion in the left extraforaminal region at L4/5, suggesting a benign tumor (Fig. 1). Physical examination demonstrated lumbar tenderness reproducing the radicular pain and a positive left straight leg raise (SLR) test at 50°. Surgical technique

The patient was positioned prone under general anesthesia with the lumbar region sterilely prepared and draped. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the entry point for the far-lateral extraforaminal AUSS approach was identified using a Kirschner wire, targeting the junction of the L4/5 disc level and approximately 2.5–3.0 cm lateral to the pars interarticularis. A 2-cm longitudinal skin incision was made at this point. Dissection was performed sequentially through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia. The paraspinal muscles were then bluntly dissected to the lateral border of the L4/5 facet joint, allowing introduction of the working sheath and endoscope alongside an electrocautery probe(Fig. 2A-C).

Key Step 1 (Tumor Exposure and Identification): Under continuous irrigation, electrocautery was used to carefully dissect the surrounding soft tissues, progressively exposing the tumor. The lesion was well-circumscribed, whitish in appearance, and of firm consistency(Fig. 3A-C).

Key Step 2 (Bony Management and Tumor Resection): To achieve adequate exposure of the tumor’s ventral and deep aspects, the lateral portion (approximately one-sixth) of the left L4/5 superior articular process was partially removed using a high-speed drill. This resection did not involve the articular surface of the facet joint or the interarticular bone, and the joint capsule was preserved. Circumferential dissection along the tumor pseudocapsule was then carefully performed using a nerve stripping ruler. Intraoperatively, grasping the tumor margin with a nucleus pulposus forcep caused the pseudocapsule to rupture, exposing friable contents. The tumor tissue situated caudal, dorsal, and ventral to the left L4 exiting nerve root was sequentially resected. The tumor extended medially to the medial border of the intervertebral foramen, laterally to the medial aspect of the psoas major, and deep to the superior endplate of the L5 vertebra(Fig. 3D-F).

Key Step 3 (Nerve Decompression and Final Inspection): Following complete tumor removal, the left L4 exiting nerve root was visualized; it appeared hyperemic and edematous but was fully decompressed, continuous, and without tension(Fig. 3G). The surgical field was meticulously inspected, confirming the absence of any residual lesion. After achieving complete hemostasis, a closed suction drain was placed, and the wound was closed in layers(Fig. 2D).

Outcome

The patient recovered without complications. His left lower limb radicular pain resolved completely, with the postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) score of 0 and a normalized SLR test(Table 1). Follow-up MRI confirmed gross total tumor resection, adequate decompression(Fig. 4B).The diagnosis of chondroma was confirmed by histopathology (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

Chondromas are classified as enchondromas or periosteal chondromas based on origin [1]. Enchondromas exhibit expansive growth, while periosteal chondromas grow exophytically. On MRI, lumbar spinal chondromas typically present as a lobulated mass with iso- to hypo-intense signal on T1- or T2-weighted sequences. A highly characteristic feature is peripheral rim enhancement following gadolinium administration, which helps differentiate them from sequestrated lumbar disc herniations that lack both a lobulated appearance and rim enhancement [3]. Key points for distinguishing chondromas from schwannomas include the frequent presence of neural foraminal enlargement with schwannomas (typically absent with chondromas), and the tendency for schwannomas to show homogeneous rather than peripheral enhancement on contrast MRI, with classic imaging being a well-defined intradural mass that is iso- to hypo-intense on T1 and hyperintense on T2 [3, 11]. Differentiation from synovial cysts is based on their typical MRI signal characteristics: low signal on T1-weighted images, high signal on T2-weighted images, and high signal on short tau inversion recovery sequences [12, 13].

LSC is most frequently epidural, with bone infiltration being rare [3], and cases of intradural LSC are exceptionally uncommon [4]. LSCs are typically unilateral and may exhibit a dumbbell-shaped growth pattern. The most common clinical presentation is low back pain with unilateral radicular pain, likely attributable to the predominance of the periosteal variant. In our case, preoperative imaging, intraoperative findings, and final histopathology were all consistent with an extraforaminal LSC compressing the L4 nerve root, precisely correlating with the patient’s symptoms.

Surgical resection is the standard care for symptomatic LSC. A review of cases over the past two decades (Table 2) indicates that the predominant techniques involve hemilaminectomies or total laminectomies. While these conventional posterior approaches provide ample exposure, they often necessitate extensive muscle dissection and partial or complete facetectomy, which risks significant soft tissue trauma and potential postoperative instability.

Details of the clinical case

The evolution of MISS has expanded the application of endoscopic techniques. However, to our knowledge, no reports exist on the endoscopic management of LSC. We present the first successful application of AUSS via a far-lateral extraforaminal approach for LSC resection, which achieved satisfactory early outcomes.

This far-lateral extraforaminal approach “bypasses” the primary posterior elements to access the pathology directly lateral to the facet joint, offering a distinct “outside-in” corridor for extraforaminal pathologies [14, 15]. The AUSS technique combines superior arthroscopic visualization with the practical benefits of single-portal, non-coaxial instrumentation [16].Its core advantage is enabling genuine bimanual manipulation through a single minimal incision, with independent control of the endoscope and instruments. During the resection, this technique demonstrated distinct benefits. First, the high-resolution arthroscopic view was instrumental in clearly identifying bony landmarks, the tumor pseudocapsule, and the nerve root, enabling precise and safe dissection. Second, the non-coaxial paradigm afforded a greater range of motion, facilitating complex maneuvers like meticulous bone removal with a high-speed drill and piecemeal resection of the firm tumor, which was crucial for complete excision.

Furthermore, the procedure promotes structural preservation. The single small incision minimized soft tissue disruption. Unlike traditional open surgeries often requiring extensive laminectomy and facetectomy, the AUSS approach maximally preserved the posterior spinal column integrity. Additionally, continuous irrigation aided in controlling minor bleeding and maintaining a clear operative field.

Building upon this success, the potential applications of AUSS could be extended to other extraforaminal pathologies, such as schwannomas or synovial cysts, offering a novel minimally invasive option for this anatomical area.

Nevertheless, this study has limitations. First, it is a single case report, necessitating validation through larger, prospective studies. Second, achieving complete tumor resection is paramount, as illustrated by a reported case where initial incomplete resection following preoperative misdiagnosis led to recurrence [5]. In the present case, the intraoperative course involved careful dissection along the mass boundary. Although the mass was mostly well-defined, it fractured upon grasping with a nucleus pulposus forcep. A portion was sent for intraoperative frozen section, which confirmed a benign diagnosis. The tumor was subsequently removed in a piecemeal fashion. The resection margins were thoroughly inspected, the surgical field was copiously irrigated, and the surrounding tissues were coagulated with a radiofrequency probe; final exploration revealed no residual tumor-like tissue. Given this technical particularity and the piecemeal resection, long-term follow-up is essential to fully evaluate oncological efficacy and local recurrence rates, requiring further investigation. Finally, the technique presents a steep learning curve. Surgeons may encounter difficulties with anatomical orientation, and operative times may be prolonged. Comparative studies are needed to elucidate differences in complication rates and surgical efficiency versus conventional open surgery.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the first successful application of AUSS with a far-lateral extraforaminal approach for resecting a lumbar spinal chondroma. Through a single minimal incision, this technique provided superior visualization for precise and complete tumor removal in the challenging extraforaminal region. Compared to conventional open surgery, it offers reduced tissue trauma, accelerated recovery, and maximal preservation of posterior spinal stability. Therefore, AUSS represents a viable and promising minimally invasive alternative for managing lumbar chondromas, with potential applications for other extraforaminal pathologies worthy of further investigation.
