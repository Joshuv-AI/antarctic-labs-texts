Red blood cell distribution width-to-monocyte ratio for predicting 90-day mortality of COVID-19 in patients with chronic kidney disease during the Omicron period: a prospective single-center study
Authors: Chaofan Li, Yue Niu, Dinghua Chen, Zhe Feng, Fei Liu, Yong Wang, Xueying Cao, Jie Wu, Jiabao Liu, Xuefeng Sun, Li Zhang, Guangyan Cai, Ping Li, Xiangmei Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to test whether red blood cell distribution width (RDW) to monocyte percentage ratio (RMR) was associated with the acute-phase prognosis of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients. Prospective enrollment and 90-day follow-up of CKD patients with COVID-19 were conducted from December 1, 2022 to January 31, 2023. Demographics, clinical data, and laboratory and radiographic findings were collected, and multiple logistic regression, subgroup analysis, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis were performed. A total of 218 patients were enrolled, with a mean age of 59 years and 69.7% being male. The 90-day mortality rate was 24.8%. The lnRMR level was 5.18 (4.91-5.43) and emerged as an independent risk factor (OR: 3.01, 95% CI: 1.72-5.85). The lnRMR-mortality association was consistent across sex, age, CKD stage, COVID-19 vaccination, and comorbidity subgroups. The area under the ROC curve of lnRMR was 0.737 (95% CI: 0.655-0.819). Our findings indicate that lnRMR is a simple and practical predictor for identifying high-risk CKD patients during the acute phase of COVID-19.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, COVID-19, prognosis, red blood cell distribution width, monocyte

Introduction

Ranging from asymptomatic cases to severe pneumonia, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) presents a spectrum of clinical manifestations [1]. Amidst this global pandemic, a significant challenge for healthcare professionals is pinpointing patients who are at a heightened risk for severe illness and mortality. COVID-19 poses a particularly elevated threat to individuals with underlying health conditions, notably those afflicted with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2].

For COVID-19 patients, the complete blood count (CBC) was the consistently reliable and extensively utilized diagnostic tool for initial screening on a global scale [3]. Notably, elevated red blood cell distribution width (RDW) levels, as determined through this test, have been shown to independently predict the severity and mortality associated with COVID-19 [4]. Furthermore, the prognostic significance of RDW in gauging the severity of CKD has been well-documented [5]. A Low monocyte percentage has been confirmed to be associated with a higher mortality rate for COVID-19 patients [6,7]. Consequently, we proposed that the application of these two biomarkers in a ratio format may enhance the predictive accuracy of COVID-19 outcomes, specifically in the CKD patient population.

Affected by immune status and medications, the RDW and monocytes levels of patients with kidney disease differed from those in the general population [5,8]. However, the predictive roles of these routine blood indicators have not been studied in CKD patients with COVID-19. Furthermore, while the prognostic value of the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio, lymphocyte to monocyte ratio, and platelet to white blood cell ratio have been documented in COVID-19 patients, the predictive potential of the RDW to monocyte percentage ratio (RMR) remains unexplored in the prognosis of COVID-19 [9].

Therefore, we hypothesized that RMR could be a potential predictor for the mortality of COVID-19 in CKD patients. Since RDW and monocyte percentage are standard CBC test components, their ratio can be easily calculated without additional tests, facilitating its broad clinical application.

Methods

Setting and participants

According to the recommended rule of thumb: n = 100 + 50i, with i = 2 for RDW and monocyte percentage, the sample size should be at least 200 [10]. From December 1, 2022, to January 31, 2023, we enrolled 253 CKD patients aged over 18 years old at the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital (PLAGH) in Beijing (Figure 1). Among these inpatients, COVID-19 patients were prospectively enrolled in this study. Patients without CBC data on admission were excluded. After admission, they were prospectively followed for a clinical outcome of 90-day mortality, verified through medical records and telephone surveys. The final follow-up date was May 1, 2023.

Ethics

The cohort study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and authorized by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLAGH (S2023-111-01). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants.

Definitions and variables

The diagnosis and severity of COVID-19 were evaluated according to the Diagnosis and Treatment Protocol for COVID-19 by the National Health Commission of China (Trial Version 10) [11]. It was classified into asymptomatic, mild, moderate, severe, and critical types (Supplementary Materials). An estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or positive biomarkers of renal injury for >90 days were considered indicators of CKD corresponding to the KDIGO guideline [12]. The eGFR was calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula [13]. CKD was stratified into different stages based on the eGFR as follows: stage 1, ≥90 mL/min/1.73m2; stage 2, [60, 90) mL/min/1.73m2; stage 3, [30, 60) mL/min/1.73m2; stage 4, [15, 30) mL/min/1.73m2; and stage 5, <15 mL/min/1.73m2.

General data on the enrolled patients during the index hospitalization was collected from the electronic medical record system of Chinese PLAGH. The collected information encompassed demographics (age, sex, and body mass index (BMI)), clinical information (underlying medical illnesses, treatments, vaccination, tobacco and alcohol consumption, and vital signs), as well as laboratory and radiographic findings.

Laboratory testing

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected within 48 h of hospital admission. They were sent to the central laboratory of the Chinese PLAGH for examination. The hematological samples were analyzed on the XN-9000 automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex, Japan) for CBC (the count and indices of red blood cells, including mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, red blood cell distribution width, and hematocrit), hemoglobin concentrations, white blood cell count with differentials of neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, eosinophils and basophils, and platelets). Serum samples were analyzed on the PA990 particular protein analyzer (Lifotronic, China) for C-reactive protein (CRP), the IMMULITE 2000 immunoassay analyzer (Siemens, Germany) for interleukin 6 (IL-6), and the Cobas c702 chemistry analyzer (Roche, USA) for lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatinine (for the calculation of eGFR). The measurements were undertaken in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions by experienced technicians without involvement in clinical management.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (the first and third quartiles). The independent t-test and Mann–Whitney test were used to compare data between two groups. The ANOVA test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used for comparison among the three groups. The categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. The Spearman correlations between laboratory indicators were evaluated.

None of the variables under study exceeded a 5% missing rate. These missing values were handled by multivariate imputation, where each incomplete variable was imputed by a separate model. Methods of imputation depended on the variable types: numerical variables were imputed through predictive mean matching, and binary variables were imputed through logistic regression imputation.

The distribution of RMR was highly right-skewed, and a natural logarithm transform was adopted to make the distribution more balanced and regression models more robust. Logistic regression was performed to examine the risk factors affecting patients’ survival. Our variable selection was based on the clinical relevance recommended in the published literature and Chinese guidelines for COVID-19, statistical significance (p < 0.05) in univariate analyses, and clinical expertise input from our teams (Supplementary Table 1). Several models were applied to test the association between lnRMR and outcomes: Crude model; Model 1 was adjusted for sex (female or male), age, and BMI; Model 2 was additionally adjusted for medical history associated with the prognosis of COVID-19, including cardiovascular disease (yes or no), cerebrovascular disease (yes or no), and COVID-19 vaccination status (vaccinated or unvaccinated); Model 3 was additionally adjusted for indicators related to COVID-19 infection, including days from infection to admission, respiratory rate, hypoxia (finger oxygen saturation <90% on air), and COVID-19-associated pulmonary abnormalities on chest CT; Model 4 was additionally adjusted for hemoglobin, platelet, calcium, and creatinine.

The effect of lnRMR was explored through subgroup analysis adjusted by the four COVID-19-related indicators mentioned above. The age and BMI were divided into two subgroups according to the boundaries of 60 years old and 25 kg/m2, respectively. The CKD stages were divided into one subgroup consisting of stages 1, 2, and 3, and the other subgroup consisting of stages 4 and 5. The significance of their interactions was estimated using likelihood ratio tests, and P values were listed without adjustment.

The discriminatory power of predicting survival status was assessed via receiver operating characteristic curves utilizing leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, cutoff, Youden index, and accuracy were calculated at the same time. To evaluate the calibration of risk factors, the F1 score, Kappa coefficient, and Brier score were calculated. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were analyzed by the statistical software R 4.3.1 via the packages mice version 3.16.0, pROC version 1.18.4, and ggplot2 version 3.4.3.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patients across lnRMR tertiles

Data from a total of 218 patients were ultimately included in this analysis (Table 1). At baseline, the mean age was 59 years old, with 69.7% (n = 152) being male. A significant proportion, 37.6% (n = 82), had received kidney transplants or were on maintenance dialysis. The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension (77.1%, n = 168) and cardiovascular disease (47.2%, n = 103). A notable 60.1% (n = 128) of patients were unvaccinated for COVID-19. Furthermore, 54.6% (n = 119) of the cohort had severe or critical COVID-19. During hospitalization, 16.1% of patients underwent endotracheal intubation. Patients were divided into three groups based on the tertiles of lnRMR. Those in the highest lnRMR tertile ([5.34, 7.57]) were older, with a mean age of 65 years (±20 years). They exhibited a lower BMI (23.06 ± 4.38 kg/m2) and lower diastolic blood pressure (74 ± 14 mmHg). This group also had a higher proportion of severe or critical COVID-19 at 65.8% and endotracheal intubation at 27.4%.

Association of lnRMR with laboratory tests

The levels of lnRMR, RDW, and monocyte percentage in the 218 patients were 5.18 (4.91–5.43), 13.40% (12.70%, 14.88%), and 0.08 (0.06, 0.10), respectively (Table 1). Lymphocyte percentage, CRP, IL-6, and LDH were common markers for assessing viral infection severity. Their respective levels were 0.16 ± 0.11, 1.52 (0.16–6.07) mg/dL, 13.93 (3.01–62.51) pg/mL, and 232.65 (189.02–337.00) U/L. The eGFR level in this study was 11.30 (5.58–42.54) mL/min/1.73 m2. Patients in the highest lnRMR tertile had higher levels of CRP (4.73 (0.33–10.83) mg/dL), IL-6 (31.86 (3.30–120.88) pg/mL), and LDH (296.90 (205.73–428.02) U/L), along with a lower level of lymphocyte percentage (0.10 ± 0.10).

A positive correlation was observed between lnRMR and CRP (r = 0.243, p < 0.001), IL-6 (r = 0.229, p < 0.001), and LDH (r = 0.329, p < 0.001). Conversely, a negative correlation was noted with lymphocyte percentage (r = −0.555, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure 1).

Correlation between lnRMR and death

After admission, the follow-up duration of the total patients was 93 (68, 109) days. The overall mortality rate was 24.8%, with 54 deaths. The median durations of follow-up for survivors and deaths were 102 days and 13 days, respectively. The level of lnRMR was significantly higher in the death group than in the survivor group (5.50 (5.21–6.12) vs. 5.10 (4.83–5.34), respectively, (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure 2). The mortality rates across the first, second, and third tertiles of baseline lnRMR were 12.32%, 18.06%, and 43.84%, respectively (Supplementary Figure 3).

In CKD patients with COVID-19, as depicted in Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 2, lnRMR emerged as a predictor of 90-day mortality (p < 0.001), with an OR of 2.82 (95% CI: 1.98–4.23). Across Models 1 to 4, lnRMR consistently qualified as an independent risk factor for mortality (p < 0.001) even after being adjusted for demographic variables, medical history, COVID-19 infection-related factors, and laboratory indicators. According to the tertiles of lnRMR, the higher baseline lnRMR levels also correlated with an elevated risk of death in the fully adjusted Model 4 (OR: 4.92, 95% CI: 1.31−20.78; p = 0.022).

Subgroup analyses

The interaction terms were used to investigate whether the association between lnRMR and outcomes varied according to different subgroups. As illustrated in Figure 3, the effects of lnRMR on mortality were consistent across subgroups of sex, age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, CKD stages, previous maintenance dialysis, and COVID-19 vaccination status. However, a significant interaction with BMI was observed (P interaction = 0.028). Notably, lnRMR was a significant risk factor for mortality in the subgroup with a BMI <25 kg/m2 (OR: 9.01, 95% CI: 3.75-26.3) instead of in the subgroup with a BMI ≥25 kg/m2.

Predictive performance of lnRMR

As detailed in Supplementary Table 3 and Figure 4, predictive performance was estimated using LOOCV. The AUC of lnRMR was 0.737 (95% CI: 0.655–0.819), which was higher than those of RDW (0.700, 95% CI: 0.621–0.778) and monocyte percentage (0.688, 95% CI: 0.595–0.781) alone. When RDW and monocyte percentage were combined, the AUC increased to 0.766 (95% CI: 0.695–0.837). The incorporation of lnRMR into the model further elevated the AUC to 0.799 (95% CI: 0.735–0.863) without additional information. Beyond the AUC, the combined model of RDW, monocyte percentage, and lnRMR also excelled in other statistical measures: it achieved an F1 score of 0.584, a Brier score of 0.150, and a Youden index of 0.505.

Discussion

In CKD, a cascade of metabolic alterations is triggered to foster a uremic environment that facilitates the buildup of diverse uremic toxins. The accumulation of these toxins contributes to vascular damage, impairs endothelial function, and stimulates the innate immune system’s activation [14]. Therefore, individuals with CKD represent a susceptible population for COVID-19, with an increasing number of cases noted among this vulnerable group [15]. For effective patient triage and resource allocation, it is essential to identify easily accessible biomarkers from routine blood tests that can efficiently detect high-risk patients at an early stage. In this cohort study, we followed the CKD patients with COVID-19 for 90 days and proposed a composite predictor based on two well-established laboratory indicators: natural logarithm-transformed RDW to monocyte percentage ratio. The association between lnRMR and COVID-19 mortality remained significant even after adjusting for other risk factors in CKD patients. Incorporating this calculated predictor into our model led to improved generalization performance as estimated by LOOCV.

In our study, all-cause 90-day mortality among CKD patients with COVID-19 was 24.8%. Previous studies have reported a short-term mortality rate of 5% to 50% for CKD patients with COVID-19 [16–18]. According to the report from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, all of the reported COVID-19 cases were Omicron variants, and BF.7 was the dominant strain from December 1, 2022, to April 20, 2023 in Beijing, where our center was located [19]. The Omicron strain, which infected our patient cohort, likely contributed to the observed differences due to its generally lower severity compared to earlier variants [20]. Furthermore, racial differences may play a role in COVID-19 mortality rates, with Asian populations potentially exhibiting higher fatality rates associated with the virus [21].

The RDW level has been extensively utilized for diagnosing and stratifying risk in COVID-19, with its value correlating positively with the severity and mortality of the disease—findings that align with our study’s results [22]. The specific mechanisms behind the changes in RDW associated with COVID-19 are not yet clear. It may increase when red blood cell production kinetics have slowed in the setting of increased white blood cell and platelet kinetics in COVID-19 [23]. As a very common metric, RDW has been reported to predict adverse clinical outcomes in stroke, critical illness, and sepsis [24–26]. Thus, it might not have a causal relationship with the progression of COVID-19 disease. The predictive power of this indicator in gauging the severity of CKD has been firmly established [5]. The high levels of RDW probably increase the risk of all-cause mortality in CKD patients. The stimulating effect of inflammatory cytokines on erythropoietin, and the correlation between malnutrition and high RDW levels could partially account for it [27]. Since RDW is correlated with various causes of death, it may be a comprehensive biomarker of dysfunction. These mechanisms may have influenced the relationship between the RMR indicator, COVID-19, and CKD disease.

Monocytes are pivotal in the effective control and clearance of viral infections and are implicated in all stages of COVID-19, contributing to the disease’s clinical heterogeneity [28]. In our study, the increased level of monocyte percentage showed protective effects for CKD patients with COVID-19. A lower monocyte level has been identified as a mortality predictor for hospitalized COVID-19 patients [29]. Researches indicated that the reduced proportion of monocytes correlated with an elevated risk of death in COVID-19 patients [6,7]. Additionally, a transient reduction in blood monocytes observed in a longitudinal study of severe cases highlights the highly time-sensitive nature of the immune response in COVID-19 [30]. In CKD, patients were characterized by the expansion of the distinct proinflammatory intermediate monocyte and increased monocyte adhesion to endothelial cells [31]. Another study showed a significant association between higher monocyte counts and risks of CKD progression [32]. Monocytes could act as a clinical predictor of inflammation and compromised health status. It was also associated with the progression of vascular disease, which in turn leads to a decline in renal function [32]. The interplay between the RMR and conditions such as COVID-19 and CKD could potentially be affected by this mechanism.

Correlation analysis revealed positive associations between lnRMR and the inflammatory markers CRP, IL-6, and LDH. The relationships provided evidence that lnRMR reflected the post-infection inflammatory condition. The relationship between lnRMR and eGFR, however, was not statistically significant, and subgroup analysis (binary classification) revealed that lnRMR’s prognostic ability remained constant throughout the CKD stages. Therefore, lnRMR serves as a death predictor in CKD patients with COVID-19, independent of renal function status. Given that lnRMR is derived from calculations on blood cells, the impact of renal function on blood cell morphology and proportion could be influenced by factors such as comorbidities and infection severity [33].

In subgroup analysis, BMI was the only factor that interacted with lnRMR’s association with outcomes. LnRMR retained its predictive value in non-overweight patients with a BMI < 25 kg/m2, but did not serve as a risk factor in patients with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. BMI has been recognized as a significant factor influencing the severity of COVID-19 [34]. A higher BMI was considered a state of low-grade inflammation, potentially impairing the immune system’s capacity for a swift response to infections [35].

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the findings are specific to the Omicron variants and may not be generalizable to other populations, given the distinct etiologies of CKD in our East Asian cohort compared to Western populations. Secondly, the study was conducted at a single medical center with a modest sample size, necessitating a cautious interpretation of the results. Thirdly, blood cell analysis was conducted using an automated analyzer, yet the potential for blood cell destruction during transportation and examination cannot be disregarded. Fourthly, the absence of serial blood tests during the hospitalization period limits our ability to fully assess the predictive efficacy of lnRMR for mortality. Fifthly, our study may not have accounted for all relevant confounding factors identified in previous research. Additionally, there is a possibility of unobserved or unmeasured confounding factors that could affect the study outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, lnRMR had an acceptable predictive value in the population of CKD with COVID-19. Though the predictive value of lnRMR was moderate, it is a datapoint that will be available to nearly 100% of hospitalized patients in most high- and middle-income counties. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the cost-effective CBC tests have the potential to offer valuable prognostic information that aligns with clinical requirements and can be utilized across regions with varying medical infrastructures. Future studies should aim to validate these findings in more extensive and diverse patient cohorts.
