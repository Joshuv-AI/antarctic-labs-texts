Dataset of Optimized Structures of Aliphatic Chains Chemisorbed on Si(110) and Si(111) Surfaces via First-Principles Methods
Authors: Sara Marchio, Francesco Buonocore, Simone Giusepponi, Massimo Celino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe functionalization of silicon surfaces with organic monolayers is a powerful strategy to tune their electronic and chemical properties, enabling applications in fields such nanoelectronics, biosensing and surface engineering. However, to effectively predict and optimize surface properties, a large-scale DFT-based screening of the broad variety of functional groups is required. To support the rational design of the interfaces, we present an open dataset comprising more than 700 density functional theory geometry-optimized configurations of alkyl, alkenyl, and 1-alkynyl chains chemisorbed on Si(111) and Si(110) surfaces, obtained exploring a wide range of adsorption geometries. The relative stabilities of these configurations are evaluated through Si–C bond dissociation energies. To demonstrate the dataset’s versatility, we highlight two representative use cases, each related to relevant application domains. This open dataset aims to accelerate computational screening and data-driven approaches in surface science.

Background & Summary

The functionalization of semiconductor surfaces through the chemisorption of densely packed organic monolayers1–3 is a promising area that can benefit greatly from large-scale screening and the generation of shared datasets. A systematic analysis of the broad range of candidate functional molecules would indeed enable the precise tailoring of surface properties to meet specific application requirements. Hydrogen-terminated silicon surfaces can be functionalized through various established methods4, such as alkene and alkyne hydrosilylation and halogenation followed by alkylation. These approaches yield stable and densely packed organic monolayers, making them especially attractive for applications in electronic devices5, where silicon remains a key material. In microelectronics, organic monolayers can passivate surface electronic states, thereby eliminating Fermi level pinning in devices with a semiconductor-metal interface6–8. Furthermore, the choice of the molecular functional group allows tuning of the work function9–11, as the dipole generated by the monolayer molecules directly influences the semiconductor’s electron affinity. In solar cells, organic monolayers have proven to be effective at preventing silicon surface oxidation when exposed to air and humidity12,13. Many other applications include biosensors14 and catalysts15,16. The requirement for high monolayer quality in terms of packing density and ordered molecular orientation17,18 requires considering several factors to achieve optimal performance. For instance, the surface binding group influences monolayer stability and tilt angle, while the terminal group modulates packing energy and surface dipole. In this context, a systematic investigation of how monolayer properties depend on molecule type is essential. High-throughput (HT) Density Functional Theory (DFT) is an effective approach for predicting both the structural stability and electronic properties of monolayers, including surface dipole, work function, and band gap19–22. Leveraging advances in High-Performance Computing (HPC) and workflow management frameworks such as AiiDA23,24, HT approaches enable the rapid screening of candidate functional groups by efficiently handling large volumes of simulations. At the same time, reproducibility is ensured through provenance tracking of both data and calculations, facilitating sharing via open datasets25–27. In recent years, data-driven methodologies have increasingly been employed to investigate adsorption phenomena on solid surfaces. A first representative example is the work of Wellendorff et al.28, who assembled a benchmark database of experimental adsorption energies for adsorbate-transition metal systems to evaluate the performance of DFT methods. Further benchmark efforts include the work by Sharada et al.29 which reports a comprehensive set of DFT adsorption energies for adsorbates on transition-metal surfaces, enabling systematic evaluation of computational accuracy and transferability. Pablo-García et al.30 studied organic molecules adsorbed on metal surfaces and exploited a DFT-generated dataset to develop a graph neural network model for adsorption energy prediction. Among studies focusing on silicon-based materials, Weiske et al.31 used a dataset of DFT trajectories to support the development of machine-learned force fields describing the adsorption dynamics of functionalized cyclooctyne molecules on Si(001). Functionalized oxide surfaces have also attracted considerable attention. Strugovshchikov et al.32 released a training dataset underlying their work, which was used to develop force fields for silica surfaces functionalized with spatially resolved chemical groups. Despite the growing availability of datasets and machine-learning models for adsorption and surface functionalization, systematic high-throughput studies targeting chemically functionalized silicon facets remain limited. In this study, we present a HT-DFT screening of C₂–C₁₈ alkyl, alkenyl, and 1-alkynyl moieties as candidates for surface functionalization of H-passivated Si(111) and Si(110). The Si(111) and Si(110) crystallographic orientations represent relevant interfaces for covalent functionalization with organic molecules and are commonly exposed in silicon nanowires grown along the Si(112) direction33,34. In the limit where confinement effects are negligible, corresponding to nanowires larger than ∼10 nm, these surfaces can be accurately described using a slab model, which effectively captures their structural and electronic properties. We generated a dataset comprising 721 fully relaxed structures of hydrogenated silicon slabs with adsorbed organic molecules. The dataset required substantial computational effort since each supercell contains up to 270 atoms, and every optimized structure was obtained through Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) relaxations, each involving up to 200 individual DFT steps. These calculations may require up to several days. Notably, the released dataset includes not only the final optimized structures, but also intermediate, not fully relaxed frames along the BFGS relaxation pathways, which can be of particular interest for applications such as the training of data-driven force fields. By providing pre-relaxed configurations, the dataset enables users to bypass these computationally demanding steps, substantially reducing the time required to explore multiple configurations from days to a few hours, while also lowering the computational effort required to setup downstream simulations or analyses. The dataset was generated using an automated workflow implemented within the AiiDA framework. In the following, we provide a detailed description of the workflow. We further validate the dataset through analysis of geometric and energetic parameters and demonstrate its transferability in two representative applications, highlighting its potential to accelerate the exploration of functionalized silicon surfaces while significantly lowering the computational cost of DFT-based studies.

Methods

Our calculations produced optimized structures of Si(111) and Si(110) surfaces functionalized with a set of organic molecules containing single, double, or triple carbon–carbon bonds, specifically alkyl (CnH2n+1), alkenyl (CnH2+), and 1-alkynyl (CnH2n−3) groups, with carbon chain lengths (n) ranging from 2 to 18. The surfaces are modelled as Si(111) and Si(110) slabs, depicted in Fig. 1(a,e), respectively. The Si(111) surface is modelled as a 15 Å-thick slab comprising five bilayers, with lateral sizes Lx = 15.47 Å and Ly = 13.39 Å. The Si(110) surface is modelled using a 13.50 Å-thick slab composed of eight layers, with lateral sizes Lx = 16.40 Å and Ly = 15.47 Å. The Si(111) slab contains 160 Si atoms and 15 H atoms, while the Si(110) slab contains 192 Si atoms and 23 H atoms. In both cases, the H atoms passivate the dangling bonds of the top slab surface.

Functionalization with the aliphatic chains is obtained by substituting one surface hydrogen atom with the molecular moiety, by positioning the lowest carbon atom C1 at the adhesion site. The chemisorption of one molecule per supercell corresponds to surface coverages of 0.48 molecules nm−2 on the Si(111) surface and 0.39 molecules nm−2 on the Si(110) surface, considering xy-plane supercell areas of ASi(111) = 207 Å² and ASi(110) = 254 Å², respectively. At the relatively low coverages considered in this work, intermolecular steric interactions are expected to be limited and are therefore not fully captured. Although this constitutes an approximation with respect to densely packed organic monolayers, the resulting relaxed configurations provide a suitable starting point for the generation of models with higher molecular surface densities. To account for the rotational degrees of freedom, different molecule adhesion configurations were investigated by first varying the azimuthal angle φ (Fig. 1b,f), while keeping the tilt angle θ (Fig. 1c,g) fixed and initialized at approximately 30° for Si(110) and 45° for Si(111). Based on surface symmetry considerations, φ was varied within a 120° interval for the Si(111) surface (Fig. 1b) and within a 180° interval for the Si(110) surface (Fig. 1f). For each molecule, the φ value corresponding to the lowest energy was selected, and then the tilt angle θ was varied, with initial values set at approximately 30°, 45°, and 60°. The simulation box height is set to Lz = 50 Å for C₂–C₁₀ molecules and 65 Å for C₁₂–C₁₈ molecules, ensuring sufficient vacuum spacing to eliminate interactions between periodic images.

The structural optimization calculations within the automated workflow are performed using DFT approach, as implemented in the PWscf (Plane-Wave Self-Consistent Field) code from the Quantum Espresso package35–37. Electron-ion interactions are described by ultra-soft Vanderbilt pseudopotentials38. Van der Waals interactions are accounted for by employing the vdW-df-cx functional39–42 Corrections for dipole interactions have also been implemented in the calculations43.

Preliminary self-consistent calculations on the total energies and forces were performed on the pseudopotentials to set the cut-offs and the k-points grid: the kinetic energy cutoff is set to 40 Ry for wavefunctions and 320 Ry for charge density and potential. A k-point mesh of 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack44 is used to sample the Brillouin zone. The systems were considered fully relaxed when the variation between two successive steps is less than 0.0027 eV and all the components of the force on each atom was below the convergence threshold of 0.05 eV/Å. The two bottom bilayers in Si(111) and the three bottom layers in Si(110) are kept fixed during structural relaxation to mimic semi-infinite slabs.

where Esm is the total energy of the system with the molecule adsorbed onto the surface, while Em and Es are the total energies of the isolated moiety and surface, respectively. The moiety energy Em is estimated by first optimizing the corresponding alkane, alkene, or alkyne molecule and subsequently performing a second relaxation after removing one hydrogen atom from a terminal group. This procedure avoids starting from the potentially non-optimal geometry extracted from the adsorbed configuration. The surface energy Es is obtained by removing a single hydrogen atom from the fully passivated slab and performing a structural relaxation of the resulting surface.

The dataset of optimized structures for Si(111) and Si(110) surfaces functionalized with aliphatic moieties was generated through an automated computational workflow. This workflow takes as input a set of candidate functional molecules and silicon surface models, systematically generating multiple adsorption configurations of the molecules on the surfaces, which are subsequently optimized via DFT relaxation calculations. The data related to these calculations are then retrieved and stored in a non-relational database to facilitate comparative analyses and support downstream applications. The resulting configurations are further analysed to extract both energetic and structural descriptors.

The computational workflow was implemented using AiiDA, an open-source Python platform designed for scientific workflows, capable of managing multiple types of calculations and large amount of data. The core component of AiiDA is its application programming interface (API), which facilitates the management of key elements, including data, codes, and calculations. The workflow logic and the execution of multiple simultaneous calculations were managed using the AiiDA daemon, while job submission was handled through the IBM Spectrum LSF (Load Sharing Facility) scheduler plugin, enabling efficient utilization of ENEAGRID HPC resources45. Each job consists of a structural relaxation calculation performed using the pw.x code via the AiiDA plugin for Quantum ESPRESSO package. This integrated environment allows for seamless execution of complex workflows while ensuring robustness and scalability.

Below, we provide a detailed description of the workflow, implemented using the WorkChain object, which divides the logic into multiple components, referred to as steps. This approach ensures that the progress of each step is preserved, even in the event of unexpected interruptions, thereby preventing data loss.

Data Records

The dataset is made available at the Materials Cloud Archive47. It contains input and output files from geometry optimization calculations performed with Quantum ESPRESSO for hydrogen-passivated Si(111) and Si(110) slabs functionalized with organic moieties having single, double, or triple C-C bonds. Specifically, it includes alkyl (CnH2n+1), alkenyl (CnH2n), and 1-alkynyl (CnH2n−3) groups, with carbon chain lengths ranging from C2 to C18.

The dataset contains both the input (.in) and output (.out) files of the Quantum ESPRESSO relaxation calculations. Relaxed geometries can be extracted directly from the final structures reported in the output files.

The dataset is organized in a hierarchical folder structure as follows:

Top-level folder: Indicates the silicon surface orientation (e.g., Si111, Si110).

Top-level folder: Indicates the silicon surface orientation (e.g., Si111, Si110).

Second-level folder: Combines the surface orientation and the type of organic group (e.g., Si111_alkanes, Si110_alkynes).

Second-level folder: Combines the surface orientation and the type of organic group (e.g., Si111_alkanes, Si110_alkynes).

Third-level folder: Specifies the carbon chain length of the molecule (e.g., C6).

Third-level folder: Specifies the carbon chain length of the molecule (e.g., C6).

For each configuration, the input and output files are named using the format theta<theta>_phi<phi>.in and theta<theta>_phi<phi>.out, where theta and phi define the orientation of the functional group relative to the slab surface. The geometric parameters for each system are listed in the DataSummary.xls file in the Top-level folder. For example, Si110/Si110_alkynes/C10/phi15_theta53.in and.out are the input and output files of the optimized structure of the C10 molecule adsorbed onto the Si(110) surface with φ = 15° and θ = 53°.

Technical Validation

The dataset of 721 optimized structures was generated via DFT calculations using ultra-soft pseudopotentials and the vdW-df-cx functional, which has been widely benchmarked against experimental data for different materials39. Specifically, it has been demonstrated its accurate description of van der Waals interactions in the case of charge transfer at silicon-organic interfaces40. A first assessment on the quality of the dataset was conducted by checking that all the calculations had converged to fully relaxed final configurations. A strict criterion was used, with the variation between two successive steps in the geometric relaxation is less than 0.0027 eV and all the components of the force on each atom below 0.05 eV/Å, ensuring that all reported configurations correspond to energy local minima. We then verified the internal consistency of the dataset by excluding the presence of outliers in key properties, including the molecular dissociation energy (Fig. 3), Si-C and C1-C2 bond lengths (Fig. 4), and the tilting angles of both the entire molecule and the C1-C2 bond (Fig. 5). The estimation of these parameters enabled a series of checks on data reliability. The Si-C bond dissociation energies for the three types of moieties (alkyl, alkenyl, 1-alkynyl) were verified to follow the expected hierarchy based on carbon hybridization. The 1-alkynyl moieties exhibit the highest dissociation energies (5.1 to 5.6 eV), followed by the alkenyl group (3.7 to 4.1 eV) and the alkyl group (3.0 to 3.5 eV). As expected, the Si-C bond strength increases from sp³ (alkyl) to sp² (alkenyl) to sp (1-alkynyl) carbon, reflecting the increasing orbital overlap. The numerical values obtained in this work are in very good agreement with the DFT theoretical study by Pei et al.48, which investigated radical chain reactions on hydrogenated Si(100)(2 × 1) surfaces. In that study, Si–C(sp³) and Si–C(sp²) bond dissociation energies (BDEs) of approximately 80 kcal/mol (3.47 eV) and 93 kcal/mol (4.03 eV), respectively, were reported using the B3LYP exchange–correlation functional. Benchmark Si–C BDE values are also available in the literature for a wide range of organosilane compounds. For instance, Si–C(sp³) bond dissociation energies typically fall within the range of 89.6–94.2 kcal/mol (3.88–4.08 eV)49.

The validation of the obtained geometries was initially carried out by analysing the bond length values. The decrease in the Si–C bond length from alkyl to alkenyl and 1-alkynyl moieties is consistent with the previously discussed bond strength trend. Moreover, the observed values, ranging from 1.91 Å for alkyl chains to 1.81 Å for 1-alkynyl chains, are in good agreement with the standard Si–C bond length of approximately 1.89 Å50.

The C1-C2 distances were also checked to ensure good agreement with literature standard values. The C1-C2 distance is approximately 1.53 Å for alkyl chains, and about 1.22 Å for 1-alkynyl chains. For the alkenyl group, the C1–C2 bond length increases from 1.34 Å to 1.36 Å for chains shorter than 10 carbon atoms and then stabilizes at approximately 1.36 Å for longer chains on both surfaces. Overall, the obtained values are consistent with standard bond lengths for single, double and triple C-C bonds51 and show internal consistency when comparing the Si(111) and Si(110) surfaces.

Figure 5 report the angles θ(C1CN) and θ(C1C2), defined as the angles between the z-axis and the vectors connecting atoms C1 to CN and C1 to C2, respectively (see Fig. 1c,g,d). We validated the values of the molecules tilting angles by comparison with literature data, for the subset of molecules where such reference parameters are available. Experimental data on C12-C18 chains covalently bound to the Si(111) surface17 report tilting angles in the ranges of 37–40° for alkyl and 22–35° for alkenyl monolayers. Compared to our most stable structures for the corresponding moieties, the deviations do not exceed 10°. Despite the difference in surface coverage, as experimental values correspond to more densely packed monolayers where intermolecular interactions and collective effects play a significant role, the discrepancy remains limited. The optimized single-molecule geometries thus provide a suitable starting point for constructing higher-coverage models, which can be generated by replication of the relaxed structure across surface sites followed by further relaxation to account for intermolecular interactions and possible reorientation. There is also good agreement with previous numerical studies for the tilt angles of the most stable configurations of C2, C452, and C18 alkyl53, and C8 alkenyl54. The values obtained for C6-C12 alkyl chains are slightly lower than a previous numerical study52 in which, however, the conformational space might not be completely explored. In the literature, only limited results are available for the tilting angles of 1-alkynyl moieties as well as for the functionalization of the Si(110) surface. Nevertheless, our calculations show consistent values between the two surfaces and reproduce systematic trends with chain length. To complete the technical validation, we tested the transferability of the dataset in two application contexts. First, pre-optimized C₆ alkyl chains on Si(111) and Si(110) were functionalized with phenyl, carboxyl, and amino terminal groups (Fig. 7a–f). These substitutions required only minimal re-optimization, with BFGS steps reduced by more than half in most cases (Fig. 7g), and structural parameters such as the θ(C1C6) angle varying by less than 5° (Table 1). This confirms that the geometries can be reliably reused as starting points for different functional groups. Second, the most stable adsorption geometries for C₈ chains were transferred to a Si nanowire (1.7 nm diameter, 96 Si and 40 H atoms33,55) with exposed (110) and (111) facets (Fig. 8). After relaxation, only small deviations in molecular orientation were observed compared to slab models (Table 2), and convergence was reached within few BFGS steps, confirming the robustness of the dataset for nanostructures. Moreover, part of the dataset has already been employed in our previous works on surface dipoles induced by aliphatic chains on Si(111)56,57 and on the optoelectronic properties of functionalized Si nanowires58, further supporting its reliability and relevance. The dataset generated in this work may be useful as part of training sets for machine-learning models aimed at predicting interfacial properties, such as molecules dissociation energies. In addition, the availability of full structural relaxation pathways, including intermediate geometries, energies, and atomic forces, is relevant for the development of data-driven force fields, which require consistent information along the relaxation trajectory. For this reason, we provide the complete set of relaxation outputs, rather than only the final optimized configurations, to enhance the potential usability of the dataset for such applications. We exploited the ENEA FARO facility59 to manage the demanding use of resources required for the generation of the dataset.
