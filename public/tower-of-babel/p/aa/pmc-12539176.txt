Risk factors for pedicle screw loosening: a comprehensive review
Authors: Qinfan Wu, Jiahu Huang, Yun Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPedicle screw fixation is commonly used in spinal surgery; however, screw loosening is a frequent complication, and adversely affects both surgical outcomes and patient prognosis. This review focuses on pedicle screw loosening, discussing its underlying mechanisms, risk factors, preventive strategies, future perspectives, and controversial issues. The aim is to provide a comprehensive summary of current research in this field, offering valuable insights for both clinical practice and further investigations.Keywords: Pedicle screw loosening, Spinal instrumentation, Postoperative complications

Background

Pedicle screws (PS), first introduced by Raymond Roy-Camille in the early 1970s [1], have revolutionized spinal surgery. Their ability to provide robust spinal stability via three-column fixation has made them a cornerstone of spinal reconstruction to improve spinal stability. However, a common complication associated with PS is loosening [2]. The incidence of PS loosening varies widely, with reported rates ranging from 1% to 60% [3–6]. PS loosening is often attributed to progressive bone loss caused by stress shielding. Loose PS can lead to a cascade of adverse clinical outcomes, including pain, limited activity, spinal instability, and even the need for revision surgery [7]. These consequences can have a profound negative impact on patients’ quality of life and their families. It is important to recognize that various definitions of pedicle screw loosening exist, leading to significant discrepancies in reported loosening rates. Additionally, not all loosening is symptomatic. Studies have shown that the incidence of screw loosening is four times greater when defined by a radiolucent rim > 1 mm compared to screw pull-out [6]. Several risk factors can contribute to screw loosening. This review seeks to summarize these potential risk factors and outline prevention strategies to mitigate the risk of PS loosening following internal fixation surgery. This review aims to raise clinicians’ awareness about the risks of PS loosening.

Pedicle screw loosening

Pedicle screw loosening can be assessed using plain radiography, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The different imaging modalities vary in their sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing screw loosening. For instance, a prospective clinical trial involving 41 patients reported a sensitivity of 43.9% and specificity of 92.1% for MRI detection of screw loosening, while CT showed a sensitivity of 64.8% and specificity of 96.7%, and plain radiography had a sensitivity of 54.2% and specificity of 83.5%. Despite advancements in MRI technology, CT remains the preferred diagnostic modality [7]. CT can evaluate the relationship between bone density and screw loosening by measuring Hounsfield units (HU). In a study of 503 patients who underwent lumbar pedicle screw fixation, the mean HU value at L1–4 was significantly lower in the screw-loosening group than in the non-loosening group (106.3 ± 33.9 vs. 132.6 ± 42.9, p < 0.001), and HU was an independent predictor of screw loosening (OR 0.977; 95% CI 0.970–0.985) [4]. MRI assesses the risk of screw loosening by calculating the vertebral bone quality (VBQ) score. In an investigation of 211 patients who underwent transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that a higher VBQ score (OR: 27.887 ± 0.514, 95% CI: 10.189–76.326) was a significant predictor of screw loosening, with a diagnostic accuracy of 78.9% [8].

Currently, the criteria for diagnosing pedicle screw loosening remain controversial. One approach involves intraoperative measurement of the screw removal torque, for which values < 1.02 Nm are defined as indicative of loosening. An analysis of 33 patients using this method reported a loosening rate of 33% [2]. Different studies vary in their definitions of screw removal torque values [7]. Another method evaluates angular changes, measuring the angle between the pedicle screw and the cranial endplate. Significant differences in this angle were observed between early and late postoperative images, with an angular change ≥ 2° demonstrating high sensitivity (75%) and specificity (89%) for the diagnosis of screw loosening [9]. Another diagnostic criterion is the identification of a radiolucent zone exceeding 1 mm in diameter surrounding the pedicle screw on CT or radiographic images [10]. Additionally, clinical symptoms may be used as supplementary indicators of screw loosening.

Review methods

The PubMed database was searched for clinical studies, clinical trials, comparative studies, controlled clinical trials, multi-center studies, observational studies, and randomized controlled trials published between 2010 and 2025. The search terms used were “screw loosening”, “pedicle screw loosening”, “pedicle screw failure”, and “screw pull-out”. Initially, 202 articles published in English were retrieved. Following a preliminary screening process focused on titles and abstracts, biomechanical studies, finite element analyses, and all forms of review articles were excluded from consideration. Original research studies were reviewed meticulously to identify comprehensive and credible risk factors for pedicle screw loosening. Building on these findings, additional searches were conducted to explore the mechanisms underpinning the association between specific risk factors and pedicle-screw loosening.

General factors

Gender is not a risk factor for PS loosening [4, 11–13]. While studies by Yuan [11] and Ohba [12] found no significant difference in age between PS loosening and non-loosening groups, this does not definitively exclude age as a risk factor, as the grouping criteria in these studies may have influenced the results. Age is a significant risk factor for PS loosening, with older individuals more likely to experience this complication [14]. This association is likely linked to age-related conditions such as osteoporosis and spinal degeneration in the elderly population [6]. Body mass index [12] showed no significant differences between the PS loosening and non-PS loosening groups. Smoking reduces both bone healing and screw fixation strength [15]. While there is presently no clinical literature explicitly addressing the relationship between alcohol and pedicle screw loosening, it is well-documented that both alcohol can influence bone metabolism and contribute to the development of osteoporosis [16].

Diabetes had an adverse effect on screw stabilization [17]. Diabetes can exert detrimental effects on the skeletal system, leading to diabetes-induced osteoporosis. This condition is characterized by reduced bone mineral density, impaired bone quality, delayed bone healing, diminished bone formation, and increased bone resorption [18,19]. These factors may contribute to the higher rates of screw loosening observed in patients with diabetes [14]. Certain antidiabetic medications are associated with increased susceptibility to fractures in individuals with diabetes [18]; however, their specific influence on PS loosening remains uncertain.

Bone mineral density

Osteoporosis is a well-established risk factor for PS loosening. The primary defining feature of osteoporosis is diminished bone mass and bone micro-architectural deterioration [20]. The reduced bone mineral density can lead to stress concentration at the screw and bone-screw interfaces [21]. During PS insertion, the torque applied to the screw initially increases and then reaches a plateau. This plateau, which represents the torque at which failure occurs, is a reliable indicator and is positively correlated with both bone mineral density and bone microarchitecture [22]. Furthermore, bone mineral density has a significant linear relationship with screw extraction torque. Lower bone mineral density correlates with lower screw extraction torque [2]. In patients with osteoporosis, there is an imbalance between osteogenesis and osteolysis [20]. This imbalance can hinder the integration of the bone–metallic implant interface, potentially leading to PS loosening [23]. Therefore, it is posited that any diseases or medications that induce osteoporosis can potentially increase the susceptibility to PS loosening. Early postmenopausal osteoporosis can be diagnosed by analysis of specific ferroptosis-related genes, offering potential avenues for the early detection and treatment of the condition [24].

Paraspinal muscles

Paraspinal muscles, including the psoas, erector spinae, and multifidus, play a crucial role in spinal stability and movement [25]. Studies have demonstrated a strong association between preoperative paraspinal muscle atrophy, fat infiltration, and PS loosening following lumbar surgery [26]. Surgical procedures, especially those involving lumbar fusion, posterior surgery, and non-minimally invasive fusion techniques, can cause significant damage to the paraspinal muscles, leading to significantly increased paraspinal muscle atrophy [27]. Furthermore, patients with greater pre-existing musculature often experience more significant postoperative atrophy [28]. Paraspinal muscle degeneration, particularly the psoas muscles and erector spinae, has been identified as an independent risk factor for PS loosening in patients with adult degenerative scoliosis [29, 30]. The close anatomical relationship between the skeletal microstructure of the lumbar pedicle and paraspinal muscles may also contribute to the influence of paraspinal muscle morphology on PS loosening [31].

Spinopelvic parameters

The sagittal balance of the spine is crucial to maintaining an upright posture. Age and lifestyle factors can contribute to changes in spinal and pelvic parameters. Degenerated intervertebral discs and enlarged lumbar spine joints can alter the sagittal balance, decreasing or increasing the anterior convexity angle of the lumbar spine and affecting pelvic parameters [32]. During surgery, spinal surgeons can use screws and rods to adjust the spinal sagittal plane parameters. This approach can also be used to reduce and fix lumbar spondylolisthesis. Zhou [33] found that deeper screw placement within the safe range can lead to better restoration of spinopelvic sagittal balance. However, a larger pelvic incidence (PI)-lumbar lordosis (LL) difference and a greater postoperative pelvic tilt have been identified as independent predictors of PS loosening [34]. Specifically, a preoperative PI-LL > 10° increases this risk [5]. Shin et al. [35] demonstrated an independent correlation between postoperative C7-S1 sagittal vertical axis(SVA) and screw loosening. A C7-S1 SVA of 36.9 mm or more was significantly associated with screw loosening. Moreover, preoperative lateral semi-dislocation of ≥ 8 mm and postoperative thoracolumbar kyphosis greater than 10° are also significant risk factors for pedicle screw loosening [12]. There are few reports on the impact of coronal imbalance on screw loosening; however, it is a research direction worth exploring.

Surgical related

Surgical segment length is a critical determinant of screw loosening. Long-segment fixation is associated with a higher susceptibility to screw loosening compared to short-segment fixation [36]. Long-segment fixation screws positioned at the proximal and distal ends are highly prone to loosening [34, 36, 37], possibly due to stress concentration at these points. Cephalad screws experience greater biomechanical stress and have a higher risk of loosening [21]. Additionally, S1 screw insertion, due to its anatomical location and higher biomechanical stress, may elevate the risk of screw loosening [38]. MacLeod [39] observed that the number of screws is the most significant determinant of screw loosening under torsion load. When six screws were employed instead of four in osteoporotic bone, increasing the number of screws from four to six can reduce stress around the nearest screw to the fracture by 59%.

TLIF is a surgical technique used for the treatment of disorders of the lumbar spine, and has been found to protect against PS loosening, reducing early screw loosening rates by up to 60% compared to posterior lumbar fusion [40]. During both flexion-extension and lateral bending activities, the incidence of pedicle screw loosening in TLIF was significantly lower compared to that in anterior lumbar interbody fusion [41]. Furthermore, direct lateral interbody fusion offers improved screw stability and a reduced risk of screw loosening compared to TLIF [42]. This may be related to posterior preservation of the spinous process and ligamentous complex, enhancing posterior stability. While variations in screw-rod systems and vertebral stress resulting from the use of different screw placement techniques have been observed [43], there has been limited analysis of the direct relationships between these factors and pedicle screw loosening. In a study of 175 patients with lumbar degeneration, it was found that resection of the bilateral facet joint significantly increased the risk of screw loosening, while fusion using interbody cages with larger load-bearing surfaces was associated with a lower rate of screw loosening [44]. Pedicle screws used for fractured vertebral bodies and non-fusion surgery are more prone to loosening [3].

The angle of screw insertion may influence the stability of the screw, with a steeper sagittal plane angle potentially enhancing stability, particularly in vertebrae with decreased bone density [45]. Inadequate screw placement can occur during surgical procedures, leading to potential complications. Ye et al. [46] compared the withdrawal strength of unsatisfactory screws to those placed using the standard Magerl screw placement path. They found variations in the decreased withdrawal strengths of unsatisfactory screws. Additionally, repositioning the screws after overlap was observed to increase the withdrawal strength at overlaps of 20% and 40%, while decreasing the withdrawal strength at 60% and 80% overlap. Biomechanical experiments have demonstrated that the funnel technique reduced the pull-out strength of the pedicle screw [47]. When pedicle screws are connected during surgery, rigid titanium rods were found to be associated with a higher risk of screw loosening during the early postoperative period (within 12 months) compared to flexible PEEK rods [48].

Screw

The pedicle screw diameter directly affects its proximity to the cortical bone and influences the screw/pedicle fill ratio. This ratio determines the PS pullout resistance and flexion-extension load [49]. A larger pedicle screw diameter can substantially enhance the mechanical stability of the screw-bone interface, particularly in cases of decreased bone mass [50]. Screws exhibit varying inner and outer diameters. A larger outer diameter and a smaller core diameter can decrease the rate of screw loosening [44]. Using preoperative and postoperative analyses, Grabala [51] demonstrated that larger screw diameters can lead to greater pedicle expansion, with observed expansion rates reaching up to 78%. This expansion has a minimal impact on the vertebral canal, and pedicle rupture often occurs through the lateral wall [52]. However, lateral wall perforation can reduce the pull-out strength of pedicle screws by approximately 23.4% [53]. Clinicians remain concerned about the potential risks associated with oversized pedicle screws rupturing the medial wall of the pedicle. To minimize these risks, thorough preoperative and intraoperative assessments are imperative. In revision surgery, larger diameter pedicle screws are often recommended to improve biomechanical stability. Krishnan [54] found that increasing the size of a revision screw by 1 mm can decrease pullout strength by 36%, whereas increasing the size by 2 mm can increase pullout strength by 35%. Research indicates that pedicle screw length can influence resistance to lateral bending, axial rotation loads, and mechanical stress at the bone-screw interface [49]. The ratio of screw length to vertebral body height is a critical factor in distributing internal fixation strength and stress within the vertebral body, similar to the principle of leverage [55]. Deeper screw insertion can provide better anchoring, while incomplete threading can lead to localized stress concentration, increasing the risk of screw breakage and loosening [56].

Thread geometry also plays a significant role in determining screw strength and stability. Deeper threads and increased pitch can reduce the rate of screw loosening [44]. Double-threaded screws have been shown to have higher pullout strength compared to single-threaded screws [57, 58]. However, there are conflicting findings, with some studies suggesting that single-threaded designs offer better stability under axial load than double-threaded designs [59]. Weiser [60] corroborated the notion that cortical threaded pedicle screws exhibit greater fatigue resistance than conventional pedicle screws in patients with low bone densities. In a separate study, Karakaşlı et al. [61] conducted a comparative analysis of six different thread designs and concluded that cortical threaded pedicle screws demonstrated superior mechanical stability. Pedicle screws with an undercut thread are less prone to migration and rotation, and are thus more stable in the bone than those with a V-shaped thread [62].

Infection

Postoperative spine infections can increase the risk of pedicle screw loosening [63]. Research indicates that pathogenic bacteria colonize up to 42.4% of loosened pedicle screws [64]. Staphylococci were the most commonly observed (53.1%-62.5%) pathogenic bacteria on screw surfaces, followed by propionibacteria, with the presence of pathogenic bacteria during surgery being directly proportional to the surgical procedure duration [63, 65]. Additionally, revision surgery can further increase the likelihood of detecting pathogenic bacteria on screw surfaces [66]. Bacterial contamination can be avoided if the screws are prepackaged under sterile conditions with an intraoperative guard [67].

Prevention strategies

Researchers have explored various strategies to mitigate the risk of screw loosening (Fig. 2). During surgical planning, it is advisable to analyze and select the segments for fusion, the method of fusion, and the trajectory for screw placement [6, 17]. Consistent anti-osteoporosis treatment is recommended for patients with osteoporosis necessitating internal spinal fixation, with teriparatide showing superior effects on osteoporosis compared to bisphosphonates [68]. Pedicle screw design modifications to the thread, pitch, and shape can improve the screw’s mechanical characteristics [44, 57, 61]. Screw surface modifications can enhance bone integration capacity [69]. Improvements in screw material can potentially alleviate stress at the screw-bone interface [70]. Bone cement screws can help prevent screw displacement and minimize the risk of pedicle screw loosening. Minimizing the surgery duration and administering preoperative antibiotics may decrease the incidence of postoperative chronic infection, further lowering the risk of screw loosening [59]. Intraoperative robotics can enhance the precision of screw placement and reduce the need for multiple screw insertions, contributing to a lower risk of pedicle screw loosening [71]. Postoperative paraspinal muscle degeneration is a significant risk factor for pedicle screw loosening. Theoretically, combining less invasive surgery with postoperative back muscle exercises and avoiding heavy physical labor after surgery may help reduce the incidence of screw loosening [72]. It should be emphasized that exercises that place high loads on the lumbar spine should be avoided during the early postoperative period [73]. However, contrary to popular belief, postoperative bracing does not appear to improve intervertebral fusion rates or reduce complications in lumbar degenerative diseases [74]. There is limited high-level evidence-based research directly addressing the potential protective effects of postoperative bracing on pedicle screw loosening.

Conclusion

Postoperative pedicle screw loosening in spinal surgery is a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by multiple factors. Therefore, clinicians must be aware of the risk factors associated with pedicle screw loosening and implement appropriate individualized strategies to minimize complications, alleviate patient discomfort, and enhance the overall success of spinal surgery.

With the increasing integration of artificial intelligence and robotics, along with advancements in pharmaceutical development, the precise prediction of the risk of pedicle screw loosening and the formulation of personalized treatment strategies will further assist surgeons in mitigating this complication. The development of novel biomaterials also represents a key research focus for reducing screw loosening. Additionally, further investigation into the biomechanical forces acting on screws in vivo and their interactions with surrounding tissues is essential for optimization of clinical decision-making.

Limitations

Given that this review is not systematic, there may be inherent biases in the selection of literature, and some pertinent publications may not have been included. The article predominantly addresses clinical issues and synthesizes widely acknowledged, clinically significant risk factors for pedicle screw loosening as reported in the literature. We posit that this review offers a thorough and valuable resource for clinicians seeking to enhance their understanding of pedicle screw loosening.
