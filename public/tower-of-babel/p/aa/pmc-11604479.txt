The penetration of therapeutics across the blood-brain barrier: Classic case studies and clinical implications
Authors: William A Banks, Elizabeth M Rhea, May J Reed, Michelle A Erickson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe blood-brain barrier (BBB) plays central roles in the maintenance and health of the brain. Its mechanisms to safeguard the brain against xenobiotics and endogenous toxins also make the BBB the primary obstacle to the development of drugs for the central nervous system (CNS). Here, we review classic examples of the intersection of clinical medicine, drug delivery, and the BBB. We highlight the role of lipid solubility (heroin), saturable brain-to-blood (efflux: opiates) and blood-to-brain (influx: nutrients, vitamins, and minerals) transport systems, and adsorptive transcytosis (viruses and incretin receptor agonists). We examine how the disruption of the BBB that occurs in certain diseases (tumors) can also be modulated (osmotic agents and microbubbles) and used to deliver treatments, and the role of extracellular pathways in gaining access to the CNS (albumin and antibodies). In summary, this review provides a historical perspective of the key role of the BBB in delivery of drugs to the brain in health and disease.

Introduction

Diseases of the central nervous system (CNS) are among the most difficult to treat. Whether the disease arises from a known event, such as a traumatic brain injury, or has unknown antecedents, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), effective therapeutic interventions are often rare or ineffectual. A main reason for this is that drug development for the brain is especially challenging, and much of that challenge arises from the difficulty of developing drugs that cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) sufficiently to achieve therapeutic CNS concentrations.1 Here, we review in a “case study” format examples of drugs whose interactions with the BBB define their CNS profiles. We have attempted to use examples that are classics in the BBB literature and relevant to current clinical practice. We often follow the underlying concept of the classical example to other clinically relevant findings, including some non-drug delivery aspects of the BBB. This review is not exhaustive but focuses on illustrative examples likely to be of interest to the clinician with an interest in drugs that affect the CNS. We will concentrate on the vascular BBB, but parallel reviews to this one could be written based on the blood-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) barrier, the blood-retinal barrier, the tanycytic barriers of the circumventricular organs, and the meningeal barrier.

To understand the classic cases and concepts, it will help to have a general overview of the BBB. The vascular BBB exists because of three major modifications of the capillary bed of the brain.2 The first modification is that wherever individual brain endothelial cells (BECs) appose, they are “cemented” together by tight junctions that greatly limit the paracellular leakage of substances. Second, fenestrae, which play a major role in leakage across some capillary beds, are absent from BECs. Third, pinocytosis is greatly reduced, resulting in very low fluid or bulk phase transcytosis. These three modifications mean that the capillary bed of the brain is not leaky and does not produce an ultrafiltrate. Without the production of a nourishing ultrafiltrate, the needs of the brain must be met in other ways. The BBB plays a nutritive role by transporting from blood to brain glucose, amino acids, vitamins, minerals, and many of the other substances needed by the brain. Efflux mechanisms that involve the BBB, such as p-glycoprotein (P-gp), help to rid the brain of endogenous and exogenous toxins. “Informational” molecules, such as some peptides and regulatory proteins, also cross the BBB by a number of mechanisms, aiding in brain-body communication.3,4 The BECs also send and receive messages at both the luminal and abluminal sides of the BBB. Some of the messages received on one side of the BEC result in a release of an informational molecule on the other side, forming a mechanism for communication between blood and brain across the BBB. Other messages affect BEC functions. For example, messages from pericytes, astrocytes, and other cells comprising the neurovascular unit induce the BECs to express the BBB phenotype. The resulting phenotype of the BBB is not static, but varies as a function of development, aging, physiological changes, injuries, and disease states. As such, the BBB is best viewed as a dynamic interface or border.5,6 There are many recent reviews that consider the structure and functioning of the BBB in greater depth as well as its roles in disease, including those by Sweeney et al.7 and Schaeffer and Iadecola.8

The importance of lipid solubility: Acylating morphine and incretins

In 1897, a young Felix Hoffmann working at the German dye company Bayer synthesized an acylated version of salicylic acid, which Bayer marketed under the trademark name of Aspirin. Eleven days later, Hoffmann synthesized diacetylmorphine, which Bayer marketed for headache and as a cough suppressant less addicting than morphine.9,10 This compound not only suppressed cough but also gave the user confidence and a heroic feeling, suggesting the trademark name of Heroin.

When injected intravenously, heroin is quickly taken up by the brain. Oldendorf used his brain uptake index (BUI), a methodology that measures the first-pass uptake of substances using an intracarotid artery injection approach, to demonstrate that heroin had an uptake of 68%, whereas the uptake of morphine was not detected.11 This tremendous rate of uptake occurs because acylation greatly increases the lipid solubility of morphine, enhancing the ability of heroin to cross the cell membranes which form the BBB.

Paul Ehrlich’s studies in 1902 showed that dyes taken up by the brain were also taken up by fat, indicating an importance of lipid solubility and providing a foundation for the concept of a BBB. However, Ehrlich never interpreted his results as caused by a barrier. Lewandowsky recognized that the capillary bed of the brain was the basis for one of the brain barriers, and Lina Stern recognized that the barrier was dynamic.12,13 Work in the 1930s showed that lipophilic compounds crossed the cell membranes of blue-green algae more readily than did hydrophilic compounds.14 Later, Oldendorf used his BUI method to show lipid solubility corrected for molecular weight is a powerful predictor of the rate at which small molecules cross the BBB.15 Some peptides were later shown to follow a similar pattern.16

Heroin and morphine illustrate the principle that small lipid-soluble molecules readily cross the BBB. Besides the opiates, other recreational drugs, such as nicotine and ethanol, access the brain by virtue of their lipid solubility. Lipid solubility plays a major role in brain uptake for most small-molecule therapeutics, explaining both their therapeutic actions and their side effects. Heroin’s rapid entry into brain underlies its addictive potential relative to morphine, despite the early marketing claims.

A main feature of lipophilic compounds crossing the BBB by membrane or cellular diffusion is that uptake is not saturable, so, all other influencing factors remaining unchanged, there is a linear correlation between the concentration of the substance in the blood and the concentration in the CNS. To be transported into the CNS, a circulating substance must transition from an aqueous environment (plasma) to a lipid environment (cell membrane) and back again into an aqueous environment (brain interstitial fluid). Substances that are too lipid soluble can become trapped in the cell membrane of the BEC, unable to partition back into the interstitial fluid. Thus, a substance can be too lipid soluble, impeding both its ability to be introduced into blood and its ability to cross the cell layer formed by the BBB. For this or other reasons, the relation between lipid solubility and entry into the brain is biphasic, peaking at an octanol/water partition coefficient of 10–10017,18 (Figure 1A). It should also be noted that lipid solubility may increase the rate of a substance’s ability to cross the BBB, but it may not necessarily increase the amount of the administered dose that enters the brain. This is because lipid-soluble drugs are taken up avidly by other tissues, such as the liver. A reduction in blood levels reduces the presentation of the drug to the BBB and so proportionately reduces its availability to enter the brain.

(C) Some endogenous substrates have specific transporters that aid in delivery from blood to brain. These transporters can be saturable (LAT1) and mediated by receptor-ligand interactions (TfR). While not always selective, some transporters have been targeted by “Trojan horse”-mediated therapeutic delivery to the brain.

(D) While lipid solubility is a good predictor of BBB penetration, charge can also play an important role. The glycocalyx and cell membrane play roles in substrate delivery due to interactions based on a substrate’s charge. CSF, brain parenchyma, and blood have slight variations in pH, impacting the charge interactions. Adsorptive transcytosis occurs when endocytosis is induced on the luminal surface, as occurs with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA).

Hypertonic solutions cause the brain endothelial cell to swell, disrupting the BBB, increasing paracellular transport. In contrast to lipid solubility, hydrogen bonding, charge, and molecular weight are all inversely related to the ability of molecules to cross the BBB19,20 (Figure 1A). Lipinski et al. used high-throughput screening data from a subset of small molecular weight compounds derived from the World Drug Index to define 4 characteristics of compounds that predict solubility/permeability.21 Termed the rule of 5, characteristics that predicted poor permeation were having more than 5 hydrogen bond donors, more than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors, a molecular weight of greater than 500, and a calculated log p greater than 5. Many other studies have supported these or related characteristics, including lipid solubility, to hold for the BBB as well, a few of which are cited here.22,23,24,25,26,27 Antibiotics, antifungals, vitamins, cardiac glycosides, drugs that use transporters, and perhaps some macrocycles are “beyond the rule of 5 (bRo5).” Compounds with the text string “Peptides” were excluded from inclusion. Peptides are likely bRo5, at least those that can assume secondary or tertiary structures that hide either lipophilic or charged domains.28 That molecular weight plays a role in countering the BBB penetration of lipid-soluble drugs is not disputed. However, whether there is an absolute molecular weight cutoff, as typically stated in reviews, is controversial. Levin29 proposed a cutoff of 400 Da, but the compounds that did not cross well in his study were all later discovered to be substrates for the brain-to-blood efflux system, P-gp. Others have proposed a cutoff of 600 Da.30 In contrast, Banks and Kastin found lipid solubility to be a good predictor of many peptides, including those of a few thousand Da.16 Davis and colleagues used halogenation to increase the lipid solubility of opiate peptides up to 900 Da, demonstrating increased analgesic effects with increased BBB permeability.31,32 Cytokine-induced neutrophil chemoattractant 1 with a molecular weight of 7,800 Da is the largest substance proposed to enter the brain by lipid solubility.33

Diacetylmorphine teaches us another lesson about drug delivery to the brain, that of latentiation or, as more popularly referred to today, the concept of a prodrug. A prodrug is a drug which is converted from an inactive or less active form to a more active form after administration. Heroin is the earliest example of a CNS prodrug. Heroin crosses the BBB by virtue of its lipid solubility, but once in the brain, it is not heroin that is the active drug. Rather, it is deacetylated to mono-acetylmorphine (MAM) and morphine. Just as hydrophilic drugs do not cross the cell membranes of the vascular BBB in the blood-to-brain direction well, they also do not cross well in the brain-to-blood direction.34,35 Thus, morphine and MAM remain in the brain where they exert their effects. For this reason, less lipid-soluble opiates are more effective than more lipid-soluble drugs after intrathecal administration because the lipid-soluble drugs exit the brain more quickly than the less lipid-soluble drugs.34 After injection into the brain, acetaminophen, a more lipid-soluble drug, has less residence time in the brain than the less lipid-soluble drug atenolol.35

It should be noted that acylated compounds do not always cross the BBB better than their non-acylated counterparts. For example, the gastrointestinal hormone ghrelin occurs in two endogenous forms, one form acylated with octanoic acid and a non-acylated form.36 The initial assumption in the literature was acyl-ghrelin would cross the BBB faster than non-acyl-ghrelin because of its higher lipid solubility. But non-acyl-ghrelin is more avidly transported across the BBB by a saturable transporter than is acyl-ghrelin.37 This illustrates that when saturable transporters are the main mechanism by which a parent substance crosses the BBB, increasing lipid solubility may not improve its uptake. Similarly, acylated incretin receptor analogs have much lower brain uptake than the non-acylated incretin analogs.38

Efflux systems: From diarrhea to dementia

Whereas degradation is the main mechanism for clearance of a substance for most tissues, BBB efflux mechanisms provide the brain with another (Figure 1B). A common clinical scenario is illustrated by the side effect of opiate use and constipation. This is because activation of the μ- and κ-opiate receptors located in the gut decreases peristalsis. However, one patient’s side effect is another patient’s therapeutic intervention, and opiates are used to treat diarrhea in infants, children, and adults. How does Lomotil (containing the opiate diphenoxylate) or Imodium (containing the opiate loperamide) prevent diarrhea without producing the CNS effects that are the hallmark of the opiates? The answer lies in how these two opiates are handled by the BBB. Both are substrates of P-gp, a promiscuous, intracellular pump. P-gp, also called multidrug resistance protein 1, was first discovered for its contribution to the acquisition of resistance to chemotherapeutics that many cancers showed after repeated treatments. However, P-gp is expressed constitutively by the gastrointestinal tract and the BBB.39 In the BBB, P-gp is intercalated into the luminal membrane of the BEC, poised to bind to xenobiotics as they attempt to cross the BBB and efflux them back to the blood stream.40 P-gp is one of many brain-to-blood efflux transporters that act as major blocks to the brain’s uptake of xenobiotics and endogenous substances circulating in the bloodstream. In the cases of diphenoxylate and loperamide, P-gp avidly binds them, blocking their accumulation in the brain. As a result, these compounds have access to peripheral opiate receptors, but not to those in the CNS. In mice in which the P-gp transporter has been deleted, peripherally administered loperamide has analgesic properties equivalent to morphine.41

P-gp has many ligands other than opiates. Ligands include substances from many different classes and categories, including lipids, steroids, peptides, glycosides, and antiretrovirals.40 Indeed, about 30% of the drugs used clinically are P-gp substrates with many modulating the expression of P-gp as well. Many of these drugs would have CNS toxicity were it not for the protection of P-gp. As examples, when the P-gp substrate and anti-parasitic drug ivermectin were given to a strain of collies and mice in which P-gp expression was absent, the collies and mice experienced seizures and death.42,43

Two characteristics of the P-gp transporter have direct clinical consequences. First, the affinity of the P-gp transporter for many substances is weak, so that they are still able to accumulate in the brain sufficiently to exert CNS effects. Second, the activity of P-gp can be modulated; for example, inflammation greatly increases the activity of P-gp.44 Most anti-seizure medications are weak P-gp substrates so that they are still able to enter the CNS in effective amounts. However, seizure activity, possibly through its induction of inflammation, increases P-gp activity, thus reducing the accumulation of anti-seizure medications in the brain. It has been proposed as a reason why status epilepticus is resistant to anti-seizure medications: the robust seizure activity enhances P-gp activity to the level that anti-seizure medications are unable to accumulate in the brain to therapeutic levels.45 This would also explain why valproic acid and anakinra, two medications with anti-seizure activities that are not P-gp substrates, often remain effective in status epilepticus.

Endogenous substances can also be P-gp ligands and loss of P-gp activity has been associated with disease.46 For example, amyloid beta protein (Aβ) is a substrate of P-gp and another brain-to-blood efflux system, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP-1). Aβ is a major component of the amyloid plaques found in AD and believed by many to contribute to AD in its various aggregated forms. Efflux activity of these two transport systems is decreased in AD and most, if not all, of the animal models of AD examined to date. The implication is that loss of efflux activity allows the accumulation of Aβ in the brain, resulting in Aβ toxicity.47 A demonstration that knockdown of LRP-1 activity in mice results in increased levels of Aβ in the brain and cognitive impairment supports this idea.48

There is an abundance of efflux transporters other than P-gp, and the varied roles that efflux systems play in medicine and the world at large are fascinating. An efflux transporter allows the tobacco hornworm to ingest nicotine.49 Decreased activity in BBB efflux transporters could underlie the increased CNS sensitivity to drugs seen with aging, AD, and Parkinson’s disease.50 The decreased efflux of Aβ protein by P-gp and other efflux mechanisms may contribute to the accumulation of Aβ protein by the brain and so the progression of AD. Premature recovery of an efflux transporter for methionine enkephalin has been proposed as the cause of alcohol withdrawal seizures.51 The amount of blood-borne IgG antibodies entering the brain is reduced by an efflux system.52 The CNS-to-blood transport of brain-derived exosomes may allow blood to be used as a liquid biopsy of brain diseases such as Parkinson’s disease.53 The treatment of CNS HIV is difficult because the protease inhibitors and zidovudine are effluxed.54,55 Disruption of the BBB is generally ineffective in increasing the brain concentration of drugs that are ligands of efflux systems.56,57 In short, efflux systems are an understudied area, but work to date shows they are involved in disease states and often dictate which drugs are neurotoxic and which are effective CNS therapeutics.

Blood-to-brain transport systems: Mucuna beans and Trojan horses

Ayurveda, a term in Sanskrit meaning “knowledge of life,” is the traditional form of medicine practiced in India. The first description of Parkinson’s disease symptoms (bradykinesia, tremor, gait disorder, depression, and dementia) in Ayurveda literature dates back to around 600 BC.58 Ingestion of a type of bean (Mucuna) was recommended for treatment of the symptoms. In 1913, Marcus Guggenheim identified and isolated the active ingredient found in a similar strain of beans (Vicia faba), levodopa (L-dopa).59,60 At a similar time, the synthetic version of L-dopa was first described by Casimir Funk61 in his search for the formation of adrenaline. Upon self-experimentation and ingestion of L-dopa, Guggenheim discovered a terrible side effect of taking L-dopa, violent vomiting, discarding the discovery for years to come. This side effect was later identified to be largely driven by the peripheral metabolism of L-dopa to dopamine. It was not until the 1950–60s that L-dopa was rediscovered for the treatment of Parkinson’s disease. George Cotzias revolutionized therapeutic treatment of Parkinson’s disease with a high dosage of L-dopa,62 administered in a dose-escalation manner that limited nauseating side effects. Yahr found similar benefits with L-dopa in Parkinson’s disease at the same time as Cotzias.63

Dopamine is a water-soluble hydrophilic drug that does not exhibit any of Lipinski’s characteristics for BBB penetration, does not have a BBB transporter, nor is it highly charged64 (Figure 1C). The transporter for dopamine, DAT1, is not present at the BBB. This prevented the use of dopamine as a therapy for patients with Parkinson’s disease. Oldendorf first identified that L-dopa could be transported across the BBB65 and later identified the transporter as the large neutral amino acid carrier system.66 Since decarboxylation of L-dopa converts it to dopamine, this facilitative transport system (L1) could be targeted to deliver L-dopa into the brain. However, as it also carries other large neutral amino acids and since this transport system is saturable, these substrates must compete for entry, in contrast to lipid-soluble substrates like heroin described earlier. This sodium-independent amino acid exchanger was later classified as LAT1 (L-type amino acid transporter small subunit 1). Cotzias initially used phenylalanine for Parkinson’s disease treatment but found this worsened parkinsonism, likely due to its competition with tyrosine (the precursor to dopamine) for entry into the brain.67 Research into limiting the chronic side effects, extending the half-life of L-dopa, and enhancing the transport of dopamine, L-dopa, and other analogs or prodrugs across the BBB has continued to build upon the seminal findings in the mid-20th century.68 That no better drug for Parkinson’s disease has been found than the first one discovered 60 years ago is an increasingly rare pharmacological phenomenon.59

In healthy individuals, LAT1 mRNA expression is 100-fold greater at the BBB compared to other tissues such as the lung, spleen, or heart.69 Therefore, LAT1 has been exploited for drug delivery and targeting to the CNS of substances other than L-dopa, including the delivery of gabapentin and baclofen for management of pain. Additionally, since cancerous cells upregulate the expression of LAT1, substrates are used as a diagnostic tool and this transport system has been taken advantage of to deliver anti-tumor agents such as L-para-boronophenylalanine used for boron neutron capture therapy and α-emitting nuclide-labeled LAT1 substrates developed for nuclear medicine treatment.70 Because of LAT1, brain levels of large neutral amino acids and their neurotransmitter derivatives (dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, and serotonin) correlate with the blood levels of large neutral amino acids.71 As such, the saturable aspect of LAT1 has been used in other therapeutic contexts. Large doses of other neutral amino acids have been used to block the entry of phenylalanine in patients with phenylketonuria.71,72 L-leucine prevents inflammation-induced depression by blocking the LAT1-dependent transport of kynurenine.73 Tryptophan has been used to increase brain levels of serotonin in the treatment of depression and is popular as a sleep aid.74

LAT1 is just one example of a predominant BBB transporter that has been used to help therapeutics gain entry into the brain. Monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), organic cation/carnitine influx transports (e.g., MCT8, OCTN1, and OATP1C1), adenine/adenosine transporters (caffeine is transported by the adenosine transport), the interleukin-1 transporter (anakinra used for the treatment of intractable seizures), and the transferrin receptor (antibody target) are examples of other transporters which have helped drive drug delivery. Targeting the pyrilamine-sensitive proton-coupled organic cation (H+/OC) antiporter, a transporter for pyrilamine, oxycodone, and diphenhydramine, is another novel brain-specific drug delivery approach, in which therapeutics (such as naproxen) are conjugated to cyclic tertiary amines to enhance delivery.75 Overall, however, surprisingly few clinically used drugs enter the brain by way of saturable transporters.

The presence of BBB transporters explains how water-soluble molecules such as glucose and amino acids that are needed in abundance by the CNS can cross the BBB. Typically, substrate transporters transport a substance 10–100 faster than expected based on lipid solubility.15 Caffeine and vitamins (including C, B12, and B6) are also transported in a saturable manner across the BBB. Casimir Funk, the same chemist that initially synthesized L-dopa, was the one that first coined the term “vitamine,” later shortened to vitamin. As many of these transporters are region specific, exploitation of transporters offers the drug development field not only high uptake rates for large, water-soluble compounds but also targeting to specific regions of the CNS.76 Some of these saturable systems are flow dependent, meaning with greater blood flow, transport is increased, as is the case for glucose. Substrates that are transported more slowly are often affected by other factors, including the regulation of hormonal transport by amino acids.76 Transporters exist for an array of differentially sized substrates from amino acids and glucose to larger peptides including cytokines77 and insulin.78,79 Many endogenous peptides and hormones are transported into the brain by saturable mechanisms.80 Some transporters are promiscuous (LRP-1 and P-gp) with a variety of ligands, while others appear to be specific (insulin and leptin).

Taking advantage of saturable systems is a promising approach to promote the transport of therapeutics81 and is a version of the Trojan horse approach. The Trojan horse hypothesis incorporates an agent that does not cross the BBB well to an agent that does cross well. Trojan horse agents include molecules such as transferrin, immune cells, and pathogens such as viral vectors.82 Antibodies targeted to a region within the transporter that is not directly involved in binding to the endogenous ligand is an approach developed for CNS drug delivery. In this way, it is hoped that the delivery system will not interfere with the transport of the endogenous ligand into the CNS. Recently developed antibodies that have been successful include a transport vehicle which consists of an Fc fragment engineered to bind to the transferrin receptor83 or CD98 (the heavy chain of LAT1),84 transporters highly expressed at the BBB. CD98 forms a heteromeric complex with LAT1, connected by a disulfide bond, aiding in translocation to the cell surface.85 A brain-shuttle system uses bispecific antibodies, one used as the targeting/transporter antibody and the other as the therapeutic agent.86 Utilizing Trojan horse approaches, anti BACE183 and TREM287 therapies have targeted the CNS to limit AD pathology and deliver enzyme therapy for a mucopolysaccharidosis, respectively.88 While delivery to the CNS may be successful for some therapies, reducing brain-wide off-target effects is an issue that must be overcome. It may be that some of these approaches are inducing adsorptive transcytosis rather than receptor-mediated transcytosis (discussed further below), which has a different mechanism and toxicity profile.89,90

Characteristics of BBB transporters, such as saturation, can lead to diseases. Maple syrup urine disease, an inherited metabolic disease that results in high levels of branched-chain amino acids in blood, has neurologic manifestations that may be caused by the inability of the other large neutral amino acids to cross the BBB in sufficient amounts to produce adequate levels of neurotransmitters.71 While saturation of transport systems is beneficial for endogenous peptides and response to physiological conditions, saturable systems have the disadvantage of functioning as a threshold for therapeutic applications, limiting the access of large amounts of peptides into the brain.91 Leptin resistance in obesity, for example, occurs in part because of saturation of the leptin transporter.92 Impaired saturable efflux of Aβ protein occurs in AD and contributes to its accumulation in the brain.93 Loss of transport of thyroid hormones into the brain results in Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome.94 HIV-1 can use the mannose-6 phosphate receptor, a transporter for lysosomal enzymes,95 to cross the BBB.96 Deficiencies in GLUT-1, the BBB transporter for glucose, result in De Vivo disease.97 In some cases, neurotransmitters can address the problem the BBB has created. For example, adrenergic stimulation can induce the transporters for leptin and some lysosomal enzymes.98,99

Charge inside and out: Antibiotics to adsorptive transcytosis

As discussed earlier, lipid solubility is a good first predictor of the ability of many small molecules to cross the BBB. But most drugs are to some degree ionized in the blood and the ionized portion has low lipid solubility. In the 1950s–1960s, the pH partition hypothesis was put forward to determine the amount of drug that would enter the brain.100 The uptake by brain and CSF of antibiotics, as well as sedatives, hypnotics, anticonvulsants, and angiographic dyes was assessed using this method. This approach calculates the partition parameter (P∗), which is the lipid solubility of the unionized version of the drug multiplied by the fraction of the drug that is not ionized. In the classic version of this hypothesis, the ionized version of the drug is ignored, although Rapoport pointed out that the contribution could be important in some cases as the partitioning of ionized substances is low, but it is not zero. This low partitioning of ionized substances likely also accounts for how they, despite their low solubility and no saturable transport system, can still cross the BBB in sufficient amounts to have CNS effects.101

Charge also is important because of a pH difference between CSF and plasma. CSF is somewhat more acidic (about 0.1 pH unit less) than plasma, and so weak bases partition into the CSF better than weak acids. Parenchymal pH can also vary from the interstitial pH by about 0.17 with interstitial pH being slightly higher.102 Charge and hydrophobic regions can also interact to determine binding to circulating proteins, either to general binders like albumin or to binding proteins that are highly selective for specific substances. Binding to plasma proteins can have complex effects on the availability of a substance to the brain: on the one hand, binding can prolong half-life in the blood, extending the therapeutic time window; on the other hand, for many drugs, only the unbound or free portion is available to the brain. Despite all these caveats, the general rule is that an uncharged molecule crosses the BBB 1,000–10,000 times better than its ionized version.

Accordingly, it may seem paradoxic that very highly charged molecules can cross the BBB quite well. Whether this charge is from amino acids, as in the case of poly-L-lysine and protamine, or from sugars such as heparan and sialic acid as in the case of glycoproteins, charged molecules can rapidly cross the BBB (Figure 1D). This mechanism has been termed adsorptive transcytosis or adsorptive-mediated transcytosis and has several unique features. First, interactions between cell membrane binding sites and the charged molecule do not follow classic Michaelis-Menten kinetics: injecting excess ligand can sometimes enhance rather than inhibit the passage rate across the BBB. Additionally, co-injection of a highly charged molecule that crosses the BBB robustly can often enhance the rate of passage for the co-injected substance. However, it seems that the co-injected substance must use a similar site to bind to the BEC, such as binding to sialic acid. Transport is vesicular but seems independent of receptor-mediated transcytosis. Classically, the substance is routed to Golgi and lysosomal compartments as well as to the abluminal membrane of BECs. Adsorptive transcytosis is often toxic, but whether the substances are simply toxic or the cellular process of adsorptive transcytosis itself is toxic is unclear.

Viruses, bacteria, and parasites that cross the BBB use a mechanism with many of the features of adsorptive transcytosis. For example, a viral glycoprotein, usually the viral attachment protein, binds to one or more glycoproteins or glycolipids on the cell membrane.103 The cell membrane binding site is not necessarily a transporter nor even a receptor and, when it is, the viral glycoprotein does not necessarily bind to the endogenous ligand’s binding site. Thus, viral uptake may follow stochastic rather than Michaelis-Menten binding kinetics that can be modulated by charged molecules such as protamine or heparan and glycoproteins such as wheat germ agglutinin.96,104 Viruses are often routed to the lysosomal component,105 and viruses capable of crossing the BBB, such as HIV-1, can survive lysosomal uptake.106

It is likely that the glycocalyx, a carbohydrate-rich surface layer that lines the lumen of endothelial cells,107 including those in the brain,108 contributes to adsorptive transcytosis. The glycoproteins and non-sulfated and sulfated glycosaminoglycans that comprise the glycocalyx confer a negative charge to the luminal surface, and so would attract positively charged molecules such as those that cross the BBB via adsorptive transcytosis. The molecular mechanisms that lead to vesicular transport following these charge interactions are presently not clear; however, a possibility is that that core proteins that are attached to glycans facilitate endocytosis once glycans bind via charge interactions or interactions with specific carbohydrate structures in the polysaccharide chain. Glycoproteins (core proteins attached to branched carbohydrates) and proteoglycans (core proteins attached to linear carbohydrates) may interact with ligands via more than one binding site with varying affinities based on the carbohydrate structure. Such interactions may explain the stochastic nature of substances that cross the BBB via adsorptive transcytosis. In addition to viruses, immune-active substances such as chemokines can cross the intact BBB via interactions with glycocalyx components.109

Charge is important in the ability of the therapeutic incretin agonists to cross the BBB. Incretins are peptides (e.g., glucagon-like peptide-1 and gastric inhibitory peptide) that enhance the release of insulin in response to oral glucose. Thus, they boost the amount of insulin released in response to a meal. Therapeutic analogs of the endogenous incretins, referred to as incretin receptor agonists (IRAs), have become major drugs in the treatment of type 2 diabetes.110 Moreover, these drugs appear to have effects on slowing the development of dementias, including AD.111 As such, their ability to cross the BBB is of interest. We have found that some of these IRAs cross the BBB very slowly, likely by the extracellular pathways (see the following paragraph), whereas others cross much faster by a mechanism that is not related to lipid solubility and is not saturable, but correlates with charge and is enhanced by wheat germ agglutinin.38 Therefore, we have speculated that these IRAs cross the BBB using an adsorptive transcytosis-like mechanism.

Our lab has suggested that adsorptive transcytosis may also be important in some versions of the Trojan horse approach to drug delivery to the brain. Naturally occurring pathogens crossing the BBB often use some form of Trojan horse, such as molecular mimicry as described earlier or residing within cells that can cross the BBB.112 As such, viral vectors used for delivery of gene therapy to the brain likely use adsorptive transcytosis to cross the BBB.113,114 In some cases of Trojan horse, the transporting agent binds to the BBB receptor at a region that is not the endogenous ligand’s binding site or uses an antibody to bind to the BBB binding site. Neither off-target binding as seen with pathogens and their toxic glycoproteins nor antibody binding to BECs is physiologic and, therefore, may induce adsorptive transcytosis.115,116,117

Charge plays other roles that affect BBB availability. Weak acids (negatively charged at neutral pH) are often substrates for BBB organic acid efflux systems. Charged drugs can bind significantly to proteins such as albumin, decreasing the amount of unbound drug available for diffusion across the BBB or decreasing the rate of clearance from the blood.

BBB disruption and extracellular pathways: Brain tumors, microbubbles, and antibodies

Despite the presence of numerous CNS barriers, these are not absolute in preventing leakage of substances from the blood into the brain. For example, CSF levels of albumin are about 0.5% of those in serum, which is largely attributed to CSF production and leakage at anatomical sites such as large pial and subarachnoid vessels, and circumventricular organs whose vasculature lacks BBB properties, termed the extracellular pathways by Broadwell.118,119 Similarly, about 0.01% or less of the injected dose of IgG enters the mouse brain depending on the region, although one paper suggests that IgG may have a saturable component of brain uptake.120 Fluid/bulk phase transcytosis across an intact BBB may also contribute to a small amount of albumin leakage.121 This latter route likely occurs as a result of the vesicular pathways involved in transcytosis of substances known to cross the BBB and so is a transcellular pathway.90 Intercellular routes of brain uptake of substances become available when there is a loss of function of the tight junction proteins that prevent the paracellular leakage of substances. Some strategies of delivering therapeutic agents to the brain have taken the approach of transiently disrupting the BBB to increase the leakage of substances across the BBB via these extracellular pathways. This approach has had clinical success in treating brain cancers, described below. Additionally, antibodies and some therapeutics can enter the CSF and brain tissue through Broadwell’s extracellular pathways.119

The first documented surgical resection of a primary cerebral tumor was by Sir Rickman John Godlee in 1884, who, together with Dr. Alex Hughes Bennett, determined the localization of the tumor based on a physical examination, as medical imaging techniques were not yet available. Complete resection of the glioma was successful, although the patient succumbed to infection 1 month later.122,123 Surgical removal continues to be the main treatment for operable brain tumors. Radiotherapy or chemotherapy are adjuvants or serve as primary treatments when the tumors are inoperable. Chemotherapeutics for brain tumors have historically posed a challenge due to difficulties in delivering sufficient quantities of the drug to the tumor. The vasculature of a brain tumor is not universally leaky, but rather exhibits heterogeneity in multiple factors. The latter include degree of leakage, efflux and influx transporter expression, and rheologic and other properties that distinguish it from the healthy brain vasculature124,125 and can limit drug delivery. This was exemplified from early studies of Adriamycin (doxorubicin), which found that brain metastatic disease progressed despite responsiveness of systemic tumors to treatment.126 This property of doxorubicin is consistent with its poor penetration across the BBB as well as it being a P-gp substrate.127 Although the blood-brain-tumor barrier (BBTB) is leakier than the healthy BBB, for many brain tumors, it is still much less permeable than peripheral tissue beds128 and is an obstacle for chemotherapeutic delivery.

In 1945, Broman and Broman first showed that intraarterial infusion of hypertonic solutions could cause BBB disruption,129 which was later supported by studies in 1971 by Rapoport, Hori, and Klatzo who demonstrated that BBB leakage induced by osmotic agents was reversible130 (Figure 1E). The mechanism of BBB disruption by osmotic agents was later attributed to enhanced leakage primarily through extracellular/paracellular pathways that are typically limited by tight junctions.131 This principle of transiently opening tight junctions to deliver chemotherapeutics was soon applied by Neuwelt et al. to show that infusion of a hyperosmotic mannitol solution through the internal carotid artery could greatly and consistently increase the delivery of methotrexate to the canine brain on the side ipsilateral to infusion, and that the BBB disruption could be monitored by computed tomography.132 This study also found that there was a poor correlation of brain and CSF levels of methotrexate, and so was among the early works indicating that CSF sampling may be insufficient for estimating drug delivery to brain parenchyma.132 This concept of transiently disrupting the BBB as a mechanism to deliver drugs to the brain has advanced to clinical applications and disruption of the BBTB has become a primary frontline treatment for chemosensitive brain cancers such as primary CNS lymphomas.125 The purported safety of transiently disrupting the BBTB for chemotherapeutic delivery is evident, from long-term (2–26 years) follow-up of subjects who mostly showed stable or improved cognition and imaging outcomes when compared to their pre-treatment baselines.125 However, it is also known that hyperosmotic disruption of the BBB is not without side effects, which include acute and subacute sterile inflammation,133 brain tissue damage,134 and perioperative seizures in a subset of patients undergoing treatment.135 Further, the increased BBB permeability from hyperosmotic infusions can disrupt the BBB of intact, healthy brain tissue proportionately more than the tumor, although the absolute disruption of the BBTB is still greater.136,137

Other technologies have been developed to induce transient BBB disruption, including the more recent method of magnetic resonance (MR)-guided focused ultrasound combined with intravenous preformed microbubble contrast agents. MR-guided focused ultrasound has various applications including brain tumor ablation as reviewed elsewhere by Hynynen,138 and Pardridge.136 MR-guided focused ultrasound with microbubbles is a technique that concentrates the acoustic energy in the vascular lumen, and the microbubbles expand and contract with ultrasound oscillations, resulting in reproducible, selective BBB disruption at relatively low frequencies.

The development of this method for clinical applications for the intact BBB has been described in detail by Burgess et al.139 Focused ultrasound with microbubbles induces both paracellular and transcellular routes of BBB disruption.136 In subjects with mild AD, it has been shown that three repeated exposures to MR-guided focused ultrasound in two week intervals were generally safe, with no serious adverse events or evidence of worse cognitive decline vs. controls up to 1 year follow-up.140 Both hyperosmotic mannitol and ultrasound/microbubble technologies have been shown in humans to improve delivery of larger therapeutic antibodies to the brain such as rituximab for primary CNS lymphomas, and aducanumab in AD.125,141 Similar to hyperosmotic BBB disruption, focused ultrasound with microbubbles also induces sterile inflammation and reactive gliosis in the brain.136 Nonetheless, the current evidence supports that controlled and transient BBB disruption approaches can be used to increase drug delivery to the brain and do not appear to accelerate cognitive decline in human cohorts with brain tumors or AD. The acute effects of inducing sterile inflammation could be beneficial for inflammatory suppression of tumor growth, or clearance of amyloid deposits.

However, even transient BBB opening can cause some damage to healthy brain tissues and so is a limitation of the BBB disruption approach for CNS drug delivery. In disrupting the brain-tumor barrier, the objective is to cause toxicity to the target cells, but this can be detrimental to the surrounding neurovascular unit when opening the BBB. Other notable limitations and considerations include the fact that certain substances that appear to distribute in CNS parenchyma following BBB disruption are limited to the basement membrane of the brain vasculature.142 Albumin and IgG are among the molecules that appear to have limited brain entry due to the basement membrane, which suggests that an intact basement membrane is needed to protect against purported neurotoxicities of serum proteins,143,144 but may also limit entry of substances like antibody biologics into brain parenchyma. The presence of efflux transporters, which are often upregulated in tumors and their vasculature, can also limit CNS entry even when the BBB is disrupted.145

The aforementioned principles are relevant to the ability of therapeutic antibodies, such as anti-Aβ protein antibodies used to treat AD, to enter the BBB. The pathways described earlier and the brain-to-blood efflux of IgG means that the accumulation of blood-derived IgG by brain is even less than that of albumin. However, that circulating antibodies do enter the brain in amounts sufficient to affect brain function is illustrated by autoimmune brain diseases, such as autoimmune limbic encephalitis and autoimmune synaptopathies.146,147 In these cases, antibodies engage CNS receptors, causing brain diseases. Current dogma states that anti-Aβ antibodies bind and clear components of the amyloid cascade with targets ranging from Aβ oligomers to plaques.148 Currently, however, the long-term effects of these antibodies on cognition is not known.

BBB disruption has been found or theorized to occur in an increasing number of CNS diseases or diseases that affect the CNS. BBB disruption can be transient, massive, localized, or chronic as illustrated by stroke, diabetes, AD, sepsis, multiple sclerosis, and traumatic brain injury. Inflammation is often implicated in BBB disruption as is loss of pericytes. Methods to detect BBB disruption are therefore needed. Imaging is the most sensitive but is expensive and cumbersome. Therefore, biomarkers, particularly those detectable in blood, are being sought and analyzed.149

Conclusions

Here, we have explored several ways in which the BBB is relevant to clinical practice. We have reviewed classic examples of historic drug development that provide insight into current and future endeavors in CNS drug development. We also have reviewed several diseases that are related to the BBB with the BBB being either a target or a cause of disease. In summary, the BBB is intimately involved in the normal functioning of the brain and its properties must be understood to successfully treat diseases of the brain.
