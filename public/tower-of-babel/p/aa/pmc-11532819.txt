Is digitalization essential for abating carbon emission growth in South Asia?
Authors: Muntasir Murshed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Output generation processes across South Asia are deemed unclean (highly emission-intensive) due to excessive reliance on fossil fuels. Thus, decarbonizing the growth processes of countries within this region has critical emphasis among policymakers. However, since the South Asian countries are not yet ready to undergo the renewable energy transition in full form, it is unlikely that their annual carbon discharge levels will subside anytime soon. Under such constraining circumstances, these countries need to limit the rate at which their respective carbon emission levels rise each year. Therefore, the impacts of digitalization on annual carbon emission growth rates in selected South Asian countries are assessed in this study. Overall, considering internet penetration rate as a proxy of digital technology adoption, the results confirm that policies aimed at digitalizing the concerned South Asian economies not only exert emission growth rate-reducing impacts via a direct channel, but also indirectly reduce emission growth rates by making natural resource-based industries more productive, expediting the energy sector greening processes, greening the financial sectors, making corruption controlling measures within institutions more effective, and limiting urbanization-induced environmental quality-worsening effects. Therefore, it is relevant for the South Asian governments to emphasize on digitalization while simultaneously ensuring that green digital technologies are adopted within the major macroeconomic sectors across this region.

Introduction

Environmental pollution, particularly those emerging from greenhouse gas emissions, is currently regarded as one of the most traumatizing global issues. Thus, such concerns have manifested the utmost need for adopting effective emission abatement measures so that the global output production processes can gradually be cleaned over time. Consequently, in response to persistently rising global carbon discharge levels, governments worldwide have ratified diverse environmental protection pacts including the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) and the Paris Accord [[1], [2]]. Accordingly, to comply with the clauses mentioned in these pacts, policymakers globally are currently under intensive pressure to come up with credible emission-controlling plans that are imperative for establishing green growth [3]. Moreover, as the world economies are currently at the crossroads of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, it is also important that steps are taken towards digitalizing the global economic output generation mechanisms so that application of green technologies can help to significantly impede emission propensities [4].

Therefore, acknowledging the target of achieving low-carbon growth, it is essential to delve into whether digital technology adoption (which also indicates the execution of the digitalization1 process) is useful in abating Carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions. From the theoretical perspective, the use of digital technologies is expected to elevate the efficiency rates at which natural resources are consumed; thus, carbon emissions generated from the natural resource sectors can be contained through digitalization [5]. For instance, developing new digital technologies can improve the efficiency rates with which natural resources are extracted and subsequently processed [6]. Similarly, from the viewpoint of economic output production as a whole, since most global economies rely heavily on fossil fuels, digitalization is not only assumed to cut down fossil fuel consumption levels, but also ensure more productive use of such unclean sources of energy; consequently, emission propensities are likely to decline further [7]. Notably, the World Economic Forum (WEF) has projected that global energy sector-based carbon emissions can be curbed by 8% provided more digital technologies, especially the green ones, are developed in the future [8].

Though it is often firmly believed that growth processes, when passed through different channels of digitalization, may become greener over time [9], it is also important to consider the dark side of digitalization which can make environments even more polluted. For instance, widescale adoption of digital technology-based services is likely to boost electricity consumption levels which, in turn, can lead to more anthropogenic discharges of CO2 [10]. This scenario is quite likely to be faced because global economies mostly produce electricity by combusting fossil fuels; notably, around 77% of global electricity output in 2015 was generated using fossil fuels (oil, coal, and natural gas) [11]. More alarmingly, it is assumed that the carbon footprints of digital tech industries are greater than the carbon footprints generated by other carbon-intensive industries [12]. Hence, under such challenging circumstances, digitalization needs to take place in a manner that can enable to control the corresponding surge in electricity demand so that carbon emissions can be abated effectively.

Against the above backdrop, this study empirically appraises the digitalization-CO2 emissions nexus in the context of selected South Asian countries. The decision to focus on this region is influenced by the fact that South Asia is highly susceptible to carbon emission-led climatic hardships [13]. More precisely, the climate vulnerability of this region is largely due to the abject failure of South Asian governments to deploy effective carbon emission-reduction policies [14]. Moreover, it is also well-established that almost all South Asian countries are heavily reliant on unclean natural resources for electricity generation purposes [15,16]; consequently, it is not surprising that this region has not been able to abate its annual carbon emission discharge levels (refer to Fig. 1). On the other hand, it is also worth noting down that a large segment of the South Asian population still lacks access to modern digital services [17]. In this regard, unstable internet supply, poor infrastructure for internet service delivery, and lack of digital literacy, in particular, are assumed to have pinned down the digitalization process in South Asia [17,18]. This current study has a couple of contributions that bridge certain voids in the related South Asian literature. First, though previous studies have checked how annual levels of CO2 emission are determined across South Asia [[19], [20]], these studies have not adequately underscored the necessity of reducing the year-on-year rate at which such emission levels have mounted over the years. Nevertheless, probing the determinants of CO2 emission growth is critically important because South Asia is a predominantly fossil fuel-reliant region whereby mitigating annual carbon emission levels can somewhat be deemed irrational, at least under the present circumstances [21]. In simple terms, unless fossil fuel dependency is substantially reduced, it is quite impossible for South Asian countries to contain their yearly CO2 emission levels. Therefore, under such situations, a more rational goal would be to aim at reducing the annual CO2 Emission Growth Rate (EGR), which, in essence, is more like an environmental damage controlling mechanism for South Asian countries. Second, although most prior studies (but none specifically in the South Asian context) have estimated how digitalization directly affects CO2 emissions [22], the indirect carbon emission-influencing mechanisms of digitalization have not received much mention in the extant literature. Hence, apart from assessing the direct impact, this study also examines how digitalization indirectly impacts the CO2 EGR-influencing impacts associated with natural resource consumption, renewable energy adoption, financial development, institutional quality improvement, and urbanization. Notably, this indirect analysis would help in understanding whether digitalization policies can be blended with other major macroeconomic policies to collectively account for lower CO2 EGRs across South Asia. Overall, the following research questions are addressed by this study:•Is digitalization necessary for resolving the CO2 emission issue in South Asia?•What role can digitalization play in abating the rate of growth of CO2 emissions in South Asia?•Does digitalization both directly and indirectly affect CO2 EGRs in South Asia?

Literature review

Theoretical literature on the digitalization-CO2 emission nexus

The theoretical explanations of how digitalization affects carbon emissions predominantly pass through the channels of technological innovation and power consumption. For instance, the spillover of clean technologies, particularly via the use of the internet, is often assumed to cut down emissions of greenhouse gases [23,24]. This scenario takes place due to digitalization aiding to improve energy productivity levels whereby the volume of energy consumed declines while energy consumption-related discharges of CO2 gets reduced in tandem [25]. In the same sense, digitalization is expected to curb CO2 emissions by greening industrial production processes [26]. Moreover, considering the issue of green (or clean) energy transition, digitalization is said to further reduce emissions by cleaning the fossil fuel-intensive structure of energy sectors; particularly but expediting the renewable energy adoption procedure as a whole. Furthermore, digitalization-led economic growth is presumed to assist the government in designing effective CO2 emission-controlling policies, as well [10]. Accordingly, such digital technologies/services enable governments to efficiently monitor surges in CO2 discharge propensities so that better environmental governance can be ensured [27]. However, despite these environmental benefits, digitalization may lead to more environmental problems by boosting emissions of CO2; especially, by raising electricity consumption levels [28]. This adverse outcome is most likely to take place due to fossil fuel-based electricity production accounting for large scale atmospheric disposals of CO2. Hence, unless digital technologies are made less energy-intensive, the potential favorable environmental outcomes to be soured from digitalization are unlikely to be realized.

Empirical literature on the digitalization-CO2 emission nexus

Based on the aforementioned theoretical arguments, past empirical studies have probed the digitalization-emissions nexus using diverse indicators of digitalization. Among these, Gyamfi et al. [29] focused on South Asian nations and considered exports and imports of Information and Communications Technology (ICT) as proxies of digitalization. Accordingly, in that study, the results verified that more exports of ICT commodities reduce CO2 emissions in the long run, while imports of such commodities enhance CO2 emission levels within South Asia. Likewise, Rahman and Ferdaous [30] appraised the digitalization-CO2 emissions nexus in the South Asian context using ICT as the proxy for digitalization; however, the results in that study affirmed that ICT diffusion is effective in reducing CO2 emission levels across this region. In another recently conducted study, Andlib and Khan [31] focused on Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, and India, and concluded that digitalization (measured by the number of internet and mobile users) improves environmental quality by reducing yearly CO2 discharges across these major South Asian nations. Likewise, in the context of six South Asian countries, Mehmood and Tariq [32] asserted that becoming socially globalized, particularly by enhancing telephone and internet service access rates lead to the following outcomes (a) CO2 emission levels initially decrease but ultimately increase in Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Nepal; (b) CO2 emission levels initially increase but ultimately decrease in Pakistan and Bhutan. Hence, it is quite apparent that these previous studies in the South Asian context have somewhat irrationally emphasized the reduction in the annual level of CO2 emissions across this region without underscoring the necessity of reducing the yearly growth rate of CO2 emissions.

Among other relevant studies, utilizing panel data regarding 10 highly-polluted Asian nations, Zhao et al. [33] concluded that digitalization (proxied by the share of internet users in the national population) contributes to lower discharges of CO2 in the long run. Accordingly, the authors recommended developing the digital infrastructure so that more internet coverage within the concerned Asian nations can improve energy efficiency levels to ultimately cut down the annual releases of CO2. On the other hand, Usman et al. [22] also considered certain Asian countries as test cases and found mixed evidence of digitalization's impact on annual levels of CO2 emissions. Precisely, the authors claimed that more use of digital services exerts CO2 emission level-inhibiting impacts in the majority of the selected Asian nations while causing environmental damage by boosting CO2 emission levels in the rest of the sampled nations. Besides, regarding some country-specific analyses of the digitalization-CO2 emission nexus from the South Asian perspective, Sahoo et al. [34] explored the environmental impacts of ICT development within the Indian economy. In that study, the authors found that as the Indian population gets more access to the internet, availing digital services becomes easier which, in turn, assists in reducing annual carbon emissions levels in India. In addition, the authors also warned about the CO2 emission-boosting impacts associated with more electricity consumption in India. Likewise, demonstrating the environmental quality-improving effect of digitalization in Pakistan, Iqbal et al. [35] remarked that more use of the internet services and cellular subscriptions negatively influence Pakistan's annual anthropogenic levels of CO2.

Among related empirical studies carried out in the context of developing regions outside South Asia, Asongu et al. [36] appraised the role of digitalization in enabling Sub-Saharan African (SSA) nations to achieve environmental sustainability. Precisely, in light of their empirical findings, the authors remarked that rising rates of internet and mobile penetrations are initially accompanied by higher CO2 emission levels while, later on, these modes of ICT adoption are likely to help reduce the annual carbon discharge levels. Accordingly, the authors affirmed the inverse U-shaped relationship between digitalization and CO2 emission levels for the SSA nations of concern. Similarly, focusing on the Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) region, Ahmed et al. [37] examined the digitalization-CO2 emissions nexus using several ICT-based digitalization proxies (including users of the internet and fixed telephones along with subscribers of broadband and mobile services). Accordingly, the authors prepared a composite ICT index and asserted that making more use of ICT is critically relevant for reducing CO2 emission levels. Meanwhile, utilizing Chinese provincial data, Wang et al. [38] also recorded evidence of internet development helping to curtail annual CO2 emission level in China. Furthermore, identical studies have also been conducted in the context of developed nations which are expected to have advanced digital economies. Among these, Park et al. [39] empirically verified that the digitalization policies followed across Europe are not in line with their environmental safeguarding objectives; precisely, the results affirmed that more internet penetration rates do not account for lower long-run levels of CO2 emission. By contrast, Gyamfi et al. [40] argued that more use of ICT applications assist in reducing CO2 emission levels in the Group of Seven (G7) countries.

Methodology (model, data, estimation procedure)

Theoretical foundations of empirical models

Referring to the theoretical foundations of the Stochastic Impacts by Regression on Population, Affluence, and Technology2 (commonly referred to as STIRPAT) model proposed by Dietz and Rosa [41], this study primarily attempts to assess the digitalization-CO2 emissions nexus in the South Asian context by utilizing the following linear empirical model:(Model 1)CARBONi,t=α+∂1DIGITi,t+∂2NATRESi,t+∂3ENESTRi,t+∂4FINDVi,t+∂5INSTQi,t+∂6URBNi,t+εi,twhere CARBON refers to the annual CO2 EGR which proxies for the environmental impacts that could be associated with digitalization and other macroeconomic variables. Since reducing the level of CO2 discharged each year is a cumbersome task given the contemporary scenario of high fossil fuel reliance across South Asia [21], it is deemed important to make sure that the rate of growth of such emissions is contained; thus, CO2 EGR can be regarded as a more relevant environmental proxy in South Asian context. On the other hand, the core explanatory variable DIGIT refers to digitalization which is proxied by the internet penetration rate3 under the core assumption that internet services are essential in delivering digital services [36]. Notably, in relation to the STIRPAT model, digitalization captures the environmental influence exerted by technological progress since digital technologies require the medium of the internet to reach the end users [37].

Among the control variables considered in Model 1, firstly, NATRES stands for natural resource use which is proxied by the cumulative value of rents derived from various natural resources including natural gas, oil, coal, minerals, and forests. Since natural resources are either extracted and/or refined mostly for producing fossil fuel-based energy, it is naturally hypothesized to be responsible for emitting CO2 into the atmosphere [42]. Besides, it is relevant to consider natural resource use within the STIRPAT model because it promotes economic growth [43]; thus, this variable is expected to indirectly capture the CO2 EGR-influencing impacts of economic affluence. Secondly, the variable ENESTR refers to the structure of the energy sector which provides an account of the depth of greening of the South Asian energy sectors. It is important to control for this variable because a fossil fuel-intensive energy sector is often associated with a highly carbon-intensive national production process [44]. Conversely, the augmentation of clean energy within the energy sector is assumed to green the production processes which, in turn, could effectively tame the annual CO2 EGR [45]. Regarding its relevance within the STIRPAT model, changes in the energy structure are anticipated to capture the effects of affluence (via the channel of energy consumption-led economic growth) and technology (via the channel of renewable energy technology development).

Thirdly, the variable FINDV indicates financial development which provides an account of how advanced the financial sectors are within the South Asian region. Notably, a developed financial sector is responsible for promoting economic growth [46] but its environmental impact can be mixed [[47], [48]]. Thus, financial development is also likely to capture the environmental impacts associated with financial development-led economic affluence within the STIRPAT model. Fourthly, the variable INSTQ portrays the condition of institutions which is specifically evaluated in terms of the efficacies of the South Asian nations in controlling the spread of corruption. Arguably, a good measure adopted to mitigate the bandwidth of institutional corruption is said to help in establishing sound environmental governance [49] which, in turn, is expected to reduce carbon emission propensity rates. Besides, regarding its position within the STIRPAT model, since an improvement in institutional quality, especially within institutions authorized to protect environmental decay, is assumed to foster economic growth [50], this variable can also be expected to capture the environmental impacts of economic affluence. Lastly, the variable URBN stands for urbanization which provides an account of the rate at which the urban populations of the South Asian countries are growing each year. Notably, for capturing the impact of population within the STIRPAT model, it is relevant to check the carbon footprints of urbanization policies because more often than not traditional urbanization policies directly stimulate economic growth at the expense of environmental degradation [51]. Conversely, greener urbanization policies are argued to exert favorable environmental impacts by curbing CO2 emissions [52].

In the above baseline model (i.e., Model 1), the parameter α is the constant/intercept, ∂z(z=1,2,…,6) are the marginal effects exerted by the independent variables on the dependent variable, and ε is the error term. In this regard, it is important to note that Model 1 can be estimated for predicting the direct impacts of digitalization and other control variables (i.e., independent variables) on the annual CO2 EGR (i.e., dependent variable). However, these estimates cannot portray whether digitalization can intervene to indirectly affect the CO2 EGR by influencing the manner in which the control variables influence CO2 EGR. Hence, to identify these possible indirect impacts, the digitalization variable (DIGIT) is interacted with each of the control variables and the interaction terms are separately augmented into the baseline model. For instance, to check whether digitalization can offset CO2 emissions associated with more use of natural resources, the interaction term DIGIT∗NATRES is inserted into the baseline model as follows:(Model 2)CARBONi,t=α+∂1DIGITi,t+∂2NATRESi,t+∂3ENESTRi,t+∂4FINDVi,t+∂5INSTQi,t+∂6URBNi,t+δ1(DIGITi,t∗NATRESi,t)+εi,twhere the value of δ1 (only if found to be statistically significant) would provide a measure of the joint impact of digitalization and natural resource use on CO2 EGRs in the concerned South Asian countries. From here, the marginal impact of natural resources on the growth rate of CO2 emissions, conditional on the level of digitalization, can be calculated using the formula given below:∂CARBONi,t∂NATRESi,t=∂2+δ1DIGITi,t‾where DIGITi,t‾ refers to the mean value of the digitalization variable. Notably, digitalization's net impact on the natural resource use-CO2 EGR nexus is given by the difference in the values of ∂2 and ∂CARBONi,t∂NATRESi,t. Similarly, the digitalization variable is interacted with the other control variables in the subsequent models as follows:(Model 3)CARBONi,t=α+∂1DIGITi,t+∂2NATRESi,t+∂3ENESTRi,t+∂4FINDVi,t+∂5INSTQi,t+∂6URBNi,t+δ2(DIGITi,t∗ENESTRi,t)+εi,t(Model 4)CARBONi,t=α+∂1DIGITi,t+∂2NATRESi,t+∂3ENESTRi,t+∂4FINDVi,t+∂5INSTQi,t+∂6URBNi,t+δ3(DIGITi,t∗FINDVi,t)+εi,t(Model 5)CARBONi,t=α+∂1DIGITi,t+∂2NATRESi,t+∂3ENESTRi,t+∂4FINDVi,t+∂5INSTQi,t+∂6URBNi,t+δ4(DIGITi,t∗INSTQi,t)+εi,t(Model 6)CARBONi,t=α+∂1DIGITi,t+∂2NATRESi,t+∂3ENESTRi,t+∂4FINDVi,t+∂5INSTQi,t+∂6URBNi,t+δ5(DIGITi,t∗URBNi,t)+εi,t

Estimation procedure

Among the several stages of investigation (as illustrated in Fig. 3), firstly, the problem of Cross-sectional Dependence (CD) is diagnosed. Although the well-established CD-testing methods proposed by Pesaran [54,55] are employed in the extant literature, these methods have recently been questioned for over-rejection of the null hypothesis of no/weak CD in panel data sets with latent/unobserved factors [56]. Hence, to address this limitation, the bias-corrected CD-testing technique introduced by Pesaran and Xie [57] is employed in this study. This latest method utilizes a randomized test statistic that predicts the existence of CD in the residuals while reducing the possibility of over-rejecting the null hypothesis [57]. Moreover, the technique of Pesaran and Xie [57] is robust to the presence of both Gaussian and non-Gaussian errors whereby this CD test is suitable for small sample-sized panel data sets. Secondly, the estimation procedure involves the diagnosis of the other important panel data problem of Heterogeneous Slope Coefficients (HSC). Conventional HSC-testing methods are said to generate biased outcomes due to ignoring the presence of CD in the data. Thus, in this study, the CD-adjusted test proposed by Bersvendsen and Ditzen [58] is utilized. Notably, this new HSC-testing method extends the traditional test of Pesaran and Yamagata [59] by including cross-sectional averages of variables within the model as additional explanatory variables for opposing the problem of CD; thus, the predicted test statistics are considered robust against CD existing in the error term and also in the variables [58].Fig. 3Stages of the empirical evaluation procedure.Fig. 3

Stages of the empirical evaluation procedure.

Thirdly, for checking stationary processes of the concerned variables, the Panel Unit Root (PUR) analysis is conducted. Traditional PUR-testing methods such as those introduced by Pesaran [60] are robust against the problems of CD and HSC but they generate biased outcomes in the context of panel datasets exhibiting the issue of a deterministic trend; consequently, these methods are not applicable for trending panel data. Moreover, these methods are not robust in the presence of heteroskedasticity, as well. Hence, to account for such limitations, the newly developed PUR test proposed by Herwartz et al. [61] is performed in this study. Notably, as affirmed by Monte Carlo simulations, this method is ideally suited to handling small sample-sized panel datasets [61]. Fourthly, for checking the long-run relationships among the concerned variables, the panel cointegration analysis is conducted. In this regard, the cointegration-testing method proposed by Westerlund [62] is used, particularly due to its superiority over the traditional methods in terms of (a) handling the issues of CD and HSC, (b) neutralizing the effect of heteroskedasticity, and (c) countering serial correlation issues. Notably, this technique predicts statistics from four tests considering no cointegration among the concerned variables as the null hypothesis; thus, rejection of the null affirms the long-run associations among the variables and provides permission to conduct the long-run panel regression analysis.

Lastly, to ascertain how the yearly per capita CO2 EGRs are determined in the long run, the panel regression analysis is conducted. In this regard, the dynamic Common Correlated Effects (CCE) regression technique proposed by Chudik and Pesaran [63] is employed. Notably, the previously developed CCE regression method of Pesaran [64] has provided solutions to the issues of CD and HSC; however, this technique falls short on accounts of handling (a) dynamic panel data models having a lagged dependent variable in the list of regressors, (b) weakly exogenous regressors, and (c) endogenous covariates. Hence, extending the traditional CCE method, Chudik and Pesaran [63] introduced the dynamic CCE estimator which is robust in the context of the above limitations of the CCE estimator of Pesaran [64]. Besides, the dynamic CCE estimator is also deemed more efficient because it (i) estimates linear models within a dynamic panel setup by including lagged level of the outcome variable as a regressor, (ii) includes sufficient number of lags and cross-sectional averages within the model to overcome bias generated by the presence of unobserved common factors in the model, and (iii) incorporates an Instrumental Variable (IV) approach to deal with the issue of endogeneity [65]. Furthermore, this method is chosen due to its satisfactory performance with small sample-based panel data sets [66]. Precisely, small sample bias is corrected with the help of re-cursive de-meaning and jackknife procedures [67].

Secondly, the empirical findings show that environmental concerns arising from CO2 emissions, in particular, are linked with the growth of the natural resource-based sectors in South Asia. Notably, the positive sign of the estimated coefficient corresponding to the variable NATRES indicates that as the share of total natural resource rents in the respective GDP of the selected South Asian countries increases by 1%, their annual per capita CO2 EGR is likely to go up by 0.21%, ceteris paribus. This particular finding supports the idea that natural resource extraction, refining, and usage contribute to higher emissions of CO2. This is mainly because natural resources are highly hydrocarbon-intensive; thus, it is quite understandable that consuming these resources would exert pressure on the annual growth rate at which CO2 is deposited into the atmosphere. Similarly, preceding studies have also stressed the environmental quality-dampening impacts related to the use of natural resources in South Asia [71] and beyond [72]. Moreover, it has been duly admitted in the extant literature that improper management of natural resource-based sectors causes environmental damage by surging the yearly CO2 figures of South Asian countries [73]. Therefore, the previous and current study's findings alarmingly imply that natural resource dependence not only boosts annual CO2 emission levels but raises the EGRs, as well.

Thirdly, the results demonstrate that greening the energy sector's structure does not impose any significant impact on the environment in South Asia. Precisely, this statement is affirmed by the statistically insignificant coefficient corresponding to the variable ENESTR. Although in many earlier studies it has been duly pointed out that renewable energy transition is likely to abate CO2 emission levels [[45], [74]], this study's contradictory finding could be due to (i) vast reliance of South Asian nations on fossil fuels for electricity generation purposes, (ii) sluggish performance in terms of undergoing renewable energy transition, and (iii) declining share of renewable energy in the energy sectors of almost all South Asian countries [11]. Notably, these disappointing facts regarding South Asian energy sectors indicate that the energy planners within this region are having a tough time in designing credible energy structure-greening policies which, in turn, is hampering the CO2 emission-abatement objectives of the concerned governments. Moreover, this study's finding, as opposed to the existing findings from the literature, implies that although a nominal rise in renewable energy's share within the energy consumption portfolios of the South Asian countries may somewhat reduce their CO2 emission levels; such a nominal rise is not sufficient to tame the rate at which their annual emission figures would rise in future.

Fourthly, financial development across South Asia is witnessed to inflict harmful environmental impacts. To be precise, the positive sign of the estimated coefficient corresponding to the variable FINDV indicates that as domestic private borrowing's share of GDP across the concerned South Asian countries increases by 1%, the annual per capita CO2 EGR is likely to go up by 0.15%, ceteris paribus. This finding is indicative of the issue that weak financial development policies followed in this region are likely to have allowed pollution-intensive firms to grow despite causing environmental damage by boosting CO2 EGRs. Moreover, this finding also points out that the financial sectors of the concerned South Asian nations are not supporting the development of green finance within this region. Similarly, Wang et al. [38] also documented empirical evidence regarding financial development causing CO2 emission levels to rise in South Asia. Contrastingly, in the context of certain emerging nations and developed countries, Acheampong et al. [75] argued that developing financial markets can be effective in mitigating their yearly carbon emissions figures in the long run.

Fifthly, regarding the issue of institutional governance of environmental quality, the results show that improving the quality of institutions, that are mostly designated with the task of protecting the environment, can help the concerned South Asian nations achieve their emission abatement objectives. Notably, the negative sign of the estimated coefficient corresponding to the variable INSTQ indicates that if the level of corruption control improves by 1%, it is likely that the annual CO2 EGRs in the concerned South Asian countries would go down by 0.37%, ceteris paribus. This is quite a justified finding because corrupt officials often let environmental misconduct go unprosecuted; thus, in the presence of a modest amount of corruption, protecting environmental traits becomes challenging. Contrarily, if the bandwidth of corruption within organizations can be contained, the possibility of using bribes to get away by generating hefty volumes of CO2 emissions can be minimized. Therefore, more proactive controlling of the spread of corruption can be perceived as an anti-rent-seeking mechanism that would help to plunge the growth of carbon-intensive South Asian industries which, eventually, may be effective in reducing CO2 EGRs across this region. Similarly, Asif et al. [76] remarked that corruption makes environmental policies dysfunctional and causes the carbon footprints of South Asian countries to rise. On the other hand, Wang et al. [77] showed that corruption-controlling measures help in improving environmental quality by dampening the CO2 emission-surging impacts associated with economic growth. Collectively, this current study's findings and those documented in the literature, endorse that not only would less-corrupt institutions pin down the level of CO2 emitted annually but also slow down the growth by which emission figures across South Asia would grow annually in the future.

Lastly, the results affirm that urban development policies followed across South Asia are against the objective of establishing a sound environment. Notably, the positive sign of the estimated coefficient corresponding to the variable URBN indicates that for every 1% increment in urban population size, the annual CO2 growth rate in the selected South Asian countries is likely to rise by 1.47%, ceteris paribus. This finding corroborates the idea of urbanization expanding the size of carbon sinks that exist underneath urban buildings and landfills [78]. Besides, urbanization-induced deforestation is also alleged to drive up carbon emission figures [79]. Similarly, in the context of selected South Asian countries, Voumik et al. [80] remarked that urbanization is responsible for boosting their anthropogenic carbon discharge levels. On the other hand, Hashmi et al. [81] argued that although urbanization disrupts environmental well-being by initially boosting CO2 emission levels in Asia, it later on accounts for better environmental well-being by reducing emission levels; accordingly, the authors claimed the urbanization-carbon emission nexus to portray an inverted U-shaped association. Therefore, in relation to mixed results available regarding urbanization and the level of CO2 emissions, this study points out that although in some cases urbanization can be associated with lower CO2 emission levels across South Asian countries, it is quite likely that urbanization would be responsible for exerting unfavorable environmental consequences by boosting their EGRs in the future. Thus, the adoption of green urbanization policies has immense significance. This finding can be explained by the understanding that the use of innovative digital technologies, especially those based on the Internet of Things (IoT), can enhance the productivity of natural resource-based industries [82]; consequently, the level of unclean natural resource consumption can be reduced which, in turn, could effectively plunge the carbon footprints of such industries.

Secondly, regarding Model 3, it is evident that although greening of the energy structure is not effective enough in reducing emission growth figures, more digitalization within the selected South Asian nations alongside greening of their energy sectors' structures may collectively assist in reducing their annual CO2 EGRs. In this regard, it can be claimed that digitalization mediates the nexus between the greening of energy sector's structure and the annual CO2 EGRs in the context of the concerned South Asian countries. This finding corroborates the idea that digitalization is deemed necessary for expediting the process of renewable energy transition [83]; consequently, the use of digital technologies relevant to boosting the production of renewable electricity may cut down power sector-related emissions of CO2 across South Asia. Similarly, the role of green innovation (which is an indicator of digitalization) in stimulating renewable technology investment across South Asia has been duly acknowledged in the literature [84].

Thirdly, as per the estimates generated in the context of Model 4, it is observed that digitalization and financial development collectively help in reducing the CO2 EGRs in the selected South Asian nations. Besides, as in the case of Model 4, the net marginal effect of financial development on the CO2 EGR5 is found to be −0.239. Therefore, it is evident that when the joint impact of financial development and digitalization is controlled, further development of the financial sector can be associated with lower growth rates of CO2 emissions. Accordingly, this finding indicates that by moderating the nexus between financial development and CO2 EGRs, digitalization helps in making the financial sector development process across South Asia greener which, in turn, is expected to cut down the yearly rate of surge in carbon emission levels. To explain this mechanism, it is pertinent to understand that digital financial technologies play an integral part in cleaning the environment by reducing discharges of CO2. Likewise, it has been argued that digitalizing the financial inclusion process within South Asia is pertinent for curbing CO2 emissions and ensuring environmental sustainability [85].

Fourthly, in the context of Model 5, once again empirical validation is found regarding the importance of digitalization in making institutions more effective in controlling emissions. Conclusion

Within the South Asian territory, economic output processes are largely deemed to be unclean, especially due to excessive reliance on natural resource endowments such as fossil fuels. Hence, reducing CO2 emissions has become an important agenda for the South Asian governments. However, as these nations are still not equipped enough to undergo the renewable energy transition (i.e., incapable of adopting clean energy in full swing), it is unlikely that their annual CO2 emission levels would reduce anytime soon. Under such circumstances, mitigating the rate at with their CO2 emission levels rise annually is of utmost importance in order to make the environment across South Asia more sustainable. Therefore, in the current era of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, the role of digitalization in plunging South Asian annual CO2 EGRs is empirically assessed in this study. In a nutshell, using internet penetration rate as a proxy of digital technology adoption, the results confirmed that policies aimed at digitalizing the concerned economies not only exert CO2 EGR-reducing impacts through a direct channel, but also indirectly reduce CO2 EGRs by making natural resources sectors more productive, expediting the energy sector greening processes, greening the financial sectors, making corruption controlling measures more effective, and limiting urbanization-induced environmental adversities.

Policy recommendations

Considering the direct and indirect importances of digitalization in mitigating CO2 EGRs across South Asia, six critically important policies can be implemented. First, to expedite the processes leading to the digitalization of the South Asian economies, it is pertinent for the concerned governments to make internet services more accessible for the respective population. In this regard, it is essential to reduce the cost of using internet services so that internet penetration rates can be improved at a faster pace. In addition, to ensure more availability of internet services, investing in infrastructure is equally important, especially for widening the bandwidths of internet service providers. Accordingly, internet coverage rates across South Asia can be enhanced substantially which, in turn, can be expected to foster their CO2 EGR reduction objectives. Moreover, greater emphasis should be provided to importing green ICT so that the rate of digitalization across South Asia can be boosted further.

Second, South Asian countries need to make sure that digital technologies are adopted within the natural resource-based industries. This is relevant from the point of view of improving natural resource productivity while keeping the carbon footprints of such industries low. Third, South Asian countries should develop digital technologies relevant to generating electricity using renewable resources. This would help these nations in greening their respective energy sectors faster since technology is often deemed as a barrier that hampers renewable energy penetration into the energy systems of the South Asian countries. Fourth, it is of utmost importance for these countries to introduce green funds so that their financial sectors extend more credit to investors interested in injecting funds into low-carbon projects. In this regard, green bonds can play a major role to boost the level of financial investment in green projects. Fifth, the South Asian government should digitalize the institutions authorized for monitoring environmental misconducts. Accordingly, these institutions can be made less corrupt and more transparent cum accountable for their actions. As a result, carbon-intensive industries would face difficulty in scaling their output levels. Lastly, when formulating future urban development policies, it is important that such policies are digitalized, particularly for limiting urban energy demand. Accordingly, with more application of digital technologies, urban energy consumption-driven CO2 emissions across South Asia can be contained by a large extent.

Limitations and future research scope

A primary limitation of this current study is that it does not consider all South Asian countries due to data unavailability. As a result, generalizing the conclusions for this entire region may not be totally relevant. Besides, since this study specifically emphasizes the South Asian region, it is unclear how digitalization would affect CO2 EGRs in countries outside this region. Furthermore, once again due to data unavailability, the internet penetration rate is used as the only proxy for digitalization; thus, uncertainty remains in terms of whether other indicators of digitalization would exert similar CO2 EGR-reducing impacts. Lastly, due to using a linear model, this study fails to ascertain the possible non-linearity that may exist regarding the digitalization-CO2 EGR nexus. Hence, it is recommended that future studies take note of these limitations and design their empirical analyses accordingly.
