Aortic Valve Endocarditis Caused by Streptococcus acidominimus Following Community‐Acquired Pneumonia in a Patient With Diabetes Mellitus: A Case Report
Authors: Menghak Heng, Chhayhout Chheang, Sophou Chit, Kimhun Ton, Saly Saint, Sopha Uy, Ing Cheng, Thy Try, Sarin Ean, Chheang Khor, Borin Hem, Vibopha Srey, Sopheak Phy, Heng Leang, Bunpaul Chhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfective endocarditis (IE) due to Streptococcus acidominimus is exceedingly rare and poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Traditionally regarded as an animal commensal, this organism is increasingly recognised as a cause of invasive human infections, especially in immunocompromised patients. We report a 40‐year‐old Cambodian man with diabetes mellitus who presented with persistent fever initially managed as community‐acquired pneumonia. Blood cultures later grew S. acidominimus , and he was discharged on oral antibiotics after transient improvement. He was readmitted with recurrent fever, and transesophageal echocardiography revealed aortic valve vegetations with severe regurgitation. The patient received targeted intravenous antibiotics and underwent successful aortic valve replacement, achieving full recovery after six weeks of therapy. This case underscores the need to consider rare pathogens in persistent bacteraemia, repeat cardiac imaging when suspicion remains high despite inconclusive findings, and adopt a multidisciplinary approach to optimise outcomes, particularly in diabetic patients and resource‐limited settings.

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a serious infection of the endocardial surface of the heart, particularly the cardiac valves [1]. It is a life‐threatening condition characterised by diverse microbial aetiologies and high morbidity and mortality, and it is considered the third most common life‐threatening infectious disease [2]. While Streptococcus viridans group and Staphylococcus aureus are the predominant causative organisms, rare pathogens such as Streptococcus acidominimus have occasionally been reported [3]. S. acidominimus is a Gram‐positive coccus belonging to the viridans group streptococci and is typically associated with bovine sources [2].

Human infection caused by S. acidominimus is exceptionally rare, particularly in the form of IE. However, it has been reported in isolated cases involving pneumonia, otitis media, brain abscess, multiloculated empyema, meningitis, pleural effusion, peritonitis, and sepsis [2]. To date, only several cases of IE attributed to S. acidominimus have been described in the literature [2].

Early and accurate diagnosis, followed by appropriate management, is essential to reduce mortality and improve clinical outcomes. We present a case that highlights the clinical significance of this uncommon pathogen in a patient with diabetes mellitus who was initially presumed to have community‐acquired pneumonia (CAP).

Case Report

A 40‐year‐old male factory worker with diabetes mellitus treated with Metformin and Glipalamide presented to Calmette Hospital, Phnom Penh, Cambodia with one week of high‐grade fever (39°C) and chills, unresponsive to an unknown 3‐day outpatient antibiotic course. Physical examination showed the presence of crackles at the left lower lung on auscultation. Chest Xray showed an alveolar opacity in the left lower lung parenchyma field (Figure 1) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was normal. Blood and urine cultures were drawn, and empirical therapy with Piperacillin/Tazobactam (4.5 g IV q8h) and Azithromycin (500 mg PO daily) was initiated.

After 72 h, his symptoms resolved and inflammatory markers declined: WBC from 26.2 to 13.4 × 109/L, C‐reactive protein (CRP) from 97.2 to 37.2 mg/L, procalcitonin (PCT) from 0.329 to 0.107 ng/mL. Blood cultures grew Streptococcus acidominimus , sensitive to Ampicillin, Ceftriaxone, Vancomycin and Ofloxacin but resistant to Erythromycin and Clindamycin (Figure 2). Urine culture was negative. Electrocardiogram showed a sinus tachycardia (Figure 3). The patient was discharged after completing a 7‐day course of antibiotics.

Discussion

IE affects approximately 15,000 patients each year and continues to carry a mortality rate approaching 40%, underscoring its status as a major health concern [4]. Despite advances in diagnostic imaging and antimicrobial therapy, the incidence of IE has not declined over the past 30 years. Current understanding of its pathogenesis highlights the interplay between endothelial injury, platelet aggregation and microbial adherence, allowing circulating bacteria to colonise native or damaged valvular tissue and form vegetations [1, 4].

Streptococcus acidominimus is rarely reported in human infections and may be overlooked in clinical practice. The most commonly isolated pathogens in IE are coagulase‐negative staphylococci (24.5%), Staphylococcus aureus (21%), enterococci (21%) and oral streptococci [5]. Emerging evidence suggests that this organism can cause severe invasive infections, including bacteraemia and endocarditis, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. Although uncommon, available case reports suggest that S. acidominimus possesses pathogenic potential similar to other viridans streptococci, with a tendency for endovascular involvement once bloodstream infection occurs.

The diagnosis of IE relies on the Modified Duke criteria, which integrate microbiological evidence, echocardiographic findings and clinical features [5]. A definitive diagnosis is established when typical microorganisms are isolated from blood cultures or valvular tissue in conjunction with characteristic echocardiographic findings or when combinations of major and minor criteria are fulfilled [1, 6]. In the present case, the initial transthoracic echocardiography was normal, which may have contributed to diagnostic delay. This underscores the limitation of early imaging and the importance of repeat evaluation when clinical suspicion persists.

Importantly, this case highlights a critical management issue. The detection of S. acidominimus in blood cultures during the first hospitalisation indicated true bacteraemia rather than localised infection. Although the patient showed early clinical improvement, the 7‐day course of antibiotics was insufficient for the bloodstream infection caused by an unusual streptococcal species. Current principles of bacteraemia management emphasise the need for repeat blood cultures to document microbiological clearance, particularly when rare or potentially endocarditis‐associated organisms are isolated [2]. In this context, premature discontinuation of antimicrobial therapy and early discharge likely allowed persistent bacteraemia to evolve into overt infective endocarditis, as evidenced by recurrent fever and subsequent echocardiographic findings.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing played a key role in guiding therapy. In this case, S. acidominimus demonstrated resistance to erythromycin and clindamycin but remained susceptible to β‐lactam antibiotics and vancomycin, consistent with prior reports. For streptococcal infective endocarditis, prolonged intravenous antimicrobial therapy for 4–6 weeks is recommended to achieve bacteriological cure [2, 3]. The relapse observed in this patient reinforces the principle that clinical improvement alone should not guide treatment duration in bacteraemia or suspected endocarditis.

Repeat echocardiographic evaluation, particularly TEE, played a critical role in confirming the diagnosis [3, 5]. Surgical intervention was necessary due to significant valvular destruction and severe regurgitation [4]. The favourable outcome in this case underscores the importance of multidisciplinary management involving internists, pulmonologists, cardiologists, infectious disease specialists and cardiothoracic surgeons.

This case illustrates the importance of recognising rare pathogens as potential causes of infective endocarditis, particularly in patients with underlying risk factors such as diabetes mellitus. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for IE in patients with persistent bacteraemia, even when initial imaging studies are negative. Early recognition and timely intervention are essential to reduce morbidity and mortality [6].

In conclusion, this case highlights Streptococcus acidominimus as a rare but potentially life‐threatening cause of infective endocarditis, particularly in immunocompromised patients, including those with diabetes mellitus. In cases of S. acidominimus bacteraemia, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for endocarditis and actively rule it out using cardiac imaging such as transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and/or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). When IE is confirmed, prompt surgical intervention in combination with appropriate antimicrobial therapy may be required to improve clinical outcomes. Surgical intervention remains a cornerstone of treatment when guided by well‐defined criteria for emergency, urgent or early intervention alongside antimicrobial treatment.
