Using an electronic frailty index and patient reported outcomes to predict sarcopenia risk
Authors: Cody A. Altherr, Xiao Luo, Haoran Ding, Ziyue Liu, Stuart J. Warden, Ranjani N. Moorthi, Erik A. Imel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The study goal was to identify sarcopenia using information from electronic health records (EHR) or prospectively collected patient reported outcomes tools among male and female adults participating in our Musculoskeletal Functioning, Imaging, and Testing (FIT Core) protocol. Participants (n = 1304) were grouped according to number of sarcopenia test thresholds (adapted from the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People) into those meeting ≥ 1 (labeled Sarcopenia-1), or ≥ 2 (Sarcopenia-2) test thresholds for sarcopenia or no sarcopenia for meeting none. The electronic frailty index (eFI) was calculated from participant EHR using a published cumulative health deficits model. The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System’s (PROMIS) Physical Function (PF) and Mobility domains were assessed prospectively. A logistic regression model based on their EHR data was applied to describe each individual’s proportionate risk for having Sarcopenia-1 and Sarcopenia-2. eFI correlated positively with Sarcopenia-1 (r = 0.77, p < 0.01) and Sarcopenia-2 (r = 0.66, p < 0.01). After multivariable adjustment, Sarcopenia-1 was best predicted by eFI (AUC 0.76, p = 0.029) and PROMIS Mobility T-score (AUC 0.76, p < 0.01). Sarcopenia-2 was best predicted by eFI (AUC 0.91, p = 0.01) and the PROMIS PF T-score (AUC 0.91, p = 0.03). The eFI and PROMIS PF and Mobility scores may be useful to predict sarcopenia risk when direct muscle quantifying measurements are unavailable.

Introduction

Frailty and sarcopenia increase the risk of health-related adverse events such as falls, hospitalization, disability, loss of independence, and death1,2. There is overlap between the conditions, yet they do differ3. Sarcopenia is a specific skeletal muscle disorder involving loss of muscle strength, mass and function4. It is often identified by low muscle strength (frequently assessed by handgrip strength) combined with low muscle mass (typically assessed as appendicular lean mass using imaging techniques)4. Frailty, on the other hand, is commonly described as age-related declines in physiological reserves, producing an increased vulnerability to acute stressors5. Compared to sarcopenia, frailty is a broader concept of function that is influenced by physical, behavioral, social, and other variables6. Both sarcopenia and frailty are modifiable risk factors7 and, thus, early detection is crucial for intervention.

However, clinical notes and diagnostic coding within the electronic health record (EHR) often lack direct indicators of these conditions, with text terms or International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes only being applied once the diagnosis is made8,9. Additionally, direct measurements related to sarcopenia are often not done clinically and are not readily accessible in EHR. Together these issues may hamper early detection of sarcopenia in the EHR.

Methods to detect sarcopenia and frailty directly from the EHR or the use of patient reported outcomes tools could facilitate more feasible and earlier sarcopenia detection in clinical practice. Our previous work used machine learning of EHR structured data to create models to predict who in a research protocol measuring musculoskeletal outcomes would meet sarcopenia criteria10. However, the resulting models required input of many variables which may not be easily implementable within the EHR broadly. Other authors developed an electronic frailty index (eFI) that uses EHR data to quantify frailty based on cumulative health-based deficits including signs and symptoms, chronic conditions, and social vulnerability11, but the eFI has yet to be validated as a predictor of sarcopenia. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is another widely used scoring system to assess overall disease burden using weighted scores from 17 comorbidities, and correlates with the risk of 10-year mortality12,13. Given the usefulness of this tool for identifying comorbidity confounders14, it is clinically relevant to understand its relationship to sarcopenia and frailty status. The physical function (PF) and Mobility domains of the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) are intended for use across disease groups and conditions8,9, and have been incorporated into clinical orthopedic care15, and may also be useful to identify the patient’s perceived limitations which could relate to sarcopenia.

The purpose of this study was to examine the relationships between the eFI, CCI and PROMIS scores with the presence of measured sarcopenia. Unravelling the associations amongst related measures to sarcopenia may enhance the development and implementation of efficient screening tools for clinical practice.

Methods

Study cohort

This retrospective cross-sectional study utilized the EHR of patients recruited into our Musculoskeletal Functional, Imaging, and Testing (FIT Core) protocol which performs comprehensive assessment of musculoskeletal mass and function. The FIT Core serves to provide: (1) standardized performance of physical function tests and patient reported outcomes related to physical function, (2) imaging outcomes for body composition and bone health, and (3) the collection and banking of biological samples. Participants were recruited to the FIT Core by self-referral from the local community or by investigators for specific research studies in various chronic conditions, measuring outcomes related to musculoskeletal health. Thus, the FIT Core participants include those having a broad range of age and of health and disease status. All participants involved in this analysis had EHR data accessible in a multi-health system EHR database. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the musculoskeletal testing research protocol and access to and analysis of EHR data was approved by the Indiana University Institutional Review Board. All participants who enrolled in this study signed informed consent for participation of all study procedures and access to EHR data.

Data from EHR and the FIT core

Data collection methods have previously been published10. Data were extracted from the EHR between January 2016 and August 2020, with the intent of providing 2 years of EHR data around each patient’s study visit.

The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) recommended sequential test thresholds for sarcopenia tests for hand grip strength (< 27 kg for men and < 16 kg for women) for muscle strength16,17, appendicular lean mass adjusted for height in meters2 (ALM/m2 < 7 for men and < 5.5 for women) as a surrogate measurement for muscle mass, and repeat chair stand test (> 15 s for 5 rises)16 and the Short Physical Performance Battery (≤ 8 point score)18 for physical performance assessments as assessment of severity. Participants in this study were classified using an adapted model based on the tests from the EWGSOP2 as no sarcopenia if they met none of these thresholds, Sarcopenia-1 if they met 1 or more sarcopenia test thresholds, and as Sarcopenia-2 if meeting ≥ 2 of these thresholds, irrespective of order. Thus, the Sarcopenia-2 group was a subgroup of Sarcopenia-1. Additionally, the probability of having Sarcopenia-1 and Sarcopenia-2 for each individual was calculated using a previously published EHR based machine learning model trained on this cohort, using logistic regression to identify an estimate of their risk10. For example, from the prior EHR regression model, an individual might have a calculated proportionate risk of 0.67 for Sarcopenia-2, indicating that person’s likelihood of meeting ≥ 2 of the sarcopenia test thresholds within that model was 0.67. This allowed for a non-binary estimation of each person’s individual risk for the binary outcome. A sub-analysis was performed by grouping participants per the EWGSOP2 criteria for normal, probable, confirmed, and severe sarcopenia. Charlson comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated as commonly used to quantify a patient’s overall disease burden for characterization12.

Patient-reported outcomes

The PROMIS questionnaire is a National Institutes of Health-developed validated framework for capturing various patient-reported outcomes (PRO). For this study, we used the PF and Mobility domains of the PROMIS Computer Adaptive Testing (version 1.2) to provide a self-reported indication of functional health. PROMIS scores are standardized and expressed as T-scores with a population mean of 50 and standard deviation of 108. Based on the reference of 0.5 S.D., minimal important differences (MID) of 5 points in PROMIS scores are considered clinically significant19,20.

Frailty

The electronic frailty index (eFI) was adapted from previous reports11. The eFI quantifies the proportion of age-related cumulative deficits present for an individual determined by the Deficit Accumulation Model of Frailty (Rockwood Model)21 and integrates 54 total deficits derived from diagnosis codes, vital signs, laboratory measures, smoking history, medications, and functional assessments from EHR. Our available EHR did not include access to structured data from the standardized Medicare Annual Wellness Visit and the population extended beyond the Medicare population. Thus, this portion of the eFI was excluded from calculation. Frailty status was categorized as either non-frail (eFI ≤ 0.10), pre-frail (0.10 < eFI ≤ 0.21), or frail (eFI > 0.21)22 based on the proportion of deficits present divided by the total number of criteria evaluable (score range 0–1)11.

Muscle strength

The musculoskeletal testing protocol assessed dominant hand grip strength (Jamar Plus + digital hand dynamometer; Sammons Preston, Bolingbrook, IL), the number of chair stands completed in 30 s, and the time taken to complete 5 chair stands16. In addition to raw values, grip strength and repeat chair stand outcomes were converted to age- and sex-specific Z-scores relative to reference data16.

Anthropometric

Participant height (to nearest 0.1 cm) and mass (to nearest 0.1 kg) were measured without shoes using a calibrated stadiometer (Seca 264; Seca GmbH & Co., Hamburg, Germany) and scale (MS140-300; Brecknell, Fairmont, MN). Body mass index (BMI; kg/m2) was calculated as body mass relative to height squared. ALM/m2, fat mass, and percent body fat were assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) using either an Hologic Discovery-W (Hologic, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) or Norland Elite (Norland Elite; Norland at Swissray, Fort Atkinson, WI) densitometer. The machines were cross calibrated and regression formulae used to adjust values obtained using the Hologic scanner to those obtained using the Norland scanner.

Physical performance

The remaining physical performance outcomes (i.e., six-minute walk test, usual gait speed, fast gait speed, and average relative peak power) were converted to Z-scores based on data from > 2500 females and > 1000 males. Time to walk 4-meters from a stationary start at normal speed (usual gait speed) and as quickly as possible without running (fast gait speed) were measured with a stopwatch and converted to speed (meters/second)23. Distance walked in 6-minutes was measured according to the American Thoracic Society24 on a 20-m long indoor course. Average relative peak power was calculated using a force platform (Leonardo Mechanograph GRFP®, Novotec Medical GmbH, Pforzheim, Germany) during the chair stand assessment.

Statistical considerations

Continuous variables were summarized by means (standard deviations, SDs) or medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs) and compared using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test depending on their normality. Normality was checked by QQ-plot and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were summarized by counts (percentages) and compared using Pearson chi-square test and Fisher exact test as appropriate. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated because of the existence of nonlinear but monotonic associations and deviations from normality.

Group differences (EWGSOP2) among eFI scores were compared using independent sample T-test. The associations of the measures of interest (eFI, PROMIS PF and mobility T-scores, and CCI) with Sarcopenia-1 and Sarcopenia-2 were evaluated using logistic regression models. Three types of models were examined as (1) unadjusted associations, (2) adjusted for age and gender, and (3) adjusted for age and gender plus the other three measures. Odds ratios (ORs), their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated. The associations of eFI, PROMIS PF and Mobility T-scores, and CCI score with ALM/kg2 were evaluated using multivariable linear regression models. The same three types of models were adopted to evaluate unadjusted and adjusted associations. Regression parameters, their p-values, and the coefficients of determination (R2) were calculated. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and SPSS Version 29.0.2.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

From the musculoskeletal testing protocol (FIT Core) study, 1304 participants were included in this analysis as having access to EHR data. Of these participants, 1055 met no criteria for sarcopenia (no sarcopenia), while 249 met at least 1 of the sarcopenia test thresholds (Sarcopenia-1). Seventy-six persons out of the Sarcopenia-1 group met ≥ 2 of the sarcopenia test thresholds (Sarcopenia-2). Significant between group differences were observed between eFI, PROMIS T-scores age, race, and sex. Specifically, the sarcopenia groups were older than those with no sarcopenia. eFI, PROMIS T-score, and CCI

Compared to other reports, our cohort displayed greater prevalence of sarcopenia and frailty25. Specifically, 25% of our cohort met at least 1 criterion for sarcopenia and out of this 25%, 60% could be also classified as frail based on eFI. Among the 294 (22.5%) that were age 65 years and above, 42.8% were frail and 30.2% were sarcopenic. The presence of frailty in our non-sarcopenic group was 19.6% based on the eFI, which indicates that a small proportion of our participants met an EHR based frailty indicator despite not meeting any sarcopenia criteria on direct testing.

Between group differences concerning eFI, PROMIS PF and Mobility T-scores, and CCI are listed in Table 1. Sarcopenia-1 and Sarcopenia-2 groups had progressively higher eFI compared to no sarcopenia. There was a greater prevalence of frail individuals in Sarcopenia-1 (53.4%) and Sarcopenia-2 (81.6%) categorized by the eFI. T-scores were used to compare the PROMIS questionnaire and demonstrated lower (worse) values in both sarcopenia groups compared to those with no sarcopenia. Specifically, the PF domain median T -score difference for Sarcopenia-1 vs. no sarcopenia was 7.2 (p < 0.01), while Sarcopenia-2 vs. no sarcopenia displayed a greater median T -score difference of 14.2 (p < 0.01). The Mobility domain results were similar with a median T -score difference for Sarcopenia-1 vs. no sarcopenia of 8.7 (p < 0.01) and median T -score difference for Sarcopenia-2 vs. no sarcopenia of 13.9 (p < 0.01). The median values between groups differed by greater than the minimally important difference of 5 for these PROMIS domains. In addition, Sarcopenia-2 had the greatest CCI (median 3 points; 77% estimated 10-year survival), which is consistent with increasing burden of disease among those meeting more sarcopenia criteria.

In a separate analysis we grouped participants as normal (n = 1173), probable sarcopenia (n = 108), confirmed sarcopenia (n = 16), and severe sarcopenia (n = 7) according to the EWGSOP2 criteria4 to compare eFI results. Similar to our adapted model, eFI progressively increased with sarcopenia severity. The eFI results by group were: normal (nonsarcopenic) 0.16 ± 0.10, probable sarcopenia 0.24 ± 0.14, confirmed sarcopenia 0.30 ± 0.16, and severe sarcopenia 0.32 ± 0.17. Those that were nonsarcopenic had lower eFI than those with probable sarcopenia, and also lower scores than those in the confirmed and severe sarcopenia groups (each (p < 0.01). However, there were not significant eFI differences between the probable and confirmed sarcopenia groups (p = 0.13) and between confirmed and severe sarcopenia (p = 0.88).

Spearman correlation revealed an inverse relationship between PROMIS T-scores and eFI in both PF (r = − 0.50; p < 0.01) and Mobility (r = − 0.52; p < 0.01;) domains, where lower PROMIS T-scores corresponded to higher eFI (Fig. 1).

Lower reported PROMIS T-scores were also associated with the individual having higher probability of having sarcopenia based on EHR data (Fig. 2) as calculated from a prior machine learning generated logistic regression model10. A strong relationship was seen between eFI and both Sarcopenia-1 risk (r = 0.77; p < 0.01) and Sarcopenia-2 risk (r = 0.66; p < 0.01). Moderate relationships were seen in all other comparisons amongst eFI, PROMIS T-scores, CCI and Sarcopenia risk.

In multivariable models overall, AUC was higher for models predicting Sarcopenia-2 compared to models for predicting Sarcopenia-1 (Table 2). The eFI and PF domain PROMIS T-score remained the strongest predictors of Sarcopenia-2 after adjusting concurrently for eFI, PROMIS Mobility T-scores, and CCI. Likewise, eFI (AUC = 0.76, p = 0.02) and PROMIS Mobility T-scores (AUC = 0.76, p < 0.01) were the strongest independent predictors of Sarcopenia-1 even after multivariable adjustments. While the CCI predicted sarcopenia, CCI was no longer significant after adjusting for the eFI and the PROMIS.

Body composition

Unremarkably, patients in Sarcopenia-1 and Sarcopenia-2 groups had lower median ALM/kg2 compared to the non-sarcopenic group [7.35 (5.75, 9.56) vs. 8.47 (7.55, 9.47); p < 0.01 and 7.55 (5.44, 9.27) vs. 8.47 (7.55, 9.47); p < 0.01)]. Higher median BMI was seen in those with Sarcopenia-1 compared to no sarcopenia [(29.7 (24.5, 35.3) vs. 26.4 (23.4, 31.5); p < 0.01)] and in Sarcopenia-2 vs. no sarcopenia [(30.9 (24.9, 38.3) vs. 26.4 (23.4, 31.5); p < 0.01)]. Total percent body fat was greater in both Sarcopenia-1 (40.46% vs. 37.41%; p < 0.01) and Sarcopenia-2 (41.12% vs. 37.41%; p < 0.01) compared to those with no sarcopenia. The eFI was also associated with BMI (r = 0.33; p < 0.01) and percent body fat (r = 0.19; p < 0.01).

Of note, the CCI was independently related to the ALM/kg2, even after multivariable adjustment for eFI and PROMIS T-scores (p < 0.05). The eFI was not related to ALM/kg2. The PROMIS PF T-score was negatively related to ALM/kg2 after adjustment for age and gender, but not independently from eFI and CCI. In contrast, the PROMIS Mobility T-score was negatively related to ALM/kg2, which persisted after multivariable adjustment for eFI and CCI (p < 0.05).

Physical performance

Individuals in Sarcopenia-1 and Sarcopenia-2 ranked significantly and progressively worse on all physical performance measures (Fig. 3) than those without sarcopenia. Notably, the Sarcopenia-2 group were 1–2 SD below the non-sarcopenia group for most measures, representing a clear impairment in performance outcomes beyond handgrip strength and chair stands.

Discussion

In this study, measures of muscle function and DXA imaging were used to quantify sarcopenia variables, while also estimating frailty status using structured data from the corresponding EHR. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use an electronic frailty measure as a predictor for measured sarcopenia. This study demonstrated strong relationships between eFI and sarcopenia and that eFI independently predicts sarcopenia. Greater eFI scores were directly related to increased likelihood of sarcopenia, and median eFI values for Sarcopenia-1 and Sarcopenia-2 were 43% and 61% larger than the non-sarcopenic group, respectively. Together, these data suggest that frailty detection using a more generalizable screening tool could add a new dimension to sarcopenia detection, which is a well-known gap in clinical practice.

To date, studies have emphasized the independent associations between sarcopenia and cardiovascular disease26, respiratory disease27, cancer outcomes28 and all-cause mortality29, and also between frailty and the same health outcomes30–32. Studies have also demonstrated co-existence of sarcopenia in frail older adults33,34, along with a higher risk of death compared to people with frailty but not sarcopenia, independent of age and functional variables35,36.

A new finding in our study indicates that self-completed questionnaires such as the PF and Mobility domains of the PROMIS questionnaire had strong relationships with sarcopenia probability and independently predicted sarcopenia risk, predicting both eFI and sarcopenia. The PROMIS tools reflect the patient’s perception of their function and limitations and could be used to identify older persons who may benefit the most from comprehensive geriatric assessment, a multi-dimensional review of medical, functional, and psycho-social needs of an older person for long-term follow-up. Administering a PROMIS tool for screening is highly generalizable to adult populations (18 and older) and could help promote equity of access to comprehensive, geriatric assessment services.

Prior studies have not compared eFI with sarcopenia assessments. Our study demonstrated that eFI and sarcopenia have a strong relationship and eFI may be an important tool for clinicians to risk stratify patients when other field base testing may be time consuming (DXA for lean mass, Grip strength, sit-to-stand, etc.). Notably, the time burden for primary care physicians to perform day-to-day patient care is already high and clinicians often have very limited time for activities beyond clinical workflow37. Other studies have demonstrated that eFI can be automatically calculated38, enhancing the use of primary care electronic medical records and mitigating screening and assessment time39. Altogether, these findings address a well-known gap in the literature and provides an alternative screening method for sarcopenia.

The physical function domain of the PROMIS was an independent predictor of sarcopenia-2 but not sarcopenia-1. This likely reflects degree of severity of sarcopenia and that the PROMIS PF questions capture some information uncharacteristic of sarcopenia. In contrast, the mobility domain of the PROMIS predicted both sarcopenia-1 and sarcopenia-2 consistent with the sarcopenia tests capturing characteristics related to mobility, even at earlier stages, and serves as a better predictor of overall sarcopenia status.

Overall, CCI was highest among those with Sarcopenia-2, consistent with this patient group having an overall larger disease burden. CCI was predictive of sarcopenia after adjusting for age and gender, yet this relationship disappeared after adjusting for multiple variables (i.e., PROMIS, eFI, age, and gender). However, the CCI remained independently associated with ALM even after multivariable adjustment, while the eFI did not. This is a complicated finding that could possibly be explained by the questions surrounding each index. For example, the CCI is derived from 17 comorbidities that include chronic conditions known to effect muscle (diabetes, cancer, heart failure, etc.). The eFI is a broader concept of overall function and is influenced by physical, behavioral, and social variables. Perhaps, these constructs that generate an eFI score are not specific to muscle mass but are more closely related to muscle function. Prior research has noted independent roles of muscle function vs. muscle quantity, in overall muscle health40. Thus, our findings may demonstrate that the eFI is more appropriate for predicting muscle function than muscle mass. It is also important to note that neither the eFI nor the CCI explained a large proportion of variance in our model. The CCI only explained 6% of the variance and so interpretation of these results should be in light of these small r2 values.

Other reports have demonstrated conflicting results concerning obesity related BMI and body fat and its role in sarcopenia. Importantly, obesity does not preclude sarcopenia, and low muscle mass and strength may contribute to obesity. One possibility is the combination of reduced muscle mass and increased visceral fat contributes to systemic inflammation and insulin resistance – leading to low muscle mass and strength in the elderly41. In addition, decreased movement due to loss of skeletal muscle function accelerates muscle fat infiltration, which is closely related to physical inactivity42. In contrast, some studies have indicated obesity may protect skeletal muscle mass in women43 and that low BMI was a predictor sarcopenia44. In our study those with sarcopenia had higher body fat and BMI than those that did not, with the highest BMI in those with sarcopenia-2. Future research is needed regarding the different mechanisms by which sarcopenia can be associated with states of both high body fat as well as with low body fat in differing clinical situations.

Our study has several strengths including standardized measurements of muscular function, inclusion of a diverse age range of over 1000 adults, and combining “gold-standard” sarcopenia assessments with complex multi-health system EHR data. Subjects who did not have sufficient EHR data were excluded to ensure that robust structured EHR data were available for both cases and those without sarcopenia. Likewise, missing functional variables were excluded from the analysis. This was likely to exclude some healthy patients free of sarcopenia, given reduced interaction with the healthcare system and less available clinical data. In addition, we used the strongest machine learning model from a prior study10 as an alternate method, to support that eFI predicted sarcopenia risk identified from the EHR similar to another model trained on sarcopenia. Our EHR data source was a large multi-health system database enhancing the generalizability of the EHR component. Lastly, we incorporated simple screening tools (PROMIS) that are more generalizable to clinical settings.

Weaknesses of our study include a limited number of individuals that met criteria for sarcopenia. Due to this, we performed analyses based on participants having either 1 or more criteria for sarcopenia or 2 or more criteria for sarcopenia, irrespective of strength outcomes4. Some of our individuals were perhaps more mildly affected, which might have decreased the differences between groups. Despite this, we did detect a strong relationship of the eFI to sarcopenia. We also calculated frailty using previously reported methods but had to exclude the portion of the eFI that required available EHR data on specific history regarding activities of daily living that were not obtainable from the structured (coded) data within EHR11. Future studies are needed to externally validate the use of the eFI and the predicted probabilities of sarcopenia in external datasets and EHR systems. This will confirm the ability to accurately predict sarcopenia and demonstrate risk factor assessment in various populations. Such studies will also require concurrent direct musculoskeletal measurements to confirm sarcopenia as well as access to the EHR and PROMIS results.

Recent evidence with respect to frailty and sarcopenia interventions is limited11. Most clinical trials that measure such interventions have been small, with heterogeneous trial populations and nonuniform screening tools, interventions, and outcome measures, all of which contribute to low-quality evidence45–48. Unpredictable patterns of functional decline can create difficulties in identifying persons with frailty early rather than when nearing the end of life. Deployment of electronic health record based eFI scores have shown to be helpful in the detection of frailty in published literature. This way of identifying frailty has advantages in care of patients through potential early detection which can facilitate intervention to increase their physiological reserve and address stressors. The novelty of our work extends the usefulness of deploying eFI scores to identify the related condition of sarcopenia early, with potential to predict outcomes based on sarcopenia and to support early intervention for sarcopenia. Care for persons with frailty should focus on (1) increasing physiological reserves (such as strength) to build robustness and resilience and (2) preventing or mitigating stressors. Potential clinical targets for frailty interventions include addressing depression, anemia, hypotension, hypothyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, and adverse drug events49,50. Assessing a person’s degree of frailty can provide a framework for applying evidence and principles of geriatric care while elucidating the risk of sarcopenia.

Conclusion

Sarcopenia and frailty are overlapping constructs, though not all frail persons have sarcopenia on measurements. Methods for detecting frailty must be clinically oriented and easily assessable within a routine outpatient setting. Given challenges in applying formal muscle measures in a busy clinical practice, eFI may provide screening tool for sarcopenia. Incorporating eFI and PROMIS measures in clinical settings could provide opportunities for detecting patients with probable sarcopenia who may benefit from targeted interventions to promote and maintain overall muscle function.
