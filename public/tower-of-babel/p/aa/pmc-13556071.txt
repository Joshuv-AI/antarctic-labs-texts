Dynamic CBME in action: rule-based digital case-based learning to evaluate antibiotic-stewardship reasoning in MBBS learners
Authors: Sunil Kumar D, Sucheta Dandekar, G Hari Prakash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance is a major global public health threat, necessitating improved antibiotic stewardship. India’s Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) framework requires robust methods to assess and cultivate clinical reasoning. This study evaluated a rule-based Digital Case-Based Learning (DCBL) module with algorithm-triggered formative feedback for antibiotic-stewardship decision-making among undergraduate medical learners.MethodsA quasi-experimental, single-group pre-post study evaluated three rule-based DCBL micro-cases (upper respiratory tract infection [URTI], urinary tract infection [UTI], and acute watery diarrhoea) in 271 third- and fourth-phase MBBS learners at a tertiary-care teaching hospital in South India. Script Concordance Test (SCT) performance was assessed using parallel pre- and post-test forms. Usability and workload were measured using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and two NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) items.ResultsOverall SCT scores changed from 5.65 +/- 4.73 to 6.05 +/- 4.48 (mean change = 0.40; 95% CI: -0.41 to 1.21; p = 0.33; Cohen’s d = 0.059). The UTI case showed a statistically significant domain-specific improvement (mean change = 0.58; p = 0.003; Cohen’s d = 0.18), which falls below the conventional small-effect threshold. URTI and diarrhoea cases showed no significant change. The mean SUS score was 62.4 +/- 18.7; 76.0% of learners rated platform usability as average or good, with moderate cognitive workload.ConclusionA single-session rule-based DCBL intervention was feasible and acceptable in routine CBME teaching and showed a domain-specific SCT signal in the UTI case. Introduction

Clinical reasoning is the cognitive process by which clinicians integrate data, formulate diagnoses, and make treatment decisions in the face of uncertainty. It is widely recognised as the cornerstone of medical competence [1, 2]. Unlike factual recall, clinical reasoning demands that learners navigate ambiguity, weigh competing hypotheses, and apply context-dependent judgement. These are skills that are difficult to cultivate through didactic lectures or conventional multiple-choice assessments [3]. This gap is particularly pressing in antibiotic prescribing, where reasoning errors directly drive antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [4].

In India, where 45–80% of patients presenting with upper respiratory tract infections (URTI) and diarrhoea receive antibiotics despite predominantly viral aetiologies, the outpatient setting remains a critical frontier for stewardship [5]. Urinary tract infections (UTIs) carry their own stewardship challenges, with guideline-discordant prescribing documented across multiple countries [6]. Despite the magnitude of the problem, antibiotic stewardship education in undergraduate curricula remains inconsistent and largely didactic [7, 8].

India’s National Medical Commission Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) framework explicitly requires that Indian Medical Graduates (IMGs) function as first-contact clinicians capable of authentic clinical decision-making [9]. However, faculty consistently report difficulties in assessing higher-order cognitive skills such as clinical reasoning [10, 11]. Digital Case-Based Learning (DCBL), particularly when designed with algorithm-triggered formative feedback, offers a promising pedagogical strategy: it embeds branching clinical logic, simulates realistic decision pathways, and delivers immediate, individualised feedback aligned to stewardship principles [12, 13]. Although generative artificial intelligence is increasingly used in medical education, its pedagogical value and ethical implications remain the subject of active debate [14, 15]; accordingly, the present study deliberately employed a transparent, rule-based design rather than a generative model.

The Script Concordance Test (SCT), originally described by Charlin and colleagues, offers a validated method for assessing clinical reasoning under uncertainty [2, 16]. Unlike conventional assessments, the SCT evaluates how new information modifies the probability of a clinical decision. Responses are scored against an expert reference panel using aggregate-credit scoring, which accommodates the legitimate variability inherent in expert clinical judgement [17]. The SCT has been validated across diverse disciplines and training levels, including emergency medicine, paediatrics, neurology, and paramedicine [18–20].

Despite growing interest in digital learning and SCT-based assessment, evidence linking DCBL with structured feedback to changes in SCT-measured clinical reasoning remains scarce, particularly in the context of antibiotic stewardship education for undergraduate learners in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Most existing studies have focused on knowledge gains measured through conventional tests, with limited attention to higher-order reasoning outcomes [21, 22]. This study therefore aimed to determine whether a single-session, rule-based DCBL intervention comprising digital micro-cases on URTI, UTI, and acute watery diarrhoea could improve SCT performance among MBBS learners. Secondary objectives included evaluating case-specific effects, characterising individual-level heterogeneity, and assessing platform usability and cognitive workload using the SUS and NASA-TLX.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a quasi-experimental, single-group pre-post study embedded within routine CBME teaching at a tertiary-care teaching hospital in South India, involving third- and fourth-phase MBBS learners. The study evaluated changes in SCT-measured antibiotic-stewardship reasoning after a rule-based DCBL intervention. It was conducted as an educational intervention within the existing CBME framework and did not involve patient clinical data. Voluntary participation was ensured, all data were de-identified before analysis, and the study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

A convenience sample of third- and fourth-phase MBBS learners enrolled in the CBME curriculum at JSS Medical College, JSS Academy of Higher Education and Research, Mysuru, was recruited from July to September 2024 (academic year 2024-25), after institutional ethics approval. Eligibility criteria included current enrolment in the MBBS programme and willingness to participate in the complete study session. A total of 271 participants provided complete paired pre-post data and constituted the analytic sample. Coded identifiers were assigned to enable data pairing while preserving anonymity. Where duplicate entries resulted from technical resubmissions on the digital platform, the most recent response was retained. The frequency of such duplicates was low and did not affect the final analytic sample of 271.

Intervention: rule-based digital case-based learning module

The intervention comprised three online digital micro-cases addressing high-volume outpatient antibiotic stewardship scenarios: (i) URTI, (ii) UTI, and (iii) acute watery diarrhoea. Each micro-case incorporated branching clinical logic with two to three decision nodes, simulating authentic clinical decision-making under uncertainty. The digital platform was built on a rule-based branching logic architecture. At each decision node, pre-authored, expert-reviewed formative feedback (labelled in the platform as a digital coach) was automatically triggered based on the learner’s selection from predefined response options. The feedback was contextualised to antibiotic stewardship principles, clinical decision thresholds, and safety-netting strategies. A large language model did not power the platform, did not use generative artificial intelligence, and did not generate de novo adaptive text. Therefore, the intervention is described in this revised manuscript as a rule-based DCBL module with algorithm-triggered feedback rather than as an artificial intelligence intervention. The micro-cases were designed to activate illness-script formation and refinement, consistent with script theory that underpins the development of clinical reasoning.

Study procedure and data collection

The study was conducted in a single sitting of approximately 45 to 60 min. The intervention sequence followed a structured protocol: (1) administration of the pre-intervention SCT (pre-SCT); (2) completion of three DCBL micro-cases with structured digital feedback at every decision node; (3) administration of the post-intervention SCT (post-SCT), using parallel items to minimise testing effects; and (4) completion of the SUS and two NASA-TLX items for usability and workload evaluation. This study is reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomised Designs (TREND) statement; a completed TREND checklist is provided as Supplementary Appendix A.

Outcome measures and instruments

Script concordance test

The primary outcome was overall SCT performance, operationalised as total SCT score and mean item score. The SCT comprised 12 items designed in accordance with established construction guidelines [16], each presenting a clinical vignette and a 5-point Likert-type scale assessing the likelihood or appropriateness of a clinical decision in light of new information. Items were developed iteratively by the research team and reviewed by an expert panel of five clinicians (three community medicine physicians and two general medicine consultants with antibiotic stewardship experience), whose responses served as the reference panel for aggregate credit scoring. Scores were weighted according to the distribution of expert responses to capture inherent clinical uncertainty. Pre- and post-test forms used parallel items, matched for clinical domain, decision node, difficulty, and format, to minimise testing effects; equivalence was evaluated by comparing domain-level difficulty indices. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.71 for the pre-test SCT and 0.68 for the post-test SCT, indicating acceptable internal consistency for a short, domain-specific clinical reasoning instrument. A detailed SCT blueprint, scoring approach, and representative item structure are provided in Supplementary Appendix B. Complete item stems and expert response distributions can be supplied to the editor and reviewers as confidential supplementary material on request, because unrestricted public release would compromise future use of the instrument in subsequent cohorts.

Secondary outcomes

Domain-specific (case-level) SCT scores for URTI, UTI, and acute diarrhoea were analysed as secondary outcomes. Case-level decision accuracy was evaluated at Node-2 (the treatment decision node) for each case, serving as the primary case-level treatment decision outcome. The System Usability Scale (SUS) was administered to evaluate platform usability, yielding a composite score (0 to 100) categorised as Poor (50 or below), Average (51 to 70), or Good (above 70). Cognitive workload was assessed using two items from the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX): self-reported mental effort and frustration, each rated on a 1–7 Likert scale. These two subscales were selected a priori as most relevant to evaluating cognitive engagement in a single-session educational technology context; full-scale NASA-TLX administration was not feasible within the study protocol.

Sample size

This study was conducted as an embedded educational feasibility evaluation using a convenience sample and was not prospectively powered for a specific effect size. The final analytic sample of 271 learners provides a preliminary estimate of the direction and magnitude of change in SCT performance, usability, and cognitive workload. Because the observed effect was very small and the study lacked a control group, effect estimates and confidence intervals should be interpreted cautiously rather than as confirmatory evidence of effectiveness.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed on paired complete cases, matched on unique learner identifiers. Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. For the primary analysis, paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-intervention total SCT scores and mean item scores. Effect size was reported as Cohen’s d (mean change divided by the standard deviation of the change), applied uniformly to the overall and case-level outcomes, with d = 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively. The 95% confidence interval for the mean change was calculated as mean plus or minus 1.96 multiplied by (SD divided by the square root of n). For case-level analyses, paired t-tests were conducted for each domain; the Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Risk differences in appropriate treatment decisions at Node-2 were computed with 95% CIs using the Wilson method. Usability outcomes were reported as counts and percentages; workload metrics were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.10 (Jupyter Notebook; NumPy 1.24, SciPy 1.10, pandas 2.0). A two-tailed p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary outcome.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, JSS Medical College, JSS AHER, Mysuru (NABH Accredited; Approval No. JSSMC/IEC/97/2024/01 NCT/2024-25; dated 10 July 2024). Data collection was undertaken after ethics approval. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 271 MBBS learners (third- and fourth-phase) provided complete paired pre- and post-data and were included in the primary analysis. All 271 participants completed the full intervention sequence, including the three DCBL micro-cases, both SCT administrations, and the usability and workload assessments, in a single sitting of approximately 45 to 60 min.

Primary outcome: overall SCT performance

Overall SCT performance is presented in Table 1. The mean total SCT score changed from 5.65 +/- 4.73 at pre-test to 6.05 +/- 4.48 at post-test, yielding a mean change of 0.40 +/- 6.77 (95% CI: -0.41 to 1.21; t = 0.97, p = 0.33). Correspondingly, the mean item score changed from 0.471 +/- 0.395 to 0.504 +/- 0.373 (mean change = 0.033 +/- 0.564; 95% CI: -0.034 to 0.100; t = 0.97, p = 0.33) (Table 1). The observed Cohen’s d for the primary outcome was 0.059, which is below the conventional 0.2 threshold and should be interpreted as negligible by standard benchmarks. The primary outcome, therefore, did not demonstrate a statistically significant overall change in SCT following this single-session intervention.

Secondary outcome: case-specific SCT performance

Case-level analyses revealed heterogeneous intervention effects across the three clinical domains (Table 2; Fig. 1). The UTI case demonstrated a statistically significant domain-specific improvement, with a mean score increase from 0.79 +/- 2.07 to 1.37 +/- 2.35 (mean change = 0.58 +/- 3.18; t = 3.00, p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = 0.18). This effect, which lies below the conventional small-effect threshold, remained statistically significant after Holm-Bonferroni correction and was accompanied by the highest proportion of individual improvers (49.8%) across all three cases. In contrast, the URTI case showed a non-significant decline in mean score from 1.80 +/- 2.11 to 1.70 +/- 1.97 (mean change = -0.10 +/- 2.70; t = -0.63, p = 0.529, Cohen’s d = -0.04), with a greater proportion of learners declining (43.2%) than improving (38.0%). Similarly, the diarrhoea case demonstrated a negligible, non-significant change (mean change = -0.08 +/- 3.20; t = -0.40, p = 0.690, Cohen’s d = -0.03), with nearly equal proportions of improvers (39.1%) and decliners (38.7%). The only statistically significant SCT signal was therefore domain-specific and limited to the UTI case.

Individual-level heterogeneity

Analysis of individual-level response patterns revealed substantial heterogeneity across all three case domains. The proportion improving ranged from 38.0% in URTI to 49.8% in UTI, while the proportion declining ranged from 34.0% in UTI to 43.2% in URTI. The UTI case exhibited the most favourable individual response profile. Across all three cases, 16.2–22.1% of learners showed no change, potentially reflecting floor or ceiling effects in pre-existing knowledge.

Case-wise change in treatment decisions

The forest plot (Fig. 2) depicts risk differences in appropriate treatment decisions at Node-2. The UTI case showed the largest positive risk difference (+ 0.059; 95% CI: -0.004 to 0.122); however, as the confidence interval crossed zero, this result did not reach statistical significance. The URTI case showed a small negative risk difference (-0.018; 95% CI: -0.072 to 0.035), and the diarrhoea case showed an essentially null risk difference (-0.011; 95% CI: -0.064 to 0.042). Non-significant findings are reported as such; no trend language is applied to results where confidence intervals cross zero.

Usability assessment

Evaluation of platform usability using the SUS (Table 3) revealed that most learners rated the DCBL platform as Average to Good. Approximately 39.1% (n = 106) rated usability as Good (SUS above 70), 36.9% (n = 100) as Average (SUS 51 to 70), and 24.0% (n = 65) as Poor (SUS 50 or below). The mean SUS score was 62.4 +/- 18.7, corresponding to marginal-to-average usability and falling below the conventional SUS acceptability benchmark of 68. Overall, 76.0% of the cohort rated usability as average or good.

Cognitive workload

Self-reported cognitive workload was moderate overall (Table 4). The median mental effort score was 4.0 (IQR: 3.0–5.0) on a 7-point scale, indicating moderate cognitive engagement. The median frustration score was 3.0 (IQR: 2.0–4.0), suggesting relatively low frustration. These findings indicate that the DCBL module imposed an acceptable cognitive load without inducing excessive frustration.

Discussion

This study examined a single-session, rule-based DCBL intervention to improve antibiotic-stewardship reasoning among MBBS learners in India. The principal finding was that the overall SCT change was not statistically significant (mean change = 0.40; 95% CI: -0.41 to 1.21; p = 0.33; Cohen’s d = 0.059). This effect size is negligible by standard benchmarks. These findings are best understood as preliminary feasibility and acceptability evidence rather than evidence of effectiveness.

The lack of a statistically significant overall change is plausible for a brief 45 to 60-minute educational exposure targeting a complex cognitive outcome. Cook and colleagues, in their meta-analysis of internet-based learning in the health professions, reported that technology-enhanced interventions typically produce larger gains when learners receive repeated, spaced exposures rather than a single encounter [23]. The SCT assesses the capacity to modulate clinical decisions in response to new and uncertain information, which is unlikely to shift substantially after one session. Naylor et al., evaluating SCT performance among paramedic students, similarly observed that the formative value of the SCT may exceed its sensitivity to short-term interventions [19].

The most instructive finding was the marked heterogeneity in intervention effects across the three clinical domains. The UTI case demonstrated a statistically significant domain-specific improvement (p = 0.003, d = 0.18) with the highest proportion of individual improvers (49.8%), while URTI and diarrhoea cases showed no significant change. Given that the UTI domain provided the only significant SCT signal, conclusions should not be generalised across all antibiotic-stewardship scenarios.

Several factors likely explain the UTI-specific response. First, baseline UTI performance was the lowest of the three domains (pre-test mean = 0.79), affording the greatest scope for improvement, consistent with a baseline floor effect that leaves greater measurable headroom. Second, UTI management involves relatively discrete, guideline-amenable decision points, including empirical antibiotic choice, treatment duration, and criteria for microbiological culture, which naturally align with the branching-logic structure of the DCBL format. In contrast, URTI stewardship primarily requires withholding antibiotics and managing diagnostic uncertainty about viral versus bacterial aetiology, a reasoning pattern less amenable to structured feedback alone. The challenge of improving URTI stewardship through brief educational interventions has been documented elsewhere [24]. Similarly, diarrhoea management involves nuanced decisions about the role of antibiotics across dysenteric and non-dysenteric presentations that likely require deeper clinical exposure than a single digital session can provide.

The substantial individual-level variability, with 38.0 to 49.8% of learners improving, 34.0 to 43.2% declining, and 16.2 to 22.1% showing no change, is not unusual in medical education research. Clinical reasoning development is highly individual, shaped by prior knowledge structures, motivation, and metacognitive capacity [25, 26]. The proportion of decliners may partly reflect regression to the mean, particularly among learners whose pre-test scores were elevated by guessing. It may also reflect temporary disruption of existing knowledge structures before cognitive reorganisation can occur, a pattern described in the knowledge reorganisation literature [27], though this interpretation is speculative in the absence of process data. Future analyses should include regression modelling to identify predictors of response. The mixed individual response profile argues for iterative, spaced DCBL exposure rather than a one-off session, consistent with evidence that distributed practice produces more durable gains for complex cognitive skills [28].

With 76.0% of learners rating the platform as Average to Good (mean SUS = 62.4) and moderate-low cognitive workload scores, the DCBL module met acceptable thresholds for educational technology usability [29, 30]. The 24.0% who rated usability as Poor cannot be dismissed. Digital literacy varies considerably among Indian medical students, and interface barriers divert cognitive resources from the learning task itself [31, 32]. Future iterations should incorporate user-experience testing with lower-performing subgroups to identify specific usability difficulties and ensure equitable access.

This study demonstrates that rule-based DCBL can be integrated into routine CBME teaching to address clinical decision-making competencies that are difficult to cultivate through lectures alone. The format aligns with CBME principles: it activates authentic reasoning, provides just-in-time formative feedback, yields individual-level analytics for programmatic assessment, and can be delivered at scale [33, 34]. The domain-specific findings point to the need for tailored instructional design. For URTI and diarrhoea, complementary strategies such as communication skills training, shared decision-making simulations, or reflective debriefing may be needed alongside the DCBL module. From an antimicrobial stewardship perspective, embedding stewardship reasoning early in the undergraduate curriculum can help establish appropriate prescribing patterns before suboptimal habits become entrenched [22].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include a substantial sample size (N = 271), use of an SCT aligned to clinical reasoning under uncertainty, expert-panel scoring with reported reliability indices, inclusion of confidence intervals and explicit effect sizes, evaluation of individual-level heterogeneity, and assessment of usability and cognitive workload. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-group pre-post design without a control group is the principal limitation: observed changes may reflect testing effects (including format familiarity even with parallel items), maturation, regression to the mean, Hawthorne effects from engagement with a novel digital activity, or practice effects from the micro-cases independent of structured feedback. Causal attribution to the DCBL intervention is therefore not warranted. The convenience sample from a single institution, the single-session design, and the absence of delayed follow-up further limit conclusions about generalisability and durability. The URTI and diarrhoea cases showed no significant change, reinforcing the finding that the results cannot be generalised across all antibiotic-stewardship domains. The absence of baseline covariate data precluded formal regression analysis of predictors of response.

Future directions

Future research should employ randomised controlled designs with active comparator groups to isolate the contribution of structured feedback from case-based learning itself. Longitudinal studies with spaced DCBL sessions and delayed post-tests would clarify whether domain-specific improvements are durable and whether repeated exposure amplifies the effect. Regression modelling with pre-specified covariates, such as academic performance and phase of training, would help identify predictors of individual responses. Multisite studies across diverse Indian medical colleges, along with alignment of case content with local antibiograms, would strengthen the evidence base for broader CBME integration.
