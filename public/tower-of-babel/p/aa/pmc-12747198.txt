True Cinnamon as a dietary phytogenic alternative to antibiotics: Effects on lipid quality, metabolic health markers, and sensory traits of broiler breast meat
Authors: Mohammed M. Qaid, Saud I. Al-Mufarrej, Maged A. Al-Garadi, Abdulaziz A. Al-abdullatif, Abdulmohsen H. Alqhtani, Rashed A. Alhotan, Ahmed A. Al-Haidary, Abdulrahman S. Alharthi, Mohsen M. Alobre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•True Cinnamon bark (CVB) at 0.2% enhanced meat flavor to levels equal to or better than the antibiotic group.•CVB, particularly at 0.2%, lowered the breast-meat n-6: n-3 ratio by up to 46 % (p < 0.001).•Serum ALT activity fell by > 35%, indicating CVB possesses hepatoprotective effects.

Introduction

Global demand for broiler meat is steadily increasing, driven not only by population growth but also by consumer interest in healthier, high-quality animal products (Suliman et al., 2023). Recent attention has focused on improving the fatty acid (FAs) profile and organoleptic traits of poultry meat through dietary interventions (Asante-Addo, 2020; Li et al., 2023). Traditional broiler diets tend to elevate saturated fatty acids (SFAs) in meat, which are associated with chronic diseases including cardiovascular conditions and type II diabetes (WHO, 2023). Enhancing meat with more polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs), especially n-3 PUFAs, and optimizing the PUFAs/SFAs and n-6/n-3 ratios are recognized strategies for producing healthier poultry meat (Kalakuntla et al., 2017; Wood and Enser, 2017). However, increasing PUFAs content raises susceptibility to oxidation, which may degrade flavor, texture, and consumer acceptability, while also posing potential health concerns (Sierżant et al., 2018; Winiarska-Mieczan et al., 2020). To address oxidative instability in poultry meat, natural antioxidants from herbs and botanicals have been increasingly investigated for their ability to stabilize unsaturated fats, enhance meat shelf life, and promote consumer health (Hashemipour et al., 2013; Rostami et al., 2017; Jachimowicz et al., 2022).

At the same time, growing concerns over antimicrobial resistance and the regulatory ban on antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) have intensified the search for safe, natural feed additives (Wickramasuriya et al., 2024; Trinchera et al., 2025). Among promising alternatives, plant-derived compounds-including essential oils and spices-have demonstrated antimicrobial, antioxidant, and performance-enhancing effects, while also improving organoleptic quality without the health risks associated with AGPs (Islam and Nishibori, 2023; Saracila et al., 2023; Rebezov et al., 2024; Vlaicu et al., 2024). True cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum), a widely used culinary spice, contains several potent bioactive constituents such as cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, and a range of fat- and water-soluble micronutrients (Rao and Gan, 2014; Slimi Khaoula, 2024; Alyamani et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2025). Its biological activities include antimicrobial, antioxidant, antidiabetic, and cholesterol-lowering effects (Bibi et al., 2024; Slimi Khaoula, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). In poultry, cinnamon supplementation has been reported to enhance digestion, feed efficiency, and metabolic balance through modulation of antioxidant defense and the expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism (Kumar et al., 2014; Bastos, et al., 2017).

Previous studies on dietary cinnamon in broilers have used widely varying doses (0.2-7%) with inconsistent results (Toghyani et al., 2011; Sang-Oh et al., 2013; Faghani et al., 2014; Hussein et al., 2016; Islam and Nishibori, 2023). This variability necessitates more standardized dosing strategies, especially using practical inclusion levels based on preliminary in vitro efficacy data (Al-Garadi et al., 2023). This study addresses several critical gaps: the lack of integrated data on performance index, meat sensory quality, FAs profile, and lipid quality, and safety biomarkers; the unclear safe upper limit of CVB inclusion; and the absence of direct comparisons with commercially used antibiotics like colistin-amoxicillin (Colimex®). The primary research question is whether practical inclusion rates of dietary CVB can reproduce the growth-promoting effects of AGPs while improving breast-meat fatty-acid quality and maintaining organoleptic acceptability without compromising liver or kidney function. Thus, this trial investigates the impact of 0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6% CVB supplementation in Ross 308 broilers from hatch to day 34, compared to both a negative control and a colimex® antibiotic-treated group on meat FAs composition, organoleptic properties, safety markers, growth performance index, and feed efficiency.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The trial protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Committee- King Saud University (KSU) (Approval Number: KSU-SE-20-44).

Zootechnical performance and dietary treatments

Upon arrival, one-day-old Ross 308 broiler chicks obtained from a commercial hatchery (Al Khumasia Company for Feed and Animal Products, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia) were sexed using the feather-sexing method. Only males were selected to ensure flock uniformity and to minimize potential sex-related variation in growth performance and lipid metabolism. A total of 150 selected chicks were then randomly assigned to five dietary treatments, each consisting of five replicates with six birds per replicate. In the first treatment, control group, broilers received a basal diet alone; in the second treatment, broilers were given a basal diet plus Colimex® antibiotic (150 mg/kg colistin and 200 mg/kg amoxicillin); and in the three remaining treatments, broilers were given a basal diet plus CVB powder at doses of 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6%, respectively. The inclusion level of Colimex was based on the study by Elbadawy and Aboubakr (2017). Broilers were housed in ecologically controlled, heated battery cages in the Experimental Poultry Research Unit at KSU and were lit with an incandescent light program of 23-hour light and 1 hour dark throughout the experimental period. Throughout the experiment, feed and water were available ad libitum. CVB was purchased from a supermarket in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and ground to a particle size of 0.25-0.30 mm in a nutrition laboratory using a grinder. CVB was added on top of the broiler basal diet in three cinnamon groups. The basal diet was formulated in mash form. All the essential nutrients meet the Ross 308 nutrient recommendation (New York, NY, USA). Feed composition and chemical composition of broilers diets.

Phytochemical analysis using GC-MS and HPLC, total phenolic content using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, and macronutrients of CVB using proximate analysis

The identity of the CVB used in this study was authenticated by the Department of Botany, KSU. The chemical profile of CVB was comprehensively characterized before use through proximate analysis and advanced chromatographic techniques. Specifically, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was employed to separate, identify, and quantify its constituents following Peeriga et al. (2025), while gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) was applied as described by Al-Garadi et al. (2023). The total phenolic content was determined spectrophotometrically using the Folin–Ciocalteu method with gallic acid as a standard, according to Oufighou et al. (2025). Macronutrient composition was estimated following the American Association of Cereal Chemists (AACC, 2000) procedures. Notably, our previous investigation Al-Garadi et al. (2023) demonstrated that extracts prepared from the same authenticated CVB exhibited potent in vitro antimicrobial activity against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as fungal strains, confirming the authenticity and functional relevance of the material used in the present experiment.

The fatty acid contents of breast meat

At 34 days, 5 male broilers were chosen at random and slaughtered, and the pectoralis major muscles were sampled. Suliman et al. (2021) described the slaughter procedure. Prior to FAs content and sensory analysis, all samples frozen at -20°C were thawed all night in the refrigerator at 4°C. According to Agnew et al. (2019), frozen breast samples were freeze-dried, crushed, homogenized, and extracted to form FAs methyl esters in order to determine FAs composition. The peak areas were calculated automatically by recording the data with gas chromatography (GC-6890 N, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), according to Semjon et al. (2020). The mass spectra of the mixtures were identified in comparison to the standards using the characterized mixtures, the GC-MS retention index, and literature and library data. Each FAs concentration was stated as a percentage of total FAs, and the mean values were used to compute the total of SFAs and PUFAs (total monounsaturated FAs “MUFAs” and PUFAs) (Qaid et al., 2023). Three points were made for each sample (n = 15 biological samples per each group).

Lipid quality and metabolic indices

The evaluation of lipid quality and metabolic indices was conducted to provide deeper insight into how dietary CVB influences fat metabolism, oxidative activity, and overall lipid quality of breast meat. These parameters serve as key indicators of the birds’ physiological response to dietary treatments and the potential of CVB to improve lipid utilization and health-related attributes of broiler meat. These indices are calculated as described in Dal Bosco et al. (2016) and Qaid et al. (2023).

Qualitative indices

The qualitative indices of breast meat fatty acids, including the polyunsaturated-to-saturated fatty acid (PUFA/SFA) ratio, omega 6 to omega 3 (n-6/n-3) ratio, linoleic acid/α-linolenic acid ratio (LA:ALA ratio), and unsaturation index (UI), were assessed to evaluate the nutritional and physiological quality of the lipid profile. These indices provide valuable insight into the healthfulness, oxidative stability, and metabolic balance of meat lipids, allowing a better understanding of how dietary CVB influences meat quality and fatty acid metabolism in broilers. The mathematical equations for qualitative indices used are:PUFAs/SFAsratio=ΣPUFAs/ΣSFAs,n−6/n−3ratio=(Linoleicacid+Eicosadienoicacid+Dihomogammalinolenicacid+Arachidonicacid)/(αLinolenicacid+Eicosatrienoicacid+EPA+Docosapentaenoicacid+DHA).LA:ALAratio=C18:2n−6/C18:3n−3.UI=(Monoenoic%)+(2*Dienoic%)+(3*Trienoic%)+(4*Tetraenoic%)+(5*Pentaenoic%)

Nutritional indices

The nutritional indices of breast meat fatty acids, including the nutritional value index (NVI), atherogenicity index (AI), thrombogenicity index (TI), hypocholesterolemic / hypercholesterolemic ratio (H/H ratio), and health-promoting index (HPI), were evaluated to estimate the potential impact of dietary CVB on meat lipid quality and human related health. These indices collectively reflect the balance between beneficial and detrimental FAs, providing a comprehensive assessment of the nutritional value, cardiovascular health implications, and overall health-promoting potential of broiler meat. The mathematical equations for nutritional indices used are:NVI=[C18:0+TransC18:1n−9]/C16:0AI=([C12:0+(4*C14:0)+C16:0])/ΣUFAsTI=([C14:0+C16:0+C18:0])/([(0.5*ΣMUFAs)+(0.5*Σn−6PUFAs)+(3*Σn−3PUFAs)+(Σn−3PUFAs/Σn−6PUFAs)])H/Hratio=([C18:1n9+ΣPUFAs])/([C12:0+C14:0+C16:0])HPI=ΣUFAs/([C12:0+(4*C14:0)+C16:0])

Metabolic indices

These indices are calculated from the activities of the desaturating and elongating enzymes of the pectoral muscle tissue.

Organoleptic properties analysis

Organoleptic analysis (juiciness, flavor, tenderness, and overall acceptability) was performed to evaluate the acceptability of broiler breast meat fed with or without cinnamon as described in Suliman et al. (2021) and Alagbe (2023). At the time of analysis, chicken breast muscles were cooked in an oven at 200°C for 30 minutes to reach an internal temperature of 70°C. Following that, the cooked meat samples were cut into 2-cm3 pieces, and given a random code number for identification. The samples were presented warm on numbered plates for examination. Five semi trained panelists were requested to rate each meat sample (n=25 biological samples per each group). The mean of all panel assessments was determined to define the sample characteristic. The organoleptic qualities were scored by testers using an eight-point hedonic scale.

Evaluation of serum creatinine and safety enzymes

Blood samples were collected from 34-day-old broiler chickens via the wing vein (5 birds per treatment). Serum was separated and centrifuged using commercially diagnostic kits and a spectrophotometer to determine aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) as indicators of liver function and serum creatinine as a kidney function indicator.

Feed efficiency and performance index

During the study, the initial and final body weights (g) of the birds (BW) were recorded to calculate body weight gain (BWG, g). Submitted and residual feed were recorded to calculate feed intake (FI, g). Feed efficiency (FE) and performance index (PI) were determined. FE = BWG/FI. PI = BWG / FCR.

Statistical analysis

The data collected were subjected to a one-way ANOVA using the general linear model (GLM) of the statistical analysis system SAS (2012). The model's equation wasγij=μ+Ti+eij

Where Yij is the individual observation, µ is the general mean, Ti is the effect of the ith treatment, and eij is the random residual error. The experimental unit of feed efficiency and performance index is n=5 replicated cages/group, and for organoleptic attributes, FAs and its related indices of breast meat are n=5 male broiler samples/group. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to the residuals to verify the assumption of normality before conducting further statistical analyses. The experiment was done using a completely randomized design. Results are presented as the statistical mean ± SEM and separated using the Tukey HSD method at p < 0.05. To understand the relationship between the FAs contents in the meat of broiler chickens and their health indices, a correlation coefficient was calculated using the Pearson correlation test.

GC-MS analysis revealed a diverse phytochemical composition in CVB extract. The major components were volatile siloxanes and aromatic silyl derivatives, including hexamethylcyclo trisiloxane and 1,4-bis(trimethylsilyl)benzene, each comprising 10.32 % of the total area, both eluting at 7.152 min. SFAs dominated the 25.536 min peak, notably (palmitic, stearic, tridecanoic, nonadecanoic, myristic, and margaric acids with respective purities of 99, 98, 83, 70, 62, 58, and 58, respectively), each contributing 4.77 %, indicating strong lipid content relevant for modulation of meat fatty acid profiles. In addition, at 11.329 min, several bioactive aromatic compounds were detected, each at 4.74 %, including (E)-Cinnamaldehyde (respective purities 99%), a major bioactive in cinnamon with known antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. Benzofuran, 4-methylcoumarin derivatives, and cinnamic acid-related esters and amides, contributing to flavor, aroma, and potential health effects. Minor compounds (<3 %) such as oxime derivatives, amino acids, and benzoic acid esters appeared at retention times (RT) of 4.108–4.211 min.

Furthermore, in the CVB extract, the caffeine level was 700 µg/g, with a RT of 5.164 minutes, a peak area of 132,732, and a peak height of 2,865 as determined by HPLC. This concentration suggests that caffeine, although not a primary constituent of cinnamon, may contribute to the extract's bioactivity, potentially enhancing metabolic or antioxidant effects through synergistic interaction with other phytochemicals. Additionally, an unidentified compound exhibited a larger peak than caffeine, indicating a higher relative abundance.

Moreover, the total phenolic content of ethanolic CVB extract was 58.18 mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry weight, as determined by the Folin–Ciocalteu method, indicating its high antioxidant capacity and potential as a functional phytogenic feed additive. These detected phytochemicals can be supports the role of CVB in preserving meat quality and protecting against lipid peroxidation

Additionally, the proximate analysis of CVB reveals its nutrient composition. It contains 10.29% moisture and 89.71% dry matter on an as-fed basis. The crude protein content is 4.43%, while the ether extract (lipid) is 4.03%. Ash accounts for 3.18% of the composition, and total crude fiber is relatively high at 24.35%. The nitrogen-free extract (NFE), which represents the readily available carbohydrates, is 64.02%, and the organic matter content is 96.82%. Regarding fiber fractions, the acid detergent fiber (ADF) is 45.75%, and the neutral detergent fiber (NDF) is 65.34%, indicating a substantial fibrous component. The gross energy content of CVB is estimated at 4974.52 kcal/kg, highlighting its potential as a high-energy dietary supplement.

Fatty acid contents

Dietary CVB supplementation significantly influenced the FAs profile of broiler breast meat (Table 3). Among SFAs, myristic (C14:0), palmitic (C16:0), and stearic acids (C18:0) differed (p < 0.05) across treatments. Palmitic acid was the most abundant. Myristic and palmitic acids peaked in the antibiotic group, while stearic acid was highest in the control. Total SFAs were lowest in the 0 and 0.2% CVB groups and highest in the antibiotic, 0.4%, and 0.6% CVB groups. MUFAs-notably palmitoleic (C16:1), oleic (C18:1 n-9), and cis-11-octadecenoic acids (C18:1 n-7)-were also affected (p < 0.05). Palmitoleic acid was highest in the 0.2% CVB group, whereas oleic acid dominated in the antibiotic group. Conversely, SFAs and MUFAs peaked in the antibiotic and 0.2% CVB groups (approximately 49%; p < 0.001), driven mainly by palmitoleic and trans-oleic acids, and were lowest in the control. PUFAs varied notably. ALA, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and total n-3 PUFAs were maximized by the 0.2% CVB group. Docosapentaenoic acid (DPA) was absent in the control group but lower in CVB groups compared to the antibiotic group. While CVB consistently reduced n-6 PUFAs, PUFAs, and UFAs (p < 0.001) compared to the control group. The similarity (p > 0.05) in nutritional indicators, including nutritional value index, atherogenic index, thrombogenic index, H/H ratio, and health-promoting index, indicates that none of the dietary treatments, including CVB and antibiotic supplementation, had a significant impact on the overall nutritional quality or health-related lipid profile of the broiler meat. Metabolic indices varied significantly among treatments (p < 0.05), while Δ5- plus Δ6-desaturases and activity index showed no significant differences (p > 0.05). Metabolic enzyme surrogates indicated that elongase and thioesterase activities declined sharply with colimex (-51% and -33%, respectively) but remained intermediate with CVB (p < 0.001) compared to the control. A higher elongase index (C18:0/C16:0) in the control and 0.2% CVB groups reflects more active elongation of C18 chains. Similarly, the elevated thioesterase index (C16:0/C14:0) suggests reduced cleavage of shorter-chain fatty acids. The Δ9-desaturase index was highest in the 0.2% CVB group, intermediate at 0.6% CVB, and lowest in the other groups (p < 0.001). This indicates that the 0.2% CVB-supplemented group enhanced enzymatic conversion of saturated fatty acids (such as C18:0) into MUFAs (such as C18:1 n-9). Thus, broilers receiving 0.2% CVB improved lipid fluidity, membrane function, and potentially better meat quality and oxidative stability.

Collectively, up to 0.6% dietary CVB modulated muscle fatty-acid composition towards a more favorable n-6/n-3 balance and maintained feed efficiency comparable to the antibiotic program, although sensory traits were not improved to the same extent as the in-feed antibiotic. The accompanying reduction in serum ALT indicates that CVB can be incorporated safely while exerting subtle health-promoting alterations in broiler meat quality.

Discussion

This study investigated the potential of CVB powder as a natural alternative to AGPs, particularly the colistin–amoxicillin combination (Colimex®), in broiler diets. The findings highlight the multifaceted influence of CVB on broiler meat sensory quality, FAs profiles, and lipid quality. Broilers raised under intensive conditions are prone to oxidative stress, compromising meat quality (Kwiecień et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2021). Since FAs are closely associated with cardiovascular health, there is increasing interest in enhancing the FA composition of broiler meat (Jachimowicz et al., 2022). In this trial, AGPs supplementation improved several FAs quality indices, notably decreasing the n-6/n-3 ratio by 46.26% and LA/ALA ratio by 81.64% while elevating MUFAs content by 19.04% and Δ⁹-desaturase activity by 8.71% compared with the control. These changes may enhance the oxidative stability and sensory attributes of meat, aligning with earlier findings on antibiotic-fed broilers (Toghyani et al., 2011; Cimrin et al., 2020). However, despite such improvements, increasing public concern regarding antibiotic residues and antimicrobial resistance continues to discourage the use of AGPs for sensory enhancement. Interestingly, the antibiotic group exhibited lower health-promoting indices (HPI: 1.77, NVI: 1.40, and UI: 104) and a higher SFA proportion (34.8 %), indicating a potential trade-off between performance and health-related FAs metrics. Nonetheless, the enhanced performance indices and reduced ALT levels suggest improved gut and liver health, which may be attributed to the antimicrobial and metabolic-modulating effects of antibiotics (Elbadawy and Aboubakr, 2017).

CVB, rich in polyphenols compounds, demonstrated particularly favorable effects at 0.2% inclusion. This level notably increased total n-3 PUFAs, especially ALA (+ 100.8%) and EPA, while decreasing total SFAs and the n-6/n-3 ratio by more than 53%. Such modulation supports a healthier fatty acid profile and agrees with the findings of Dalkilic et al. (2009), increased PUFA levels in poultry meat supplemented with cinnamon oil. The improved LA/ALA ratio observed here, an indicator of desaturase enzyme activity (Das, 2006), approached the optimal 1-2:1 range, contrasting with the 10:1 ratio typical in Western diets (Simopoulos, 2016; Draycott et al., 2019). Moreover, the 0.2% CVB group exhibited elevated Δ9-desaturase activity and superior unsaturation and health-related indices (HPI: 2.13; AI: 0.69; H/H ratio: 2.27), reflecting improved fatty acid quality. These results align with earlier observations by Ciftci et al. (2010), who found that cinnamon supplementation reduced SFAs and increased PUFAs in broiler meat. Elevated MUFAs levels in the present study likely resulted from enhanced desaturation of palmitic (C16:0) and stearic (C18:0) acids. The highest palmitoleic and trans-oleic acid concentrations were recorded in the 0.2% CVB group, possibly indicating improved intestinal absorption or metabolic conversion (Wood and Scollan, 2022). From a cardiovascular health standpoint, the reduced atherogenic and thrombogenic indices and increased H/H ratio further suggest that 0.2% CVB contributes to a more favorable, heart-healthy lipid profile. This indicates a favorable shift toward hypocholesterolemic FAs (oleic acid, PUFAs), which are inversely related to LDL cholesterol (Chen et al., 2016). These beneficial shifts may be linked to the modulation of hepatic desaturase and elongase pathways and the bioactive properties of cinnamaldehyde.

From a sensory perspective, CVB inclusion maintained or improved flavor attributes across treatments, while tenderness and juiciness were highest in the Colimex group. This observation agrees with reports that phytogenic additives enhance flavor and aroma but may not fully match AGPs in texture improvement Chowlu et al. (2019). The slightly reduced juiciness observed in higher CVB groups may be associated with higher SFA content and a lower unsaturation index, which could influence moisture retention. MUFA levels, particularly in the 0.2% CVB group, correlated positively with flavor perception (R² = 0.62, p = 0.014), whereas an elevated n-6/n-3 ratio negatively impacted tenderness (R² = -0.83, p = 0.0001). These findings reinforce the relationship between fatty acid composition and meat texture. CVB supplementation also mitigated excessive n-6 PUFAs levels, a favorable outcome considering the predominance of linoleic acid in typical human diets. Although flavor was maintained across CVB levels, tenderness remained slightly lower than in the antibiotic group. Previous studies on cinnamon's sensory impact have reported mixed findings- some noting improved aroma and reduced off-flavors (Jung et al., 2024), while others found negative effects at high concentrations (Zawani et al., 2022)-suggesting that optimal dosage and possible synergistic combinations with other herbs or proteolytic enhancers may further improve tenderness.

Phytochemically, GC–MS analysis of CVB confirmed the presence of cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, and various phenolic and aromatic compounds, which contribute to both lipid metabolism modulation and sensory preservation. These bioactives possess antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Bhavaniramya et al., 2019; Favaretto et al., 2020; Khan et al., 2023), and their phenolic components can inhibit myoglobin oxidation and lipid peroxidation, thereby reducing spoilage and off-flavors (Wood and Scollan, 2022; Nuerjiang et al., 2023).

Regarding safety, all CVB inclusion levels significantly reduced serum ALT activity (by 35-49%) relative to the control, without affecting AST, indicating hepatoprotective effects. These findings agree with earlier studies showing cinnamon’s protective role against oxidative stress and bacterial-induced liver damage (Tabatabaei et al., 2015; Khan et al., 2023).. The decline in ALT activity may reflect reduced hepatic inflammation and improved membrane integrity, though further enzymatic analyses would help clarify underlying mechanisms.

Overall, dietary supplementation with 0.2-0.6% CVB maintained overall BWG and FI leading to maintained growth performance index and feed efficiency comparable to those achieved with the antibiotic program. In agreement with Toghyani, et al (2011)and Chowlu, et al. (2019), cinnamon supplementation, irrespective of inclusion level, did not significantly affect FI or FE. This outcome may reflect the birds’ tolerance of cinnamon’s mild citrus, sweet, and spicy flavor. The comparable FI among treatments further suggests that CVB supplementation at the tested levels did not impair appetite or alter feeding behavior during the experimental period. Although the antibiotic-supplemented group performed similarly to the control, this likely reflects the absence of a microbial or health challenge during the trial. Under such optimal conditions, the benefits of antibiotics on gut microflora balance and nutrient utilization are often limited. Thus, the comparable performance among treatments suggests that CVB effectively sustained digestive efficiency and metabolic stability even without such challenges, underscoring its potential as a natural alternative for maintaining growth and FE in antibiotic-free production systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with CVB, particularly at 0.2%, improved broiler meat’s FA composition by enriching n-3 PUFAs, reducing pro-inflammatory lipid ratios, and enhancing flavor without compromising safety or performance. The higher unsaturation index of the group with 2 g of CVB indicates a higher rate of healthy FAs in their meat but also a higher possibility of autoxidation of FAs. The PUFAs content and oxidative stability of the meat can be enhanced by CVB, which is a natural antioxidant that contains phytocontaminants. These outcomes suggest that CVB can serve as a natural and effective alternative to AGPs like Colimex®, supporting both poultry health and consumer preference in antibiotic-reduced production systems. The current study’s duration (34 days) aligns with commercial practices but lacks post-chill meat stability or oxidative degradation data. Future studies should include lipid oxidation markers, microbial and molecular profiling, and shelf-life evaluations to better understand the long-term impact of CVB on meat quality and safety.
