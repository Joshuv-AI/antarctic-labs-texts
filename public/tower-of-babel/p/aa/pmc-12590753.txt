Sexual behaviors and self-reported sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among male and female youths in Nigeria
Authors: David Aduragbemi Okunlola, Oluwatobi Abel Alawode, Chioma Veronica Opara, Anthony Idowu Ajayi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain a global public health issue and are highly prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, with current literature indicating that risky sexual behaviors are major risk factors. Studies have shown that STIs are more common among young people engaging in risky sexual behaviors. We examined the associations between sexual behaviors and self-reported STIs, including the role of marital status, among male and female Nigerian youths (aged 15–29).MethodsWe pooled and analyzed data from the 2008, 2013, and 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Surveys (NDHS). The secondary data were collected using a stratified two-stage cluster sampling technique across the states in Nigeria. We categorized sexual behaviors (in the last twelve months) into six groups ranging from abstinence to non-condom use and sexual infidelity, and measured STI status through self-reporting. We performed chi-square tests of associations and multivariable logistic regressions to examine the relationships between sexual behaviors and self-reported STIs among married and never-married youths.ResultsThe findings of this study revealed that the associations between sexual behaviors and self-reported STI depended on sex and marital status. Among never-married women, those who did not use condoms and who were engaged in sexual infidelity were four times more likely to report having a STI (OR = 4.88; 95% CI = 2.84–8.37) compared to those who practiced abstinence. Among married women, the odds of self-reporting of a STI were seven times higher for those who used condoms but engaged in sexual infidelity (OR = 7.60; 95% CI = 2.06–28.09). For never-married men, those who used condoms but engaged in sexual infidelity were five times more likely to report STIs (OR = 5.54; 95% CI = 2.87–10.68) than those who practiced abstinence. For married men, sexual behaviors were not significantly associated with self-report of STI.ConclusionThis study showed that sexual behaviors were associated with self-reported STIs among never-married and married female youths and never-married male youths in Nigeria. These findings can inform programs aimed at reducing STI transmission among youths in Nigeria. Specifically, they can guide the development of educational programs to increase youth awareness of the risks associated with their sexual behaviors.

Introduction

Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) contributes 93 million cases of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) every year [1]. In 2019, age-standardized incidence rates of STIs were highest in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (19,973.12 per 100,000 persons), Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (17,033.30 per 100,000 persons), and Western Sub-Saharan Africa (13,358.91 per 100,000 persons) [2]. Globally, STIs are more prevalent among young people, ranging from 40 million among persons aged 15–19 years to at least 120 million among persons aged 30–34 years [2]. In Nigeria, self-reported STIs increased from 1.0% to 7.7% from 2003 to 2018 among women and men aged 15-49 years, affecting younger women aged 15-29 more disproportionately than younger men of the same age [3–6]. STIs are associated with pelvic inflammatory disease, preterm delivery, stillbirth, risk of HIV infection, infertility, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and cancer [7–12].

Risky sexual behaviors rank high among the factors that are positively associated with the risk of STIs [13, 14]. Risky sexual behaviors include unprotected sexual intercourse (nonuse or incorrect use of condoms), sexual infidelity, inconsistent or lack of STI testing, and casual sex [15, 16]. On the other hand, consistent and correct use of condoms, testing regularly for STIs, observing sexual fidelity, and combining these behaviors may have a protective effect on young people’s sexual health [17, 18]. However, many scholars tend to view young people’s sexual behaviors as dichotomous—regardless of whether individuals use protection or not—or ignore the interaction of sexual behaviors, thus neglecting nuances in the expression and measures of sexual behaviors. Such approaches erroneously characterize individuals who do not use condoms—but use injectables, emergency contraceptives, and preexposure prophylaxis—as engaging in risky sexual behaviors even when they adhere to sexual fidelity [15, 16]. The concurrence of condom use and sexual fidelity provides a relatively more comprehensive and realistic assessment of STI risk and prevention strategies among young people. Even though each sexual behavior may individually reduce the risk of STI [17, 19], their protective values are often interdependent in practice.

A more elaborate measure of the spectrum of young people’s sexual behaviors has been developed—by combining (un)protective sex and sex occurrence outside (or within) a marital or nonmarital union (i.e., sexual (in)fidelity)—and their health implications [20]. The measures are characterized by (a) virginity, (b) sexual inactivity, (c) contraceptive use and sexual fidelity, (d) contraceptive use and sexual infidelity, (e) nonuse of contraceptives and sexual fidelity, and (f) nonuse of contraceptives and sexual infidelity [20, 21]. The main goal of this study was to examine which different sexual behaviors are associated with increase or decrease in the likelihood of self-reporting STIs among youths in Nigeria. Given that youths are the most at risk for STIs, it is important to understand how their sexual behaviors affect their exposure to STIs. Such evidence could inform programs aimed at reducing STI transmission and improving the sexual and reproductive health and well-being of youths in the country.

Marriage is a form of social control that is negatively correlated with risky sexual behaviors and STIs [22]. However, contemporary demographic trends suggest that the institution of marriage is weakening in sub-Saharan Africa due to widespread urbanization, leading to individualism, secularization [23], extramarital affairs [24], as well as premarital cohabitation. Even among married and unmarried adults, there is an increased craving for sexual gratification [23]; thus, increasing their risk of contracting STIs. However, the literature is silent on whether marital status moderates the associations between sexual behaviors and STIs among youths in Nigeria. More importantly, given the threats of secularization to the marriage institution, it is unclear whether there are specific sexual behaviors that are more associated with STIs among married female and male youths than among unmarried female and male youths. Conversely, since the culture of marriage is still valued across cultures and continues to exert social control and stigmatize sexual infidelity in Nigeria, it is logical to assume that there are no significant associations between sexual behaviors and STIs among married female and male youths (compared to their unmarried counterparts).

In this study, we tested the following hypotheses that compared to abstinence: (a) other SBs are positively associated with self-reported STI among married female youths; (b) other SBs are positively associated with self-reported STI among single female youths; (c) other SBs are positively associated with self-reported STI among single male youths; and (d) other SBs are positively associated with self-reported STI among married male youths in Nigeria.

Methods

Data source

We pooled and analyzed the individual recode files from the 2008, 2013, and 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS), publicly available at https://www.dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm. The surveys were cross-sectional and nationally representative, providing demographic and health indicators. In each survey, a stratified, two-stage cluster sampling technique was used to select eligible respondents (men and women aged 15–49), allowing for accurate and reliable estimates of demographic and health indicators at the national and state levels [25–27].

Study population

This study considered male and female youths (aged 15–29) who were never married or married for one or more years. The age group aligns with the National Youth Policy [28]. For the married group, we considered respondents who were married for at least twelve months and had no history of multiple unions as an attempt to ensure that the indicators of sexual behaviors and self-reported STI were measured in the last twelve months. This was done to put sexual behaviors in the context of marriage or cohabitation (in the last twelve months) to measure infidelity. Those who have had multiple unions might have reported multiple sexual partnerships (excluding their male partners) before marriage or cohabiting with their male partners. They might have engaged in paid sex before marriage or cohabiting in the last twelve months, which does not necessarily mean infidelity by the time they were married in the same twelve months and at the time of the survey. The survey question also did not ask about their infidelity while they were married or cohabiting in the last 12 months. This approach minimized overestimation bias. From the 2008, 2013, and 2018 NDHS, we analyzed weighted samples of 3,711, 4,142, and 1,739 never-married or married men, respectively, and 12,029, 13,685, and 13,926 never-married or married women, respectively, who met the inclusion criteria. The differences in women’s and men’s samples were due to sample size calculated by the DHS Program rather than lower participation of men.

Measures

Outcome and explanatory variables

The outcome variable in the study was self-reported STI. The respondents were asked, “During the last 12 months, have you had a disease that you got through sexual contact? Those who said “yes (1)” had STI; otherwise, “no STI (0)”.

The primary explanatory variable was SBs. We adopted the approach from a recent study measuring this variable based on four categories of SBs: (a) sexual experience, (b) sexual abstinence in the last 12 months, (c) condom used with the last and second-to-last sexual partner, and (d) practice of sexual infidelity [21]. A woman (or a man) was said to have practiced sexual fidelity if she did not have any sex partner apart from or including her partner in the last 12 months. We combined the four categories to form five mutually exclusive categories of sexual behavior: (1) abstained from sex over the last 12 months, (2) used a condom and sexual fidelity, (3) used a condom and no sexual fidelity, (4) sexual fidelity and no condom, and (5) no condom and no sexual fidelity. From the sample respondents, no one was sexually inexperienced; hence, we considered abstinence over the last 12 months the most protective category (i.e., the reference category), against which we compared categories 2 to 5, which are non-protective sexual behaviors.

Covariates

We controlled for some explanatory variables identified in previous studies as correlates of SBs and/or self-reported STIs. These explanatory variables are individual-, household-, and community-level variables linked to risky sexual behaviors [20, 21, 24, 29, 30]. Theoretically, the multilevel explanatory variables could be considered part of socio-ecological factors that may independently and/or jointly predict health behaviors and outcomes [21, 29]. At the individual level, we considered age (15–19, 20–24, and 25–29 years), level of education (uneducated, primary, secondary, and higher education), level of awareness of STI and HIV—a composite score ranging from 0 (unaware) to 2 (highly aware) after summing two questions (with a possible response of (1) yes or (0) no) about respondents’ awareness of STI and HIV—and religion (Christianity, Islam, and Traditional/others) [20, 21, 24, 29, 30].

At the household level, we considered wealth quintiles, a measure developed by the DHS program [24]. Its levels range from the least (the poorest), second, middle, and fourth to the highest (the richest) group. The DHS Program published the computational procedure at https://www.dhsprogram.com/topics/wealth-index/Wealth-Index-Construction.cfm. We included marriage type (monogamous and polygynous or polygamous) [24].

The community-level variables were residence (urban and rural) and region of residence (North-Central, North-East, North-West, South-East, South-South, and South-West) [24, 29, 30]. We also controlled for state-level HIV prevalence rates, which we presumed to be a potential predictor of self-reported STI and the spread of STIs within states. Information about state-level prevalence rates was obtained from the 2019 Nigeria National HIV/AIDS Indicator and Impact Survey Technical Report [31]. The prevalence rates range from 4.8% in Benue and Akwa Ibom States to 0.3% in Jigawa and Katsina States [31]. We also controlled for survey years due to the yearly variation in self-reported STI from the DHS Program StatCompiler (https://www.statcompiler.com/en/). The importance of time in explaining the variations in human development and health-related phenomena is situated within the socioecological theory of human development [32–36].

Statistical analyses

We described all the categorical variables with frequencies and percentages. We conducted Chi-square tests of independence to assess the bivariate associations between the explanatory variables and self-reported STI. We conducted separate analyses for (a) never-married women, (b) married women, (c) never-married men, and (d) married men.

Given the dichotomous outcome variable, we fitted multiple binary logistic regression (MBLR) at four stages to examine the relationship between SBs and self-reported STI. The models were fitted separately for never-married women, married women, never-married men, and married men. More importantly, we estimated multiple Firth logistic regressions (FLRs) for married men because of their low weighted cell sizes (when SBs and self-reported STI were cross-tabulated). This was done to prevent perfect separation and associated bias due to the small sample size or rare events [37]. In the first model, we examined the relationships between SBs and self-reported STI. The second model contained only awareness of STI or HIV and the state-level prevalence of HIV. The third model contained only age, level of education, wealth quintiles, residence, region, and survey years. In the fourth and last model, we examined the relationships between SBs and self-reported STI while adjusting for the effects of the (control) variables in models 2 to 3.

The extent of relationships in the models was estimated with odds ratio (OR), i.e., the odds of self-reported STI (1) versus no report of STI (0). The data were set up for analyses with the “svyset” command to specify the weight, cluster, and strata parameters (StataCorp, 2015). We subsequently restricted our analyses to non-partnered young (15–29 years) women by combining the “svy” and the subpopulation (“subpop”) commands for the descriptive analyses and MBLR, but used the “if” command for the FLR [38]. The probability values associated with the ORs were tested against a 5% significance level; their 95% confidence intervals were also computed.

Bivariate analyses among never-married men and married men

Table 2 presents the associations between explanatory variables and self-reported STIs. Among never-married men, SBs are associated with self-reported STIs. The proportion of self-reported STIs was highest among never-married men who did not use condoms and engaged in sexual infidelity (8.9%), followed by those who used condoms but engaged in sexual infidelity (6.8%). Other variables associated with self-reported STIs were age and survey year. Among married men, non-protective sexual behaviors were related to self-reported STIs. The proportion of self-reported STIs was highest among young men who did not use condoms and engaged in sexual infidelity (4.3%), followed by those who used condoms and practiced sexual infidelity (4.2%). Education, religion, residence, and region were associated with self-reported STIs among married men.

Discussion

Our study examined whether sexual behaviors (and which of them) were associated with self-reported STIs among Nigerian youth. We also investigated whether this association varied by marital status. Our findings confirmed the hypotheses that, compared to abstinence, other sexual behaviors were positively associated with self-reported STI among (a) married female youths, (b) never-married female youths, and (c) never-married male youths in Nigeria. STI risk mirrors youth sexual behaviors, with higher odds of self-reported STI for never-married women who (a) used condoms and practiced sexual fidelity, (b) used condoms but practiced sexual infidelity, (b) never used condoms and practiced sexual fidelity, and (c) never used condom and practiced sexual infidelity compared to the never-married who abstained from sex in the past year. Among all the categories of sexual behaviors, the likelihood of self-reported STI was highest among those who never used condoms and practiced sexual infidelity.

Our findings suggest that whether they use condoms or practice sexual fidelity or not, never-married women are more susceptible to STIs. They can contract STIs from other sex partners (excluding their male partners) or even get infected through their infected male partners who have engaged in multiple sexual partnerships. The risk of STIs due to being unmarried suggests that marriage may offer some protection against the risk of engaging in high-risk sexual behaviors and STIs [22]. This is because marriage is culturally considered a marker of responsibility that promotes sexual exclusivity and other forms of social controls [22, 39].

In contrast, given the trending secularization and the craving for sexual gratification even among married young people in Africa [23], married women are not immune to STIs because they may also engage in high-risk sexual activities. In support of this, we found that the likelihood of self-reported STIs was higher among married women who used condoms but practiced sexual infidelity and among those who neither used condoms nor practiced sexual fidelity compared to the married ones who abstained from sex in the last year. Our findings demonstrate that regardless of marital status, risky sexual behaviors are positively associated with STI risk [13, 14]. However, the observed high odds of STIs among married women who reported condom use while engaging in infidelity is counterintuitive and likely reflects inconsistent condom use.

Among the never-married men, we found higher odds of self-reported STIs among those who (i) used condoms but practiced sexual infidelity and those who neither used condoms nor practiced sexual fidelity compared to those who abstained from sex in the past year. Previous studies have documented that prolonged bachelorhood is associated with risk of risky sexual behaviors and STIs [40, 41].

Conversely, we found no evidence that sexual behaviors were positively associated with self-reported STI among married men. Specifically, the odds of reporting STI were not significantly different between married men who abstained from sex and (a) those who used condoms and practiced sexual fidelity, (b) those who used condoms and practiced sexual infidelity, (c) those who did not use condom and practiced sexual fidelity, and (d) those who neither used condom nor practiced sexual fidelity. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that social desirability bias, underreporting of sexual activities and STI status, and the absence of other sexual behaviors (such as cunnilingus and transactional sex) might contribute to our null findings among the sample of married men. Additionally, the findings for married men should be interpreted with caution, as the small sample size of abstaining married men limited the statistical power.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. First, respondents self-reported their STI status, and owing to the sensitivity of disclosing such information, there is a high likelihood of underreporting sexual activity and STI status, including the possibility of not capturing the asymptomatic cases because some respondents might not have been diagnosed with any STIs. Some might be ignorant of their status, even though they were infected, but still stated that they were not infected. Thus, we may have underestimated the prevalence of STIs among the target sample. Future studies should objectively assess STIs using appropriate biomarkers. Additionally, given the cross-sectional nature of the data and the lack of temporal ordering between sexual behaviors and self-reported STIs, we could not establish causality. Besides, we cannot rule out the possibility of bidirectional associations between sexual behaviors and self-reported STI. Readers should interpret our findings within the context of these limitations. The use of aggregated STI data may have concealed important behavioral risk patterns linked to specific infections, thereby limiting the specificity of our findings.

However, our findings are valuable for educating young people about the risks associated with STIs and sexual behaviors. Given the high prevalence of STIs and their adverse consequences, the development of behavioral change programs targeting youth, especially the never-married male and female youths, including married female youths, should be a priority for increasing testing, consistent condom use and encouraging sexual fidelity in Nigeria. Promoting sexual fidelity, even among condom users, could further reduce STI prevalence among Nigerian youths.

Conclusion

This study shows that sexual behaviors are associated with self-reported STIs among never-married and married female youths and never-married male youths in Nigeria. Our study adds to the growing body of knowledge that the combination of condom use and sexual fidelity may determine the likelihood of STIs among youths. These findings can inform programs aimed at reducing STI transmission among youths in Nigeria. Specifically, they can guide the development of educational programs to increase youth awareness of the risks associated with their sexual behaviors. More importantly, our composite measures of sexual behaviors and findings may assist policymakers and other health-sector stakeholders in designing more comprehensive STI risk assessments, followed by developing, implementing, and evaluating dual interventions that promote condom use among youths in Nigeria and sensitize them on the risk of sexual fidelity.
