Enhanced Functional and Surgical Outcomes With 3D Printing in Orthopedic Oncology: A Comparative Meta‐Analysis Against Conventional Techniques
Authors: Peter Joseph Mounsef, Benjamin Blackman, Ojasvi Sharma, Ahmed Aoude, Anthony Bozzo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThree‐dimensional printing (3DP) technology has increasingly gained attention in orthopedic oncology, where complex tumor resections and reconstructions demand high precision. 3DP enables the creation of patient‐specific models and prostheses, which can improve postoperative quality of life for patients while assisting surgeons in preoperative planning, enhancing surgical accuracy, and improving outcomes in complex oncologic cases. Despite its potential, comprehensive data on the effectiveness and applications of 3DP in orthopedic oncology are limited. This paper assesses whether using 3DP compared to conventional techniques results in better outcomes in orthopedic oncology.MethodsA comprehensive search of Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted until November 2024. Studies comparing 3D printing to conventional methods in orthopedic oncology and reporting outcomes such as operative time, blood loss, recurrence rates, or functional scores were included. Weighted means and meta‐analyses were conducted to compare these outcomes. Statistical heterogeneity was adjusted by using a random‐effects model.ResultsFourteen studies comprising 478 patients met the inclusion criteria. Our primary findings were improved MSTS scores (mean difference [MD]: 2.17, p = 0.00) and decreased blood loss (MD: −69.8 mL, p = 0.00) in the 3D printing groups. There was no significant difference in operative time between 3D printing and conventional techniques (MD: −12.2 min, p = 0.32). Tumor recurrence rates did not differ significantly between groups (relative risk: 0.88, p = 0.50). Subgroup analyses indicated that 3D‐printed implants showed the greatest benefit in reducing OR time and blood loss, with the other subgroups showing no significant difference in OR time, blood loss, or recurrence rate.ConclusionThe findings suggest that 3D printing in orthopedic oncology may enhance surgical precision by reducing OR time, intraoperative blood loss, and improving postoperative function, without affecting recurrence rates. Substantial heterogeneity limits confidence in these findings.Level of EvidenceLevel III.

Introduction

Cancer is a major global health concern, with millions of new cases and high mortality rates reported each year [1]. Musculoskeletal tumors, particularly sarcomas of bone, represent a small yet challenging subset of these malignancies due to their complex anatomical locations and variable biological behavior. Common types of bone sarcomas, such as osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and chondrosarcoma, typically require comprehensive treatment approaches that include surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation therapy to achieve the best possible outcome [2]. Advances in limb‐salvage procedures have reduced the need for amputations in many patients, but surgeons still face significant difficulties in removing these tumors while preserving bone integrity and function [3].

One of the biggest challenges in orthopedic oncology is achieving negative surgical margins while minimizing complications [4]. Even with the use of advanced imaging techniques and modular reconstruction implants, traditional surgical approaches can be associated with longer operating times, high blood loss, and a risk of local tumor recurrence [5]. Therefore, there is a strong interest in improving preoperative planning and intraoperative precision to optimize patient care.

In recent years, three‐dimensional (3D) printing has emerged as a tool that may help address these challenges in surgical oncology [6]. By converting imaging data into patient‐specific 3D models, surgeons can plan resections with more accuracy and even create custom surgical guides or implants tailored to individual anatomical structures [7]. Early studies suggest that this approach might shorten operating times, reduce blood loss, and lower recurrence rates by allowing more precise tumor resections, although current research is mostly from smaller case series and observational studies [8].

Beyond oncological control and surgical efficiency, post‐op functional outcomes are a major focus in orthopedic oncology. The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) scoring system was developed in 1993 as an objective tool to measure functional outcomes in patients affected by upper or lower extremity neoplasms [9]. Over the past decades, the treatment of musculoskeletal oncologic diseases has increasingly prioritized patient function and quality of life, recognizing their impact on long‐term recovery and well‐being. Functional outcome measures, such as the MSTS score, play a crucial role in guiding clinical decision‐making, helping to balance oncologic control with limb preservation strategies. Studies have shown that integrating functional assessments into treatment planning not only optimizes surgical approaches but also enhances patient satisfaction and overall quality of life [10].

We performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis of the existing literature to clarify whether 3D printing truly improves surgical and oncologic outcomes compared to conventional techniques. This study aims to provide a clear synthesis of current evidence regarding operative efficiency, blood loss, local recurrence, and functional outcomes.

Methods

Search Strategy

A complete medical literature search was executed through November 2024 using Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science in compliance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Literature was searched using text words and MeSH terms, including 3D printing, additive manufacturing, rapid prototyping, bone neoplasms, sarcoma, osteosarcoma, and chondrosarcoma. Boolean operators (OR, AND) and asterisks for truncation were used to optimize the search results. References of relevant studies were also reviewed.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria required that studies: (1) used 3D printing as the primary intervention, guide, or implant, (2) were orthopedic oncology procedures, (3) reported outcomes, and (4) compared outcomes between 3D printing and conventional interventions.

Studies were excluded if: (1) no comparison between 3D printing and conventional interventions was made, (2) they were non‐orthopedic oncology, (3) the study was not in English, (4) the study was a review, editorial, technique, methods paper, conference proceeding, or incomplete (e.g., only abstracts available), (5) the study used animals, or (6) the study used cadavers.

Literature Screening and Data Extraction

Search results from all databases were uploaded to Covidence, and duplicates were automatically removed. Articles were initially screened independently by two reviewers based on titles and abstracts. The remaining articles were screened based on the full text. Disagreements were discussed between the two reviewers to reach a consensus, and conflicts were resolved by the senior author, an orthopedic oncologist.

Of the final studies, extracted information included: study title, author list, year of publication, sample size, mean age, sex, underlying condition, intervention, how 3D printing was used, primary comparison, total operative time, blood loss volume, fluoroscopy time, recurrence, follow‐up, reported complications, MSTS scores, and other conclusions from the author.

Outcome Reporting and Statistics

Pooled estimates and meta‐analyses compared OR time, blood loss, and recurrence rates between 3DP and conventional techniques. Forest plots were created to pool studies across the same outcome measures (DataParty, Python 3.8.10). To assess heterogeneity, the I 2 test was used with values between 25% and 49% considered “low,” 50% and 74% considered “moderate,” and values greater than 75% considered “high” statistical heterogeneity.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS‐I V2 tool. An independent evaluation was conducted by two authors. In cases of disagreement, an agreement was reached through discussion with a third party.

Results

Literature Search

The initial literature search yielded 3570 studies, of which 1017 duplicates were removed. Among the remaining 2553 unique articles, 2522 were removed following title and abstract screening. Systematic screening and assessment of eligibility yielded 14 full‐text studies that satisfied inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Inter‐rater reliability analysis showed that substantial agreement and almost perfect agreement were achieved at the title and abstract screening stage (k, 0.68; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.52–0.82) and full‐text (k, 0.87; 95% CI: 0.69–1.0) stages of screening, respectively.

Study Characteristics

This review included 14 studies comprising 478 patients. There were 214 and 264 patients in the 3DP and traditional groups, respectively. The mean age of patients in the 3DP group was 43.4 (13.9), compared to 38.6 (9.4) in the conventional group. Chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, and Ewing's sarcoma were the most common tumor types reported in the included studies. Specifically, nine studies included patients with chondrosarcoma, eight with osteosarcoma, and six with Ewing's sarcoma. The most frequently operated anatomical regions were the pelvis and the extremities, each reported in six studies, followed by the spine in two studies. It is important to note that some studies included multiple tumor types and anatomical locations. Five studies examined the use of 3DP guides only, five examined 3DP implants only, and four examined the use of 3DP guides and implants when used together. Total Group Outcomes

A meta‐analysis of 10 studies examining OR time found that operations in the 3DP group were a non‐statistically significant 12.2 min shorter (95% CI: −36.18 to 11.79, I 2 = 89%, p = 0.32) (Figure 3A). Out of 12 studies comparing OR time between groups, 6 found a significant reduction in OR time, 5 found no significant difference, and 1 found a significant increase. A meta‐analysis of 11 studies reporting blood loss found a statistically significant overall decrease in mean blood loss of 69.8 mL in the 3DP group (95% CI: −109.6 to −30.02, I 2 = 93%, p = 0.00) (Figure 3B). Out of 12 studies evaluating blood loss, 5 found a significant decrease in the 3DP group, 6 found no difference, and 1 found a significant increase. The studies did not specify if blood loss was estimated or calculated. A meta‐analysis of 14 studies that reported tumor recurrence rates revealed a non‐statistically significant difference between the 3DP and conventional groups (relative risk [RR]: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.59–1.29, I 2 = 0%, p = 0.50) (Figure 3C).

Forest plot (random effects) comparing (A) operating room time, (B) blood loss, and (C) recurrence rates in all studies with accompanying risk ratio calculations and 95% confidence intervals.

Guided Group Outcomes

Four studies compared 3DP to conventional surgical guides. There was no statistically significant difference in meta‐analyses of two, two, and four studies looking at OR time (mean difference [MD]: −13.35, 95% CI: −57.65 to 30.94, I2: 91%, p = 0.55), blood loss (MD: −95.01, 95% CI: −271.77, 81.75, I2: 47%, p = 0.29), and recurrence rate (RR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.54–1.44, I 2: 6%, p = 0.60), respectively. These forest plots can be seen in Figure 4.

Implant Group Outcomes

Five studies compared 3DP to conventional implants. A meta‐analysis of four studies found a statistically significant decrease in OR time (MD: 23.11, 95% CI: −38.16 to −8.01, I2: 18%, p = 0.00) and a statistically significant decrease in blood loss (MD: −214.52, 95% CI: −326.5 to −102.54, I2: 98%, p = 0.00). There was no statistically significant difference between groups for recurrent rate (RR: 1.04, 95% CI: 0.38–2.81, I 2: 0%, p = 0.94). These forest plots can be seen in Figure 5.

Guides and Implant Group Outcomes

Four studies compared the use of 3DP implants and guides together with conventional techniques. There was no statistically significant difference in meta‐analyses of four studies looking at OR time (MD: −7.72, 95% CI: −82.08 to 66.64, I 2: 95%, p = 0.84), blood loss (MD: 110.24, 95% CI: −149.52 to 370.0, I 2: 61%, p = 0.41), and recurrence rate (RR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.23–1.94, I 2: 0%, p = 0.46), respectively. These forest plots can be seen in Figure 6.

MSTS Scores

The MSTS score is graded out of 30 and assesses six functional domains: pain, function, emotional acceptance, supports, walking ability, and gait. Eight studies compared MSTS scores between 3DP and conventional groups. A meta‐analysis of these studies found a statistically significant improvement in scores favoring the 3DP group (MD: 2.17, 95% CI: 1.4–2.95, I 2 = 58%, p = 0.00) (Figure 7).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the use of 3D printing in orthopedic oncology, comparing its impact on surgical outcomes (OR time and blood loss), oncological outcomes (tumor recurrence rate), and functional outcomes (MSTS scores) with conventional techniques. The primary findings were that 3D printing significantly benefits certain aspects of orthopedic oncologic surgery, particularly in reducing OR time, blood loss, and improving postoperative functional scores, without affecting recurrence rate. Significant heterogeneity was driven by differences in tumor types (e.g., long bone vs. pelvic tumors), the application of 3D printing (implants and/or surgical guides), and variations in outcome measures such as MSTS scoring methods and follow‐up durations. Additionally, inconsistencies in surgical techniques, reconstruction approaches, and surgeon experience may have further influenced the results (Table 3).

Minimizing blood loss and operative time remains a priority as surgical advancements evolve. Excessive bleeding is well known to increase the risk of perioperative complications, prolonged hospital stays, and the need for transfusions, while longer procedures raise the likelihood of surgical site infections, anesthesia‐related risks, and overall patient morbidity. In orthopedic oncology, these concerns are even more pronounced. Among cancer patients, anemia has been associated with a 65% increase in mortality [11], and perioperative blood transfusions have been linked to a higher risk of cancer recurrence and overall mortality in carcinomas of the ovary, colorectal, bladder, larynx, pancreatic head, and liver [12, 13, 14]. Although our study showed a statistically significant decrease in blood loss by 69.8 mL, blood loss in large sarcoma pelvic surgeries is often over 1000 mL, which questions the clinical relevance of this reduction. Additionally, studies failed to specify whether blood loss was estimated or calculated, which may introduce bias and skew the results. While this change may not be a strong indication to adopt this technology, it does suggest that 3D printing does not introduce additional intraoperative risk. However, in the implant subgroup, there was a significant decrease in blood loss by 214.42 mL. Additionally, in four studies that evaluated 3D printing in pelvic surgeries, the weighted MD in blood loss was 568 mL less in the 3DP group. These reductions in blood loss are significant enough to reduce transfusion requirements and minimize hemodynamic instability.

For oncology patients, who may already be immunocompromised from chemotherapy or prone to anemia, limiting blood loss is critical in reducing postoperative complications such as poor wound healing, delayed recovery, and increased infection risk. Similarly, shorter operative times help minimize overall surgical stress, decrease post‐anesthesia complications, and improve functional recovery. Due to the complexity of tumor resections, which often require precise bone and soft tissue removal, 3D printing presents a promising approach to improving surgical efficiency. By enabling patient‐specific preoperative planning, enhancing intraoperative precision, and potentially reducing the need for excessive bone resection or prolonged adjustments, 3D printing may help mitigate some of these risks and optimize surgical outcomes in orthopedic oncology.

Although the difference in MSTS scores was statistically significant, it is important to note that there is currently no established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the MSTS scoring system. Without an MCID, it is difficult to determine the extent to which this observed difference translates to a clinically meaningful improvement in patient function.

In addition to the MSTS score, other scales have been developed to assess functional outcomes for more specific pathologies. Two studies compared customized 3D‐printed prostheses with conventional techniques following the resection of periacetabular tumors and used the Harris Hip Score (HHS) to evaluate the postoperative hip function, and both studies found a significant difference favoring the 3D‐printed group [15, 16]. Additionally, the Mayo Wrist Score was used in two studies evaluating functional outcomes after distal radius resection for giant cell tumors [17, 18]. In both studies, preoperative Mayo Wrist Scores were similar between the two groups, however, postoperatively, the 3D‐printed groups had significantly better scores than the conventional groups. Future studies could benefit from region‐specific patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) to better capture functional recovery, pain levels, and quality of life specific to the anatomical site of tumor resection.

These findings highlight the promising role of 3D‐printed implants and surgical guides in improving functional outcomes after tumor resection. While variations in scoring systems and study designs contribute to some heterogeneity, the overall trend suggests that 3D printing enhances postoperative function by taking into account patient‐specific anatomy. Despite the observed benefits, there is a risk of bias when assessing these functional scores because neither patients nor healthcare professionals were blinded to the intervention.

Despite its potential advantages, the widespread implementation of 3D printing in surgery is restrained by two key limitations: cost and time. The included studies showed that costs associated with 3D printing include not only the printer and materials, but also design and manufacturing of the prosthesis, post‐processing, and delivery [15, 19]. The included studies used manufacturing companies located in the same country as the study. Total costs of 3D‐printed pelvic endoprosthesis, including design and patient‐specific instrumentation, ranged from 3000 to 7000 USD, which is approximately 1.5 times as much as conventional methods such as a titanium mesh cage or screw‐rod systems [15, 16, 19, 20]. Compared to conventional methods, the additional financial burden of 3D printing may limit its accessibility in resource‐constrained health settings. The time required for design, manufacturing, and delivery may also delay surgery, which could be suboptimal in oncologic cases requiring timely intervention. Studies reported an average of 2–4 weeks from designing the prosthesis to implant delivery and surgery, with the manufacturing process itself taking 3–7 days [15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 21, 22].

Other technologies are also emerging to improve efficiency in orthopedic oncology, such as navigation, robotic‐assisted surgery, and augmented reality (AR) systems. While 3D printing guides provide surgical precision, they often require more soft tissue dissection, which may alter functional outcomes. In contrast, navigation reduces dissection and offers real‐time precision, though it still has its own limitations. Robotic‐assisted surgery, when coupled with 3D‐printed implants, has the potential to offer greater accuracy and customization. AR systems can also be integrated with these technologies to provide enhanced visual guidance for more precise surgical planning and execution. Beyond orthopedic oncology, 3D printing has shown significant advantages across multiple surgical fields, reinforcing its potential to enhance surgical precision, reduce complications, and improve surgical training [23]. 3D printing has been shown to reduce OR time, blood loss, and radiation exposure in orthopedic trauma, spine, and pediatrics [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. 3D printing patient‐specific models are especially advantageous in pediatric surgery, where the complexity of surgery can vary due to the wide range of unique anatomy in children. Various studies have shown improved operative and functional outcomes in treating pectus excavatum, limb deformities, and skeletal cancer in children [29, 30, 31, 32]. In high‐risk surgical fields such as neurosurgery, cardiac surgery, or emergency medicine, where the training of new surgeons in the operating room is limited by concerns for patient safety, 3D‐printed models have been utilized to help training and enhance surgical precision outside of the OR. Yoo et al. used printed models of congenital heart disease to allow 81 surgeons or trainees to perform simulated surgical procedures under professional supervision [33]. Similar studies have been done for arthroscopy training, pedicle screw placement, airway management, and various other procedures [34, 35, 36, 37].

While this review provides insights into the comparative efficacy of 3D printing in orthopedic oncology, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the included studies were predominantly retrospective and varied in their methodologies, leading to potential biases and a high level of heterogeneity in the data. Additionally, the lack of uniformity in the reporting of certain outcomes, such as postoperative complications, limited the comprehensiveness of the analysis. With regard to outcomes, future studies should specify whether blood loss is estimated or calculated. Information regarding surgical margins, in addition to recurrence rate, would also be helpful to measure oncological safety and surgical precision. Another limitation is the potential for measurement bias in functional outcome assessments, as neither patients nor clinicians were blinded to the intervention and knowledge of the technology used may influence results. The MSTS score is also clinician‐administered, which could raise potential bias. Future studies should incorporate blinding where possible to ensure objective evaluation of postoperative function. Further research comparing specific interventions or procedures is needed to allow conclusions to be drawn with less heterogeneity. Prospective randomized controlled trials that standardize outcome measures, such as recurrence rates or postoperative complications, are also needed to validate the benefits of this technology.

Conclusion

This meta‐analysis suggests that 3D printing in orthopedic oncology may offer advantages in reducing OR time and intraoperative blood loss, while also significantly improving postoperative functional outcomes. Studies utilizing the MSTS Score consistently demonstrated superior functional recovery in the 3D‐printed group. These findings highlight the potential of 3D printing not only to optimize surgical efficiency but also to enhance patient mobility, pain reduction, and overall quality of life after tumor resection. As 3D printing technology becomes more accessible, it is important to validate its long‐term impact on function, implant durability, and patient outcomes through standardized, high‐quality trials. With continued advancements, 3D printing has the potential to become a key tool in personalized surgical planning and reconstruction.

Synopsis

This meta‐analysis evaluates the effectiveness of 3D printing in orthopedic oncology, comparing it to conventional surgical techniques. The study focuses on outcomes such as operative time, blood loss, recurrence rate, and functional scores, with findings showing favorable outcomes in the 3D printing group. The results highlight the potential of 3D printing to improve surgical precision and enhance functional recovery in orthopedic oncology, although substantial heterogeneity across studies limits the generalizability of the findings.
