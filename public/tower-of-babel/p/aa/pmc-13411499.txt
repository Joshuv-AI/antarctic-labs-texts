How Do Parents Experience Overnight Respite? Parent‐Reported Outcomes in Families of Children With Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities
Authors: Sarah S Mire, Erik W Carter, Sara Tomek, Carlee Hollinger, Shannon R Eshman, Melody V Escobar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOvernight respite care can be a lifeline for parents of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities. We explored the multifaceted parent‐reported outcomes of respite and potential demographic variations related to this broad impact.MethodParents (n = 178) reported respite effects on their personal well‐being, spousal relationships, family relationships, spiritual needs and ability to complete other activities, which correspond to five subscales of the novel Respite Impact Survey.ResultsMost parents agreed/strongly agreed with 22 of 23 items, particularly related to rest, recharge and relationships. Univariate ANOVAs revealed differences with small‐to‐moderate effect sizes based on child sex (η 2 = 0.03, 0.05), parent age (η 2 = 0.07, 0.13) and parent education level (η 2 = 0.06).ConclusionParents endorsed many benefits of overnight respite, with few differentiating effects observed within this sample. However, participants represented a relatively homogeneous and comparatively affluent group of parents connected to a single respite provider. Future research should include more racially and socioeconomically diverse samples to better understand how respite experiences may vary across families.Keywords: families, intellectual and developmental disabilities, overnight respite

Introduction

Raising children with intellectual and developmental disabilities can promote positive outcomes for families such as joy, gratitude and strengthened relationships (Beighton and Willis 2017). However, parents also experience unique caregiving challenges. Parents raising children with intellectual and developmental disabilities have elevated stress compared with parents of children without disabilities (McConnell and Savage 2015), and experiencing ongoing stress can undermine parents' emotional and physical well‐being in numerous ways. Heightened caregiving demands, financial strain and social isolation can contribute to parents' stress, burnout and even mental health challenges (Scherer et al. 2019). They must also navigate a complex web of medical, educational and behavioural needs and services throughout their children's lives. These ongoing demands can tax families' emotional, physical, relational and financial resources in ways that require ongoing support. Children with disabilities are disproportionately represented in child welfare systems (Slayter and Springer 2011), highlighting the structural vulnerabilities many families face and underscoring the importance of family‐focused support.

One form of support is respite, which allows temporary relief from caregiving responsibilities and offers opportunities for resting, replenishing physical and emotional resources, and engaging in personal or social activities. Respite services are essential for families of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities, whose care needs often exceed what typical childcare can provide (Whitmore and Snethen 2018). Being able to step away, even briefly, from caregiving responsibilities while knowing that their child is safely cared for is associated with many positive effects on parents and caregivers. Study findings have linked respite with lowered stress (Chan and Sigafoos 2001; Christi et al. 2023), improved mental health (Abrahams and Kleintjes 2023; Christi et al. 2023) and strengthened marriages (Easler et al. 2022; Norton et al. 2016).

Respite care can enhance parent well‐being by providing opportunities for practicing self‐care, pursuing personal goals and maintaining social networks (Dyches et al. 2016; Harper et al. 2013; Whitmore and Snethen 2018). As they restore their physical and emotional resources, parents may be better equipped for their caregiving roles (Avison et al. 2018). Such benefits ripple can through families. Respite care has been linked with lower conflict in the home and better parent–child interactions (Harper et al. 2013; Norton et al. 2016; Whitmore and Snethen 2018). Over time, such positive effects can contribute to long‐term resilience in families and of caregivers (Easler et al. 2022; McConnell and Savage 2015). Unfortunately, a substantial proportion of families who report needing respite do not receive it. Indeed, respite care is the largest unmet family health services need among those raising children who have developmental disabilities (Lindly et al. 2016). The most prominent barriers to respite are structural, including limited availability, high cost, lack of information and inconvenient scheduling. Families of children with more complex conditions, public insurance coverage, and/or older age (i.e., adolescence) are more likely to encounter barriers to respite (Graaf et al. 2022). Together, the documented benefits of respite care and the persistent structural barriers to access underscore the need for more research addressing how respite functions as a resource for families navigating chronic caregiving demands. Further, understanding whether respite experiences vary across child, parent and family characteristics has practical implications for service delivery. Identifying families who may experience respite differently may help tailor supports, inform referral practices and guide policy decisions about equitable access to respite resources.

The Double ABCX model of family adaptation suggests that family outcomes (X) are shaped by stressors (A), available resources (B) and how families perceive those stressors (C) (McCubbin and Patterson 1983). The model is named based on these four interacting components (A, B, C and X), which together help explain how families adapt to stress. Later expansions of this model recognised that stressors, resources and perceptions all accumulate over time and affect longer‐term adaptation. Within this framework, respite may function as a resource that helps families manage caregiving demands and supports positive adaptation (Avison et al. 2018; Christi et al. 2023; Cuzzocrea et al. 2016). Ecological systems theory also highlights that multiple and interrelated systems influence individual development and family dynamics over time (Bronfenbrenner 1977; Bronfenbrenner and Morris 2006). Respite care may benefit the full family because it operates within the mesosystem, bridging the child's immediate environment (microsystem) and broader societal supports (macrosystem). This framework emphasises how important contextual factors are when examining respite impacts.

Although prior research highlights the benefits of respite care for parents of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities, much of this work has focused on a narrow set of outcomes—most commonly reductions in caregiver stress or improvements in marital quality (Christi et al. 2023; Harper et al. 2013; Norton et al. 2016). However, this restricted focus may overlook the multifaceted ways respite may positively affect families, including how it supports parent self‐care, enhances family relationships and promotes resilience. Moreover, it is currently unclear whether respite may affect parents differently, depending on child or family characteristics.

For example, since parents of younger children and those whose children have high support needs or frequent challenging behaviours tend to report higher stress and burnout (Christi et al. 2023; Cuzzocrea et al. 2016; Scherer et al. 2019), they may derive greater benefit from respite care. Studies have also found that caregiving demands may weigh heavier on parents as they age (Namkung et al. 2018; Piazza et al. 2014), when they are unpartnered (Dyches et al. 2016; Whitmore and Snethen 2018) or among those with lower income (Scherer et al. 2019) or education (Christi et al. 2023). Parents from racially and/or ethnically minoritised groups who are raising children with disabilities experience compounded caregiver stress related to facing provider bias, inequitable access to services, structural barriers, cultural stigma surrounding disability and limited culturally relevant supports (Christi et al. 2023; Pearson et al. 2022).

The definitions and delivery of respite can differ across agencies, service populations and mechanisms; though any time a parent spends away from their responsibilities for caregiving is considered respite. Respite might be provided through formal programming or informally via family members; regularly or occasionally; in‐home or in the community. Longer breaks, however, have been found to have greater effects on caregivers compared with shorter breaks (Kirk and Summers 2020). Overnight respite provides extended, uninterrupted time away from caregiving demands, which may create opportunities for deeper rest, meaningful time with partners or other children and reduced decision fatigue. Additionally, differences in child and family characteristics may affect how respite is experienced by caregivers. However, quantitative research about respite outcomes is typically narrowly explored (e.g., effects on one caregiver and/or on a single construct such as stress; Abrahams and Kleintjes 2023; Kirk and Summers 2020).

Guided by Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner 1977; Bronfenbrenner and Morris 2006) and the Double ABCX model of family adaptation (McCubbin and Patterson 1983), we conceptualised overnight respite as an exosystem‐level support that can shape family adaptation processes. Within the Double ABCX framework, families' adaptation to caregiving demands is influenced by available resources and how parents perceive these. Access to structured, dependable overnight respite may support parents' coping and adaptation by alleviating stressors within the caregiving microsystem and adaptation within the broader family system. With this in mind, we explored two research questions:

Method

Recruitment and Participants

Parents were recruited from one non‐profit organisation that provides overnight respite to families via two‐night weekend or one‐night weekday stays. Programming is offered in six states, although most (54.5%) participating families lived in one major metropolitan area in Northern Virginia. This organisation serves families who have one or more child aged 6–22 with an intellectual disability, although many children had additional conditions as well. Inclusion criteria for this study included being a parent or primary caregiver for a child who had received respite care from this organization across a 6‐month period.

A total of 230 parents of children completed our survey. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. To remove dependency when both parents from the same family completed the survey, we randomly selected one parent when both participated. This resulted in a random removal of 52 (22.6%) surveys, resulting in a final sample of 178 parent participants. In this final sample, 82.0% were married or living with a partner, and 83.7% had more than one child. Almost all families (94.9%) had only one child with disabilities who had received respite care. For the remaining nine families who had had either two (n = 8) or three children (n = 1) in respite, we used responses relevant to their oldest child for analyses.

Most (82.5%) parents were between 40 and 59 years old, and most (80.3%) reported White as their race. More than two‐thirds (67.9%) had four or more people in their household. Most (84.8%) parents had a bachelor's degree or higher, and only 14.6% were currently unemployed. More than half (51.1%) reported a combined yearly income of $100 K or greater, although 28.6% of parents did not report income. The majority (75.8%) lived in suburban areas, with 18.5% living in urban and 3.9% in rural locations. Of those with religious affiliation, more than half (56.2%) reported Protestant or Roman Catholic as their present religion, although 58.4% of parents reported being not at all or minimally involved with a faith community. Overall, the sample was relatively homogeneous and disproportionately comprised of White, highly educated, middle‐aged parents, many of whom reported relatively high household incomes. Procedure

We invited 388 families to complete the survey; 230 parents responded. We sent the study invitations directly to families following the organisation's email to families. If families had shared both parents' emails, we sent invitations to both parents separately with up to three reminders at 1, 2 and 3 weeks after the initial invitation. Our email described the study, noted their individual survey responses would not be identifiable to the organisation, and emphasised that respite services would not be affected by their decision whether to participate. Surveys were hosted on the secure, online Qualtrics platform; survey completion took approximately 15 min. We used skip logic within the survey to streamline relevance of questions presented. For example, unmarried parents were not asked about marriage, and those with only one child were not asked about other children. Those who completed the entire survey received a $10 gift card to a choice of three stores. We followed procedures reviewed by our institution's IRB (Ref. no. 2076981).

Measures

Our survey queried child, parent and family demographics, as well as the impact of respite.

Demographic Questions

We asked parents a series of demographic questions about their child, including age and sex. They identified their child's disability or diagnosis types by selecting all relevant options from a list (e.g., ADHD, autism/ASD, hearing impairment; see Table 1). We included an option to write in any additional diagnosis/disability. Parents estimated the amount of support their child needs for daily activities using a 5‐point, Likert‐type scale, ranging from no support = 1 to total support = 5. Similarly, they described the frequency of challenging behaviours such as aggression, self‐injury and property destruction using a 6‐point, Likert‐type scale (never = 1 to multiple times daily = 6). We asked about their child's mobility (i.e., walks independently, walks with help, uses crutches or walker, uses wheelchair) and typical communication style (i.e., talking, pictures or communication device, sign language, gestures/facial expressions/sounds/body movements and no intentional communication; as well as an open‐text option). Finally, we asked them to estimate the number of times their child had participated in the non‐profit's overnight respite program.

We also gathered parent demographics and information about their household. We asked about their relationship to the child (i.e., mother, father and open‐text box), marital status (i.e., single, married/living with partner, divorced/separated, widowed), age range (i.e., 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70+), highest education attained (i.e., some high school, high school diploma or GED, some college, certificate program, associate's/2 year degree, Bachelor's/4 year degree, Master's degree, doctoral degree, other), current job status (i.e., unemployed, part‐time, full‐time, other), total yearly family income (open text) and type of community (i.e., rural, suburban, urban). We used the US Census Bureau's categories for race and ethnicity (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Hispanic/Latino, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White and open‐text box). Participants reported the total number of children in the family and total number of people living in the household. Finally, we asked about present religion (i.e., Protestant, Roman Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, Hindu, Atheist or Agnostic, nothing in particular, other) and level of current involvement in local faith community using a five‐point, Likert‐type scale (not at all = 1 to highly involved = 5).

Respite Impact Survey

The Respite Impact Survey was developed for this study because we were unable to identify an existing measure that captured the breadth of parent‐reported outcomes associated with overnight respite care. Item generation was informed by a narrative review of respite literature documenting parent‐ and family‐reported outcomes related to caregiver stress, emotional well‐being, daily uplifts, marital quality, family relationships, opportunities for rest or practical activities, and broader caregiver and family functioning (e.g., Dyches et al. 2016; Harper et al. 2013; Kirk and Summers 2020; Norton et al. 2016; Whitmore and Snethen 2018). Our goal was not to develop a comprehensive measure of caregiver functioning, but to capture a broad range of parent‐reported outcomes potentially associated with overnight respite participation. Example items in the Respite Impact Survey included ‘Gave me time to recharge’, ‘Improved my relationship with spouse/partner’ and ‘Allowed me to do something fun’. Our team created the 23‐item Respite Impact Survey to capture parents' perceptions about the impact of respite care. All items were measured via six‐point, Likert‐type scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = mildly disagree, 4 = mildly agree, 5 = agree and 6 = strongly agree. Principal components analyses (PCAs) identified subscales related to five areas: personal well‐being, spousal relationships, family relationships, spiritual needs and ability to engage in other activities. For each subscale, the first principal component included significant and similar weightings for each item, indicating an equal weighting of the items to be a valid assumption. We created the final subscale scores by averaging the items. Higher scores reflect a greater level of agreement for each area of potential impact. All subscales, their reliability estimates, and their constituent items are included in Table 3.

Promoting Well‐Being

Ten survey items addressed parents' own well‐being. The PCA examined whether a single linear combination could be computed for these 10 items. The first eigenvalue was equal to λ = 4.93 and accounted for 49.3% of the total variance in the 10 items. Internal consistency for the resulting composite was high (α = 0.87).

Enhancing Spousal Relationships

Three items (α = 0.84) were collected from married or partnered parents only (n = 146) and addressed the change in their relationship with their spouse due to overnight respite care. The PCA yielded a first eigenvalue equal to λ = 2.28 and accounted for 76% of the total variance in the three items.

Improving Family Relationships

Three items addressed parents' perceptions of how respite care impacted relationships within the family and with the other children in the family. Most (83.7%) participants indicated they had more than one child in their family. Using a skip logic function, the two items referring to other children were not presented to those with only one child (n = 29). PCA supported a single component, with the first eigenvalue equal to λ = 1.83, accounting for 61% of the total variance across the three items. Internal consistency for this scale was acceptable given the small number of items (α = 0.68).

Addressing Spiritual Needs

Two items addressed parent‐reported effects of respite care on parents' spiritual needs. The PCA yielded a first eigenvalue equalling λ = 1.75 and accounted for 88% of the total variance of the two items. Internal consistency was high (α = 0.85), though estimates for two‐item subscales warrant cautious interpretation.

Enabling Other Activities

Five items (α = 0.80) addressed the ability of the parent to engage in additional activities both within and outside of the home that they attributed to overnight respite care. For this subscale, the PCA results indicated the first eigenvalue was equal to λ = 2.79 and accounted for 56% of the total variance of the five items.

Data Analysis

For analyses, sex of the child was male or female. Child age was categorised as: 7–10 years, 11–14 years, 15–18 years and 19+ years of age. Level of support the child needs was categorised as: no support or little support, some support, a lot of support and total support. Frequency of child's challenging behaviours was categorised as: never, yearly, monthly, weekly, daily and multiple times daily. Parents' marital status was dichotomised as married or not married. The total number of children in the family was captured as 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 or more children. Parents' race/ethnicity was dichotomised as White or not White because of the low frequency of respondents who endorsed belonging to a racially/ethnically minoritised group. Parent age was categorised as: under 40, 40–49, 50–59 and 60+ years of age. Parent education level was categorised as: less than a college degree (which included high school education or some college), bachelor's degree, master's degree and doctoral degree.

Prior to conducting analyses, normality assumptions were checked; group sizes were created in order to have enough power to detect differences, and homogeneity of variance assumptions were examined. All assumptions were determined to be acceptable for the planned analyses. To examine whether child, parent and family characteristics were associated with mean differences across the five Respite Impact Survey subscales, we conducted a series of univariate ANOVAs (Tables 4 and 5). We tested differences across four child characteristics (sex, age, support needs and frequency of challenging behaviour) and five parent and family characteristics (marital status, the total number of children within the family, parent race/ethnicity, parent age and parent education). When statistically significant differences were detected at α = 0.05, Tukey's post hoc tests were used to examine pairwise differences. We did not make alpha corrections because the analyses were exploratory; instead, we emphasise effect sizes to contextualise the magnitude of observed differences. We conducted analyses using SPSS Version 28.0.

Results

RQ1: What Parent‐Reported Outcomes Are Associated With Overnight Respite Care Across Domains of Personal, Relational, Family and Spiritual Functioning?

Table 3 displays the percentage of parents agreeing with each area of impact, along with means and standard deviations. Overall, parents reported high levels of agreement that access to respite care was associated with positive outcomes. Indeed, the majority of parents indicated they agreed or strongly agreed with 22 of the 23 items. Items receiving the highest level of agreement related to improved mental and emotional health; access to sleep and time to recharge; increased peace and stress reduction; more time with their spouse/partner (if married); more time with their other children (if applicable) and more opportunities to do something fun. For more than 100 parents in the sample, responses for these items were strongly agree.

Lower levels of agreement (though still high) were found for the following items: improved physical health; feeling less isolated; feeling more valued; improved relationships with other children (if applicable); improved spousal relationship and marriage (if married); improved family well‐being and more time for attending to their own spiritual, personal and household needs, as well as for community involvement.

RQ2: How Do Child and Parent Characteristics Relate to Variation in Parents' Appraisal and Adaptation Outcomes Following Access to Overnight Respite Care?

Findings for RQ2 are organised according to child and parent characteristics examined across the five outcome domains. Mean scores for each of the five subscales are displayed in Tables 4 and 5, with significant differences displayed in bold and mean values reported for each level of the demographic factors. Observed effect sizes were generally small, with a small number of moderate effects. Notably, these results should be interpreted within the context of a relatively homogeneous sample with limited variability across several demographic characteristics.

Child Factors Affecting Parents' Reported Outcomes

We conducted a series of one‐way ANOVAs to examine differences in respite impact based on four child factors (i.e., sex, age, support needs and challenging behaviour; see Table 4). Within this sample, no statistically significant differences were detected in parent‐reported personal well‐being across the child demographic factors examined. Similarly, among married or partnered parents, no statistically significant differences were found in perceptions about spousal relationships across child demographic factors. In relation to child and family relationships, parents of female children reported significantly higher levels of agreement than parents of male children. Parents with female children also reported significantly higher levels of agreement regarding their ability to complete activities inside or outside the home. No statistically significant differences were detected across child age, support needs or challenging behaviour for these outcomes. Finally, no statistically significant differences were detected across child demographic factors in ratings related to addressing parents' spiritual needs.

Parent and Family Factors Affecting Parents' Reported Respite Outcomes

We also conducted a series of one‐way ANOVAs examining differences in parents' perceptions of respite impact based on five parent factors (i.e., marital status, total children, race/ethnicity, age, education; see Table 5). No statistically significant differences were detected in parental well‐being ratings across the demographic variables examined. However, compared with those in other age groups, parents aged 40–49 years old reported significantly less agreement regarding the impact of respite care on their spousal relationships. No other parental demographic factors significantly affected ratings in this area.

No statistically significant differences in child and family relationships were detected across the parent and family demographic variables examined. Agreement regarding the perceived impact of respite on parents' spiritual needs was significantly higher for parents within the 50–59 age groups as compared to all other age groups. Additionally, the higher the education level, the less agreement the parents reported with regard to respite care addressing their spiritual needs. No other parental demographic factors significantly impacted agreement in this area. No statistically significant differences in their ability to engage in activities inside or outside of their home were detected across the demographic variables examined.

Discussion

The positive impact of brief respite on families have been documented through several studies, and our findings are consistent with the broader body of literature in this way. The present results extend this literature by describing how parents attribute a range of perceived benefits to overnight respite across multiple domains of family functioning. These findings are also consistent with family quality of life perspectives, which emphasise that family well‐being reflects the interaction of individual needs, family relationships and access to supports and resources (Zuna et al. 2010). In addition to considering multifaceted outcomes, we explored whether parents' ratings of these perceived benefits varied across child (e.g., sex) and parent (e.g., age, education) characteristics, with relatively few differences observed. Importantly, these findings reflect the experiences of parents already connected to a single overnight respite provider and should not be interpreted as representative of all families of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities.

Parents Attributed Many Benefits for Themselves and Their Families to Overnight Respite

Parents reported that accessing respite care was associated with perceived improvements in their own rest, recharge and relationships. Areas where parents reported the strongest agreement all related to the gift of time that respite offered them: time to spend with other family members or to do something fun and refreshing for themselves. From an ecological‐systems theory (Bronfenbrenner and Morris 2006), these perceptions are consistent with the idea that respite operates as a mesosystem resource by connecting families with broader support across home and community environments. Similarly, the Double ABCX theory (McCubbin and Patterson 1983) highlights how access to external supports may shape families' appraisals of stress and coping. In our study, parents' reports suggest they perceived respite as a resource associated with less strain.

Better emotional and mental health, lower stress and increased peace were frequently endorsed as being associated with access to overnight respite. These perceptions are consistent with family stress frameworks, such as the Double ABCX model, which emphasise how access to supportive resources may help parents restore emotional and psychological capacity (McCubbin and Patterson 1983). While some parents endorsed items related to addressing spiritual needs, interpretation of these findings warrants caution. Koenig (2012) defines spirituality by its connection to the sacred or transcendent, which may be expressed through or extend beyond religious beliefs and practices. While respite may afford time for meaningful, self‐directed activities, our data do not allow conclusions about the role of respite in supporting spiritual engagement broadly, particularly given measurement constraints and the limited applicability of these items for a subset of participants. Accordingly, perceptions related to spiritual needs are best viewed as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than as a central outcome of respite care. More detailed and inclusive measurement approaches are needed to clarify whether and for whom respite supports spiritual or meaning‐making activities.

Few Detected Differences Among Perceived Positive Benefits of Respite

Our study contributes to the respite literature by examining whether parents' ratings of perceived respite‐related benefits varied across child and parent characteristics. Within this relatively homogeneous sample, few statistically significant differences were detected across child age, behavioural challenges, support needs, parent marital status, race/ethnicity, education, household income or number of children, and the small number of observed differences generally reflected small‐to‐moderate effect sizes. The absence of significant differences, however, should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent benefits across all families. Ceiling effects, sample homogeneity and limited variability in responses may have reduced sensitivity to subgroup differences, and meaningful differences may not have been detectable in the present analyses. Accordingly, the findings suggest that few differences were observed within this sample rather than that demographic characteristics are unrelated to families' respite experiences more broadly.

Among the small number of detected differences, parents of female children reported higher agreement that they perceived respite improved family relationships and their ability to complete other activities, compared with parents of male children. This finding may reflect gendered pathways to neurodevelopmental disorder diagnosis, as girls may be underdiagnosed or diagnosed later than boys unless they present with greater support needs (Estrin et al. 2021)—characteristics that have also been linked with elevated stress in families raising children with intellectual and developmental disabilities (Christi et al. 2023; Scherer et al. 2019). As a result, families of girls with disabilities may already be navigating greater caregiving needs by the time they receive respite, which could increase families' perceived value and impact of those services. These interpretations are speculative, and future research is needed to examine whether and how child sex interacts with caregiving context and respite use.

Parent age was also associated with modest differences in two domains. Parents aged 40–49 reported lower agreement regarding perceived improvement in spousal relationships, whereas those aged 50–59 reported higher agreement on items related to spiritual needs. These patterns may reflect life‐stage differences, as parents in their 40s often juggle competing demands related to careers, parenting and caregiving for their own children and parents concurrently; this may limit the extent short‐term supports like overnight respite influence marital relationships. Increased endorsement of spiritual needs among older parents may reflect greater engagement in meaning‐making processes later in adulthood (Wink and Dillon 2002).

Finally, parents with higher educational attainment reported less agreement that respite supported their spiritual needs or allowed them to participate more fully in a faith community. This pattern may reflect general differences in coping preferences or religiosity, as higher educational attainment has been associated with lower religious participation in prior research (Pew Research Center 2017; Upenieks and Schieman 2022). However, two aspects of the Respite Impact Survey likely influenced these findings. First, there was not a ‘not applicable’ option, meaning that parents who did not participate in a faith community at all (30% of sample) were forced to rate or skip the item. Second, unlike items tailored to marital status or number of children, the item ‘Allowed me to participate more fully in my church’ was presented to all participants. Notably, this item had the highest skip rate on the survey. Given these measurement constraints, findings related to spiritual needs should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory. Nonetheless, for some families of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities, involvement with faith communities can serve as a resource by fostering social support, belonging and resilience (Pearson et al. 2022).

Although exploratory, these findings indicate that families perceive substantive benefits of respite across multiple domains beyond stress reduction. Policymakers and service systems evaluating respite programs may therefore benefit from considering a broader range of family outcomes when assessing program value and allocating resources. Similarly, providers may consider discussing respite as a multidimensional family support rather than solely as a stress‐management resource.

Limitations and Future Directions

Future research building on our findings would benefit from longitudinal designs, inclusion of respite dose and timing, use of validated or expanded measures with inclusive response options, and sampling strategies that better capture families facing structural barriers to service access. These methodological advances would help address several limitations of the current study. Our sample was comprised of parents with a college degree or higher, and families were relatively affluent. Participants were primarily middle‐aged, White, married mothers. These characteristics reflect families who accessed services through this provider rather than the broader population of families raising children with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Demographic data were not available for the provider's broader service population or for families who did not participate in the study, limiting comparisons to nonrespondents or to the population served. Future studies are needed to examine the impact of overnight respite for other demographic groups underrepresented in our study. Given the compounded stress experienced by single parents (e.g., Dyches et al. 2016) and/or parents from minoritised groups (e.g., Pearson et al. 2022), understanding the impact of respite for these families is especially critical, yet they were not well reflected in our sample. Our participants were also already connected with an organisation that provided respite, but this is not the case for many parents raising children with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Too few providers, funding restrictions, complex eligibility criteria, geographic disparities and providers with insufficient experience serving high support needs are barriers to access. Moreover, in rural communities and among families from diverse cultural communities, respite awareness is limited and professional referral practices are inconsistent (Kirk and Summers 2020). Future research and policy work is needed to expand access. Additionally, the number of overnight respite nights received (i.e., dose) was not incorporated into analyses, which may have limited detection of dose–response patterns, although ceiling effects suggest such differences may have been difficult to observe in this sample.

Our participants also underrepresented fathers. Fathers' experiences, perceptions and coping differ from mothers' (Grebe et al. 2021; Piazza et al. 2014), as well as experiences of stress (Easler et al. 2022). Respite has been associated with similar and positive increases in marital quality among mothers and fathers raising children with intellectual and developmental disabilities (Harper et al. 2013), yet less is known specifically about their overnight respite experiences and warrants further exploration. Expanding research on father‐specific needs and the potential benefits they may derive from respite care remains a critical gap in the literature. This work is situated within a broader mixed methods study, including a qualitative component that used purposive recruitment to increase the representation of fathers, as well as from parents from diverse racial, educational and family backgrounds, helping to broaden whose perspectives are included and inform next steps (Carter et al. 2025). Future work is also needed that focuses on how overnight respite affects siblings, as well as on those with intellectual and developmental disabilities themselves.

Another consideration for interpretation is that our Respite Impact Survey measure was researcher created. We did not find an existing scale that adequately addressed the breadth of impacts typically unexplored in respite research (Kirk and Summers 2020), so our measure was designed to evaluate caregiver outcomes across multiple areas through a set of conceptually informed items. Our preliminary analyses (PCAs) did support internal consistency, suggesting items capture distinct and meaningful aspects of how respite care affects parents. Nevertheless, these analyses represent an initial step in measurement development and do not constitute a comprehensive validation. The constructs represented by these subscales are inherently complex and may not be fully captured by the item set, as suggested by modest variance explained across some subscales and ceiling effects observed in item endorsement, which likely limited variability. The use of a forced‐choice response format without a neutral option may have further contributed to restricted range in responses. The greatest variability and lowest mean score was on the Attending to Spiritual Needs subscale, which was likely influenced by the questions posed and response options offered, as discussed earlier. This was also the one construct that only contained two items, which further decreases our ability to infer beyond the scope of the items posed. We included these items because of our holistic consideration of potential respite impacts, and involvement with faith communities is a resource for many families. Yet, including additional survey items may more fully capture this area. Nonetheless, the goal of better capturing the holistic impact of respite is an important one (e.g., Otsuki et al. 2020) and our study contributes to this growing area by offering a quantifiable and multidimensional approach to assessing parent‐focused outcomes of respite. Such an approach is consistent with calls in the literature to evaluate respite within family systems and ecological frameworks (Kirk and Summers 2020; Woodgate et al. 2024). However, comprehensive validation, including additional reliability and validity evidence, remains needed.

Summary

Parents in this relatively homogeneous and comparatively affluent sample overwhelmingly reported that access to overnight respite care had a strong and positive impact on their own well‐being, their marital and family relationships, and their ability to engage in both everyday activities. Across subscales, they endorsed high levels of perceived benefit, suggesting that even brief breaks from caregiving responsibilities can be deeply restorative. Few child, parent or family characteristics were associated with differential outcomes. However, the absence of detected differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent benefit across all families. At the heart of these findings is the recognition that respite care is not experienced by families in siloed or narrow ways. We asked parents to reflect on a single type of support—overnight respite—and consider how it shaped outcomes that are difficult to disentangle in lived experience. Physical, emotional, relational and spiritual well‐being are interconnected, and parents perceived that their children's respite offered meaningful support across these overlapping dimensions. Because participants were already connected to an established respite provider and represented a relatively narrow demographic profile, these findings should be viewed as descriptive of this sample rather than broadly generalisable to all families of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities.
