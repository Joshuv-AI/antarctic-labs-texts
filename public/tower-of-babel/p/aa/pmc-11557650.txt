Evaluation of Drug–Drug Interaction Potential of Talquetamab, a T-Cell-Redirecting GPRC5D × CD3 Bispecific Antibody, as a Result of Cytokine Release Syndrome in Patients with Relapsed/Refractory Multiple Myeloma in MonumenTAL-1, Using a Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Model
Authors: Marie-Emilie Willemin, Jue Gong, Brandi W Hilder, Tara Masterson, Jaszianne Tolbert, Thomas Renaud, Christoph Heuck, Colleen Kane, Loeckie De Zwart, Suzette Girgis, Xuewen Ma, Daniele Ouellet
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytokine release syndrome, commonly associated with T-cell immunotherapies, was observed with talquetamab, a T-cell-redirecting bispecific antibody, in the phase I/II MonumenTAL-1 study, leading to elevated interleukin (IL)-6, which can suppress cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme activity.ObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate the potential impact of elevated IL-6 on the exposure of co-administered CYP450 substrates for two scenarios: (1) the observed median IL-6 profile and (2) a profile with the highest IL-6 maximum concentration following talquetamab treatment.MethodsA physiologically based pharmacokinetic model was developed based on the literature and simulations performed using observed IL-6 profiles from patients in MonumenTAL-1 who received the subcutaneous recommended phase 2 doses (RP2Ds) of talquetamab: 0.4 mg/kg weekly (QW) and 0.8 mg/kg every other week (Q2W).ResultsMedian IL-6 maximum concentration was 18.4 and 7.1 pg/mL, and maximum IL-6 maximum concentration was 213 and 3503 pg/mL for talquetamab QW and Q2W RP2Ds, respectively. For the median IL-6 profile, no interaction between IL-6 and studied CYP substrates was predicted at either RP2D. The maximum IL-6 profile predicted weak-to-moderate impact on exposure of CYP2C19, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5 substrates and minimal impact on exposure of CYP1A2 substrates at both RP2Ds. Impact on exposure of CYP2C9 substrates was predicted as minimal at QW and minimal-to-weak at Q2W RP2Ds. Time to return to 20% difference from baseline enzymatic activity was predicted as 7 and 9 days after start of cycle 1 for QW and Q2W RP2Ds, respectively.ConclusionsThese modeling results suggest that IL-6 release due to talquetamab-induced cytokine release syndrome has limited impact on potential drug–drug interactions, with the highest likelihood of impact occurring from initiation of talquetamab step-up dosing up to 7 (QW) or 9 (Q2W) days after first treatment dose in cycle 1 and during and after cytokine release syndrome.Plain Language SummaryMultiple myeloma can be treated with immunotherapies such as the bispecific antibody, talquetamab, which binds the novel antigen G protein-coupled receptor family C group 5 member D on multiple myeloma cells and CD3 on T cells and induces T-cell-mediated lysis of multiple myeloma cells. Following talquetamab treatment, many patients experience cytokine release syndrome, an inflammatory immune response where levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-6, are increased. Interleukin-6 can suppress the activity of important enzymes in the body (cytochrome [CYP] P450s) that are involved in drug clearance. This study used a physiologically based pharmacokinetic computer model to investigate the potential impact of increased IL-6 levels on CYP450 enzymes to determine subsequent impact on drugs that are metabolized by CYP450 enzymes. The results showed no predicted interaction between median levels of IL-6 observed in patients and CYP substrates (such as caffeine and omeprazole) with talquetamab. In a simulation that assessed higher (maximum) IL-6 levels observed in patients, the predicted impact of IL-6 was minimal to weak for most of the CYP substrates assessed. The effect on CYP450 enzymatic activity was highest from initiation of talquetamab step-up dosing up to 7–9 days after the first treatment dose of talquetamab. These results suggest that, in this treatment time period, elevated IL-6 levels due to talquetamab-induced cytokine release syndrome have limited impact on drugs that are CYP substrates that may be used in conjunction with talquetamab, but that the concentration and toxicity of these drugs should be monitored and the dose of CYP substrate adjusted as required.

Key Points

Elevated interleukin-6, due to cytokine release syndrome, has limited interaction with cytochrome P450 substrates following talquetamab treatment, and thus limited clinical significance.The highest risk of drug–drug interaction is predicted to be from the start of step-up dosing to 7–9 days after first full treatment dose of talquetamab.During this period, cytochrome P450 substrates should be assessed for toxicity, as changes in concentrations of cytochrome P450 substrates with a low therapeutic index may lead to serious adverse events.

Introduction

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is a non-antigen-specific inflammatory response that can occur following treatment with T-cell immunotherapies, monoclonal antibodies, and bispecific antibodies [1]. During CRS and other chronic and acute inflammatory conditions, such as rheumatoid arthritis and sepsis, elevated levels of cytokines released can alter the activity of cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes [2–4]; the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin (IL)-6 is the most notable suppressor of CYP enzyme activity [4, 5]. Systemic cytokine changes driven by therapeutic proteins (TPs) can result in reduction of CYP enzyme activities, which can in turn impact the exposure of co-medication metabolized by CYP enzymes and generally lead to increased drug exposures [3, 5–8]. Administration of TPs that are cytokine modulators, including the anti-IL-6 receptor monoclonal antibody tocilizumab or the anti-IL-6 monoclonal antibodies sirukumab and siltuximab, have been shown to restore CYP enzyme activity in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, resulting in a consequent decrease in exposure of co-administered therapeutics [6, 9–11].

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have provided guidance for industry to facilitate assessment of the need for drug–drug interaction (DDI) studies for TPs; these guidance documents provide systematic risk-based approaches and include recommendations of DDI assessment tools, such as physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling [12, 13]. A PBPK modeling framework can be used to simulate drug exposure, mechanistically quantify pharmacokinetics, explore DDIs, and inform dosing strategies [14–16]. Many examples where PBPK modeling has been applied to CYP-mediated TP DDIs or disease DDIs have been published [6], including to assess the impact of changes in systemic levels of IL-6 on the exposure of CYP substrates in patients with rheumatoid arthritis [17] and other inflammatory diseases [18], during a cytokine storm in patients with coronavirus disease 2019 [19], and in patients treated with cytokine modulators [20, 21]. In hematologic malignancies, PBPK modeling was used to evaluate the effect of a transient increase in IL-6 levels on CYP450 suppression following treatment with the bispecific antibody blinatumomab, prior to its approval for the treatment of CD19-positive B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia [22, 23], and more recently, following treatment with bispecific antibodies mosunetuzumab, glofitamab, and teclistamab, which are approved treatments for relapsed/refractory follicular lymphoma, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM), respectively [24–30].

Talquetamab is the first approved, off-the-shelf, T-cell-redirecting bispecific antibody that targets both the novel myeloma antigen G protein-coupled receptor family C group 5 member D (GPRC5D) and the CD3 receptor on T cells, inducing T-cell-mediated lysis of GPRC5D-expressing multiple myeloma cells [31]. Talquetamab was approved by the FDA (accelerated approval) and the EMA (conditional marketing authorization) in August 2023 for the treatment of patients with RRMM who have received ≥4 and ≥3 prior lines of therapy, respectively [32, 33]; these approvals were based on the efficacy and safety results of the phase I/II MonumenTAL-1 (NCT03399799/NCT04634552) study of talquetamab in patients with RRMM [34–36]. In MonumenTAL-1, two subcutaneous (SC) recommended phase 2 doses (RP2Ds) were identified: 0.4 mg/kg weekly (QW) or 0.8 mg/kg every other week (Q2W), after two or three step-up doses, respectively [34]. As of 17 January, 2023, at the last reported cutoff for these data, median follow-up in patients with RRMM who had no prior exposure to T-cell-redirection therapies was 18.8 and 12.7 months in the 0.4-mg/kg QW and 0.8-mg/kg Q2W cohorts, and overall response rates were 74.1% and 71.7%, respectively [34]. Because of the T-cell-redirecting mechanism of action of talquetamab, CRS was commonly observed in MonumenTAL-1; most CRS events were grade 1 or 2 and occurred during initial step-up doses and the first full treatment dose [34, 35].

In the current analysis, the impact of IL-6 serum levels observed after talquetamab treatment in MonumenTAL-1 was assessed on predicted exposures of the CYP substrates caffeine (CYP1A2), S-warfarin (CYP2C9), omeprazole (CYP2C19), midazolam (CYP3A4 and CYP3A5), cyclosporine (CYP3A4 and CYP3A5), and simvastatin (CYP3A4) using a PBPK modeling approach. This work provides guidance to healthcare professionals on co-medication with talquetamab during step-up dosing and first treatment doses, when CRS is most likely to occur.

Methods

Clinical Study Design

The study design and detailed methods of the phase I portion of MonumenTAL-1 have been previously reported [34, 36]. Patients enrolled in the phase II portion of the study were adults with RRMM per International Myeloma Working Group criteria [37] and documented progressive disease per International Myeloma Working Group 2016 criteria [38] or within 12 months of the last line of therapy and who had received three or more prior lines of therapy, including a proteasome inhibitor, an immunomodulatory drug, and an anti-CD38 antibody [36]. The current analysis was conducted using data from patients in phase I and II of MonumenTAL-1 who received either of the two SC RP2Ds of talquetamab: 0.4 mg/kg QW or 0.8 mg/kg Q2W.

Step-up doses in MonumenTAL-1, implemented to mitigate CRS events, were administered prior to the first treatment dose of talquetamab for both dosing schedules; patients received 0.01 and 0.06 mg/kg for step-up doses 1 and 2 for both dosing schedules and 0.3 mg/kg as step-up dose 3 in the 0.8-mg/kg Q2W schedule. Cycle 1 (i.e., the first treatment dose) started a minimum of 96 h (0.4-mg/kg QW cohort) and a minimum of 168 h (0.8-mg/kg Q2W cohort) after the first step-up dose, and peak IL-6 concentrations in cycle 1 were observed 24–48 h (0.4-mg/kg QW cohort) and 48 h (0.8-mg/kg Q2W cohort) after the start of cycle 1 (i.e., a minimum of 120–144 and 216 h, respectively, after the first step-up dose). Administration of tocilizumab, an anti–IL-6 receptor monoclonal antibody, was advised for the treatment of grade ≥2 CRS and permitted for grade 1 CRS.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonization. The study protocol, amendments, and relevant documents were approved by the local independent ethics committee or institutional review board at each study site. All patients provided written informed consent.

IL-6 PBPK Model and Simulation Design

The PBPK model of IL-6 used in this analysis has been previously published [30]. The modeling and simulations were performed using SimCYP version 21 (SimCYP Ltd, a Certara company, Sheffield, UK). Briefly, the IL-6 model was reconstructed using information in the literature [9, 20]. Interleukin-6 in the body was modeled via an intravenous infusion, with the dosing schedule adjusted to recover the observed IL-6 kinetics profile. Distribution was modeled by a minimal PBPK model, and elimination was modeled non-mechanistically. Other than CYP1A2, for which an induction effect was modeled per clinical data, a suppression effect was modeled for all studied CYP enzymes [20]. The effect of IL-6 on gut enzymatic activity was accounted for by using the same stepwise approach as described by Willemin et al. [30]. The simulations reported in this article were performed in healthy volunteers; model performance of IL-6 PBPK alone and its impact on CYP substrates were evaluated and fully verified using data from the literature [9, 20, 21, 23], as previously described [30]. The robustness of the model and resulting predictions provided confidence in the current IL-6 model to predict the prospective impact of serum IL-6 exposure on the studied CYP substrates. Drug–drug interaction liability was assessed toward caffeine, S-warfarin, omeprazole, midazolam, simvastatin, and cyclosporine. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by decreasing the value for concentration that supports half-maximal induction or suppression of the studied CYP enzymes by ten-fold, in agreement with EMA guidelines [39], to determine the influence of this parameter (issued from in vitro experiments [40]) on the exposures of selected CYP450 substrates. For CYP3A4 substrates, the sensitivity analysis was performed only on the CYP3A4 substrate for which the highest DDI liability was predicted. Corresponding compound files from SimCYP version 21 were used.

Prospective Simulations

Observed IL-6 data from patients in MonumenTAL-1 who did not receive any tocilizumab in cycle 1 and from patients who received tocilizumab after the highest IL-6 maximum concentration (Cmax) occurred were collected; in the latter case, only IL-6 concentrations before tocilizumab administration were retained to ensure that predicted changes in CYP enzymatic activity, at either IL-6 profile, were not affected by modulation of IL-6 levels by tocilizumab. In total, 100 (0.4-mg/kg QW cohort) and 95 (0.8-mg/kg Q2W cohort) patients who experienced CRS were included in the analyses. Statistics were performed on the IL-6 concentration–time profile of these patients, and only two kinetics profiles (corresponding to two scenarios) per dosing schedule were retained to assess the DDI liability of IL-6 on the studied CYP substrates.

Scenario 1 was the median IL-6 concentration per time event up to cycle 1 day 15. Scenario 2 represented the highest individual observed concentration of IL-6 among all patients occurring during cycle 1, before any administration of tocilizumab, following treatment with either of the talquetamab RP2Ds (representing the maximum DDI liability that can be expected). As described in Willemin et al. [30], this approach, considering two scenarios for the IL-6 kinetics profile separately (median and highest peak of serum IL-6 concentrations), was used to account for the very high observed inter-individual variability in the IL-6 kinetics profile in the selected dataset, which is not adequately captured by one single simulation in the developed IL-6 PBPK model. Consequently, scenarios 1 and 2 represent the median and maximum risk, respectively, in terms of DDI liability due to IL-6. It should be noted that for the 0.8-mg/kg Q2W cohort, the patient presenting the highest Cmax of IL-6 (scenario 2) among all patients received tocilizumab on day 4 after the first step-up dose; therefore, only IL-6 concentrations up to 72 h after the first step-up dose were retained in the analysis.

Five trials of 100 patients (aged 20–50 years, 50% female) were simulated for each scenario. The IL-6 dosing schedule was adjusted to recover the observed IL-6 kinetics profile for each of the scenarios. As described by Willemin et al. [30], two simulations (runs 1 and 2) were performed to account for the IL-6 effect on gut enzymatic activity, which is not captured by the software. In run 1, simulations of the IL-6 positive substrate probe were performed until steady state or a return to baseline enzymatic activity was reached, at which time the maximum impact on enzymatic activity in the liver was noted. In the population section, the abundance of the corresponding enzyme in the intestine and colon were reduced by the percentage of remaining activity that was observed in the liver. Run 2 was then performed by repeating run 1 with the adjusted population. The ratio of mean Cmax or mean area under the curve (AUC) from time zero to infinity (AUCinf) from the inhibition arm of run 2 was then calculated over the control arm in run 1, giving the overall impact of IL-6 on exposure of the probe. As omeprazole offers mechanism-based inhibition toward CYP2C19, the impact of IL-6 toward CYP2C19 activity for transient peak IL-6 concentrations was first assessed with mechanism-based inhibition turned off. In run 2, mechanism-based inhibition was then turned back on, with changes in CYP2C19 abundance due to IL-6 then applied to the intestine and colon [30]. Mean (minimum–maximum) exposure ratios from the five trials were reported for scenarios 1 and 2. Categorization of DDI liability was then based on both the mean Cmax and mean AUC ratios. In addition, the time to reach maximum change in CYP activity due to IL-6 and return to only a 20% difference from baseline enzymatic activity were evaluated, with the start of cycle 1 as the reference; a 20% difference from baseline enzymatic activity was considered a reasonable cutoff at which impact on exposures would be considered low.

Results

Simulation of IL-6 Kinetics

The simulated and observed IL-6 systemic concentration profiles from patients enrolled in MonumenTAL-1 until cycle 1 day 15 are shown for the 0.4-mg/kg QW and 0.8-mg/kg Q2W cohorts in Figs. 1 and 2, respectively. Baseline levels of IL-6 were captured by the model, in addition to the multiple peaks of IL-6, including after the two and three step-up doses in the 0.4-mg/kg QW and 0.8-mg/kg Q2W cohorts, respectively. Observed data (median IL-6 kinetic profile and IL-6 kinetic profile of the patient with the highest Cmax) were well captured overall. For both dosing schedules, the observed variability in the IL-6 kinetics profile was not well captured by the model, justifying the separate simulation to recover the observed PK profile using the patient with the highest IL-6 Cmax (i.e., scenario 2).

Predictions with IL-6 as a Perpetrator of CYP Substrates

The median IL-6 kinetic profile (scenario 1) with an IL-6 Cmax of 18.4 pg/mL (0.4 mg/kg QW) and 7.07 pg/mL (0.8 mg/kg Q2W) following talquetamab treatment was predicted to result in no interaction between IL-6 and CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4, or CYP3A5 substrates at either RP2D (highest mean AUC ratio of 1.13 [0.4 mg/kg QW] and 1.08 [0.8 mg/kg Q2W] for both omeprazole and simvastatin; Table 1). For both RP2Ds, the IL-6 profile with the highest Cmax (scenario 2), with an IL-6 Cmax of 213 pg/mL (0.4 mg/kg QW) and 3503 pg/mL (0.8 mg/kg Q2W), predicted minimal interaction between IL-6 and CYP1A2 (with mean AUC ratios of 0.83 and 0.82, respectively), and a weak-to-moderate inhibition of CYP2C19, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5 substrates (with mean AUC ratios that ranged from 1.82 for midazolam to 2.14 for omeprazole [0.4 mg/kg QW] and from 2.49 for midazolam to 3.41 for omeprazole [0.8 mg/kg Q2W]). A similar pattern was observed for the CYP2D6 substrate (dextromethorphan); no interaction was predicted with the median IL-6 kinetic profile (0.4 mg/kg QW), and a weak inhibition was predicted with the highest IL-6 Cmax profile (0.8 mg/kg Q2W) [data not shown]. Among the three studied CYP3A4 substrates, simvastatin was the CYP3A4 substrate for which the predicted change in exposure was the highest. A minimal impact on exposure of CYP2C9 was predicted at the 0.4-mg/kg QW RP2D (mean AUC ratio 1.21), whereas a minimal-to-weak inhibition on exposure of CYP2C9 was predicted for the 0.8-mg/kg Q2W RP2D (mean AUC ratio 1.46). The extent of the interaction with the IL-6 profile with the highest Cmax was stronger with the 0.8-mg/kg Q2W dosing schedule compared with the 0.4-mg/kg QW dosing schedule, considering the approximately 16-fold difference in terms of IL-6 Cmax.

For the median IL-6 profiles, the maximum change in enzymatic activity for the studied CYP substrates occurred 2–3 days (0.4 mg/kg QW [Fig. 3a; Table 3]) and 3 days (0.8 mg/kg Q2W [Fig. 4a; Table 3]) after the first full treatment dose. For the profiles with the highest IL-6 Cmax, the return to 20% difference from baseline enzymatic activity was predicted as occurring up to 7 days (0.4 mg/kg QW [Fig. 3b; Table 3]) and up to 9 days (0.8 mg/kg Q2W [Fig. 4b; Table 3]) after the first full treatment dose of talquetamab. For simvastatin and omeprazole, the two substrates most sensitive to DDI, the return of enzymatic activity to 20% difference from baseline after the highest IL-6 Cmax observed following 0.4-mg/kg QW dosing resulted in predicted AUCinf DDI ratios of 1.44 and 1.46, respectively, corresponding to weak inhibition. Following 0.8-mg/kg Q2W dosing, predicted AUCinf DDI ratios for simvastatin and omeprazole were 1.52 and 1.74, respectively, also corresponding to weak inhibition. Therefore, for simvastatin and omeprazole, considering the scenario with the highest IL-6 Cmax following 0.8-mg/kg Q2W dosing, the predicted interaction changed from weak-to-moderate inhibition when the maximum change in enzymatic activity was observed, to weak inhibition when enzymatic activity returned to a 20% difference from baseline.

Discussion

Cytokine release syndrome is commonly observed following treatment with several T-cell immunotherapies, including bispecific antibodies, and results in elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, which can suppress CYP enzymatic activity [1–4]. Although the incidence and severity of CRS vary across T-cell immunotherapies [41–43], tocilizumab is commonly used as a supportive measure in the management of CRS, including in patients with RRMM during treatment with the bispecific antibodies talquetamab or teclistamab [36, 41, 44]. The PBPK modeling and simulation results presented here suggest that DDI effects of IL-6 on CYP enzymatic activities following talquetamab treatment in MonumenTAL-1 have limited clinical significance. The median IL-6 kinetic profile used in the simulations predicted no interaction between IL-6 and exposure of CYP substrates at either RP2D. Considering the profile of the patient with the highest IL-6 Cmax, minimal interaction between IL-6 and the CYP1A2 substrate and weak-to-moderate inhibition of CYP2C19, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5 substrates were predicted for both RP2Ds. A minimal impact on exposure of the CYP2C9 substrate was predicted at the 0.4-mg/kg QW RP2D, whereas a minimal-to-weak inhibition on exposure of the CYP2C9 substrate was predicted for the 0.8-mg/kg Q2W RP2D. Maximum change in exposure for the studied substrates occurred 2–4 days after the start of cycle 1 (i.e., first full treatment dose), and a return to 20% difference from baseline enzymatic activity took up to 7 days (0.4 mg/kg QW) and 9 days (0.8 mg/kg Q2W) after the start of cycle 1 for the patient with the highest IL-6 Cmax. Although minimal, weak, or moderate impacts were observed across CYP substrates and up to 7 days (0.4 mg/kg QW) or 9 days (0.8 mg/kg Q2W) after the first full treatment dose in cycle 1, changes in CYP activity had no influence on the safety profile of patients in MonumenTAL-1; as of 16 May, 2022, at the last reported cutoff for these data, comparable levels of CRS were observed in the 0.4-mg/kg QW (79.0%) and 0.8-mg/kg Q2W (72.4%) dosing cohorts [35]. The median time to CRS onset after the most recent dose of talquetamab and median duration of CRS were also comparable across the RP2Ds (both 2.0 days) [35].

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling has previously been applied to a CYP-mediated TP-DDI evaluation to help predict observed changes in the exposure of CYP substrates as a function of changes in systemic levels of IL-6 [17–21, 23–25, 45]. In a PBPK modeling study with the B-cell maturation antigen × CD3 bispecific antibody teclistamab that used the same CYP substrates, very similar results were reported as those observed in the current study [30]. With teclistamab at a single dose of 1.5 mg/kg QW (the RP2D from the MajesTEC-1 study) [44, 46], there was no inhibition of any CYP substrates using the mean IL-6 kinetic profile (Cmax 21 pg/mL), which is consistent with talquetamab using the median IL-6 kinetic profile (Cmax 18.4 pg/mL and 7.1 pg/mL at 0.4 mg/kg QW and 0.8 mg/kg Q2W, respectively). The mean AUC ratio for the kinetic profile of the patient with the highest IL-6 Cmax in the teclistamab modeling study (288 pg/mL) predicted a minimal-to-moderate inhibition, which is consistent with mean AUC ratios for talquetamab and also represented minimal-to-moderate inhibition of all studied CYP substrates. These results are in line with outcomes of recent similar PBPK modeling analyses of IL-6-mediated DDIs, in which changes in CYP-mediated clearance were predicted to generally be less than two-fold, a threshold that has been described as rarely producing clinically significant DDIs [24, 25, 30, 47].

Talquetamab can cause increases in the expression of other cytokines (and cytokine receptors) in addition to IL-6, including IL-10 and IL-2 receptors [32]. The current analysis did not account for the impact of these additional cytokines on CYP activity, similar to recently published PBPK studies [19, 23–25, 30]. Interleukin-2 (but not IL-10) can suppress CYP enzymatic activity in vitro, although the effects are less than those observed with IL-6 [2, 47]. No information is available for IL-2 receptors. There are no conclusive clinical data demonstrating that cytokines other than IL-6 have a direct effect on CYP activity [47]. Nevertheless, based on limited data, it can be concluded that synergistic effects of IL-6 with other cytokines would likely lead to a similar effect on CYP activities as IL-6 alone [47].

The PBPK analysis reported here was included in the filing of talquetamab in 2023. The FDA considered this modeling approach inadequate to support the magnitude and duration of effect of elevated IL-6 on CYP activities, as an exposure–response relationship between plasma IL-6 levels and changes in CYP activities has not yet been established. In addition, there is a paucity of data regarding interaction effects on CYP substrates following transient increases in IL-6 and of PBPK analyses to predict the time course of effect of IL-6 on CYP activity [48]. Instead, the FDA based their evaluation of the potential risk of DDIs using different methodology, by looking at the available clinical data in the literature from the transient peak of IL-6 and understanding the postulated mechanisms of IL-6-mediated CYP modulation and estimated time of recovery, in addition to the IL-6 kinetic profile observed after talquetamab administration [48]. The FDA recommended to prolong the monitoring period for the potential risk of drug interaction to 14 days. The FDA’s position toward PBPK modeling of DDI evaluation due to IL-6 for talquetamab is in line with their position on similar modeling work performed for blinatumomab [49], mosunetuzumab [50], and glofitamab [51] but not for teclistamab [52] (recently summarized by Zhang and Zhao [53]), although the IL-6 profile signature and modeling approach were similar for both teclistamab and talquetamab. For teclistamab, the FDA stated that the association of modeling predictions and clinical data enabled support of the magnitude and duration of effect of elevated IL-6 on CYP activities [52]. For talquetamab, the FDA made a recommendation using only data available in the literature on IL-6 and the IL-6 signature after talquetamab administration [48]. Consequently, the statement included in the US label is more conservative than the predicted results of the analyses presented here, stating that “increased exposure of CYP substrates is more likely to occur from initiation of talquetamab step-up dosing up to 14 days after the first treatment dose and during and after CRS”. Interestingly, the EMA seemed to follow the same conclusions as our model-predicted results, and only requested additional analysis that are shared in this article. In the European label, it is stated that “the highest risk of drug-drug interaction is expected to occur from initiation of talquetamab step-up phase up to 9 days after the first treatment dose and during and after CRS”, which is in line with the outcomes of this PBPK modeling analysis. Therefore, acceptance of PBPK modeling approaches to address DDI liability in the context of CRS following bispecific antibody treatment appears dependent on the health authority.

Conclusions

Interleukin-6 release due to CRS following talquetamab treatment at either RP2D has limited interaction with CYP substrates and is predicted to have limited clinical significance. In many cases, there is unlikely to be any interaction between IL-6 and CYP substrates (based on the median IL-6 Cmax data) and only in patients with very high IL-6 levels do these interactions reach weak-to-moderate inhibition. The highest likelihood of DDI in these patients is predicted to be from initiation of talquetamab step-up dosing up to either 7 days (0.4 mg/kg QW) or 9 days (0.8 mg/kg Q2W) after the start of cycle 1. During this period, the toxicity or concentration of agents that are CYP substrates should be monitored, as changes in concentrations of substrates with a low therapeutic index may cause serious adverse reactions, and the dose of the concomitant CYP substrate should be adjusted as required. From a clinical pharmacology perspective, these findings may be of interest in terms of providing guidance around co-medication during talquetamab treatment.
