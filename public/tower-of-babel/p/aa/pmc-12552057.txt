Clinical Spectrum, Pathology, and Mechanisms of Anti-LGI4 Antibody–Positive Autoimmune Nodopathy
Authors: Xu Zhang, Jun-Ichi Kira, Akira Yokote, Ayako Sakoda, Ken-Ichiro Yamashita, Mikio Mitsuishi, Hidenori Ogata, Takumi Tashiro, Noriko Isobe, Ryota Sato, Takashi Kanda, Toshihiro Ide, Haruki Koike, Takayasu Mishima, Yoshio Tsuboi, Masaki Kobayashi, Kazuo Kitagawa, Yukihiro Namihira, Yusuke Ohya, Yuko Fukata, Masaki Fukata, Tomohiro Imamura, Guzailiayi Maimaitijiang, Yuri Nakamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to elucidate the clinical spectrum and mechanisms of autoimmune nodopathy (AN) with autoantibodies against leucine-rich repeat leucin-rich glioma inactivated 1 (LGI) family member 4 (LGI4) localized at juxtaparanodes and satellite glia in the dorsal root ganglion.MethodsWe developed a live cell-based assay for LGI4-immunoglobulin (Ig) G and surveyed 131 patients with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy. LGI4-IgG, anti-LGI4 antibody–negative CIDP-IgG, or healthy control (HC)-IgG were applied to Schwann cells, and cell proliferation was assayed using 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine labeling. Quantitative real-time PCR was used to assess the expression of Krox20 and Prx, both of which independently control peripheral nerve myelination. LGI4-IgG or HC-IgG was intraneurally injected into mouse sciatic nerves, and the effects were immunohistochemically and morphometrically assessed.ResultsEight anti-LGI4 antibody–positive (LGI4+) patients were identified; dominant tissue-binding IgG subclasses were IgG4 in 4 patients and IgG2 and IgG3 in 2 patients each. Patients had a relatively old onset age (median 72 years, range 41–90 years). Four patients had acute/subacute monophasic courses, and 4 had chronic progressive/relapsing courses. Predominant symptoms/signs were motor weakness (8 cases), muscle atrophy (4), deep and superficial sensory impairment (8 and 7, respectively), Romberg sign (4), and hand/finger tremor (5). CSF showed extremely high protein levels (median 253 mg/dL). Three chronic cases had nerve hypertrophy. IVIg and efgartigimod were partially (7/7 patients) and markedly (1/1 patient) effective, respectively. Notably, 1 chronic patient with predominant tissue-binding LGI4-IgG2 but without LGI4-IgG4 showed a moderate reduction in myelinated fibers with endoneurial edema, numerous onion bulbs, and few inflammatory cell infiltrates in the biopsied sural nerve. Electron microscopy demonstrated onion bulb formations around the axons and subtle detachment of myelin terminal loops from axonal membranes in the paranodes. In Schwann cell culture, proliferation was significantly higher, and Krox20 but not Prx mRNA levels were lower after incubation with LGI4-IgG from chronic (but not acute-onset) cases compared with HC-IgG incubation. With intraneural injection, both LGI4-IgG4 and LGI4-IgG2 deposited mainly at the nodes extending toward the paranodes and caused nodal/paranodal alterations.DiscussionLGI4+ AN manifests as acute/subacute monophasic and chronic progressive/relapsing diseases, sometimes exhibiting nerve hypertrophy with onion bulbs through nonmyelinating Schwann cell proliferation.

Introduction

Demyelinating neuropathy with autoantibodies against nodal/paranodal proteins, such as neurofascin 155 (NF155), NF186, contactin-1 (CNTN1), and contactin-associated protein 1 (CASPR1),1-10 is now termed autoimmune nodopathy (AN) and is considered distinct from chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) because of its unique features, including deep sensory impairment leading to sensory ataxia, tremor, distal dominant motor weakness, and atrophy; poor response to IV immunoglobulin (Ig); extremely high CSF protein concentrations; and peripheral nerve hypertrophy.1-10 Given that the IgG subclass of these antibodies is predominantly IgG4,1-10 the characteristic features of AN are assumed to be attributable to the special properties of IgG4.11,12 However, some features of AN—particularly, nerve hypertrophy—are difficult to attribute to the unique functions of IgG4. Moreover, in panNF antibody–positive (panNF+) AN, acute monophasic cases frequently harbor panNF-IgG1–3.13,14

We recently identified a novel autoantibody against leucine-rich repeat LGI family member 4 (LGI4), localized at juxtaparanodes and satellite glia in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG),15 in patients with CIDP seronegative for known nodal/paranodal antibodies.16 Although all 4 identified anti-LGI4 antibody–positive (LGI4+) patients showed acute-to-subacute onset masquerading as Guillain-Barré syndrome or relapsing course, the aforementioned AN characteristics were also observed.16 We, therefore, proposed that LGI4+ neuropathy be considered as a form of AN, despite its unknown neuropathology.16

In this study, we have established a live cell-based anti-LGI4 antibody assay using a stable transformant to expand the clinical spectrum of LGI4+ AN, adding new cases with chronic progressive courses, marked peripheral nerve hypertrophy, and typical onion bulb formation. We then experimentally studied the in vitro and in vivo effects of LGI4-IgG with distinct subclasses on cultured Schwann cells and peripheral nerves, respectively.

Methods

Participants

Of 113 consecutive patients with CIDP (76 male, 37 female) seronegative for anti-NF155/-CNTN1 antibodies from the Department of Neurology, Kyushu University, and who fulfilled the definite electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP from the European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society,10 4 patients were seropositive for anti-LGI4 antibodies16 and were enrolled in this study. Furthermore, 18 consecutive patients with CIDP (15 male, 3 female) from the Department of Neurology, Fukuoka Central Hospital (a tertiary neurology hospital), who met the aforementioned criteria were also enrolled and evaluated for anti-NF155, anti-NF186, and anti-CNTN1 antibodies using flow cytometry (FCM) with Δmean fluorescence intensity (ΔMFI) (eMethods)4,9; anti-CASPR1 antibodies by ELISA17,18; and anti-LGI4 antibodies, as described in the following section. In addition, 127 controls (45 male, 82 female), with a mean age of 48.6 ± 17.8 (range 13–87) years, were enrolled to undergo anti-LGI4 antibody assay. These included 35 healthy controls (HCs) and 92 patients with other neurologic diseases who were examined at Kyushu University Hospital and Fukuoka Central Hospital, as described in our previous study.16

Cell-Based Indirect Immunofluorescence Assay for Anti-LGI4 Antibodies

Human embryonic kidney 293T cells, maintained in a medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), were transfected with both an LGI4-FLAG plasmid (ID RC219293, OriGene Technologies, Inc.) and a disintegrin and metalloprotease domain–containing protein 22 (ADAM22)-HA plasmid (constructed by MF and YF), or with the ADAM22-HA plasmid only, using the Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as described previously.16 The transfected cells were cultured in the selection medium containing 1 mg/mL of G418 (Life Technologies). When single colonies formed an adequate size for clonal expansion, individual colonies were picked up using a sterile cloning cylinder and scaled up to larger volumes. Transformed cell lines stably coexpressing human LGI4 and ADAM22 were used for subsequent cell-based assay (CBAs). For fixed CBA to detect surface-bound human IgG, transfected cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 minutes and blocked with 20 mg/mL of bovine serum albumin for 60 minutes. Next, cells were incubated with each participant's serum IgG (1:100 dilution) and anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, 1:1,000 dilution) at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by staining with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated or 594–conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For live CBA, the cells were blocked with 10% FBS for 10 minutes at room temperature. Next, cells were incubated with the patients' serum (1:20 dilution) and anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, 1:250 dilution) at 4°C for 1 hour. Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated or 594–conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used as the secondary antibodies. The cells were washed 3 times with 2% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) after incubation with the aforementioned primary and secondary antibodies and were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 5 minutes. Images were captured using an A1 confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon) or a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE).

LGI4-IgG Subclass Analysis

An indirect immunofluorescence DRG tissue-based assay (DRG-TBA) was used for IgG subclass analysis in all 8 patients who were positive for anti-LGI4 antibodies in fixed and/or live CBA.16 Lumbar DRG was removed from mice, fixed in 10% formalin, and processed into 3-µm paraffin sections. Indirect immunofluorescence assays (IFAs)were performed on these specimens using serum samples from patients (1:60 dilution). Alexa Fluor 488–labeled anti-human IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as the secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) for LGI4-IgG, and IgG subclass profiles were determined using secondary fluorescein isothiocyanate–conjugated mouse monoclonal anti-human IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4 antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich). IgG subclass profiles were also determined with live CBA when serum samples of LGI4-IgG–positive patients were available, using secondary fluorescein isothiocyanate–conjugated mouse monoclonal anti-human IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4 antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich).

Histopathologic and Electron Microscopic Analyses of the Biopsied Sural Nerve

Biopsied sural nerve specimens from 1 patient with LGI4+ AN (Case 7) were evaluated histopathologically. At the time of biopsy (4.5 years after onset), sural nerve sensory nerve action potentials were evoked. Biopsied sural nerve specimens were fixed with 2.1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4), washed with 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, and postfixed with 1% OsO4. After dehydration, the specimens were embedded in epoxy resin to make semithin sections (800 nm), which were stained with toluidine blue. For electron microscopy, the epoxy resin–embedded specimens were cut into 70-nm-thick ultrathin sections and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, as previously described.19

Preparation and Immunostaining of Schwann Cells

A Schwann cell line (IFRS1, COSMO BIO Co., Ltd.) maintained in medium was seeded onto eight-well chamber slides (5.5 × 104 cells/well) 24 hours before immunostaining. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 minutes. After washing, cells were permeabilized with ice-cold 100% methanol at −20°C for 10 minutes, blocked with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% FBS, and incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-LGI4 (Novus Biologicals, 1:100 dilution) or rabbit polyclonal anti-ADAM22 (Abcam, 1:200 dilution) antibodies, followed by staining with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated or 594–conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured using an A1 confocal laser scanning microscope or a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope.

IgG Purification

Purification of IgG from human serum samples was performed using Protein G HP SpinTrap columns (GE Healthcare) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cell Proliferation Assay

Cell proliferation was assayed using a 5-bromo-2′-deoxy-uridine (BrdU) Labeling and Detection Kit I (Roche). Purified serum IgG from patients and controls was heated to 56°C for 30 minutes to inactivate complements. Next, plated rat Schwann cells were incubated with diluted IgG (1:10) overnight. The cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′-6-diamidino-2-phenylindole in the mounting medium (Vector Laboratories), as described in the manufacturer's manual. Percentages of BrdU-positive nuclei were calculated from 5 images of separate fields that were chosen randomly for each sample.

Evaluation of the Effects of Human Serum IgG on Schwann Cells

Serum IgG was obtained from 5 patients with LGI4+ AN (Cases 2, 3, 5, 7, and 8; LGI4-IgG), 7 patients with LGI4- CIDP (CIDP-IgG), and 7 HCs (HC-IgG). Samples were heated, diluted 1:10 in a cell culture medium, and added to plated rat Schwann cells overnight (as mentioned previously in Methods). Thereafter, total RNA was extracted from small interfering RNA-treated rat Schwann cells using a SuperPrep Cell Lysis & RT Kit for qPCR (#SCQ-101, TOYOBO). RNA was reverse transcribed using Fast SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subjected to quantitative real-time PCR analysis for E3 SUMO-protein ligase EGR2 (Krox20) and periaxin (Prx) expression in a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Genes of interest were compared and expressed as ratios relative to the reference gene, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primer sequences were the same as previously reported.16

Intraneural Injections of Human Serum IgG

Anesthetized male C57BL/6 mice (9–13 weeks old) were intraneurally injected twice (with a 1-week interval between injections) with 5 µL of purified IgG (7 mg/mL) from 3 patients with LGI4+ AN (Cases 2, 7, and 8), 1 patient with NF155-IgG4+ AN (predominant IgG subclasses: IgG4 > IgG1 > IgG2, ΔMFI of NF155-IgG4 using FCM = 55),4 and 1 HC. Intraneural injections were performed under anesthesia with isoflurane by dissecting the sciatic nerves at the sciatic notch before injection with a 33-gauge syringe (Hamilton). Mice were killed 1 week after the second injection. Four mice were used in each experimental group.

Immunohistochemical Analysis of Mouse Sciatic Nerves

The IgG-injected sciatic nerve was rapidly removed and was immediately prefixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes for teased fibers. Teased fibers were then permeabilized with 2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 minutes before being blocked in 10% goat serum and 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 60 minutes at room temperature. Next, immunostaining was performed using anti-CASPR polyclonal antibodies (Abcam, 1:500 dilution) alone, or together with anti-pan sodium channel monoclonal antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich, 1:500 dilution). Alexa Fluor 488–labeled anti-human IgG, 488–labeled or 594–labeled anti-rabbit IgG, or 594–labeled anti-mouse IgG was used as secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500 dilution). Images were captured using a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope. Teased fibers were analyzed from 4 animals for each group. For morphometric analysis, all immunostained nodal complexes observed in the teased nerves were counted: 725 in the control group, 801 in the LGI4-IgG2 group, and 802 in the LGI4-IgG4 group. For histopathologic analysis, sciatic nerves were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS overnight at 4°C before being postfixed in 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M PBS for 1 hour. Nerves were then dehydrated and embedded in epoxy resin (Epon). Transverse semithin sections were stained with toluidine blue and examined using light microscopy. J-I. Kira collected serum samples and clinical information from the patients, M. Mitsuishi conducted the intraneural injections and prepared the sections of IgG-injected sciatic nerves, and X. Zhang performed the blinded immunohistochemical assessments of the injected sciatic nerves.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The research protocol for this retrospective study and the data privacy procedures for human samples were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committees of the International University of Health and Welfare (20-Ifh-025) and Kyushu University (2020-789). This study was conducted according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.20

Statistical Analysis

Significance was assessed using unpaired 2-tailed Student t tests, 1-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, χ-squared tests, or χ-squared goodness-of-fit tests. All analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Data Availability

Any data not published within the article will be shared in anonymized form on request from any qualified investigator.

Results

Clinical Features and IgG Subclasses of Patients With LGI4+ AN

In addition to the 4 previously reported cases, 4 patients were newly identified as positive for anti-LGI4 antibodies using live CBA (Table 1, Figure 1, eTable 1, eFigure 1). Except for 1 patient (Case 8) who was double-positive for anti-LGI4 and anti-NF155 antibodies (NF155-IgG4–positive, ΔMFI of NF155-IgG4 using FCM = 6,764) and had a fulminant course (unable to walk within 1 year), all patients were negative for anti-NF155, anti-CNTN1, and anti-CASPR1 antibodies. The patients with LGI4+ AN had a relatively old age at onset (median [range] 72 [41–90] years) (Table 1). The ratio of men to women was 4:4. Six patients presented with typical CIDP, and 2 exhibited multifocal CIDP.10 Four patients showed acute/subacute onset with monophasic courses, and 4 showed chronic onset with progressive (3 patients) and relapsing (1 patient) courses. The predominant symptoms/signs were motor weakness in 8 cases; muscle atrophy in 4 cases; deep and superficial sensory impairment in 8 and 7 cases, respectively; Romberg sign in 4 cases; and hand/finger tremor in 5 cases (Table 1). All but 1 patient (whose CSF was drawn after immunotherapy) had very high CSF protein concentrations (median [range] 253 [108–618] mg/dL) (Table 1). All patients fulfilled the electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP,10 displaying demyelinating patterns such as decreased motor and sensory nerve conduction velocity, increased distal and F wave latencies, and temporal dispersion and conduction block in nerve conduction studies (Table 2). Of these, the prolongation of distal and F wave latencies was particularly prominent. In addition, 1 patient (Case 8) showed prolonged P100 latency in visual evoked potentials. IVIg was partially effective in all 7 administered patients, and corticosteroids were also effective in 3 of 4 patients (Table 1, eAppendix). Of note, 1 patient (Case 7) rapidly showed a favorable response to subcutaneous efgartigimod alpha, including a marked reduction in coarse hand/finger tremor together with improved bilateral median and ulnar nerve conduction and decreased IgG binding to DRG satellite glia (eFigure 2A, Tables 1 and 2, Video, eAppendix). In the LGI4-IgG subclass analysis by DRG-TBA, IgG4 was predominant in 4 patients and IgG2 and IgG3 were predominant in 2 patients each (Table 1, eFigure 2B). Using live CBA and serum samples (available from 5 patients), IgG1 predominated in 3 patients and IgG4 and IgG1/2 predominated in 1 patient each (Figure 1, eTable 1, eFigure 1).

Peripheral Nerve Hypertrophy and Onion Bulb Formation in Patients With LGI4+ AN

Spinal root hypertrophy was observed in 3 cases: 1 typical CIDP case with diffuse hypertrophy (Case 7) (Figure 2) and 2 multifocal acquired demyelinating sensory and motor neuropathy cases with focal hypertrophy (Cases 5 and 6). Sural nerve biopsy in Case 7 showed a moderate reduction in myelinated fiber density with moderate endoneurial edema but no inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 3, A and B), accompanied by frequent moderate-sized onion bulb formations (Figure 3C). Inflammatory cell infiltration was not conspicuous. Electron microscopy confirmed onion bulb formations around the axons (Figure 4, A and B). The cytoplasm of macrophages containing myelin debris was observed adjacent to axons and Schwann cells (Figure 4C). In addition, the mild detachment of myelin terminal loops from axolemma was observed at the paranodes (Figure 4D).

LGI4-IgG Induces Schwann Cell Proliferation

The indirect immunofluorescence assay confirmed that cultured Schwann cells expressed both LGI4 and its receptor, ADAM2213,21 (eFigure 3A). Schwann cell proliferation (assessed by BrdU staining) was significantly higher after incubation with LGI4-IgG from 2 patients with chronic-onset, progressive/relapsing courses than with HC-IgG (Cases 5 and 7 vs HCs, p = 0.0159 and p = 0.0079, respectively); this was particularly prominent in Case 7, who showed onion bulb structures in the biopsied sural nerve (eFigure 3B). However, no significant differences in Schwann cell proliferation were observed with IgG from 2 patients with acute/subacute onset, monophasic courses. Next, quantitative real-time PCR revealed that LGI4-IgG incubation significantly decreased the mRNA levels of Krox20—a major positive regulator of peripheral myelination—compared with CIDP-IgG incubation (p = 0.0087) and had a similar but nonsignificant effect compared with HC-IgG incubation (p = 0.052) (eFigure 3C). In particular, when only chronic-onset LGI4+ patients were compared with HCs, Krox20 expression levels were significantly lower in chronic-onset LGI4+ patients than in HCs (p = 0.0238) and patients with CIDP (p = 0.0238). However, no significant changes were observed in the mRNA levels of Prx, another peripheral myelination regulator, after LGI4-IgG, CIDP-IgG, or HC-IgG treatment (eFigure 3D).

Intraneural Injection of LGI4-IgG Induces Nodal and Paranodal Alterations

HC-IgG did not stain nodes or paranodes/juxtaparanodes after double injection and did not disrupt CASPR or Nav immunostaining (Figure 5, eFigure 4), indicating that paranode stretching during dissection did not diffuse or deposit injected IgG. By contrast, both LGI4-IgG2–dominant IgG (Case 7) and LGI4-IgG4–dominant IgG (Case 2) (according to DRG-TBA), deposited mainly at the node extending toward the juxtaparanodes beyond CASPR-immunostained paranodes, destroyed CASPR immunostaining and altered immunostained Nav localization (Figure 5, eFigure 4). Compared with LGI4-IgG4, LGI4-IgG2 appeared to deposit more extensively in the juxtaparanodes through the more disrupted paranodes/nodes. NF155-IgG4 also penetrated the paranodes, reduced CASPR immunostaining, and altered immunostained Nav localization, but to a limited degree compared with LGI4-IgG (Figure 5, eFigure 4). Of interest, nerves injected with LGI4 and NF155 double-positive IgG (Case 8) showed a combined pattern of LGI4-IgG and NF155-IgG injections in terms of IgG deposition and CASPR and Nav immunostaining.

To confirm these observations, a morphometric analysis was performed on the CASPR-Nav immunostaining. Decreased and/or disrupted CASPR immunostaining was observed more frequently in LGI4-IgG–injected mice than in HC-IgG–injected mice (LGI4-IgG1/2 vs HC-IgG, p < 0.0001; LGI4-IgG4 vs HC-IgG, p = 0.0210). These decreased/disrupted CASPR-staining paranodes were more frequent in LGI4-IgG1/2–injected nerves than in LGI4-IgG4–injected nerves (p < 0.0001) (eFigure 5A). Moreover, altered immunostained Nav localization nodes were observed more frequently in LGI4-IgG–injected mice than in HC-IgG–injected mice (both LGI4-IgG2 vs HC-IgG and LGI4-IgG4 vs HC-IgG, p < 0.0001; LGI4-IgG2 vs LGI4-IgG4, no significant difference) (eFigure 5B). The lengths of paranodes with altered immunostained Nav localization were significantly shorter in LGI4-IgG–injected mice than in HC-IgG–injected mice (LGI4-IgG2 vs HC-IgG, p < 0.0001; LGI4-IgG4 vs HC-IgG, p < 0.0001); this decrease in length was more severe in LGI4-IgG2–injected nerves than in LGI4-IgG4–injected nerves (p = 0.0083) (eFigure 5C). Similarly, for all nodal complexes, the diameter of nodes (as determined by Nav immunostaining) and paranodes and the length of paranodes (as determined by CASPR immunostaining) were significantly shorter in LGI4-IgG–injected mice than in HC-IgG–injected mice (both nodes and paranodes: LGI4-IgG2 vs HC-IgG, p < 0.0001; LGI4-IgG4 vs HC-IgG, p < 0.0001) and in LGI4-IgG4–injected mice than in LGI4-IgG2–injected mice (p < 0.0001) (eFigure 6).

LGI4-IgG Preferentially Affects Large-Diameter Axons

To investigate whether nodal/paranodal alterations are associated with a specific axonal subset, axon diameter distributions were analyzed. Analyses of total and normal paranodes revealed significant differences in axon diameter distributions between LGI4-IgG2–injected and HC-IgG–injected mice (p < 0.0001), and between LGI4-IgG4–injected and HC-IgG–injected mice (p < 0.0001), with a preferential decrease in relatively large-diameter axons (approximately 4–9 µm) in both LGI4-IgG2–injected and LGI4-IgG4–injected mice compared with HC-IgG–injected mice (eFigure 5, D–F). Moreover, the decreased/disrupted CASPR-stained paranodes were preferentially observed in relatively small-diameter axons (with a peak at 1.5 µm) in both LGI4-IgG2–injected and LGI4-IgG4–injected mice (eFigure 5, D–F).

Discussion

In this study, we have shown that LGI4+ AN manifests as a chronic progressive disease with nerve hypertrophy, in addition to an acute/subacute-onset disease masquerading as Guillain-Barré syndrome as previously reported.16

In our patients, the detected LGI4-IgG subclass frequencies were similar for each case between DRG-TBA and live CBA. However, the predominant IgG subclasses were somewhat different between DRG-TBA and live CBA; IgG4 was predominant with DRG-TBA, whereas IgG1 and IgG2 were predominant with live CBA. This discrepancy may be attributable to distinct tissue binding according to the IgG subclass.22,23 In AN, IgG1, IgG2, and IgG3 subclasses have been reported in combination with IgG4, or even as the predominant subclass.4-6 In childhood AN, NF155-IgG2 predominance has also been reported.24 Furthermore, IgG3 panNF+ AN is reportedly a fulminant disease.13 IgG subclasses other than IgG4 may, therefore, modify or even aggravate the disease. It is important to note that subclass switching occurs from either IgG1–3 to IgG4 or IgG4 to IgG1–3 in AN, and this substantially affects responsiveness to immunotherapy (particularly to IVIg).25 Moreover, IgG subclass switching from IgG4 to IgG1 is reportedly associated with acute exacerbation, suggesting that the IgG subclass is a possible biomarker of disease activity.26

The sural nerve biopsy of Case 7 also revealed a moderate reduction in myelinated fibers with endoneurial edema, numerous onion bulbs, and few inflammatory cell infiltrates. Electron microscopy demonstrated onion bulb formations around the axons and mild detachment of myelin terminal loops from the axolemma at the paranodes. This subtle detachment between axolemma and myelin terminal loops at the paranodes may be a nonspecific finding; however, it may also be that such detachment allows pathogenic autoantibodies to access the juxtaparanode. NF155+ AN has frequently been documented to exhibit peripheral nerve hypertrophy, which is essentially diffuse, although asymmetric or focal hypertrophy also exists.5,27,28 Asymmetric and focal involvement has also been reported in other ANs.29 In our case series, proximal nerve hypertrophy was observed in 3 of 4 chronic-onset LGI4+ patients with progressive/relapsing courses. We thus consider that proximal nerve hypertrophy on MRI is a characteristic feature of chronic progressive/relapsing LGI4+ cases, although nerve hypertrophy is also observed in CIDP.30 Notably, 1 of our patients with nerve hypertrophy (Case 7) showed marked onion bulb formation in the biopsied sural nerve, suggesting that onion bulb formation is 1 of the mechanisms of nerve hypertrophy in LGI4+ AN. To date, 1 other case of AN with onion bulbs has been reported; they carried NF155-IgG1–3 but not NF155-IgG4.31 Case 7 also carried LGI4-IgG2 and LGI4-IgG1, but not LGI4-IgG4. It is thus possible that IgG subclasses other than IgG4 may also contribute to onion bulb formation in AN.

Of interest, LGI4-IgG from chronic progressive/relapsing cases, but not from acute/subacute monophasic cases, increased cultured Schwann cell proliferation and decreased the expression of Krox20, a key peripheral nerve myelination signal.32,33 It is, therefore, possible that LGI4-IgG acts directly on Schwann cells, interrupts LGI4 and ADAM22 interaction,34,35 dampens myelination signals,21 and induces unmyelinated Schwann cell proliferation. Such mechanisms may occur particularly in chronic progressive/relapsing LGI4+ AN. The direct action of NF155-IgG on Schwann cells is also reported to prevent paranodal complex formation in vivo.36 Although clinical and functional correlations according to subclassifications of acute/subacute monophasic and chronic progressive/relapsing diseases are critical, these preliminary observations should be confirmed by large-scale studies.

By intraneural injection, both LGI4-IgG4 and LGI4-IgG2 deposited around the nodes and induced the distortion/disruption of nodal/paranodal structures, as shown by CASPR and Nav immunostaining. Decreased paranodal CASPR immunostaining has been reported with intraneural injection of CNTN1-IgG3,23 and altered immunostained Nav localization has also been observed with intraneural injection of anti-NF155 and anti-CNTN1 antibodies37 as well as in an active immunization model using GM1 ganglioside.38 Our findings are in line with these previous reports. We also observed the modest shortening of paranodal length together with decreased/disrupted CASPR immunostaining in LGI4-IgG–injected nerves, which is consistent with the reported modest paranode shortening in CNTN1-IgG–injected nerves.23 Although elongated nodes/paranodes have also been reported in human AN cases,5 this discrepancy may be explained by the short time course in the animal experiments vs the long-time exposure in human AN.

Disruption of the nodal/paranodal complexes was more prominent in LGI4-IgG2–injected nerves than in LGI4-IgG4–injected nerves, even in the absence of human complements. This is in accordance with observations that NF155-IgG4 and NF155-IgG2 double-positive AN has a more severe disease course than NF155-IgG4 single-positive AN.13 IgG4 is only monovalent in vivo because of a half-molecule exchange, whereas IgG2 exists bivalently and can internalize target antigens.11,12 Such functional differences may make LGI4-IgG2 more hazardous than LGI4-IgG4.

The observed decrease in nodal axon diameters (i.e., the shift toward smaller axon diameters) in the intraneural injection experiments may reflect nodal structure impairment by LGI4-IgG, particularly in large-diameter axons that convey proprioceptive sensation.

Of interest, 1 superaged-onset patient (Case 8) had both NF155-IgG and LGI4-IgG in our systematic search for nodal/paranodal antibodies in the second cohort. Anti-NF155 and anti-CASPR1 antibody double-positive AN has also been reported in a systematic survey of nodal/paranodal autoantibodies.39 Given that younger onset is a cardinal feature of NF155+ AN,4,27,28 our identified case appears to be beyond the scope of NF155+ AN. Case 8 also exhibited proteinuria and hypoalbuminemia. Both CNTN1+ AN and panNF+ AN are complicated with nephrotic syndrome/membranous nephropathy because CNTN1 and NF186 are expressed in the kidney.9,14 Given that LGI4 is also expressed in the kidney, particularly in the proximal renal tubular cells, with a role in transmembrane transport,40 renal symptoms might be a manifestation of LGI4+ AN.

IVIg was partially effective in LGI4+ AN in our patient series. Our patients with LGI4+ AN harbored various subclasses of IgG other than IgG4, which may cause peripheral nerve inflammation. A recent sciatic nerve biopsy study demonstrated many perineurial inflammatory cells, including T and B cells, in an NF155-IgG4+ AN case.31 Thus, although no inflammatory cell infiltrates were present in the distal portions of peripheral nerves, such as biopsied sural nerves, proximal segments with nerve swelling may have had inflammation that could have been targeted by IVIg. Notably, 1 patient (Case 7) in this study rapidly responded to the subcutaneous injection of efgartigimod alpha, a neonatal Fc receptor antagonist, which can swiftly decrease IgG by preventing endogenous IgG recycling.41 Markedly improved coarse hand/finger tremor and sensory ataxia, alongside nerve conduction improvements, may be produced by nodal complex recovery. In addition, anti–B-cell therapy, such as rituximab, is reportedly effective in AN by targeting memory B cells and IgG4-producing cluster of differentiation 20+ short-lived plasma cells.25,42 The early introduction of neonatal Fc receptor antagonists and B cell–depleting therapy may thus be promising for treating AN, including LGI4+ AN.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not purify each IgG subclass, and minor amounts of nondominant IgG subclasses may exert potent effects. Second, because single intraneural injections of nodal/paranodal antibodies reportedly show no effects,22 we adopted twice-repeated injections based on previous twice-repeated intraneural injection reports.23,37 We morphometrically compared the effects between LGI4-IgG and HC-IgG injections. However, because repeated intraneural injections may increase the mechanical destruction of the blood-nerve barrier and nodal structures, our results should be cautiously interpreted. In particular, the significance of the altered immunostained Nav localization with LGI4-IgG injection needs to be confirmed without the use of intraneural injections in the future. Third, because the IgG-injected areas were very limited, we conducted immunohistochemical but not electrophysiologic studies; our intraneural injection results should thus be regarded as preliminary. Fourth, the observed positive frequency for anti-LGI4 antibodies was much higher in cases from Fukuoka Central Hospital than in those from Kyushu University. This discrepancy may be partly attributable to the distinct natures of these cohorts. The former was a tertiary neurology hospital cohort in which selected patients with CIDP who did not sufficiently respond to usual immunotherapies were referred, whereas the latter cohort included any patient with CIDP in Japan who was referred for anti-nodal antibody assay. Fifth, interlaboratory consistency in the determination of anti-nodal IgG antibody subclasses is reportedly poor depending on the methodology applied,43 indicating that the subclass-dependent results should be interpreted with caution. Finally, our study populations were relatively small because of the disease's rarity.

In conclusion, the clinical features of LGI4+ cases are more compatible with the reported characteristics of AN—such as sensory ataxia, coarse hand/finger tremor, and very high CSF protein levels—than with those of CIDP. Given the expression of LGI4 in juxtaparanodes, LGI4+ neuropathy is suggested to be a type of AN. However, LGI4 is also expressed in DRG satellite glia, and Schwann cells also express LGI4 and its receptor ADAM22. Satellite glia and Schwann cells might, therefore, be directly attacked by LGI4-IgG, interrupting LGI4-ADAM22 interaction; this may modify disease manifestations beyond the scope of AN, such as onion bulb formation. Because LGI4+ AN may exhibit a chronic progressive/relapsing course in addition to an acute/subacute monophasic course, LGI4-IgG tests are recommended in aged patients with acute-onset or chronic-onset demyelinating neuropathy for early diagnosis and effective immunotherapy.
