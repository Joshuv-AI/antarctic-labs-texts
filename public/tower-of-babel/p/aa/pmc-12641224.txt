Introduction

Polygonum perfoliatum L., a Chinese herbal medicine, is derived from the dried aerial parts of Persicaria perfoliate (L.) H. Gross., and is a key element of Miao medicine in Guizhou Province. It is abundant in resources and is extensively distributed across China, Nepal, India, Russia, and Korea [1]. Polygonum perfoliatum L. was incorporated into the 2025 edition of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China, which highlights its properties of heat-clearing, detoxifying, promoting diuresis, and reducing edema. Additionally, it is recognized for its food, nutritional, and medicinal values in traditional use [2]. Both the leaves and fruits of this plant are edible. In spring, the young leaves are first blanched and then cooled, making them suitable for consumption as a wild vegetable, and they also serve as an excellent feed for livestock and poultry. Regular consumption and feeding of this plant contribute positively to the health of both humans and animals. In autumn, the fruits of the PP reach maturity and can be consumed directly as a fruit, exhibiting a sweet and sour flavor profile. In addition, PP has an extensive history in traditional medicine and is widely used in the treatment of medical, surgical and gynecological diseases. Recent studies have revealed that PP exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, encompassing anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, anti-liver fibrotic, antitussive, expectorant, antitumor, and antioxidant effects [3]. To date, a total of 80 chemical compounds, such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, anthraquinones, and alkaloids, have been identified from P. perfoliatum [4].

Polysaccharides, classified as biomacromolecules, typically comprise more than ten monosaccharide units linked by glycosidic bonds to form linear or branched structures, with molecular weights ranging from several kDa to several MDa [5,6]. These natural polymers, extracted from botanical, zoological, mycological, and algal sources, have garnered significant scientific interest owing to their multifaceted pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, anti-aging, and anti-tumor effects [[7], [8], [9]].

Given the documented anti-inflammatory properties of PP and the bioactive potential of its polysaccharide, optimizing extraction yield represents a critical prerequisite for selecting appropriate techniques. Conventional extraction methods, characterized by low efficiency and extended processing times, are increasingly incompatible with contemporary industrial demands [10]. UAE technique has garnered considerable attention as an environmentally friendly, safe, and efficient technology [11]. This technique leverages the mechanical effects produced by ultrasound to disrupt plant cell walls, thereby facilitating solvent diffusion and enhancing the dissolution of bioactive compounds. Consequently, UAE enhances polysaccharide extraction efficiency and yield [12,13]. Furthermore, modulated sonication under optimized parameters enhances the structural integrity of polysaccharides while augmenting their bioactivity [14]. Consequently, UAE has gained widespread application for isolating bioactive compounds from diverse botanical materials. Numerous process variables within the UAE procedure significantly influence the yield of plant-derived natural products [15]. Systematic optimization of these parameters is thus essential to achieve enhanced yield. Common optimization techniques encompass response surface methodology and GA-BP neural network models [16]. RSM, a collection of statistical techniques, facilitates experimental design, model development, and assessment of process variable effects on target responses [17]. A back propagation (BP) artificial neural network is an arithmetic model composed of neurons, which can realize multiple linear regression of weights and biases, and make up for the limitations of RSM in dealing with nonlinear problems [18]. However, the poor fitting or overfitting due to inaccurate weights and thresholds in BP neural networks can be improved by combining with genetic algorithms (GA), which are used to find the optimal extraction scheme by natural selection and genetic mechanisms, to find the optimal solution, optimize the weights and thresholds, and improve the accuracy, which, as a complementary model to the RSM, is increasingly attracting the attention of scholars engaged in the study of optimizing extraction conditions [19,20].

In this study, Polygonum perfoliatum L. polysaccharides (PPP) was extracted by UAE, and the optimal extraction process parameters of AMRP were predicted by response surface methodology and two neural network models using PPP transfer rate and purity as the main evaluation indexes, and they were evaluated and compared. Cellulose column chromatography and dextran gel column chromatography were used to purify PPP sequentially, and S-PPP3 was obtained. The structure of S-PPP3 was characterized using various carbohydrate analysis techniques, including HPLC, UV, FTIR, and NMR. Finally, the in vitro anti-inflammatory activity of S-PPP3 was evaluated in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Polygonum perfoliatum L. was obtained from Zhejiang Chinese Medicine University Decoction Piece Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). All chemical reagents were analytical grade.

Extraction of PPP

PP was initially pulverized using an herbal grinder and subsequently sieved through a 30-mesh screen. The prepared herb powder was then transferred to a stoppered conical flask, followed by the addition of a measured volume of distilled water. The extraction was carried out using an ultrasonic extractor, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. Under continuous stirring, ethanol was added dropwise until the ethanol concentration achieved 80 %. The mixture was subsequently kept at 4 ℃ for 24 h. Finally, the resulting crude polysaccharide precipitate was collected by centrifugation, employing the aqueous ethanol precipitation technique [21]. The obtained precipitate was dried, weighed, and then redissolved in distilled water for further determination.

Single-factor experiment

Polysaccharide yield is influenced by several factors, including the number of extraction cycles, liquid-to-solid ratio, extraction duration, ultrasonic power, and temperature [22]. Therefore, extraction cycles (1, 2, 3, 4, 5 times), liquid-to-solid ratio (20, 30, 40, 50 mL/g), extraction time (20, 30, 40, 50 min), ultrasonic powers (200, 300, 400, 500 W) and extraction temperature (40, 50, 60, 70 ℃) on the transfer rate and purity of polysaccharides were investigated.

Box-Behnken experiment

Box-Behnken Design (BBD) of RSM has become a popular approach in recent years for optimizing multi-variable experimental conditions. This method aims to identify the optimal extraction parameters by developing regression equations that describe the functional relationships between independent variables and response metrics [23]. Based on single-factor experiment outcomes, operational ranges for each parameter were established. BBD with four independent variables at three levels was subsequently employed using Design-Expert 12 software, generating 29 experimental runs to optimize parameter combinations. Comparation of ANN

BP neural network, a fundamental algorithm in artificial intelligence, was originally proposed by Rumelhart, McClelland, and colleagues [24]. It is a multi-layer feed-forward neural network. The model is trained by continuously backpropagating information to fine-tune weights and thresholds, ensuring the output gradually approximates the actual result. Meanwhile, GA simulates the natural evolutionary process by retaining well-adapted samples while eliminating poorly adapted ones. By means of iterative optimization, the GA identifies the optimal weight and threshold values, which are subsequently integrated into the BP neural network for additional refinement [25]. The BP neural network employs a three-tiered architecture consisting of an input layer, hidden layer, and output layer [26]. Neurons within the input layer capture incoming signals and forward them to the hidden layer. In the hidden layer, the signals are processed with the “tansig” activation function before being transmitted to the neurons in the output layer. The output layer then applies the “purelin” activation function to refine the results, which are ultimately provided as the final output [27].

A three-tier neural network was developed using MATLAB 2020a software, with the input layer comprising four neurons corresponding to the liquid-to-solid ratio, extraction time, extraction temperature, and extraction power. The output layer consists of a single neuron representing the composite score [28]. The size of the training dataset has a considerable impact on the model’s performance, affecting both its efficiency and accuracy. An excessively large training dataset can prolong training time and increase the risk of overfitting, whereas an insufficient amount of training data may result in poor model generalization and reduced predictive accuracy. Consequently, a widely adopted strategy in neural network modeling is to divide the dataset, allocating 70 % for training and 30 % for testing. This division ensures a balance between model learning and performance evaluation, enhancing both accuracy and the ability to generalize [25,29]. The model was also employed to predict the optimal extraction conditions. The model's performance was evaluated utilizing the coefficient of determination (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE) [30]. R2 serves as a key metric for evaluating model fit quality, where values closer to 1 indicate superior fitting performance. Concurrently diminished MAE signifies greater proximity of predictions to actual measurements, directly quantifying prediction accuracy. Similarly, smaller RMSE values reflect reduced overall deviation between predicted and observed values, denoting enhanced model robustness.

Isolation and purification of PPP

Polysaccharides extracted through UAE typically result in a crude form that contains proteins, pigments, and other impurities [7]. These contaminants must be removed initially before isolating the polysaccharide components. The Sevag method is one of the most common and effective methods for removing protein [31]. Polysaccharide separation and purification were primarily accomplished through combined ion-exchange and gel-permeation chromatography [7]. Utilizing the denaturation of proteins in organic solvents like trichloroethane, the crude polysaccharide extract from section 2.2 was combined with Sevag’s reagent, shaken, and centrifuged. Following deproteinization, the solution was subjected to column chromatography on a DEAE-52 cellulose column (6.0 × 100.0 cm) [32]. The crude polysaccharide was eluted using a linear NaCl gradient (0–0.5 mol/L) at 4.0 mL/min. The neutral polysaccharide D-PPP1 and acidic polysaccharides D-PPP2, D-PPP3, D-PPP4, and D-PPP5 were subsequently collected, dialyzed, and freeze-dried [33]. Based on the yield of individual fractions and initial activity screening using the kit, D-PPP3 was selected for further purification. S-PPP3 was subsequently isolated via gel-permeation chromatography on a Sephadex G-100 column (3.0 × 100 cm) using deionized water as eluent at 0.5 mL/min.

Structural characterization of S-PPP3

Molecular weight determination

The molecular weight of S-PPP3 was determined by high-performance gel permeation chromatography (HPGPC) using an Agilent 1260 system (Agilent, USA) equipped with G7162A refractive index detector [34]. Separation was performed on a Shodex SUGAR KS-804 ligand exchange column (8.0 × 300 mm, 7 μm) with 20 μL injections of 2 mg/mL polysaccharide solutions. A calibration curve was established using dextran standards. Samples and standards were eluted with 0.2 M NaCl mobile phase at 0.6 mL/min, with the column maintained at 40 ℃.

Monosaccharide composition

S-PPP3 underwent acid hydrolysis with 4 M trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) at 110 ℃ for 2 h, followed by derivatization with 1-phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP) [34]. Monosaccharide composition was analyzed using a Waters Alliance e2695 HPLC system (Waters, USA) equipped with a UV detector and employing a Diamonsil C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). Separation used 10 μL injections at 245 nm with a column temperature maintained at 25℃. The mobile phase consisted of two components: phosphate buffer solution (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution was carried out according to the following scheme: 0–20 min, A: 86 %; 20–35 min, A: 86 %–78 %; 35–55 min, A: 78 %–84 %; 55–65 min, A 84 %–86 %.

SEM

Approximately 3 mg of dried S-PPP3 purified sample was weighed and coated with a gold layer for approximately 40 s, until a metallic film appeared on the surface. The sample was then placed in a SEM for observation. The magnification was set to 2000×, 5000 × and 10000 × .

UV spectroscopy

Samples of S-PPP3 were dissolved in deionized water to a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and subjected to analysis using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan), with a scan covering the full wavelength range of 200–600 nm [35].

FTIR spectra

Approximately 2 mg of S-PPP3 was weighed and combined with dried KBr at a 1:10 mass ratio. The resulting mixture was ground and compressed into flakes before being analyzed with an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA), scanning across the wave number range of 4000–400 cm−1 [36].

Congo red assay

A 1 mL solution of S-PPP3 (1.0 mg/mL) was prepared and combined with 1 mL of 0.4 mmol/L Congo red solution. Incremental additions of NaOH solution adjusted the final NaOH concentration from 0.0 to 0.5 mol/L. The maximum absorption wavelengths for each NaOH concentration were measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). A graph was then generated, with NaOH concentration plotted on the x-axis and the corresponding maximum absorption wavelength on the y-axis [37].

Periodic acid oxidation

S-PPP3 was synthesized by reacting with a 50 mmol/l sodium periodate solution, and the total volume was adjusted to 25 mL. The reaction mixture was kept in a dark environment. At 6-hour intervals, 0.1 mL aliquots were withdrawn from the reaction mixture, diluted to 25 mL, vortex-mixed, and absorbance was measured at 223 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The reaction continued until the absorbance stabilized. NaIO4 consumption was determined based on a standard curve (y = 16.624×−0.0275, R2 = 0.9995). Subsequently, a 2 mL aliquot of the reaction mixture was withdrawn, and the liberated formic acid was titrated against 0.01 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution using phenolphthalein as the indicator [38].

Methylation analysis

The S-PPP3 sample (2 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of anhydrous DMSO, and sodium hydroxide was then rapidly added for ultrasonic dissolution. Methyl iodide was added and the reaction was carried out at 30 ℃ with magnetic stirring in a water bath for 60 min. After quenching at 2 ℃ for 90 min, the methylation reaction mixture was evaporated under reduced pressure until dry. The residue was redissolved in double-distilled water, reduced with 60 mg of sodium borohydride for 8 h, and neutralized with glacial acetic acid. After evaporation, the sample was dried and then acetylated with acetic anhydride at 100 ℃ for 1 h and cooled. Excess acetic anhydride was removed by repeated co-evaporation with toluene under reduced pressure. The acetylated product was dissolved in CH2Cl2, transferred to a separation funnel, and washed with distilled water. The organic layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and diluted to 10 mL. Analysis was performed using an Agilent 6890-5973 GC–MS system (Agilent, USA). Fragment ions were compared with the standard mass spectrometry library to determine the derivative pattern of S-PPP3.

NMR

The S-PPP3 sample (54 mg) was dissolved in 0.5 mL of D2O, and analyzed using an Avance III 600 MHz digital NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). The associated NMR spectra were then processed with MestReNova software.

Anti-inflammatory activity in vitro

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin at 37 ℃ under 5 % CO2.

Cell viability assay

Mouse peritoneal macrophage RAW264.7 cells were employed to assess the cytotoxicity of S-PPP3. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. After the incubation period, the cells were exposed to various concentrations of S-PPP3 (0, 16, 32, 63, 125, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, MTT solution was added, and the cells were incubated for 4 h. Subsequently, DMSO was introduced, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a Microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

LPS induction and polysaccharide administration

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well. Once the cells reached approximately 80 % confluence, appropriate concentrations of polysaccharide solutions were added to the treatment groups. The normal and model control groups were treated with PBS, while the positive control group received dexamethasone (100 μg/mL). After 24 h of incubation, the cells were exposed to 1 μg/mL LPS for 24 h. LPS was not added to the normal group.

Levels of NO, TNF-α and IL-6 secretion

After 24 h of cell incubation, the supernatant was harvested and subjected to centrifugation. Thereafter, the concentrations of NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 were quantified utilizing commercially available assay kits.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed following the manufacturer's instructions provided with the commercial qPCR kits (Accurate Biochemical Co., Ltd., Hunan, China). Briefly, total RNA was extracted from harvested cells using the SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit. After quantification and dilution to a uniform concentration, RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Evo M−MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver.2. The reverse transcription conditions were as follows: 37 °C for 15 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s. Quantitative PCR was carried out on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Rox Plus). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. All primer sequences used in this study are provided in Table 3. GAPDH was used as an internal control, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method [39].

Data analysis

Experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with each test conducted in triplicate. Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS 26 and GraphPad Prism 9.5. Significance thresholds were set at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01.

Results and discussion

Extraction optimization

Single-factor experiments for UAE

The influence of various factors on the polysaccharide transfer rate, purity and comprehensive score was illustrated in Fig. 1. The transfer rate exhibited an overall upward trend, while the purity initially rose before subsequently declining. This may be since as the number of extraction cycles, liquid-to-solid ratio, extraction time and temperature increase, the content of impurities rises [40]. As the number of extraction cycles is a discrete variable, only four factors—liquid-to-solid ratio, extraction time, temperature, and power—were included in the BBD design. Subsequently, the point with the highest composite score and its two adjacent points were chosen as the three levels for the BBD design: liquid–solid ratios of 25:1, 30:1, and 35:1 mL/g; extraction time of 25, 30, and 35 min; extraction temperatures of 65, 70, and 75 ℃; and ultrasonic power of 350, 400, and 500 W, respectively.

Comparison between RSM model and GA-BP neural network model

The GA-BP neural network employed a genetic algorithm to optimize both thresholds and connection weights. Initially, a population of 30 individuals was generated through binary encoding. The genetic algorithm parameters were configured as follows: a crossover probability of 0.8, a mutation probability of 0.2, and a maximum iteration count of 50. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, the correlation between fitness and generational evolution was assessed. The optimal fitness value reached 2.67528, while the mean fitness was determined to be 11.8749. By applying selection, crossover, and mutation within the population, the weights and thresholds were iteratively refined. Ultimately, the parameters were adjusted until they satisfied the predefined convergence criteria. The refined weights and thresholds were implemented in the BP neural network for predictive analysis (Table 5). The training procedure was designed to run for 1000 iterations, with a predefined error tolerance of 0.00001. As the training advanced, the neural network’s weight update frequency rose, causing the model to transition progressively from underfitting to overfitting. Fig. 3D illustrated the performance variations of both the training and test sets across different epoch counts. The optimized extraction parameters included a solid–liquid ratio of 35 mL/g, an extraction time of 29 min, an extraction temperature of 72 ℃, and an extraction power of 415 W, yielding a comprehensive score of 96.96.

Performance comparison of two models.

The two models were assessed and compared using R2, MAE, and RMSE, with a summary of the results provided in Table 5. Among them, the GA-BP model demonstrated the smallest MAE and RMSE values, suggesting its superior predictive accuracy. The GA-BP model achieved an R2 value of 0.9851, indicating the highest degree of fit among all models and further confirming its superior predictive performance. The BBD model establishes a quantitative relationship between dependent and independent variables, allowing for the prioritization of variable significance and their interactions. However, this method required extensive experimentation and was limited to representing variable relationships through quadratic regression. In contrast, the GA-BP neural network model exhibited strong generalization and nonlinear mapping capabilities by adaptively adjusting weights and parameters, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy and efficiency, providing more reliable optimal process conditions, and offering a more comprehensive model performance evaluation. Nevertheless, its effectiveness depended on the availability of sufficient training data. In conclusion, to ensure more reliable results, it is advisable to employ at least two models for comparative analysis.

Validation experiment

The GA-BP model and BBD model were chosen for predicting the optimal process conditions and conducting verification. The GA-BP model achieved the highest comprehensive score (96.96 ± 6.23) %, significantly outperforming the BBD model (85.27 ± 4.38) % (Table 6). In summary, the GA-BP model demonstrated superior performance, and the obtained parameter values for the four variables represent the optimal process conditions.

SEM analysis

The surface and internal structure of S-PPP3 at different magnifications were observed by scanning electron microscopy. Magnify 2000 ×,5000 × and 10,000 × (Fig. 5A), S-PPP3 exhibited an irregular, dispersed, and fragmented chain-like structure with numerous protruding spherical entities on its surface. Literature suggested the finding that a porous internal architecture with abundant voids in polysaccharides was indicative of weaker intermolecular forces, whereas a compact and complex surface morphology implied stronger ones [10,22]. Consistent with this, S-PPP3 exhibited a loose, highly porous internal framework containing multiple voids, suggesting relatively weak intermolecular interactions within its structure.

UV analysis

S-PPP3 exhibited no absorption peaks at 260 nm and 280 nm, confirming the absence of both nucleic acids and proteins (Fig. 5B).

FTIR analysis

As shown in Fig. 5C, the broad absorption at 3421 cm−1 was assigned to O-H stretching vibrations, while the peak at 2931 cm−1 corresponded to C-H stretching in alkyl groups [43]. The absorptions at 1736 cm−1 and 1617 cm−1 were assigned to carboxylate (C=O) stretching vibrations, while the band at 1420 cm−1 indicated C-H bending. The peak at 1241 cm−1 corresponds to the C–O stretching vibration in S-PPP3[15,18,44]. Characteristic strong bands at 1146, 1098, and 1022 cm−1 arose from C–O–C glycosidic linkages and C-O-H deformations [45]. Furthermore, absorptions at 850 cm−1 and 927 cm−1 suggested the coexistence of α- and β-glycosidic linkages in S-PPP3.

Congo red assay analysis

Polysaccharides possessing a triple-helical structure could interact with the acidic dye Congo red under alkaline conditions, resulting in a red shift of the maximum absorption wavelength [46]. As illustrated in Figure D, the S-PPP3 solution exhibited a red-shifted maximum absorption wavelength relative to the Congo red control solution, confirming the existence of a triple-helical structure.

Periodic acid oxidation analysis

The process of periodate oxidation could be inferred from the consumption of periodate, and the type of product, and its production, which in turn provide information about the glycosidic bond position and type [28]. The measured NaIO4 consumption was 4.03 ± 0.07 mmol, while the formic acid production reached 0.54 ± 0.03 mmol. The generation of formic acid indicated the presence of 1 → 6 glycosidic bonds. Additionally, the NaIO4 consumption exceeded twice the amount of formic acid produced, suggesting the existence of 1 → 2 and 1 → 4 glycosidic bonds, with the potential presence of 1 → 3 glycosidic linkages as well.

NMR analysis

In the 13C NMR spectrum, α-anomeric carbons resonated at 98–103 ppm, while β-anomeric carbons appeared at 103–110 ppm. Correspondingly in 1H NMR, α-anomeric protons showed signals at 5.0–5.4 ppm, contrasting with β-anomeric protons at 4.3–4.9 ppm [47]. Peaks in the δ1.1 to 1.4 region of 1H NMR and in the δ16 to 18 13C NMR region indicated the presence of –CH3 [48]. The signal at 174.87 ppm in the 13C NMR spectrum was assigned to the carboxyl carbon of GalAp residues. The remaining high-intensity peaks could be attributed to protons and carbon atoms at C-2 to C-5 or C-6 in the monomer, whose chemical shifts varied due to their different chemical environments in which they are found [49]. In the 1H NMR spectrum (Fig. 6A), the signal at 1.26 ppm and the signal at 16.53 ppm in the 13C NMR spectrum (Fig. 6B) were assigned to H-6 and C-6 of α-Rhap [50]. Anti-inflammatory activity of S-PPP3

Effects of S-PPP3 on LPS-induced inflammation of RAW 264.7 cells

NO is a reactive nitrogen species essential for numerous physiological processes, particularly in the regulation of inflammatory responses and disease pathogenesis [52]. Therefore, evaluating NO production under inflammatory conditions could provide valuable insight into the cellular anti-inflammatory properties of S-PPP3. In RAW 264.7 macrophages stimulated with LPS (model group), NO levels were markedly elevated compared to the untreated control group (normal group). Following S-PPP3 treatment, NO levels in LPS-stimulated cells were significantly decreased (Fig. 10B). The findings indicated that S-PPP3 may suppress NO oxidation and enhance its anti-inflammatory effects by competing with oxygen for nitrite radicals, consistent with observations in previous studies on plant extracts [53].

TNF-α and IL-6 are crucial pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted by RAW 264.7 macrophages upon infection or LPS stimulation [54]. These pro-inflammatory cytokines intensified the inflammatory response and are essential contributors to the progression of inflammation [55]. Dysregulated TNF-α and IL-6 secretion drives inflammatory pathogenesis by initiating and perpetuating disease progression [52,56]. Therefore, evaluating TNF-α and IL-6 levels could provide insight into the anti-inflammatory potential of bioactive components. The model group exhibited a significant increase in TNF-α and IL-6 secretion compared to the normal group. However, treatment with 16 μg/mL and 32 μg/mL S-PPP3 led to a marked reduction in their secretion, suggesting that S-PPP3 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating TNF-α and IL-6 production. Polygonum perfoliatum L. is widely distributed and has been used in Guizhou Miao medicine for centuries. The dried aerial parts of Polygonum perfoliatum L. are indicated for inflammatory conditions including eczema and furuncles, with a clinical dosage range of 15–30 g. Furthermore, Polygonum perfoliatum L. effectively treated pelvic inflammatory disease in rat models induced by intrauterine plastic tube placement. The treatment reduced endometrial congestion and edema while ameliorating histopathological damage, including uterine adhesions/occlusions, epithelial cell degeneration/necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration [57]. However, research on the pharmacological properties of its polysaccharides remains limited. In this study, we explored the anti-inflammatory activity of S-PPP3 and observed that it significantly mitigated LPS-induced inflammation in RAW264.7 cells at concentration up to 16 μg/mL. This activity was primarily due to the suppression of NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 production by S-PPP3. These results provide valuable insights for the continued development and application of Polygonum perfoliatum L. polysaccharides. However, additional studies were necessary to fully understand its mechanism of action.

Effects of S-PPP3 on cytokine mRNA expression in a model of cellular inflammation

In macrophages, NO overproduction is primarily mediated by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which is absent under physiological conditions but induced by high concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Once expressed, iNOS can form a positive feedback loop with these cytokines, exacerbating the inflammatory response. To elucidate the anti-inflammatory mechanism of S-PPP3, we measured the mRNA expression of IL-6, TNF-α, and iNOS in RAW264.7 cells. As shown in Fig. 11.A–C, the model group significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of all three cytokines (p < 0.01). In contrast, treatment with S-PPP3 significantly reversed this dysregulation, leading to a marked reduction in their expression (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). These results confirm that S-PPP3 effectively attenuates the inflammatory response in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells. Moreover, the current data provided only a theoretical basis for exploiting S-PPP3 as an anti-inflammatory agent. Future research should include well-designed clinical trials to evaluate the potential benefits of S-PPP3 in humans.

Conclusion

Polygonum perfoliatum L. polysaccharides were extracted using ultrasound-assisted methods. After a comprehensive comparison between the BBD model and the GA-BP neural network model parameters, the GA-BP neural network model was selected for optimizing the extraction process. The optimal extraction conditions determined were: material-to-liquid ratio of 35 mL/g, extraction time of 29 min, extraction temperature of 72 ℃, and extraction power of 415 W, resulting in a transfer rate of 155.73 %, a purity of 38.18 %, and a comprehensive score of (96.96 ± 6.23) %. Post-purification, S-PPP3 was obtained and analyzed by SEM, UV, FTIR, HPLC and NMR, which showed that it had a smooth surface, rough interior, and triple-helical structure, devoid of nucleic acids and proteins. S-PPP3 was composed of six monosaccharide types: L-Arabinose, D-Galacturonic acid, D-Galactose, D-Glucose, D-Mannose, and L-Rhamnose, in a molar ratio of 4.57:1.71:6.76:1.00:1.40:5.60, and had a molecular weight of approximately 79 kDa. The main backbone of S-PPP3 consisted primarily of repeating units of → 4)-β-D-Galp-(1 → and → 4,6)-β-D-Galp-(1 → . Side chains are linked at the C-6 position of → 4,6)-β-D-Galp-(1 → residues and comprise structural motifs including T-α-L-Araf-(1 → 5)-α-L-Araf-(1 → 5)-α-L-Araf-(1→, T-α-D-Manp-(1 → 3)-β-D-Glcp-(1 → 4)-α-D-GalAp-(1→, and T-α-L-Rhap-(1 → 2,4)-α-L-Rhap-(1 → 2,4)-α-L-Rhap-(1 → .In an LPS-induced inflammation model using RAW264.7 cells, S-PPP3 significantly inhibited the production of NO, TNF-α, and IL-6, demonstrating potent anti-inflammatory properties. These results provide insights for optimizing Polygonum perfoliatum L. polysaccharide extraction and establish a foundation for developing S-PPP3 as a functional food or anti-inflammatory therapeutic agent.
