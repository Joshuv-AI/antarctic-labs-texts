Improvement in Patient‐Reported Outcomes, High Rate of Return to Sport, and Secondary Surgery Rate Ranging From 0% to 9.8% After Hip Arthroscopy in Dancers and Athletes in Flexibility Sports: A Systematic Review
Authors: David R Maldonado, Andrew R Schab, Benjamin G Domb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo systematically review and report patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), rates of return to sport, and secondary surgeries in dancers and flexibility sport athletes who underwent hip arthroscopy.MethodsUsing the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses criteria, a systematic review was performed with a literature search of the PubMed and Embase in August 2025. Inclusion criteria were studies investigating surgical outcomes on dancers and flexibility sport athletes undergoing hip arthroscopy. Exclusion criteria included non‐English language studies, reviews, meta‐analyses, systematic reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, and editorial commentaries. Data for patient demographics, follow‐up time, PROMs, and rates of return to sport and secondary surgeries were collected. The minimal clinically important difference and patient acceptable symptom state rates were reported when available.ResultsSeven articles were included with 440 patients (460 hips). All studies had a retrospective study design, with mean follow‐up times from 23 to 79.1 months, with most of the patients being females (ranged from 61.2% to 100%). All studies reported baseline and postoperative PROMs data with significant improvement. Two studies reported minimal clinically important difference rates ranging from 76.9% to 85.7%. Return‐to‐sport rates ranged from 75.6% to 97%. Rate for revision hip arthroscopic surgery ranged from 0 to 9.8%.ConclusionsDancers and flexibility sport athletes who underwent hip arthroscopy showed significant improvement in PROMs with minimal clinically important difference rates ranging from 76.9% to 85.7%, a high average rate of return to sport of 84.6%, and an average revision hip arthroscopy rates ranging from 0% to 9.8% at short‐to‐mid‐term follow‐up.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, systematic review of Level III and IV studies.

Hip microinstability, generalized ligamentous laxity, and features of hip dysplasia, when combined with femoroacetabular impingement syndrome, are common clinical issues in dancers and athletes involved in flexibility sports. 1 , 2 , 3 Flexibility sports are activities that inherently require supraphysiologic range of motion, such as dance, cheer leading, and martial arts. 3 Hip arthroscopy has become an important tool for managing hip joint pathologies, especially among these athletes, who often sustain injuries due to extreme ranges of motion, repetitive movements, and the high demands placed on their hip joints. 4 Although favorable outcomes have been consistently reported across diverse patient populations, the specific subgroup of dancers and athletes involved in flexibility‐demanding sports presents unique challenges. These individuals place extraordinary physiologic demands on the hip joint, requiring not only pain relief and functional improvement but also restoration of high‐level range of motion and performance. Additionally, the pressure to perform and return to their sport—which predominantly affects a young population—creates a challenging situation following hip arthroscopy. 5 , 6 , 7

From a surgical perspective, restoring labral function, 8 accurately correcting bony morphology when necessary, 9 and managing the capsule through closure or plication are crucial for achieving favorable outcomes. 10 Understanding the rates of clinical improvement, achievement of meaningful outcomes, and return to preinjury activity levels is essential to guide both surgical decision‐making and patient counseling. However, the current body of literature undeniably falls short in providing a comprehensive synthesis of all available evidence on this topic, underscoring the necessity for a thorough and unified analysis.

The purpose of this study was to systematically review and report patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), rates of return to sport, and secondary surgeries in dancers and flexibility sport athletes who underwent hip arthroscopy. It was hypothesized that significant improvement in PROMs, high rate of return to sport, and low rate of secondary surgeries would be shown.

METHODS

Literature Search

In accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses criteria, 11 a systematic review was performed with a literature search of the PubMed and Embase in August 2025.

This systematic review was conducted to answer the following question: “In dancers and athletes involved in flexibility‐demanding sports undergoing hip arthroscopy, what are the outcomes in terms of patient‐reported outcome measure improvement, return to sport, and secondary surgery rates?”

The structured PICO format was as follows:

Population (P): dancers and athletes engaged in flexibility‐demanding sports (e.g., ballet, gymnastics, figure skating, martial arts) with femoroacetabular impingement or other intra‐articular hip pathology.

Intervention (I): hip arthroscopy with or without associated procedures (labral repair, femoroplasty, acetabuloplasty, capsular management).

Comparison (C): preoperative baseline status or other athletic populations reported in the included studies.

Outcomes (O): improvement in validated PROMs, rates of return to sport, including return to preinjury level of participation, and incidence of secondary surgeries, including revision hip arthroscopy or conversion to total hip arthroplasty.

Key words included in the PubMed search query were (“Hip Arthroscopy”[Mesh] OR “Arthroscopy”[Mesh] OR “hip arthroscopy” OR “hip preservation” OR “hip surgery”) AND (“Dance”[Mesh] OR dancer* OR ballet OR ballerina* OR “flexibility sport*” OR gymnast* OR gymnastics) AND (“Treatment Outcome”[Mesh] OR “Outcome Assessment, Health Care”[Mesh] OR outcome* OR “return to sport” OR RTS OR “athletic performance” OR “patient reported outcome*” OR PROM* OR PRO OR “hip score” OR HOS OR “Hip Outcome Score”). The search strategy was registered with the PROSPERO registrar (PROSPERO ID # CRD420251007965). Studies underwent full‐text review. Bibliographies and citation results were also searched for additional relevant articles.

Study Eligibility

The studies were reviewed by 2 researchers (A.R.S., D.R.M). Inclusion criteria consisted of clinical studies investigating surgical outcomes of patients undergoing hip arthroscopy in patients who were dancers or flexibility sport athletes. Exclusion criteria included non‐English language studies, reviews, meta‐analyses, systematic reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, and editorial commentaries. Any discrepancies in study inclusion were resolved by discussion until consensus was obtained, with the senior author providing the final decision (B.G.D).

Data Collection

The studies included in the review were evaluated for the year of publication, journal, institution, study design, dates of study inclusion, and level of evidence (LOE). The preoperative diagnosis, indications, and surgical techniques were recorded. Additional data extraction included patient demographics, radiographic indices, intraoperative findings, length of follow‐up, baseline and most recent PROMs, adverse effects, and secondary surgeries. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) and patient acceptable symptom state rates were reported when available. Data pooling was avoided due to the heterogeneity of the data included and an overall low LOE among studies. Statistical significance was established using a P value of .05.

Quality Assessment

Two authors (A.R.S., D.R.M.) independently assessed each selected article using the validated Methodological Index for Non‐randomized Studies criteria. 12 The Methodological Index for Non‐randomized Studies criteria are a scale with a maximal score of 16 points for noncomparative, nonrandomized studies and 24 points for comparative, nonrandomized studies based on a study's follow‐up period, statistical analysis, and quality of the control group. The LOE for each study was determined using the standard criteria reported by Hohmann et al. 13

Ethical Approval

This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. This study was carried out in accordance with relevant regulations of the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). Details that might disclose the identity of the subjects under study have been omitted. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board. (IRB: 5276).

RESULTS

Study Characteristics

The database search yielded 72 studies (Figure 1). There were 8 duplicates found in the database search. A total of 53 articles were removed because they did not include the terms “hip arthroscopy,” “outcomes,” or “dancer” or “flexibility athlete.” Eleven full‐text articles were reviewed for eligibility. An additional 4 studies were excluded by article type. There were 2 systematic reviews, 1 editorial commentary, and 1 article without PROMs or focused on nonoperative management. The 7 included articles were published from 2009 to 2024 (Table 1). 4 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 All studies had a retrospective study design, with mean follow‐up times from 23 to 79.1 months, and reported postoperative PROMs. There were 2 LOE III study 15 , 18 and 5 LOE IV. 4 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 19 The overall mean Methodological Index for Non‐randomized Studies score was 12.2 for the 5 noncomparative studies 4 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 19 and 19 for the 2 comparative studies (Table 2). 15 , 18 Common methodologic domains of weakness included no prospective calculation of study size, greater than 5% loss to follow‐up, and possible bias in endpoint assessment.

Patient Demographics

In the 7 studies, 4 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 there were 440 patients (460 hips) who were dancers or flexibility sport athletes. All patients underwent hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome or labral tears (Table 3). For dancers and flexibility sports athletes, the mean age and body mass index ranged from 19.5 to 36.1 years and 22.7 to 23.8 kg/m2, respectively. All the cohorts reported patient populations that ranged from 61.2% to 100% female patients.

Preoperative and Perioperative Data

Preoperative radiographic variables were reported in all studies (Table 4). 4 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 For dancers and flexibility sport athletes, the average preoperative lateral center‐edge angle and alpha angle ranged from 22.2° to 32.6° and 53.1° to 61.3°, respectively. For dancers and flexibility sport athletes, the average postoperative lateral center‐edge angle and alpha angle ranged from 21.5° to 30° and 37.0° to 47.9°, respectively. Intraoperative findings and procedures are documented in 6 of the 7 studies. Acetabular labral repair was performed in 84% to 100% of hip arthroscopies, while capsular repair or plication was performed in 86.3% to 100% of hip arthroscopies. 4 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

PROMs

Dancers and flexibility sport athletes who underwent hip arthroscopy showed significant improvement in all PROMs. The PROMs documented in the studies were Harris Hip Score, modified Harris Hip Scores (mHHS), Non‐Arthritic Hip Score, International Hip Outcome Score, Hip Outcome Score Sport‐Specific Subscale (HOS‐SSS), Hip Outcome Score Activities of Daily Living, Short Form 12, and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain (Table 5).

Average preoperative values for mHHS, HOS‐SSS, and VAS ranged from 57 to 77.1, 40.3 to 49.7, and 5.3 to 7.8, respectively. Return to Sport and Secondary Surgeries

Return to sport was reported in 6 of the 7 studies, 4 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 19 with return‐to‐sport rates ranging from 75.6% to 97%. Secondary surgeries were documented in 6 of the 7 studies. 4 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 18 , 19 Saks et al. 15 reported 10% of patients undergoing secondary surgery, whereas Larson et al. 16 reported 0% of patients needing secondary surgeries. Maldonado et al. 4 reported a rate of 9.8% revision arthroscopies and 7.8% for conversions to total hip arthroplasty. In borderline dysplastic and nondysplastic flexibility athletes, Jan et al. 18 reported a rate of 5.9% revision arthroscopies, and conversion rates to total hip arthroplasty of 0% for borderline dysplastic and 1% for nondysplastic flexibility athletes. Martinez et al. 19 reported no conversions to total hip arthroplasty.

DISCUSSION

The main findings of this systematic review were that significant improvement for several PROMs and a high rate to return to sport can be expected in dancers and flexibility sport athletes following hip arthroscopy with a revision surgery rate of 6.6% to 9.8% at short‐to‐mid‐term follow‐up.

Hip microinstability is a significant condition characterized by abnormal hip motion that can lead to pain, often accompanied by symptoms of hip joint instability. 20 , 21 This issue arises from either soft tissue damage or deficiencies in bone structure, and it has a profound impact on a dancer's hip function. 17 , 18 , 22 Saks et al. 15 have clearly shown that athletes involved in flexibility sports experience higher rates of femoral head cartilage lesions and more frequent tears of the ligamentum teres when compared with their counterparts in nonflexibility sports.

For dancers and other athletes in flexibility‐demanding sports, capsular closure or plication appears especially important. The propensity‐matched analysis by Jan et al. 18 showed that flexibility sport athletes with borderline hip dysplasia who underwent hip arthroscopy with capsular plication had outcomes and return‐to‐sport rates comparable to flexibility athletes without dysplasia, suggesting that restoring capsular tension may mitigate instability risk in a vulnerable subgroup. Similarly, Arshad et al. 3 reported that closure or plication of the capsule in hypermobile patients achieved PROMs comparable to nonhypermobile populations, suggesting that meticulous capsular management can mitigate instability risks in this subgroup.

In a magnetic resonance imaging‐based motion analysis, Charbonnier et al. 23 showed that female dancers experience dynamic impingement and femoral head subluxation, especially in the split position, with maximum stress concentrated in the posterosuperior area of the hip joint. Furthermore, Mitchell et al. 1 conducted a thorough analysis of 47 dancers using 5 types of plain radiographs, and their findings unequivocally indicate that hip subluxation occurs during splits for the majority of professional ballet dancers. Notably, they found that the magnitude of subluxation is significantly greater in women than in men, establishing a direct correlation between the extent of subluxation and an increasing alpha angle.

Ukwuani et al. 14 established that dancers had significant short‐term follow‐up PROMs’ improvement and were able to return to sport at high rates and at preinjury or higher levels after primary hip arthroscopy, 31% and 62.5%, respectively. Similarly, Larson et al. 16 reported significant improvement for the mHHS and VAS for pain at a minimum 3‐year follow‐up in dancers following primary hip arthroscopy. Subspine decompression, capsular plication, and femoroplasty were key surgical procedures performed in this cohort. As supraphysiologic hip motion that leads to femoroacetabular impingement syndrome is a common finding in these patients, the authors recommended extending the femoral osteochondroplasty distally to improve hip biomechanics.

At minimum of 5‐year follow‐up, Maldonado et al. 4 reported that primary hip arthroscopy also yields significant improvements in PROMs with a rate of returning to sport of 89.3% with 68% returning to the preinjury or higher level. Interestingly, none of the included studies reported cohorts achieving maximum scores in any of the PROMs. This may reflect the reality that even with significant clinical improvement, residual symptoms or functional limitations often persist in dancers and flexibility athletes who place extraordinary demands on the hip joint. Another important consideration is that reported PROMs may lack sensitivity to capture higher level performance outcomes specific to this population, thereby limiting the likelihood of observing ceiling effects. Taken together, these factors suggest that although hip arthroscopy provides meaningful improvements, existing PROMs may not fully reflect the unique functional recovery expectations of dancers and flexibility athletes.

Arthroscopic surgical hip management in dancers and flexibility sport athletes requires a combination of thoroughly arthroscopic techniques and personalized postoperative rehabilitation program. Curley et al. 24 proposed a stepwise ballet‐specific rehabilitation program to achieve this goal. The program emphasizes objective, criteria‐based progression rather than rigid timelines, guiding dancers from foundational rehabilitation through modified work, center exercises, impact activities, and finally performance‐level movements. Key milestones include pain‐free restoration of hip range of motion, restoration of strength symmetry, and dynamic postural control before advancing to higher demands. Return to sport reintroduction occurs around 3 to 4 months, center and across‐the‐floor work between 4 and 6 months, jumping and plyometric elements by 6 to 8 months, and performance or partnering typically after 8 to 9 months. This framework highlights the unique technical and physiological demands of ballet, underscoring the importance of sport‐specific rehabilitation strategies to optimize safe return to performance in dancers.

Limitations

The authors acknowledge limitations to the present study. All studies included were LOE III and IV; therefore, this review lacked high‐quality evidence. Moreover, the studies included reported on relatively a small sample population. The majority of patients reported in included studies were predominantly females, so the summarized outcomes in this review may not be generalizable to male flexibility athletes. This systematic review has the limitation of potential patient overlapping between included studies, particularly in cohorts originating from high‐volume centers that have published multiple reports on similar populations. Such overlap may lead to inadvertent duplication of cases, which could artificially inflate sample size and bias pooled outcome measures. Although the authors carefully screened study periods, inclusion criteria, and reported demographics to minimize this risk, the possibility of crossover cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings reported, and this can limit the generalizability of the findings in this review. A comparison between dancers and athletes in flexibility‐focused sports to the general athletic population was not part of this systematic review. Interpreting outcomes for dancers and athletes in flexibility‐focused sports presents unique challenges because their demands on the hip often surpass those of the general athletic population. Ballet and flexibility sports require an extreme range of motion, frequent turnout, and prolonged end‐range positions, placing significant stress on the hip capsule and labrum. As a result, a successful return to sport for these athletes often requires achieving nearly normal strength and aesthetic performance, whereas athletes in field or court sports may be allowed to participate at a lower functional level. Additionally, the repetitive microtrauma associated with these disciplines increases the risk of capsular laxity, labral injuries, and the potential need for secondary surgeries. These differences highlight the importance of interpreting outcomes cautiously when comparing dancers to the broader athletic population. Finally, this systematic review included studies with short‐term follow‐up; therefore, longevity of outcomes cannot be robustly assessed in this study.

CONCLUSIONS

Dancers and flexibility sport athletes who underwent hip arthroscopy showed significant improvement in PROMs with MCID rates ranging from 76.9% to 85.7%, a high average rate of return to sport of 84.6%, and an average revision hip arthroscopy rates ranging from 0% to 9.8% at short‐to‐mid‐term follow‐up.
