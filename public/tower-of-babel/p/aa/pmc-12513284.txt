Exploring rare mitochondrial DNA in Leber hereditary optic neuropathy
Authors: Shanshan Cao, Yong Liu, Mingming Sun, Yuan Zhang, Yonghua Sun, Quangang Xu, Shihui Wei, Huanfen Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder primarily caused by mutations in MT-ND1, MT-ND4, and MT-ND6, leading to retinal ganglion cell degeneration and severe vision loss. While 90%–95% of cases involve three common mutations (m.11778G ​> ​A, m.3460G ​> ​A, m.14484T ​> ​C), the genetic and clinical profiles of rare mutations remain poorly characterized, contributing to diagnostic challenges.MethodsThis cohort study analyzed 26 genetically confirmed LHON patients harboring rare mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations. Patients underwent best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), optical coherence tomography (OCT) measurements (peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer [pRNFL] and macular ganglion cell layer [GCL] thickness), and neuroimaging findings. Prognostic outcomes were compared between pediatric (≤16 years) and adult (>16 years) subgroups.ResultsThe cohort (male:female ​= ​4.2:1) exhibited a median onset age of 17 years (range:4–42), with 30.77% unilateral involvement. Rare mutations were distributed in MT-ND4(34.62%,m.11696G ​> ​A), MT-ND1(34.62%,including m.3733G ​> ​A/m.3866T ​> ​C), and MT-ND6 (23.08%, m.14502T>C), with 26.92% harboring dual mutations. Younger patients showed significantly better visual recovery (59.09% vs. 22.73% achieving BCVA ≥ 0.3, P ​= ​0.014), despite comparable baseline vision and structural OCT parameters (pRNFL/GCL thickness, all P ​> ​0.05). T2 hyperintensity in the optic nerve magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was present in 38.46% of cases.ConclusionsOur study probes into the clinical and genetic diversity of LHON with rare mtDNA mutations, revealing varied clinical presentations, such as more frequent unilateral involvement and enhanced optic nerve T2 MRI signals. Visual recovery was significantly better in the younger cohort. These results suggest the need for broader genetic testing in atypical LHON cases and offer insights into better prognostic strategies for new therapies.Keywords: Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy, Maternal inheritance, Mitochondrial DNA, Rare mutation

Introduction

Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA(mtDNA), which results from primary degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and primarily affects the optic nerve, leading to acute or subacute painless vision loss.1 It predominantly occurs in males aged 15 to 35, and has a prevalence of 1 in 30000–50000.2 LHON is caused by point mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) that impact NADH dehydrogenase, which is part of complex I in the electron transport chain. Consequently, this condition is maternally inherited.3 Over 50 mtDNA mutations are linked to LHON, but the three most common mutations that can independently cause the disease are m.11778G ​> ​A in ND4, m.3460G ​> ​A in ND1, and m.14484T ​> ​C in ND6.4 The remaining mitochondrial mutations are rare, meaning they may also occur in the normal population, but at lower frequencies than in patients with LHON. However, these rare mutations may enhance the pathogenic potential and penetrance of LHON, when they are associated with a primary mutation.

Approximately 90%–95% of patients are affected by the three prevalent primary mutations: m.11778A ​> ​G, m.3460G ​> ​A, and m.14484T ​> ​C.5, 6, 7 There have been several reports on common mutations associated with LHON. However, the range of mtDNA mutations causing the residual 5%–10% of cases remains incomplete and often poorly characterized. Additionally, the complexity of the disease complicates diagnosis for ophthalmologists, often resulting in underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis. After decades of research, ophthalmologists have become familiar with the clinical presentations and genetic features of the three common mtDNA mutations. However, Research on individuals with rare mtDNA mutations is somewhat restricted owing to the limited number of instances.

To address this gap, this cohort presented clinical data from LHON patients with rare mutations, analyzing their gene mutations and clinical features, to enhance and provide insights for better diagnosing LHON and laying a foundation for future research.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twenty-six LHON patients with rare mutations who visited the department of neuro-ophthalmology in the Chinese People's Liberation Army General Hospital between March 2017 and December 2022 were selected. Inclusion criteria: 1) acute or subacute painless visual loss; 2) visual field defects; 3) confirmation of optic nerve dysfunction through Visual Evoked Potential (VEP) examination; 4) confirmation of mtDNA mutations through complete mtDNA sequencing. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) uncertainty regarding the onset of the disease; 2) concurrent with other ocular conditions.; 3) other types of optic neuropathies, including compressive, infiltrative, toxic, nutritional, traumatic, ischemic, and radiation-induced optic neuropathies, as well as patients with these predisposing factors.

All patients provided written informed consent, and for those under 18, their guardians signed the consent form on their behalf. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Chinese People's Liberation Army General Hospital (S2021-127-01) and strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki Principles.

Examination

Basic information about the patients including age, sex, follow-up duration, and mutations was collected. Patients received an extensive eye examination that included an assessment of Best Corrected Visual Acuity (BCVA), a slit lamp inspection, evaluation of the retina, visual field tests, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and additional imaging tests.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) . Normally distributed data are shown as mean ​± ​standard deviation (mean ​± ​SD). For non-normally distributed data, the median (interquartile range, IQR) was used for statistical description, whereas count data are depicted as percentages. Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the Pearson chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher's exact test. A two-sided significance test was conducted with a threshold of α ​= ​0.05. P ​< ​0.05 was considered significant.

Results

This study included 26 patients diagnosed with LHON resulting from rare mutations. The median age of onset was 17 years (range of 4–42 years) (Fig. 1A), with 21 males (80.77%) and 5 females (19.23%), resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 4.2:1 (Fig. 1B). Eighteen patients (69.23%) had bilateral involvement, while eight patients (30.77%) had unilateral involvement (Fig. 2). Of the 44 affected eyes, 79.55% (35 eyes) recorded a BCVA of 0.1 or lower at the nadir. Ten (38.46%) patients received intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP), while 21 patients (80.77%) were treated with coenzyme Q10 and Idebenone as energy replacement therapy for mitochondrial dysfunction. Among the 26 participants, the mean follow-up duration was 50.77 ​± ​16.78 months. Detailed pedigree investigations were conducted for all 26 LHON patients, and patient 17-who carries m.3733G ​> ​A variant-had an affected maternal uncle previously diagnosed with LHON and confirmed to harbor the identical mutation. Discussion

In this single-centre retrospective study, we enrolled patients with LHON harbouring rare mutations, characterised their clinical phenotype, and compared visual outcomes across age-stratified cohorts. These LHON patients carrying rare mutations showed a diversity of clinical phenotypes.

The continuous advancement of genetic testing technology, starting from the PCR method that detects only three primary mutations to full-length sequencing of mitochondrial ring genes and even whole exome sequencing and whole genome sequencing, has led to the development of a new mitochondrial ring gene sequencing technology.8 Consequently, an increasing number of rare mutations associated with LHON have been identified.9 Our study identified four main rare mutations: m.11696G ​> ​A (34.62%, 9 patients), m.14502T ​> ​C (23.08%, 6 patients), m.3733G ​> ​A (11.54%, 3 patients), and m.3866T ​> ​C (11.54%, 3 patients). Among these mutations, m.11696G ​> ​A was the most prevalent rare mitochondrial mutation associated with LHON, while two patients also carried m.11778G ​> ​A, and one patient had m.14568C ​> ​T and this mutation occurs at position 11696 in the MT-ND4 gene, where guanine (G) is replaced by adenine (A), leading to an amino acid change from isoleucine to valine at position 312.10The pathogenic roles of the mutations, which one is dominant, and their impact on disease prognosis require further study. Research has identified the m.11778G ​> ​A mutation in mtDNA as the molecular basis of visual loss in this pedigree.11 Furthermore, the m.11696G ​> ​A mutation may act synergistically with the m.11778G ​> ​A mutation to influence the phenotypic expression of LHON.12 This synergistic effect may be associated with haplogroup D4, which is relatively prevalent in Chinese clinical populations. The study also suggests that other modifying factors (such as nuclear modifier genes and environmental factors) may influence the phenotypic variability observed in LHON.12

Among the three common LHON types, the m.11778G ​> ​A variant is the most common and has the worst prognosis,13 with the m.14484G ​> ​A mutation carrying the most favorable visual prognosis.5,14 In our study, we found that the vision of two patients with the m.11778G ​> ​A mutation did not show significant improvement after treatment. In contrast, the vision of patients with the m.14484T ​> ​C improved to 0.5, which is consistent with the aforementioned description.15

The high prevalence of unilateral involvement (30.77%) observed in our cohort with rare mtDNA mutations represents a striking deviation from the classic bilateral presentation typically seen in LHON patients harboring common mutations (m.11778G ​> ​A, m.14484T ​> ​C, m.3460G ​> ​A).16,17 While 75% of classic LHON cases develop sequential bilateral vision loss and 25% experience simultaneous bilateral onset, our findings suggest that rare mutations may follow distinct disease expression patterns. This atypical unilateral manifestation could be explained by several mechanisms: First, mutation-specific pathobiology: rare mutations might induce a milder bioenergetic defect, permitting transient compensation by unaffected retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) in the contralateral eye. Second, haplogroup interactions: The co-occurrence of rare mutations with specific mitochondrial haplogroups could modify disease penetrance and laterality. Third, nuclear modifier genes: Unidentified nuclear-encoded factors may provide transient protection to one eye, delaying contralateral involvement. The presence of atypical visual field defects further supports the concept of mutation-specific phenotypic spectra in LHON. These findings challenge the conventional diagnostic paradigm that considers unilateral presentation as exclusionary for LHON. From a clinical perspective, this implies that we need to expand genetic testing for rare mitochondrial DNA mutations in patients with unilateral optic neuropathies. At the same time, it is important to closely monitor the unaffected eyes of individuals who carry these mutations over time.

In this study, six patients (23.08%) had the m.14502T ​> ​C mutation, the second most common rare mutation. This mutation substitutes isoleucine with valine at position 58 in ND6 (I58V), which is a highly conserved region. Most cases of this mutation are from Asia, especially China.18,19 One patient carried both m.14502T ​> ​C and m.14484T ​> ​C mutations, and one patient carried both m.14502T ​> ​C and m.3635G ​> ​A. Research indicates that patients with only the m.14502T ​> ​C mutation, without other common primary mutations, typically experience moderate to mild visual impairment.20 Some studies hypothesize that the m.14502T ​> ​C mutation acts as a phenotypic modifier of LHON rather than as a pathogenic mutation,14 and it often occurs alongside a primary mutation that worsens mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction.21,22 In the Chinese population, the m.14502T ​> ​C variant, which is usually a secondary or modifier mutation, has also been reported as a primary pathogenic mutation in the absence of other mitochondrial DNA changes.19,21,23In another study, protein modeling predictions indicated that the m.14502T ​> ​C mutation has a slight effect on protein structure. In our study, six patients (nine eyes) with m.14502T ​> ​C mutation had the worst VA at nadir<0.3, However, five of these eyes improved to 0.3 or above at follow-up, which indicates patients carrying the m.14502T ​> ​C mutation tend to have a better prognosis.

This study identified seven patients with dual mitochondrial DNA mutations who exhibited distinct clinical features, including high rates of bilateral involvement (85.71%, 6/7 cases) and poor visual outcomes (76.92% of affected eyes with final VA < 0.3). These findings suggest that dual mutations may serve as both a marker for disease severity and a predictor of worse visual prognosis in LHON. The observed mutation burden effect highlights the importance of comprehensive mitochondrial genome analysis for accurate prognosis and personalized management. The clinical implications are twofold: first, patients with dual mutations require closer monitoring due to their higher risk of bilateral disease and poor visual recovery; second, these findings may inform genetic counseling and therapeutic decision-making. Further validation through multicenter studies is required to confirm these findings and examine potential mutation-specific synergistic effects, which may enable more accurate risk assessment and personalized treatment approaches for this patient population.

This study reveals that while pediatric and adult LHON patients experience similar initial visual impairment, pediatric patients demonstrate significantly better visual recovery (59.09% vs 22.73% achieving BCVA ≥ 0.3, P ​= ​0.014). This suggests enhanced neuro-regenerative capacity in pediatric patients, potentially due to greater neuronal plasticity in the developing visual system.24 The absence of significant structural differences in OCT despite disparate functional outcomes implies that molecular mechanisms rather than anatomical preservation primarily drive this age-dependent recovery pattern. These findings have important clinical implications, highlighting pediatric LHON as a potentially more treatment-responsive subtype that may benefit from early intervention. While our results underscore age as a key prognostic factor, further research with larger cohorts is needed to investigate potential interactions between age and genetic background.

This study included 12 patients (22 eyes) aged 16 years or younger and 14 patients (22 eyes) aged over 16 years old. Of these, a total of 42 eyes were completed with OCT. No significant differences in OCT parameters, including those in the macular and optic disc regions, were found between the two groups. Among younger patients, 21 eyes (95.45%) presented with a nadir BCVA < 0.3, compared with 18 eyes (81.82%) in the older cohort; this difference did not reach statistical significance (P ​= ​0.342). At final follow-up, 13 eyes (59.09%) in the younger group achieved BCVA ≥ 0.3 versus 5 eyes (22.73%) in the older group, indicating a significantly better visual prognosis for younger patients (P ​= ​0.014).

Previous studies have reported that MRI T2 STIR imaging shows increased signal intensity in the optic nerves, chiasm, and tracts of LHON patients.25, 26, 27 We analyzed the optic nerve MRI of 26 patients within 6 months, and 38.46% of patients showed an increased T2 signal on MRI scans, which line with earlier research regarding MRI observations of the optic nerves and chiasm in patients with LHON,28 where T2 high-signal lesions were not linked to mutation types from the onset of symptoms to the MRI assessment. T2 hyperintense lesions can result from axonal degeneration, demyelination, or damage to astrocytes and oligodendrocytes.29,30 Our findings may suggest that T2 sequence enhancement on MRI is not uncommon in LHON patients with rare mutations. Notably, MRI anomalies in LHON represent nonspecific indicators, and clinicians should exercise caution when incorporating these imaging results into the diagnostic criteria for this condition.

OCT assessments were completed for 42 eyes, revealing no statistically significant differences in macular or optic disc parameters between age groups (all P ​> ​0.05). Histological studies confirm that LHON primarily impacts RGCs and optic nerve function while leaving photoreceptor cells and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells unaffected.31 Fifty percent of RGCs in the retina are found in the macula.32 LHON primarily causes damage to the small caliber fibers in the central part of the papillomacular bundle (PMB), while the larger caliber fibers in the peripheral area are less affected. The pRNFL, particularly the unmyelinated encapsulated nerve fibers in the front part of the sieve plate, has the highest demand for mitochondrial energy supply.33 This provides a foundation for analyzing the pRNFL and GCL layers in LHON patients using OCT to evaluate pathological changes. Although pRNFL and GCL thickness did not differ significantly between patients aged 16 years or younger and those older, future studies will examine structural outcomes across rare versus common mutation sites.

However, this study has several limitations: First, the study employed a retrospective approach, which may be subject to selection bias and incomplete data records. Secondly, the limited sample size may undermine the precision of the findings. Thirdly, the absence of multi-center clinical validation could result in a biased interpretation of the findings. Furthermore, although this study underscores the variability of clinical phenotypes and enhances our understanding of patients with rare mitochondrial DNA mutations, it lacks evidence explaining their mechanistic basis.

Consequently, future research should aim to expand the sample size, consider employing a prospective design, conduct multi-center clinical validation, and include laboratory tests to confirm our findings and explore potential mechanisms for rare mutations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that LHON associated with rare mtDNA mutations (m.11696G ​> ​A, m.14502T ​> ​C, m.3733G ​> ​A, m.3866T ​> ​C, m.3497C ​> ​T, m.4160T ​> ​C, m.4171C ​> ​A, m.13051G ​> ​A, and m.13513G ​> ​A) exhibits distinct clinical features, including atypical unilateral involvement, frequent optic nerve T2 hyperintensities, and better visual recovery in pediatric patient. While the m.14502T ​> ​C mutation showed relatively favorable outcomes, genotype-phenotype correlations were confirmed by the poor prognosis of m.11778G ​> ​A variants. These findings expand the clinical spectrum of LHON, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive genetic testing for accurate diagnosis and prognosis, particularly in atypical cases. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to further characterize these rare mutations and optimize personalized management strategies for this genetically heterogeneous disorder.
