Malignant Hyperthermia, Rhabdomyolysis, and Critically Ill Myopathy in the Same Child Require Workup for Muscle Disease
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Recently Accamma et al. article about a 16-year-old diabetic female admitted with severe diabetic ketoacidosis (pH 6.89) and treated with intubation and mechanical ventilation, rehydration, and insulin came to our attention.1 Despite these measures, the patient suffered cardiogenic shock and acute renal failure.1 In addition, malignant hyperthermia (MH) developed 21 hours after admission with a maximum creatine-kinase value of 59549 U/L, which was treated with cooling, saline infusions, paracetamol, tracheotomy, and extra-corporal membrane oxygenation.1 Although this treatment was complicated by pulmonary embolism and critically ill myopathy, she was successfully weaned off and discharged with normal cardiac and renal function after 124 hospital days.1 Several points require discussion.

The first limitation is that the patient was not screened for mutated genes associated with MH susceptibility (MHS). All patients who experience MH or MH-like symptoms and signs should undergo genetic panel testing for known MHS genes (e.g., RYR1, CACNA1S, STAC3).2 If this is not conclusive, whole exome sequencing should be performed. Knowing whether MHS is due to a genetic cause or secondary (e.g., hyperglycemic, hyperosmolar syndrome) is crucial not only for genetic counseling but also for assessing the recurrence risk.

A second limitation is that the family history was not mentioned.1 If one assumes that MH has a genetic cause, we should know whether the parents or other first-degree relatives have ever had MH or whether either of them had a neuromuscular disorder known to be associated with MHS.

A third point is that the index patient was not screened for neuromuscular disorders, some of which are known to be associated with MH.3 Malignant hyperthermia has been particularly reported in central core disease, muscular dystrophies, King-Denborough syndrome, and metabolic myopathies, and therefore should be excluded in the reported patient. Since the index patient suffered from diabetes and developed lactic acidosis during the MH phase, a mitochondrial disorder in particular should be excluded. In patients with rhabdomyolysis and especially in those who have developed critically ill myopathy, evaluation for neuromuscular disorders is generally essential. If someone develops critically ill myopathy, subclinical myopathy should be also suspected.

A fourth point is that the medications the patient received from admission until the onset of rhabdomyolysis were not mentioned. Since several medications can trigger MH or rhabdomyolysis, it is imperative that all of them are listed and discussed as potential triggers of MH respectively rhabdomyolysis.4 Did the patient receive desflurane, sevoflurane, isoflurane, methoxyflurane, halothane, enflurane, depolarizing muscle relaxants, or neuroleptics? Has malignant neuroleptic syndrome been ruled out as one cause of MH? Did the patient receive propofol and was propofol infusion syndrome ruled out?

We disagree that the cardiogenic shock was refractory.1 Since the patient survived, the disease was either treatable or resolved spontaneously. The cause of hyperglycemia should be specified. Was it due to forgetting insulin or oral anti-diabetic treatment, dietary failure, poor nutrition, or an associated acute illness?

In summary, patients with MH, rhabdomyolysis, and critical ill myopathy, require thorough work-up for subclinical or only mildly manifesting myopathy.
