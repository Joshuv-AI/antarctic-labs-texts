Potential urine NMR Metabolomic signatures for early diagnosis of paediatric tuberculosis
Authors: Andrea López-Suárez, Patricia Comella-del-Barrio, Pilar Alonso-Moreno, Yaiza Marín, Palmira Villa-Valverde, Mar Santos-Sebastián, Antoni Noguera-Julian, Anna Gamell, Paula Rodríguez-Molino, Jara Hurtado-Gallego, Daniel Blázquez-Gamero, Ana Belén Jiménez-Jiménez, Adriana Navas, Elena del Castillo, Marta Llorente, Talía Sainz, José Domínguez, José Luis Izquierdo-García, Begoña Santiago-García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly diagnosis of tuberculosis (TB) in children is hampered by non-specific symptoms, low bacillary loads, and difficulties in obtaining respiratory samples. We evaluated urinary metabolomics using high-resolution nuclear magnetic resonance (HR-1H-NMR) spectroscopy as a non-invasive tool to discriminate TB disease, TB infection (TBI), and healthy controls (HC). Urine samples from 101 children enrolled in the Spanish Paediatric TB Network (pTBred) were retrospectively analysed, including 62 TB cases (26 microbiologically confirmed [cTB]), 17 TBI, and 22 HC. Metabolic fingerprints were generated via1 H-NMR spectroscopy. Group discrimination was assessed using partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) with cross-validation. Metabolites were selected using VIP > 2 and p < 0.05. Validated PLS-DA models (TB vs. HC, cTB vs. HC and cTB vs. TBI) achieved ≥ 70% accuracy (AUC-ROC 0.867–0.971), with sensitivities ranging from 69.1% to 90.0% and specificities from 77.3% to 86.7%. Discrimination was strongest for cTB vs. TBI (AUC-ROC 0.971; sensitivity 90.0%; specificity 86.7%). A set of discriminatory metabolites was identified across validated comparisons. Compared with HC, TB disease was characterized by increased levels of phenylalanine and two unidentified NMR signals, together with reductions in several energy and nitrogen-related metabolites; benzoate and histidine showed borderline differences. In contrast, TBI was associated with higher levels of isoleucine, N-acetylglutamine, glutamine, creatinine and 2-hydroxyvalerate. Urine HR-1H-NMR metabolomics suggests potential distinct metabolic signatures across TB states. While not yet suitable as a confirmatory test, these preliminary findings show potential as a triage tool to help prioritise children for microbiological testing and may eventually reduce the need for invasive sampling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40619-y.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains the leading cause of death from a single infectious pathogen worldwide, despite being both preventable and curable. In 2023, 10.8 million new cases were reported, with 1.3 million (12%) cases in children under 14 years, accounting for 15% of global TB-related deaths1. There is an urgent need for improved diagnostic strategies that require minimal sample preparation and suitable for point-of-care (POC) or near-POC use in high-burden settings1,2.

Children—especially those under 2 years—are highly vulnerable to Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection (TBI), progression to active disease, and severe extrapulmonary forms due to their immature immune system1. Limited access to care, the clinical complexity of paediatric TB, and the suboptimal performance of existing tests further complicate diagnosis. Symptoms are often non-specific and radiological findings frequently overlap with other respiratory conditions. Immunological tests such as the tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon gamma release assays (IGRAs) cannot discriminate TBI from active disease3 and have limited sensitivity in early childhood. Microbiological confirmation from respiratory samples remains the reference standard for pulmonary TB, but young children rarely expectorate and often require invasive procedures (gastric lavage or nasopharyngeal aspiration). Even when obtained, culture and molecular tests perform less well in low-bacterial-load specimens3; consequently, microbiological confirmation is achieved in fewer than half of paediatric cases, delaying diagnosis and treatment4,5.

In recent years, the use of alternative, non-invasive specimens has moved from research into clinical practice6–8. Since 2021, World Health Organization (WHO) recommends stool-based GeneXpert MTB/RIF Ultra (Cepheid, USA) for paediatric TB, with moderate sensitivity (40%-75%) and high specificity (98%) versus culture9. Urine-based tests such as the Alere Determine TB LAM (Abbott Diagnostics, USA; for HIV-positive individuals) and the emerging Fujifilm SILVAMP TB LAM (FujiLAM®, Fujifilm Corporation, Japan)10 further reflect a shift toward child-friendly and field-adapted diagnostics11,12. In parallel, WHO target product profiles (TPPs) emphasise that non-sputum tests should achieve high specificity for confirmatory use, while also recognising the value of triage/screening tools to prioritise children for further testing13.

Metabolomics—the large-scale study of small molecules in biofluids, cells, tissues or whole organisms14,15—offers a path to TB biomarker discovery. The main analytical platforms are mass spectrometry (MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy16. MS provides higher analytical sensitivity, whereas NMR offers excellent reproducibility, robustness and minimal sample preparation, with the added potential of portable benchtop devices17. Metabolomics has yielded insights into disease progression and treatment responses across infectious and chronic conditions18–20, and can capture biochemical signatures across the infection-disease spectra of TB21. While blood22–24 and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)20,25 have been studied, relatively few works have explored urine metabolomics for paediatric TB diagnosis26–28.

This multicentre study explores high-resolution proton NMR (HR 1H NMR) urine metabolic fingerprints to discriminate among children with TB disease, TBI and healthy controls (HC). By leveraging the quantitative capability of 1H-NMR and the minimal preparation required for urine, this approach aims to support the development of accessible, non-invasive diagnostic strategies for childhood TB29, within the framework of WHO TPPs for non-sputum diagnostics13.

Results

Study population and clinical characteristics

During the study period, urine samples were collected from 101 children recruited from the Spanish Paediatric TB Research Network (pTBred) cohort across eight centres. Among them, 62 were diagnosed with TB: 26 (41.9%) had microbiologically confirmed TB (cTB) and 36 (58.1%) had unconfirmed TB (ucTB). Additionally, 17 children were diagnosed with TBI, and 22 were HC (Fig. 1). Overall, 41.9% of the participants were female, and the median age was 6.7 years [IQR: 6.4–9.3], with no significant differences in sex or age across study groups. Comorbidities were identified in 9 (8.9%) children with TB (6 cTB and 3 ucTB), including celiac disease, urinary tract infection, cardiovascular disease, neurological disorder, asthma, Down syndrome, Poland syndrome, prematurity and sickle cell disease. No participants with known HIV infection were enrolled in the study. Ten children (16.1%) had been vaccinated with BCG (Table 1).

Significant differences were observed between the cTB and ucTB groups in terms of anatomical site and disease severity. Among cTB cases, 13 (50.0%) had pulmonary TB (PTB), 9 (34.6%) extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), and 4 (15.4%) both PTB and EPTB. In contrast, nearly all ucTB cases (35; 97.2%) had PTB, with only 1 (2.8%) presenting both PTB and EPTB (p < 0.05). Disease severity also varied significantly: 14 (53.8%) of cTB cases were classified as severe, compared to only 4 (11.1%) in the ucTB group (p < 0.05).

Exploratory data analysis (unsupervised PCA)

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on HR 1H NMR urine spectra from all 101 participants to assess inherent clustering patterns. The PCA did not show clear group separations. Nine samples were identified as outliers in the PCA score plot—6 from the cTB group, 1 from the ucTB group, and 2 from the TBI group—and were excluded from subsequent analyses (Suppl. Fig. 1).

Metabolomic signatures (supervised PLS-DA models)

The results presented below are based on total area normalization. For completeness, we also performed normalization using the “pooled sample from group” procedure; the corresponding PLS-DA models are shown in the Supplementary Results and were virtually identical, yielding comparable model performance, VIP profiles, and permutation test statistics.

To evaluate the robustness of the supervised models and rule out overfitting, we performed PLS-DA and OPLS permutation tests (n = 20) for all group comparisons.

The TB vs. HC, cTB vs. HC, and cTB vs. TBI models passed permutation testing, with statistically significant R²Y and Q² values (OPLS) and PLS-DA permutation p-values ≤ 0.05, supporting the validity of these discriminations (Suppl. Table 1). In contrast, the TB vs. TBI comparison did not reach statistical significance in the permutation test and therefore shows a high risk of overfitting. Accordingly, this model is interpreted as exploratory, and VIP values from this comparison were not used to support metabolite selection. Detailed permutation plots and results are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

TB vs. HC

The PLS-DA model comparing all TB cases (cTB + ucTB) to HC achieved 70% accuracy (R²=0.36, Q²=0.13) (Fig. 2A). Permutation tests confirmed the statistical robustness of this model (p < 0.05), supporting that the separation is unlikely to be due to random class structure. Twenty-five spectral regions corresponding to 11 metabolites were identified, with a VIP-score > 2 as relevant for this classification, including phenylalanine, glucose, glutamine, creatinine, citrate, hippurate, mannitol, glutamate, glycine, 1-methylhistidine and 2-aminoadipic acid and an unknown NMR signal (δ 7.95). Of these metabolites, only 9 (phenylalanine, glucose, citrate, hippurate, mannitol, glutamate, glycine, 1-methylhistidine and 2-aminoadipic acid) showed statistically significant differences between the studied groups. Additionally, two metabolites (lactate, creatine) and an unknown NMR signal that did not predefined VIP threshold, were also found to be statistically significant (Table 2). The model yielded an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.867 (95% CI, 0.721–0.954) (Fig. 3A), with 69.1% sensitivity (95% CI, 59.9–81.3) and 77.3% specificity (95% CI, 59.8–94.8).

cTB vs. HC

When focusing on cTB vs. HC, model performance improved, achieving 78% accuracy (R²=0.78, Q²=0.25) (Fig. 2B). The permutation test yielded p = 0.05 for the PLS-DA model, suggesting borderline but acceptable robustness. OPLS permutation results (R²Y and Q² < 0.05) further support the statistical validity of this comparison. Twenty-one spectral regions corresponding to 8 metabolites were selected using a VIP-score > 2: phenylalanine, glutamine, creatinine, citrate, hippurate, mannitol, lactate and glutamate, and an unknown NMR signal (δ 7.95). Among these, only 6 metabolites (phenylalanine, citrate, hippurate, mannitol, lactate and glutamate) showed statistically significant between the groups. In addition, 3 other metabolites (2-hydroxyvalerate, glycine and 2-aminoadipic acid) and 2 unknown NMR signals were identified as statistically significant despite not meeting the predefined VIP-score threshold (> 2) (Table 2). The AUC-ROC was 0.899 (95% CI, 0.722–1.000) (Fig. 3B), with 85.0% sensitivity (95% CI, 69.4–100) and 81.8% specificity (95% CI, 65.7–97.9).

TB vs. TBI

The model comparing all TB cases with TBI achieved 77% accuracy (R² = 0.30, Q² = 0.0034). The AUC-ROC was 0.775 (95% CI: 0.526–0.954). However, this model did not pass permutation testing, indicating a high risk of overfitting. Therefore, this comparison is considered exploratory, and no claims regarding discriminatory biomarkers are made based on this model. VIP values from this comparison were not used in downstream interpretation.

cTB vs. TBI

A separate model restricted to microbiologically confirmed TB and TBI showed stronger discrimination. The PLS-DA model (Fig. 2C) achieved 77% accuracy (R² = 0.92, Q² = 0.32), with 22 spectral regions corresponding to 9 metabolites, including benzoate, phenylalanine, histidine, N-acetylglutamine, glutamine, 2-hydroxyvalerate, citrate, hippurate and glutamate and one unknown NMR signal (δ 8.09). Among these, glutamate with a p-value = 0.08 did not show statistically significant between the groups. Furthermore, three additional metabolites (isoleucine, mannitol and lactate) were identified that did not reach the VIP-score threshold but were nevertheless statistically significant in discriminating between the two groups (Table 2). The AUC-ROC (Fig. 3C) was 0.971 (95% CI: 0.869–1.000), with 90.0% sensitivity (95% CI, 76.9–100) and 86.7% specificity (95% CI, 69.5–100). Both PLS-DA (p = 0.05) and OPLS permutation tests (R²Y < 0.05, Q² < 0.05) supported the robustness of this model, indicating that the cTB vs. TBI separation is statistically meaningful and unlikely to arise by chance.

Other comparisons

PLS-DA models comparing ucTB to TBI, and stratifying TB cases by anatomical site (PTB vs. EPTB) failed to identify clear separation or metabolic clustering. The exploratory model between PTB and EPTB showed low discrimination between groups (accuracy 47%, R² = 0.92, Q² = −0.47) and did not pass permutation testing, likely reflecting the limited number of EPTB cases (Suppl. Fig. 3).

Metabolite intensities across study groups

Table 2 summarizes the relative intensities of the identified metabolites across study groups. In the comparison between TB disease and healthy controls (TB vs. HC), significantly higher levels were observed for phenylalanine and one unknown NMR signal (δ 7.95), whereas significantly lower levels were found for glucose, citrate, hippurate, mannitol, lactate, creatine, glutamate, glycine, 1-metylhistidine, 2-aminoadipic acid (with an unidentified contribution), and another unknown NMR signal (δ 7.68). Benzoate and histidine showed borderline differences in this comparison.

When restricting the analysis to microbiologically confirmed TB (cTB vs. HC), phenylalanine and the unknown signals (δ 7.95 and δ 8.09) remained significantly increased, while 2-hydroxyvalerate, citrate, hippurate, mannitol, lactate, glutamate, glycine, and 2-aminoadipic acid were significantly decreased; glucose (p-value = 0.05) also showed significant differences between groups.

In the comparison between cTB and TBI, several metabolites differed significantly between groups. cTB was characterized by higher levels of benzoate, phenylalanine, histidine and one unknown signal (δ 8.09), whereas TBI showed significantly higher levels of isoleucine, N-acetylglutamine, glutamine and 2-hydroxyvalerate. In addition, citrate, hippurate, mannitol and lactate were significantly lower in cTB compared with TBI (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This multicentre study suggest that urine-based HR 1H NMR metabolomics has the potential to discriminate among children with TB, TBI, and HC, identifying condition-specific metabolic signatures that may serve as candidate non-invasive biomarkers for disease classification. This approach addresses critical diagnostic challenges in paediatric TB, particularly the need for accessible, accurate tools suitable for children.

Current TB diagnostics in children rely primarily on respiratory samples requiring deep bronchial secretions, which young children are typically unable to produce spontaneously. As a result, nasopharyngeal aspiration and gastric lavage are often necessary. These procedures are invasive, must be performed early in the morning by trained personnel, cause stress and discomfort for the child, and remain suboptimal in terms of bacteriological performance30. Given these challenges, there is increasing research into non-invasive alternatives—including stool31, oral swabs32, exhaled breath33, or sweat34—with variable results. In this context, our study supports the use of urine—a safe, simple, and readily available specimen—as a highly promising alternative. Urine collection is non-invasive, does not require specialized staff or equipment, and is particularly advantageous in infants and young children.

The WHO calls for TB diagnostics that are accurate, rapid, affordable, and where possible, non-invasive. For paediatric TB, its TPPs set minimum sensitivity thresholds that vary by setting and specimen type; for non-sputum tests these minima are ≥ 65% for POC, ≥ 75% for near-POC, and ≥ 80% for low-complexity laboratory assays, with a required specificity > 98% for a single test versus liquid culture13. In our study, NMR-based urine metabolomics achieved sensitivities from 69.1% (TB vs. HC) to 90.0% (cTB vs. TBI), meeting at least the POC minimum across all models and, in several comparisons, meeting the near-POC or low-complexity thresholds. Performance was highest in microbiologically cTB. Importantly, our models also separated cTB from TBI, a clinically relevant and diagnostically challenging distinction within the infection-disease spectra35.

Specificity ranged from 73.3% to 86.7%, below WHO TPP requirement (> 98%) for a stand-alone confirmatory test; accordingly, this approach should not be used as a rule-in assay. Rather, its non-invasiveness and sensitivity profile support its use as a triage/screening test to prioritise children for microbiological confirmation. Apparent false positives may reflect early or subclinical disease or limitations of the reference standard. In practice, such a test could trigger prompt referral and reduce invasive sampling, particularly in resource-limited or high-burden settings36.

Importantly, although several supervised models showed promising diagnostic performance, their robustness varied across group comparisons. Permutation testing demonstrated that the TB vs. HC, cTB vs. HC, and cTB vs. TBI models passed statistical validation, indicating that their separation is unlikely to result from overfitting. In contrast, the TB vs. TBI model did not reach statistical significance in the permutation test, suggesting that this comparison may reflect weak group structure and should be interpreted as exploratory. For this reason, VIP values from the TB vs. TBI model were not used for metabolite selection, and the main biological interpretations of this work rely on the validated models. This pattern highlights that metabolic differences distinguishing confirmed TB from both TBI and HC are stronger and more reproducible than those distinguishing the broader clinical category of all TB cases, which includes children without microbiological confirmation.

The strong and validated separation between cTB and TBI is particularly relevant for clinical translation within the infection-disease spectra35. Differentiating infection from disease remains a major barrier in paediatric TB, as immunological tests such as TST and IGRA cannot achieve this distinction. Our results suggest that urinary metabolic alterations driven by active disease—rather than by mere immunological sensitization—may offer non-invasive diagnostic insights. The metabolites driving the cTB vs. TBI discrimination overlap with those identified in adult urine-based metabolomics studies, including phenylalanine, citrate, hippurate, and benzoate derivatives, supporting cross-cohort consistency despite differences in age, disease severity, and comorbidities.

The absence of validated discrimination between TB (combined cTB + ucTB) and TBI warrants discussion. Children with ucTB typically have early or paucibacillary disease, and their clinical presentations may overlap metabolically with TBI. This biological continuum is well described in paediatric TB immunology and may explain why the mixed TB group shows weaker metabolic separation. Our findings emphasize that metabolomic signatures are more pronounced and stable when restricted to microbiologically confirmed cases, where disease burden is higher and pathophysiological processes more distinct.

Urine HR 1H NMR metabolomics offers practical implementation advantages over MS—reproducibility, robustness and minimal sample preparation—albeit with lower analytical sensitivity16. Recent low-field (LF-NMR) benchtop instruments, (portable, low-maintenance, and user-friendly), suggest a path toward decentralised or near-POC workflows, especially in remote or resource-limited settings17, whereas high-resolution systems provide broader metabolite coverage for discovery. Within this framework, our results support a complementary role for NMR-based urine screening, rather than a stand-alone confirmatory test, in paediatric TB diagnostic pathways.

Most metabolomic studies in adults with TB have relied on invasive matrices such as CSF, plasma or serum22,37,38, often focusing on severe or advanced forms of disease22,38,39. By contrast, fewer studies have explored urine as a non-invasive sample in adults or children (Table 3). Our previous adult NMR work identified urinary metabolites that discriminate TB disease from TBI and pneumococcal pneumonia40, suggesting that urine metabolomics can discriminate TB from other respiratory conditions in clinically relevant settings. In paediatrics, previous metabolomics studies have mainly focused on CSF—primarily in TB meningitis25,41—or blood-based matrices (plasma/serum) to differentiate TB from HC42,43; some reports also include TBI as a separate group24,44. Applications using urine remain limited. More recently, urine has gained attention as a non-invasive alternative, including in children with TB meningitis27,45. Building on our previous Haitian paediatric study—where accuracy for presumptive TB vs. HC reached 68% and AUC-ROC 0.65—we here report higher performance in a low-burden setting (TB vs. HC accuracy 70%, AUC-ROC 0.867), while acknowledging that differences in case mix, reference standards and burden of disease constrain direct comparisons26. In the present study, conducted in a low TB prevalence setting, our models are able to discriminate cTB from TBI using urine samples, contributing to the ongoing effort to address a key diagnostic challenge in paediatric TB and supporting the applicability of urine metabolomics across diverse clinical and epidemiological contexts.

Our models identified 18 differential metabolites across study groups. Five—benzoate, phenylalanine, histidine and two unknown NMR signal—showed higher relative intensity in children with TB, consistent with host metabolic shifts linked to inflammation, immune activation, tissue damage and disease progression46,47. Elevated phenylalanine, also reported in paediatric serum studies23,42, points to disruptions in aromatic amino-acid and energy metabolism47, and therefore in an altered nitrogen balance, processes known to support macrophage activation and inflammatory signalling. Conversely, adult reports have sometimes found lower phenylalanine in TB disease compared to TBI48, a discrepancy that may reflect age, biofluid matrix, disease stage or analytical platform.

The depletion of creatine—a key compound in muscular energy reserve—in children with TB may be closely linked to cachexia and loss of muscle mass. In contrast, creatinine levels were not significantly altered in TB when compared with HC, although borderline differences were observed in the comparison between confirmed TB and TBI. Elevated levels of glutamine and glutamate are generally indicative of chronic inflammation and metabolic adaptation49, required for proper functioning of the immune system In this study, glutamate was significantly reduced in children with TB compared with HC, while glutamine showed a more pronounced decrease in confirmed TB relative to TBI, suggesting altered immunometabolic regulation during active disease.

Urinary glucose levels were significantly lower in TB relative to HC, pointing to dysregulated systemic energy metabolism and/or altered renal handling during active disease. Combined with the marked reduction in citrate, this pattern is indicative of accelerated glucose utilization coupled with the depletion of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates. Citrate, a pivotal metabolite and a key link between energy production and biosynthetic pathways, may be increasingly consumed or redistributed during disease, reflecting metabolic reprogramming to sustain immune activation and inflammatory responses50. The reduced urinary levels of hippurate further suggest perturbations in aromatic amino acid metabolism, including pathways involving tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine40.

Children with TBI showed higher concentrations of isoleucine, N-acetylglutamine, glutamine, creatinine and 2-hydroxyvalerate, which may indicate adaptive or protective responses during infection containment46,47. Taken together, these group-specific signatures aid discrimination among disease states and are hypothesis-generating for biomarker panels, warranting targeted quantification and external validation in independent cohorts, as well as inclusion of other respiratory diseases to enhance model specificity47,51. Elevated levels of creatine, glutamate, glycine, 1-metylhistidine and 2-aminoadipic acid in HC compared to TB patients, reflect metabolic alterations induced by disease state. There is an increase in energy demand and immune activation enhanced by catabolism of aminoacids and high-energy compounds, resulting in lower concentrations of these metabolites25.

Strengths of this study include its multicentre design and a relatively large paediatric sample from a low TB burden setting, which enhances the robustness of the predictive modelling. The use of a non-invasive specimen (urine) improves feasibility and acceptability, particularly in young children who cannot easily provide respiratory samples. Importantly, the study reflects the inherent diagnostic limitations of paediatric TB in low-incidence settings, where microbiological confirmation is frequently not achievable. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. These include the single-country setting, limited sample size in certain diagnostic subgroups, the absence of an independent external validation cohort, and the lack of a disease control group. In addition, variability related to urine sample collection is unavoidable in paediatric studies, as different collection methods are required for continent children and non-continent infants and young children, which may introduce additional variability. Potential unmeasured confounders related to paediatric age—such as age-related differences in renal maturation and metabolite handling, fasting or nutritional status, hormonal development, and intercurrent medications—may also have influenced urinary metabolite profiles. Within these constraints, all samples were processed using identical procedures, no systematic artefacts were detected during spectral analysis, and age did not correlate with the main PCA or PLS-DA components, indicating that the observed metabolomic patterns are unlikely to be driven primarily by age or methodological variability.

A further limitation of this study is the absence of absolute metabolite quantification. As in most untargeted ¹H-NMR urine metabolomics workflows, our protocol was optimized for fingerprinting rather than full quantitative accuracy. Creatinine, although a clinical reference for urine analysis, could not be used as a normalization factor because it differed significantly between groups and has repeatedly emerged as a discriminatory metabolite in our previous TB metabolomics studies26,48. This longstanding challenge reflects an intrinsic limitation of NMR urine metabolomics for TB, as no stable, disease-independent urinary denominator is currently available. Although models were internally cross-validated, external validation is required to verify robust performance across different settings and populations. Another limitation is the absence of universally accepted paediatric urine metabolomic reference databases for TB, which restricts cross-study comparison. Although several discriminatory metabolites in our study overlap with those reported in adult TB cohorts, the lack of standardized paediatric reference data highlights the need for larger harmonized datasets in future research. In addition, the current workflow relies on HR 1H NMR infrastructure; translation to LF-NMR instruments or simplified platforms requires further development and validation. Finally, the specificity of our model did not reach the WHO TPP benchmark (> 98%) for confirmatory tests; this supports the potential utility of urinary metabolomics as a triage or screening tool, rather than a stand-alone rule-in test.

Conclusions

In summary, our results suggest that urine HR 1H NMR metabolomics could enabled non-invasive discrimination between paediatric TB, TBI and HC, including the clinically important differentiation of cTB from TBI. While sensitivity met WHO´s minimum TPP thresholds for non-sputum-based tests, specificity fell short of confirmatory thresholds, supporting the role of this approach as a screening tool to prioritise children for microbiological confirmation and potentially reduce invasive sampling. These findings motivate prospective, multi-setting validation—including targeted assays and LF-NMR implementations—to define its utility across diverse paediatric care pathways.

Methods

Ethics statement

Ethics approval was obtained from the Hospital General Universitario Gregorio Marañón (HGUGM) Ethics Committee, Madrid, Spain (code PI20/01607) and was subsequently ratified by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of all participating centres. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians at the time of enrolment, and written informed assent was obtained from adolescents older than 12 years. Procedures for sample collection and experiments were conducted in compliance with applicable guidelines and regulations.

Study population

For this study, we retrieved bio-banked urine samples from children and adolescents under 18 years enrolled in the multicentre prospective cohort of pTBred, between September 2019 and September 2022.

Children were prospectively enrolled in pTBred cohort if they present clinical signs or symptoms suggestive of TB52, were undergoing migrant TB screening, or were contacts of TB patients. Baseline demographic data (age, gender, body mass index [BMI]) and medical history (Bacillus Calmette-Guérin [BCG] vaccination, clinical presentation, radiologic findings, site of disease, microbiologic results, outcome) were collected and managed into the pTBred database, using REDCap electronic data capture tools53, hosted at HGUGM (Madrid, Spain).

All children underwent a physical examination. Anteroposterior and lateral chest-X-ray (CXR) were performed at the time of diagnosis, and evaluated by hospital radiologists following local protocols. Thoracic computed tomography (CT) was performed in children whose CXR were inconclusive. Immune response against M. tuberculosis was evaluated by the commercially available QFT‑Plus blood test54. Additionally, some patients underwent the TST, by intradermal injection of 0.1 mL of tuberculin (purified protein derivative, PPD) on the inner surface of the forearm. Results were interpreted based on Spanish guidelines5. Children with presumed TB were admitted, and 3 respiratory samples were collected on consecutive days for sputum/gastric aspirate microscopy, culture, and molecular testing (GeneXpert® MTB/RIF Ultra; Cepheid, USA). For cases of EPTB, culture was performed on invasive samples. All patients were treated according to the Spanish guidelines5. For TB contacts with a negative TST/IGRA and a normal CXR, diagnostic tests were repeated two months after the baseline consultation to rule TB infection. Children under 5 years exposed to TB received preventive treatment until infection was excluded.

Study definitions

Participants were categorized into 4 study groups52: (1) cTB, considered in children with clinical or radiological signs suggestive of TB and laboratory confirmation of M. tuberculosis through culture or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection; (2) ucTB, in children with clinical or radiologic signs suggestive of TB, and at least one of the following: (a) favourable response to anti-TB therapy, (b) immunologic evidence of M. tuberculosis infection (positive TST and/or positive IGRA), (c) close M. tuberculosis exposure; 3) TBI included children with a positive TST and/or IGRA, who were asymptomatic and presented normal CXR; 4) healthy controls (HC) comprised children who were TB contacts or underwent screening upon arrival from a high-prevalence region, with no clinical symptoms or signs of disease, negative for TST and/or IGRA, and had follow-up data confirming the absence of TB.

TB disease was further classified based on anatomical location into 3 subtypes: PTB55, EPTB—except for peripheral non-complicated lymph-node TB—, and both PTB and EPTB. Severe TB56 was defined in children with respiratory TB presenting with significant airway obstruction, extensive pulmonary involvement (including multilobar disease, cavitary TB, or miliary TB), or complex pleural effusion.

Exclusion criteria

For all groups, children receiving immunosuppressive therapy or already on anti-tuberculosis treatment at enrolment were excluded, as well as those with insufficient clinical, microbiological, radiological, or immunological data to allow accurate group classification, or urine samples of inadequate quality for NMR analysis.

Urine sample handling

Urine samples were collected at diagnosis in sterile 100 mL plastic containers at participating centres. For continent children, midstream urine sample was collected; for incontinent infants, samples were obtained via a sterile urine collection bag. Samples were sent to the pTBred biorepository at HGUGM within 24 h, where they were aliquoted into sterile 2 mL cryovials and stored at -80 °C. At Hospital Sant Joan de Déu (HSJD), samples were stored in its own biobank in 0.5 mL aliquots at -80 °C. Aliquots were then shipped to the ICTS Bioimagen Complutense, BIOIMAC (Universidad Complutense de Madrid, Spain), where they were thawed in batches and mixed with a standardized deuterated buffer for NMR spectrometry measurements. The buffer consisted of a 0.2 M phosphate solution containing 0.09% NaN₃ and 0.3 mM trimethylsilyl propanoic acid (TSP), adjusted to pH 7.448. A 600 µL aliquot of the buffered urine sample was transferred into 5 mm NMR tubes.

Acquisition of NMR spectra

Urine samples were examined using HR 1H NMR spectroscopy. To minimize sample degradation, NMR analysis was performed at 4 °C on a Bruker Avance III HD 500 MHz spectrometer (11.7 T) equipped with a 5 mm BBI probe at BIOIMAC (Fig. 5). Analytical quality was monitored using NMR-specific procedures: all samples were acquired in a randomized order; TSP intensity, linewidth (FWHM), and chemical-shift position (ppm) were checked throughout the run; and uniform acquisition parameters (pulse sequence, relaxation delay, temperature, number of scans) were applied to all samples. No evidence of spectral drift or instability was observed during data collection.

Standard solvent-suppressed spectra were acquired into 32k data points, averaged over 256 acquisitions, with a total acquisition time of approximately 19 min. A sequence based on the first increment of the NOESY pulse sequence (relaxation delay-90º-t1-90º-tm-90º-acquire FID), with a secondary radio frequency irradiation field applied at the water resonance frequency during the 2-second relaxation delay and the mixing period (tm = 150 ms), with t1 fixed at 3 µs, was used. A spectral width of 7002.80 Hz was applied. Data were zero-filled before Fourier transformation, and free induction decays (FIDs) were multiplied by an exponential line broadening of 0.3 Hz.

A number of 1H, 1H 2D experiments were performed to carry out the component assignments. Gradient-selected COSY90 was acquired with water presaturation during relaxation delay, a spectral width of 6009 Hz in both dimensions, 2 K data points in f2 and 384 increments in f1. An unshifted sinusoidal window function was applied in both dimensions, and zero-filling was used in the f1 dimension.

1H, 1H TOCSY was acquired in TPPI phase-sensitive mode, with water presaturation during relaxation delay, a spectral width of 6009 Hz in both dimensions, a 70 ms mixing time, 2k data points in f2, and 384 increments in f1. Zero-filling in f1 and an unshifted squared sinusoidal window function in both dimensions were applied before Fourier transformation (Suppl. Fig. 4).

1H,13C 2D experiments were performed to assign the components. HSQC experiments were acquired with the following parameters: GARP13 C decoupling, 6009 Hz and 25,153 kHz spectral widths in the1 H and13 C dimensions, respectively, 2k data points in f2 and 256 increments in f1. Zero-filling in f1 and unshifted squared sinusoidal window function in both dimensions were applied before Fourier transformation.

Processing of NMR spectra

NMR data were processed and edited using MestReNova software (v.15; Mestrelab Research, Spain) according to established protocols described in a previous study57. From the raw NMR spectra, the chemical shift regions from 4.85 to 5.15 ppm and from 5.65 to 6.20 ppm were excluded to remove random variations due to water and urea resonance suppression. Metabolite signals were shift-aligned using the regional spectral alignment tool in MestReNova. Baseline correction was automatically performed using the ‘Whittaker Smoother’ algorithm. Binning (also known as bucketing) was applied to the NMR spectra, reducing the data to equal-length integral segments (bins) of δ = 0.01 ppm to account for variations in resonance positions. All spectra were initially normalized to the total sum of spectral intensities (each bin divided by the sum of all NMR signals) to compensate for differences in overall signal amplitude and to enable comparison across samples. Relative intensities therefore represent the bin intensity scaled to the total spectral area. To address potential bias associated with total-area normalization—particularly relevant in urine metabolomics—we additionally applied the “Normalization by a pooled sample from group” procedure implemented in MetaboAnalyst 5.0. For each comparison, a pooled reference spectrum was generated from the appropriate control group (HC for TB vs HC; TBI samples for cTB vs. TBI). Each sample spectrum was then divided by the corresponding pooled reference, thereby correcting for global dilution effects in a manner conceptually similar to probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN). Creatinine normalization was evaluated but deemed inappropriate for this dataset for two reasons. First, creatinine was significantly different between groups, violating the assumption of stability required for normalization. Second, creatinine has previously emerged as a discriminatory metabolite in three independent NMR-based urine metabolomics studies of tuberculosis from our research group (two adult cohorts and one paediatric cohort)26,48,58. Because creatinine reflects biologically relevant metabolic alterations in TB, normalizing to creatinine would have removed meaningful biological variation and introduced bias.

Statistical analysis

Clinical and demographic characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, with associations between groups assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test for two groups, or the Kruskal-Wallis test for more than two groups.

Data from HR 1H NMR spectra were analysed using MetaboAnalyst 6.059. pooled sample from group Unsupervised PCA was applied to the data to identify patterns and differences between samples without prior knowledge of group labels60. PCA score plots were used to highlight statistical outliers by calculating the Mahalanobis distance for each sample, which measures the distance from the centre of the PCA space.

Supervised PLS-DA was employed to identify metabolic patterns that discriminate between predefined groups (cTB, ucTB, TBI, and HC)60. The robustness of the PLS-DA models was validated using 5-fold cross-validation, assessing performance metrics such as accuracy, R², and Q². In addition to PLS-DA, orthogonal partial least squares (OPLS) models were generated to separate predictive from orthogonal variance. To evaluate the risk of overfitting in supervised models, we performed permutation testing for all PLS-DA and OPLS models using MetaboAnalyst (n = 20 permutations). For each comparison, class labels were randomly reassigned and the resulting R²Y and Q² values were contrasted with those of the original model. Models with permutation p-values ≤ 0.05 (PLS-DA) and significantly lower permuted R²Y and Q² values (OPLS) were considered statistically validated. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were calculated to identify the most significant spectral regions for group differentiation. Spectral regions with VIP scores greater than two were selected for developing classification models. The performance of these models was evaluated by calculating accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the AUC-ROC.

Metabolites corresponding to the significant spectral signals used in the classification models were identified using Chenomx software and cross-referenced with the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) library, utilizing characteristic cross-peaks from 2D NMR spectra. The statistical significance of differences in these metabolites between groups was determined using Student’s t-test.
