Investigating the factors influencing vertical stress ahead of the undercutting line in block caving
Authors: Yong Cao, Xinzhu Hua, Shaobin Zhai, Huaibin Li, Heng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In block caving mines, stress evolution induced by undercutting poses a significant threat to the integrity of extraction structures. Focusing on the Pulang Copper Mine, this research analyzes the controlling factors behind the vertical stress concentration ahead of the undercutting line. Numerical and field data revealed that the undercutting span, burial depth, and internal friction angle of the rock mass were the dominant influencing factors. Specifically, vertical stress intensifies with larger spans and greater depths. Conversely, the extent of the failure zone ahead of the face increases with burial depth but decreases with a higher internal friction angle. This study provides both a mechanistic understanding and a practical basis for optimizing undercutting parameters under similar conditions.

Introduction

Block caving is a large-scale, low-cost, and highly efficient mining method, which is often regarded as the preferred choice for developing massive, low-grade ore bodies due to its economic competitiveness with open-pit mining1–6. This method involves undercutting a block of ore to induce caving under a combination of gravitational stress, geological structures, and the arching effect7–10. A critical aspect of its success lies in managing the stress redistribution ahead of the undercutting line, as excessive stress concentrations can severely damage the underlying extraction structures and compromise their service life11,12.

However, stress-induced stability issues remain a major challenge in practice. As shown in Fig. 1a, stress accumulation ahead of the undercutting line at the Pulang Copper Mine in Yunnan, China has caused extensive rib spalling and collapse in the undercutting roadway. The severity of the damage necessitated the development of a new roadway, as shown in Fig. 1b. In addition, stress buildup at the undercutting level has been transferred to the production level, leading to structural damage. Field investigations revealed that despite the use of concrete columns near the drawpoints and steel arches with cast concrete at the brow for support, as shown in Fig. 2a, noticeable cracking has occurred in some drawpoint pillars, as shown in Fig. 2(b). These issues significantly disrupt normal mining operations and jeopardize the safety and service life of the extraction infrastructure3,13.

These field observations underscore the urgent need to quantitatively understand the stress evolution mechanisms during undercutting. Early studies, grounded in the pressure arch theory, analyzed the forces on the surrounding rock. For instance14, identified that tangential stress concentration ahead of the face drives continuous caving and is influenced by factors such as the undercutting span and depth. Field monitoring by15 at the same mine further delineated the spatial extent of this stress influence on extraction structures. Complementary laboratory experiments by9,16 demonstrated that vertical stresses are affected by the draw area and advancement distance. These findings are corroborated by field observations linking undercutting operations to structural damage2,11,17 and highlighting the impact of advancement rates on stress buildup3,6.

While previous research has unequivocally established the presence of high stress concentrations and identified several influencing parameters, the existing body of work remains largely descriptive. Studies often rely on isolated field monitoring, specific numerical models2,18, or qualitative theoretical frameworks. However, for a single advancing front, a systematic and quantitative analysis that decouples and ranks the influence of these key factors remains absent. A systematic and quantitative analysis of how key geological and geometrical factors—such as the undercutting depth, span, and the rock mass’s internal friction angle—collectively govern the magnitude and distribution of vertical stress (as the dominant component) is still lacking. A systematic quantitative study that decouples and ranks the influence of key factors (e.g., depth, span, and rock mass properties) on the magnitude and spatial extent of vertical stress remains absent to date.

To address this research gap, this study focuses on the initial mining area of the Pulang Copper Mine. A comprehensive numerical model, as shown in Fig. 3, has been established for a representative zone to systematically quantify the distribution patterns of vertical stress ahead of the undercutting line and to elucidate the influence mechanisms of its main controlling factors. The findings will provide theoretical support for optimizing the mine’s undercutting parameters, ultimately contributing to enhanced long-term stability and service life of extraction structures in block caving operations4,6.

Theoretical analysis

Stress concentration mechanism in surrounding rock ahead of the undercutting line

Mechanism of stress concentration formation

The mechanism behind surrounding rock collapse in block caving is generally recognized as pressure arch theory19,20. Essentially, a pressure arch forms around the excavation space of the rock body to resist the uneven deformation produced by the disturbances of surrounding rock mining and self-regulation effects6,21. Taking the elastic-plastic mechanics of the circular chamber as an example. To illustrate this, consider the elastoplastic mechanics of a circular opening, as shown in Fig. 4, the excavation of the circular chamber will make the peripheral radial stress decrease, and the tangential stress increase. The radial stress is always less than the original rock stress, and the tangential stress is greater than the original rock stress, which can be observed in front of the undercutting advancing line of the peripheral rock stress concentration; as such it is manifested in the tangential stress concentration. When the tangential stress of the surrounding rock is smaller than the strength of the rock body, the rock body stores energy to form an elastic stress-increase zone within the arch. The boundary of the pressure arch is the boundary of the chamber, as shown in Fig. 4(b). When the tangential stress of the surrounding rock is larger than the strength of the rock body, the surrounding rock starts to form a rupture zone, and the boundary of the pressure arch shifts outward, as illustrated in Fig. 4(a). The block caving relies on the undercutting project to induce the concentration of tangential stress in the surrounding rock16,22. This results in the tangential stress being greater than the strength of the rock mass, the formation of a rupture zone that continuously shifts outward, and a continuous collapse.

Manifestation of stress concentration

To analytically derive the stress field, the following fundamental assumptions are made: (1) Rock Mass Constitutive Assumption: The rock mass is modeled as a homogeneous, isotropic, and continuous elastic medium, obeying Hooke’s law prior to yielding; (2) Stress Field Assumption: The excavation is simplified as a symmetric rectangular opening. The stress field is assumed to be free of shear stress (τxy = 0) at the scale of the analysis, satisfying the applicability conditions of the Westergaard stress function; (3) Coordinate System Assumption: The origin of the Cartesian coordinate system is set at the geometric center of the undercutting space. The x-axis is aligned with the undercutting advance direction, and the y-axis is vertical (perpendicular to advance). The polar coordinate system shares the same origin, with the polar angle θ defined as 0°along the positive x-axis direction.

Vertical stress concentration distribution patterns and influencing factors in front of the undercutting propulsion line

Excavation in a rock body will cause stress redistribution in the surrounding rock28, and the tangential stress around the excavation space is approximately equal to the vertical stress concentration. Furthermore, the surrounding rock near the undercutting line not only has to bear the load of its own overlying rock body, but also must bear the load transferred from the roof overhanging the hollow area. This results in a peak value of vertical stress concentration that constantly moves forward with the undercutting line. Therefore the vertical stress distribution pattern in front of the undercutting line is like that shown in Fig. 6.

Modeling

A Rhinoceros-FLAC3D coupled simulation model was established according to the design parameters of the Pulang copper mine, with the model structure is shown in Fig. 7. The model has a strike length of 500 m, a vertical strike length of 500 m, a height of 217 m, and a total of 1,302,620 units. A total of 4 exit roadways, 6 undercutting roadways, 49 exit approaches, and 49 gathering ditches are laid out at the mine exit level. The model mesh comprises primarily hexahedral elements, with local refinement around the undercutting roadway and extraction level to ensure computational accuracy. The model boundaries are fixed in the normal directions, with stress boundaries applied on the surface to simulate the overburden pressure6,31.

Ground stress field application

According to the results of geostress measurements, the geostress in the mining area is dominated by horizontal tectonic stress. Therefore, the maximum principal stress in the three-dimensional model is applied horizontally, in the direction perpendicular to the strike of the ore body, i.e., in the y-direction. The intermediate principal stress is applied in the strike direction of the ore body, i.e., in the x-direction. And the minimum principal stress is applied to the vertical direction of the self-gravitational stress, which is the z-direction. The ratio of horizontal stress to vertical stress, called the lateral pressure coefficient, was measured in the mine area to be 2.09 and 1.45, respectively. This is basically consistent with the distribution law of lateral pressure coefficients for the regional geostress in mainland China. Scenario I: undercutting progression and stress evolution

Since the length of the elastic-plastic damage zone in front of the undercutting line cannot be obtained, we take the structural parameters of the advancement type undercut of the Pulang Copper Mine and the physical-mechanical parameters of the rock body as an engineering background, and use a horizontal burial depth for the undercutting level of 300 m. Using Eq. (15), the ground stress field is imposed, and numerical simulation is employed to monitor the distribution of the vertical stress (or vertical stress concentration) in front of the undercutting line, with the following design scheme:

First in Fig. 8(b) in the number ② position of the excavation, the initial chipping is formed. Scenario IV: influence of rock mass strength parameters

To investigate the influence of rock mass strength parameters on the stress distribution ahead of the advancing line, it is necessary to select key variables from macroscopic strength criteria. The compressive strength of a rock mass is typically governed by both its cohesion and internal friction angle. Compared to cohesion, the internal friction angle exerts a more pronounced influence on the rock mass’s dilatancy characteristics and the extent of the plastic yield zone. Furthermore, in empirical strength criteria such as Hoek-Brown, the internal friction angle serves as a core parameter linked to the rock mass’s geological strength index. Therefore, this study selects the internal friction angle as a representative variable, aiming to provide an in-depth analysis of its controlling effect on the development of the yield zone and the characteristics of stress concentration.

In order to verify the influence of the internal friction angle on the stress distribution characteristics of the rock surrounding the undercutting line, the value of the internal friction angle of the rock body is taken as 37.8° in the model, and the undercutting roadway is horizontally arranged at a burial depth of 500 m. Discussion

This study provides a systematic, quantitative analysis of the factors controlling vertical stress ahead of the undercutting line, which has previously been primarily described qualitatively. Our integrated theoretical and numerical approach reveals that. While prior research has confirmed the existence of significant vertical stress concentration ahead of the undercutting line12,15, predictive models for its distribution and the extent of the resulting failure zone remain underdeveloped. This study addresses this gap by establishing a theoretical model (Eq. 11) that quantifies the relationship between the elastoplastic damage zone and key parameters, namely the undercutting depth, span, and rock mass strength.

A critical finding of our model is the explicit formulation for the length of the damage zone. The analysis reveals that the damage zone length increases quadratically with mining depth and linearly with span, but decreases with higher uniaxial compressive strength. Notably, the stabilization of the vertical stress concentration factor beyond a critical span indicates the formation of a new, stable pressure arch within the overlying rock mass. Once this arch is established, further increases in span do not significantly increase the load transferred to the arch abutments ahead of the undercutting line. It is crucial to clarify the role of the internal friction angle (φ): a smaller φ value signifies lower shear strength according to the Mohr-Coulomb criterion, which consequently leads to a larger and more developed damage zone, as evidenced in our FLAC3D simulations.

The consistency between our theoretical predictions and numerical simulations strengthens the model’s validity. For instance, the FLAC3D results under the Pulang Copper Mine conditions demonstrate that increased undercutting depth leads to higher peak vertical stress, while a larger undercutting span results in a more gradual stress gradient. Most importantly, the simulated influence of the internal friction angle aligns perfectly with the theoretical prediction, confirming that weaker rock masses (lower φ) are prone to more extensive failure.

When contextualized with existing literature, our model offers a mechanistic explanation for empirical observations. The predicted location of the peak stress within the damage zone aligns with field data from12, who identified the stress concentration peak within 10 m of the face. Furthermore, the stress concentration factors derived from our model fall within the typical range (2.0–3.0) reported in field studies15, thereby bridging the gap between theoretical analysis and empirical evidence.

In summary, this research provides a novel theoretical and numerical framework for analyzing stress-induced damage in block caving. The findings offer concrete guidance for optimizing undercutting parameters by quantifying the risk of excessive failure zone development, ultimately contributing to the improved design and stability of extraction structures.

Conclusions

This study theoretically and numerically investigated the stress concentration mechanisms ahead of the undercutting line in block caving. The main conclusions are as follows:The vertical stress concentration ahead of the undercutting line and the extent of the associated failure zone are predominantly controlled by the undercutting depth, span, and the internal friction angle of the rock mass.The failure zone length increases quadratically with depth and linearly with span, but decreases with higher internal friction angle, as predicted by our theoretical model (Eq. 11) and confirmed by numerical simulations.At the Pulang Copper Mine, the peak stress concentration factor reaches 1.7 ~ 1.9, located within 10 m of the face under the given conditions.These factors collectively determine the stress transfer to the extraction level, highlighting the need for integrated parameter design.
