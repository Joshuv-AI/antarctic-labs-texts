Reconsidering uniform treatment: magnetic resonance imaging-based insights into the multiform pathophysiology of sprained ankles
Authors: Hidefumi Koiwai, Mikio Kamimura, Akira Taguchi, Jun Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnkle sprains are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries, yet their clinical complexity is often underestimated. The relationship between ankle sprains and bone bruises in both the ankle joint and midfoot remains underexplored. At our institution, patients with moderate to severe ankle sprains routinely undergo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and receive conservative treatment with a short leg posterior cast splint. This study specifically focused on bone involvement assessed by MRI, rather than soft tissue or functional parameters.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed MRI scans of 70 patients with acute moderate (Grade 2) to severe (Grade 3) ankle sprains who presented consecutively between November 2014 and December 2019. Patients with mild sprains (Grade 1) were excluded. Bone bruises were assessed, and all patients were treated with a short leg posterior cast splint extending from the lower leg to the forefoot. Crutches were prescribed when pain persisted during weight-bearing after splint application. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the associations between MRI-detected bone bruises and crutch use following fixation.ResultsThe fixation period, available in 63 patients, ranged from 8 to 72 days (mean: 29.5 days). Walking pain requiring crutches, used as a proxy indicator of persistent weight-bearing pain rather than a validated clinical outcome, was observed in 32 /70 (45.7%) of patients. All patients experienced resolution of ankle joint pain following treatment. MRI revealed bone bruises in the ankle and midfoot in 57/70 (81.4%) of cases. Midfoot involvement, including the tarsal bones and Chopart joint, was present in 24/70 (34.3%) of cases. The talus was the most frequently affected bone 42/70 (60.0%), followed by the tibia 31/70 (44.3%), tarsal bones 18/70 (25.7%), and calcaneus 15/70 (21.4%). Bone bruises on the talocrural joint surface of the talus were significantly associated with crutch use after fixation (P = 0.041). Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified both younger age (odds ratio [OR] 0.957, 95% confidence Interval [CI] 0.930–0.985, P = 0.003) and bone bruises on the talocrural joint surface of the talus (OR 4.012, 95% CI 1.174–13.713, P = 0.027) as independent predictors of crutch use after fixation. All patients reported resolution of ankle joint pain by the final follow-up, with no persistent or recurrent symptoms documented during the observation period.ConclusionBone bruises in the ankle and midfoot are frequently associated with moderate to severe ankle sprains. MRI findings indicate a multifaceted pathophysiology, underscoring the importance of individualized treatment strategies. In cases where bone bruises are present on the talocrural joint surface, midfoot immobilization and a temporary period of non–weight-bearing may be appropriate considerations.Keywords: Magnetic resonance imaging, ankle sprain, bone bruise

Introduction

Ankle sprains are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries. The most frequent mechanism involves a combination of foot inversion and adduction during plantar flexion, leading to damage to the lateral ankle ligaments.

The primary goals of ankle sprain treatment are to manage pain and preserve joint function. For severe cases, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) recommends immobilization with a short leg walking cast for 2 to 3 weeks [1]. Further, several studies have shown that even for severe sprains, a shorter immobilization period (maximum 10 days) using a below-knee cast or similar rigid support can be advantageous [2–6]. The current standard treatment for ankle sprains includes short-term immobilization, functional rehabilitation, and early mobilization with support. Treatment goals focus on facilitating an early return to daily activities and sports [2, 7–9]. However, little attention is given to the potential long-term complications.

Studies indicate that ankle sprains can lead to chronic issues, including persistent pain, recurrent swelling, and instability, which are more serious than commonly perceived [10–13]. McKay et al. reported that basketball players with a history of ankle injury were nearly five times more likely to sustain another ankle injury (odds ratio (OR) 4.94, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.95–12.48) [10]. Hubbard et al. found that up to 75% of initial lateral ankle sprains result in recurrent sprains and chronic symptoms [11].

Research on the relationship between ankle sprains and bone bruises in the ankle joint and midfoot is limited. In a retrospective study of 711 ankle inversion sprains, Søndergaard et al. found that the dorsal ligaments and capsule of the midtarsal joints were involved in 33% of cases (237 cases). Furthermore, in 24% of cases (172 cases), injuries appeared to be isolated to these joints [7]. However, midtarsal sprains are not commonly recognized as being associated with ankle sprains.

Recent systematic reviews have highlighted that diagnostic imaging for ankle sprains often focuses primarily on ligamentous injuries, particularly the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL). Colò et al. reported that ultrasound demonstrates high sensitivity for detecting ATFL tears and may even outperform MRI in this regard [14]. However, ultrasound cannot assess bone bruises or midfoot involvement, which may accompany moderate-to-severe ankle sprains. This limitation underscores the clinical relevance of evaluating bone involvement using MRI, as performed in the present study.

Ligament-dominant soft-tissue in the ankle typically do not cause joint pain during weight-bearing when rigid fixation, such as a splint, is applied correctly. However, in clinical practice, weight-bearing sometimes induces pain even with proper ankle fixation. This suggests that ankle sprains may involve not only ligament injuries but also damage to other structures, such as the bones around the ankle.

Although bone bruises have been extensively investigated in the knee—particularly in association with anterior cruciate ligament injuries—their clinical relevance, characteristic distribution patterns, and relationships with concomitant injuries have been well documented in recent years [15–17].

In contrast, similar investigations focusing on ankle sprains remain limited. This discrepancy highlights a significant research gap, suggesting that bone bruises around the ankle may also contribute to persistent symptoms or associated injuries but have not been sufficiently explored. Addressing this gap is essential for improving our understanding of the pathophysiology of ankle sprains and optimizing treatment strategies.

Moreover, ankle sprains are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries. However, few studies have investigated the relationship between ankle sprains and the distribution patterns of bone bruises. In particular, within the scope of our investigation, we were unable to identify any studies examining their association with the tarsal bones.

In our institutions, patients with moderate to severe ankle sprains are routinely evaluated with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to examine the bones around the ankle and are treated conservatively using short leg posterior cast splints, including those extending to the forefoot.

This study aims to describe the distribution patterns of bone bruises around the ankle, including the midfoot, using MRI, and explore their associations with clinical indicators such as weight-bearing pain.

This study was previously posted to Research Square preprint server on October 9, 2020 [18].

Materials & methods

The grading of ankle sprains in this study was based on clinical examination. Although no universally standardized grading system exists for ankle sprains, a three-grade classification according to the severity of ligament injury is widely used in clinical practice. We adopted this commonly used system, and our criteria are consistent with those described by the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) [1].

Grade 1 represents microscopic ligament injury caused by stretching without tearing and is characterized by mild swelling and pain, absence of mechanical instability, and the ability to walk in most cases. Grade 2 corresponds to a partial ligament tear, accompanied by noticeable swelling and ecchymosis, significant pain during movement, and occasionally mild joint instability. Grade 3 indicates a complete ligament rupture, presenting with marked swelling and ecchymosis, clear mechanical instability, and difficulty walking.

This study included 70 patients diagnosed with moderate (Grade 2) to severe (Grade 3) ankle sprains who presented consecutively between November 2014 and December 2019 [1]. Pain location and tenderness were systematically assessed at the initial visit by board-certified orthopedic surgeons through standardized physical examination, including palpation of the medial and lateral malleoli, midfoot, and surrounding structures. The presence and location of tenderness were recorded in the medical charts at the time of examination. As part of our institutional protocol, all Grade 2 and Grade 3 patients routinely underwent MRI [1].

MRI was employed to evaluate the bones around the ankle and forefoot. The study was designed to investigate bone bruises. Other clinical parameters, such as ligament injury grading or functional assessments, were not included, as the focus was placed solely on bone involvement.

Patients were treated conservatively with a semi-rigid short leg posterior cast splint until walking pain resolved without requiring additional external fixation. For patients experiencing pain during weight-bearing after fixation, one or two crutches were prescribed to alleviate discomfort.

Walking pain was assessed based on patient-reported symptoms during clinical examination. Patients were asked whether they experienced pain during weight-bearing or ambulation, and immobilization was continued until they reported resolution of walking pain. No standardized pain scale or functional outcome measure was used, and the assessment relied on patient self-report and clinical judgment.

The duration of immobilization was determined based on a symptom-based protocol, in which the cast splint was maintained until walking pain resolved. Pain was assessed at each follow-up visit, and immobilization was discontinued once patients were able to walk without pain during daily activities.

Crutch use followed a stepwise protocol: patients with pain during weight-bearing were instructed to use two crutches, progressed to one crutch when pain-free with two, and discontinued crutches once ambulation with a single crutch became pain-free.

Patients with mild sprains were excluded from this study and treated conservatively with a soft splint. Cases of slight sprains (Grade 1) were excluded [1].

The fixation period was unknown for four cases, and three patients were lost to follow-up after the MRI examination. The three patients who were lost to follow-up discontinued clinic visits during the course of treatment, and their subsequent clinical outcomes were therefore unknown; these cases were excluded from analyses requiring follow-up information.

For the four cases with unknown fixation duration, the medical records did not contain documentation of the immobilization period, and these cases were included only in analyses that did not require fixation-period data. Among the remaining 63 cases, the fixation period ranged from 8 to 72 days (mean: 29.5 days). As shown in Fig. 1, ankle joint pain resolved in all 67 patients who completed conservative treatment.

MRI protocol

MRI was performed using a 1.5 T unit (Siemens Symphony TIM; Siemens) with an extremity coil positioned around the ankle. The patient’s foot was placed in a relaxed position with slight plantarflexion and external rotation.

The interval between injury and MRI acquisition ranged from 3 to 28 days (mean: 11.1 days). MRI timing was not standardized, as examinations were performed at the time of clinical presentation and scheduling availability.

The acquisition parameters for T1-weighted imaging using a turbo spin echo (TSE) sequence were consistent across both sagittal and coronal views. In sagittal views, the time of repetition (TR) was set to 500 ms, the flip angle (FA) to 90°, and the time of echo (TE) to 20 ms. The same parameters were applied to coronal views, with TR = 500 ms, FA = 90°, and TE = 20 ms.

For short τ inversion recovery (STIR) imaging, also performed using a TSE sequence, the acquisition parameters varied slightly depending on the imaging plane. In sagittal views, TR was 4000 ms, FA was 90°, TE was 60 ms, and the time of inversion (TI) was 150 ms. Coronal views were acquired with a TR of 4500 ms, while maintaining the same FA, TE, and TI values. Axial views used a TR of 4000 ms, with FA, TE, and TI identical to those used in sagittal views.

Regions with high signal intensity on STIR and low signal intensity on T1-weighted imaging were carefully assessed by two independent board-certified orthopedic surgeons and one radiologist. Bone signal abnormalities were confirmed when changes were evident in both imaging modalities. For smaller bone regions, signal intensity was assessed using STIR imaging alone. Discrepancies between observers were resolved through consensus discussions.

In standing posture, joints subjected to vertical loading are classified as weight-bearing areas, whereas the remaining joints are classified as non-weight-bearing areas.

Statistical analysis

Univariate logistic regression analyses were first performed to assess the associations between MRI-detected bone bruises and crutch use following fixation. Variables identified in the univariate analyses, together with clinically relevant factors, were considered for inclusion in a multivariate logistic regression model using a forward (likelihood ratio) selection approach, in order to limit the number of predictors relative to the number of outcome events. Although forward selection was used, variables were restricted based on both statistical and clinical considerations to minimize the risk of overfitting.

Age and sex were included as covariates due to their established roles as potential confounders in musculoskeletal conditions and recovery.

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and no significant multicollinearity was observed (all VIFs < 2.0). Cases with missing data were excluded from the respective analyses, as the proportion of missing values was minimal (< 5%). A total of 32 patients required crutch use and were considered as outcome events. A two-sided significance level of P < 0.05 was used.

Given the exploratory nature of this study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed, and the results should be interpreted with caution due to the potential risk of type I error. The number of outcome events and predictors in the final model were also evaluated to assess the events-per-variable ratio.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All medical records and imaging data were anonymized prior to analysis.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shinshu University School of Medicine (approval No. 2681 and No. 4449). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

The cohort comprised 37 males and 33 females, with a mean age of 32.5 years (range: 10–93 years). The right ankle was affected in 32 cases and the left in 38 cases. Sports-related injuries accounted for 40/70 (57.1%) of the sprains. The time from injury to the initial visit ranged from 0 to 16 days (mean: 2.2 days), and the time to MRI examination ranged from 3 to 28 days (mean: 11.1 days). All patients received treatment with a short leg posterior cast splint. Following fixation, 32/70 (45.7%) of patients experienced residual walking pain, necessitating the use of crutches.

The incidence of bone bruises detected by MRI in the ankle and midfoot associated with ankle sprains was 57/70 (81.4%). Bone bruises in the midfoot, including the tarsal bones and Chopart joint, were found in 24/70 (34.3%) of ankle sprains (Table 1).

Tibia

Tibial bone bruises occurred in 31/70 (44.3%). Medial malleolar bruises were more common than lateral (23/70 [32.9%] vs. 8/70 [11.4%]). Bruises on the tibial weight-bearing surface of the talocrural joint were observed in 8/70 (11.4%) (Table 1).

Talus

Talar bone bruises were the most frequent, occurring in 42/70 (60.0%). Joint-surface bruises were present in 38/70 (54.3%), including 27/70 (38.6%) in weight-bearing and 29/70 (41.4%) in non-weight-bearing regions. Bruises on the talocrural and talocalcaneal joint surfaces occurred in 20/70 (28.6%) and 14/70 (20.0%), respectively. Medial bone bruises (combined tibial and talar medial surfaces) were found in 31/70 (44.3%), all of which had corresponding medial pain and tenderness documented at the initial examination (Table 1).

Calcaneus and tarsal bones

Calcaneal bruises were observed in 15/70 (21.4%), often involving the talocalcaneal or Chopart joint surfaces. Tarsal bone bruises occurred in 18/70 (25.7%), including 9/70 (12.9%) in the inner row (navicular and cuneiforms) and 14/70 (21.4%) in the cuboid (Table 1).

Kissing lesions

Kissing lesions were identified in 24/70 (34.3%) of joint-surface injuries and 19/70 (27.1%) of non-weight-bearing areas. In weight-bearing regions, they occurred in 5/70 (7.1%) of talocalcaneal joints and 4/70 (5.7%) of talocrural joints (Table 1).

Associations with crutch use

Univariate analysis revealed a significant association between bone bruises on the talocrural joint surface of the talus and the use of crutches after fixation (P = 0.041). Crutch use after fixation also showed a tendency to correlate with bone bruises within the intraosseous area of the talus (P = 0.053) (Table 1).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, both age (OR 0.957, 95% CI 0.930–0.985, P = 0.003) and bone bruises on the talocrural joint surface of the talus (OR 4.012, 95% CI 1.174–13.713, P = 0.027) were independently associated with crutch use after fixation (Table 2).

Discussion

In this MRI-based study of moderate and severe ankle sprains, bone bruises were detected in 57 (81.4%) of patients. These bruises were widespread, often occurring in the weight-bearing areas and medial aspects of the ankle, as well as in the midfoot. Based on the bone damage observed in MR images, these findings indicate that ankle sprains involve a multiform pattern of tissue injury, extending beyond isolated ligament damage. The findings of this study are exploratory and observational in nature, and causal inferences cannot be drawn.

A detailed anatomical subclassification was intentionally included in this study because understanding the diverse distribution patterns of bone bruises is essential for interpreting the multifactorial pathophysiology of ankle sprains. These patterns provide insight into injury mechanisms that cannot be explained solely by lateral ligament damage, such as axial loading or rotational forces.

Although the Results section contains extensive descriptive data, all anatomical subcategories were statistically analyzed to ensure comprehensive and unbiased reporting. To maintain readability, the main text focuses primarily on clinically and statistically relevant findings.

Low-intensity findings on T1-weighted imaging and high-intensity findings on T2-weighted or STIR imaging typically indicate bone marrow edema or hemorrhage, which are not specific to bone fractures.

However, histologic–radiologic correlation studies have shown that these MRI signal abnormalities often represent microstructural bone damage rather than simple edema. Zanetti et al. demonstrated that so-called “bone marrow edema patterns” in osteoarthritic knees consist largely of necrotic or remodeled trabeculae, fibrosis, and microfractures, rather than true edema [19].

Furthermore, Roemer et al. reported that progressive or newly developing bone marrow lesions strongly predict subsequent cartilage loss, indicating that such microtrabecular injuries play a key role in the structural progression of osteoarthritis [20]. Guermazi et al. also noted that MRI-detected bone bruises may reflect microfractures that contribute to OA development [21].

Taken together, the bone bruises observed in ankle sprains may reflect microtrabecular stress or injury rather than definitively indicating microfracture, suggesting a potential risk for later degenerative changes if insufficiently protected during the early healing phase.

Although ultrasound is highly sensitive for diagnosing ATFL injuries, as demonstrated by Colò et al. [14], it cannot detect bone bruises or midfoot involvement. In our cohort, bone bruises were present in 81.4% of cases, including lesions in the Chopart joint. These findings highlight the added value of MRI when evaluating moderate-to-severe ankle sprains, particularly when bone involvement contributes to persistent pain.

These findings suggest that certain clinical cues—such as medial tenderness or persistent pain after adequate fixation—may indicate underlying bone bruises, in which case temporary non–weight-bearing with crutches could be considered as part of an individualized treatment approach.

Ligament-dominant soft-tissue injuries would not be expected to cause joint pain during weight bearing if rigid fixation is properly applied, and joint instability is eliminated. However, in this study, 32 of 70 patients (45.7%) complained of ankle joint pain during weight bearing even after external rigid fixation. Univariate analysis revealed a significant association between bone bruises of the talocrural joint surface and the use of crutches after fixation (P = 0.041).

Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that both younger age (OR 0.957, 95% CI 0.930–0.985, P = 0.003) and bone bruises on the talocrural joint surface of the talus (OR 4.012, 95% CI 1.174–13.713, P = 0.027) were independently associated with crutch use after fixation. In this context, in the treatment of ankle sprains, if the patient continues to experience joint pain after solid fixation, it may indicate the presence of microtrabecular stress injury, including in the weight-bearing surface of the bone. In such cases, crutches may be considered to prevent weight bearing and facilitate bone recovery.

The finding that bone bruises on the talocrural joint surface of the talus were significantly associated with weight-bearing pain and the need for crutch use represents a key feature linking clinical symptoms with imaging findings in acute ankle sprains. Notably, previous literature has also reported that a large bone marrow lesion of the talus is a prognostic factor for developing chronic ankle instability after a first lateral ankle sprain [22], indicating a certain degree of similarity in the clinical relevance of talar bone involvement.

To contextualize these findings within the existing literature, it is important to compare them with prior studies examining prognostic factors in ankle sprains. Unlike the previous study that focused on long-term prognosis after a first lateral ankle sprain [22], the present study evaluated acute moderate-to-severe sprains and characterized bone bruise distribution involving the talocrural joint surface and midfoot. Our results suggest that talar bone bruises may have implications not only for acute functional limitation but also potentially for longer-term outcomes, highlighting both similarities and distinctions between the two studies.

Ankle sprains are typically considered to involve injuries to the lateral ankle ligaments, with little attention given to medial side injuries. In this study, bone bruises on the medial side of the ankle were observed in 31(44.3%) of cases, and all these cases were accompanied by medial pain and tenderness. In cases with medial bone bruises in this study, medial pain and tenderness were documented in all patients based on the initial clinical records. Although pain assessment was not standardized due to the retrospective design, this consistent documentation suggests that medial symptoms may reflect underlying medial injury. In patients with medial side pain and tenderness, bone damage to the medial side may be present, and solid external fixation may be warranted.

Although various external fixation methods have been proposed for ankle sprains, fixation of the midfoot is often overlooked. Typically, fixation limits the range of motion to the ankle joint, restricting inversion and eversion while allowing for dorsal and plantar flexion [8, 23]. In this study, bone bruises in the midfoot associated with ankle sprains were detected in 24(34.3%) of cases.

Since it is difficult to accurately assess midfoot ligament injuries using MRI, it is possible that midfoot damage is more prevalent if ligament injuries were also assessed. In moderate or severe cases of ankle sprain, external fixation should not only target the ankle but also the midfoot, given the potential for midfoot injury.

Various fixation periods have been proposed for the external immobilization of ankle sprains, ranging from short to long durations. Recent studies have shown that a short period of immobilization can be beneficial even for severe ankle sprains [2–6]. A meta-analysis by Kerkhoffs et al. found that functional treatment was a favorable strategy for treating acute ankle sprains compared to long-term immobilization [2].

However, Hubbard et al. reported that up to 75% of initial lateral ankle sprains lead to recurrent sprains and chronic symptoms [11]. While short-term immobilization may alleviate symptoms, some cases persist with chronic pain and instability even after treatment [24–27].

Hubbard and Cordova observed that ankle laxity did not significantly decrease over an 8-week period [28]. Joshua et al. reported that the remodeling phase of ligament healing begins 15 to 28 days post-injury. During this phase, newly formed collagen fibers align longitudinally, and cross-linkages form. By 3 weeks, as collagen maturation continues, the ligament may regain approximately 60% of its tensile strength. By 3 months, the ligament may regain its full strength [29]. Ligament injuries require a prolonged recovery period.

On the other hand, Yammine et al. reported that patients with bone contusions of the talus treated with non-weight-bearing casts for 5 weeks had a good clinical outcome [30]. For Lisfranc (midfoot) injuries, the AAOS recommends a nonsurgical treatment plan that includes wearing a non-weight-bearing cast or boot for 6 weeks [1]. Such evidence underscores the need for appropriate duration of immobilization for both ligament and bone healing, as well as the restoration of joint stability following an ankle sprain.

In this study, no cases of persistent pain were observed following the treatment of ankle sprains. However, the duration of external fixation required to alleviate pain varied from 8 to 72 days (mean: 29.5 days). Given the wide range of bone and ligament damage, it is expected that the fixation period would vary significantly. As this period should be tailored to each individual case, recommending a fixed duration of fixation may not be optimal.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a potential concern with long-term immobilization. In the present series of acute cases, however, no DVT events were observed. Several factors may have contributed to the relatively low risk of DVT in this cohort.

The gastrocnemius is a biarticular muscle and is not fully immobilized when the knee joint is not fixed. In addition, the ankle joint was immobilized using a semi-rigid splint, which allowed a small degree of both active and passive motion. The treatment protocol also permitted intermittent mobilization, rather than continuous rigid immobilization, which may have further reduced prolonged immobility. These characteristics of the protocol likely contributed to minimizing DVT risk in our study.

Moreover, 57 out of 70 patients (81.4%) in our cohort exhibited bone bruises around the ankle joint, indicating that MRI may be useful for more precise evaluation of ankle joint sprains. From a treatment perspective, it is crucial to consider the presence of both bone bruises and ligament injuries in the ankle and midfoot. A thorough visual inspection and palpation may be considered to identify the injured areas, which may then be managed with immobilization for an appropriate duration.

If pain persists during walking after fixation, crutches may be considered to reduce weight-bearing stress. Ultimately, if this treatment approach is followed, MRI may not be necessary. In this study, MRI served primarily as a research tool to characterize the patterns of bone and ligament injury. Clinically, MRI may be selectively useful when symptoms do not improve as expected or when the injury pattern is unclear.

In this retrospective MRI series, bone bruises in ankle and midfoot were common in Grade 2–3 ankle sprains and were associated with persistent weight-bearing pain [1]. Prospective studies assessing standardized immobilization strategies and long-term clinical outcomes are warranted.

Limitations

This study has several limitations.

First, although MRI was routinely performed for moderate (Grade 2) to severe (Grade 3) ankle sprains at our institution, this practice does not represent standard care in many clinical settings. The use of MRI reflects an institution-specific clinical policy rather than guideline-based management, which may introduce institutional or selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Because MRI was performed only for Grade 2–3 sprains, selection bias may have influenced the observed prevalence of bone bruises. In addition, the study population consisted of patients who visited orthopedic clinics, and therefore the cohort may not have included the most severe cases of ankle sprains with extensive bone bruising.

Second, the interval between injury and MRI examination varied widely among patients (3–28 days). Because bone bruises and bone marrow edema are time-dependent findings, this variability may have affected both the detectability and the apparent extent of bone marrow abnormalities. Although additional analyses stratified by MRI timing were considered, such analyses were not feasible due to small subgroup sample sizes and the inability to control for time-dependent confounders.

Third, walking pain was assessed based on patient-reported symptoms without the use of a standardized pain scale or functional outcome measure. Analgesic use may have influenced pain assessment and the need for crutches. Crutch use after fixation was employed as a surrogate marker of persistent weight bearing pain; however, this outcome has not been validated and may be influenced by physician instruction and patient compliance.

Fourth, this study included patients with a wide age range (10–93 years). Age-related differences in skeletal maturity, healing capacity, and pain perception may have influenced clinical outcomes. Because the sample size within each age category was limited, age-stratified analyses were not feasible, and the influence of age heterogeneity remains a limitation.

Fifth, although 32 patients required crutch use, the number of outcome events relative to the number of predictors included in the multivariate model was limited. The resulting events-per-variable ratio met commonly recommended thresholds; however, some degree of model instability and potential overfitting cannot be excluded.

Sixth, MRI findings were interpreted independently by two orthopedic surgeons and one radiologist; however, formal interobserver reliability statistics (such as kappa coefficients) were not calculated. The absence of quantitative reliability data represents an additional limitation of this study.

Finally, given the retrospective and exploratory nature of this study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed, and causal inference is not possible. This study was not designed to evaluate treatment effectiveness or compare management strategies; therefore, any implications regarding immobilization duration or weight-bearing recommendations should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Bone bruises were frequently observed not only around the ankle but also in the midfoot in patients with moderate to severe ankle sprains. These MRI findings suggest that ankle sprains may involve multifaceted patterns of bone injury. Bone bruises on the talocrural joint surface of the talus were associated with an increased likelihood of crutch use following fixation.

However, crutch use was employed as a surrogate marker of persistent weight-bearing pain, and this outcome may be influenced by physician instruction and patient compliance; therefore, the clinical implications of this association should be interpreted with caution.

In addition, this association should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment effectiveness, as the study was not designed to evaluate different management strategies or determine optimal immobilization or weight-bearing protocols.

The interval between injury and MRI examination varied widely, and because bone marrow edema evolves over time, this variability may have influenced the detectability and distribution of bone bruises.

Furthermore, the study included patients with a wide age range, and differences in skeletal maturity, healing capacity, and pain perception across age groups may have affected clinical outcomes.

Given the retrospective and exploratory nature of this study, further prospective research is warranted to confirm these findings and clarify their clinical relevance.
