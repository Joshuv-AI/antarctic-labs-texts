Saliva-based monitoring of chronic kidney disease: comparative evaluation of three collection methods for creatinine, urea, calcium, and PTH
Authors: Mohammad Abu Raihan Uddin, Tuan Salwani Tuan Ismail, Wan Nor Fazila Hafizan Wan Nik, Wan Nor Arifin, Salbiah Isa, Noor Huda Ismail, Muhammad Imran Kamarudin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) requires regular biochemical monitoring of renal function and mineral metabolism; however, the invasiveness of serum-based testing limits frequent assessment, prompting interest in saliva as a non-invasive alternative despite ongoing challenges related to collection methods and biomarker specific limitations. In this cross-sectional study, paired saliva and serum samples were collected from 47 healthy adults and 47 CKD patients. Saliva was obtained via passive drool, Salivette®, and cotton roll. Creatinine, urea, calcium, and PTH were measured using standardized assays. Inter-method agreement was assessed with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Bland-Altman analysis, correlations salivary and serum biomarkers were assessed using correlation analyses evaluated salivary-serum associations, and linear regression examined predictive performance. ICC analysis showed moderate agreement for salivary creatinine, urea, and PTH across collection methods, with average-measure ICCs consistently higher than single-measure, while calcium had poor agreement. Bland-Altman plots revealed narrower limits of agreement for creatinine and urea, especially using cotton roll and passive drool, versus wider limits for calcium and PTH. Salivary creatinine and urea correlated moderately to strongly with serum levels, highest with cotton roll (creatinine ρ = 0.584; urea ρ = 0.648), and predicted serum concentrations in CKD patients (R² = 0.214, 0.339) better than in healthy controls, whereas calcium and PTH showed weak correlations and minimal predictive value. Salivary creatinine and urea show reliable agreement with serum levels and modest predictive value, supporting their potential utility as non-invasive biomarkers for renal function monitoring in CKD, although further validation is required before clinical implementation, whereas calcium and PTH are less valid, with cotton roll collection performing comparably to passive drool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54228-2.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Salivary biomarkers, Creatinine, Urea, Calcium, Parathyroid hormone, Saliva collection methods, Cotton roll, Noninvasive diagnostics, Renal function monitoringSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research, Nephrology

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive and irreversible condition characterized by a sustained decline in renal function, leading to the accumulation of nitrogenous waste products and disturbances in mineral metabolism1. Globally, CKD affects approximately 10% of the adult population and represents a growing public health burden due to its association with cardiovascular morbidity, bone mineral disorders, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality2,3. As kidney function deteriorates, routine monitoring of biochemical markers such as creatinine and urea, along with mineral metabolism markers including calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH), becomes essential for disease staging, therapeutic decision-making, and long term monitoring4.

Currently, monitoring of CKD relies almost exclusively on repeated blood sampling. Although serum-based measurements are clinically reliable, frequent venepuncture is invasive, uncomfortable, and associated with reduced patient compliance, particularly among elderly patients, individuals with poor venous access, and those requiring lifelong monitoring5. These limitations have driven increasing interest in non-invasive alternatives that could facilitate more frequent assessment while reducing patient burden and healthcare costs.

Saliva has emerged as a promising biological fluid for non-invasive diagnostics due to its ease of collection, safety, low cost, and ability to reflect systemic physiological and pathological changes. Salivary biomarkers have been investigated in a wide range of systemic conditions, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and renal dysfunction6,7. Several studies have reported positive correlations between salivary and serum concentrations of urea and creatinine, particularly in patients with advanced CKD, suggesting that salivary measurements may reflect impaired renal excretory function. However, reported correlations vary widely across studies, limiting the translation of salivary biomarkers into routine clinical practice8.

One of the major sources of variability in salivary biomarker research is the lack of standardization in saliva collection methods. Saliva composition is influenced by flow rate, glandular contribution, stimulation, and collection materials, all of which can substantially affect analyte concentrations. For unstimulated methods, spitting is a widely used saliva collection method due to its simplicity, but the method may yield insufficient sample volumes, especially in individuals with low salivary flow rates (e.g., xerostomia or CKD patients). Passive drool is also often preferred for its simplicity and minimal stimulation, providing samples that closely reflect the basal state of salivary secretion8. Stimulated methods, while yielding larger sample volumes, may dilute or alter biomarker concentrations9.

Commercial devices such as the salivette® have been developed which is standardized for only cortisol measurements to reduce contamination during saliva collection and are widely used in hormonal and clinical research. Nevertheless, these devices are relatively costly and may not be feasible in low-resource settings or large-scale screening programs10. The cotton roll method, which involves saliva absorption using an inert cotton material, represents a simple, inexpensive, and patient-friendly alternative. Despite its practical advantages, concerns remain regarding analyte retention, assay interference, and variability, and its performance relative to established saliva collection methods has not been systematically evaluated for renal biomarkers.

In addition to biochemical markers, disturbances in mineral metabolism are a hallmark of CKD progression. Altered calcium homeostasis and secondary hyperparathyroidism, reflected by elevated PTH levels, contribute to CKD-related mineral and bone disorders11. While serum measurements remain the gold standard, exploration of salivary calcium and PTH may provide insight into non-invasive monitoring of CKD-associated mineral abnormalities. However, existing evidence regarding salivary calcium and PTH is limited and inconsistent, with studies reporting weak or variable correlations with serum values. Methodological heterogeneity, particularly in saliva collection techniques, further complicates interpretation12,13.

Given these gaps, there is a clear need for systematic evaluation of saliva collection methods to determine their reliability, agreement, and clinical relevance for both biochemical and mineral markers in CKD. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate salivary creatinine, urea, calcium, and PTH collected using three different saliva collection methods passive drool, salivette®, and cotton roll in both healthy adults and patients with CKD. Specifically, this study assessed (i) the agreement of biomarker concentrations across the three saliva collection methods, (ii) the correlation between salivary and serum biomarkers for each method, and (iii) the predictive value of cotton roll collected salivary biomarkers for estimating corresponding serum parameters. By identifying a reliable, practical, and patient-friendly saliva collection approach, this study seeks to contribute to the methodological standardization of saliva-based diagnostics and to evaluate the comparative performance of different collection approaches for renal biomarkers in CKD.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional comparative study conducted to evaluate biochemical markers (urea and creatinine) and mineral markers (calcium and parathyroid hormone [PTH]) in saliva and serum among healthy adults and patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Saliva was collected using three different methods: passive drool (unstimulated), Salivette® (stimulated), and a cotton roll method evaluated as a potential alternative collection approach.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Hospital Pakar Universiti Sains Malaysia (HPUSM), Kubang Kerian, Kelantan. Recruitment of CKD patients and blood sampling were performed at the Nephrology Clinic, while healthy participants were recruited from outpatient clinic waiting areas. All saliva and serum samples were processed and analyzed at the Endocrine Laboratory, HPUSM.

Study population

The study population comprised two groups: healthy adults and patients diagnosed with CKD. All participants were aged ≥ 18 years. Healthy individuals were recruited as a reference group, while CKD patients were recruited from routine nephrology follow up clinics. Eligibility was assessed prior to enrolment by dental officer to ensure suitability for salivary and serum biomarker analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Healthy participants

Healthy participants were included if they were aged ≥ 18 years, self-reported as healthy with no known chronic systemic diseases, and provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included smoking, pregnancy or lactation, presence of chronic diseases that are not related to CKD (e.g., diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular or autoimmune diseases), history of infection within four weeks prior to sampling, oral pathological conditions including candidiasis, stomatitis, periodontitis, gingivitis, mucosal lesions, or swelling of major salivary glands, and hyposalivation.

CKD patients

CKD patients were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18 years, had a confirmed diagnosis of CKD stage 3b or higher (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 45 mL/min/1.73 m²), were not receiving renal replacement therapy, attended routine follow up at the Nephrology Clinic, and provided informed consent. Patients receiving dialysis were excluded due to dialysis-related alterations in serum saliva relationships. Additional exclusion criteria included smoking, pregnancy or lactation, kidney transplantation, comorbid conditions unrelated to CKD (e.g., diabetes mellitus, autoimmune diseases, hepatic disease, malignancy, or active infection), oral pathological conditions including candidiasis, stomatitis, periodontitis, gingivitis, mucosal lesions, or swelling of major salivary glands, and hyposalivation.

Sample size estimation

Sample size calculations were performed according to the study objectives. For agreement analysis among saliva collection methods using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), a minimum of 67 participants was required based on a Type I error of 5% and power of 80%. After accounting for a 10% anticipated dropout rate, the target sample size was increased to 75 participants14.

For correlation analysis between salivary and serum biomarkers, sample size estimation using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) indicated a minimum of 84 participants, which was increased to 94 after adjusting for potential sample loss. For predictive analyses using linear regression models, a minimum of 55 participants was required based on an effect size (f²) of 0.15, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80. The largest calculated sample size (n = 94) was adopted for the study and divided into healthy and CKD groups15,16.

Saliva and blood sample collection

Participants were instructed to refrain from food, beverages, and oral hygiene activities for at least 30 min prior to saliva collection. Blood samples (3 mL) were collected into plain tubes, allowed to clot for 1 h, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and serum aliquots were stored at −80 °C until analysis17.

All saliva and blood samples were collected in the morning between 8:00 AM and 10:00 AM, using three methods in a standardized sequence: passive drool first, followed by either Salivette® or cotton roll in randomized order, with a five-minute interval between methods. Passive drool saliva was collected by allowing saliva to pool in the mouth and drip into a sterile container over 5–10 min until 2 mL was obtained. Not all participants were able to provide the required saliva volume within the specified collection period. Two participants were excluded due to inability to obtain 2 mL of saliva within 10 min, likely reflecting reduced salivary flow. Salivary flow rate was measured during passive drool collection and recorded as volume per unit time (mL/min) for each participant. For Salivette®, participants chewed the swab for approximately 60 s before returning it to the collection tube. For the cotton roll method, a sterile cotton roll was placed in the mouth for approximately one minute without chewing or sucking. Although salivary flow rate was recorded, biomarker concentrations were not normalized to flow rate or total protein. The analysis was performed using raw salivary concentrations to maintain consistency with standard clinical reporting formats. All saliva samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C within 30 min of collection. Total time between blood and saliva collection did not exceed one hour17.

Biochemical and hormonal analyses

Creatinine, urea, and calcium concentrations in serum and saliva were measured using a Beckman Coulter AU680 analyzer. Creatinine was quantified using the kinetic Jaffe method, urea using an enzymatic urease-UV assay, and calcium using the NM-BAPTA colorimetric method. Parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels were measured using a two-site sandwich electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on the Cobas e 601 analyzer. Saliva and serum samples were analyzed using the same analytical platforms under standardized laboratory conditions. To minimize inter-assay variability, samples were processed and analyzed in batches with consistent calibration procedures and internal quality controls at multiple concentration levels.

Quality control and assay validation

All assays were performed according to manufacturer’s protocols. Calibration was conducted using traceable standards, and internal quality controls at low, medium, and high concentrations were analyzed with each batch. The laboratory participated in external quality assessment program for all analytes. Method verification was performed to ensure suitability of assays for salivary matrices, including assessment of precision, linearity, and limits of detection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 and R Studio version 4.6. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Agreement among saliva collection methods was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement) and Bland-Altman analyses. Correlations between salivary and serum biomarkers were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution. Predictive relationships between salivary and serum biomarkers were examined using linear regression models.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Sains Malaysia (JEPeM-USM; approval code: USM/JEPeM/KK/25040391). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 94 participants were included, comprising 47 healthy controls and 47 patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). The mean age of healthy participants was 37.06 ± 11.83 years, while CKD patients were older (57.89 ± 14.76 years). Sex distribution was comparable between groups (healthy: 23 males, 24 females; CKD: 26 males, 21 females). All participants were free of clinically significant oral pathology. CKD staging followed KDIGO 2024 criteria, with all patients classified as stage 3b-5 (non-dialysis) (Table 1).

Agreement of salivary biochemical and mineral markers across collection methods

Agreement among the three saliva collection methods was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Bland-Altman analyses for both healthy adults and CKD patients.

Through, Intraclass correlation coefficient analysis, moderate agreement was observed for salivary creatinine and urea across collection methods in both groups (Table 2). For creatinine, ICC values ranged from 0.28 to 0.54 in healthy participants and 0.31–0.58 in CKD patients. Urea demonstrated similar agreement, with ICCs of 0.25–0.50 in healthy adults and 0.37–0.64 in CKD patients. Calcium showed poor agreement among collection methods in healthy participants (ICC ≤ 0.13), with slightly higher but still limited agreement in CKD patients (ICC up to 0.34). In contrast, PTH demonstrated moderate agreement across methods in both healthy adults (ICC up to 0.55) and CKD patients (ICC up to 0.58).

Bland Altman analysis in healthy adults (Fig. 1), creatinine measurements demonstrated small systematic bias across all pairwise comparisons, with mean biases ranging from − 3.04 to 1.74 µmol/L. The corresponding limits of agreement were relatively narrow (e.g., −14.27 to 8.20 µmol/L for passive drool vs. salivette®), and more than 93.6% of observations fell within the LoA, indicating acceptable agreement despite minor method dependent differences. Urea showed comparable agreement, with mean biases between − 1.07 and 1.57 mmol/L and LoA spanning − 6.82 to 7.14 mmol/L, with ≥ 93.6% of values within the limits, suggesting modest but consistent systematic variation among methods. Calcium exhibited minimal bias across collection techniques in healthy participants, with mean differences close to zero (−0.03 to 0.07 mmol/L) and narrow LoA (e.g., −0.31 to 0.37 mmol/L for passive drool vs. cotton roll). The high proportion of observations within the LoA (93.6–97.8%) indicates strong agreement and minimal influence of collection method on calcium measurements. PTH measurements showed generally good agreement; however, comparisons involving the cotton roll method demonstrated a consistent positive bias (mean bias 4.18–4.26 pg/mL), with wider LoA (up to −3.41 to 11.76 pg/mL), indicating a tendency toward higher PTH values with cotton-based collection.

Among CKD patients (Fig. 2), creatinine measurements showed small mean biases (−8.56 to 3.08 µmol/L), but substantially wider LoA compared with healthy participants (−65.34 to 48.22 µmol/L for passive drool vs. salivette®), reflecting greater biological variability in this group. Nevertheless, ≥ 95.7% of observations remained within the LoA across comparisons. Urea demonstrated similar patterns, with mean biases ranging from 0.63 to 2.95 mmol/L and wide LoA (up to −13.52 to 16.68 mmol/L), while maintaining acceptable proportions within the limits. Calcium measurements in CKD patients remained consistent across methods, with small mean biases (0.02–0.12 mmol/L) and relatively narrow LoA (−0.58 to 0.62 mmol/L), supporting good agreement irrespective of collection technique. For PTH, agreement was acceptable but less consistent, particularly for comparisons involving the cotton roll method, which demonstrated a persistent positive bias (mean bias 3.77–4.30 pg/mL) and wider LoA (up to −9.57 to 17.11 pg/mL). Correlation between salivary and serum biomarkers across collection methods

Correlation analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between serum and salivary biomarker concentrations across three saliva collection methods (passive drool, salivette®, and cotton roll) in healthy adults and CKD patients (Table 3). For creatinine, moderate correlations between serum and salivary concentrations were observed, with method dependent differences. In healthy participants, passive drool demonstrated the strongest association (r = 0.534, p < 0.001), followed by cotton roll (ρ = 0.357, p = 0.014), while salivette® showed no significant correlation (r = − 0.102, p = 0.495). In CKD patients, correlations were generally stronger, particularly for cotton roll (ρ = 0.584, p < 0.001) and passive drool (r = 0.443, p = 0.002), whereas salivette® again showed a weak and non-significant association (ρ = 0.258, p = 0.080). Urea exhibited consistent and statistically significant positive correlations across all collection methods in both groups. In healthy participants, correlation coefficients ranged from 0.362 to 0.506 (p ≤ 0.012), with the strongest association observed for salivette®. In the CKD group, correlations were higher overall, particularly for cotton roll (ρ = 0.648, p < 0.001), followed by salivette® (ρ = 0.572, p < 0.001) and passive drool (ρ = 0.393, p = 0.006), indicating robust concordance between salivary and serum urea levels irrespective of collection method.

In healthy adults, salivary creatinine demonstrated a significant positive association with serum creatinine (β = 1.527, 95% CI: 0.620–2.435; p = 0.001), explaining 20.3% of the variance (R² = 0.203). Salivary urea also showed a statistically significant relationship with serum urea (β = 0.181, 95% CI: 0.026–0.335; p = 0.023), although with lower explanatory power (R² = 0.110). Among CKD patients, stronger predictive relationships were observed for salivary biochemical markers. Salivary creatinine remained a significant predictor of serum creatinine (β = 3.267, 95% CI: 1.389–5.146; p = 0.001), with an R² of 0.214. Salivary urea showed the highest predictive performance across all biomarkers (β = 0.642, 95% CI: 0.373–0.912; p < 0.001), explaining 33.9% of the variance in serum urea levels (R² = 0.339). Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive comparative evaluation of three saliva collection methods (passive drool, Salivette®, and cotton roll) within a single cohort, focusing on their impact on biochemical (creatinine, urea) and mineral (calcium, PTH) markers in CKD and healthy adults. By simultaneously assessing agreement across methods, correlation with serum, and predictive utility, we identify cotton roll as practical, low cost, and patient friendly approaches for non-invasive CKD monitoring. This work addresses a critical translational gap, contributing to the methodological foundation necessary for future clinical validation of saliva-based diagnostics for high-frequency or resource limited monitoring, particularly for biochemical markers where invasive blood sampling remains burdensome. The high variability in serum creatinine and PTH observed in CKD patients likely reflects the heterogeneous severity of renal impairment within the non-dialysis CKD stage 3b-5 cohort.

The difference in mean age between healthy controls and CKD patients represents a potential source of variability, as age may influence salivary composition, renal function, and hormonal regulation. However, this disparity reflects the real-world epidemiology of chronic kidney disease, which predominantly affects older populations. Importantly, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate agreement, correlation, and predictive performance of salivary biomarkers within each group and across collection methods, rather than to perform direct comparisons between age-matched cohorts. Nevertheless, the potential confounding effect of age should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Agreement analyses revealed moderate concordance for creatinine and urea across collection methods, whereas calcium exhibited poor agreement and PTH moderate agreement. Bland-Altman assessments showed minimal systematic bias for creatinine and urea, although wider limits of agreement were observed in CKD patients, reflecting increased biological variability. Our findings corroborate prior studies demonstrating the robustness of salivary urea and creatinine8,18, while extending them by directly comparing three collection strategies within the same cohort and highlighting the superior reliability of cotton roll and passive drool over Salivette®, likely due to dilution effects in stimulated saliva9,10,19.

Correlation and predictive analyses further emphasize clinical utility. Salivary urea and creatinine demonstrated significant, method dependent correlations with serum levels, strongest for passive drool and cotton roll, particularly in CKD patients, whereas calcium and PTH correlations remained weak and inconsistent8,12,20,21. Cotton roll collected saliva explained up to 34% of the variance in serum urea and 21% in serum creatinine, supporting its potential as a noninvasive surrogate for biochemical monitoring. In contrast, mineral markers were not reliably predicted, underscoring intrinsic limitations of salivary calcium and peptide hormones for clinical surveillance12,13.

The observed differences in salivary-serum correlations among biomarkers may be explained by their distinct physiological pathways into saliva. Small, water-soluble metabolites such as urea and creatinine primarily enter saliva through passive diffusion from serum, allowing their salivary concentrations to more closely reflect systemic levels22. In contrast, calcium is actively regulated and secreted by salivary gland epithelial cells, and its salivary concentration is influenced by local glandular transport mechanisms and salivary flow rather than directly reflecting circulating serum levels. This may account for the weaker and more variable correlations observed for calcium in this study. Similarly, protein-based hormones such as PTH have limited permeability into saliva and may be subject to degradation or selective transport, further reducing their diagnostic consistency23,24.

A major strength of this study is the comprehensive methodological comparison across multiple saliva collection techniques and analytical approaches in both healthy and CKD populations. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The absence of age matching between groups may have contributed to variability, although this reflects real world CKD demographics. Salivary flow rate, which was measured but not used for normalization, may influence biomarker concentrations due to dilution effects and variability in glandular contributions, particularly in CKD patients who may exhibit reduced salivary secretion. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of longitudinal performance and intra individual variability. Finally, the study focused on non-dialysis CKD pa2tients, limiting generalizability to dialysis dependent populations. Future studies should consider flow-adjusted normalization or total protein correction to improve the interpretability and comparability of salivary biomarker measurements.

Conclusions

In conclusion, salivary urea and creatinine demonstrate acceptable inter-method agreement, significant serum correlations, and measurable predictive value, particularly in CKD patients, supporting their potential as candidate non-invasive biomarkers, pending further validation in larger and longitudinal studies of renal function. In contrast, salivary calcium and PTH exhibit limited biological and predictive validity despite methodological agreement. These findings supporting the exploratory feasibility of salivary diagnostics for renal biomarker assessment in chronic kidney disease.
