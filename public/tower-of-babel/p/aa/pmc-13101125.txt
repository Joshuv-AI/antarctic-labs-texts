Exploring the lived experiences and coping strategies of couples after stillbirth in Ho Municipality, Ghana: a phenomenological approach
Authors: Anita Fafa Dartey, Francis Mawougnon Sagbo, Regina Aku Nyakpo, Alimatu Issifu, Diana Dede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStillbirth is a devastating event that affects millions of families worldwide, with over 2 million cases reported annually. Despite efforts to reduce stillbirth rates, progress has been slow, particularly in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Ghana. The experiences of couples who have had stillbirths in Ghana, especially in the Volta Region, remain underexplored. Psychological and social factors play a crucial role in the grieving process of couples who have experienced stillbirth, impacting their mental health and overall well-being. This study explored the untold Stories of Couples who have experienced Stillbirth in Ho Municipality, Ghana, capturing their voices and bringing to light their journey of grief and resilience.MethodsThe research employed a qualitative phenomenological approach to explore participants’ experiences. Study participants were selected through purposive sampling. Saturation was achieved after the 19th participant was interviewed. Data collection involved conducting semi-structured interviews, and thematic content analysis was applied to identify and scrutinize emerging themes.ResultThe study revealed four main themes and fourteen subthemes, providing an in-depth understanding of the emotional journeys of couples who have experienced stillbirth. The identified themes encompass experiences during pregnancy, emotional responses to stillbirth incidents, consequences of stillbirth on couples, and their resilience in such difficult moments.ConclusionThe study revealed the diverse emotional challenges faced by couples. This insight contributes to a more holistic approach in addressing the emotional complexities associated with stillbirth emphasizing the need for targeted support for affected individuals and families.

Introduction

The event of childbirth is a life-changing experience that sometimes brings excitement to families and has immense significance [1]. Pregnancies that result in live births can strengthen family bonds, inspire shared aspirations, and foster a sense of community. However, not all pregnancy journeys end in a live birth. For some families, the loss of a baby through stillbirth can be deeply distressing, bringing grief and emotional challenges that require resilience, support, and understanding [2]. According to Nuzum et al. [3], parents do not expect their pregnancy to end in stillbirth, leaving them bereaved and in shock [4].

Stillbirth is a global concern, especially in low and middle-income countries. The United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund estimates that there were 2.6 million stillbirths worldwide, with a rate of 13.9 per 1,000 live births [5]. There were, however, variations in the rates of occurrence across continents and between sub-regions. Stillbirth rate is a crucial indicator of healthcare quality and health system strength, requiring urgent action from international organizations, governments, and partners to prevent deaths [6]. The impact of stillbirth extends beyond clinical outcomes, affecting families emotionally, socially, and psychologically. Understanding the lived experiences of couples who have experienced stillbirth is therefore essential to inform healthcare provision, improve support services, and guide interventions that address both the physical and psychosocial consequences of stillbirth [7].

Partners who have had stillbirth experience a variety of effects, including changes to their psychological well-being, spirituality, and physical health [8] Grief does not only affect human emotions but also impacts their physical health [9]. The loss of a child can also lead to a sense of helplessness and separation from spiritual values. In some countries, stigma or social silence may hinder open discussions about the incident of stillbirths [10] According to research by Human et al. [8], stillbirth significantly alters a person’s life Parents who experience stillbirth have a considerably higher risk of reporting symptoms of depression, anxiety, insomnia, loss of appetite and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) compared with parents with live births [11] Notwithstanding, parents who go through the tragedy of stillbirth can become resilient and gain new abilities and skills for life [10].

While there exists a growing body of literature examining the psychosocial, emotional, and cultural dimensions of stillbirth in various global contexts, there is a noticeable gap in research specifically exploring the lived experiences of couples who have endured stillbirth in Ho Municipality, Volta Region, Ghana. The incidence of stillbirth in the Volta Region, particularly within Ho Municipality, remains high, with reports indicating rates of up to 31.3 per 1,000 births at Ho Teaching Hospital [12]. This relatively elevated burden underscores the need to understand the contextual factors shaping the experiences of affected couples in the region. This is essential for the development of effective support systems and interventions tailored to the local context. The existing literature predominantly emanates from high-income countries (HICs), which may not fully capture the nuances and challenges faced by couples in LMIC settings like Ho Municipality due to differences in health system structures, socio-cultural beliefs, economic conditions, and available support services [13, 14]. Considering the limited context-specific evidence from the Volta Region, this study employed a phenomenological approach to explore the lived experiences of women and their partners who had experienced stillbirth in Ho Municipality, Ghana. Through in-depth individual interviews, the study examined how participants perceived, interpreted, and coped with the loss within their relational, social, and cultural contexts.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative approach with a phenomenological research design. This design assisted the researcher in identifying the essence of human experiences concerning a phenomenon as stated by study participants. When examining sensitive subjects or marginalised groups, qualitative research enables researchers to record the subjective feelings, lived experience and viewpoints of participants [15].

Sampling and data collection strategy

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit couples who had experienced stillbirth, as they were best positioned to provide rich descriptions of the phenomenon under study. Data saturation, defined as the point at which additional interviews no longer generated new themes or insights relevant to the study aim [16], was reached after the nineteenth participant. Recruitment and interviews were conducted iteratively, with ongoing analysis guiding continued recruitment until subsequent interviews yielded repetitive information. Prior to participant recruitment, the researchers sought administrative permission from the management of purposively selected public health facilities in Ho Municipality that provide maternity and delivery services and had records of stillbirth cases. This permission was obtained to allow access to relevant hospital records and to facilitate identification of potential participants who met the inclusion criteria, as well as to ensure institutional awareness and ethical oversight of the study.

The study population comprised couples who had experienced stillbirth in the selected health facilities and were residing in Ho Municipality at the time of the study. A couple was defined as two partners in an intimate relationship at the time of the stillbirth, regardless of marital status. Inclusion criteria were couples aged 16 years and above who had experienced stillbirth at least six months prior to the interview and were willing to share their experiences. Each couple, having agreed among themselves to participate in the study, could be present together for the interview or have one partner present depending on their availability. Separate informed consent was obtained from all participating individuals. Couples or individuals who declined or withdrew consent, were experiencing severe psychological distress, or were unable to participate in an in-depth interview due to health or communication challenges were excluded.

Eligible participants were identified through hospital delivery and maternity records, with the assistance of designated hospital staff. Because the participants had been discharged and were residing in their communities at the time of the study, initial contact was made using telephone numbers recorded in hospital registers. Potential participants were contacted by phone calls, during which the purpose of the study was explained, and visits were scheduled with those who expressed interest in participating.

Written and verbal informed consent were obtained from all participants prior to data collection. For participants below 18 years, parental assent was also sought to ensure ethical participation. This age group was included because stillbirth can occur in adolescents, and their experiences are important for understanding the phenomenon across all reproductive ages. Participants were assured of confidentiality, privacy, and anonymity, and were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any stage without any consequences. To protect privacy, identifying information was not linked to interview data, and participants were assigned codes during transcription and analysis.

Data were collected through face-to-face, in-depth interviews using a semi-structured interview guide designed to explore lived experiences, challenges, and coping mechanisms following stillbirth. Interviews were conducted at locations preferred by participants, including their homes or other private and quiet settings, to ensure comfort and confidentiality. Only the participant(s) and the researcher were present during interviews, and care was taken to conduct interviews in spaces where conversations could not be overheard.

Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 50 min and was conducted by the second author, who had prior training and experience in qualitative data collection. No research assistants were involved in the data collection process. Interviews were digitally audio-recorded with participants’ permission, and field notes were taken to capture non-verbal cues and contextual information. An average of one to two interviews were conducted per day, as participants were drawn from various communities within the large municipality and were allowed to select interview dates and times that were most convenient for them. The entire data collection process spanned approximately four weeks.

All audio recordings and transcripts were securely stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team. Transcribed data were anonymized by removing any potentially identifying information, and codes were used in place of participants’ identities throughout data analysis and reporting to ensure anonymity.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using qualitative content analysis with an inductive approach. Analysis commenced concurrently with data collection to allow emerging insights to inform subsequent interviews. The analytic process followed systematic steps consistent with content analysis. First, all audio-recorded interviews were listened to repeatedly and transcribed verbatim. Interviews conducted in local languages were translated into English during transcription to preserve meaning and context. Transcripts were read several times to achieve familiarization and gain an in-depth understanding of the data.

In the second stage, open coding was conducted by identifying meaningful units of text that reflected participants’ experiences, challenges, and coping mechanisms. Codes were derived directly from the data rather than from predefined categories, reflecting the inductive nature of the analysis. Field notes taken during interviews were reviewed alongside transcripts to capture non-verbal cues and contextual details and to verify the completeness of the data.

The qualitative data analysis software NVivo (version 14) was used to support data management and organization. Transcripts were imported into NVivo, where coding, sorting, and retrieval of data were facilitated. Similar codes were grouped into categories, which were continuously reviewed and refined to ensure they accurately represented the data.

Trustworthiness was ensured by addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. This was achieved through careful documentation of analytic decisions, cross-checking transcripts against field notes, and ongoing reflexive engagement throughout the analysis process to minimize researcher bias and enhance analytic rigor. Direct quotations from participants were used to illustrate the categories and support the interpretation of findings.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

Over the course of the study, 23 potential participants were contacted by phone. Of these, 19 consented and participated in the interviews reaching saturation, one could not participate due to ill health, and three were no longer residing in the Ho Municipality at the time of recruitment. In terms of age distribution at the time of their stillbirth, almost half (9, 47.4%) of participants were 26–35 years old. The gender distribution shows a predominance of females (15, 78.9%). Most participants identify with Christianity (18, 94.7%), and the majority are married (15, 78.9%). Occupation-wise, over half of the respondents are self-employees (10, 52.6%), while 5, 26.3% are government employees and 4, 21.1% are unemployed. Educational attainment varies, with the largest proportion having reached the tertiary level (7, 36.8%). Akan is the predominant tribe (9, 47.4%), followed by Ewe (7, 36.8%). Regarding the duration since the incidence of stillbirth, the majority of reported cases occurred within the 1–5 years range (13, 68.4%). In terms of the number of stillbirths experienced, most participants (16, 84.2%) had one stillbirth, while three participants (3, 15.8%) had experienced two or more incidents.

Theme 1: pregnancy experiences leading to stillbirth

Within this theme, two distinct subthemes that encapsulate the complexities of these experiences were uncovered. First, pregnant women navigate through a spectrum of health challenges, from pregnancy-induced malaises to unforeseen medical issues, adding layers of uncertainty and vulnerability to their pregnancy journey. The emotional landscape is further shaped by encounters with maltreatment from male spouse, mostly from their families, underscoring the need for compassion and understanding during pregnancy.

Health challenges of the pregnant woman

Theme 3: impacts of stillbirth faced by couples

Delving into the extensive consequences of stillbirth, the fourth main theme reveals the significant impacts of stillbirth on the family. Five distinct subthemes were identified, each highlighting a different dimension of participants’ experiences. These subthemes illustrate the emotional, relational, and practical challenges faced by couples following stillbirth, including changes in family dynamics, interpersonal relationships, social support, and professional life.

Sleeplessness and overthinking

The participant’s account further emphasizes the profound impact of stillbirth on mental and emotional well-being, particularly in terms of sleeplessness and overthinking. The pain of losing a child not only hinders work productivity but also disrupts the ability to find solace in sleep. Discussion

This study provides insight into how stillbirth is experienced within the relational, socio-cultural, and economic context of Ho Municipality. Although women’s pregnancy experiences varied, ranging from uncomplicated gestations to episodes of illness and interpersonal maltreatment, the broader pattern suggests that pregnancy occurs within layered social structures that can either protect or endanger women. Reports of emotional neglect, in-law tensions, and partner violence indicate that some women navigate pregnancy within gendered power dynamics that may heighten vulnerability [17]. While some participants experienced supportive partners and families [18], these protective factors were unevenly distributed, highlighting variability in relational environments.

Participants’ emotional responses to stillbirth were intense and prolonged, including crying, withdrawal, denial, and deep sadness. Crying was nearly universal, particularly in cases where the baby had been deeply desired [19, 20]. Male participants also reported sobbing, challenging societal expectations of stoicism [21, 22]. Denial functioned as a psychological defense against the shock of loss [23] while withdrawal and prolonged mourning reflected the deep emotional toll of stillbirth [3, 19].

A critical finding was the theme of blame directed toward women following stillbirth. Women were often held responsible for the loss, resulting in guilt, anger, and depression [24, 25]. This blame appears rooted in socio-cultural norms that position women as primary custodians of pregnancy outcomes, compounded by limited public understanding of biomedical causes, spiritual interpretations of pregnancy loss, and extended family influence [26–28]. Such dynamics contributed to marital strain, emotional distress, and, in some cases, relocation or divorce. At the same time, some couples reported strengthened bonds following shared loss, indicating that relational outcomes depend on pre-existing communication patterns and mutual support [3, 29].

The economic consequences of stillbirth were also significant, particularly for self-employed women, who reported loss of income due to medical appointments, time away from work, or reduced client engagement [30, 31]. Mental and emotional challenges, such as sleeplessness, persistent overthinking, and reduced productivity, further underscore the psychological impact of stillbirth [31, 32].

Participants employed a range of coping strategies to manage the emotional and social consequences of stillbirth. Faith-based practices, including prayer, attending religious services, and engaging in spiritual rituals, were central to meaning-making [33–35]. Family support, from mothers, partners, and extended relatives, provided both emotional comfort and practical assistance [19, 36, 37]. Professional support, especially compassionate care from nurses, helped participants navigate distress [38, 39]. Participants also used engagement in work or attention to existing children as diversional strategies to redirect thoughts and maintain emotional stability [19, 40].

Implications

This research provides insights into the lived experiences of couples following stillbirth, highlighting the emotional, relational, and social challenges they face. Future research should examine the potential relationship between maltreatment during pregnancy and the occurrence of stillbirth, particularly within socio-cultural contexts where intimate partner violence and other forms of abuse may remain underreported. In addition, further studies should explore the effectiveness of support systems and interventions in helping couples navigate the loss. Advocacy for comprehensive healthcare coverage for mental health support, awareness campaigns to dispel societal misconceptions, and workplace policies that acknowledge the challenges faced after stillbirth are crucial in fostering a supportive environment.

Limitation

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Not all partners in each couple were interviewed, so some aspects of the shared experience may not have been captured. The study also relied on self-reported accounts, which could be influenced by recall bias or social desirability. Nevertheless, the rich narratives offer valuable insights into the lived experiences of couples following stillbirth.

Conclusion

This study provides an in-depth understanding of couples’ lived experiences following stillbirth, highlighting the emotional, relational, and social challenges they face. Participants drew on faith, professional support, and social networks to cope with the loss, while also navigating family dynamics and societal pressures. The findings emphasize the importance of supportive interventions tailored to couples experiencing stillbirth. Advocacy is therefore needed for mental health services, awareness campaigns, and workplace policies that acknowledge and address the challenges associated with stillbirth.
