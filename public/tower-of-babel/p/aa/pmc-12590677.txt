The power of tendon autograft in osteochondral defects: one step closer to hyaline cartilage
Authors: Şafak Aydın Şimşek, Gamze Altun, Tolgahan Cengiz, Şerife Tütüncü, Süleyman Kaplan, Ahmet Pişkin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the role of combined treatment of tendon autograft and microfracture technique in the formation of hyaline cartilage tissue, which has a very limited regeneration capacity, in an experimental osteochondral defect model created in the rabbit knee.Method and materialsForty-three New Zealand rabbit knee joints were used in the study. Rabbits were divided into five groups: Tendon autograft-microfracture, Tendon autograft, Microfracture, Empty defect (Sham) group, and Intact group. After creating a full-thickness cartilage defect in the right knee medial femoral condyle of the rabbits, the treatment methods belonging to the groups were applied. At the end of the six-week follow-up period, macroscopic and microscopic evaluations were made. Regenerative processes were evaluated by means of stereological, histopathological, immunohistochemical, and electron microscopic techniques on the sections obtained from paraffin and resin blocks. Newly formed cartilage and connective tissue volumes were analyzed with volume fraction.ResultsIn rabbits that received a tendon autograft, a smooth surface and a cartilage surface compatible with the surrounding tissue were observed, while a fibrous and defective healing was observed in the microfracture group. In the empty defect (Sham) group, healing was minimal. In immunohistochemical analyses, higher cartilage matrix formation and type II collagen structure were detected in the tendon autograft groups. In scanning electron microscopic evaluation, healthy isogenous groups were observed in the group that received tendon autograft and microfracture treatment.ConclusionThe use of tendon autograft allows chondrocyte adhesion and proliferation as a cytoskeleton, providing a better regeneration more like healthy cartilage tissue.Keywords: Knee, Articular cartilage, Tendon, Osteochondral defect, Autograft, Volume fraction

Introduction

Articular cartilage has a complex structure and function. Disruption of this structure and function leads to osteoarthritic degeneration in synovial joints. The regenerative capacity of cartilage tissue with complex structure and function is limited [26, 34]. In recent years, rapid advances have been made in articular cartilage repair as researchers and practitioners continue to learn more about the biomechanics, physiology, and repair of cartilage.

Current treatment options for symptomatic focal cartilage lesions in the knee joint can be divided into three groups: mesenchymal cell stimulation (microfracture, drilling, and chondroplasty), replacement therapy (osteochondral autograft transfer system, osteochondral allograft), and cell-based biological replacement options (autologous chondrocyte implantation, stem cell therapy, and tissue engineering) [37]. In treating advanced cartilage damage, marginal surgical options such as arthroplasty are applied. Microfracture repair is the most commonly used first-line treatment option for full-thickness cartilage defects due to its minimal invasiveness, technical ease, limited surgical morbidity, and high cost-effectiveness [27, 32]. Osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation is a versatile treatment option for cartilage and osteochondral defect patients. OCA transplantation is a uniquely helpful treatment option for patients with significant, high-grade osteochondral defects and for athletes who need to return to sports as quickly as possible. Early reoperation after OCA transplantation is often standard [7]. While both chondrocytes and mesenchymal stem cells have shown the capacity to generate hyaline-like cartilage in experimental models, clinical outcomes frequently reveal repair tissue composed of a mixture of fibrocartilage and hyaline cartilage [10, 13, 18]. More recent treatment modalities have aimed to provide effective osteochondral repair using tissue engineering and stem cell therapies utilizing biphasic and triphasic scaffolds [45]. Naturally based polymers are intended to create an ideal environment for cell proliferation as they are produced from materials that form typical natural cellular environments [19]. Despite all the methods developed, cartilage tissue healing is often characterized by type I collagen production and fibrocartilaginous tissue formation rather than normal hyaline cartilage development. The avascular nature of cartilage tissue is beyond the reach of reparative growth factors and cells. Therefore, there is no chance of following the normal tissue damage response. Indeed, numerous surgical options have been developed for chondral defects, which is a testament to the difficulty in restoring hyaline cartilage function.

The design of this animal experiment was based on the idea that the autograft made from the patient’s tendon, which is inexpensive and technically easy to perform, would enable the reconstruction of damaged cartilage tissue. Current knowledge also supports the idea that the tendon acts as a scaffold that allows cell adhesion, development, and differentiation in the biological process leading from tendon to cartilage healing [47]. Our method is a new T approach to the treatment of osteochondral defects. Tendon graft can be easily harvested, and when used as an autograft, it can undergo fibrocartilaginous metaplasia under the influence of cyclic compressive loads in weight-bearing areas. Our study offers a promising approach to treating lesions by focusing on the osteochondral unit. This innovative technique minimizes the number of surgical interventions and side effects in treating chondral injuries while being cost-effective and practically applicable. The aim of this study is to induce hyaline-like cartilage formation using tendon autografts in focal cartilage defects.

Materials and methods

Formation of groups

Animal experiments were conducted at Ondokuz Mayıs University Experimental Animals Application and Research Centre. The protocols and experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Ondokuz Mayıs University (HADYEK 2023/04 and application number E-68489742-604.02.03-409384). The study used 43 adult New Zealand rabbits, 16 weeks old and weighing an average of 2000 g. All rabbits were housed in single cages under-ventilated, 22 ± 10 °C, 55–60% humidity conditions, 12 h light/dark cycle, and fed ad libitum with standard rabbit feed and water systems.

A power analysis test using the PASS 15.0.5 program (α = 0.005, Power = 97.04%) determined the number of subjects belonging to the study groups. The rabbits were then randomly divided into four groups of 10 rabbits for each. To compare the healed tissues with healthy cartilage tissue, three rabbits were added to the study as a healthy group, and both knees of these rabbits were included in the study. Of the 40 rabbits, 20 were randomly selected as male and 20 as female, and they were divided into 4 groups with an equal number of males and females. Study groups and treatment applications

Creation of traumatic focal cartilage defect

One day before surgery, the instruments were sterilized in a steam autoclave. The experimental animals were anesthetized with xylazine (Rhompun, Bayer, Istanbul, Turkey at a dose of 8–10 mg/kg) and ketamine (Ketalar® at a dose of 35–50 mg/kg, Eczacıbası, Istanbul, Turkey), and the antibiotic cefazolin (CEZOL 1 g IM/IV Powder for Injection, Deva Holding/ Istanbul, Turkey) was administered 30 min before surgery. The surgical area was shaved and sterilized with 10% povidone-iodine. A 2.5 cm longitudinal incision was made in the right knee. A cylindrical full-thickness chondral defect 3 mm in diameter and 3 mm deep was created in the medial femoral condyle using a drill, and the joint was irrigated with saline (Fig. 1).

Osteochondral defect 3 mm in diameter and 3 mm deep in the medial femoral condyle

Microfracture application

After the osteochondral defect was created in the Groups 1 and 3, a microfracture was performed with the help of a 1 mm Kirschner wire in the defect area to pass the subchondral bone, and the microfracture was confirmed to be at the appropriate depth by observing the bleeding from the bone (Fig. 2).

Microfracture of the subchondral bone with 1 mm Kirschner wire from 2 different points in the osteochondral defect area

Tendon autograft application

After the microfracture procedure was applied to the Group 1 and Group 2, a 10 × 3 mm tendon taken longitudinally from the quadriceps tendon of the same knee joint was folded on itself and filled into the defective area (Fig. 3). The quadriceps tendon was used as the donor site for the tendon to minimise surgical stress and infection risk. No procedure was performed for fixation of the autograft, which was stable in passive flexion and extension.

Group 1 (n:3) (Healthy control group): Represents untreated experimental animals for obtaining baseline values and comparing normal cartilage structure with data obtained between groups. Rabbits in this group were sacrificed for examination of both knees.

Group 2 (n:10) (Microfracture Group): Represents experimental animals in which a microfracture was applied to the subchondral bone at the base of the defect using a 1 mm Kischner wire after creating an osteochondral defect in the medial femoral condyle.

Group 3 (n:10) (Tendon Autograft Group): Represents the group of experimental animals to which a longitudinal excised tendon autograft from the quadriceps tendon was applied to the site where the osteochondral defect was created.

Group 4 (n:10) (Microfracture + Tendon Autograft Group): Represents experimental animals in which a tendon autograft was applied after microfracture was applied to the site where the osteochondral defect was created. This group was created to evaluate whether the microfracture application has an additive effect on the tendon autograft application.

Group 5 (n:10) (Sham Group): Represents experimental animals left to heal spontaneously after the osteochondral defect was created. This group was created to observe the natural progression of non-treated osteochondral defects.

After the surgical procedures, the joint capsule, subcutaneous tissue, and skin were closed retrogradely. After cleaning the wound area with povidone iode solution (Bayem Medical, Batikon, Istanbul, Turkey), the dressing was applied, and dexketoprofen (ARVELES 50 mg/ 2 ml Ampoule Containing Solution for Injection, Menarini, Istanbul, Turkey) was administered intramuscularly at a dose of 1 mg/kg to provide postoperative analgesia. The knee and ankle were flexed 90 degrees, and a long circular cast for the leg was applied with a 2-inch-wide synthetic plaster cast. Antibiotics (CEZOL 1 g IM/IV Powder for Injection, Deva Holding/ Istanbul, Turkey and analgesics (ARVELES 50 mg/ 2 ml Ampoule Containing Solution for Injection, Menarini, Istanbul, Turkey) were administered for 4 days following the surgical procedure. The long leg circular cast was discontinued at the 3rd week, and active knee joint movements were allowed. After six weeks of postoperative follow-up, the experimental animals were necropsied following general anesthesia and sterile field dressing, and the distal femur was excised with the help of a saw. The excision material was dissected from the soft tissues, and a pathology sample was obtained. Sacrificial material obtained from two rabbits from each group was used for electron microscopic analysis. Blocks taken from material obtained from other experimental animals were used for microscopic analyses and immunohistochemical analyses.

Microscopic evaluation and paraffin blocks

The excision material was cleaned from muscle and connective tissues and fixed in a mixture of 4% paraformaldehyde + 4% glutaraldehyde (Merck Millipore, USA) for 15 days. Decalcification was performed with a gradually increasing formic acid solution (Supelco, USA) for 60 days in a controlled manner. The tissues were embedded in paraffin blocks and prepared using 5 μm-thick sections for stereological analyses and 4 μm-thick sections for histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluations according to the systematic random sampling [16]. Sections were cut as a ribbon and floated on a water bath maintained at 45 °C to stretch the paraffin section and kept in an oven at 58 °C to remove paraffin. Stereological sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin, and histopathological sections were stained with Masson trichrome stain [12].

Electron microscopic tissue processing and resin blocks

After 4 times washes with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, tissue samples were treated in 1% osmium tetroxide in the dark for 1.5 h. Following this procedure, tissues washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer were kept in acetone series (50%, 75%, and 95%, respectively) for 15 min for dehydration. Tissues kept in 100% acetone for 40 min were kept in propylene oxide for 20 min twice during the infiltration phase, and then in a mixture of propylene oxide and resin (Araldite) for one hour. Following this, tissues were placed in pure Araldite and kept for 1 h, then embedded in silicone molds. Tissues kept in a desiccator overnight were then polymerized in an oven at a gradually increasing temperature (45–50 to 55–60 °C). Finally, the tissues were kept in the oven at 62–63 °C for 48 h and removed from the plastic molds, and 500 nm thick sections were obtained using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica Microsystems, Inc., Germany). The sections were stained with toluidine blue (1%) and sodium borate (0.2%) solutions, and light microscopic analysis was performed. Resin blocks of tissues were also examined bya scanning electron microscope (JEOL JSM-7001 F).

Stereological evaluation

The systematic random sampling rule, which gives every tissue area an equal chance of being sampled, constitutes one of the basic principles of stereology [16]. Serial sections taken by this principle were stained with Masson trichrome and then covered with entellan. After the infiltration of the entellan into the section was completed, volume estimation were performed on the serial sections using the Cavalieri method [16]. After the completion of entellan infiltration, volume calculations were performed on the sections using the Cavalieri principle (Fig. 4a, b).

For calculations, computer-aided cellSens Entry microscopy software and a light microscope with camera attachment (Olympus, BX43) were used to obtain images at x100 magnification. Images were analyzed with Image J (National Institutes of Health, USA; RRID: SCR_003070) analysis software, and sections were taken at a ratio of 1/60 according to the systematic random sampling rule. The appropriate number of sections, sampling rate, and point area of the point counting test grid were determined by a pilot study. The point area of the grid used for tendon, connective tissue, immature/immature cartilage, and integration areas was 1662.976 μm2, 419.43 μm2 for blood vessel and adipose tissue, and 9698.31 μm2 for total defect area, respectively. The coefficient of error (CE) was kept at less than 5%. Volume estimation using the Cavalieri method was used to calculate the volumes of connective tissue, blood vessels, adipose tissue, integration area, mature and immature cartilage, the volume of tendon graft material, and the total defect volume and total cartilage volume in the tissue. In addition, volume fractions were calculated by proportioning the obtained volume values to the total volume [2].

Immunohistochemical analyses

The presence of type I and type II collagen in 4 μm thick sections taken from paraffin blocks was demonstrated using the streptavidin-biotin-complex method of immunohistochemical methods [40]. Anti-Collagen I (1/200, ab233080, Abcam) and anti-Collagen II (1/200, ab34712,RRID: AB_731688, Abcam, USA) were used as primary antibodies, and HRP/DAB Detection IHC kit (ab64264) was used as the secondary antibody. The sections were deparaffinized and heated in the microwave with Citrate Buffer solution, and endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After washing with phosphate buffer solution (PBS), blocking solution was applied. primary antibodies were applied at a 1:200 dilution and incubated overnight at + 4 °C. After washing, secondary antibody and streptavidin-HRP complex, 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used as chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The preparations were covered with entellan and photographed with a light microscope with camera attachment (Olympus, BX43). Immunohistochemical evaluations were performed by assigning values from 0 to 3 according to no staining (−), weak staining (+), moderate staining (+++), and strong staining (++++) [42].

Statistical analysis

SPSS (v21 IBM Corp, NY, USA, RRID: SCR_002865) software was used to analyze the data. Four experimental groups were formed in this study. The data were divided into two categorical and numerical. Frequency and percentage values were used for categorical data, and mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and interquartile range (IQR) were used for numerical data. It was evaluated whether the data distribution was normal, and non-parametric tests were preferred for the data that did not show normal distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for intergroup comparisons. For results found to be significant, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Dunn–Bonferroni correction. If a significant difference was found in the Kruskal–Wallis test, the Mann–Whitney U Test was applied to compare subgroups. The Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test was used on paired data for comparisons within groups. The Median Test was additionally applied for the comparison of medians. These statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 software. Results were analyzed at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Forty rabbits underwent the planned surgical procedures, and one rabbit was lost during the 6-week follow-up period. At the end of six weeks, forty-two rabbits were sacrificed.

Macroscopic findings

No signs of local infection, such as wound redness, temperature increase, or discharge, were observed in experimental animals. After sacrification in the Group 1 and Group 2, it was observed macroscopically that the tendon autograft adapted to the cartilage surface and formed a smooth surface. In the microfracture group, a rough, defective healing area was observed macroscopically in the defect area. In the sham group, it was observed that the defect area was rough, and the healing tissue in the defect area was less than the microfracture tissue when examined macroscopically after sacrification. To compare the healing tissues of the healthy group animals in histopathological examinations, bilateral knee joints were opened, and distal femur sacrification was performed. The structure of tissue was normal, this tissue in this group was used for evaluation of experimental groups tissues.

Stereological findings

The mean initial weight of forty-two rabbits was 2221 ± 320 g and 2327 ± 499 g at the last weighing after six weeks of follow-up. The mean absolute weight change for six weeks was 285.07 ± 169.65 g, and there was no statistically significant difference in the mean weight and absolute weight change of the rabbits at baseline and the 6th week. There was no statistically significant difference between the mean weights of the animals in the Groups 1, 2, 3, and 4 at baseline and 6 weeks postoperatively (H (3) = 2.239 p = 0.52 and H(3) = 2.86 p = 0.41). Furthermore, the statistical analyses performed in our study revealed that being female or male did not significantly affect the distribution of results within the group (p > 0.05).

Light microscopy findings

Light microscopic evaluation was performed on semi-thin sections obtained from resin blocks and paraffin blocks of all groups.

Tendon-microfracture group (Group 1)

In the Tendon-Microfracture group, mature and immature cartilage, as well as blood vessels and connective tissue infiltrations close to the graft area, were observed in the defect area. Healthy cartilage formation was detected in the connective tissue infiltration areas. The density of blood vessels in the defect area close to the graft area was remarkable. Infiltration areas were observed in the connective tissue around the graft area. It was noticed that the mature and immature cartilage structures observed in the connective tissue infiltration areas were healthy (Figs. 5 and 6).

Tendon group (Group 2)

In the tendon group, dense tendon structure, surrounding blood vessels, and connective tissue infiltration were observed in the graft defect area. Immature cartilage structure covered a larger area than mature cartilage structure, and healthy cartilage tissue formation was detected in regeneration foci. Regeneration foci were detected in the grafted area, and the healthy structure of the newly formed cartilage tissues was observed (Figs. 5 and 6).

Microfracture group (Group 3)

In the microfracture group, connective tissue integrations, immature cartilage formation, and dense adipose tissue were observed in the defect area. In the sections where the density of blood vessels in the defect area attracted attention, it was determined that mature cartilage and cartilage matrix were formed in the areas close to the connective tissue infiltration areas. These regions are thought to be an indicator of cell formation and arrangement, which tends to grow from the outside to the inside. In addition, mature cartilage structures in the defect area were observed close to the connective tissue infiltration area. It was noticed that a cartilage matrix was formed close to the blood vessels (Figs. 5 and 6).

Empty defect group (Group 4)

In the sham group, intense infiltration of adipose tissue, blood vessels, and connective tissue was observed in the defect area, while immature cartilage formation was concentrated around connective tissue and blood vessels. In this group, where connective tissue infiltration was more intense compared to the other groups, mature cartilage structures were also detected in the connective tissue regions. Notably, connective tissue infiltration areas were more intense in this group than in the other defect groups (Figs. 5 and 6).

Intact group (Group 5)

The tissues were examined microscopically to observe the normal morphology of the knee joint of the rabbits in the healthy group. The bone trabeculae in the tissue were observed to have a healthy morphology. When the bone marrow in the subchondral area was examined, the presence and normal morphology of fat cells and hematopoietic cells were observed (Figs. 5 and 6).

Scanning electron microscopic findings

In the tendon autograft + microfracture group (Group 1), the presence of chondrocytes in isogenous groups was observed. It was noticed that they were well organized. It was observed that cartilage regeneration occurred in this group. In addition, the presence of cartilage matrix that preserved its cellular integrity in this group was accepted as evidence of the healing process (Fig. 7a). Discussion

The main aim of treating osteochondral defects is to restore joint integrity by creating a healing tissue with the same or similar biomechanical properties as articular cartilage. If the treatment is unsuccessful, it may lead to the formation of osteoarthritis later. It is difficult to reverse the damage in a joint that has lost its function, so it is important to slow the progression of the disease and improve function in the early stage before advanced joint problems develop.

Mesenchymal cells, multipotent stromal cells, migrate from the gap spaces within loose connective tissue. These cells then condense and differentiate into chondroblasts, synthesizing an extracellular matrix rich in collagen and proteoglycans. This matrix fills the gaps and forms the basis for cartilage development. The process continues as these cells transform into chondrocytes, entering the matrix they secrete and settling into the newly formed cartilage [9, 30]. These specialized regions, therefore, play an important role during embryonic development, where the connective tissue regions act as scaffolds for future cartilaginous structures. The interstitial growth of cartilage also plays a role here because it allows the cartilage to expand through the matrix [14]. It is especially important in areas such as joints, where cartilage must fill certain gaps to ensure proper articulation. In our study, the regions where we calculated the volume as integration areas are reserve areas for this specialized chondrogenesis. When the histological images of our study are examined, it is thought that these regions, which were observed mostly in connective tissue in the microfracture and empty defect groups, are thought to be caused by the tendency of cell arrangement and formation to grow from the outside to the inside.

The microfracture group showed the highest volume and volume ratio when the integration area volumes were analyzed. Since p = 0.05 in terms of integration area volume ratio, it can be accepted that there is a statistically borderline significant difference between the groups. This may be interpreted as a more intense connective tissue-induced chondrogenesis stimulation in the microfracture group. New experimental studies in which the healing process is examined over longer periods can be designed, and more precise results can be obtained regarding microfracture application and tendon autograft treatment for mature cartilage formation in the future.

Tendons can respond to mechanical forces by changing their structure, composition, and mechanical properties, a process called mechanical adaptation of the tissue [44, 46]. With the recent biologic development of molecular techniques, mechanical stress sensing, and signal transduction mechanisms have been gradually elucidated with the identification of the mechanosensor in tendon cells and the master regulator in tendon development. Cells in the tendon are responsible for adaptive changes. They can alter gene expression, protein synthesis, and cell phenotype in response to mechanical loads, and the extracellular matrix acts as a scaffold, allowing cell adhesion, cell development, and differentiation [31]. In rabbit meniscal reconstruction models using semitendinosus tendon autografts, histological and biomechanical analyses at 12–24 weeks demonstrated fibrocartilaginous differentiation, proteogylican deposition, and type II collagen expression, with morphological and functional properties approaching those of native meniscus; importantly, these grafts also showed protective effects against articular cartilage degeneration [23]. Similarly, the combination of tendon autografts with bone marrow aspirate has been reported to enhance defect filling and to attenuate degenerative changes on the articular surface [17]. These findings suggest that tendon autografts not only provide mechanical support but also possess the biological potential to undergo fibrocartilaginous metaplasia and contribute to chondral protection or regeneration under appropriate conditions. In our study, tendon tissue provided a very good adaptation to the articular surface macroscopically in both groups. Compared to the other two groups, the tendon was applied and served as a scaffold without filling defects. In addition, the histopathological evaluation of the preparations in the tendon microfracture group and tendon group showed dense blood vessels and immature cartilage around the tendon autograft areas, suggesting that the tendon autograft may be a cytoskeleton suitable for cell adhesion and proliferation that provides tissue reconstruction for chondrocytes. It is also noteworthy that a nonvascularized tissue shows intense vascularisation around the autograft despite using a nonvascularized autograft.

The differentiation of tendon-derived cells (TDCs) into hyaline-like cartilage tissue occurs through the interaction of numerous molecular signalling pathways. This process enables tendon tissue to have potential for cartilage repair. Furthermore, tendon and cartilage share overlapping developmental pathways. For example, TGF-beta signaling is critical for recruiting mesenchymal stem cells from surrounding tissues, including cartilage, to generate tendon progenitor cells. Once formed, tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) differentiate in response to mechanical stimulation and signals from neighboring tissues such as muscle and cartilage. TGF-beta and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMP) play a critical role in the chondrogenic differentiation of tendon-derived cells. These factors promote SOX-9 activation and COL2A1 expression, thereby increasing the synthesis of cartilage matrix components [24]. This suggests a relationship between tendon development and surrounding cartilage structures, with each tissue influencing the development of the other during early embryogenesis [4, 8]. In the experimental design of our study, tendon tissue was used as a scaffold. Regarding the volume ratio of newly formed immature cartilage, the volume ratio of immature cartilage was lower in the tendon group than in the microfracture group, and a statistically significant difference was observed (U = −11.74, p = 0.03 for group 2 and group 3). The difference between the combined tendon and microfracture group and the microfracture group was borderline significant (p = 0.05 for the tendon microfracture group and the microfracture group). There was a statistically significant difference between groups in the ratio of total cartilage volume to total volume (p = 0.03). However, in the subgroup comparisons, only a borderline significant difference was found between the tendon group and microfracture group (p = 0.05). In this case, although the total cartilage volume ratios in the four experimental groups were approximately similar, the lower immature cartilage volume ratio in the tendon only and the tendon + microfracture groups, and the statistically significant difference, especially in the tendon only group, can be interpreted as the tendon scaffold induces more mature cartilage development. This may be a promising result, suggesting that tendon tissue could be a valuable tissue source for inducing cartilage development, not only in terms of stem cell reserve but also as a cytoskeleton alone.

Thanks to its elastic structure, it can easily adapt to the joint surface, and if it can form a load-bearing surface against compressive loads, the patients’ complaints may disappear, and degeneration can be stopped by preserving joint functions. Considering the mechanical and biochemical properties of the tendon, it raises the question of whether it can be effective in treating osteochondral defects.

A study performed by Turhan et al. in 1999 inspired the hypothesis of our study. An osteochondral defect was created in the medial femoral condyle of the canine knee and fixed to the defect area with a femoral tunnel formed using the tendon of the extensor digitalis lateralis muscle taken from the same lower extremity [43]. They reported that after 24 weeks, this surgical procedure in ten canine knees, the defect area filled by the tendon was shiny and smooth, and the tendon was intact but distinguishable from articular cartilage. However, in that experiment biomechanical tests, electron microscopic and immunohistochemical analysis were not performed, and the characteristics of the new tissue were not evaluated. This study conducted with experimental animals reports that the tendon autograft macroscopically adapts well to the defect area, but beyond this, it does not provide sufficient data on the characteristics of the resulting cartilage tissue. The clinical study by Turhan et al. in 2021, in which tendon autograft was used to treat osteochondral defects, attracted attention. In this human study, 22 knee joints were studied, debridement was performed until the bleeding bone tissue reached the osteochondral defect area, and then microfracture was performed. Tendon autograft from the plantaris longus tendon was fixed to the defect area and the patients were retrospectively evaluated after a minimum follow-up of two years. The defect area was evaluated functionally and radiologically by Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS: Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score). Intraoperative images of a patient with stage 4 osteochondral lesion who underwent secondary arthroscopy at a one-year follow-up showed that the defect area was filled with well-integrated, smooth-surfaced, and stable cartilage [41]. However, no control group or comparison group was used in the study. Clinically planned studies do not ethically allow histopathological examination of the healing tissue. Although existing studies have emphasized the successful results of tendon autograft application, macroscopic evaluation of the healing tissue reveals the need for clearer evidence on the clinical applicability of the study.

Microfracture and debridement account for more than 90% of procedures due to their low cost, easy applicability, and are currently the first-line restorative treatment for full-thickness chondral defects less than two or three cm2 [29]. Microfracture is still the first-line treatment option for knee joint cartilage defects. However, there is evidence that the results of microfracture deteriorate over time and may be detrimental to the results of cell-based therapies. In their systematic review on microfracture technique, Goyal et al. reported that the use of microfracture for the treatment of small lesions in patients with low postoperative expectations resulted in good clinical results in short-term follow-up, but five years after surgery, treatment failure can be expected regardless of lesion size [15]. A systematic review of 28 studies by Mithoefer et al. also showed that revision surgery was frequently required between two and five years after microfracture (23–31%) [28]. Follow-up of 110 patients for 10–14 years after microfracture revealed that 46% of patients had poor outcomes and required arthroplasty [36]. In our study, in the macroscopic evaluation, although the microfracture group filled the defect area better than the empty defect group during the same treatment period, it was found that there was an obvious filling defect compared to the tendon groups. When the fat tissue volumes were analyzed, it was found that more fat tissue was formed in the empty defect and microfracture groups compared to the other groups. There was a statistically significant difference in terms of fat tissue volume between groups (p = 0.01). In subgroup comparisons, it was found that this high in the microfracture only group had a statistically significant difference with the tendon and tendon + microfracture group (p = 0.03). This indicates that microfracture application stimulates adipose-derived stem cell reserve and stimulates chondrogenesis by increasing adipose tissue volume intensively in parallel. Histopathological data also show a consistency with this result. The regenerative immature cartilage tissue formed by microfracture application shows that there is a mesenchymal tissue-derived chondrogenesis stimulation, especially around the integration areas and contains dense adipose tissue. In addition, when immunohistochemical analyses were evaluated in terms of type I collagen antibody, intense type I collagen staining was found in the regenerative cartilage developed after microfracture application.

In current therapeutic approaches, parameters such as incomplete tissue remodeling, scaffold degradation, and insufficient cellular infiltration prevent the formation of strong interfaces between the repair site and the surrounding healthy tissue [21]. This is particularly important in classical bone marrow stimulation techniques and autologous osteochondral grafting, where deterioration in outcomes is observed two years post-treatment, especially in young patients. A meta-analysis reported in 2021 demonstrated that the autologous matrix-induced chondrogenesis (AMIC) procedure for focal chondral defects in the knee generally performed better over approximately 3 years of follow-up [25]. Additionally, forming fibrous or fibrocartilage tissue instead of hyaline-like cartilage prevents achieving successful outcomes. In our study, macroscopic evaluation revealed that, in the groups treated with tendon autograft tissue, the defect area was filled without deterioration and conformed to the articular surface at the six-week. Tendon autograft application creates a compatible area on the articular surface, influenced by an active joint range of motion and mechanical loads. Moreover, a low immature cartilage volume ratio in the tendon-treated groups not only shows a statistically significant difference but also support by a reduced formation of immature cartilage tissue in terms of immunohistochemical analyses. A literature review by Jessop et al., demonstrated that immunohistochemical examination shows intense staining of type II collagen in immature cartilage, whereas, in mature cartilage, it exhibits a layered, non-homogeneous distribution [20]. The presence of maturation and immature cartilage at different healing times can be examined in more detail.

The histological demonstration of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and type II collagen indicates that they are produced as distinctive extracellular matrix (ECM) components of hyaline cartilage. The observation of minimal levels of type I collagen compared to type II collagen suggests that the matrix reflects the structure of hyaline cartilage rather than fibrocartilage [39]. In our study, a very weak staining of type I collagen compared to type II collagen in the Tendon + Microfracture group further supports the idea that combining the microfracture method with tendon autograft can stimulate hyaline-like cartilage formation.

Stereological methods use two-dimensional (2D) images to provide information about the volume, surface area, length, and number of structural components in three-dimensional (3D) structures. It is crucial to note that unbiased stereological methods are validated through mathematical proofs [5]. Therefore, these methods are appropriate for the accurate and unbiased histological evaluation of cartilage repairment. The Cavalieri method is a stereological volume estimation technique in which the volume of any object can be calculated if the surface area and thickness of parallel sections obtained from the object are known. This method can be used to assess the amount of tissue filling a defect and quantify tissue composition ratios. Moreover, it can be similarly applied to images obtained from other imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging [11]. Therefore, we preferred the Cavalieri method to assess the regenerative cartilage tissue. This technique was chosen to determine the individual volumes of newly formed structural components within the regenerative tissue and the ratio of each component to the total volume.

Type II collagen, which may emerge in the subjects, is the primary collagen type of hyaline cartilage, responsible for the stability of healthy articular cartilage and regulating cellular biological functions [1]. To assess the type of collagen present in the regenerative cartilage tissue, immunohistochemical analyses were included in the methodology, allowing for the evaluation of the quality of the newly formed cartilage. Additionally, the reliability of the results was enhanced by comparing the volume ratios obtained from the estimated volumes of tendon graft tissue, connective tissue, blood vessels, adipose tissue, integration zones, and immature and mature cartilage tissues relative to the total defect volume. Beyond the volumetric assessments of immature and mature cartilage tissues, the presence of type II collagen-containing cartilage formation areas, observed in both the tendon-only and tendon combined with microfracture groups through immunohistochemical analysis, strongly indicates that tendon application promotes the formation of hyaline-like cartilage.

Finally, including both male and female animals in experimental studies is crucial for several reasons: They may exhibit significant physiological and hormonal differences that can influence their responses to treatments or environmental changes [3]. Including both gender ensures the research reflects a broader biological spectrum, leading to more accurate and generalizable findings. It also aids in developing effective treatments for both gender and prevents biased data that may arise from studying only a single gender [33]. For this purpose, the rabbits used in the experiment were randomly selected to include gender. Statistical analyses revealed that being male or female did not significantly affect the distribution of outcomes within the groups. An equal number of male and female rabbits were used in the experimental groups, and no statistically significant differences were observed in the comparison of variables based on sex within the groups (p >0.05).

When scanning electron microscopy images were examined, the inducing effect of different treatment approaches on cartilage regeneration was revealed. It can be considered that tendon graft provides a suitable environment for the formation of the cytoskeleton. Specifically, the high rate of regenerative response is highlighted by the appearance of highly organized isogenous groups in microfracture treatment in conjunction with tendon autograft. Microfracture application may have made an important contribution to the healing process in terms of directing stem cells. The response in the tissue in the empty defect group led to an inorganized morphology. This situation shows that the healing in this group was low.

Tendon autograft application is a cost-effective, technically feasible, and reproducible treatment method for osteochondral lesion management, regardless of lesion size, as it reduces the number of surgical interventions and the associated surgical complications. Furthermore, autologous graft eliminates disadvantages commonly associated with allografts, such as limited graft availability, immunogenicity, graft rejection, and the risk of disease transmission (e.g., HIV and hepatitis). A comparative analysis of the qualitative and quantitative properties of the repair tissue presented with a robust methodology stands out as a strength of this study compared with similar studies in the literature [41, 43]. Additionally, early active joint range of motion was allowed during the postoperative period, and early-phase assessment of the repair tissue was conducted, aligning with treatment protocols commonly followed in humans. During the 6-week postoperative follow-up, the tendon autograft was observed to have adapted to the defect area, forming a stable, smooth surface. We propose that during the biological process leading to cartilage regeneration, the tendon functions as a scaffold, supporting cell adhesion, development, and differentiation. Future studies could focus on combining tendon autografts as biological scaffolds with cell-based therapies to achieve repair tissue most closely resembling native hyaline cartilage.

In clinical practice, tendons such as the quadriceps tendon, peroneus longus, semitendinosus or gracilis can be used as donors for tendon autografts. The use of hamstring tendons in postoperative knee flexion may be associated with complications such as flexor strength loss, particularly at high angles, and semitendinosus/gracilis muscle atrophy [22]. Although complications are very rare with the use of the peroneus longus tendon, there have been reports of decreased ankle range of motion and lateral ankle sensory loss [48]. It has been reported that when the quadriceps tendon is harvested using minimally invasive techniques, fewer anterior knee problems are seen [38]. Furthermore, after tendon autograft use, problems such as paraesthesia, haematoma, infection, and rarely acute compartment syndrome may be seen at the donor site incision site [6]. The use of minimally invasive techniques will minimise donor site problems. Furthermore, in clinical practice, the size of the focal cartilage defect may limit the effectiveness of tendon autograft application. Future studies evaluating both the size of the osteochondral defect area and the effectiveness of tendon autograft application will reveal the circumstances under which this method may be more effective.

Nevertheless, certain limitations of our study should be acknowledged. The study was conducted on cartilage from experimental animals. However, biologically, rabbit cartilage shares characteristics like human cartilage. Rabbits were chosen due to their high chondrocyte content, which facilitates faster biological healing, and the sufficient size of the knee joint, allowing for the practical application of the surgical technique. Unlike the chronic nature of osteoarthritis and osteochondral defects, a traumatic focal cartilage injury was induced in this model study. However, considering previous similar studies [35], a comparable cartilage defect model was created using the same method, making this limitation negligible. Furthermore, although the number of experimental animals was determined through a power analysis conducted at the beginning of the study, potential issues such as tissue loss and folding during histological tissue tracking procedures would be considered as a limitation of this study. To mitigate the effects of such issues, stereological analysis with blinded manner was performed, including both volume measurements and volume ratios for comparative analysis between groups. Additionally, the immunhistochemical evaluation was performed using semi-quantitavie scoring. Although this approach allowed comparative assessment, it does not provide a precise quantitavie measurement of collagen expression. Further studies should apply standardized quantitavie image analysis methods to strengthen reproducibity and accuracy. Furthermore, a 6-week follow-up period is too short to capture full maturation, long-term stability, or remodeling of the regenerated cartilage. Future studies with longer follow-up intervals are needed to confirm the durability of the findings. Another limitation of our study is the absence of a biomechanical evaluation. Comparing hyaline-like cartilage tissue with healthy cartilage tissue biomechanically in future studies will further strengthen the results of tendon autograft application.

Conclusion

The presented study investigated the efficacy of tendon autograft application in the treatment of cartilage defects in an experimental osteochondral defect model in the rabbits. Macroscopic, histopathological, and immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated the success of tendon autograft application in promoting the formation of hyaline cartilage-like regenerative cartilage. Notably, in groups treated with tendon autografts, healing more closely resembling the original cartilage tissue was observed, with better histological compatibility with the surrounding healthy cartilage tissue. While the microfracture technique showed positive results in the short-term healing process, it appeared more limited compared to tendon autograft application in the long term due to the lower quality of the regenerative cartilage tissue. Furthermore, the use of a larger number of animals and longer follow-up periods in experimental groups could provide more definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of the tendon autograft method. These findings offer valuable insights for clinicians seeking alternative approaches for the treatment of osteochondral lesions and suggest that tendon autografts could be a practical, reproducible, and cost-effective treatment option.
