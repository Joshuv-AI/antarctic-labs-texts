Simulated evaluation of large language model stepwise diagnostic reasoning with real-world chest pain encounters and Bayesian networks
Authors: Conrad W Safranek, Vimig Socrates, Donald Wright, Thomas Huang, Alaa Alashi, Kent McCann, R Andrew Taylor, David Chartash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReal-world evaluation of large language models (LLMs) as clinical diagnostic aids is limited by the reliance on static vignettes and retrospective data, which inadequately reflect the dynamic, iterative nature of clinical decision-making and may overestimate LLMs’ performance. A Bayesian network (BN) trained on the structured cohort data imputed clinical data not collected in the original encounter to create a more robust simulation environment. The BN furthermore enabled derivation of mutual-information-optimal query pathways. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have been increasingly explored as diagnostic decision-support tools, yet most evaluations have relied on static clinical vignettes lacking the complexity of real clinical scenarios, potentially inflating performance estimates [1–3]. While clinical case challenges and medical licensing exams test general medical knowledge, they do not represent real-world disease prevalence and, furthermore, fail to reflect the dynamic, iterative process of clinical decision-making, which involves gathering, synthesizing, and re-evaluating diverse data to reach evidence-based diagnoses and treatments. Simulated evaluation of LLM stepwise data acquisition and diagnostic reasoning using real-world clinical data remains challenging due to selection biases and missing clinical information inherent in retrospective datasets, which only reflect data collected during the encounter rather than the full spectrum of potentially obtainable information [4].

Bayesian networks (BNs) provide a principled solution to these challenges by explicitly modeling the conditional probabilistic relationships central to clinical reasoning and enabling robust data imputation [5]. BNs have been widely adopted in healthcare research to represent the sequential decision making process in which clinicians iteratively choose the next informational query that is expected to maximally reduce diagnostic uncertainty [6, 7]. Yet, to our knowledge, no prior sequential-query evaluations of LLMs using real-world electronic health record (EHR) data have used BNs or similar probabilistic methods to impute unrecorded labs, vitals, or imaging results; instead, existing studies (e.g. Hager et al., 2024) simply assume normal findings or return ‘Unavailable’ when the model requests data not present in the chart [8–10]. BN-based data imputation offers the opportunity for more robust simulation by exposing an LLM to a statistically plausible data landscape. Furthermore, by quantifying how clinical data reduces uncertainty through mutual information (MI) principles, BNs can explicitly define optimal diagnostic query pathways and thereby offer a normative, interpretable reference standard against which the sequence and content of an LLM’s information requests can be objectively benchmarked.

Building on this probabilistic framework, we focus on non-traumatic chest pain—a common but diagnostically challenging emergency department (ED) presentation in which clinicians must rapidly identify life-threatening conditions such as acute coronary syndromes (ACS) while avoiding unnecessary testing and over-treatment of benign causes [11, 12]. In this study, we evaluate how a state-of-the-art black-box LLM, GPT-4o, performs in simulated open-ended diagnostic scenarios for ED patients presenting with chest pain. We leverage a validated BN trained on structured ED clinical data as a normative probabilistic benchmark of rational-choice diagnostic information seeking, comparing GPT-4o’s decision-making to that of both the original treating physicians and the BN-derived optimal diagnostic pathways. By incorporating empirical clinical data, including probabilistically imputed missing data, we create a richer, dynamic evaluation framework compared to static retrospective analyses. Through this, we aim to highlight both the strengths and limitations of LLMs for diagnostic support.

Methods

In this section, we outline the evaluation framework developed to assess GPT-4o’s (model version 2024–08-06; OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) diagnostic capabilities regarding chest pain assessment in the ED (overview in Fig. 1). This research was deemed exempt under 45 CFR 46.104 (4) by the Human Research Protection Program of Yale University under Human Investigation Committee #2000035077; informed consent to participate was waived. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations.

Overview of methods workflow: (1) curate a real-world ED chest-pain cohort; (2) train a Bayesian network (BN) on structured variables to enable imputation of data not collected in the original encounter as well as to determine mutual-information-optimal diagnostic pathways; (3) simulate GPT-4o diagnostic conversations with stepwise clinical reasoning and data queries; and (4) evaluate GPT-4o’s diagnostic decision making patterns against the BN optimal pathways and against physicians

Data and preprocessing

Our cohort included all adult ED encounters with a chief complaint of chest pain across one suburban and two urban EDs within the Yale New Haven Health System from January 2014 to April 2022. We identified 7 emergent diagnoses of interest based on clinical guidelines (ACS, aortic dissection, esophageal rupture, pericarditis, pneumonia, pneumothorax, or pulmonary embolism), and encounters were classified accordingly—or as Other Diagnosis for all remaining encounters (of note, per standard ED operations, all encounters had an associated diagnostic code for billing purposes, regardless of whether the patient was diagnosed with emergent pathology).

We preprocessed clinical data, including demographics (age, sex, race, and smoking status), past medical history, Emergency Severity Index (ESI), vitals, laboratory results, and imaging findings. All demographic variables, except age, were treated as categorical. International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9th/10th Revision diagnostic codes from patients’ past medical history were mapped to the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Clinical Classification Software (AHRQ) categories [13], with the top 15% most frequent conditions retained and verified for quality by a board-certified ED physician. To reduce data sparsity, we included only lab values recorded in 1% of encounters, retaining the first measured value per patient. Given its diagnostic relevance, Troponin was further processed to include both the initial value and the between the first two measurements when available. The first recorded vital signs (pulse, peripheral capillary oxygen saturation, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature) and oxygen therapy status were also included. Continuous variables were binned into five equal-width intervals, except for Troponin values, which were categorized using clinically validated reference ranges [14] to improve interpretability and diagnostic relevance. The cohort was split into randomized, stratified training (95%) and test (5%) sets. After preprocessing, the dataset contained 136 variables (Supplementary Table S1). Notably, history of present illness and physical exam findings were not available in the structured dataset and thus were excluded from model inputs; this limits comparisons to physician behavior, as both the LLM and BN lacked access to these key diagnostic elements that often guide early clinical reasoning.

Emergent pathology classification of imaging consults

To enable integration of imaging data into the BN, free-text radiology reports were classified, indicating the presence/absence of an emergent finding. This approach allowed us to focus on clinically significant outcomes while reducing data dimensionality. We extracted all available radiology reports, de-identified them using a custom pipeline, and categorized imaging orders by modality, resulting in seven imaging categories (Supplementary Table S1). Due to computational constraints, a sample of 66,205 reports (14.74% of total consults) was used for classification.

A black-box LLM (GPT-4o-mini, version 2024–07-18; OpenAI, USA) was used to extract emergent findings. Despite slightly lower accuracy relative to GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini maintained strong overall performance on this classification task and was selected for cost efficiency. Each imaging modality was assigned one of four outcomes: No test ordered; Test ordered, unknown result; Test ordered, no emergent finding; or Test ordered, emergent finding. Classification performance is reported in the Appendix (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Derivation of the Bayesian network

The BN was trained on the training set using the Algorithm for the Reconstruction of Accurate Cellular Networks (ARACNE) [15], a constraint-based method that accommodates missing data by leveraging clinical context (e.g., absence of an ordered test may reflect deliberate clinical judgment). ARACNE estimates pairwise MI between variables, retaining edges based on statistical thresholds and refining structure via Data Processing Inequality [16] to reduce spurious correlations. This produced an undirected graph of variable relationships. To validate network structure, we sampled random walks covering 50% of nodes. Two board-certified ED physicians independently reviewed each of these BN-derived paths for clinical plausibility; disagreements were adjudicated by a third board-certified ED physician. We recorded the proportion of paths judged clinically plausible and assessed inter-rater reliability using raw percent agreement and Gwet’s AC1 statistic [17]. BN directionality was assigned using the dot algorithm [18]. Conditional probabilities between connected nodes were then trained using the maximum likelihood estimator.

To derive a normative information-seeking benchmark, we generated a mutual-information-optimal query sequence from the trained BN: at each step, the yet-unobserved variable with the highest conditional MI with the diagnosis node was greedily selected, iteratively reducing diagnostic uncertainty [19]. Because early diagnostic choices carry disproportionate clinical weight, this MI-driven ordering foregrounds the highest-value initial queries and forms the deterministic “optimal” pathway, against which LLM request patterns were later compared. Of note, we did not use the BN’s final diagnosis classification as ground truth; the BN was used only for probabilistic imputation and to derive a MI-optimal query ordering. Consequently, we retained empirical class prevalences and did not apply class rebalancing or cost-sensitive training, which would distort the joint distribution used for imputation and MI-based ordering.

We report the classification performance of the BN for the seven emergent diagnoses; given the imbalanced nature of rare emergent pathologies in our data set, we also calculated F1-scores and a weighted macro-averaged F1-score.

LLM diagnostic conversations

As shown in Fig. 1, after training the BN, the LLM was evaluated in a simulated diagnostic setting using a convenience sample of 500 chest pain cases. Each evaluation began with a brief patient introduction encompassing typical triage-level details (chief complaint, age, sex, and ESI level). The LLM was then prompted, per prior protocol [8], to request information from the provided list of possible data elements (full prompt in Supplementary Fig. S2). This prompting included explicitly instructing the LLM to clinically reason at each step prior to requesting the next piece of data. Requested structured data with units was provided if available; otherwise, the BN inferred the most likely outcome. If a radiology consult had been ordered but the report was unavailable, the LLM was informed that the test was believed by the physician to be indicated and had been performed but the result was unknown. Once the LLM determined it had sufficient information, it selected a diagnosis from the 7 life-threatening conditions or Other Diagnosis. To mirror the average number of information requests in real-world encounters, the LLM was required to assign a diagnosis if it had not yet done so after 39 turns.

In addition to the primary evaluation, we examined the impact of disease prevalence and prompt context on diagnostic performance. Initial testing showed that the LLM overdiagnosed rare life-threatening conditions, so a new set of prompt testing was performed with prevalence data provided in an attempt to better align LLM predictions with realistic probabilities. Additionally, to reduce potential priming bias, we tested a neutral prompt without diagnostic categories. For this prompt, each final response was mapped to one of the 7 diagnoses (or Other Diagnosis) to enable consistent comparisons.

Performance across prompting strategies was assessed using confusion matrices and comparisons to the original clinical diagnosis. Sensitivity and specificity confidence intervals were computed using the Wilson score method for binomial proportions. We then compared LLM request sequences to the MI-optimal BN pathways using rank-biased overlap (RBO), a top-weighted similarity metric for ranked lists that tolerates different lengths/incompleteness and ranges from 0 to 1 (1 = identical ordering) [20]. RBO was computed with the Python rbo library (RankingSimilarity.rbo_ext, default parameters) on the baseline (diagnosis-list) prompt, and we report mean RBO by diagnosis with 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals obtained by resampling encounters within each diagnosis stratum. Additionally, we counted LLM information-request turns per encounter to determine average step counts by prompting regime. We furthermore analyzed the total number of information elements by type (e.g., vitals vs. labs) requested by the LLM versus those requested by physicians.

Emergent pathologies, as previously defined, accounted for 2.14% of cases, while 97.86% had non-emergent or alternative diagnoses. 95% of this cohort was used to train the BN.

Regarding BN structure validation, the two ED physicians agreed on 60/70 paths (85.7% raw agreement), with inter-rater reliability of Gwet’s AC1 = 0.82, indicating high agreement [17]. After adjudication by a third ED physician, 65/70 paths (92.9%; 95% confidence interval 84.3–96.9, Wilson score method) were judged clinically plausible, supporting the face validity of the learned BN structure.

We first present the diagnostic classification accuracy of the trained BN across the test set via confusion matrices (Supplementary Fig. S3). In general, the likelihood of emergent pathologies across the overall cohort was low, from 0.01% for esophageal rupture to 1.03% for ACS. The BN had a significant number of false positives in the three most frequent conditions (ACS, pulmonary embolism, and pneumonia), leading to poor F1-scores (range: ; full data in Supplementary Table S3). However, recall was the highest of all diagnoses for ACS (0.714). Notably, the BN produced zero true-positive predictions for aortic dissection, esophageal rupture, and pericarditis. The weighted macro-averaged F1-score across all conditions was 0.046. The support-weighted average recall, a more critical metric for identifying relevant diagnostic tests for life-threatening “can’t-miss” conditions, achieved a modestly higher performance of 0.475.

We next compare the performance of the three prompting strategies on the evaluation set of 500 patients: the baseline prompt including a finite set of diagnoses, versus including disease prevalences for emergent conditions in the overall cohort or removing the categorical restrictions on diagnoses of interest (Table 2; Supplementary Fig. S4). Baseline prompt (with diagnostic categories) achieved the highest sensitivity (79.3%, 95% confidence interval [CI] 73.4–84.1) with low specificity (45.2%, 95% CI 39.5–51.1), reflecting frequent over-calling of emergent conditions. Adding prevalence information markedly increased specificity (83.0%) with a tradeoff in sensitivity (30.4%, 95% CI 24.7–36.8). Removing diagnostic category constraints further increased specificity (94.7%, 95% CI 91.4–96.8) with minimal sensitivity (8.8%, 95% CI 5.7–13.3). We additionally represent the results as differences between the confusion matrices as a percent of the total cases (Fig. 2). Relative to the baseline prompt, adding prevalence cues reduced false-positive emergent calls among Other Diagnosis cases by up to 11% (median −1.4%; one cell showed a + 0.4% increase) and increased true-negative Other Diagnosis classifications by 21%; this improvement came at the expense of sensitivity, which fell most for ACS (−7%), with only minor shifts (−0.6% to +0.4%) across the other emergent diagnoses. These trends were similar but amplified when prompting without providing diagnostic category constraints: relative to baseline, false-positive emergent calls were reduced by up to 13% (range 0.4–13% decrease; median 1.8%), true-negative Other Diagnosis classifications increased by 28%, and sensitivity decreased by 0.0 to a max of 10% for ACS.

Given GPT-4o’s low overlap with the MI-optimal sequence, this comparison provides additional context for understanding how the LLM’s information-seeking behavior may align with or diverge from physician decision-making, particularly in the types of data prioritized during the original encounter. Across all LLM prompting strategies, physicians requested significantly more laboratory (>10-fold) and vital sign (>2-fold) data relative to GPT-4o. In contrast, GPT-4o requested 30%+ more imaging studies. Discussion

Real-world evaluation of LLMs as diagnostic tools has largely relied on static vignettes and simplified retrospective tasks that poorly capture the dynamic, iterative nature of clinical reasoning. This study evaluated a black-box LLM (GPT-4o) in a stepwise diagnostic task for ED chest pain using retrospective EHR data, augmenting missing data via BN imputation and benchmarking against clinicians and mutual-information-optimal query paths. Three key findings emerged. First, the LLM systematically over-predicted rare life-threatening etiologies such as aortic dissection and pericarditis; prompting with explicit prevalence cues tempered but did not eliminate this bias. Second, the model’s information-seeking diverged markedly from physicians, with more imaging requests and substantially fewer vitals and laboratory studies. Third, these behavioral differences contributed to minimal overlap with the BN-defined MI-optimal request sequences (RBO 0.10), suggesting misalignment between LLM-driven and probabilistic models of rational-choice diagnostic processes.

Our study extends prior work on stepwise LLM diagnostic reasoning with real-world EHR data. In the only other published work on this topic to our knowledge, Hager et al. converted MIMIC-IV encounters with confirmed acute abdominal disease into interactive diagnostic dialogues and showed that LLMs struggle with incremental reasoning [8]. In contrast, our cohort is defined by the common ED chief complaint of chest pain—rather than confirmed acute pathology—and thus spans both emergent (e.g., pneumothorax) and benign conditions (e.g., musculoskeletal pain). This heterogeneity presents a more authentic calibration challenge, exposing the LLM’s tendency to over-select “zebra” diagnoses even when true prevalence information was provided. Such miscalibration, likely rooted in skewed representation in LLMs’ training data, may distort diagnostic accuracy and drive unnecessary testing, consultations, and downstream utilization.

We also address a methodological limitation of prior stepwise evaluations, including Hager et al. and preprint efforts by Schmidgall et al. and Sun et al., that return a within-normal-limits reference value or an “Unavailable” token whenever a requested lab, vital, or image is absent [8–10]. This practice can inflate apparent LLM performance by oversimplifying real-world variability or introducing data leakage, where the absence of an order implicitly reveals clinician reasoning. In our framework, the BN generates statistically plausible, context-conditioned values for data not collected in the original encounter, yielding a fuller and more realistic data landscape. The relatively low diagnostic accuracy we observed may partly reflect this closer adherence to real-world complexity. Imputation-based approaches of this type may be useful as a methodological standard for future simulated LLM diagnostic tasks.

BN modeling also enabled determination of MI-optimal query trajectories to benchmark the LLM’s stepwise reasoning, revealing low diagnostic sequence overlap. This misalignment was reflected in information-seeking behavior: the LLM requested more imaging, while physicians relied more on labs and vitals. A possible explanation is that in the ED, labs and vitals are often obtained in panels or continuous monitoring, whereas the LLM treated them as separate requests, inflating its apparent request count. Furthermore, although we prompted the LLM to reason “as a clinician would,” we did not explicitly instruct it to weigh test cost, workflow burden, or potential harms (e.g., radiation), which may have further tilted its requests toward imaging. These are reminders of how small methodological differences in LLM pipeline integration or prompting can have significant impact on outcomes, reinforcing previous studies’ findings [21, 22]. Because ED chest-pain evaluations account for an estimated $10B in annual U.S. spending, and imaging strategy strongly affects costs and downstream utilization, any model-induced shift toward greater imaging could carry meaningful cost implications [23].

Several complementary techniques developed in synthetic or sandbox settings could improve accuracy but have not yet been tested in stepwise trials using real EHR data. Beyond the chain-of-thought prompting already implemented in this study and in Hager et al., examples include uncertainty-aware multi-agent frameworks that encourage abstention and additional data requests when confidence is low [24], retrieval-augmented generation that injects guideline snippets when relevant [25], self-play reinforcement fine-tuning in which a clinician-LLM repeatedly trains against a simulated patient with automated critique [1], and probabilistic scaffolds in which a hidden BN steadies LLM recommendations [26, 27]. Evaluating these strategies in real-world EHR-based tasks represents an important next step to determine whether the gains seen in synthetic settings translate to real-world clinical data.

This study has several significant limitations. First, both the BN and LLM lacked history of present illness and physical-examination findings, which are critical in chest-pain evaluation. While this omission does not affect the comparison between the LLM and BN, it limits the validity of comparisons to physicians, who rely heavily on these elements. Second, despite expert review confirming that the learned BN structure generated largely plausible diagnostic paths, its baseline classification performance was poor, likely reflecting extreme class imbalance: each emergent pathology had prevalence 1%, and for the three rarest diagnoses () the BN performed no better than a constant ‘all-negative’ model. The BN was designed to model diagnostic pathways rather than maximize predictive accuracy (e.g., via class rebalancing or cost-sensitive approaches), which may have limited its performance, and extreme class imbalance further complicates interpretation of clinical validity. Despite these limitations, our findings highlight significant deviations between the LLM’s diagnostic approach and the BN’s optimal pathway, as well as notable changes in LLM diagnostic predictions secondary to prompting modification.

Although the configuration studied here is not yet suitable for clinical deployment, future similar tools could be integrated into EHR workflows as clinician-facing decision support—for example, prompting high-yield queries or expanding differentials. The miscalibration, suboptimal information-seeking, and imaging overuse we observed underscore the need for safeguards such as probabilistic calibration and real-time clinician oversight. Any deployment will also require prospective validation with live clinician feedback. More broadly, our findings suggest that current diagnostic LLMs remain highly sensitive to prompt context and struggle with real-world clinical probabilities, supporting a cautious stance toward autonomous or patient-facing diagnostic use. With better calibration and tighter integration into probabilistic frameworks, LLMs could better support diagnostic reasoning. We hope this work informs efforts to develop more clinically aligned AI tools.
