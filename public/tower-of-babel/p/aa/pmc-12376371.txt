General practitioners’ experience about synchronous teleconsultation in primary care: a mixed-method systematic review
Authors: Alphonse Sowanou, Gabin F Morillon, Marlène Guillon, Thomas G Poder, Maude Laberge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral practitioners (GPs) extensively used synchronous teleconsultation (STC) during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although this utilization decreased after the state of sanitary emergency was lifted, it remains higher than pre-pandemic levels.MethodsThe aim is to summarize the scientific evidence on the factors influencing GPs’ decision to conduct STC instead of face-to-face consultation. We conducted a systematic review and reported our results following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guideline. We searched four electronic databases: MEDLINE, Cumulative Index of Nursing Literature and allied health, Web of Science and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and conducted the last search on March 23, 2023. Two independent reviewers selected English/French articles reporting on GPs’ use, attitude, satisfaction, and experience with STC. We assessed the studies’ quality using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, and a narrative approach was performed to synthesize findings.ResultsThe screening of 9,288 references resulted in the inclusion of 34 studies, for a total of 5,563 participants. Results show that GPs’ decision to use STC is influenced by six categories of factors: (1) consultation, such as consultation purpose (e.g., follow-up care, administrative requests); (2) information and communication technology, such as quality of equipment and bandwidth (internet connection and the effectiveness of the hardware and software); (3) GP, such as convenience (the flexibility offered by the STC); (4) patient, such as access barriers (ex., physical, geographical, financial); (5) GP-patient relationship, such as ease of diagnosis; and (6) the institution, such as organizational and peer support. Lack of a reliable internet connection, need for physical exams, and limited visual cues were the main barriers to using STC.ConclusionsGPs’ utilization of STC depends on the interplay of six categories of factors, the most important being the purpose of consultation. Equity in access and a fair payment model are key elements to consider in designing health policies aimed at supporting adoption and appropriate use of STC.Review’s Protocol registrationPROSPERO CRD42024505744Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-02975-y.Keywords: Teleconsultation, Telemedicine, Online visit, Teleconsultation’s utilization, General practitioners, Video consultation, Phone consultation

Background

Synchronous teleconsultation (STC) via video or phone has become a common practice in primary care; the COVID-19 having largely contributed to its rapid adoption [1]. Global use rose from 57 million users in 2019 to over 116 million in 2024 [2]. STC is now commonly used to deliver routine primary care across many countries [3, 4].

STC may improve health system performance by enhancing quality dimensions such as timeliness, patient-centeredness, efficiency, safety, and equity [5]. It has been linked to reduced diagnostic and treatment delays in primary care [6, 7]. It also helps overcome barriers related to geography [8–10], mobility issues [8, 10], and cost [11]. One systematic review reported that STC in primary care reduced the rates of face-to-face consultations and hospitalizations in secondary care, as well as the time spent by patients including travel, wait, and consultation time [5]. Furthermore, it enhances safety during epidemics [12] and supports patient-centered care by aligning with digital preferences [7], and can be considered more convenient [5, 7]. However, its effectiveness may be limited by technological issues (e.g., poor internet connection) [13], lack of in-person contact [14], and providers’ limited digital literacy [7]. Further, a systematic review reported mixed results regarding equity, which varies across studies and depends on socioeconomic status, residence, and ethnicity, which are likely influenced by contextual factors [5]. These challenges highlight the complexity of STC’s adoption.

Although STC’s utilization by general practitioners (GPs) declined after the state of sanitary emergency related to COVID-19 was lifted, it remains higher than pre-pandemic levels [15]. For instance, in a study conducted in California, United States of America (USA), STC accounted for 68% of consultations during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 [16]. The pandemic was seen as a “window of opportunity” [17] for long-term adoption of STC. Yet, use among GPs decreased. It dropped from 59% in 2020 to 32% in 2023 in a large USA plan with 12.7 million members that had an integrated care model with a STC option for more than a decade [15]. Similarly, declines were observed in Denmark, from 2.9% in 2020 to 1.2% in 2022 and in the United Kingdom (UK), from 2.6% in 2021 to 2.2% in 2022 [18]. Overall, GPs embraced STC during the pandemic, but usage decreased as COVID-19 related restrictions were lifted in most countries.

One reason for this decline may be the lack of understanding of the factors influencing GPs’ use of STC. According to Roger’s theory of diffusion of innovation [19], the complexity associated with the adoption of a health innovation is often driven by an interplay of factors such as aspects of the technology, providers’ interests, the decision-making process, and the social context factors.

Previous reviews investigated GPs’ satisfaction [20], experience [4, 14], and barriers and facilitators to the implementation of STC [13, 21], but few have focused on why GPs choose STC over face-to-face consultation. For instance, Lindenfeld et al. [13] reviewed barriers and facilitators broadly related to telemedicine, without insight on the individual reasons for adoption of STC by GPs.

Therefore, this review aims to summarize the scientific evidence on factors that influence GPs’ choice of STC over face-to-face consultation, to document the reasons driving GPs’ utilization. The research questions are: what are the determinants of GPs’ use, satisfaction, attitude, and experience with STC? What factors influence GPs’ preferences for STC modalities over the face-to-face modality?

Method

Design

We conducted a systematic review since it enables a comprehensive search for studies focusing on a specific and well-defined topic (in our case, GPs’ experience of STC modalities) [22], as well as the systematic evaluation of the quality of studies according to precise and pre-established criteria [23]. It is the most suitable review type to synthesize the evidence on current practices [24]. We registered the review protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), after validating the search strategy (available at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42024505744). We adopted the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline for reporting the results [25].

We used the PICOS framework, namely Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design as recommended [26–28]. We included studies from any country that are written in either English or French. Table 1 shows the criteria utilized and the rationale behind each of them.

Definitions

We adopted the World Health Organization’s definition of STC, which defines it as a remote consultation between a patient and a healthcare provider by using information communication and technology such as telephone (PC) and videocommunication (VC) platforms [31]. We used Olesen et al. [32]’ definition of GP as a front-line physician providing prevention, diagnosis, and follow-up care to the population. Preference is defined as the order in which a person ranks options according to their respective value when seeking for the “optimal choice”. Preferences are assessments of values in connection with rationality in practice [33]. GPs’ experience can be defined as what happened during the provision of care. The quality of a physician’s experience can affect patients’ outcomes and their satisfaction [34]. Satisfaction is the feeling (good or bad) related to GPs’ condition, including their workload and revenue. GPs’ satisfaction is important because it has been associated with the quality of care provided, and with patient satisfaction [35]. Use and intention to use refers to current service/technology utilization and future willingness to use it, offering insights into adoption patterns. GPs’ attitudes involve their belief system, which influences their medical decision-making process [36]. According to Ward et al. [37], GPs’ attitude can be a significant factor in the acceptance and efficiency of use of information technology in practice.

Data sources and search strategy

We designed the search strategy in collaboration with a librarian specialized in health sciences literature reviews. We searched four electronic databases: MEDLINE via PubMed, Cumulative Index of Nursing Literature and allied health (CINAHL) via EBSCO, Web of Science and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, from 1990 to March 23, 2023. Relevant keywords and associated truncations used in the searches were linked to the following concepts: teleconsultation, GP, experience, satisfaction, use, preference, and attitude [38]. The Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were used to refine or extend search parameters, quotations marks (“”) to obtain results with exact expressions, and parentheses to group search terms. One member (GFM) of the research team revised and integrated comments through an iterative process to develop a final version of the search strategy that was approved by all investigators. The search strategy developed for each database is in Appendix 1.

Studies selection and data extraction

We used the review management tool Covidence® for each stage of the selection process. Six reviewers (AS, GFM, STM, MG, ML and TGP) conducted each step independently, with each reference being examined by at least two reviewers. To ensure coherence among reviewers, a standardization session was held at each new stage with all the team members. Meetings were organized with the three team leaders (MG, TGP and ML) to resolve conflicts at every selection stage. Two systematic reviews were planned and conducted together, with separation at the last stage. One review was on GPs’ utilization and the other on patients’ utilization of STC. The remaining number of references after excluding duplicates was exported into Covidence®. On these references, we conducted the first selection (on title and abstract), which led to identifying references for full text review. We assessed each article on the eligibility criteria to determine its inclusion (or rejection) in the GPs’ utilization review [27], (see PRISMA diagram in Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram with searches completed up to March 23, 2023

We developed a data extraction grid in an Excel sheet (Appendix 2) following the PRISMA framework [26], that was validated during a team meeting. The extraction grid included the following information: name of the first author, year of publication, country where the study was conducted, target population, theoretical framework used if applicable, study design, type of sampling, number of participants retained/considered for the analyses, STC modality, results, the corresponding defined outcome, the approach used, the type of analyses carried out, the dependent and the explanatory variables (for the quantitative analyses), the guide used for reporting (if applicable), the number of attributes (themes and subthemes identified). For quantitative results, we extracted the direction of the relationship between the defined outcomes and the determinants of STC, along with the level of statistical significance (p <.05). For qualitative results, we extracted relevant quotations describing the relationship between the defined outcomes and the determinants of STC. Two reviewers (AS and GFM) independently extracted the data and double-checked each other’s extraction.

Studies quality assessment

Two reviewers (AS and GFM) independently evaluated the methodological rigor of the included articles, applying the 2018 version of the mixed methods appraisal tool (MMAT) developed by Hong et al. [39], and disagreements between the reviewers were resolved by consensus. This tool was designed for systematic reviews including qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods studies; thus, allowing the application of a single tool for all included studies [39]. The MMAT tool allow to assess each design of study (qualitative study, randomized controlled trial, nonrandomized study, quantitative descriptive study, mixed-method study) on five criteria [39]. For each criterion, a choice between three options is possible (Yes, No or Can’t tell) [39]. The ‘Can’t tell’ response means that the article does not report appropriate information to answer ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ [39]. We did not calculate an overall score based on the ratings of each criterion or exclude studies with low methodological quality, as recommended by the authors of the tool [39]. We used this assessment to interpret the data in the synthesis.

Data analysis and synthesis

Compared to a quantitative synthesis, the qualitative synthesis allowed us to summarize data into themes [40], resulting in the most appropriate output to answer our research question. Textual narrative synthesis has proven useful for summarising evidence from various types of studies, including qualitative, quantitative research, which is relevant to our mixed-study review [41].

Initially, a single reviewer (AS) coded the extracted data using content analysis under the direct supervision of a senior researcher (ML). Quantitative data (e.g., means, percentages, estimates) were first interpreted to derive their textual meaning, upon which codes were developed, while qualitative content was directly coded. These codes were then organized into relevant themes, according to barriers to and facilitators of STC use by GPs. Several rounds of discussion among co-authors were conducted to validate identified themes and define relevant categories, ensuring both validity and reliability. Finally, we mapped the identified themes onto the predefined categories (Appendix 3). We interpreted and reported only the most relevant themes [27, 40], referred to as factors, to address our initial research question.

Results

Study selection

After deletion of duplicates, the initial search yielded 9,288 references. After the selection process against our criteria, 34 articles were included (see the PRISMA flowchart in Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The 34 included articles comprised a total of 5,563 participants (GPs). Sample sizes ranged from 4 GPs [42], to 1,605 GPs [10], who experienced at least VC or PC.

The publication year ranged from 1992 to 2023. Eleven studies (11/34, 32.35%) were conducted before 2019 [3, 6, 43–51]. A total of 19 studies (19/34, 55.88%) were conducted during the COVID-19 period (2020–2021) [8–11, 52–66]. Four studies (4/34, 11.76%) published in 2020 [67], 2021 [42], and 2022 [68, 69] did not report their study period.

Almost one-third (11/34, 32.35%) of the included studies were conducted in the UK [6, 9, 44, 46–50, 52, 53, 58] whereas four (4/34, 11.76%) were conducted in the USA [51, 57, 65, 66]; three (3/34, 9%) were conducted in Norway [42, 60, 63]; two (2/34, 6%) each were conducted in Canada [8, 68], and Denmark [54, 64]. The remaining studies were conducted in European [43, 45, 56, 62, 67, 69], Oceania [3], Asian [59], and Middle Eastern [61] countries. Moreover, there were three (3/34) studies involving GPs from multiple countries [10, 11, 55].

In terms of methodology of the included studies, 19 were of qualitative design [9–11, 43–45, 47, 49, 50, 52–55, 58, 59, 63–65, 68], 8 of mixed-method design [3, 6, 8, 42, 46, 57, 62, 67] and 7 of quantitative design [48, 51, 56, 60, 61, 66, 69]. Outcomes measurement

As mentioned in the methods section, four main outcomes were used: experience, satisfaction, use or intention to use, and attitudes. Seven studies used reliable and valid measurement tools to assess the defined outcomes, while the remaining studies did not provide relevant information. Among the eight studies, two used the Technology acceptance model (TAM) to assess GPs’ experience [65] and GPs’ acceptance of STC [69]. To assess attitudes, Atherton et al. [44] used Weiss’ theory-based evaluation approach framework, Fernandes et al. [55] used the Lau et al. (2016) framework and Hasani et al. [59] used the Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA). To assess GPs’ use or intention to use STC, Jiwa et al. [3] applied the Theory of Planned Behavior framework and Silver et al. [66] the Plan-Do-Study-Act framework.

In total, 44% (15/34) of studies reported on attitude [3, 6, 8, 11, 43–47, 50, 55, 56, 59, 68, 69] and experience [9, 10, 42, 52–54, 57, 58, 60–65, 67], while 6% (2/34) of studies reported on use/intention to use [49, 66] and satisfaction [48, 51].

Quality assessment of the included studies

In total, we evaluated 26 of the 34 included studies (76.47%) as containing qualitative data and 8 studies (23.53%) as containing quantitative data. Overall, most studies (24/34; 70.59%) met at least 80% of the quality criteria and can be considered of high methodological quality. However, some concerns were noted in the evaluation of 10 studies, raising doubts about the reliability of their results. The detailed quality assessment of the included studies is provided in Appendix 4.

Among the qualitative studies, 20 out of 26 (77%) met at least four out of five (4/5) evaluation criteria, including the criterion related to coherence between qualitative data sources, data collection, analysis, and interpretation [8–11, 43, 45–47, 49, 50, 52, 54, 55, 58, 59, 62–65, 68]. The remaining six studies lacked such coherence and met only 3/5 [6, 44, 53], 2/5 [57], or 1/5 [67] of the quality criteria, raising concerns about the reliability of their results. Among the quantitative studies, 4 out of 8 (50%) met at least four-fifths of the evaluation criteria, including the criterion concerning the appropriateness of measurement, defined as having a clear origin, known validity, or using a standard instrument [3, 48, 61, 69]. In contrast, the studies by Johnsen et al. [60] and Silver et al. [66] met 3/5 criteria but lacked sample representativeness; the study by Stahl et al. [51], also meeting 3/5 criteria, lacked blinding of the assessor, and the study by Florea et al. [56], which met only 1/5 criteria, lacked appropriate measurement tools among others.

To prevent the key findings from being disproportionately influenced by lower-quality studies, we critically evaluated whether major themes (factors) were predominantly identified in high-quality studies. Our analysis confirmed that the most frequently cited factors were predominantly supported by high-quality studies (see Appendix 3). Nevertheless, the code “ethnicity”, which contributed to the development of the patient sociodemographic factor, emerged solely from the study by Atherton et al. [44]. Additionally, the code “lack of staff training”, part of the organizational and peer support factor, emerged from two studies, one of which was Atherton et al.‘s study [44].

We did not conduct a methodological quality assessment for mixed-method studies because the identified mixed-method studies, as noted by the original author, lacked a component that integrated both quantitative and qualitative results. These studies reported either qualitative [6, 8, 42, 46, 57, 62, 67] or quantitative [3] results.

Factors influencing GPs’ utilization of synchronous teleconsultation modalities

Twenty factors emerged from the 34 articles, reflecting GPs’ perspectives on factors that influence their choice of STC over face-to-face consultation. We grouped these factors into six categories of factors: (1) category of consultation, (2) category of Information and Communication Technology (ICT), (3) category of GP, (4) category of patient, (5) category of GP-patient relationship and (6) category of the institution (the clinic). Figure 2 illustrates the overall relationship between the categories, based on the number of citations from the included studies. The organization of the categories, particularly their order from left to right, reflects the frequency of citation, in terms of the number of studies. The greater the height and width of a category, the more frequently it was mentioned by many studies. A list of the main factors is provided with details in Table 3.

Category of consultation-related factors

We identified two mains factors that support the importance of factors related to consultation in the use of STC.

The first factor is consultation purpose, which is referred to as the aim of the consultation. In total, 19 articles (56%) reported results on the fact that STC’s utilization depends on the reasons for consulting, namely a physical or a mental health issue, a known health problem, an administrative request, etc. GPs reported in 11 studies that STC was suitable for conducting follow-up care [8, 11, 42, 43, 49, 50, 54, 59, 60, 63, 65]. In one cross-sectional study, 855 GPs assessed 1,921 VCs, which were follow-ups of previous problems. VC was viewed as either superior or equivalent to face-to-face consultation, in more than 60% of the visits [60]. GPs also deemed STC suitable for simpler and more straightforward issues, including administrative requests like for a sick leave or a medical certificate, in five studies [9, 10, 42, 62, 63]. For example, a GP reported that “Much sick leave follow-up that does not require a physical examination is much better on video. It saves a lot of time for both doctor and patient” [63]. Moreover, four studies reported that STC would be best suited for triage, so to deciding which patient requires face-to-face consultation [10, 47, 63, 67]. Besides, it is mentioned in several studies that VC was appropriate for dermatological conditions [6, 9, 45, 50, 54] and PC was appropriate for both renewing prescription [8, 10, 43, 59, 62] and discussing or requesting test results [8, 47, 49, 66].

The second factor, consultation quality, describes how timely and appropriate STC is for a given medical condition, leading to a better outcome. It occurred in 68% (23/34) of the studies. GPs expressed their concerns about the potential risk of misdiagnosis due to their inability to perform a physical exam on patients, in 14 studies [3, 8–11, 42, 43, 46, 49, 50, 53, 55, 57, 68]. They reported that, for some pathologies, it is harder to establish a diagnosis without a physical exam, making STC difficult. For example, new musculoskeletal cases and pregnancy require face-to-face consultation, as do some acute mental health and pain cases, particularly for new patients [11, 49, 57]. Further, a GP stated about VC: “I suppose common day to day, you can’t put a stethoscope down a camera, so if you can’t do that, then that should generally rule out anything that you would generally be using that for” [43]. Five studies reported that GPs were afraid of not detecting signs of a serious illness due to the lack of non-verbal communication (e.g., active listening, checking patient’s presence, picking up signals, etc.), which mostly exist in PC [6, 8, 45, 49, 58]. VC offers some advantages as reported by Mckinstry et al. [49] and Koch et al. [67], GPs pointed out benefits such as “Visual contact’’. In addition, six studies [8, 11, 54, 55, 59, 63] noticed the effectiveness of STC to ensure safety and continuity of care in the context of contagious infection and epidemic contexts.

Category of ICT-related factors

We identified two factors within ICT in the use of STC.

The first factor is the quality of equipment and bandwidth, which refers to the efficiency of the internet connection and the effectiveness of the hard and soft materials to enable high-quality image, voice, and video transmission, from both GPs and patients’ sides. It was reported in 35% (12/34) of the studies. Technological issues such as insufficient bandwidth (e.g., lack of good internet connection) were discussed in 11 studies [6, 8, 9, 11, 42–44, 54, 55, 57, 65]. Lack of good internet connection was a serious obstacle to using VC [43, 44]. In this sense, GPs reported practical issues, such as the reliance on appropriate internet provision and broadband reliability [6]. Additionally, five studies discussed the quality of technological devices and the availability of appropriate equipment [8, 11, 42, 44, 68]. As reported by one GP, “the quality of the video camera and the sharpness of the image, if you want to observe, for example, a rash or swelling, it requires the patient being able to get the camera angled in the right place and that the light and the distance to what we want to see is okay (…); It is also preferable to use a Wi-Fi connection. A 3G network pixelates it and makes it blurry. It also means that some contrasts and sharpness are lost, and then you cannot really use it” [54]. Technical issues occurred most of the time with VC leading GPs to drop to PC. GPs stated that PC is more intuitive, quicker, and reliable [9].

The second factor, STC’s risks, pertains to the risks associated with the practice of STC, which was mentioned in 38% (13/34) of the studies. Studies noted privacy (e.g., patient conducting a STC in inappropriate place such as grocery) and confidentiality issues during the GP-patient’ encounters in 12 studies [6, 11, 43, 45, 47, 54, 55, 59, 62, 63, 68]. Listeners (i.e., family members) on the patient’s side can disturb or keep an eye on VC with or without making their presence known to the GP. For example, ‘‘other people in the room disturb, want to participate in the conversation, or have their own consultation (referring to family members who also have questions). It can also be challenging regarding confidentiality if patients conduct video from wherever…! (in the) home situation, it can be difficult to tell personal things - for example when a doctor wants to ask about sexual abuse, violence, and previous trauma experiences in relation to issues the patient raises (…). You also cannot know whether the patient’s communication with the doctor is monitored by relatives and thus controlled’’ [63]. Security was another particular concern in four studies [43, 45, 50, 55], for which GPs expressed the need for General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance. Thus, a GP stated: “I think that if there was some platform within our software package that’s GDPR compliant where we can view that patient in a clinical setting with good background information and experienced clinicians on the far side” [43]. Additionally, physicians thought that STC could raise medicolegal issues [6, 9, 55, 64]. Their concern was whether seeing a patient through STC would be acknowledged as “a proper basis for diagnosing and initiating treatment.” As commented by a GP, ‘‘You’re behind on points. If something goes wrong.then, naturally, there’s a big difference in whether you have seen the patient or not seen the patient from a medico-legal point of view’’ [64].

Category of GP-related factors

This category discusses four factors that highlight the significance of physicians’ factors in the use of STC.

The first factor is GPs’ sociodemographic, which refers to characteristics related to age, sex, ethnicity, education and income. It appeared in 35% (12/34) of the studies. One study reported on the importance of age, as younger physicians were more confident with STC and found it helpful [9]. Another study found that working from home through VC improved the quality of life of older physicians in an epidemic context [11]. Among the reviewed studies, eight identified additional training (complementary knowledge required to be skilled in digital literacy) as an important element for conducting successful STC [9–11, 43–45, 50, 59]. As a GP stated, “There is nothing related to the low quality of care, but as I said, we need staff who are confident to reach to the diagnosis by history and without examination” [50]. Another important sociodemographic characteristic reported in seven studies, is ‘’income’’, which refers to the amount of revenue that STC can generate for GPs [10, 11, 42, 45, 55, 56, 67]. In a study, GPs agreed that paying for STC that are comparable to face-to-face consultation is crucial to encouraging higher utilization [10].

The second factor is ability, which should be understood as GPs’ competency to use STC. Several studies (4/34) identified GPs’ ability to conduct STC, as a prerequisite to overcome uncertainty. In other words, GPs with extensive STC experience and technical skills are more likely to use it [11, 55, 63, 69]. Furthermore, a study demonstrated that the more autonomous GPs feel, the more they feel the need to utilize STC [69].

The third factor is GPs’ attitudes toward STC, which is their personal perceptions and preferences regarding VC and PC. Eight studies discussed GPs’ attitudes toward STC [9, 11, 48, 50, 53, 54, 63, 67]. In one study, GPs primarily compared VC to PC and stated that VC provided a welcomed opportunity to gain a complete overview of patients’ health status including facial expression and level of symptom severity [11, 63]. They also found positive aspects of VC that included patients’ comfort as well as GPs’ possibility to observe patients’ home environments [11]. However, one study found that GPs were highly satisfied with both PC (85%) and face-to-face consultation (87%) [48].

The fourth factor is convenience. It describes the flexibility of STC to provide options that fit in well with the needs of GPs in terms of workload management, working date, working time, working place, etc. It occurred in 29% (10/34) of the studies. Six studies identified flexibility as an important determinant of STC [10, 11, 57, 63, 67, 68]. The major benefits reported were the flexibility to work remotely in a location of GPs’ choice, as well as having more control over their schedule. As expressed by a GP: ‘‘Video consultation give more flexibility for me as a physician, flexibility to work from home and flexibility regarding working time’’ [67]. Five studies described STC as a mean to facilitate access to care for patients [47, 50, 52, 55, 58]. A GP said that VC: ‘‘not only improves access for patients but also for doctors. I mean, it’d be much easier for me to go to a patient’s home via the computer than to physically drive there’’ [50]. Another study quotes a GP: ‘‘STC (telemedicine) is far more time-efficient (GP2) than full days of face-to-face appointments.’’ [58].

Category of GP-patient relationship

This category describes the role of the GP-patient relationship in building trust, ensuring continuity of care, as well as facilitating the diagnosis process. It covers three factors.

The first factor is building trust, referring to how STC contributes to maintaining or strengthening the relationship. Four studies mentioned GPs’ challenges in establishing trust with new patients, including missing human contact and non-verbal cues that are essential to building a therapeutic relationship, particularly through PC, when compared to face-to-face appointments [47, 49, 63, 68]. VC was perceived as a strength to improve the relationship between GP and patient as they see each other, compared to PC, in three studies [11, 50, 68]. As commented by a GP, ‘‘I suppose just being able to see your doctor could go a long way to improving the doctor-patient relationship.‘’ [50]. Overall, VC appears to be superior to PC in terms of communication and relationship building.

The second factor is continuity of care, which is concerned with the provision of quality care over time. It was mentioned in four studies [10, 47, 54, 55], in which GPs recognized the effectiveness of STC to ensure the continuity of care in contagious disease or epidemic contexts.

The third factor is the ease of diagnosis, which demonstrates how familiarity based on the existing relationship between the GPs and patients enables GPs to readily make a diagnosis. It occurred in 29% (10/34) of the studies. Ten studies reported that GPs were able to understand and diagnose patients’ issues through STC, even by PC, when it is a known patient with an established relationship [9, 42, 46, 47, 49, 54, 55, 60, 62, 63]. This is supported by the following quote: ‘‘Yes, we are risk takers. We rely on the fact that we can pick up the underlying message behind what somebody is saying, often you will be picking up unconsciously different resonances from what the patient is telling you, and of course if you know the patient you are adjusted to that anyway. You do use it, you can tell from somebody’s voice if they are depressed’’ [49]. In one study, when GPs knew their patients through prior established relationships, they deemed VC to have “greater” or “same” suitability compared to face-to-face consultation in 57% of cases; a percentage that dropped to 32% when the patients were ‘‘unknown’’ [60].

Category of patient-related factors

This category describes and provides important elements to consider when GPs determine which patients are most likely to be suitable for a STC. There are five factors to be considered.

The first factor is patients’ sociodemographic, which is related to the social and demographic characteristics; and reported in nine articles. Eight studies reported that STC use was associated with patients’ age. According to GPs, conducting STC, particularly PC, with elderly patients leads to suboptimal patient-GP interaction, as it consumes time due to the probability of communication barriers, because they are not familiar with the use of STC [46, 53, 54, 59]. Meanwhile, younger patients seem to be best suited to STC because they react well [42, 55, 58]. Another patients’ characteristic mentioned was ethnicity. A study reported that PC was best suited with native English-speaking patients (this to highlight the shared common language, as the study was conducted in the UK) [44]. A GP commented about PC: ‘‘I do notice that generally the patients that are born and raised in the UK, you can process their problems quicker’’ [44].

The second factor is barriers to access. This refers to any patients’ situation that hinders access to healthcare services. In four studies, GPs associated STC’s use with patients facing diverse difficulties to access care [8–11]. GPs highlighted that STC improved equity in access to care for patients who live in remote areas, patients with mobility issues, or patients experiencing financial barriers, who would have faced serious challenges if they were to use face-to-face consultation due to their situations [8–11].

The third factor is ability, understood as patients’ competency to use STC; and it occurred in 26% (9/34) of the studies. GPs reported in six studies that patients’ ability to communicate through digital devices was a reason to use STC. They felt more confident in conducting VC when the patient was knowledgeable and competent to use technology [44, 46, 50, 63, 68]. Meanwhile, if a patient is unable to use technology and has low digital literacy, GPs prefer a PC [11]. In addition, for issues concerning children, GPs viewed parents’ good communication and verbal skills, along with the ability to accurately describe symptoms, as essential for a PC [6, 46, 49].

The fourth factor is patients’ attitude toward STC according to GPs, which is referred to as patients’ personal perceptions and preferences regarding VC and PC. It appeared in seven studies. Greenhalgh et al. [9] and Hallam et al. [47] reported that, for GPs, some patients were unwilling to try VC simply because they did not wish to appear on video. A GP commented, “I offer it (VC) to most people with mental health conditions, and most of them say, “I’d rather just do the phone.” [9]. On the contrary, some patients preferred VC, despite the face-to-face consultation modality (option) [42, 68]. Finally, according to GPs, a group of patients preferred only face-to-face consultations, perceiving STC to be somehow ‘‘substandard’’ to maximize their patient-physician relationship [10, 50, 58].

The fifth factor is convenience and describes the flexibility of STC to provide an option that fits with the needs of patients in terms of place, date, time of appointment, etc., according to GPs. It occurred in six studies. In these six studies, GPs describe the convenience role of STC for patients. In this sense, McKinstry and al. [49] reported that, according to GPs, PC was used by patients to maintain long-distance relationships and receive advice from them (e.g., when patients are working away or on holiday) or to verify whether a visit to their office was necessary. Another advantage perceived by GPs for patients was saving on time and on parking and transit costs, as patients don’t need to travel to and from their appointment place or take much time off work [9, 11, 65]. GPs also believe that STC improves timeliness and decreases waiting time in clinic for patients [10]. Furthermore, GPs believe that VC provides comfort to patients because they are in their own environment while talking on the screen [55].

Category of institutional factors

This category describes four factors to be considered within one clinic to facilitate the adoption of STC, from GPs’ perspective.

The first factor is organizational and peer support, which refers to professional assistance (e.g., GPs sharing their experience and providing feedback on STC with their colleagues) and institutional assistance (e.g., training provided by the clinic). In five studies, GPs noted that the adoption of STC requires a supportive organizational culture, a preexisting teleworking culture, and a perceived need for adaptation [9, 10, 54, 55, 59]. Moreover, GPs reported in seven studies that feeling supported and being able to discuss difficult STC with colleagues within the same health center were key elements to using STC [9, 10, 46, 54, 55, 58, 59].

The second factor pertains to guidelines related to the protocols and regulations regarding the practice of STC. Ten studies reported the critical need to establishing protocols and guidelines for STC practice within each clinic, and the lack of guidelines and protocols constitute barrier to STC’s adoption [10, 42, 43, 45, 47, 55, 58, 59, 62, 68]. This involves strong organizational commitment, government policies for STC’s practice, constant updates, and training activities to keep GPs on track to provide quality care.

The third factor is the workload and demand, related to how the growing demand of care influences STC’s adoption. This occurred in six studies. An increase in healthcare demand was the reason for introducing STC in many clinics, as reported in six studies [6, 8, 44, 45, 49, 50]. It is reported that the recognition that reception staff and phone lines were overwhelmed by patients demands and that to manage that demand and improve efficiency, STC was a good avenue [44]. The decision to adopt STC does not depend only on GPs; it also depends on the organization within one clinic as to whether it perceives STC as an alternative that could offer flexibility to both staff and patients. For GPs, the main motivation for introducing alternatives (STC) to face-to-face consultations was to help them manage their workload, as it helps them decide when and in what order to see a patient [44].

The fourth factor is financial support, meaning the funding required to implement STC in clinics, which was reported in six studies. In these studies, the offer of financial support and the availability of funding for staff training such as, GPs receiving funding from research institutions, facilitated the adoption of STC in several clinics [6, 10, 43–45, 59]. GPs described barriers to adoption as issues related to finances, including a general lack of investment and, more specifically, lack of funding allocated to human resources and supporting the costs of systems set-up and maintenance [10].

Beyond clinical conditions that were generally suitable for STC (both VC and PC), there were cases where GPs explicitly differentiated VC from PC. These situations involved various influencing factors, which are summarized in Table 4 below.

Discussion

Using a systematic review, we identified 34 studies from which, we extracted data representing 5,563 GPs’ perspectives. Using these data, we analyzed and reported studies’ findings, based on the narrative synthesis approach, to answer our research question: what factors influence GPs’ choice of STC modalities (VC and PC) over face-to-face consultation. We found that GPs’ attitude toward, experience with, satisfaction with, and intention to use STC modalities is influenced by an interplay of multiple factors that we grouped into six categories of factors, including consultation, ICT, GP, patient, GP-patient relationship, and the institution (the clinic). For the sake of simplification, we preferred to employ the term ‘‘use of STC’’.

Implications for policy and practice

In this section, we will discuss each of these categories along with their implications.

The first category of factors pertains to consultation. Our review reveals that GPs’ decision to use STC is influenced by the purpose of consultation, such as follow-up care or administrative requests, and the ability of STC to meet the quality required for such care. These findings are in alignment with previous reviews on STC’s use by GPs [13, 14]. This suggests that primary care settings can easily adopt STC, implying a potential increase in its use in the future. However, a recent study in five Northern European countries found that GPs and staff in these settings use of VC decreased in the post-pandemic era [18]. It suggests that even if GPs become familiar with VC, there may still be some individual, organizational, and cultural or unknown barriers that hinder its widespread use, necessitating further investigations. In this sense, GPs expressed concern about some pathologies for which they were unable to establish a diagnosis due to the lack of a physical exam [11, 49, 57]. The purpose of consultation should be a primary factor in guiding decisions regarding the modalities of consultation when the goal is to deliver quality care in primary healthcare.

The second category of factors pertains to ICT. This review found that to use STC, particularly VC, GPs look for a good internet connection and the effectiveness of the hard and soft materials to enable high-quality interaction between GPs and patients. These findings are aligned with previous reviews [13, 14]. Apart from confidentiality issues, this review identified barriers to STC’s adoption, such as patient privacy (e.g., a patient conducting a STC in an inappropriate place such as a grocery store), data security, and ethical concerns, which are similar to those reported by Kruse et al. [70]. This implies that to use STC, GPs not only need appropriate internet provision and reliable broadband in their office, but they also need to ensure the same requirement from patients’ side. GPs must fulfill their responsibility by ensuring patients’ privacy and confidentiality, which they can achieve by asking questions such as “Where are you?” or “Are you alone?” at the beginning of the conversation, for example. To ensure the continued use of STC, health services organizations must master the issue of data security effectively. Additionally, the absence of a reliable connection can pose a barrier to adoption, prompting consideration of resource availability when implementing new technologies.

The third category of factors pertains to GPs. Our review revealed that sociodemographic factors (e.g., financial incentive, training), ability (e.g., positive previous experience, level of digital literacy), attitude, and perceived convenience related to STC (e.g., flexibility) influence GPs’ use of STC. Ours results align with some results of previous reviews conducted on the adoption and use of STC in primary care, including convenience and positive previous experience [13, 14, 70], as well as reimbursement policies [13]. This highlights the importance of flexibility, digital skills, and the financial incentive for GPs to use STC. Therefore, to sustain the utilization of STC in primary care, it is crucial that policy discussions surrounding reimbursement meet a fair payment model for STC.

The fourth category of factors concern the GP-patient relationship, which can significantly influence GPs’ decision to use STC. An established relationship fosters trust through increased GP-patient communication, ensures continuity of care, and facilitates diagnosis. Kruse et al. [70] also reported in their systematic review an increase in GP-patient communication through VC. Several studies included in this review mentioned that PC lacks human contact and non-verbal cues, which are essential to establish trust with new patients and build therapeutic relationships [47, 49, 63, 68]. This suggests that PC may be most appropriate for follow-up care with known patients while VC could be most indicated for both known and unknown patients in general practice.

The fifth category of factors is concerned with patients. This review found that GPs’ decision to use STC is influenced by patients’ sociodemographic factors (e.g., age, ethnicity), abilities (e.g., level of digital literacy), attitudes, perceived convenience related to STC (e.g., flexibility), and access barriers. In four studies, GPs associated STC’s use with patients who live in remote areas, patients with mobility issues, or patients experiencing financial difficulties [8–11]. This suggests that GPs are more motivated to use STC when patients encounter more barriers to accessing clinics. While GPs might perceive STC as a mean to improve care accessibility for patients living in remote areas, patients with invalidity, and patients with financial barriers, it does not benefit those with low levels of digital literacy and those with language barriers (e.g., not proficient in the GP’s language). This indicates that the suitability of STC varies across patients’ groups, and the increased use of STC might contribute to reproducing certain forms of inequality in health care access. Therefore, future policies regulating the usage and delivery of STC should always prioritize equity in access. Furthermore, to ensure the appropriate uptake of STC in primary care, health care authorities must enhance the population’s capability, such as by providing training for individual groups in need, to enable them to use it freely and independently when possible.

The sixth category of factors pertains to the institution, specifically the clinic. We found that the existence of organizational and peer support, financial support, guidelines, and the perceived increase in workload (the ability of STC to decrease workload) influence the use of STC by GPs within an institution. In their systematic review, Hoff et al. [20] and Carrillo de Albornoz et al. [12] also reported on the role of STC to reduce GPs’ workload, while Lindenfeld et al. [13] reported on the role of peer support to facilitate STC adoption. This implies that even GPs’ will to use STC may not be enough for adoption; they should account for the organizational support. As highlighted by Rogers [19], employees’ decision to adopt an innovation does not depend on them but rather the organizational managers and policymakers. The institution plays a critical role in the individual’s adoption process by providing financial support, training, and other resources.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

One of the strengths of this review is its specific focus on the perspectives of GPs regarding the factors influencing their utilization of STC. This approach potentially enhances the relevance of the findings. Additionally, we included qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method studies in this synthesis to guarantee the inclusion of all types of findings, thereby reducing the possibility of missing any evidence. The review’s focus on synchronous teleconsultation is a unique aspect, because most existing reviews on the use and adoption of the ICT in healthcare have either focused on telemedicine or VC/PC. Furthermore, the methodological quality assessment showed that most of the included studies were of high quality (70.59%). However, 23.53% of studies were considered of low quality due to methodological concerns in several domains, such as consistent analysis and interpretation of qualitative data, representativeness and appropriate measurement for quantitative data. While these studies contributed to identifying some factors, our analysis revealed that the most often mentioned and emphasized factors came mostly from high-quality studies. Although, no formal sensitivity analysis was performed in the conventional statistical sense, we tested the consistency of our findings by examining the frequency distribution of studies across factors.

Limitations

Despite these strengths, there are several limitations to this review.

First, most evidence comes from qualitative studies as we had few quantitative studies. Only three studies [48, 51, 66] performed multivariate analyses to examine the link between explanatory variables and the type of consultation (STC and face-to-face consultation), including two [48, 51] that compared STC to face-to-face consultation. In addition, these two quantitative studies with multivariate analyses had small sample sizes of less than 20 GPs. While qualitative data provided rich contextual insights into GPs’ use of STC, the scarcity of robust multivariate analyses limited our ability to assess the effect size of covariables on GPs’ decision to use STC when interpreting quantitative associations between determinants and STC use by GPs. Multivariate analyses are essential in health services research because they allow researchers to account for the influence of multiple variables simultaneously, improving the internal validity of findings [71, 72]. Their absence weakens the generalizability and comparability of findings across settings and populations [73]. Consequently, while qualitative findings offer valuable depth, the limited statistical modeling restricts the strength of conclusions that can be drawn about the predictive value or relative weight of identified factors. Future reviews should prioritize the inclusion of rigorous and numerous quantitative studies with multivariate analyses to better inform policy and clinical practice on the utilization of STC by GPs.

Second, a potential limitation of this review is the temporal concentration of the included studies, being conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., 2020 and 2021). This review includes 56% of studies (19 out of 34) that were conducted during the COVID-19 period. This raises concerns about temporal bias, as the extraordinary context of the COVID-19 pandemic could have artificially inflated or distorted GPs’ experience, satisfaction, use, attitude toward, and acceptability of STC, which in turn could have impacted the identified factors. During the pandemic, STC adoption was often mandated by health authorities and influenced by emergency funding, regulatory relaxation, and constrained in-person care options [74, 75]. As such, the factors that GPs reported as influencing their decision to use STC during this period may not reflect typical practice conditions or be sustained in the long term. This context-dependent usage of STC suggests that findings may not actually represent the influencing factors of GPs’ utilization of STC for the integration of STC in post-pandemic primary healthcare. Fortunately, the review also includes 44% of studies conducted outside the COVID-19 period, whose results were consistent with those of studies conducted during the pandemic regarding the twenty factors.

Third, most included studies were conducted in high-income countries such as the USA, the UK, Canada, Norway, Australia, and Israel. As a result, our findings may not fully reflect the factors influencing the use of STC among GPs in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in regions such as Africa. This limitation highlights several underlying issues. One key challenge is the lack of infrastructure in many African countries, including limited access to electricity and reliable internet connectivity [76], both of which are essential for the effective implementation of digital health technologies. Another barrier is the limited capacity and expertise required to produce and use scientific evidence, which contributes to the scarcity of health data in the region [77]. These disparities contribute to a knowledge gap in the use of STC by GPs in this region. Therefore, future research should prioritize STC studies in underserved regions, such as Africa, to promote a more inclusive and global understanding of its adoption and use by GPs.

Finally, although the inclusion of publications only in English or French was a deliberate methodological choice, it might have narrowed the scope of the findings, potentially leading to an occidental perspective on the use of STC by GPs. However, most of the reviewed publications were written in English, and none were excluded solely based on the language criterion. Of the 725 articles assessed for inclusion, only 11 were written in other languages and were excluded on multiple reasons (e.g., not GP-specific, not about STC). Nonetheless, research should consider adopting multilingual approaches to gain a more thorough and globally representative understanding.

Conclusions

This review summarized the evidence on the factors influencing GPs’ use of STC. The main findings of the review highlight a multifactorial decision-making process, which considers several groups of factors, including consultation, ICT, GP, patient, GP-patient relationship, and the institution (the clinic).

The results suggest that GPs should first consider consultation-related factors to ensure the appropriate use of STC in primary care. To support the sustained use of STC, policy discussions on reimbursement should promote fair payment models and consistently prioritize equity in access. Future studies could further examine GPs’ preferences for various consultation modalities. Such studies could determine which factors prevail over others and to what extent, given that GPs’ preferences are crucial to enhancing their experience so as to achieve the Quadruple Aim-enhancing patient experience, improving population health, reducing cost, and improving the work life of health care providers [78], and equity of health systems.
