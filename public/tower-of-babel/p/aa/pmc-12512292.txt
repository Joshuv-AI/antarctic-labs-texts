Preparing graduate students for collaborative practice in chronic care: a mixed-methods evaluation of an interprofessional student-led education module
Authors: Yaël Slaghmuylder, Gabriël Cantaert, Peter Pype, Pauline Boeckxstaens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterprofessional collaboration has become indispensable in chronic care. Embedding interprofessional education (IPE) in health-related curriculums can prepare students to be fit for practice in this interprofessional field. However, further research is required on the design of IPE to underpin and monitor modules. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a multi-day, student-led interprofessional module in enhancing interprofessional socialization and awareness concerning the benefits of IPC in chronic care among students with differing health-related backgrounds (i.e., Medicine, Pharmaceutical Sciences, Health Care Management and Policy, Nursing and Midwifery, Occupational Therapy, and Health Promotion).MethodsWe applied a mixed-methods pre- and post-test design, using two questionnaires on awareness and interprofessional socialization and students’ reflection reports about the team dynamics and their experiences with the module.ResultsOverall, students with various backgrounds showed a significant improvement in interprofessional socialization, which was also represented in the reflection reports. Medicine and Pharmaceutical Sciences students showed the lowest baseline scores on awareness, but illustrated the most growth towards awareness and interprofessional socialization post-module, especially students with no prior experience in youth movements and health-related work. Nevertheless, lower awareness levels at baseline were predictive for lower levels of interprofessional socialization post-module. These findings imply the importance of building awareness early on in health-related curriculums as a catalyst for change towards interprofessional socialization.ConclusionsIPE should be provided on a continuum, ranging from early-on immersion in undergraduate programmes to graduate and continuing education. In this, student-led modules seem promising. However, possible awareness mismatches between different students and programmes and its effect on learning outcomes should be considered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07961-7.

Background

There is a growing number of people with chronic conditions compounded by an aging population and increasing life expectancy. Moreover, patients with chronic diseases often suffer from multiple concurrent conditions in which biological, psychological, and social factors interplay both in the exacerbation and management of the conditions [1]. Hence, this population is often in need of various care services provided by different health and social care professionals. To offer such complex care, interprofessional collaboration (IPC) and the integration of care services are required. Especially as inadequate IPC can hinder the continuity of care and contribute to fragmentation of care services [2].

Various initiatives for IPC have already been undertaken in research, education, and practice. However, it is not always evident how to effectively implement IPC across health and social care settings [3]. A key barrier is professionals’ unfamiliarity with the roles and expertise of other disciplines, leading to role ambiguity, communication breakdowns, and reinforced professional boundaries [4–6]. Awareness of one’s own and others’ roles, values, and assumptions can act as a catalyst for interprofessional socialisation, fostering openness to interprofessional learning (IPL) and supporting the development of an interprofessional identity (IPI) that enables effective collaboration. Still, the limited shared educational opportunities and interprofessional experiences during both undergraduate and graduate education pose a real challenge to practicing professionals [4].

Embedding interprofessional education (IPE) in health- and social-related curriculums - targeting awareness and interprofessional socialisation - could be promising in preparing students to be fit for practice in this interprofessional field. IPE involves students in a co-learning process, encouraging them to build meaningful relations with students from other disciplines and addressing their beliefs regarding the value and importance of collaboration [7, 8]. Fortunately, insights into the design and implementation of IPE have been increasing during recent years [7–10]. While the optimal timing for introducing IPE has long been debated, there is growing support for early and longitudinal integration of IPL to foster collaborative skills and positive attitudes among students from the beginning of their training [11, 12]. Reeves et al. emphasize the importance of incorporating active learning approaches in IPE, such as small group work and case- or problem-based learning to enable meaningful engagement with peers from different disciplines [13]. To maximize the impact of these approaches, educators should introduce cases and problems that are relevant to all participants, reflect the complementary roles of different professionals, and require an equal distribution of tasks - for example, in the context of chronic care [6, 14].

IPE typically relies heavily on faculty facilitation of these approaches, which are associated with logistical challenges such as scheduling conflicts and faculty training. These barriers limit scalability, particularly in programs with large student cohorts or competing curricular demands [15, 16]. Hence, organizing non-supervised, student-led IPE offers a pragmatic alternative that could also foster self-regulated collaboration within student groups [17]. Moreover, student-led IPE necessitates collaborative leadership, where students rotate or collectively align team goals, facilitate participative decision-making, and mediate conflicts [18, 19]. Little is, however, known about the team dynamics of non-supervised interprofessional small group work and how differences in students’ disciplines and individual characteristics (e.g., prior experiences with collaboration and the healthcare context, whether familial, social, or clinical) may affect learning outcomes [18].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the awareness and interprofessional socialisation of students with differing backgrounds before and after participating in a multi-day, student-led IPE module concerning IPC in chronic care. Additionally, we aimed to explain these quantitative results through the written experiences of team leaders regarding how student-led collaboration unfolds.

Method

Study sample and setting

Graduate students of the faculties of ‘Medicine and Health Sciences’ and ‘Pharmaceutical Sciences’ of Ghent University (Belgium) who participated in the LISa Chronic Care module in academic year 2019–2020 were recruited. LISa is a Dutch abbreviation for ‘Leren Interprofessioneel Samenwerken’, which translates in English to ‘Learning Interprofessional Collaboration’. This education module is part of the LISa interprofessional learning continuum at Ghent University.

The following master programmes take part in the LISa Chronic Care module: Medicine, Pharmaceutical Sciences (PS), Health Care Management and Policy (HCMP), Nursing and Midwifery (NM), Occupational Therapy (OT), and Health Promotion (HP). The four latter programmes include students who have already obtained a health or social-related degree, which means that they potentially bring in multiple years of professional and clinical experience from their occupation.

Education module

This specific LISa module spans three days and applies a problem-based approach in which groups of 10 to 12 students are selected a priori, ensuring an equal mix in educational background. These students are expected to collaboratively tackle nine questions about the organization of chronic care, starting from a case of a patient with a chronic condition (i.e., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), multiple sclerosis (MS), or system sclerosis (SS)).

The learning objectives of this module are based on the interprofessional competency framework of the Interprofessional Education Collaborative [20]. The objectives are formulated in relation to chronic care, such as ‘to integrate the knowledge and experience of other students and professionals to solve complex questions pertaining to the organization of chronic care both on the level of individual patients and society in general’.

As the groups are student-led, no supervision by teaching staff is arranged. Nevertheless, one student is assigned the role of team leader in consensus among the group members rather than imposed by faculty. This role is described to the students as follows: ‘to apply leadership practices that support collaborative practice and team effectiveness’. In concrete terms, this entails tasks such as delegating and monitoring assignments, moderating team meetings, and facilitating teamwork as well as handling conflicts. While no formal leadership training is provided, students are expected to draw on leadership skills acquired during prior coursework, workplace, or extracurricular experiences. This approach mirrors interprofessional teams in practice, where leadership may emerge informally based on situational expertise and prior experiences [19]. Additionally, an expert panel is involved in the final day of the module. Selected student groups present their preparations of the nine questions in front of both the panel and fellow students. This panel is composed of educators from the various participating master programmes, including professors, teaching assistants, and researchers. It also includes patients and external stakeholders with expertise in chronic care.

To conclude the module, students wrote an individual report in which they reflected on the learning objectives of the module in terms of the strengths (S), weaknesses (W), opportunities (O), and threats (T) of the healthcare system for patients with chronic conditions. The student leaders received a different assignment and were asked to reflect on the process of IPC within their group, in particular with regard to their experiences of being a leader. More detailed information on the education module is presented in Additional File 1.

Data collection and materials

We opted for a convergent parallel mixed-methods pre- and post-test design using questionnaires and reflection reports of team leaders to evaluate the effectiveness of the student-led module in enhancing student’s awareness and interprofessional socialization, as well as in exploring the experiences of students. This approach involves the simultaneous collection of both qualitative and quantitative data, followed by their comparison [21].

The following questionnaires were administered online using the REDCap tool (i.e., Research Electronic Data Capture) [22], two weeks before the start of the module (T1) and between one and two weeks after having participated in the module (T2). Demographics

The following demographic data were collected: age, sex, programme, year of enrolment, prior experience with health-related jobs or youth movements, and parents or siblings working in health care. The latter two were combined in an additional variable to group students with both parents and siblings working in health care. Similarly, a composite variable was coded to include students who both have experience in a youth movement and health-related work.

Awareness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (AIPLS)

In order to evaluate the LISa interprofessional learning continuum, the original Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS) was translated to Dutch, revised as a unidimensional scale, and reduced from 19 to 8 items (Additional File 2) [23]. This scale is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). See Cantaert et al. for more information on the translation and revision process [24]. The Dutch version of the AIPLS was used to determine the awareness of students towards interprofessional learning, which is vital in the first stages of developing a readiness for IPC.

Interprofessional Socialization and Valuing Scale (ISVS)

The Dutch version of the ISVS was used to assess the impact of the module on students’ interprofessional socialization. This scale is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all or to a very small extent; 5 = to a very great extent), with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes towards IPC. In line with other validation studies, only the mean total scores were calculated to conduct the data-analysis [25–28].

Reflection reports

The reflection reports of the team leaders were collected for analysis as these offer insight into the team dynamics and experiences with the module. The SWOT reports were excluded from the analysis because they did not reflect students’ experiences with the module and, therefore, were not relevant for this evaluation. The submitted reports were obtained from the VLE through the course organizer. These reports were anonymized by a trusted third party.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM®, New York). Before analysis, the dataset was cleaned by removing duplicate, missing and extreme values. Then, demographic data were analysed using frequency and descriptive statistics. The AIPLS and ISVS mean scores were treated as continuous and the demographics as categorical variables. Given the sufficiently large sample size and on the basis of a normal distribution of data that was confirmed by inspecting the histograms, parametric tests encompassing paired and independent t-testing as well as one-way ANOVAs were used to calculate the within-group differences from T1 to T2 and the between groups differences at T1 and T2. The Levene’s test was used to assess homogeneity of variance (p >.5) and the Tukey correction was applied in case of multiple pairwise comparisons. The sample of OT students and the two composite variables were not included in the subgroup analysis within each programme due to the low sample size. When applicable, one-sided p-values, mean (M), mean difference (MD), standard deviation (SD), degrees of freedom (df), t value and 95% confidence interval (CI95%) were reported. In addition, the effect size (Cohens’ d) was reported as a measure of educational effectiveness in case of significant results [29, 30]. The calculation was based on the SD of the pre-test, as this value is not influenced by the intervention, and accounted for the correlation between the pre- and post-test scores [31, 32]. An effect size above 0.40 was desired, with an effect size between 0.15 and 0.40 indicating a small effect and greater than 0.70 representing a strong effect on students’ learning [29, 30].

A multiple linear regression model was constructed to determine the extent to which education, sex, prior experience, and family working in health care predict students’ scores at T1 and T2. The assumptions for multiple regression were met given the sufficiently large sample size (n = > 50 + 8*10), the absence of multicollinearity (Tolerance > 0.10 and Variance inflation factor < 10) and by checking for normality, linearity and homoscedasticity of the residuals through inspection of the scatterplots [33]. First, a correlation analysis was conducted to determine the strength and direction of the linear relationship between both scales. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) indicates whether the relationship is small (0.10-0.29), moderate (0.30-0.49), or large (0.50 − 1.0) [34]. Then, each of the predictors was simultaneously entered into the model and evaluated in terms of their predictive power and degree to which they explain the variance in students’ scores. Afterwards, the baseline scores on the AIPLS were added as a predictor to check whether a lower or higher level of awareness influences the degree to which students’ ISVS scores increase or decrease while correcting for students’ demographics. When significant predictors were identified, the R square (R2) of the models was reported in addition to the standardised coefficients Beta (b). R2 represents the amount of variance in the scores on the AIPLS and/or ISVS that the model predicts, while the value of b represents the increase or decrease in mean scores resulting from a unit change in the predictor [35].

Finally, the reflection reports were investigated through a thematic analysis grounded in principles of constant comparison [36]. The reports were analysed inductively by one author (YS). Concrete experiences were presented as concepts, then grouped and categorized into various overarching themes. Subsequently, two authors (PB, PP) each read ten reports and reviewed whether their findings matched the proposed thematic concepts. Finally, the obtained list of themes and concepts was presented and discussed within the research team (YS, GC, PB, PP) until a consensus was reached.

The results of the pre-post comparison are presented in Table 2. At T1, Medicine and PS showed the lowest mean scores on the AIPLS compared to the other programmes. Similarly, Medicine and PS also had the lowest mean scores on the ISVS. In view of a significant ANOVA (< 0.005) in baseline scores between the programmes on both scales (AIPLS F = 3.83 and ISVS F = 3.305), a post-hoc analysis was conducted. Pairwise comparisons indicated that Medicine students scored significantly lower than HP students on the AIPLS. Additionally, both Medicine and PS students scored significantly lower than the HCMP students on the ISVS.

At T2, a significant difference in mean scores was detected solely for the ISVS (p <.05). In this, Medicine, OT, and HP students scored the lowest. This time, however, both Medicine and HP students scored significantly lower (p <.05) than HCMP students.

From T1 to T2, the AIPLS scores increased significantly in Medicine and PS students with an effect size of 0.22 and 0.33, respectively. When looking at individual characteristics, students without a parent or sibling working in health care and those with no prior experience in a youth movement or a health-related job attained a significantly higher growth on the AIPLS. The largest effect sizes, however, were found among male students and students who neither had prior experience with regard to both youth movements and health-related work. Interestingly, students who already had experience in both domains did not experience any change whatsoever. Further subgroup analyses within each programme (Additional File 3) revealed that the significant increase among male students is limited to Medicine and NM only, although the latter is difficult to interpret due to the limited number of students. Likewise, only the Medicine students without a parent and/or sibling working in health care had a significant increase in scores with an effect size ranging from 0.23 to 0.28. Conversely, a significant increase is noted among students in PS with one or more siblings working in health care and a significant decrease in NM and HP among students with one or more parents working in health care, although caution is again advised given the small sample sizes. Solely the Medicine students without experience from a health-related job and the students from both Medicine and PS who have no prior experience in a youth movement or neither experience with youth movements or health-related work gained significantly higher scores.

Pertaining to the ISVS, students from all educational programmes showed significant improvements, with the exception of OT students that experienced a non-significant decrease. The highest effect size was observed in PS students, followed by HCMP, NM, Medicine, and HP. Subsequent analyses showed significant results for the ISVS regardless of students’ demographics. Similar to the AIPLS, the effect sizes on the ISVS are higher among students who are male, do not have any parent and sibling working in health care or who do not have any experience with regard to a youth movement and a health-related job. However, these findings differ across programmes, as can be consulted in Table 2.

Multiple linear regression

Correlation analysis showed a stronger positive bidirectional relationship between pre-AIPLS and post-ISVS scores (p <.001; r =.41) compared to pre-AIPLS and pre-ISVS scores for the programs combined (p <.001; r =.21). This indicates that higher AIPLS scores at baseline are associated with higher ISVS scores after participation than at baseline. The association between pre-AIPLS and post-ISVS scores is strong among HCMP students (p <.001; r =.64), yet moderate in Medicine (p <.001; r =.39), PS (p <.01; r =.40), and NM (p <.05; r =.39). Conversely, there is a strong positive correlation in HP between pre-AIPLS scores and pre-ISVS scores (p <.05; r =.36) but not with regard to the post scores.

Multiple regression analysis composed of the demographic variables revealed that the model explains 5.4% of the variation in AIPLS scores and 6.6% in ISVS scores at T1, in contrast to 1.4% and 5.5% at T2, respectively. At T1, being a student in Medicine (p <.05; b=−0.19 and p <.01; b=−0.22) or PS (p <.05; b = 0.13 and p <.005; b=−0.22) is predictive of lower scores on the AIPLS and the ISVS. At T2, Medicine (p <.005; b=−0.26) and HP (p <.005; b=−0.20) are significant predictors for lower ISVS scores. After adding the AIPLS and ISVS baseline scores to the model, the R2 Changes to 34.5% and 32% when explaining the variance in AIPLS and ISVS scores at T2, respectively. In this case, both AIPLS (p <.001; b = 0.56) and ISVS (p <.05; b = 0.10) baseline scores significantly predict AIPLS scores at T2. Likewise, AIPLS (p <.001; b = 0.37) and ISVS (p <.001; b = 0.32) baseline scores are significant predictors for ISVS scores at T2. These results can be consulted in detail in Additional File 4.

Reflection reports

We received 52 reflection reports (i.e., a response rate of 96%), including 26 from Medicine students, 13 from HCMP, 6 from NM, 4 from PS, and 3 from OT. The reports ranged from one to nine pages. Eight themes were identified, covering the experiences of the student leaders both in their leadership roles and as members of an interprofessional team.

Reasons behind taking up the leadership role. Three main reasons for volunteering as team leader were described. First, they felt obliged to partake in this role as no one else showed any interest.“I mentioned that I was interested in taking on the role of clinical leader. Since no one else volunteered, I was assigned the role.” (HCMP - SS12).

“I mentioned that I was interested in taking on the role of clinical leader. Since no one else volunteered, I was assigned the role.” (HCMP - SS12).

Second, some student leaders already had prior experience with leading a (clinical) team and therefore felt eligible.“I deliberately chose to take on the leadership role, to avoid unnecessary delays. As a student representative, I have attended many meetings where I felt things could be handled in a more structured and efficient way. As team leader, I wanted to prioritize that.” (Medicine - SS11).

“I deliberately chose to take on the leadership role, to avoid unnecessary delays. As a student representative, I have attended many meetings where I felt things could be handled in a more structured and efficient way. As team leader, I wanted to prioritize that.” (Medicine - SS11).

Third, many student leaders perceived this particular role to be a learning opportunity.“When no one initially volunteered to be the team leader, I said I wouldn’t mind taking on the role if no one else felt called to do so. Since I’m currently studying ‘Health Care Management and Policy’, I saw this as a valuable opportunity and learning experience for the future.” (HCMP - COPD7).

In the following days, I was pleasantly surprised by the work some of my team members had already done.” (Medicine - SS11).

Discussion

Health and social care students are expected to be ready for collaborative practice when entering the workforce. However, facilitating IPL in graduate students presents challenges in its implementation, monitoring, and evaluation and raises questions concerning feasible and effective designs [18]. Therefore, we evaluated the awareness and interprofessional socialization of students participating in a student-led module while taking into account their discipline and individual characteristics. Additionally, we were interested in how student-led collaboration unfolded in this module through the written experiences of the team leaders.

Overall, students from all educational programmes - with the exception of Occupational Therapy students - showed a significant improvement in their interprofessional socialization. When looking at individual programmes, Medicine and Pharmaceutical Sciences students appeared to take the greatest advantage of their participation in the module, given their moderate to high effect sizes on both scales. Interestingly, these students showed the lowest baseline scores. Accordingly, previous studies showed that medical students tend to have lower baseline scores with the explanation that these students are informally socialized to be more reluctant toward collaboration and to perceive themselves to be hierarchically superior in terms of knowledge and skills [37, 38]. In PS, students might have started with lower scores due to stereotypical images such as ‘shopkeeper’ [39]. Moreover, both Medicine and PS students had not yet commenced their longitudinal clinical placements and/or were experiencing their first interprofessional interactions. The PS programme, for example, is situated in a separate campus, isolated from other health programmes. Their significant growth in awareness might be related to PS students feeling more recognized and understood, having had the opportunity to explore other professional roles among peers [39]. For instance, a PS student leader mentioned in their reflection report that they felt relied upon as the final decision maker. The medical student leaders, on the other hand, expressed in their reflection reports a perceived lack of knowledge and experience compared to students from other programmes. Hence, the case-based group discussions may have led students to self-assess and compare their knowledge to that of their peers [40]. Consequently, positive interprofessional interactions - derived from the student-led design - may have countered traditional beliefs pertaining to hierarchy.

Interestingly, two subgroups of the Medicine and PS students had the lowest baseline scores and exhibited the most notable gain on both scales. The first subgroup entailed male Medicine students, confirming literature on the tendency of women showing higher interest and being more receptive toward IPC [41, 42]. The second subgroup encompassed Medicine and PS students without prior health care or youth movement experience. Students - who had prior experiences with collaboration either in a social or clinical context - could presumably possess a greater awareness of the benefits of collaboration, owing to the way they were socialized [41, 43, 44]. Young undergraduates may value IPC more positively based on personal characteristics, prior life experiences, and social interactions [44, 45]. Accordingly, student leaders - with prior experience in leadership and collaboration - were able to articulate more explicitly what they valued in teamwork and which aspects they aimed to address in their leadership role during the module. These findings shed a positive light on anticipatory socialization, which has primarily been associated with the development of misconceptions and stereotypes before enrolment in higher education [46–48]. To conclude, gaining an understanding of the influence of personal and contextual factors helps to discover critical learning points in evolving towards an IPI.

This notwithstanding, we noted a ceiling effect in AIPLS scores among the students in OT, NM, HCMP, and HP. These students started out with a high degree of awareness, presumably due to their more advanced education and prior work experiences, which could mean that the capacity for further growth in awareness is limited [49]. However, a high degree of awareness should neither be seen as problematic nor a gold standard in evaluating the effectiveness of IPE, but rather a necessary condition for developing and strengthening an IPI [50]. Indeed, this study identified AIPLS scores as a significant predictor of ISVS scores post-intervention. Although Medicine and PS students - starting with lower AIPLS scores at baseline - underwent the most growth, our study also found that higher AIPLS scores at baseline are associated with higher ISVS scores after participation in the IPE module. As such, HCMP, NM, and HP students showed a significant improvement in the ISVS scores at group level, which was also reflected in the individual reports of the student leaders. For example, HCMP student leaders often mentioned having prior experiences with leading a team and felt boosted in their confidence as the module confirmed their leadership skills within a ‘safe’ environment [51].

Lastly, the question remains how an informal learning environment - such as the one in which this module took place - influences IPL, particularly in relation to awareness and interprofessional socialization. In the reflection reports, the informal context was recurrently mentioned as a facilitator for IPE and collaboration. The existing literature confirms that informal learning environments allow students to feel safe and ready to contribute to teamwork [40, 52]. However, we have to be cautious as students may not feel responsible for contributing when the learning environment is too informal [52]. In such cases, the participating student leaders often described it to be their responsibility to ensure equal engagement from every team member. Some student leaders reported vocalizing their own clinical reasoning or thinking out loud which encouraged other team members to join in reflection and conversation, leading to new insights [53].

Limitations

An important limitation of this study was the restricted population of learners and the uneven representation of certain educational programmes and sexes, hindering significant and valid results among subgroups of students. Second, the one-group design requires caution about making causal assumptions. Third, our conclusions are based on a three-day education module, with a lack of longitudinal data. A more longitudinal approach could have aided the identification of critical learning moments among different students (e.g., at the start versus the end of a study programme, before versus after IPE activities). As such, we may gain a better understanding of instances when scores may rise or drop. For example, among those students who become less enthusiastic and receptive toward IPE over time, and how such instances may relate to identity threat [37]. Fourth, it would have been interesting to capture the experiences of the other team members in relation to those of the student leaders, which could serve as an avenue for future research.

Implications

We wanted to draw conclusions on the appropriateness of the module’s student-led design and its effectiveness in enhancing awareness and interprofessional socialisation. The co-evolution of both scales (i.e., AIPLS and ISVS) highlights the importance of minimal baseline awareness levels in more advanced IPE modules, such as LISa Chronic Care. Our results suggest that cultivating a willingness for IPL early on will prove to be beneficial for forthcoming IPE activities [44]. In this regard, misconceptions may be challenged in the first undergraduate years before they become more resistant to change in the later years [54].

Additionally, it is important for students to possess sufficient role knowledge and clinical experience to feel secure in their professional identity when participating in IPE modules. Before each learning activity, knowledge gaps should be addressed and beliefs explicated to heighten students’ readiness for IPL and their sensitivity to incoming information [55, 56]. For example, becoming familiar with the unique roles of their own profession as well as the roles of others within the healthcare team may aid in becoming aware of any preconceived assumptions and stereotypes [47]. Furthermore, addressing these gaps beforehand can ensure that group members are able to contribute satisfactorily to the process of collaborative problem-solving. In the LISa Chronic Care module, students gain insight into other disciplines’ roles in chronic care, raising their awareness about collaborative leadership and addressing stereotypical images such as the hierarchical position of physicians. However, more research is required on the effects of an awareness mismatch between participating students of different programmes (e.g., Medicine and PS versus more advanced programmes with prior education and work experiences), raising the question which level of mismatch might be beneficial for learning outcomes (i.e., students’ zone of proximal development) [57].

Furthermore, student leaders play a significant role in IPE facilitation within student-led groups [58]. Contributing factors are leaders holding interprofessional beliefs and their readiness for IPE facilitation [59]. Overall, collaborative leadership - in which each professional can act as a leader within their team based on situational expertise - should be explicitly prompted throughout a student’s education, as the reflection reports indicated a variety of leadership styles that do not always facilitate IPC [19].

Finally, team learning can be facilitated through peer assessment. Peer assessment elicits reflection on the degree one feels part of the team and perceives a shared understanding and a collaborative mindset with team members [60–62]. This entails - among other things - a literacy in interprofessional feedback so that professionals are confident in speaking-up and in providing feedback to others as a way of fostering team learning [63, 64]. In this study, multiple reflection reports mentioned a lack of peer feedback as a means to avoid relational conflicts during a short-term assignment. Student leaders often regretted this course of action afterward, as they would have handled the situation differently in a clinical context.

Conclusions

This study implies the importance of building awareness early on in health and social-related curriculums as a catalyst for change towards interprofessional socialisation and the development of an interprofessional identity. Hence, IPE should be provided along a continuum, ranging from early-on immersion in undergraduate programmes to graduate and continuing education. To counter this demanding IPE design, student-led education modules seem promising in enhancing awareness and interprofessional socialisation. Furthermore, evaluation of IPE modules is essential to ensure alignment and complementarity between participating curriculums, especially when organizing student-led interprofessional groups.
