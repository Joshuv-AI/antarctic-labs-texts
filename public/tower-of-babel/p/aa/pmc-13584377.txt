Three-dimensional relationship between the hyoid bone and the stylohyoid complex: a cone-beam computed tomography study introducing a novel angular parameter
Authors: Mürşide Burcu Kiriş, Nihal Avcu, Gökçen Akçiçek, Hanife Avcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the relationship between hyoid bone morphology and stylohyoid complex morphometry using cone-beam computed tomography and to investigate the influence of age and sex on these characteristics.MethodsA retrospective cone-beam computed tomography analysis of 338 individuals was performed to evaluate hyoid bone morphology, styloid process length, angulation, elongation type, and calcification patterns. The lesser horn–body angle was measured as a novel angular parameter to assess its relationship with the stylohyoid complex anatomy.ResultsThe study included 338 individuals (226 females, 66.9%; 112 males, 33.1%) with a median age of 43 years. No significant associations were found between hyoid bone morphology and styloid process length, angulation, elongation type, or calcification pattern (p > 0.05). Type U was the most common hyoid bone morphology overall and in males, whereas Type B predominated in females; however, the association between hyoid bone morphology and sex was not statistically significant. Although the highest lesser horn–body angle difference was observed in Type H and the lowest in Type B, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.076). Styloid process length, medio-lateral angulation, and antero-posterior angulation were significantly greater in males than females bilaterally (p < 0.05). Type I (elongated) and Type II (partial calcification) were the predominant stylohyoid complex elongation and calcification patterns, respectively, both showing bilateral symmetry. Age-related differences were identified among styloid process elongation types on both sides, with Type III (segmented) occurring at younger ages on the right side (p = 0.006) and Type I (elongated) being associated with older age on the left side (p < 0.001). No significant associations were found between hyoid bone morphology and styloid process length, angulation, elongation type, or calcification pattern (p > 0.05).ConclusionsCBCT demonstrated bilateral symmetry in stylohyoid complex morphology and angulation. Significant sex and age-related differences were observed in selected stylohyoid complex characteristics, whereas no association was found between hyoid bone morphology and stylohyoid complex morphometry. The lesser horn–body angle exhibited high reproducibility and bilateral consistency, although its significance remains unclear.Keywords: Anatomy, Cone beam computed tomography, Hyoid bone, Temporal bone

Introduction

The hyoid bone (HB) and the stylohyoid complex (SHC) are anatomically and functionally important elements of the head and neck area. The HB is the only bone in the body that does not directly articulate with other bones, instead being related to adjacent tissues by muscles and ligaments. This anatomical characteristic facilitates its critical functions in swallowing, speech, and respiration [1, 2], as well as its association with craniofacial configuration, head-neck posture and airway patency [3, 4].

Although conventional anatomical descriptions assert that the HB is symmetrical and U-shaped or horseshoe-shaped, it actually exhibits a variety of morphologies. While a widely accepted taxonomy for HB morphology is absent, initial research has identified two primary types: hyperbolic and parabolic [5, 6]. This methodology was then broadened into a five-category classification comprising Type U, Type H, Type B, Type D, and Type V, along with an asymmetric Type HK variant, which illustrates the transition between the H and D types and has been incorporated into the literature [7, 8]. Previous studies primarily relied on cadaver assessments or traditional radiography and computed tomography (CT) imaging; however, recent research indicates that quantitative metrics, including the distance, angle and length ratios between the horn tips, are now being analyzed utilizing advanced cross-sectional imaging techniques [5]. Morphometric features of the HB have been employed in forensic and anthropological contexts for age and sex assessment [9, 10].

The lesser horn of the HB, stylohyoid ligament (SHL), and styloid process (SP) constitute the SHC. Developmental ossification or calcification within its components may lead to elongation or angular deviations of the SP [11]. The typical length of the SP is estimated to be between 20 and 30 mm, with measurements surpassing this range classified as elongated [12, 13]. The SHC is clinically significant due to its proximity to the internal carotid artery, jugular vein, and many cranial nerves. Elongation or calcification of the styloid process may result in disorders such as Eagle syndrome or stylo-carotid syndrome [14, 15].

Eagle syndrome is a clinical disorder marked by symptoms including cephalic and cervical pain, dysphagia, a sensation of a foreign body, otalgia, and tinnitus linked to the extension of the SP or ossification of the SHL [16, 17]. In its classic presentation, symptoms typically exacerbate with swallowing, chewing, and head movements and it has been shown that scar tissue formation post-tonsillectomy may induce mechanical discomfort [18]. The vascular form (stylo-carotid syndrome) is not influenced by a history of tonsillectomy and arises from protracted or medially deviated SP compressing the internal or external carotid arteries [19]. Involvement of the external carotid artery results in neck and suborbital pain that intensifies with head rotation, whereas involvement of the internal carotid artery is linked to more severe neurological symptoms including headache, visual abnormalities, vertigo, and transient ischemia episodes [20]. While elongated SP is prevalent, the majority of instances are asymptomatic; in symptomatic individuals, manifestations may mimic cranial nerve neuralgias, temporomandibular joint disorders, and dental conditions, underscoring the necessity of differential diagnosis [21, 22]. SHC has been categorized in several manners according to radiographic characteristics, ossification patterns, and embryological origins [23–25]. Recent investigations are examining the three-dimensional morphology and morphological changes of SHC with increased precision utilizing cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) [26, 27].

CBCT generates a lower radiation dose compared to CT, although a higher dose than panoramic imaging, commonly utilized in dental applications. CBCT provides high-resolution three-dimensional imaging with reduced radiation exposure, enabling thorough assessment of complex head and neck anatomy [28, 29]. Analyzing the morphometric relationship between the HB and SHC by CBCT improves our understanding of the structural and functional integrity of the region.

Although several studies have explored HB or SHC independently, there is a scarcity of research analyzing their connection in three dimensions [30]. Unlike previous CBCT studies that primarily focused on the linear measurements of the SP or the general morphology of the HB, the present study introduces the lesser horn–body angle as a novel angular parameter reflecting the intrinsic configuration of the HB and its potential relationship with the SHC. This study aims to evaluate HB morphology, styloid process length, elongation type, angulation, and calcification pattern using CBCT, as well as to investigate the connections among these characteristics in an adult population.

Methods

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No: SBA 24/980). All CBCT data were anonymized and restricted to investigator access only.

This retrospective study analyzed 10,843 CBCT scans acquired between January 2017 and August 2024 from patients referred to the Department of Dentomaxillofacial Radiology, Hacettepe University, Ankara, Türkiye. The study cohort predominantly represented an Anatolian adult population. The inclusion criteria comprised participants aged 18 years and older, a voxel size of 0.2 mm, and complete imaging of the HB and SHC. Patients with congenital abnormalities, trauma, surgical history, imaging artifacts or pathological lesions at the examined sites were excluded. A total of 338 scans fulfilled the criteria for analysis. Given the retrospective nature of the study, no attempt was made to achieve homogeneous distributions among hyoid bone morphological subgroups. All identified morphology types were included to reflect the natural anatomical variation of the study population and to investigate potential associations with stylohyoid complex characteristics.

Images were obtained using an i-CAT Next Generation scanner (Imaging Sciences International, USA) at 120 kVp, 5 mA, with an exposure duration of 14.7–17.8 s and a field of view measuring 16 × 12–17 cm. DICOM files were examined using InVivo5 software (version 5.2, Anatomage, USA). All measurements were conducted by a sole observer. 15% of the sample (n = 51) was randomly selected and assessed by a second observer. One month later, this sample (n = 51) was re-evaluated by both observers to assess intra- and inter-observer reliability.

Hyoid bone analysis

The measurements, morphological evaluation, and classification were performed by digitally cutting the surrounding tissues to isolate only the HB. Observers used software-assisted image enhancement and guidance tools such as brightness, contrast, and magnification to more distinctly identify reference points. The morphological classification of 6 subtypes of HB.

(U, H, B, D, V, and HK types) was performed in the images [8].

Type U

HB possesses a semi-circular body in the anterior with the greater horns extending laterally in the same direction, resulting in a “U” configuration.

Type H

It features a slender anterior body with the greater horns that extend posteriorly at a broad angle, their tips bending inward to create a horseshoe configuration.

Type B

When observed from the frontal perspective, the body has a flattened morphology, with the greater horns extending laterally, forming a configuration resembling a boat hull.

Type D

The front portion of the body is semicircular; the greater horns extend at varying levels or orientations, resulting in an asymmetrical appearance.

Type V

The angle between the body and the greater horns is acute, with the horns ascending in a pronounced “V” shape, creating a triangular configuration.

Type HK

It has characteristics that are in between the D and H types, and the greater horns are noticeably asymmetrical. One of the greater horns is typically horizontal but the other is more inclined and possesses a hook-like configuration (Fig. 1).

Three reference regions were delineated: based on the emergence point of the lesser horns from the body, the tip of the lesser horn, and the apex identified along the projection line of this tip on the body. The angle created by the lesser horns and the body was assessed at these locations (Fig. 2a). In HBs where lesser horns were not anatomically observed, the angle was recorded as 0° (Fig. 2b).

Stylohyoid complex analysis

The total length of the SP was measured from its origin at the temporal bone to its distal tip [18] (Fig. 3a). In the medio-lateral angulation (MLA) measurement, the angle between the line connecting the SP bases in the coronal plane and the long axis of the SP on the relevant side was used as a reference [31]. In the antero-posterior angulation (APA) measurement, the angle between the Frankfurt horizontal plane and the long axis of the SP on the sagittal plane was used as the basis [32]. Anterior angulation measurements were performed digitally using ImageJ software (Fig. 3b and c). All angular measurements were performed in degrees using standardized multiplanar reconstructions, with the Frankfurt horizontal plane as the reference for sagittal angulation and the coronal plane oriented perpendicularly.

a SHC length measurement. b Medio-lateral angulation measurement. c Antero-posterior angulation measurement

The elongation type was classified according to Langlais and Reddy (Types I-IV) [23, 24].

Type I - elongated SHC

This type is characterized by the continuous and uninterrupted observation of mineralized stylohyoid structures as a single piece (Fig. 4a).

SHC classification of elongation types; a Elongated (Type I), b Pseudo-articular (Type II), c Segmented (Type III), and (d) Distant ossification (Type IV). SHC calcification pattern classification; e Calcified Outline (Type I), f Partial Calcification (Type II), g Nodular Calcification (Type III), h Complete Calcification (Type IV)

Type II – pseudo-articular SHC

This type has one pseudo-articulation point that comes from the SP fusing with the mineralized stylo-mandibular or stylohyoid ligament (Fig. 4b).

Type III - segmented SHC

This type has several separate segments of the SP and/or a mineralized ligament structure (Fig. 4c).

Type IV - distant ossification

This type of ossification occurs in the distal portions of the ligamentous structure rather than in the cranial part (Fig. 4d).

The calcification pattern of the SHC was assessed following Langlais classification (Types I-IV) [23].

Calcified outline - type I

It is characterized by a radiolucent area in the center, surrounded by a thin, distinct radio-opaque contour, resembling the silhouette of a long bone (Fig. 4e).

Partial calcification - type II

This variant is characterized by the presence of small, discontinuous radiolucent regions within an opaque structure, bordered by a thick, dense radio-opaque outer contour (Fig. 4f).

Nodular calcification - type III

It has an irregular, lumpy or scalloped outer contour and may be seen with partial or complete calcification (Fig. 4g).

Complete calcification - type IV

The SHC appears entirely dense and radio-opaque, devoid of any radio-lucent areas (Fig. 4h).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using the free and open-source software R (version 4.4.1, https://cran.r-project.org), SPSS for Windows Version 23.0 statistical package (Chicago, IL) by an academic biostatistician. Normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and variance homogeneity was tested using Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics were presented as median (25th percentile – 75th percentile) and frequencies (percentages) as appropriate. To compare differences between groups, Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables, and the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was applied for categorical variables. Differences in numerical measurements according to gender groups were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test. The relationship between continuous variables was examined using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate differences between right and left side measurements and the marginal homogeneity test was used to compare right–left elongation types and calcification patterns. Inter- and intra-rater agreement was evaluated using quadratic weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient for categorical variables and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for continuous variables. Weighted kappa coefficients were calculated using the ‘irr’ package in R software [33]. The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The research included 338 individuals, comprising 226 females (66.9%) and 112 males (33.1%), with ages ranging from 18 to 81 years (mean age: 43 years).

Reliability analysis

Reliability analysis demonstrated excellent reproducibility for all evaluated variables. For categorical assessments, intra-observer weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficients ranged from 0.873 to 0.986 for the first observer, 0.806 to 1.000 for the second observer, while inter-observer kappa coefficients ranged from 0.727 to 1.000. For continuous measurements, intra-observer intraclass correlation coefficients ranged from 0.986 to 0.994 for the first observer and from 0.969 to 0.996 for the second observer. Inter-observer intraclass correlation coefficients ranged from 0.969 to 0.993. All agreement coefficients indicated substantial to almost perfect agreement (p < 0.001).

Styloid process length and angulation

A total of 676 styloid processes were assessed. Styloid processes of ≥ 30 mm were identified in 58.75% of instances. The median length of the SP was 30.5 mm (range 5.6–91.7 mm) on the right and 30.9 mm (range 8.1–92.8 mm) on the left, with no statistically significant difference observed between sides (p = 0.810). A statistically significant difference was identified between the right and left sides in MLA (p = 0.045), while no significant side-related difference was detected in APA (Table 1). No significant correlations were observed between age and MLA measurements on either side (p > 0.05). In contrast, age demonstrated a very weak but statistically significant positive correlation with APA measurements on both the right (rs = 0.134, p = 0.014) and left sides (rs = 0.141, p = 0.009) (Table 2). Additionally, males demonstrated significantly longer SPs on both the right (p = 0.010) and left (p = 0.006) sides, with MLA and APA values likewise being higher in males on both sides. No significant correlation was observed between age and SP length on either the right (rs = − 0.008, p = 0.885) or left side (rs = 0.011, p = 0.838) (Table 2).

Elongation types and calcification patterns

Type I (elongated) SHC was the predominant elongation type (n = 411; 60.8%), succeeded by Type II (pseudo-articular) (n = 173; 25.6%), Type III (segmented) (n = 64; 9.5%), and Type IV (distant ossification) (n = 28; 4.1%). Partial calcification (Type II) was the predominant calcification pattern (n = 548; 81%). The distributions of elongation types and calcification patterns were analogous on both the right and left sides, indicating bilateral symmetry (Table 1).

Type I (elongated) SHC was observed significantly more frequently in females (p = 0.019), whereas Type III (segmented) was more prevalent in males on the left side (p = 0.031); no significant difference was observed on the right side.

According to the calcification pattern of the SHC, Type II (partial calcification) was significantly more prevalent in males on both the right (p = 0.014) and left sides (p = 0.018). In contrast, Type IV (complete calcification) was more frequently observed in females on the right side, whereas Type III (nodular calcification) was more frequently observed in females on the left side. Overall sex-related differences in calcification pattern distribution were significant on both sides (right: p = 0.014; left: p = 0.018).

Age-related analyses revealed significant differences among elongation type categories on both sides. On the right side, individuals with Type III (segmented) elongation were significantly younger than those with Type I (elongated) and Type II (pseudo-articular) elongation (p = 0.006). On the left side, Type I (elongated) SP was associated with significantly older age than the other elongation types (p < 0.001). No significant associations were identified between age and calcification patterns on either side (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Hyoid bone morphology and lesser horn–body angle

According to the HB morphological classification, the most prevalent type was Type U (n = 105; 31%), followed by Type B (n = 98; 29%), Type D (n = 61; 18%), Type V (n = 29; 9%), Type H (n = 23; 7%), and Type HK (n = 22; 6%). Type U morphology was more frequent in males, whereas Type B predominated in females; however, no statistically significant association was observed between sex and hyoid morphology (p = 0.546).

The median lesser horn–body angle was 48.2° on the right and 49.6° on the left, with no significant bilateral difference (p = 0.151). Notably, the lesser horn–body angle of 0° was observed in 39 individuals (11.5%) on the right side and 31 individuals (9.2%) on the left side. Moderate positive correlation was observed between lesser horn–body angles (rs = 0.54, p < 0.001), indicating bilateral angular consistency. No significant association was observed between age and HB morphology (p = 0.908). Likewise, neither the right nor left lesser horn–body angle demonstrated a significant correlation with age (p = 0.548 and p = 0.368, respectively) (Table 2).

Intergroup comparisons and correlation analyses

No statistically significant differences were found between HB morphology types and SP length and HB’s lesser horn-body angle (Table 3). Although the highest lesser horn–body angle difference was observed in Type H and the lowest in Type B, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.076).

Post-hoc analysis revealed significantly lower median lengths in Type II (partial calcification) and Type IV (complete calcification) compared with Type I (calcified outline), while Type III (nodular calcification) demonstrated a significantly greater median value than Type II (Table 6). Discussion

This CBCT-based study revealed a generally bilateral symmetry in SP length, elongation type, calcification pattern, and angular measurements. No statistically significant difference was observed between the right and left sides for SP length, APA, lesser horn-body angle, and SP’s elongation type and calcification pattern. However, a statistically significant difference was identified between the right and left side in MLA.

The bilateral symmetry of the SHC indicates that embryological development is synchronized and that the craniofacial and cervical regions are adapting to the biomechanical conditions in a balanced manner. Although SP elongation was often bilateral in the present sample, the clinical manifestations of Eagle syndrome are typically unilateral [34]. This suggests that morphological symmetry may improve functional compensation and diminish symptom expression. Consequently, features such as angulation, closeness to neurovascular systems, and distinct soft tissue anatomy are likely more influential in symptomatology than SP length alone. The prevalence of SP elongation observed in the present study should be interpreted with caution, as the threshold used to define elongation remains controversial. Although a SP length exceeding 30 mm is traditionally accepted as elongated and has frequently been associated with Eagle syndrome [35], several authors have suggested alternative cut-off values of 40 mm [36–40] or even 45 mm [41]. Cadaveric studies have reported considerable variation in SP length, ranging from 15.2 to 47.7 mm, and have proposed 40 mm as a more appropriate threshold for distinguishing normal from elongated SP [42–45]. In the present study, 58.75% of SPs were classified as elongated using the conventional 30 mm criterion. However, when a more conservative threshold of 45 mm was applied, only 16.42% met the definition of elongation. These findings demonstrate that the reported prevalence of elongation is highly dependent on the selected cut-off value. Therefore, elongation defined solely by length may not necessarily indicate anatomical abnormality or clinical significance, particularly in asymptomatic individuals, and the chosen threshold should be interpreted in relation to the study population, imaging modality, and measurement methodology.

In the current study, SP length was markedly higher in males than in females on both sides. This finding is in agreement with previous CBCT studies reporting longer SPs in males [36], but contrasts with studies reporting no significant sex-related variation [46, 47]. These inconsistencies may be attributed to population differences, imaging modality, sample size, and variation in anatomical reference points used for measurement.

Previously reported MLA values range between 52.2° and 72.7° [31, 37, 44, 48, 49]. In the present study, the median MLA values were measured as 70.8° on the right and 70.2° on the left which are consistent with the range reported in the literature. Even though MLA measurements are often taken from similar anatomical landmarks, the differences in these measurements are probably due to differences in imaging methods and the use of extra measurement planes in some studies. On the contrary, APA exhibits significant heterogeneity in the literature, primarily contingent upon the chosen reference plane. Reported mean APA values differ significantly, ranging from approximately 25.6° to 93.5° when derived from the Frankfort plane or McRae line, and exceeding 90° when utilizing cranial base landmarks such as the nasion–opisthion [32, 36, 44, 48–50]. In the present study, sagittal APA median values were 61.1° (right), 61.5° (left) and there was a weak but significant positive correlation between APA and age. Similar age-related increases have been reported by Burulday et al. [51] for MLA measurements, with hypothesizing that age-related changes in ligament elasticity and connective tissue structure might influence the spatial orientation of the SHC. The observation of age-related increases in both APA and MLA measurements suggests that aging may influence different angular characteristics of the SHC. Although the observed associations were weak, such variations could be clinically relevant and warrant further investigation in future studies. Also, these variations highlight the lack of standardization in SP angulation assessment. Differences in reference landmarks, measurement planes, and imaging protocols can have a substantial impact on reported angulation values. This highlights the need for standardized measurement protocols to improve reproducibility and comparability across studies.

Type I (elongated) SHC was the most prevalent elongation type in the present study. This pattern corresponds with prior radiological studies conducted across other groups [46, 47, 52–55]. Age-related analyses further revealed that individuals with Type III (segmented) elongation were significantly younger than those with Type I (elongated) and Type II (pseudo-articular) elongation on the right side, whereas Type I elongation was associated with older age on the left side. These differences may reflect age-related progression of SHC. The predominance of segmented elongation in younger individuals and elongated type in older individuals is consistent with the possibility that discontinuous ossifications gradually develop into more continuous forms over time. Likewise, partial calcification (Type II) emerged as the predominant calcification pattern, aligning with the findings of Chu et al. [48] and Raviraj et al. [46].

A statistically significant difference was noted among calcification pattern groups concerning left-sided SP length, suggesting that calcification morphology may affect perceived SP length. No correlation was identified between the calcification pattern and the lesser horn–body angle, indicating that the calcification pattern of SHC does not significantly influence the native hyoid angular configuration.

Previous studies have shown that the morphological types of the HB vary across different populations and between genders [7, 55–58]. In the present sample, Type U and Type B hyoid morphologies were the most prevalent, consistent with previous cadaveric study of Kopuz and Ortug [8] in Anatolian population. Similarly, Kurbanova et al. [59] reported that Type B was the most common form, followed by Type U. In our study, Type U morphology was predominant in males, while Type B was more prevalent in females; however, no statistically significant correlation was observed between HB morphology and SP length, angulation, elongation type or calcification pattern.

These findings suggest that HB morphology and SHC morphometry may represent largely independent anatomical characteristics. Although no statistically significant association was found between HB morphological types and the differences in lesser horn–body angles (p = 0.076), the highest angle difference was observed in Type H and the lowest in Type B. Further studies with larger sample sizes, including broader age and sex distributions, and different populations are needed to clarify this relationship.

To the best of our knowledge, neither CBCT-based nor cadaveric studies have previously evaluated the lesser horn–body angle. The rationale for evaluating this angle was based on the anatomical attachment of the stylohyoid ligament to the lesser horn of the hyoid bone, leading to the hypothesis that variations in SHC morphology might be associated with alterations in hyoid angular configuration. Although the lesser horn–body angle demonstrated high bilateral consistency and high measurement reproducibility, its associations with the evaluated SHC characteristics were generally limited. A weak negative correlation was identified between the right MLA of the SP and the right lesser horn–body angle, suggesting a limited spatial interaction between SHC orientation and hyoid morphology. However, no consistent associations were observed with other SHC characteristics. Therefore, the present findings should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. Nevertheless, the observed bilateral consistency of the lesser horn–body angle suggests that it may represent a stable morphometric characteristic of HB anatomy. Its potential value may complement existing morphometric assessments, rather than replace them. However, its relationship with functional, developmental, or clinical variables remains unknown and warrants further investigation. Future studies involving larger populations, different ethnic groups, and functional or clinical assessments are required to determine its anatomical, biomechanical, and potential forensic relevance. In addition, validation through cadaveric investigations and studies correlating radiological and anatomical measurements may help establish the biological significance and practical applicability of this parameter.

Karaketir [30] evaluated the morphometric relationship between the SP and the HB using CT images of 180 individuals. While all linear HB measurements were significantly greater in males, SP length and angulation did not differ by sex in that study. In contrast, we found that SP lengths were significantly greater in males. The coronal angulation values were comparable in both investigations, but the sagittal angles were higher in Karaketir’s study. This may be because the cranial base was used as a reference plane. Moreover, Karaketir noted a substantial positive link between the distance from the SP to the lesser horn and the inter-lesser horn base distance, alongside a large negative correlation between SP length and HB body width. Nonetheless, our investigation revealed no significant relationships between SP and HB morphological types, despite the larger sample size and the assessment of additional morphological parameters. These differences may reflect methodological variations and population-specific anatomical characteristics.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and absence of clinical symptom data constrained the direct evaluation of the correlation between morphometric findings and Eagle syndrome. Additionally, the predominance of an Anatolian adult population may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with different ethnic or craniofacial characteristics. The predominance of females in the sample and the limited field of view in CBCT, which may result in the partial exclusion of the HB—especially in males—could have affected morphological distribution. It should also be noted that the unequal distribution of sex and HB morphological subgroups may have reduced the statistical power of certain intergroup comparisons, particularly for less frequently represented morphology types. Another limitation was the inability to anatomically identify the lesser horns in some cases, and therefore the angle was recorded as 0°. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to better understand the morphology of these cases.

Future research should utilize prospective, multi-center designs with equitable sex distribution and consistent imaging protocols. The combination of clinical symptoms, functional tests, and advanced three-dimensional or geometric morphometric analysis may provide further insight into the clinical and biomechanical importance of the interactions between the HB and the SHC.

Conclusion

The present CBCT-based study marked generally bilateral symmetry in the morphology and angulation of the SP. Males exhibited significantly greater SP length, higher MLA and APA values, while age-related differences were noted among SP elongation types. However, no corresponding association was observed between HB morphology and the morphometric parameters of the SHC.

Furthermore, the lesser horn–body angle was introduced as a novel angular parameter and demonstrated high bilateral consistency and measurement reproducibility. However, given the limited associations observed with the evaluated SHC characteristics, the present findings should be considered exploratory. Further studies are needed to determine its possible anatomical, clinical, and forensic significance.
