Bridging knowledge and practice: Adolescent sexual reproductive health in Northern Vietnam’s mountainous ethnic communities
Authors: Huyen Thi Hoa Nguyen, Anh Phuong Hoang, Giang Huong Nguyen, Tran Ngoc Tran, Ngan Thi Thuy Nguyen, Quyen Thu Do, Anh Chau Nguyen, An Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines adolescent sexual and reproductive health (ASRH) awareness, confidence, and lived experience in a mountainous area of Northern Vietnam, focusing on the impact of cultural factors and local norms. A qualitative study using semistructured interviews and focus group discussions with adolescents aged 12–18 from ethnic minority groups in two communes of Thuan Chau district and four wards of Son La city. The interviews explored their knowledge and confidence in practicing ASRH, perceptions of ASRH services, and barriers to accessing care. Data were analyzed thematically to identify key trends in ASRH awareness and behaviors. The findings reveal a critical gap between knowledge and action: although many participants knew where to access contraceptives, they lacked the confidence to use them, driven by fear of judgment from family, peers, and healthcare providers. This gap is widened by deeply ingrained cultural norms, including traditional gender roles and expectations of early childbearing, which severely limit the decision-making autonomy of adolescents, particularly girls from ethnic minority communities. Addressing ASRH challenges in this context requires interventions that move beyond simple information dissemination. Efforts must focus on building adolescent confidence by developing youth-friendly, confidential ASRH services and implementing community-level interventions aimed at transforming the restrictive cultural beliefs and social stigma that hinder safe ASRH practices.Keywords: adolescent health, reproductive health, contraception, cultural characteristics, rural health services, Vietnam

INTRODUCTION

Currently, sexual activity of adolescents varies markedly by region and gender; in Latin America and the Caribbean, more boys are sexually active than girls, while the opposite is true in sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, and Central Asia (Chandra-Mouli et al. 2014, Ababor et al. 2019, Clark et al. 2020). Early sexual debut increases the risk of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV, and can result in unintended pregnancy and early childbearing (Salam et al. 2016). It is, therefore, critical for countries to engage with this significant portion of the population and be able to address their health needs (Atuyambe et al. 2015). Addressing adolescent sexual and reproductive health (ASRH) needs is now recognized as a global public health priority, embedded within the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality), and reinforced by the Global Strategy for Women’s, Children’s and Adolescents’ Health (2016–2030) (World Health Organization 2015, 2018a, 2022). These frameworks call for comprehensive, evidence-based interventions to ensure adolescents can access the information, services, and support necessary to safeguard their health and well-being.

Meeting the ASRH needs of adolescents and young people (AYP) aged 10–24 is a global public health challenge (UNAIDS 2016, World Health Organization 2018b), which requires increased investments in innovative strategies to improve the acceptability and accessibility of services. Despite global attention, low- and middle-income countries continue to face significant ASRH challenges. Recent studies reveal that many adolescents perceive themselves as mature enough for sexual activity yet remain inadequately informed about the risks of unprotected sex (Moreira et al. 2023). For instance, a 2023 qualitative investigation found that youths lack essential knowledge regarding STIs and related consequences, despite engaging in early sexual behavior (Ventura-Miranda et al. 2023). This knowledge deficit is compounded by persistently weak ASRH information, with schools and health systems often failing to deliver comprehensive, adolescent-appropriate education (Janighorban et al. 2022, Sidamo et al. 2024).

Despite global support and attention, the historical lack of national policy focus and investment in ASRH has exposed adolescents to risky sexual practices and early parenthood in developing countries (Morris and Rushwan 2015, Achala et al. 2025, Ibingira et al. 2025). Globally, about 13 million women aged 15–19 years give birth annually, of which pregnancy and childbirth-related deaths are the primary cause of women’s death (Ababor et al. 2019). In a paper published in 2019, 23% of girls aged 15–19 years were already mothers or pregnant with their first child in Uganda (Ochen et al. 2019). In Southeast Asia, the adolescent fertility rate stands at 43 births per 1000 girls (15–19 years old), compared to 26 in South Asia and 7 in East Asia (UNFPA 2021). Particularly in Vietnam, the adolescent fertility rate was 27.4 births per 100 women (15–19 years old) in 2019, with higher rates observed in the Northern Midlands and Mountains and the Central Highlands [General Statistics Office of Viet Nam (GSO) 2020]. These figures indicate that a growing number of adolescents are becoming sexually active before marriage, which in turn contributes to a higher rate of unplanned pregnancies in this age group—particularly among those with unmet needs for contraceptives.

Efforts to engage AYP and increase their access to services have previously been made in mountainous areas in Northern Vietnam. Son La, which is a mountainous province in the northwest highlands of Vietnam, comprises 12 ethnic groups. This region has harmful social customs and practices such as bride kidnapping, and child marriages, and women do not have the right to decide on their future family (Nam 2021). Knowledge gap exists in addressing access to ASRH services to populations, particularly ethnic minority adolescents in Vietnam’s northern mountainous regions, despite the increase in demand and challenges (Nguyen 2024). Reproductive health services for adolescents in mountainous areas of this province are limited, difficult to access, and of low quality. Reproductive health consultations and services for adolescents are neglected by health workers with a lack of comprehensive education in rural and remote communities (Burns et al. 2024). Besides, little is known about reproductive health perceptions in Son La province or the barriers to effective interventions. Therefore, we selected Son La as a locality in the 4-year intervention project, titled “Improving reproductive health & preventing child marriage in Son La Province, period 2021–2025” conducted by HealthBridge—a nongovernmental organization in six communes of this province where adolescents are at risk of unintended pregnancy and early childbearing. This leads to adverse health outcomes, school dropout, and long-term social and economic challenges. This study was part of the formative phase of the larger project, designed to inform project initiatives. This study aimed to investigate the knowledge and confidence in practicing sexual reproductive health among adolescents in Son La province. Data for this specific study were collected before the full implementation of educational and direct support activities for the adolescent participants within these specific communes and wards. Therefore, the participants had not yet been direct beneficiaries of the project’s core educational interventions at the time of data collection.

MATERIALS and METHODS

Study design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative approach to explore the knowledge, confidence, and lived experience of adolescents regarding ASRH. Given the limited prior research on the topic, a qualitative approach was chosen for its ability to generate rich, detailed data that complementary to quantitative findings carried out under the umbrella of the project “Improving reproductive health & preventing child marriage in Son La Province, period 2021–2025,” might allow more individuals’ experiences and insights (Creswell and Poth 2018; Liamputtong 2020).

Settings and timeframe

Data collection took place in March 2022, in two communes of Thuan Chau district and four periurban wards of Son La city. These sites were selected for their ethnic diversity and their relevance to the project’s geographic focus on underserved, mountainous communities. Son La is home to several ethnic minority groups and faces disproportionately high rates of adolescent pregnancy and early marriage.

Participants

Participants were school-going adolescents aged 12–18 years, including both male and female students from three ethnic groups: Thai, Mong, and Kinh. To be eligible, participants had to be (i) currently enrolled in secondary or high school, (ii) be residents of the selected areas, and (iii) be able to speak and understand Vietnamese or the local dialect. With participants who preferred to use their local language, interpreters were provided. These translators were instructed on research ethics and the importance of accurate and nonleading translation to ensure inclusivity and data quality.

Recruitment and sample size

Participants were recruited through a convenience sampling method, with assistance from local schools and community leaders. Although random sampling was not feasible due to logistical constraints, the sample was intended to reflect the demographic diversity of the region. After being briefed about the study, interested students were provided with information sheets and consent forms for their parents or guardians to review and sign.

The final sample consisted of 15 students, including a balanced representation of genders and ethnic groups common in the region. The sample size was determined based on the richness and depth of information provided by participants while following the principle of saturation, where data collection continued until no new themes or insights emerged (Creswell and Poth 2018, Liamputtong 2020), while also in line with previous publications in the area (Frederick et al. 2019, Isaksen et al. 2022).

Given the age of the participants, informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian before the students were invited to participate in the study. Students also needed to express a willingness to participate and be available during the data collection period, serving as their assent.

Interview process

Data were collected using semistructured individual interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs). The interview guide was developed by the lead researcher, drawing on literature and project objectives, and refined through three pilot interviews and consultations with qualitative experts and local educators. Six individual interviews were conducted to capture private, personal experiences that participants may not have felt comfortable sharing in a group setting. Three FGDs were organized to elicit shared experiences and social dynamics related to ASRH topics. These included both mixed-gender and single-gender groups, depending on participant comfort and local norms (Guest et al. 2017).

Individual interviews and FGDs lasted ∼30–45 min each and were conducted in quiet, private spaces within schools or community centers to ensure confidentiality and comfort. Interviewers were trained in adolescent-friendly and culturally sensitive communication. While some participants initially expressed hesitation, particularly around sensitive topics, most opened up after reassurances about confidentiality and respect for their views.

Data analysis

The recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim in Vietnamese, with all identifiable information removed to maintain participant confidentiality. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussions between the first and second authors, and a consensus was reached to maintain consistency. A portion of the transcripts was reviewed by an external qualitative expert to validate the accuracy and consistency of the analysis. The analysis followed Krippendorff’s content analysis approach (Krippendorff 2004), which involved several interconnected stages that transformed raw transcription data into meaningful research findings. Decontextualization was the initial stage where meaning units such as words, phrases, or paragraphs were systematically identified from the transcriptions based on their frequency and relevance to the research questions. Following this identification process, recontextualization took place, during which these meaning units were condensed while maintaining focus on the key context of each response to preserve the essential meaning without unnecessary detail. The condensed data then underwent subcategorization, where similar thematic elements were grouped together into subcategories based on their shared characteristics and conceptual relationships. Finally, through compilation, these subcategories were further refined and consolidated into broader key categories that ultimately formed the foundational structure for the study’s findings and conclusions (Fig. 1).

Ethical implications

This study was conducted under the broader project “Improving Reproductive Health & Preventing Child Marriage in Son La Province, period 2021–2025,” which was approved by HealthBridge Vietnam in December 2017 (project code: VN02-011) and the Son La Provincial People’s Committee (decision no. 433/QĐ-UBND). Ethical approval was also obtained from Vinmec Institutional Ethical Review Board (ethics approval no. 33/2022/QĐ-VMEC) to ensure compliance with both Vietnamese and international research standards. In addition to obtaining informed consent from parents or legal guardians, cultural norms were carefully considered to ensure that discussions about ASRH were conducted in a culturally sensitive manner, especially in communities where such topics remain stigmatized. We did this by using language and terminology aligned with local values and norms and involved trained facilitators familiar with local customs.

Availability of data

The qualitative data supporting the findings of this study are not publicly available due to privacy and ethical considerations. Access to the data will be considered on a case-by-case basis, in alignment with ethical guidelines and the need to protect participant confidentiality.

RESULTS

Understanding of adolescent sexual and reproductive health

Adolescents demonstrated a basic awareness of ASRH concepts, particularly in relation to early childbearing and unprotected sex, which they associated with adverse health and social consequences. However, their understanding was often fragmented and shaped by school-based messages and informal community knowledge.

These descriptions of knowledge were largely declarative; adolescents could name risks and methods but often lacked procedural detail (how to obtain or correctly use methods) and contextual guidance (how to negotiate use within relationships and community settings).

An 18-year-old female (Kinh) noted a visible shift in attitudes toward early marriage in her peer group:

“When I was in secondary school, many friends in grades 7 and 8 (around 12 and 13 years old) were married and had children. But in high school, I didn’t see any friends like that anymore. So, I think my friends know that getting married early has many disadvantages.”

This participant’s observation points to perceived generational change: adolescents notice shifting norms but rarely link those changes to concrete reasons or to personal strategies for avoiding early marriage or pregnancy.

Several students emphasized the importance of consent, protection, and readiness in sexual relationships. A 17-year-old female (Thai) explained:

“I think girls nowadays, even after going through puberty and being physically healthy, still have every right to refuse sexual intercourse if they do not want to and are not prepared with safe practices.”

Complementing this view, a 17-year-old high school boy (Kinh) added:

“Sexual intercourse is not a representation of love. Only if both parties are of legal age and can use contraceptive methods as taught by their teachers, then it may prevent serious consequences.”

Although these statements express normative ideals (consent, mutual responsibility), several participants later described difficulties putting these ideals into practice, revealing a gap between stated beliefs and perceived ability to act. A 16-year-old female (Mong) shared in a focus group:

“We have learned about this at school and also read about it on various websites. If we were to get pregnant at this age, our bodies are still not fully developed. How could we carry a pregnancy and take care of a baby? I think it would be impossible and very challenging, and it would also affect our families.”

Knowledge of contraceptive methods such as condoms, pills, and intrauterine devices (IUDs) was commonly reported. A 15-year-old female (Kinh) said:

“I find that condoms are common and easy to buy. Occasionally, I see teachers introducing them during sex education sessions, and they are also available at local health stations.”

Another student (16-year-old female, Thai) observed contraceptive use among adult women in her village:

“Occasionally, I see my mother and other women talking about going to the health station to get an IUD (intrauterine device) or buying contraceptive pills if they want to avoid unintended pregnancies or unsafe sexual practices.”

These vignettes show that adolescents’ knowledge draws from multiple sources (school lessons, online material, observation of adults), creating a hybrid information environment in which accurate facts coexist with gaps in practical know-how.

When asked where they would seek help for ASRH concerns, most students cited parents and teachers as their primary sources of support. For example, a 14-year-old female (Mong) shared: “I usually share issues about puberty with my mother, as well as with my teachers. After talking with my teachers, I feel very comfortable.” Similarly, a 15-year-old male (Kinh) recalled: “Once, a classmate of mine experienced intense menstrual pain and heavy bleeding just as class was ending. We were too nervous to take her to the hospital, so we sought out our teachers to get medicine and assist her.” These accounts illustrate that teachers are often viewed as trusted and approachable figures, not only for academic matters but also for sensitive health concerns. Trust in teachers therefore functions as an important, culturally acceptable channel for immediate support, but students nonetheless distinguish between general health help (e.g. menstrual pain) and more stigmatized ASRH needs (e.g. contraception), where they are less likely to seek adult help. The willingness to approach them reflects the strong role of the school environment in providing immediate and culturally acceptable ASRH assistance—particularly in communities where seeking help from formal health services may be hindered by stigma or lack of confidence.

Barriers in practicing safe adolescent sexual and reproductive health

Hesitation in purchasing and using contraceptives

While students demonstrated foundational knowledge, many expressed low confidence in applying this knowledge—particularly regarding contraceptive use. Fear of judgment from adults, peers, and community members was a significant deterrent.

In a focus group, a group of 16–18-year-old mixed-genders (Thai and Kinh) hesitated before admitting:

“We are afraid people will judge when we go to the pharmacy to buy condoms.”

Participants described the pharmacy encounter as a microsocial test: in small communities, the pharmacist is known, pharmacy visits are visible, and those social logistics (who sees you, who knows your family) frequently deter purchase even when methods are available.

Consistent with the experiences shared earlier, participants in this study reported feeling uncomfortable and lacking confidence in accessing and using contraceptive methods, even though they were aware of their availability. An 18-year-old girl (Kinh) said:

“I know about it because our teachers briefly mentioned it during lessons, and I occasionally read about it online. However, I’ve never received any formal guidance on how to use contraceptive methods properly, and I’m not confident that I know how to use all of them correctly.”

This emphasizes a procedural knowledge gap: knowing that methods exist is not the same as having been taught or practiced how to obtain and use them confidentially, which undermines self-efficacy. Besides, the adolescents’ readiness to access the available healthcare services of ASRH was not shown among the participants. Although both secondary and high school boys and girls possessed basic information about where to seek help, they were still shy and worried about asking about ASRH issues:

“I understand that health stations and pharmacies sell condoms, but I’ve never bought them and feel uncomfortable asking. I’ve also noticed that some pregnant girls hesitate to seek help from healthcare workers.” (15-year-old male, Mong)

Age, gender, and community tightness modified this hesitation: younger adolescents and girls more often described avoidance, and participants from more tightly knit villages emphasized reputational risk as a stronger deterrent.

Reliance on unreliable sources due to fear of stigma

While many participants described positive relationships with teachers, several also revealed that they avoided asking sensitive questions in class for fear of punishment or embarrassment. As a result, they often turned to the internet:

“I’m afraid of being scolded by teachers or possibly punished for asking questions outside the lesson content if I inquire about topics like sex and reproductive health. When we need information, my friends and I search on Google.” (14-year-old female, Kinh)

Turning to online sources was framed as a safety strategy: anonymity reduces immediate social risk, but it also exposes adolescents to variable-quality information and removes opportunities for corrective, face-to-face guidance. The fear of stigma deters adolescents from accessing accurate information and prevents them from forming a strong foundation of knowledge that is essential for making informed decisions about their sexual health. This preference reflects not a rejection of adult guidance, but a trade-off between accuracy and anonymity:

“We don’t always know what’s true or not online, but it’s easier than asking someone in person. At least online, no one knows what you’re looking up.” (16-year-old male, Thai)

This trade-off creates information ecology where privacy is prioritized over accuracy, leaving adolescents reliant on peer-circulated or algorithmically surfaced content that may lack practical relevance.

Cultural norms and adolescent sexual and reproductive health decision-making

Influence of traditional beliefs on adolescent sexual and reproductive health behaviors

In several communities, early sexual activity was normalized as a romantic rite of passage, disconnected from health risks. A 13-year-old male (Mong) reflected:

“In my village, some peers who are the same age as I have started engaging in sexual activity at an early age. They say that sexual intercourse is a sign of love and that pregnancy is a natural outcome of love.”

Among Mong participants, traditional dating practices remained strong:

“It’s believed that sexual relations are a sign of true love. Young people, around 16 or 17 years old, work in the fields during the day, and in the evenings, boys from the village spend time with girls. They rarely go to health stations to get medicine or buy condoms.” (17-year-old female, Mong)

These accounts show how descriptive norms (what peers do) can normalize risky practices even when adolescents are aware of health risks; in parallel, injunctive norms (what elders expect) may discourage protective behaviors, producing mixed signals for young people. It can be suggested that community norms—not outdated education—shape many adolescents’ understanding of sexual relationships. Locally embedded rituals and peer scripts therefore play a strong role in shaping behavior, often overriding school-based messages that lack local resonance.

Limited decision-making power for women in adolescent sexual and reproductive health matters

Girls, especially from minority communities, described a lack of autonomy in reproductive decision-making, often influenced by family expectations and gender roles:

“In my village, women are expected to have children early. If they use contraception and then can’t conceive later, it’s seen as their fault.” (16-year-old female, Mong)

Male authority figures—fathers, husbands, and elders—frequently controlled reproductive decisions:

“The men in my village decide when to have children. Women don’t really get to say when they’re ready.” (15-year-old female, Thai)

These testimonies point to structural and interpersonal mechanisms (patriarchal decision-making, reputational pressures, fertility anxieties) that limit girls’ reproductive autonomy and increase the social cost of seeking contraception. In some cases where traditional gender norms dominate, even discussion of ASRH was sometimes viewed with suspicion:

“If you talk about contraception or sexual health, people think you’re encouraging girls to disobey their families or to be reckless.” (17-year-old female, Kinh)

This reflects not direct resistance to formal programs, but deep-seated concerns that ASRH education could undermine traditional values, especially around female obedience and modesty. Consequently, interventions focused solely on information provision risk being rejected or ignored unless they also engage with family and community norms and create safe, confidential pathways for girls to act.

Comparison between in-depth interviews and focus group discussions

Clear distinctions emerged in the type and depth of information shared across the two methods. In-depth interviews (IDIs) often elicited more personal and emotionally nuanced narratives, particularly on sensitive experiences such as romantic relationships, contraception use, and family expectations. For example, a 16-year-old female (Mong) disclosed in an interview:

“When my boyfriend wanted to have sex, I didn’t know how to refuse. I was scared to tell anyone in my family because they would be angry.”

IDIs therefore revealed private constraints, emotions, and incidents of coercion or fear that participants were unwilling to disclose publicly. In contrast, FGDs tended to generate collective accounts, socially acceptable narratives, and discussion of broader community norms. Group settings encouraged participants to share experiences framed in general terms, sometimes as “what others do” rather than personal experiences. For example, during a mixed-gender FGD, a 17-year-old male (Thai) commented:

“Some people here think it’s fine for teenagers to be together at night. They don’t tell us to use anything; it’s just normal.”

Notably, sensitive topics, such as fear of stigma when purchasing contraceptives, were more candidly expressed in IDIs, whereas FGDs were more effective at surfacing shared cultural expectations and peer dynamics. This methodological complementarity allowed the study to capture both individual-level barriers and community-level influences on ASRH.

By combining IDIs and FGDs, the research team was able to contrast private experiences (revealed in interviews) with public narratives (revealed in groups), which helps explain why stated knowledge and community norms do not always translate into individual protective action.

DISCUSSION

This study provided an overview of the knowledge, perception, and confidence in practicing ASRH in a mountainous area of Vietnam. In general, the adolescents appeared to possess a basic knowledge about ASRH, yet they lacked the confidence to perform proper actions, which is mainly related to cultural and local factors. The discussion is divided into three main themes drawn from the findings including awareness and preventative understanding of ASRH, confidence gap, and the influence of sociocultural norms and stigma.

Although participants demonstrated some awareness of the harms of child marriage, unprotected intercourse, and unintended pregnancy, their understanding was often superficial. For instance, they recognized that early marriage was disadvantageous, but the specific health, social, or economic consequences were argued. Likewise, while they knew condoms were available, many lacked confidence in using them correctly. This gap likely reflects limited, inconsistent information provided during school and community health education sessions—a pattern also reported in rural and mountainous contexts in Vietnam and internationally (Nguyen et al. 2024). While awareness campaigns may have succeeded in disseminating basic messages, they have not yet equipped adolescents with the comprehensive, practical knowledge needed for confident decision-making. Interventions must therefore target both informational and psychosocial dimensions of adolescent health literacy. Several methods to improve these sessions have been highlighted in previous literature, including the utilization of real-life situations and inclusion of small-group discussions and other active learning applications to enhance engagement (Van Hung et al. 2019). Furthermore, other methods of ASRH education than education sessions, such as having reading materials on this topic in schools or public libraries, should be employed to ensure comprehensive and multifaceted exposure to ASRH knowledge in this population (Ha and Fisher 2011).

The most significant barrier identified in this study was the gap between what adolescents know and what they feel confident enough to do. Adolescents in this study appeared to know where to seek help for basic ASRH issues, such as locations to seek available contraceptive methods. This is similar to findings in another study exploring the perceptions and barriers toward ASRH services accessibility, availability, and quality among adolescents in the second cities of Rwanda, most adolescents reported to be aware of ASRH services provided at health facilities (Ndayishimiye et al. 2020). The role of teacher and parental support is extremely crucial in facilitating safe ASRH practices and has been highlighted in literature (Leekuan et al. 2022). However, in a different study conducted in Sri Lanka, adolescents were usually hesitant to seek help, and they commonly discussed their sexual health concerns with friends instead of teachers or healthcare providers (Agampodi et al. 2008). This finding also reflects a prevalent characteristic of adolescents in our study, highlighting an issue of hesitation of adolescents using and accessing health services due to cultural barriers, confidence, and/or lack of information (Ndayishimiye et al. 2020). In a close-knit community, this fear is amplified, making actions such as buying condoms at a pharmacy feel like a public declaration of sexual activity. The resulting reliance on unreliable online sources is a consequence of this confidence deficit. The issue is therefore a lack of information, along with a lack of safe, confidential, and nonjudgmental spaces where adolescents can ask questions and access services. This suggests the development and promotion of confidential youth-friendly health services that are staffed by providers trained to be nonjudgmental, thereby reducing the social risk that currently deters access. Future health education campaigns and community intervention programs should focus on empowering adolescents to effectively utilize the proper reproductive health services (McCuistian et al. 2023). In order to empower and encourage adolescents to actively seek ASRH services per their demand, many parties are to be involved, including parents, teachers, and ASRH service providers (Le and Yu 2021, Huang et al. 2022, Lewis et al. 2022). Coordination between these parties is crucial in providing a safe space for young people to practice their reproductive rights to ASRH services.

In the last decade, in developing countries, a significant reason for ASRH issues among adolescents was the lack of knowledge and information on this topic (Ababor et al. 2019). Findings in this study suggest that adolescents in this area have experienced a dual challenge. Adolescents’ understanding, while present, is often incomplete, and this is compounded by a profound lack of confidence to act. This lack of confidence exceeds individual attributes and is integrated with pervasive cultural norms and the nature of the ASRH information they receive and can serve as a factor for ASRH complications. In remote areas where minority ethnic groups have been inhabitants for decades, cultural norms (the expectation of early childbearing, traditional gender roles that limit female autonomy in ASRH decision-making, and the strong social stigma surrounding premarital sexual activity and accessing contraceptives) exist as a foundation affecting their perceived knowledge and readiness to take action. The perception of early sexual activity as a natural outcome of “love” and the expectation for women to prioritize childbearing over their own reproductive choices create an environment where ASRH is not viewed through a health lens, but through a social and cultural one. This severely limits the autonomy of adolescent girls, rendering their personal knowledge or preferences secondary to familial and partner expectations. Pressure is placed on women to conform to traditional expectations of fertility and motherhood, which are often intertwined with a family’s social standing and community norms (Lappegård et al. 2021). This lack of decision-making capacity of ethnic minority women is reflected in previous studies, as autonomy decisions on sexual intercourse and contraceptive use are significantly lower among ethnic women compared to Kinh women (UNFPA Vietnam). Consequently, adolescents possess basic awareness of ASRH issues, such as the availability of contraceptives; they hesitate to act on this knowledge due to fear of judgment from family, peers, and even healthcare providers. The “lack of information” thus transforms from a simple absence of facts into a lack of access to actionable, confidential, and nonjudgmental guidance that would empower them to translate knowledge into confident practice. For example, knowing that condoms exist is different from feeling confident enough to purchase them, negotiate their use, or use them correctly, especially when cultural narratives implicitly discourage such proactive behaviors among young, unmarried individuals.

From this, further research should (i) design, implement, and evaluate operational strategies that address the sociocultural and economic drivers affecting ASRH service utilization, specifically focusing on reducing sociocultural barriers such as stigma and traditional gender norms for adolescents in Son La province; (ii) focus on empowering adolescents, particularly girls from ethnic minorities, in executing their ASRH rights and seeking help from parents, teachers, and healthcare professionals through gender-sensitive educational programs and the establishment of youth-friendly, confidential ASRH services; (iii) work to remove existing unscientific beliefs and outdated norms among local residents regarding sexual and reproductive health issues, promoting community dialogues that involve elders and leaders to foster a more supportive environment for ASRH; and (iv) evaluate the effectiveness of multistakeholder approaches in improving adolescent confidence in accessing and utilizing available contraceptive methods.

The study has several limitations that should be considered. The perspectives of adolescents not enrolled in school, who may face different or more severe ASRH challenges, were not captured. Given the sensitive nature of ASRH, social desirability bias may have influenced some participant responses, although we sought to mitigate this by assuring confidentiality and building rapport.

CONCLUSION

Adolescents from the mountainous areas of Son La province in Northern Vietnam showed their awareness of common local ASRH issues and their consequences. However, they faced some limiting barriers to accessing ASRH services such as inadequate knowledge, lack of self-efficacy, resource insufficiencies, and cultural norms. Interventions should combine confidence-building through peer-led activities with culturally sensitive education modified to ethnic communities. Training for school staff and health workers in adolescent communication is also essential. Community-based feedback systems could empower adolescents to codevelop responsive, localized solutions. These findings may inform similar interventions in comparable mountainous regions, though local contextual factors should be carefully considered.

The qualitative data supporting the findings of this study are not publicly available due to privacy and ethical considerations. Access to the data will be considered on a case-by-case basis, in alignment with ethical guidelines and the need to protect participant confidentiality.

The data underlying this article cannot be shared publicly due to the privacy of individuals that participated in the study and the funder’s request. The data will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author with the permission of HealthBridge Vietnam.
