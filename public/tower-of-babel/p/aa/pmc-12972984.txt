Targeting VEGFR2 inhibition within a spatially-confined conduit promotes nerve self-resolution and alleviates mechanical allodynia
Authors: Xiaobin Luo, Toshitatsu Nagayasu, Yuhao Zhang, Zhi He, Jia Yang, Chengli Li, Guman Duan, Qunchuan Zong, Yun Liu, Kaiyuan Yang, Kaige Mao, Pengchao Ma, Zifan Li, Yating Zhao, KunKoo Kim, Jie Zhang, Zide Wang, Yuyang Luo, Jingsong Lu, Sengpav Tong, Jia-kuo Yu, Zhe Zhao, Zheng Cao, Shuhui Yang, Yongwei Pan, Xiumei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStump neuromas represent nonfunctional redundant tissue that frequently elicits neuropathic pain through disorganized axonal regeneration, pathological angiogenesis, scar hyperplasia, and chronic neuroinflammation. Current therapeutic strategies fail to adequately address the underlying pathophysiology, particularly the excessive production of reactive oxygen species resulting from dysregulated vascular proliferation and activation. To tackle this issue, we developed photo-crosslinked gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) microspheres covalently functionalized with methacryloyl-modified anti-VEGFR2 peptides (MAVP) to modulate pathological angiogenesis. These functionalized microspheres were fabricated into linear arrays via 3D printing and integrated within a progressively spatial-constrictive conduit. This platform facilitated the self-resolution of the nerve end interface, including sustained suppression of VEGFR2 phosphorylation and coordinated mechanotransduction signaling, thereby inhibiting neuroinflammatory responses and angiogenesis. In a sciatic nerve ligation model, GelMAMAVP MPs system demonstrated markedly superior analgesic efficacy over the conventional VEGFR2 inhibitor vandetanib. In a stump neuroma model, GelMAMAVP MPs effectively normalized the peripheral end interface microenvironment by inhibiting neovascularization, M1 macrophage polarization, and fibrotic scar formation. Furthermore, GelMAMAVP MPs distributed in the peripheral nerve stumps indirectly downregulated pain-related proteins (TRPA1/CGRP) in dorsal root ganglia and suppressed spinal microglial activation. Overall, this study presents a comprehensive and safe vascular-targeted strategy promoting nerve end interface self-resolution and prevention of neuropathic pain.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve neuromas represent nonfunctional redundant tissue that form through aberrant nerve regeneration following traumatic injury [1]. The pathological process involves uncontrolled proliferation of myofibroblast and disorganized axonal sprouting [1], further compounded by neovascularization and scar tissue formation that collectively hinder proper nerve repair [2,3]. The maladaptive nerve regeneration leads to chronic mechanical allodynia, characterized by pathological hypersensitivity to normally non-nociceptive stimuli [4,5]. Current therapeutic strategies for neuroma management encompass both pharmacological interventions (e.g., oral analgesics, regional nerve blocks) [6] and surgical approaches (e.g., targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) [7,8], embedding the regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI) within muscle [9], and protecting nerve stumps using nerve caps). Nerve caps, typically composed of bioabsorbable materials, have been shown to reduce the recurrence rate of neuromas and alleviate neuropathic pain responses both in animal [10,11] and clinical studies [12]. Nevertheless, an optimal therapeutic solution for neuromas remains elusive. In our previous study, a progressively spatial-constrictive conduit composed of poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) and mineralized collagen (MC) was shown to effectively decelerate axonal regeneration while maintaining aligned orientation of spontaneously regrowing axons, thereby preventing neuroma formation [5]. Building on this physical guidance strategy, we have now incorporated biologically targeted peptides to further refine axonal regeneration and improve therapeutic specificity. These peptides are designed to selectively inhibit vascular endothelial cell proliferation, thereby suppressing angiogenesis and neuroinflammation.

Researches indicated that pathological angiogenesis can create an oxidative stress microenvironment, thereby inducing excessive ROS production and contributing to neuropathic pain [13,14]. The ROS expression at a physiological level is essential for neuronal differentiation, polarization, synapse maturation, and neurotransmission [15], however, ROS overexpression disrupts Schwann cell (SC) proliferation and differentiation, thereby compromising axonal remyelination and impeding nerve regeneration [16]. Additionally, ROS perpetuate chronic inflammation by activating pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades and microglial cells, prolonging the inflammatory phase and delaying the transition from inflammatory to reparative microenvironment [17]. This persistent inflammation induces secondary damage, further hindering nerve regeneration [18]. Following peripheral nervous system trauma, vascular endothelial cells undergo oxidative stress-medicated dysregulation, leading to pathological ROS overproduction [19]. Crucially, this process is driven primarily by uncontrolled vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) upregulation in vascular endothelium, which promotes aberrant angiogenesis and exacerbates the inflammatory milieu [[20], [21], [22]].

VEGFR2 targeting inhibitors have been shown to significantly relieve inflammatory infiltration, which thereby mediates pain syndromes such as cystitis [23] and osteoarthritis [24]. Researches have suggested that inhibiting angiogenesis may be helpful in suppressing the formation of neuroma [25]. However, most VEGFR2 inhibitors are known to have fast drug metabolisms and both physical and medical side effects when administered systemically such as oral and intravenous routes [26]. Systematic research of VEGFR2 inhibition specifically for neuromas via a viable strategy is currently lacking. Consequently, the effective treatment of neuroma-induced allodynia with VEGFR2 inhibitors requires the development of sustained-release delivery systems that can maintain stable drug concentrations throughout the prolonged neuroregenerative process.

In this study, we developed photo-crosslinked gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) microspheres covalently functionalized with methacryloyl-modified anti-VEGFR2 peptides (MAVP) to modulate pathological angiogenesis via the delivery and sustained release of MAVP. These functionalized microspheres (GelMAMAVP MPs) were fabricated into linear arrays via 3D printing and integrated within a progressively spatial-constrictive conduit (PCL@MC). The conjugation of MA to anti-VEGFR2 peptide (AVP) is designed to enable covalent cross-linking with GelMA MPs. This strategy aims to preserve bioactivity while creating a sustained release system, thereby extending the functional duration of AVP in vivo. To evaluate the effect of vascular-targeted inhibition via functionalized microspheres (GelMAMAVP MPs) on pain behavior, we employed a classic partial sciatic nerve ligation (pSNL) pain model. Mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia were assessed using von-Frey filament testing and thermal assays, respectively, while the suppressive effect on neuroinflammation was also examined. Additionally, we utilized a stump neuroma model to investigate the inhibitory effect of GelMAMAVP MPs-loaded confinement conduit on both neuroma formation and neuropathic pain. In addition, the interplay mechanisms among vascular pathological regeneration in the stump nerve, neuroinflammation, and neuropathic pain were also investigated.

Results

Biosafety and functional activity assessment of GelMAMAVP MPs

The morphological characteristics of GelMA MPs both pre- and post-UV crosslinking were observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging, as shown in Fig. 1A. Size distribution analysis showed that more than 80% GelMA MPs fell within the 51-70 μm range (Fig. 1B). Cell viability assessments showed the excellent biocompatibility, with 7-day live/dead staining outcomes confirming robust survival of both rat Schwann cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Fig. 1C). As demonstrated in Fig. 1D, both GelMA MPs and GelMAMAVP MPs constructs maintained cell viability exceeding 95%, confirming that neither the carrier system nor the anti-VEGFR2 peptide components exhibited significant cytotoxicity.

The rhodamine-phalloidin cytoskeletal staining of HUVECs (Fig. 1E) revealed that 10 μM MAVP showed no significant reduction in cell number, with cellular viability comparable to the untreated control (Fig. 1F). Notably, 30 μM MAVP exhibited marked anti-proliferative effects, showing statistically distinct differences from both control and 100 μM MAVP treatments (p < 0.05), thereby demonstrating a concentration-dependent anti-proliferative response. Further validation via scratch wound healing assays (Fig. 1G) confirmed the inhibitory effects of MAVP on HUVEC migration, with dose-dependent reduction in wound closure rates (Fig. 1H). Notably, parallel assays using PC12 neural cells revealed no significant impairment of migration by MAVP treatment, demonstrating selective action on endothelial cells (Fig. S1).

Quantification of ROS generation in oxidatively stressed HUVECs was performed using DCFH-DA fluorescence imaging (Fig. 1I). Both MAVP and VAN treatment groups demonstrated significant attenuation of ROS production compared to vehicle (PBS) controls, with MAVP exhibiting superior efficacy in reducing both ROS+ cell population (Fig. 1J) and mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1K), indicating its potent antioxidant capacity. To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, real-time calcium imaging during chemical stimulation on live cells were performed (Fig. 1L). Allyl isothiocyanate (AITC)-induced pain simulation triggered maximal GFP (Ca2+) fluorescence intensity of RSCs (Fig. 1M) and the highest number of calcium-responsive cells (Fig. 1N). Particularly, both MAVP and VAN treatments substantially attenuated calcium transients, while MAVP achieved the most pronounced pain signal suppression, suggesting its ability to mitigate pathological pain signaling without inducing hyperalgesia side effects.

3D-bioprinting characteristics of GelMAMAVP MPs

It was found that the absorption rate of water by GelMA MPs exceeds 12 times of that of dry GelMA (Fig. S2). Rheological analysis under frequency sweeps (0.01–1.0 Hz) revealed distinct viscoelastic properties of GelMA MPs and GelMAMAVP MPs composites. The GelMA MPs system exhibited storage moduli (G′) of 998.1 Pa and loss moduli (G″) of 216.6 Pa, whereas GelMAMAVP MPs demonstrated enhanced mechanical parameters with G' = 1790.2 Pa and G'' = 242.4 Pa (Fig. 2A). Both formulations maintained stable viscoelastic profiles throughout testing, with G′ consistently exceeding G″, indicative of preserved structural integrity under dynamic loading without fragmentation. Furthermore, the elevation in G′ for GelMAMAVP MPs relative to GelMA MPs confirms its superior mechanical resilience to external stresses. The shear thinning test was used to detect the nonlinear rheological properties of GelMAMAVP MPs when the shear rate increases from 10/s to 100/s (Fig. 2B). The result implied that the consistency of GelMAMAVP MPs was suitable for extrusion 3D printing. Moreover, the results of cyclic step-strain time sweep tests demonstrated that GelMAMAVP MPs hydrogel possesses self-healing abilities at a microscale (Fig. 2C). Fig. 2D illustrates the impact of variations in extrusion pressure and printing speed on the 3D printing performance of GelMAMAVP MPs. The results showed that at extrusion pressures of 15 kPa, 20 kPa and 25 kPa, higher pressure resulted in a larger printed diameter of the GelMAMAVP MPs hydrogel. Conversely, increasing the printing speed led to a gradual decrease in hydrogel diameter (Fig. 2E). This approach enables precise control over the printing of GelMA hydrogel microspheres, allowing for their accurate alignment within 2 mm-diameter nerve conduits. Moreover, it facilitates the straightforward fabrication of GelMA hydrogel patterns in various other geometries, thereby accommodating a broad range of future application needs (Fig. 2F).

Targeting anti-angiogenesis test of GelMAMAVP MPs

The VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling axis serves as a critical pathway for vascular regeneration, with VEGF165 being the predominant isoform utilized in VEGFA-related studies [27]. Thus, to evaluate the targeted anti-angiogenic effects of MAVP on HUVECs, VEGF165 was selected as the stimulus. Rhodamine-labeled phalloidin staining performed after 7-days of culturing revealed that compared to the control group GelMA MPs, the GelMAMAVP MPs system significantly attenuated VEGFR2 activation and effectively counteracted VEGF165-mediated vascular responses (Fig. 2G). When compared to the GelMA MPs control group, downstream genes of VEGFR2 in GelMAMAVP MPs group, including VEGFR2, Map3k5, and Map2k4, were significantly inhibited, confirming the sustained inhibitory effect of this strategy. In addition, the use of MAVP can also inhibit the expression of neuroinflammatory gene, such as TNF-α (Fig. 2H). Following the seeding of HUVECs onto 3D-printed GelMAMAVP MPs, cytoskeleton staining revealed a significant inhibition of tube formation within the microsphere regions (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, the number of HUVECs in the non-microsphere regions was also markedly reduced compared to control GelMA MPs (Fig. 2J). These findings demonstrate that the sustained release of the UV-crosslinked MAVP from the microspheres effectively induces targeted biological responses.

Role of GelMAMAVP MPs in promoting nerve end interface self-resolution

The process of nerve end interface self-resolution includes anti-angiogenesis, scar removing, and alleviating neuroinflammation. Fig. 3A depicts a schematic neuropathic pain model of the sciatic nerve ligation [28] wrapped with electrospun PCL lamina and GelMAMAVP MPs. At 25 °C, the UV-crosslinked (15 s) GelMA microspheres exhibited stable and prolonged degradation performance in RBS solution (Fig. S3). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) is defined as the concentration that reduced cell growth by 50%, which was calculated to be 31.92 μM (Fig. S4). In vitro release studies demonstrated that MAVP exhibited a burst release from GelMAMAVP MPs during the first week, with the released concentration exceeding the IC50 (31.92 μM) by day 2 and reaching a cumulative release of 4.8% (36.25 μM) (Fig. S5A). Thereafter, the release profile plateaued from day 5 onward, maintaining a steady cumulative release of 6.0% (45.31 μM) at day 7 and reaching 57.1% at week 12 (Fig. S5B). These findings indicate that the sustained release profile of the GelMAMAVP platform fulfills the requirements for long-term in vivo applications. The long-term sustained release behavior of MAVP also met expectations, as it is covalently grafted onto GelMA—meaning that as GelMA degrades, the MA chain breaks, and MAVP is released. Previous studies have substantiated that endothelial cell-mediated activation of VEGFR2 signaling initiates YAP-dependent mechanotransduction to drive vascular regeneration [29]. Fig. 3B specifically shows the compromised VEGFR2/YAP mechanotransduction process in all groups. In comparative analysis of the established VEGFR2 inhibitor vandetanib (VAN), G-MAVP demonstrated precise phospho-VEGFR2 signaling inhibition (Fig. 3C) and potent suppression of YAP-mediated mechanotransduction (Fig. 3D), thereby culminating in superior vascular inhibitory efficacy.

The immunofluorescence (IF) analysis of dual staining for the vascular marker CD31 and proliferation marker Ki67 showed distinct vascular regenerative patterns across experimental groups (Fig. 3E). Control specimens exhibited robust vascular proliferation. G-VAN groups demonstrated measurable attenuation of neovascularization. Empirical comparative analysis revealed that the G-MAVP group achieved the highest efficacy in targeted suppression of vascular proliferation compared to conventional VEGFR2 inhibitor G-VAN group, establishing G-MAVP as the most potent suppressor of angiogenic activity in this experimental paradigm (Fig. 3F). IF analysis of macrophage infiltration (F4/80, Fig. S7) and dual-labeling with proliferation markers (F4/80 and Ki67 co-localization, Fig. 3G) demonstrated that the G-MAVP group exhibited the most potent suppression of macrophage proliferation. This targeted inhibition directly correlated with a marked reduction in neuroinflammatory responses (Fig. 3H). IF analysis using scar-specific alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) revealed differential fibrotic responses across treatment groups (Fig. 3I). Control specimens exhibited substantial scar tissue deposition, whereas the vehicle group (G-Veh) showed moderate fibrotic attenuation. The G-VAN intervention achieved marked reduction in scar tissue accumulation. Notably, G-MAVP-treated samples demonstrated elevated therapeutic efficacy compared to other groups, attaining the lowest quantified fibrotic area percentage among all experimental groups (Fig. 3J).

Efficacy of GelMAMAVP MPs in alleviating neuropathic pain

Behavioral assessment of pain responses following partial sciatic nerve ligation showed distinct therapeutic trajectories among experimental groups. Groups were categorized based on drug-loading variations: G-MAVP group, G-VAN group, and G-Veh group, with traditional muscle embedding surgery serving as the control group. In the 16-week von Frey filament testing (Fig. 4A), mechanical pain thresholds showed no significant difference between the vehicle group and control group throughout the observation period. While the G-VAN group demonstrated comparable efficacy to the G-MAVP group during the initial four weeks, its analgesic effects diminished progressively from the fifth week onward, ultimately converging with vehicle and control group levels, indicating transient therapeutic activity. In contrast, the G-MAVP group exhibited a sustained improvement profile, with mechanical pain thresholds increasing continuously through week 7 before plateauing at an elevated level, demonstrating long-term therapeutic maintenance. Hot plate (Fig. 4B) and acetone tests (Fig. 4C) corroborated with this observation. The response durations of G-VAN group showed progressive decline from week 5, eventually matching control levels, while G-MAVP intervention produced the most pronounced and persistent alleviation of sensory abnormalities across both thermal and cold-evoked pain modalities. These multimodal assessments consistently demonstrated the superior capacity of G-MAVP group for maintaining therapeutic effects compared to the transient efficacy profile observed in G-VAN group.

In terms of toxicity, histopathological evaluation via H&E staining of major organs (liver, heart, kidney) presented no significant morphological alterations in G-MAVP-, G-VAN-, or G-Veh-treated groups compared to traditional surgery controls (no drug) (Fig. S9). These findings confirm the absence of systemic toxicity associated with localized sustained drug release. The underlying mechanism of GelMAMAVP MPs in relieving neuropathic pain in stump neuroma

To elucidate the pain-resolving mechanism of GelMAMAVP MPs at the nerve stump, we conducted a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments. Suppression of angiogenesis, axonal sprouting, and neuroinflammation may contribute to the prevention of neuroma formation, thereby alleviating neuropathic pain. Regarding angiogenesis, in vitro assays demonstrated that MAVP exerted a stronger inhibitory effect than AVP, significantly suppressing both VEGFR2 phosphorylation (p-VEGFR2) and VEGFA expression in HUVECs (Fig. S11). Consistently, in vivo study revealed that the G-MAVP group exhibited the lowest average integrated density of VEGFA expression according to immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 6A and B). More than that, it was found that treatment with GelMAMAVP MPs significantly suppressed nerve growth factor (NGF) levels (Fig. 6C and D), which could directly influence nerve regrowth and neuroma pain. In terms of immunomodulation, in vivo results showed that MAVP significantly decreased the total macrophage count (Fig. 6E and F) as well as the number of M1-polarized macrophages (Fig. S12). Concurrently, a reduction in scar-associated fibroblasts was observed. The inhibition of α-SMA-labeled neural scar tissue was most pronounced following GelMAMAVP MPs treatment (Fig. 6G and H). The amelioration of the scar tissue microenvironment and the modulation of inflammation-related macrophages are beneficial for the subsequent remyelination process. The myelination rate of regenerated nerves, defined as the ratio of the myelin basic protein (MBP)-positive area to the axonal protein (NF200)-positive area (Fig. 6I), showed no significant difference between the G-MAVP and G-VAN groups. However, both groups differed significantly from the G-Veh group (Fig. 6J), indicating that early vascular inhibition is crucial for promoting myelination during nerve regeneration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GelMAMAVP MPs not only facilitate the self-resolution of the nerve stump by pruning excessive neuropathological tissue within the neuroma, but also enhance neural self-protection by promoting the myelination process.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms downstream of VEGFR2, we examined the role of YAP signaling in modulating HUVEC tube formation and its mediation of pain-related inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, CCL-2, and IL-12a). In a serum-free culture system supplemented solely with VEGFA, HUVECs in all groups were stimulated with 20 μg/L VEGFA. Tube formation assay illustrated that robust HUVEC tube-like structure formation was observed in the Control group following VEGFA stimulation, this angiogenic response was significantly attenuated in the MAVP group (Fig. S13), owing to the antagonistic effect of MAVP against VEGFA. The inhibitory effect of MAVP on HUVEC tube formation resembled that of the YAP inhibitor verteporfin (VP), suggesting the VEGFA-induced pro-angiogenic activity was markedly suppressed upon the administration of VP. YAP acts as a downstream mediator of MAVP and suppresses HUVEC tube formation. More than that, in vitro q-PCR assays demonstrated that both MAVP and VP significantly suppressed the production of inflammatory cytokines in pro-inflammatory HUVECs, further confirming that YAP signaling acts as the downstream mediator of MAVP in regulating inflammatory cytokines expression (Fig. S14).

In terms of neural pain receptors, assessments of the pain sensitivity protein calmodulin gene-related peptide (CGRP) [30] and the mechanical stimulus pain related protein transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) [31] (Fig. 6K) showed that GelMAMAVP MPs could significantly inhibit the expression of both CGRP (Fig. 6L) and TRPA1 (Fig. 6M) at the nerve stump, exhibiting the strongest inhibitory effect among all groups. This provides rationale for the superior pain-relieving efficacy of the G-MAVP group. Pain signals ascend via sensory fibers of the peripheral nerves to the DRG [32]. At the DRG site, we observed marked inhibition of p-VEGFR2 (Fig. 7A) and VEGFA (Fig. 7B) protein expression. Although GelMAVAN MPs also showed inhibitory effects on vascular signaling, their efficacy was substantially weaker than that of GelMAMAVP MPs (Fig. 7C and D). In addition, GelMAMAVP MPs can critically minimize the expression of pain-related signals (CGRP and TRPA1) in the DRG (Fig. 7E), a trend consistent with the downregulation of p-VEGFR2 and VEGFA (Fig. 7F and G). The results indicate that inhibiting VEGFA signaling reduces the expression of pain-associated proteins. In vitro study further confirmed that MAVP significantly reduced the expression of the mechanosensitive pain-related receptor TRPA1 in the DRG neuronal cell line (Fig. S16). The treatment with GelMAMAVP MPs at the nerve stump also attenuated signaling in the spinal cord dorsal horn, as evidenced by decreased expression of p-VEGFR2 and VEGFA protein (Fig. 7H and I) and the greatest inhibitory effect among all experimental groups (Fig. 7J and K). This indicates that sustained intervention via GelMAMAVP MPs in peripheral nerves exerts indirective inhibition on spinal sensory centers. In addition, immunofluorescence staining revealed a corresponding decrease in microglial numbers (Fig. 7L), with the G-MAVP group showing the fewest microglia, followed by the G-VAN group (Fig. 7M). These findings suggest a potential pain-relief mechanism: targeted inhibition of VEGFR2 directly reduces p-VEGFR2 and VEGFA in the DRG, thereby lowering the expression of pain-related proteins and attenuating pain signal transmission. Similarly, suppression of p-VEGFR2 in the posterior horn of the spinal cord inhibits microglial cell proliferation, consequently preventing pain hypersensitivity (Fig. 7N).

In summary, we proposed an engineered strategy for preventing neuroma formation and alleviating neuropathic pain, with the overall design and therapeutic mechanism summarized in Fig. 8. The pre-prepared functionalized GelMA MPs (Fig. 8i) were utilized to fabricate directionally-aligned arrays via 3D printing (Fig. 8ii). These arrays were then stacked within a spatially-confined PCL@MC conduit to promote nerve self-resolution and alleviate mechanical allodynia. The sustained release of MAVP alleviates neuropathic pain by attenuating YAP signaling downstream of VEGFR2. Importantly, local antagonism of VEGFR2 at the injury site indirectly modulates pathological responses in the dorsal root ganglion and spinal dorsal horn (Fig. 8iii).

Discussion

The treatment of chronic neuropathic pain resulting from traumatic neuromas presents a significant clinical challenge, primarily due to the high recurrence rate caused by pathological angiogenesis [6], as well as disorganized axonal regeneration [33,34] and persistent neuroinflammation [35]. Direct suppression of neuropathic signaling can partially alleviate neuroma-related pain [36], however this strategy fails to address the prevention of neuroma recurrence, which represents the primary cause of persistent post-excision pain [7]. Evidences suggest that inhibiting pathological vascular regeneration may be critical to suppressing neuroma recurrence [25,37]. Unlike the early vascularization that promotes regeneration at the site of nerve defects [38], stump neuromas arise at the proximal end of the nerve. Even in the absence of regenerative demands, the nerve stump undergoes uncontrollable and irreversible axonal sprouting, accompanied by pathological angiogenesis. This aberrant vascularization is a key factor contributing to the formation of painful neuromas [1,39]. In this specific biological context, we hypothesize that early and localized inhibition of neovascularization at the nerve stump can suppress aberrant axonal proliferation, thereby effectively forestalling the progression of neuroma formation. To verify this hypothesis, we present a targeting anti-VEGFR2 strategy leveraging GelMAMAVP MPs within the progressively spatial-constrictive conduits. This approach results in significantly suppressed neuroma-associated mechanical allodynia by reducing the generation of pain-inducing reactive oxygen species.

Vandetanib, a traditional VEGFR2 inhibitor, has been reported to alleviate pain in other disease contexts, such as osteoarthritis-related pain [24,40,41] and tumor-related pain [42]. However, systemic administration of this drug has been plagued by its rapid metabolism and off-target toxicity [43]. In this study, the engineered GelMAMAVP MPs system circumvented these limitations, demonstrating enhanced efficacy in sustained inhibition of VEGFR2 without systemic side effects, as evidenced by histopathological organ safety profiles. The duration of action for the GelMAMAVP MPs in vivo was significantly longer than that of the GelMAVAN MPs, as evaluated by behavioral assessments. This prolonged effect is attributed to the gradual degradation of GelMA microspheres, which enables continuous and sustained pharmacological activity. In vitro cumulative release studies confirmed that the covalent grafting of MAVP to GelMA microspheres enables a sustained release profile. Moreover, we demonstrated that MAVP does not affect the migration of neural-like cells, including PC12 cells, further indicating that it unlikely to impair healing at the nerve stump interface.

The perception of pain typically originates from tissue injury caused by physical or chemical stimuli, which triggers the release of pro-inflammatory mediators. These mediators subsequently activate nociceptors, generating action potentials that propagate along primary afferent fibers to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. The signals then ascend to the thalamus and are further relayed to multiple cortical regions for processing, ultimately resulting in the subjective experience of pain in brain [4]. Mechanistically, evidence indicates that the Hippo pathway effector YAP serves as a critical signal transducer in VEGF-VEGFR2 signaling during angiogenesis [29]. In vivo, we demonstrated that MAVP concurrently inhibits both YAP-mediated mechanotransduction and VEGFR2 phosphorylation in vascular endothelial cells of peripheral nerve tissues (Fig. 3B). This ultimately leads to suppressed proliferation of these endothelial cells. Tube formation assays using a YAP antagonist in vitro further demonstrated that YAP functions as an effector protein downstream of VEGFR2. In addition, GelMAMAVP MPs significantly inhibited the neuroinflammatory response at the peripheral nerve stump and indirectly alleviated microglial proliferation in the spinal dorsal horn, while also attenuating the expression of TRPA1 and CGRP signaling proteins in DRG and spine cord. These changes weakened the transmission of pain signals to the brain, which disrupted the neuroimmune crosstalk that sustains central sensitization. In conclusion, GelMAMAVP MPs effectively prevented the formation of pain sensitization circuits via sustained VEGFR2 inhibition at peripheral sensory nerves.

Given the spatial constraints of a 2 mm-diameter conduit, 3D printing is essential to achieve the precise alignment of hydrogel microspheres—a requirement that conventional fabrication methods cannot reliably meet. In this study, we combined microfluidics with 3D printing to establish a scalable, standardized, and controllable fabrication process. The resulting GelMAMAVP MP array-loaded conduit effectively suppressed ROS and attenuated angiogenesis, fibrotic scarring, and inflammation within regenerated nerve tissue. This localized intervention markedly mitigated autophagic behavior and improved gait patterns in rats with neuromas. Collectively, these findings position GelMAMAVP MPs as a versatile and effective platform for preventing traumatic neuroma formation and reducing neuropathic hypersensitivity, underscoring their significant potential for clinical translation.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the chronic pain microenvironment involves complex interactions among immune cell (e.g., T cells, mast cells, neutrophils) that warrant further investigation [44,45]. Second, while our dual strategy combining spatial constriction with VEGFR-2 inhibition effectively alleviated pain, incorporating additional inhibitors targeting pain-related receptors may achieve more complete resolution, and the potential synergistic interactions among these strategies merit further exploration. Third, our findings lack validation in large animal models, which is essential for assessing long-term side effects and translational potential. Moreover, systemic pharmacokinetic profiles and precise drug concentrations at the neural interface 12 weeks post-surgery require further elucidation. Fourth, given that post-amputation pain can persist for years, the long-term in vivo stability and durability of PCL@MC conduits encapsulating GelMAMAVP MPs need to be evaluated. Finally, the detailed retrograde signaling cascade from the periphery to the central nervous system remains to be fully elucidated and will be a focus of our future mechanistic studies.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of MAVP

MAVP was synthesized through the covalent conjugation of methacryloyl groups to the N-terminal α-amino group of the known VEGFR2-targeting antagonist sequence ATWLPPR peptides [46]. To reduce the influence on the peptide's three-dimensional conformation, a triglycine (GGG) spacer was inserted between the MA group and alanine residue (MA-GGG-ATWLPPR). The peptide was custom-synthesized and purified by Scilight-Peptide Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Its purity was verified by analytical high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to be >98%.

Fabrication of spatially-confined conduits

0.5 g of freeze-dried MC powders were added to 20 mL of 10% (w/v) polycaprolactone solution (PCL, Mw = 250,000, Jinan Daigang Biomaterial Co., Ltd) in hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and stirred vigorously for over 48 h to form a homogeneous electrospinning sol. Following electrospinning, hydraulic compression, and mold fitting procedures, the material was fabricated into a spatially constricting nerve conduit. Comprehensive methodological details are available in our prior publication [5].

GelMA preparation

10 g of Type A gelatin (300 Bloom, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water at 50 °C under continuous stirring. Once fully dissolved, 8 mL of methacrylic anhydride (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was gradually added to the solution while stirring at 500 rpm. The reaction was maintained at 50 °C for 3 h. To terminate the reaction, distilled water (five times the original reaction volume) was added. The mixture was then collected and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min to remove any impurities. The resulting supernatant was subjected to dialysis using a 13.5 kDa membrane against distilled water for one week to remove residual impurities. Finally, the purified solution was lyophilized to obtain methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) powder, which was stored at −20 °C for future use.

Preparation of GelMA MPs via microfluidics

A microfluidic T-junction chip was connected to two pressure-driven pumps (Wuhan Jeremy). One pump, set to 100 Pa, delivered a 10 wt% GelMA prepolymer solution containing 0.25 wt% lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP; Shanghai Yinchang Xincai) as the photoinitiator, maintained at 35 °C to prevent premature gelation. The second pump, set to 200 Pa, supplied mineral oil supplemented with 2% Span-80 (Biosharp, China) as a surfactant. Within the T-junction, the GelMA/LAP solution was introduced into the main channel, where shear forces caused by the flow of oil fragmented the solution into spheres. This emulsion was directed through transparent tubing (Tygon, USA) exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light at 405 nm with an intensity of 5 mW/cm2, initiating crosslinking. The tubing length was calculated to ensure each droplet received approximately 20 s of UV exposure. Post-crosslinking, the emulsion was collected and subjected to centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 2 min to separate the oil phase. The resulting GelMA microspheres were resuspended in deionized water and centrifuged under the same conditions to remove residual oil; this washing step was repeated three times. Finally, the purified GelMA microspheres were lyophilized and stored at −20 °C for future applications.

Observation of GelMA MPs morphology

To observe GelMA microspheres using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), lyophilized microspheres were mounted onto a conductive adhesive. Images were then obtained via an SEM (Merlin, Zeiss, Germany).

Preparation for GelMAMAVP MPs

After freeze-drying (Alpha 1-2 LDplus, Germany), GelMA MPs can be stored for later use. To prepare the GelMAVP MPs bioactive ink, rehydrate the freeze-dried GelMA MPs in an appropriate concentration of MAVP solution. Subsequently, remove excess MAVP solution using negative pressure filtration. During the 3D bioprinting process, UV light exposure enables covalent and stable binding between GelMA MPs and MAVP via methacryloyl (MA) groups forming GelMAMAVP MPs.

In vitro release studies of MAVP

At room temperature (25 °C), 1000 μL of MAVP solution (1 mg/mL in PBS, pH 7.4) was gently added to a test tube containing 10 mg of dry GelMA microspheres; this moment was defined as time zero. After 15 s of UV cross-linking, 50 μL of the supernatant was sampled immediately following gentle homogenization of the mixture and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The collected sample was analyzed by analytical HPLC (Agilent 1260) to determine the concentration of non-adsorbed MAVP in solution. The initial mass of MAVP adsorbed onto the microspheres (Mi) was then calculated by subtracting the non-adsorbed mass from the total MAVP added. Subsequently, at predetermined time intervals, 50 μL of supernatant was similarly collected and replaced with fresh PBS. The cumulative amount of MAVP released (Mt, μg) was determined over time, and the cumulative release percentage (%) was calculated using Eq. (1), where Mi represents the initially adsorbed amount. The results were plotted as cumulative release versus time to obtain the in vitro release profile for each formulation. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from the rat DRG cell line FH-R182 (Fuheng Biology, China) and from HUVECs (NICR, catalog number 4201PAT-CCTCC02060, Beijing, China) using Cell Lysis Buffer for Western Blot and IP (Beyotime, P0013J). Protein concentration was measured with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 7.5% or 15% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-TRPA1 (Santa Cruz, sc-376495), anti-VEGF-A (Cell Signaling Technology, #50661), anti-phospho-VEGFR2 (Cell Signaling Technology, #3770), and anti-β-actin (Abcam, ab6276). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a Bio-Rad imaging system and quantified with Image J.

Cytotoxicity assay and cytoskeleton structure observation

Cytotoxicity of the fabricated GelMA was performed via live/dead staining. After 15 s crosslinking of UV, GelMA MPs and GelMAMAVP MPs were added to each well and the cells were treated via direct co-culture with the microspheres. Next, RSCs and HUVEC were seeded onto a 48-well plate at 4000 cells/well density, followed by 400 μl of culture medium. After seven days of culturing, the culture medium was removed, and Calcein AM (2 μM, Invitrogen, USA) was added to the wells, followed by 40 min of incubation in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% of CO2 at 37 °C. Subsequently, Propidium iodide (PI, 5 μM, Invitrogen, USA) was added to the wells and left at room temperature for 10 min, after which the wells were washed with PBS to remove excess dye. Live/dead staining images were obtained via a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM980, Germany).

MAVP solution was performed with 15 s of UV irradiation, RSCs were cultured in MAVP medium containing different concentrations (0 μM, 10 μM, 30 μM, and 100 μM) for 7 days. On day 7, cytoskeleton staining was performed to visualize changes in cell proliferation. For staining, RSCs were first washed three times with PBS, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h to preserve cellular structures. After fixation, cells were rinsed again with PBS and incubated with rhodamine phalloidin (1:200 dilution, Invitrogen R415). Excess stain was removed with additional PBS washes. Stained cytoskeletons were then imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM980, Germany) with excitation at 540–565 nm.

Cellular reactive oxygen species assay

To induce oxidative stress, HUVECs were exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 12 h. Following this treatment, the cells were cultured in a 6-well plate at 100 k cells/well. After 15 s of UV irradiation, a 50 μM solution of MAVP, AVP, VAN or Veh was administered to the respective wells. To measure intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, a ROS Assay Kit (S0033, Beyotime, China) was used. Once the cells reached approximately 70% confluence, DCFH-DA solution (1:1000 dilution) was added to each well, and incubated (37 °C, 5% CO2 atm.) for 1 h. The stained cells were captured under 505–525 nm light via a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM900 with Airyscan 2, Germany).

Calcium imaging

To assess the effect of each drug (i.e., MAVP, AVP, VAN, Vehicle) on peripheral nerve cells for pain response, a fast response calcium ion fluorescence probe (Beyotime, China) was used to observe the intracellular calcium response in RSCs. Transfer pCMV jGCaMP7 plasmid and Lipo8000 ™ (Beyotime, China) at a ratio of 1:1.6 were added to RSCs, and was left overnight. Following this, RSCs were treated with MAVP (50 μM), VAN (50 μM), Veh or 1 mM AITC (Sigma, Shanghai), respectively. The results were then obtained via a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM900, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm. The data were expressed as both the real-time live-cell intensity (a.u.) and the responsive cell proportion (%).

Scratch wound healing assay

To perform scratch wound healing assay, HUVEC and PC12 (NICR; 1101RAT-PUMC000024, Beijing, China) were seeded into a 6-well plate and cultured until reaching 90–100% confluence. A sterile 200-μL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch across the cell monolayer, and any detached cells and debris were removed through thorough washing of the wells via PBS. The cells were then incubated with or without GelMA microspheres. Images of the scratch area were captured at 0 h and 48 h. The wound closure rate was quantified by measuring the remaining wound area at each time point and normalizing it against the original wound area using ImageJ.

3D printing of GelMAMAVP MPs

A PBS solution containing 0.25% LAP and MAVP (2 mg/mL) was first prepared, after which lyophilized GelMA was added and suspended via ultrasonication. The mixture was then left undisturbed for 10 min before excess liquid was removed via vacuum filtration, yielding a slurry. For 3D printing preparation, the GelMA microsphere slurry was first transferred into a 20 mL syringe then packed. A tapered extrusion tip, either 22 G (gauge) with an inner diameter of 410 μm or 25 G with an inner diameter of 250 μm, was attached. The 3D printing models were initially designed using SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes) and then transferred to proprietary 3D printing software (Bioscaffold GiSim, Germany) for model slicing and printing path generation. During the printing process, extrusion pressures of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 kPa were applied, while printing speeds were set at 2, 6, 10, and 14 mm/s. A layer height of 0.3 mm was maintained throughout, and each layer was cured using 405 nm UV radiation at an intensity of 5 mW/cm2 for 15 s. Aligned microsphere fiber arrays were fabricated via 3D printing to provide directional topographical cues for orderly axonal regrowth. To retain this aligned configuration after loading microspheres into the nerve conduit, the 3D-printed microsphere constructs were sequentially subjected to freeze-drying to immobilize their structure, sterilization via cobalt-60 gamma irradiation (6 kGy), and subsequent implantation for in vivo animal experiments.

Rheometer testing of 3D printed samples

Rheological assessments were conducted using a modular compact rheometer (MCR302e, Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria). The rheological properties of each UV irradiated sample were evaluated with an 8 mm parallel plate at 37 °C. Storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded through a frequency sweep ranging from 0.1 to 10 Hz at a constant strain of 1%. Statistical analysis was then performed to determine the storage modulus. The viscosity of the prepolymer was measured via an amplitude sweep at 28 °C, with a shear rate ranging from 0.1 to 100 s−1. To assess the microscale self-healing capability of the hydrogel samples, a three-cycle step-strain time sweep was conducted at 1 Hz, alternating between low strain (730 Pa) and high strain (1350 Pa).

Cellular RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Freshly frozen rat sciatic nerve pieces were pulverized into a fine powder with the help of rapid freezing via liquid nitrogen. RNA was then extracted from Schwann cells, fibroblasts, and nerve tissue homogenate using the RNeasy Microkit (Qiagen), following the steps provided by the kit manufacturer. The obtained RNA was then denaturized and used to synthesize first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) with SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) and random hexamers (Roche). Quantitative analysis was conducted via real-time recording of fluorescence using the Light Cycler 96 (Roche). The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Animal experiment

The animal experiments involved in this study have been approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tsinghua University. 24 Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were used for the model of partial ligation of the sciatic nerve (pSNL) (n = 6/group). Another 48 SD rats were used for a traumatic neuroma model (n = 12/group). The partial sciatic nerve ligation model is used to study neuropathic pain; the stump neuroma model is used to study neuroma formation. All rats were male, 8 weeks of age, weighing 250 ± 10 g, and were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Tsinghua University. Regarding housing conditions, the room temperature was kept at 25 °C ± 1 °C with constant circulation of air, while the rats were provided ample food and water. Night and day cycles were simulated via a 12 h photocycle, which switched at 7:00 and 19:00.

For each surgical model, masked gas anesthesia via isoflurane mixed with oxygen at concentrations ranging from 2% to 4% was used, with a flow rate maintained at 1.8-2.0 L per minute. For the nerve ligation model [28], the left hind legs of the rats were shaved and sterilized, followed by a 2 cm linear incision, exposing the sciatic nerve. After passing an 8-0 nonabsorbable suture through the center of the sciatic nerve, a surgical knot was tied, securing the suture. This procedure artificially creates persistent peripheral nerve pain. The experimental setup included four treatment conditions: control group: PCL-wrapped GelMA microparticles (G) without additional agents at the ligation site; negative control group: PCL-wrapped GelMA MPs loaded with the vehicle-phosphate-buffered saline (G-Veh); positive control group: PCL-wrapped GelMA MPs loaded with vandetanib (2 mg/mL, G-VAN); experimental group: PCL-wrapped GelMA MPs loaded with MAVP (2 mg/mL, G-MAVP). A precise mass of 10 ± 1 mg of dry GelMA MPs was loaded into each group. All groups maintained consistent surgical placement at the ligation site. Rats of this model were kept for a maximum of 16 weeks.

For the traumatic neuroma model, the sciatic nerve was first exposed, followed by the removal of 1 cm of the nerve, simulating nerve amputation. The experimental design comprised four distinct groups: the control group received direct alignment of the proximal nerve stump with adjacent muscle as the baseline intervention (G-Control group); The negative control group were implanted with spatially constrained nerve conduits containing GelMA MPs exclusively adsorbed with PBS (G-Veh group); The positive control group were treated with spatially constrained nerve conduits incorporating GelMA MPs loaded with VAN (2 mg/mL, G-VAN group); The experimental group received spatially constrained nerve conduits containing GelMA MPs loaded with MAVP (2 mg/mL, G-MAVP group). A precise mass of 10 ± 1 mg of dry GelMA MPs was loaded into each group. All conduit configurations maintained anatomical matching with the proximal nerve stump. Rats of this model were kept for a maximum of 12 weeks.

Angiogenesis experiment of HUVEC

To observe the angiogenesis of HUVEC, the cytoskeleton staining was performed. Samples were divided into two groups: GelMA MPs + VEGF165 (30 μM) group and GelMAMAVP MPs (30 μM) + VEGF165 (30 μM) group. After one week, all samples were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h, washed again with PBS, and then treated with rhodamine (1:200, Invitrogen R415). The samples were subsequently washed with PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope with an excitation light wavelength range of 540–565 nm (Zeiss, LSM980, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

Peripheral nerve, dorsal root ganglion (L4-L5), and posterior horn of spinal cord (L4-L5) were prepared for IF staining. Briefly, samples harvested from rats were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Following fixation, the tissues are cryoprotected using 15% and 30% sucrose gradient for 48 h, and embedded in an optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound before being sectioned into 8 μm thick slices and mounted onto glass slides via a cryostat. These samples are then permeabilized with a PBS based detergent containing 10% goat serum and 0.3% Triton for 1 h to allow antibodies to access intracellular targets.

The following primary antibodies were used for the subsequent steps: anti-Yap (mouse, 1:200, Santa sc-376830); anti-p-VEGFR2 (rabbit, 1:100 Invitrogen, PA5-105765); α-SMA (rabbit, 1:200, Proteintech 14395-1-AP); Reca-1 (mouse, 1:200, Santa sc-52665); anti-CD31 (mouse, 1:200, Santa sc-13537); anti-Ki67 (rabbit, 1:150, Cell Signaling 9129S); anti-NF-200 (mouse, 1:200, Sigma, SAB4200747); anti-MBP (rabbit, 1:200, Abcam ab218011); anti-F4/80 (mouse, 1:200, Santa sc-377009); Iba-1 (rabbit, 1:150, Abcam ab178846); anti-CGRP (rabbit, 1:400, Abcam ab283568); anti-TRPA1 (mouse, 1:200, Santa sc-376495); anti-CD86 (rabbit, 1:200, Proteintech 30691-1-AP); CD206 (rabbit, 1:200, Proteintech 18704-1-AP). These primary antibodies, diluted with PBS accordingly, were added to the prepared slides, and left to incubate in a 4 °C refrigerator overnight. Following thorough washing via PBS, corresponding secondary antibodies (diluted against PBS at 1:800) and DAPI (Abcam, ab104139, against blocking buffer solution) was applied and incubated at room temperature for 1h, after which the slides were washed thrice.

Nerve sample slides were examined under a high-sensitivity SCOMS monochromatic camera at a scanning resolution of 0.22 μm/pixel via an automated digital slide scanning system (Axio Scan.Z1 Zeiss). High-resolution images were captured using a 20 × numerical aperture (NA) objective on an LSM980 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss). Imaging was performed at room temperature, and the acquired images were processed and optimized using Arivis Vision4D software (Carl Zeiss).

Assessment of pain-like behaviors

To assess the pain-like behaviors in pSNL models, assessments of mechanical allodynia, cold hypersensitivity, and thermal hyperalgesia were done once a week before (baseline) and after pSNL surgery in G-MAVP, G-VAN, G-Veh, and G-control group rats. Furthermore, all mechanical allodynia responses were also assessed before and after the various antiangiogenic interventions.

The mechanical threshold was measured via the up-and-down paradigm (Ugo, Italy). Cold hypersensitivity was assessed by measuring the acute nociceptive response to acetone-evoked evaporative cooling [28]. Thermal hyperalgesia was measured by exposing the whole plantar surface of the hind paw to a heated board, whereas the front paws were attached with heat-proof stickers. Paw withdrawal latency was then recorded as the time from the onset of the heat (50 °C) to the withdrawal response (Bioseb, France). The mean withdrawal latency was calculated through three measures. The interval between each trial on the same paw was at least 5 min, and the heating was cut off after 15 s to avoid tissue damage in case of the failure of paw withdrawal.

It is widely known that monitoring the dynamic changes in rat gait patterns can serve as a proxy for pain levels. The Catwalk gait analysis (Catwalk XT, Noldus Netherlands) was used to assess neural pain manifestations in rats at postoperative weeks 0, 2, 4, 8, and 12. To visualize the pain-like behaviors of the neuroma model, autotomy scoring and the Catwalk analysis were performed before (baseline) and after traumatic neuroma surgery in G-MAVP, G-VAN, G-Veh, and control rats (time points at 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks). Details on the methodology and criteria of autotomy scoring and the Catwalk analysis refer to our previous studies [5].
