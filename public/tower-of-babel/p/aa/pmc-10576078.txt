Oncogenic dependency plays a dominant role in the immune response to cancer
Authors: Jinyu Li, Stephen D’Amico, Varvara Kirillov, Oleksi Petrenko, Nancy C. Reich
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCombination immunotherapy with KRAS/MEK inhibitors causes complete regression of advanced PDAC (Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma) in mice. Genetic KRAS ablation provides therapeutic benefit regardless of KRAS dependency values, whereas the efficacy of pharmacological KRAS inhibition inversely correlates with KRAS dependency. These results have implications for our understanding of cancer biology and therapy resistance.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the deadliest malignancies and is characterized by rapid progression, poor prognosis, and a high propensity for metastatic spread. Patients with advanced pancreatic cancer have a median survival of less than 12 mo. A striking feature of pancreatic cancer is that activating KRAS mutations are found in up to 90% of cases. The key role of mutant KRAS in cancers of the pancreas, colon, and lungs has driven concerted efforts to target it directly, resulting in recent discoveries of bioactive KRAS inhibitors (KRASG12C and KRASG12D) (1–4). It is widely accepted that mutant KRAS is required for cancer cells to maintain their malignant phenotypes. However, for a variety of reasons, direct KRAS inhibitors are not always effective as single agents (5, 6). Primary or acquired KRAS independence is a recognized route for resistance and tumor relapse (7, 8). Moreover, evidence that cancer cells can survive in the absence of KRAS expression highlights the need for orthogonal approaches (9–11). Lung and colon cancers bearing the KRASG12C mutation showed only modest responses to KRAS-targeting drugs, but a combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) achieved enhanced anticancer activity (1, 2).

We previously showed that oncogenic KRAS plays a profound immunosuppressive role in cancer maintenance. KRAS-deficient cells retain their growth, survival, and pro-tumorigenic characteristics in immunodeficient mice. However, they fail to evade the host immune system in syngeneic wild-type mice, triggering a strong antitumor response (11). These data suggest that PDAC treatment could benefit from this therapeutic strategy. However, pancreatic cancer is considered immunologically cold, and metastatic PDAC has an overall lower infiltration of immune cells, and hence, immunogenicity, relative to primary tumors, while tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) can be rendered dysfunctional or exhausted by the tumor environment (12, 13). Thus, uncovering the mechanisms by which mutant KRAS subverts the immune system is the key to successful treatment.

In this study, we investigate a mechanistic link between KRAS effector pathways and tumor immune infiltration in late-stage (advanced) PDAC. We also explore the challenges in pharmacological targeting of pancreatic tumors in order to understand how the dominant drivers of KRAS-sponsored evasion of the immune system can be circumvented. We find that two major subtypes of KRAS mutant cancer, commonly referred to as KRAS-dependent and independent, have markedly different properties in terms of T cell infiltration and immune escape mechanisms. While genetic ablation of KRAS expression provides therapeutic benefits regardless of KRAS dependency values, the efficacy of pharmacological KRAS inhibition inversely correlates with KRAS dependency. Hence, oncogenic dependency, rather than KRAS mutation per se, plays a dominant role in the immune response to cancer. Assessing this model in human PDAC, we show that at all stages of cancer our results are directly applicable to classifying human tumor samples as being strongly dependent on KRAS/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling or less KRAS-dependent with the induction of a more permissive immune environment. Last, we show that regardless of the type of tumor categorization, dual RAS/MAPK pathway blockade combining KRAS and MAPK extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) kinase (MEK) inhibitors is more effective than the selective KRAS inhibitor alone in attenuating MAPK activation and unblocking the influx of T cells into the tumor. Combination immunotherapy with KRAS/MEK inhibitors causes complete regression of advanced PDAC in preclinical models.

Results

Oncogenic Dependency Shapes Antitumor Immunity in Human PDAC.

We sought to assess the relationship between mutant KRAS and tumor immunity in human PDAC at different stages of cancer. To that end, we analyzed data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, n = 150), Amsterdam UMC (AUMC, n = 90), and the PanCuRx Translational Research Initiative (COMPASS, n = 300) (14–17). TCGA cohort represents early-stage PDACs (stage I/II), while the AUMC and COMPASS datasets represent locally advanced and metastatic tumors (stage III and IV), respectively. Despite a large number of subtype models that have been proposed, we felt that there is an unmet need for tumor stratification based on KRAS biology, to provide a framework to study the impact of KRAS inhibition in mouse models and patients.

A model of differential engagement of KRAS effector pathways in supporting oncogene addiction served as a useful approximation of cancer-specific dependencies in early and late-stage PDAC (18). We grouped the tumor samples in two ways: by a RAS dependency index (RDI) and molecular subtype. To that end, we categorized them as KRAS-dependent or independent and KRAS or ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK)-type, as they relate to previously published classifications of KRAS mutant cancer (18, 19). The RDI categorizes samples based on a predicted level of KRAS addiction and epithelial differentiation (19); KRAS-type tumors depend on the RAS/MAPK pathway, while RSK-type tumors are less dependent on the RAS/MAPK pathway, but maximally activate RSK/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling (18). Both classification schemes (RDI and KRAS/RSK) exploit gene expression patterns to predict and characterize cancer dependencies and tumor cell fitness (20).

Aligning TCGA tumors according to these signature scores revealed two oncogenic patterns: one with high KRAS downstream signaling activity and the other with high RSK activity (K1 and R1, respectively) (Fig. 1A). Samples that did not stratify well showed either dual activation (H1) or suppression (H2) of KRAS and RSK signature genes and were classified as hybrids according to previous annotations (14) (Fig. 1B). Mutations in KRAS (92% in KRAS-type vs. 86% in RSK-type) were evenly distributed between KRAS and RSK subtypes. Changes in gene expression occurred at clearly defined thresholds that segregated these tumors into either KRAS or RSK-type (exemplified in SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Assessing the correspondence between the RDI and KRAS/RSK signature scores revealed that the two metrics are not uniformly equivalent. RDIs accurately reflect KRAS activation in early-stage tumors, while KRAS/RSK scores represent a more accurate and valid measure of KRAS dependency in late-stage cancer (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). According to single-nucleus PDAC profiling (21), the development of KRAS-dependent tumors is associated with the acquisition of classical or squamous-like features, while RSK-type malignancies bear signatures of a mesenchymal and neural-like progenitor program (P < 0.00001 by Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 1C). No significant overlap was found with the previously described classical and basal-like subtypes (22) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Aligning tumor samples from the AUMC and COMPASS cohorts also segregated them into KRAS-type, RSK-type, and hybrids (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A).

In each case, enrichment analysis revealed strong negative correlations between KRAS dependence and TIL infiltration, with KRAS-independent tumors showing higher levels of genes expressed by CD4 and CD8 T cells. RSK-type tumors had the highest immune scores, and KRAS-type tumors had the lowest scores (Fig. 1D). Type 1 helper, T regulatory, T follicular helper, natural killer T, and memory CD8 T cells were among the most abundant immune cell types (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Beyond TILs, KRAS independence is positively correlated with C3 type (inflammatory) immunity and improved overall survival (11, 20, 23). For instance, the top 20 genes predicting unfavorable prognosis co-segregated with KRAS dependence, while the top 20 genes predicting improved cancer survival co-segregated with KRAS independence (24). Pathway activity scores in KRAS-type vs. RSK-type tumors were calculated using curated gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database. Data analysis showed that the signatures of KRAS and AKT activation were enriched in KRAS-type tumors, whereas TGF-beta and Ca2+ signaling signatures were enriched in RSK-type tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). As reported, changes in morphology (epithelial vs. mesenchymal) and metabolism (glycolytic vs. oxidative) are two major distinctions between the KRAS and RSK types (18). To identify changes that correlated with cancer treatment, we examined the gene expression profiles of 800 pre- and post-treatment samples. These tumors again fell into two groups, with high/low KRAS activity and high/low RSK activity, supporting the concept of differential KRAS dependency (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). This pattern was consistent for all the datasets surveyed (~45% KRAS-type and ~35% RSK-type), ruling out the impact of cytotoxic therapy. Tumor types that did not form distinct clusters (H2 hybrids) showed elevated scores for the cell cycle and DNA replication (E2F targets, G2/M checkpoint, and MYC targets), supporting published results from mice (25) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).

Functional Classification of KRAS Dependence in Metastatic PDAC.

Next, we curated the data from the COMPASS cohort, and only metastases were evaluated to avoid differences observed in the primary malignancies. Metastases were derived from different sites, with the most common being the liver (n = 84) (14). Comparison of curated datasets showed that tumor metastases could also be segregated into four groups, namely KRAS-type, RSK-type, and Hybrids (Fig. 1E). However, while classifying tumors into KRAS or RSK types showed high consistency across all datasets, tumor metastases displayed only weak associations with immune cell infiltration (Fig. 1F). With 28 annotated immune cell populations (26), metastases showed reduced T cell and an overall proportion of immune cells (Fig. 1G). Two features of metastatic tumors stood out in particular: 1) based on data from the COMPASS study (14), RSK pathway activity was reduced in a manner that did not affect activation of the KRAS pathway (Fig. 1H); and 2) a strong correlation between RSK and mesenchymal gene expression was no longer observed. Principal component analysis of merged primary and metastatic tumors clearly distinguished two tumor types, KRAS (epithelial) and RSK (mesenchymal), whereas metastases clustered together and exhibited hybrid states (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). The expression of immunomodulatory genes (e.g., TGF-beta, CXCL12, and CD surface markers) was altered accordingly (Fig. 1I). Somewhat counterintuitively, mutant KRAS copy gains in association with whole-genome duplication were more prevalent in the RSK group (53% in RSK-type vs. 31% in KRAS-type, n = 35) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). KRAS allelic imbalance is a rare phenomenon in primary PDAC (14, 15). However, merely 19% of cases with KRAS gene amplification showed congruent expression changes, suggesting that only some may be functionally relevant (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). For instance, we classified the well-studied case of liver metastasis Psi_0729 (four mutant KRASG12R alleles, no WT) as a KRAS-independent, RSK-type (14). Other generic dependencies, such as the frequency of SMAD4 mutations, remained largely unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). In summary, these data reveal two classes of PDAC metastases: KRAS dependent and less KRAS dependent, showing weak dependence on RSK. The data suggest that hyperactive RSK and concomitant up-regulation of RSK-dependent genes may be selected against as tumors progress to the metastatic stage.

To validate these observations, we analyzed 70 treatment-naïve PDACs (14 matched pairs; 42 unmatched). Within matched tumor samples, we identified 38% RSK-type primary tumors, and these phenotypic features were retained in only 14% of the metastases (27) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). A similar trend was observed for untreated metastases from the PancSeq (Dana-Farber Cancer Institute) and POG570 (BC Cancer Foundation) cohorts (n = 42, 7% RSK-type) (28, 29) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). To offer a potential explanation for this phenomenon, we evaluated single-cell sequencing data of five liver metastases (30, 31) and found that TILs were selectively enriched in RSK-type samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Similar results were observed for colorectal carcinomas (TCGA, PanCancer Atlas) with KRAS mutations. The tumors again segregated into those with high/low KRAS signaling activity and high/low RSK signaling activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). RSK-type tumors overwhelmingly fell into a mesenchymal molecular subtype (32) (P < 2.2E-16 by Fisher’s exact test) and showed strong TIL infiltration (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). However, certain trends prevail, as ~70% of CRC metastases no longer display the mesenchymal characteristics of their primary tumors (33). In summary, KRAS independence is associated with enhanced immune infiltration and decreased metastasis, consistent with the role of RSK kinases as negative regulators of MAPK signaling in cancer cells. The RSK type depends on numerous effector pathways, most notably TGF-beta (18). We used these findings to assess the impact of KRAS inhibition in preclinical models of pancreatic cancer.

Complete Regression of Advanced PDAC by Combination Immunotherapy.

To assess the impact of KRAS on the immune interaction and maintenance of fully established PDAC, we used a doxycycline-inducible mouse model (termed iKRAS), in which oncogenic KRASG12D is only expressed in the presence of doxycycline (dox), whereas removal of doxycycline renders the transgene inactive (34). Two independent iKRAS p53R172H cell lines stably expressing firefly luciferase were transplanted into the pancreas of nude (NU/J) or syngeneic FVB mice that were fed with dox, and the animals were observed for tumor growth (Fig. 2A). Tumors from the injection of 104 cells were detected within 2 wk (Fig. 2B). At necropsy, metastases were present in 80% of the mice. The abdominal lymph nodes were the most common metastatic site, followed by the liver and spleen. When tumors reached ~250 mm3 (8 mm diameter), mice were withdrawn from dox. Previous studies have shown that KRASG12D inactivation leads to tumor regression, with the time to complete regression ranging from 3 to 4 wk, but up to 70% of tumors relapse due to KRAS-dependent or independent mechanisms (34–36). Against this background, we analyzed the efficacy of both short-term (2 wk) and long-term (2 mo) KRAS inactivation. Examination of mice withdrawn from dox for 2 wk showed minor changes in primary tumor size, consistent with our previous results (11). The median tumor weights were 0.45 g in nude mice and 0.225 g in FVB mice (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). None of the tumors in nude mice (n = 21) regressed at later time points; instead, they continued to grow at a slower rate (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). In contrast, we observed nearly complete regression of secondary tumors. Bioluminescence enabled the detection of small metastatic lesions that were not apparent on visual or histological examination (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). TILs were assessed by FACS and IHC, and were readily detected in tumors of mice maintained in the absence of dox, but not in its presence (Fig. 2C). Tumor-specific T cells (TST), particularly CD8 T cells, were increased 10-fold in tumors withdrawn from dox (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). However, these TST cells showed phenotypic features of dysfunction with high expression of PDCD1, CTLA4, and LAG3 immune checkpoint genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). T cells from KRAS-ablated tumors likewise express elevated levels of PDCD1, CTLA4, and LAG3 (11).

We next performed a set of experiments in which iKRAS tumor-bearing mice were treated with anti-PDCD1 and anti-CTLA4 ICIs, with or without anti-CD40 agonist antibodies, to activate antigen-presenting cells. Evidence indicates that ICI monotherapy is ineffective in pancreatic cancer, but CD40 agonists have the ability to sensitize tumors to checkpoint inhibition (37, 38). Both treatment regimens returned the expected results, as they enhanced tumor regression only in the absence of KRASG12D expression (i.e., without dox administration), but not in its presence (Fig. 2 D and E). Metastases also regressed only after dox had been withdrawn (Fig. 2 F and G). Complete regression, in the span of 2 wk, occurred in 2/8 (25%) FVB mice treated with ICIs and in 12/14 mice (86%) treated with a combination of ICI/CD40 antibodies. All cured mice displayed striking tissue repair and regeneration (Fig. 2H). The isotype control antibodies (negative controls) did not induce tumor regression.

Tumor relapse was assessed upon readministration of dox over a 2-mo period and was found to occur in just 2/12 (17%) mice treated with the combination of ICI/CD40 antibodies (Fig. 2I). In contrast, all mice in the control group without immunotherapy showed tumor relapse (SI Appendix, Fig. S5F). The effect of ICI/CD40 therapy was diminished when tumors were grown in immunodeficient NU/J or nonobese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficiency (NOD/SCID) mice, as primary tumors failed to regress in all cases, and liver metastases were found in over 50% of the cases (SI Appendix, Fig. S5F). All immunodeficient mice (100%) died within 30 d. These data provide proof of principle that combination immunotherapy can eliminate primary and metastatic PDAC in immunocompetent mice.

The overriding theme throughout was that tumor growth and tumor cell survival are governed by different mechanisms. Altering KRASG12D levels by adding or removing dox minimally affected the growth of tumor cells in culture or nude mice. In contrast, silencing KRAS in wild-type mice resulted in widespread albeit incomplete tumor regression, consistent with previous studies of lung, pancreatic, and colorectal cancer (34–36). Similarly, silencing KRAS enhanced T cell infiltration and improved the efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors. Gene expression profiling of metastatic and locally advanced human PDAC confirmed that KRAS wild-type tumors have increased T cell recruitment (Fig. 2J) (14). This suggests that tumors devoid of aberrant KRAS signaling activity may have different therapeutic sensitivities, providing insight into how they can be unlocked for therapy.

RAS/MAPK Pathway Dependence of Pancreatic Tumor Maintenance.

We sought to evaluate the role of the MAPK pathway in tumor maintenance and T cell-mediated immunity. To that end, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate isogenic iKRAS cells lacking BRAF, CRAF, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). In each case, inactivation of these genes via indels restrained tumor growth in wild-type mice but had only marginal effects in nude mice (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). A comparison of BRAF and CRAF KO cells revealed important similarities and differences. While BRAF knockouts showed markedly reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation, CRAF knockout cells retained ERK1/2 phosphorylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Although ERK is usually associated with proliferation, iKRAS cells lacking BRAF displayed no growth defects and formed tumors in nude mice with 100% efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). However, loss of BRAF completely abolished tumor growth in wild-type mice, confirming our previous findings in the KRASG12D/+; p53R172H/+ (KPC) model (11), while loss of CRAF merely delayed tumor development (Fig. 3A). Metastases were detected in 2/6 mice (33%) injected with CRAF KO cells, showing that BRAF can partially compensate for the loss of CRAF in the iKRAS model. Selective ablation of MEK1, ERK1, or ERK2 also restrained tumor development in wild-type but not nude mice (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Moreover, ICI/CD40 therapy induced regression of established tumors lacking either ERK1 or ERK2 within 2 wk of treatment (residual tumor burden less than 20%), while the control tumors doubled in size (Fig. 3B). No metastases were found in any of the mice treated with ICI antibodies. IHC staining showed that CD8 TILs were increased by a factor of >10 in tumor samples lacking either ERK1 or ERK2 (Fig. 3C). The results are consistent with the oncogenic KRAS and MAPK pathway collaborating in tumor maintenance.

Tumor Regression upon Pharmacological KRAS Pathway Blockade.

We investigated the effect of the pharmacological inhibition of RAS/MAPK signaling using non-covalent inhibitors of KRASG12D (MRTX1133, Mirati Therapeutics), RAF (HM95573, Genentech), MEK (GSK1120212, Novartis), and ERK (SCH772984, Merck). Mice bearing iKRAS tumors (~6 mm diameter) were treated with daily injections of vehicle or drug solutions, either alone or in combination with anti-PDCD1/CTLA4/CD40 (ICIs) for 2 wk. At autopsy, the tumors were dissected and weighed, allowing confirmation of tumor size. Tumors treated with MRTX1133 alone were not significantly affected, whereas MRTX1133 combined with ICIs showed partial regression (≥50% reduction in tumor size, P = 0.005) (Fig. 3D). The combination of MRTX1133 with MEK inhibitor GSK1120212 (Trametinib) enhanced the anti-tumor effect of ICIs (≥80% reduction in tumor size, P < 0.05), leading to partial remission (Fig. 3D). MEK inhibition has been reported to synergize with agonist anti-CD40 antibodies in tumor therapy (39). The presence of remission was supported by a complete lack of metastases. No clinical signs of toxicity or treatment-related effects on body weight were observed. On a cautionary note, treatment of mice with MRTX1133 alone failed to block tumor growth and tumor-induced mortality. The optimal parameters for non-covalent KRASG12D inhibition have yet to be determined.

In comparison, mice exposed to the pan-RAF inhibitor HM95573 (10 mg/kg/day) and ERK inhibitor SCH772984 (25 mg/kg/day) showed no meaningful tumor regression, either alone or in pairwise combinations with ICIs (Fig. 3D). There were no significant differences in the tumor weights between the groups. Both liver and lymph node metastases were observed in vehicle and drug-treated mice. Similar results were obtained using two different mouse strains and four independent cell lines. Previous studies have shown that RAF inhibitors MLN2480, GW5074, PLX8394, and LSN3074753 exhibit no therapeutic effects on RAS-driven tumors (40, 41). Thus, more potent and selective RAS/MAPK inhibitors are needed to recapitulate the genetic results.

KRAS/MEK Inhibition Enhances Tumor Regression in the KPC Model of Pancreatic Cancer.

Evidence indicates that primary or acquired KRAS independence is at the root of therapy resistance and tumor relapse (42–44). We examined the association between various degrees of KRAS independence and tumor escape. To that end, we used a panel of KPC cell lines with endogenous KRASG12D expression derived from tumors with low and high T cell densities [referred to as TCL (T cell low) and TCH (T cell high), respectively] (Fig. 4A) (45, 46). To a reasonable approximation, these cell types recapitulate major features of human PDAC. Tumor escape mechanisms employed by each cell type involve either T cell exclusion (TCL) or dysfunction (TCH), with a corresponding increase of T cell infiltration in TCH compared to TCL tumors (45). We assigned cell lines to the KRAS and RSK types based on single-cell gene expression signatures of mouse PDAC (11) (Fig. 4B). To assess the effect of tumor immunity, both cell types were injected into the pancreas of nude or C57BL/6J mice lacking CD4 or CD8 T cells. In all cases, TCL lines gave rise to metastatic tumors regardless of the immune status of the recipient mice. In contrast, TCH clones formed metastatic tumors only in nude and CD4 KO mice, but not CD8 KO or WT mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). These results are in line with previous reports showing that antitumor immunity in PDAC is mediated in large part by cytotoxic CD4 T cells (37, 47). Treatment of mice with MRTX1133 plus ICI/CD40 antibodies achieved partial regression of TCH tumors (>80% regression after 2 wk, n = 8), but only moderate regression of TCL tumors (≥30%, n = 4) (Fig. 4C). Combining MRTX1133 with GSK1120212 and ICI/CD40 enhanced tumor regression three to fourfold. Complete remission (absence of tumor for at least 1 mo) was achieved in 8 out of 10 mice (Fig. 4 D and E). Similarly, combining MRTX1133 with GSK1120212 and ICI/CD40 suppressed TCL tumors, albeit to a more modest degree. Four out of the eight treated mice (50%) showed complete regression. No metastases were found in any of the cured mice 2 mo post-treatment (exemplified in SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Tumors that didn’t regress completely showed a significant reduction in size (residual tumor burden less than 20%). IHC staining of the tumor sections demonstrated that the KRAS/MEK inhibitor combination was more effective than the selective KRAS inhibitor alone in attenuating MEK-driven ERK activation and unblocking the influx of T cells into the tumor (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). These results are consistent with earlier reports that MEK inhibition does not perturb T cell function in vivo in tumor therapy (39, 48, 49).

KRAS Independence Promotes Immune Activation and Therapeutic Response.

To explore factors associated with resistance to combination therapy, we tested the effects of co-occurring mutations. A notable feature of human PDAC is the high frequency of SMAD2 and SMAD4 mutations (50% KRAS-type and 23% RSK-type, P = 0.0058 by Fisher’s exact test) and a profound change in SMAD2/4 mRNA expression levels in the KRAS-dependent type (Fig. 4F). To examine the functional impact of these changes, we generated CRISPR-edited KPC cell lines with an emphasis on gain and loss of function mutations in KRAS effector pathways that confer KRAS dependence (RAS/MAPK) or independence (TGF-beta/SMAD). Loss of SMAD4 or other tumor suppressors (e.g., CDKN2A) is notably absent from spontaneous KPC tumors (46, 50). The differences in outcomes were highly context-specific suggesting distinct clinical characteristics. CRISPR-mediated deletion of KRAS severely curtailed tumorigenesis in wild-type mice irrespective of cell type (Fig. 4G). Conversely, sustained activation of the MAPK module via ectopic expression of BRAFV600E or MYCT58A conferred increased tumorigenesis and growth kinetics on TCH but not TCL cells (Fig. 4G). This supports our previous observation that BRAF and MYC are key mediators of KRAS-driven tumor immune suppression (11). The opposite response was observed in SMAD4 knockouts, as loss of SMAD4 had no apparent effect on TCL tumor growth and did not modulate TILs. In contrast, SMAD4 deficiency inhibited tumor development by TCH cells (Fig. 4 G and H). To circumvent the redundant complexity of TGF-beta pathway, we generated isogenic cell lines lacking TGFBR2 (transforming growth factor beta receptor 2), which regulates both SMAD4-dependent and independent signaling. Loss of TGFBR2 more strongly affected TCH tumor growth, and reduced the penetrance of tumors twofold (Fig. 4 G and H). Thus, SMAD4 or TGFBR2 deletion has no discernable impact on KRAS-dependent cells, whereas KRAS-independent cells exhibit selective sensitivity to the knockdown of TGF-beta signaling node. Furthermore, combination treatment of mice with SMAD4 or TGFBR2-null tumors led to 100% complete response (Fig. 4I). Gene Ontology analysis of differentially expressed genes showed that loss of KRAS is associated predominantly with the activation of immune and inflammatory responses (>80 genes), whereas loss of SMAD4 has a broader impact on the molecular pathways involved in metabolic signaling (>400 genes) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 D and E). This fits the logic of how genetic patterns define distinct molecular subtypes and resistance to therapy in pancreatic cancer.

Discussion

In this study, we show that the tumorigenic potential of PDAC cells is inextricably linked to their ability to suppress antitumor immunity. Two major subtypes of KRAS mutant cancer, commonly referred to as KRAS-dependent and independent, have markedly different properties in terms of immune cell infiltration and tumor escape mechanisms. While this classification provides hints at biology, both KRAS mutant tumor subtypes depend upon the integrity of the RAS/MAPK pathway for sustained growth and survival. The data support the concept that treatment of PDAC and, by extension, of other KRAS mutant cancers will require inhibition of KRAS and concurrent activation of antitumor immunity. There are two significant findings in our results. First, genetic KRAS ablation provides therapeutic benefit regardless of KRAS dependency values, whereas the efficacy of KRASG12D pharmacological inhibition, with or without immunotherapy, inversely correlates with KRAS dependence. This distinction is critical, accurately highlighting the malignant behavior and therapy resistance of KRAS mutant cancer. Second, lowering KRAS signaling activity in established tumors promotes immune infiltration, but with a limited antitumor effect, while combining dual KRASG12D and MEK inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade achieves durable regression. The data imply that rational drug combinations can improve KRASG12D inhibitor-based therapeutic efficacy. Dual RAS/MAPK pathway blockade combining KRAS and MEK inhibitors is more effective than the selective KRAS inhibitor alone in attenuating MAPK activation and unblocking the influx of T cells into the tumor. These results are directly applicable to classifying human PDAC samples as being strongly dependent on KRAS signaling or less KRAS-dependent and the ability to predict the more responsive immune environment. The proposed framework can be expected to have a significant impact on treatment standardization.

Metastasis is a hallmark of cancer progression and remains incurable under current therapeutic strategies. Treating metastatic cancer is an enormous challenge owing to its multiorgan involvement, lack of clearly defined mechanisms, and sheer genetic diversity of cancer cells. The results of our study support a purported relationship between oncogenic KRAS signaling activity and the cancer-killing prowess of the immune system. The major implication of our results is that metastatic cancer can be cured. However, there is still a need for cautionary note, as no mouse model can fully recapitulate human disease. First, we found no evidence that KRAS mutation per se determines tumor grade or patterns of recurrence and survival. Therefore, one can only make limited inferences regarding various degrees of KRAS dependency in human cancer. Second, our data do not support a model wherein KRAS gene dosage links intratumor heterogeneity to metastatic ferocity (51). A model in which KRAS copy gains may be passengers to genome duplication is more appropriate for describing the lack of relationship between copy number and gene expression changes. From a statistical standpoint, both KRAS-dependent and independent subtypes have mutant KRAS imbalances (14). Along the same lines, KPC PDAC cells in mice commonly exhibit EMT-like phenotypes despite KRAS amplification (50, 52). Finally, we did not investigate the efficacy of combination chemotherapy such as GAFCP (45). Substantial evidence indicates that genotoxic drugs promote mutation acquisition, metastatic relapse, and cancer mortality (29).

Like any transformative trend, the discovery of bioactive KRAS inhibitors presents both major opportunities and significant challenges. The premise that cancer treatment can be tailored to a single gene must be revisited. In this study, we focused on establishing the basic parameters of KRAS signaling heterogeneity. We analyzed the gene expression profiles of all pancreatic cell types at different developmental stages and determined that the expression pattern of KRAS dependency overlaps with that of ductal progenitor-like cells of the human pancreas (53), supporting the ductal origin of PDAC. The activation of duct-specific genes (ELF3, HNF4A, GATA6, KLF5, etc.) defines this progenitor group (53). Conversely, the signature of KRAS independence appears to be an indicator of differentiation commitment, as it exhibits overlap with the discrete populations of the adult mouse pancreas (54). This relationship was maintained at the single-cell level, demonstrating that our model of oncogenic dependencies is not influenced by tumor purity. Unanswered questions remain regarding the exact cells of origin and oncogenic dependencies of KRAS-independent tumors that cannot be unambiguously mapped to the RSK subtype. From a clinical perspective, the stratification of human PDAC into KRAS-dependent (KRAS-type) and independent (RSK-type) is relatively straightforward. On average, RSK-type tumors comprise 30% of primary PDAC and 15% of metastases. Our data reveal that immunotherapy combined with KRAS and MEK inhibition prevents the growth and spread of RSK-type tumors in preclinical models, suggesting that these combinations are strong candidates for clinical trials. A major challenge is to understand which approach to adopt and which treatment modalities can cure patients with KRAS-dependent malignancies.

Methods

Mammalian Cells and Reagents.

The iKRAS p53R172H/+ cell lines (A9312 and A9993) and KRASG12D p53R172H cell lines (KPC) (FC1199, FC1242, 2838c3, 6419c5, 6499c4), were described (34, 45, 46). Cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS. For proliferation assays, cells were seeded in 6-well plates (4 × 105 cells per well) and counted with a Coulter counter for each time point. Cell viability was measured using propidium iodide (PI) staining. For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockouts, we used sgRNAs specific for KRAS, BRAF, SMAD4 (11), CRAF (5′ gcttggaagacgatcagcaa and 5′ tggctttggactcaaagatg), MEK1 (5′ acattctagtgaactcacgt), MEK2 (5′ catcctggtgaactctcgcg), ERK1 (5′ gggaactgctggggtcgtcc), ERK2 (5′ cccggagatggtccgcgggc), and TGFBR2 (5′-ccttgtagacctcggcgaag-3′) cloned into LentiCRISPRv2. Recombinant viruses were produced by transient transfection of 293T cells according to standard protocols. Knockout efficiency was measured by western blotting. Multiple technical replicates were obtained for each CRISPR-targeted gene. For KRAS knockouts, genomic DNA from clonal cell lines was collected using the QiaAmp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). PCR products for sequencing were amplified using Taq DNA polymerase (Invitrogen) and cloned into a pBluescript vector. At least 10 to 15 bacterial colonies were sequenced per cell line. Two to three clonal cell lines from each knockout (separate technical replicas) were injected into wild-type or nude mice. No clone-specific effects on cancer progression were observed.

Tumorigenicity in Mice.

All animal studies complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal testing and research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Stony Brook University (protocol 2011-0356). We used C57BL/6J, FVB/NJ, CD4 KO, CD8 KO, NU/J, and NOD/SCID mice (The Jackson Laboratory). Experimentally naïve, adult male and female mice (8 to 10 wk old) were housed individually, except for a 2-wk period prior to surgery. Orthotopic injections into the pancreas of mice were performed using standard procedures (55). The animals were observed for tumor development by palpation and bioluminescence. The end point was tumor volume of ~360 mm3. Metastatic lesions were quantified by visual observation, histology, and bioluminescence imaging of resected organs. Frequency of tumor-initiating cells was determined using ELDA software (http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/). Anti-PD1 (BE0146), CTLA4 (BE0164), CD40 (BE0016), or rat IgG2a control antibodies (BioXCell) were administered at a dose of 20 to 30 μg each on days 3, 6, 9 and 12 of treatment via IP injection, unless otherwise specified. All antibodies were diluted to the appropriate concentrations in 100 μL of sterile PBS (pH 7.4). The drinking water was supplemented with neomycin (0.5 mg/mL) and ampicillin (1 mg/mL) (56). MRTX1133 (Chemietek), GSK1120212 (Selleckchem), HM95573 (MCE), and SCH772984 (AChemBlock) were administered daily for 2 wk via IP injection at 5 to 25 mg/kg/day. Vehicle consisted of 5% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% saline. For flow cytometry, the tumor cells were dissociated using collagenase and hyaluronidase (Stem Cell Technologies). Live cells were scored using PI exclusion and stained with FITC or APC-conjugated antibodies to CD3, CD4, CD8, CD19, CD45RA, and F4/80 (eBioscience), and analyzed using FACSCalibur (BD) with CellQuest software.

Pancreatic Cancer Gene List Development.

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma expression profiles generated by TCGA, International Cancer Genome Consortium, and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium were downloaded from cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org), along with additional tumor and clinical annotations. PDAC datasets from the AUMC, PanCuRx Translational Research Initiative (COMPASS), Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (PancSeq), BC Personalized Onco-Genomics program, and Shanghai Cancer Institute were used as described (14, 15, 17, 27–29). Tumor samples were classified into KRAS dependent or independent using curated gene sets (18, 19). Pathway activity scores were determined using curated gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (www.gsea-msigdb.org) (SI Appendix, Table S1). We determined the activity score for each pathway as the sum of the expression levels of genes in this pathway. Tumor immune data compiled by TCGA were used as described (23). Gene Set enrichment analysis was performed using the application available from the Broad Institute (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/). Heat maps and scatter plots were generated using Excel software.

Statistics and Reproducibility.

Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed Student’s t test, ANOVA analysis, Fisher’s exact test, or Wilcoxon test, as appropriate for the dataset. An FDR-adjusted P-value (q-value) was calculated for multiple comparison correction. Data were analyzed using the R Project for statistical computing (https://www.r-project.org). Individual mice and tumor cell lines were considered biological replicates. Statistical details for each experiment are denoted in the corresponding figures and figure legends. Micrographs (H&E and IHC images) represent at least three independent experiments. For the quantification of IHC, the number of fields is indicated and P values between two groups were determined using the two-tailed t test at the 0.05 CI. The data are presented as the mean ± SD. In box and whisker plots, the middle line is plotted at the median, the upper and lower hinges correspond to the first and third quartiles, and the ends of the whisker are set at 1.5 × IQR above the third quartile and 1.5 × IQR below the first quartile (IQR, interquartile range or difference between the 25th and 75th percentiles).

Immunostaining.

Mouse tissues were harvested and processed as described (11). Paraffin-embedded 3-μm sections were processed and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The following antibodies were used for IHC: rabbit polyclonal anti-CD3, CD4 (Biolegend), CD8, and CD68 (Abcam), supported with services of HistoWiz (Brooklyn, NY) and Stony Brook University Research Histology Core facility.

Expression Analysis.

Western blotting was performed using whole cell extracts prepared by lysing cells in buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X100, 40 mM NaVO4, 0.1% SDS, and 1x protease inhibitors (Roche). Western blots were imaged and quantified using Image Studio software (LI-COR).
