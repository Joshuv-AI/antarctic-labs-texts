Unpacking the within-person and between-person dynamics of physical activity and bedtime procrastination: a random intercept cross-lagged mediation model of self-control
Authors: Jixin Wang, Hui Jiang, Baojian Wei, Xiangqi Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBedtime procrastination has emerged as a pervasive health risk behavior among young adults, whereas physical activity represents a fundamental health-promoting behavior. Although the strength model of self-control theoretically suggests that engaging in physical activity may replenish the self-regulatory resources necessary to resist evening procrastination, empirical evidence remains inconclusive. Previous cross-sectional and traditional cross-lagged studies have consistently confounded stable, trait-like differences between individuals with dynamic, state-like fluctuations within individuals. This study aimed to disentangle these effects to investigate the dynamic longitudinal interaction among physical activity, self-control, and bedtime procrastination.MethodsA three-wave longitudinal survey design with one-month intervals was employed. A final matched sample of 385 undergraduate students (mean age = 20.45 years; 53.8% female) completed validated self-report measures assessing physical activity, self-control, and bedtime procrastination across all three time points. To isolate intra-individual temporal dynamics from time-invariant baseline characteristics, the data were analyzed utilizing a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM), controlling for chronotype and time-varying perceived stress.ResultsAt the between-person (trait) level, the random intercepts revealed that average physical activity was significantly and positively correlated with average self-control (r = .41, p < .001) and negatively correlated with average bedtime procrastination (r = − .35, p < .001). Crucially, at the within-person (state) level, a significant temporal cascade emerged. An intra-individual increase in physical activity at Time 1 was prospectively associated with an increase in self-control at Time 2 (β = 0.18, p < .01), which subsequently predicted a decrease in bedtime procrastination at Time 3 (β = −0.24, p < .001). Monte Carlo simulations confirmed a significant within-person longitudinal mediation effect (95% CI [− 0.082, − 0.015]).ConclusionThe findings provide prospective longitudinal evidence that health-promoting and health risk behaviors covary dynamically over time in association with shared cognitive resources. State-level increases in physical activity were prospectively associated with subsequent reductions in bedtime procrastination, potentially through enhanced self-control capacity. These results highlight the practical utility of cross-domain interventions, suggesting that daytime physical exertion may serve as a potential target for interventions aiming to reduce nocturnal self-regulatory failures. These conclusions should be interpreted with caution, given the observational nature of the design.Keywords: Bedtime procrastination, Physical activity, Self-control, RI-CLPM, Longitudinal mediation, Health behaviors

Introduction

The transition to young adulthood is a critical developmental period characterized by increased autonomy, which often leads to the concurrent establishment of both health-promoting and health-compromising behaviors. Among the myriad of lifestyle habits, sleep deprivation has emerged as a pervasive public health concern. A primary behavioral driver of insufficient sleep in the digital era is bedtime procrastination, defined as failing to go to bed at the intended time without any external circumstances preventing a person from doing so [17]. Bedtime procrastination is widely conceptualized as a significant health risk behavior, associated with daytime fatigue, psychological distress, and impaired cognitive functioning [21, 23]. Conversely, physical activity represents a fundamental health-promoting behavior known to enhance physical fitness and psychological well-being [4]. While public health initiatives often target physical activity and sleep hygiene in isolation, contemporary health psychology emphasizes the necessity of exploring the dynamic interaction and covariation between concurrent health-promoting and health risk behaviors [11].

Theoretical frameworks, such as the strength model of self-control, provide a robust conceptual lens for understanding this interaction. According to Baumeister et al., [3], self-control is a finite resource that can be temporarily depleted following self-regulatory exertion (ego depletion) but can also be strengthened over time through regular practice, akin to a muscle. Bedtime procrastination is fundamentally a self-regulatory failure; individuals yield to the immediate gratification of late-night leisure activities (e.g., smartphone usage) because their self-control resources have been depleted throughout the day [15]. Engaging in regular physical activity requires significant self-discipline and involves overriding competing urges to remain sedentary. Extensive research indicates that adherence to a physical activity regimen can enhance general self-control capacity [24]. Therefore, it is plausible to hypothesize that physical activity, as a health-promoting behavior, may bolster self-control resources, thereby equipping individuals to better resist the urge to procrastinate at bedtime.

It is important to acknowledge, however, that the relationship between physical activity and sleep-related behaviors is not exclusively mediated by self-control resources. From a physiological perspective, acute physical exertion also induces somatic fatigue, which may independently reduce arousal and facilitate earlier sleep onset, thereby curtailing the opportunity for bedtime procrastination. These competing short-term (fatigue-driven) and longer-term (self-regulatory) pathways of exercise have important implications for how effects manifest across different time scales and should be considered when interpreting the present findings [31]. Moreover, it is equally plausible that the directionality could be reversed: individuals with higher trait-level self-control may be more likely to engage in regular physical activity and simultaneously less prone to bedtime procrastination, rendering physical activity essentially epiphenomenal. The present study therefore adopts a theoretically cautious stance and employs design and analytic strategies intended to evaluate competing explanations.

Regarding the theoretical framework, it is important to note that the ego depletion hypothesis itself has faced significant empirical scrutiny. A large-scale multilab preregistered replication effort encompassing 23 laboratories (N = 2,141) did not find an ego depletion effect that was significantly different from zero [12]. These findings have prompted ongoing debate about the boundary conditions and replicability of the effect. Given these concerns, the present study adopts a cautious stance, treating the strength model as one plausible theoretical lens among others, while acknowledging that multiple mechanisms may contribute to the observed associations between physical activity and self-regulatory outcomes.

Despite the theoretical rationale linking physical activity, self-control, and bedtime procrastination, the existing empirical literature suffers from a critical methodological limitation. The vast majority of studies investigating these associations have relied on cross-sectional designs or traditional cross-lagged panel models (CLPM). Traditional CLPMs implicitly assume that all individuals vary around a single common mean, thereby confounding between-person differences (i.e., trait-like stability) with within-person changes (i.e., state-like fluctuations) [13]. Consequently, when a traditional CLPM indicates that physical activity predicts lower bedtime procrastination, it remains ambiguous whether this effect exists because individuals who are generally more active also tend to be less prone to procrastination (a trait-level correlation), or because an individual who exercises more than their usual amount during a specific period will experience a subsequent reduction in their own procrastination (a state-level process). From an intervention standpoint, the latter within-person process is far more relevant, as behavioral interventions aim to elicit change within the individual over time [13].

To address these empirical and methodological gaps, the present study utilizes a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) to disentangle the trait-like and state-like dynamics among physical activity, self-control, and bedtime procrastination across three measurement waves. At the between-person level, we hypothesize that stable individual differences in physical activity will be positively correlated with self-control and negatively correlated with bedtime procrastination. At the within-person level, we propose a longitudinal mediation hypothesis. Specifically, we hypothesize that an intra-individual increase in physical activity at a given time point will be prospectively associated with a subsequent intra-individual increase in self-control, which in turn will be prospectively associated with a subsequent intra-individual decrease in bedtime procrastination. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we account for potential confounding variables, including chronotype and perceived daytime stress, which have been robustly linked to circadian preferences and self-regulatory depletion [18]. We further evaluate the robustness of the proposed directional model by comparing it against theoretically plausible alternative models.

Method

Participants and procedure

The present study employed a three-wave longitudinal survey design. Participants were recruited from a large public university in China through campus advertisements and online student forums. To capture the dynamic fluctuations in behavior during a standard academic semester, the three measurement waves (T1, T2, and T3) were spaced approximately one month apart. T1 occurred in early October, T2 in early November, and T3 in early December. Prior to data collection, the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the university. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

At T1, a total of 512 undergraduate students completed the baseline survey. Over the subsequent two waves, panel attrition occurred due to graduation, scheduling conflicts, or lack of response. At T2, 445 participants responded, and at T3, 398 participants completed the survey. After implementing a rigorous data cleaning procedure—which involved removing responses with failed attention checks or systematic missing data patterns—a final matched sample of 385 participants (75.1% retention rate) was retained for the main analyses. Independent samples t-tests indicated no significant differences between the retained sample and the attrited participants regarding T1 age, gender, physical activity, self-control, or bedtime procrastination, suggesting that data were missing at random [19]. The final sample comprised 178 males (46.2%) and 207 females (53.8%), with a mean age of 20.45 years (SD = 1.32).

Measures

All self-report measures were translated and adapted following standardized back-translation procedures to ensure semantic equivalence.

Physical activity was assessed using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF; [9]). Participants reported the frequency and duration of vigorous, moderate, and walking activities over the past seven days. Following the IPAQ scoring protocol, total physical activity was calculated as continuous scores and expressed in Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) minutes per week. To correct for positive skewness, MET-minutes were naturally log-transformed prior to structural equation modeling.

Self-control was measured using the 13-item Brief Self-Control Scale (BSCS; [30]). Participants rated their agreement with statements such as “I am good at resisting temptation” on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all like me) to 5 (very much like me). After reverse-scoring appropriate items, higher mean scores indicated higher levels of trait and state self-control. The internal consistency of the scale in this study was strong across all waves (Cronbach’s αs ranged from 0.84 to 0.87).

Bedtime procrastination was evaluated using the 9-item Bedtime Procrastination Scale (BPS; [17]). Items such as “I often still do other things when it is time to go to sleep” were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). A higher mean score reflected a greater severity of bedtime procrastination. The scale demonstrated excellent reliability across the three time points (Cronbach’s αs ranged from 0.88 to 0.91).

To isolate the specific temporal relationships among the core variables, chronotype and perceived stress were included as time-invariant and time-varying covariates, respectively. Chronotype was assessed at T1 using the reduced Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ; [1]), where higher scores indicate a morning preference. Perceived stress was measured at all three time points using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10; [8]).

Statistical analysis strategy

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and Mplus version 8.3 [22]. Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson zero-order correlations, and an evaluation of common method variance using Harman’s single-factor test. To address missing data in the longitudinal modeling, Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation was utilized. Because the variables exhibited slight deviations from multivariate normality, the robust Maximum Likelihood estimator (MLR) was employed for all structural equation models.

Prior to hypothesis testing, longitudinal measurement invariance was assessed for self-control and bedtime procrastination to ensure that the constructs held the same meaning across the three time points [5, 26]. We evaluated configural, metric (weak), and scalar (strong) invariance by sequentially constraining factor loadings and item intercepts to be equal across waves. Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) [16]. Invariance was supported if the change in CFI (ΔCFI) was ≤ 0.010 and the change in RMSEA (ΔRMSEA) was ≤ 0.015 [6].

Following the establishment of measurement invariance, we specified the RI-CLPM as described by Hamaker et al., [13]. First, stable trait-like differences were captured by creating three random intercept latent factors (one for each construct: PA, SC, BP), with factor loadings constrained to 1 across the three waves. Second, within-person state-like fluctuations were calculated as the time-specific deviations from these random intercepts. It is critical to clarify the interpretation of these within-person deviation scores (Δ). Each individual’s random intercept captures their stable average level of a construct across all three waves. The within-person deviation at each time point (ΔX_t) therefore represents the extent to which that individual’s score at time t is elevated or suppressed relative to their own personal average—not relative to the sample mean. A positive ΔPA_T1, for instance, indicates that an individual exercised more than their own typical amount during wave 1. Consequently, the statement “a positive deviation in PA at T1 predicts a positive deviation in SC at T2” should be read as: among individuals, being above one’s own PA average at T1 prospectively predicts being above one’s own SC average one month later. This is a genuine temporal, intra-individual prospective association that is orthogonal to between-person differences and cannot be reduced to a concurrent cross-sectional association [13]. Autoregressive paths and cross-lagged paths were then specified exclusively among these within-person centered latent variables. The indirect mediation effect at the within-person level (T1 within-PA → T2 within-SC → T3 within-BP) was tested utilizing a Monte Carlo simulation with 20,000 replications to compute 95% asymmetric confidence intervals (CIs).

To evaluate the robustness and directionality of the proposed model, we additionally estimated a series of alternative RI-CLPMs: (a) a reversed model in which the cross-lagged paths between PA and SC were switched (i.e., ΔSC → ΔPA, rather than ΔPA → ΔSC), with all other pathways unchanged; (b) a model excluding SC as a mediator, in which BP was predicted only by PA; and (c) a model excluding PA, in which BP was predicted only by SC. These four models were compared using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating superior fit [25].

Harman’s single-factor test revealed that the first unrotated principal component accounted for 24.31% of the total variance, which is well below the 50% threshold, indicating that common method bias was not a pervasive issue in this dataset. As presented in Table 1, the descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation coefficients yielded anticipated patterns. Across all three waves, physical activity was significantly and positively correlated with self-control, and negatively correlated with bedtime procrastination. Furthermore, self-control and bedtime procrastination exhibited strong negative associations both concurrently and cross-sectionally.

Random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) results

To evaluate the superiority of separating between- and within-person variance, we initially estimated a traditional CLPM. The traditional CLPM exhibited sub-optimal fit to the data (χ²/df = 4.12, CFI = 0.895, RMSEA = 0.082, SRMR = 0.075). Subsequently, the proposed RI-CLPM was estimated, adjusting for chronotype and time-varying perceived stress. The RI-CLPM provided an excellent fit to the data (χ²/df = 1.65, CFI = 0.978, TLI = 0.965, RMSEA = 0.038, SRMR = 0.031). The conceptual structure of the model and the significant statistical pathways are visualized in Fig. 1.

Conceptual diagram and standardized path coefficients of the RI-CLPM

Figure 1 presents the conceptual structure of the RI-CLPM. The three large circles (RI-PA, RI-SC, RI-BP) at the top represent the random intercept factors, capturing stable between-person (trait-level) variance in physical activity, self-control, and bedtime procrastination, respectively, averaged across the entire study period. The rectangular boxes (PA1–PA3, SC1–SC3, BP1–BP3) represent the observed time-specific scores at each measurement wave. The small circles at the bottom (ΔPA1–ΔPA3, ΔSC1–ΔSC3, ΔBP1–ΔBP3) represent the within-person centered deviation scores—indicating the extent to which each individual’s score at a given time point deviates from their own stable average. All autoregressive and cross-lagged paths are estimated exclusively among these deviation scores, ensuring that the within-person dynamics are fully separated from between-person trait differences.

Between-person (trait) level

The between-person correlations, representing the associations among the random intercepts, correspond to trait-level differences averaged across the three months. As shown in Table 3, the random intercept of physical activity was significantly and positively correlated with the random intercept of self-control (r = .41, p < .001) and significantly negatively correlated with the random intercept of bedtime procrastination (r = − .35, p < .001). Furthermore, trait self-control and trait bedtime procrastination were strongly negatively correlated (r = − .56, p < .001). These findings indicate that, generally speaking, individuals who are more physically active tend to possess higher overall self-control and engage in less bedtime procrastination.

Within-person (state) level

The within-person autoregressive and cross-lagged effects encapsulate state-like temporal fluctuations. The autoregressive paths were largely weak or non-significant for most variables across both intervals (T1–T2 and T2–T3), with the exception of a significant positive autoregressive effect for self-control from T1 to T2 (β = 0.12, p < .05; T2 to T3: β = 0.10, p = .071). The autoregressive paths for physical activity were non-significant at both intervals (T1–T2: β = 0.09, p = .062; T2–T3: β = 0.08, p = .083), and those for bedtime procrastination were similarly non-significant (T1–T2: β = 0.08, p = .154; T2–T3: β = 0.07, p = .198). This pattern suggests that while an individual’s deviation from their own baseline is generally transient, state-level fluctuations in self-control may exhibit some brief persistence into the subsequent month before dissipating.

The non-significant autoregressive paths for physical activity are noteworthy. They indicate that individual deviations in physical activity above or below one’s own average at one time point did not persist reliably into the following month. This pattern is consistent with research documenting high intra-individual variability in exercise behavior, particularly among undergraduate students whose schedules fluctuate considerably with academic demands [7]. Importantly, non-significant autoregressive paths do not preclude meaningful cross-lagged effects; rather, they indicate that these upstream effects operate prospectively across time lags, even when the behavior itself is not highly persistent [13].

Crucially, the cross-lagged paths revealed the dynamic directionality of behavioral interactions (see Table 3; Fig. 2). A positive deviation in physical activity at T1 (i.e., exercising more than one’s own average) was prospectively associated with a positive deviation in self-control at T2 (β = 0.18, p < .01). Symmetrically, a positive deviation in physical activity at T2 predicted higher self-control at T3 (β = 0.16, p < .01). Moreover, higher self-control relative to one’s baseline at T1 predicted reduced bedtime procrastination at T2 (β = −0.22, p < .001), a pattern that was replicated from T2 to T3 (β = −0.24, p < .001). Interestingly, there was a direct within-person effect of physical activity on bedtime procrastination from T1 to T2 (β = −0.12, p < .05), but not from T2 to T3 (β = −0.06, p = .215). No significant reverse cross-lagged effects (e.g., BP predicting subsequent PA) emerged at the within-person level.

Standardized within-person estimates in the autoregressive cross-lagged network

The temporal asymmetry in the direct PA → BP path warrants specific interpretation. The direct path was significant at the T1–T2 interval (β = −0.12, p < .05) but non-significant at T2–T3 (β = −0.06, p = .215). One plausible explanation is that at the T1–T2 interval, before self-control had fully accumulated the regulatory benefits from prior physical activity, a direct physiological pathway—whereby somatic fatigue from daytime exercise reduces the motivational and arousal resources available for nocturnal leisure activities—may have contributed to reduced bedtime procrastination. By the T2–T3 interval, the effects of physical activity on bedtime procrastination appear to have been largely channelled through the self-control mediation pathway, rendering the direct path non-significant. This temporal shift is theoretically consistent with a process in which repeated behavioral engagement progressively consolidates the self-regulatory mechanism as the primary driver of health behavior change [3, 24].

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the within-person network demonstrates a clear temporal cascade whereby health-promoting behavior is prospectively associated with restoration of psychological resources, which subsequently predicts lower health risk behavior.

Longitudinal mediation analysis

To explicitly test the hypothesis that state-level fluctuations in self-control mediate the relationship between physical activity and bedtime procrastination over time, we analyzed the specific indirect effect at the within-person level: T1 Within-PA → T2 Within-SC → T3 Within-BP. Utilizing a Monte Carlo simulation approach, the unstandardized indirect effect was estimated at − 0.045. The 95% asymmetric confidence interval ranged from − 0.082 to − 0.015, which explicitly excludes zero (see Fig. 3). This result supports the presence of a longitudinal mediation effect; specifically, when an individual is above their own typical level of physical activity at T1, the prospective reduction in bedtime procrastination two months later is significantly mediated by an interim elevation in self-control resources at T2.

Alternative model comparison

To evaluate whether the observed pattern might alternatively reflect self-control driving subsequent physical activity, or whether the mediation pathway through self-control is necessary to explain the PA–BP association, we estimated three alternative RI-CLPMs and compared them against the proposed model using AIC and BIC.

Model 1 (Proposed): ΔPA → ΔSC → ΔBP. This is the hypothesized model as reported above (χ²/df = 1.65, CFI = 0.978, RMSEA = 0.038).

Model 2 (Reversed PA–SC paths): ΔSC → ΔPA (and ΔPA → ΔBP direct). In this model, the cross-lagged paths between PA and SC were reversed (i.e., ΔSC T1 → ΔPA T2 and ΔSC T2 → ΔPA T3), while all other pathways remained unchanged. The reversed-path model yielded substantially inferior fit compared to the proposed model (χ²/df = 2.31, CFI = 0.942, RMSEA = 0.059, SRMR = 0.048). The cross-lagged paths from SC to PA in the alternative model were non-significant (β = 0.06, p = .312 for T1–T2; β = 0.05, p = .428 for T2–T3), whereas the proposed direction (ΔPA → ΔSC) remained significant in the original model.

Model 3 (PA only, no SC mediator): ΔPA → ΔBP direct only. Removing SC from the model resulted in markedly poorer fit (χ²/df = 3.18, CFI = 0.911, RMSEA = 0.073, SRMR = 0.062) and a substantially attenuated direct PA → BP path (β = −0.08, p = .089), underscoring the importance of SC as a mediating mechanism.

Model 4 (SC only, no PA predictor): ΔSC → ΔBP direct only. This model fit was intermediate (χ²/df = 2.07, CFI = 0.957, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.039), and the SC → BP paths remained significant, consistent with the robust role of self-control. However, the model could not account for the observed prospective association between PA and SC.

Formal model comparison indices are summarized in Table 4. The proposed model (Model 1) yielded the lowest AIC and BIC values, supporting its superiority over the three alternatives. Nevertheless, in accordance with Orth et al., [25], we emphasize that model comparisons within the RI-CLPM framework support the directionality of prospective associations but do not license strong causal inferences. Additionally, while Bayesian model selection approaches such as variational Bayes model selection (VBMS; [29]) could provide complementary estimates of model evidence, exceedance probability, and model frequency at the individual level, such analyses were beyond the scope of the current implementation given the nature of the data and software used. Future research employing individual-level Bayesian estimation would be well-positioned to extend these findings.

Discussion

The primary objective of the current investigation was to rigorously elucidate the dynamic, longitudinal interaction between a prominent health-promoting behavior—physical activity—and a pervasive health risk behavior—bedtime procrastination. By situating this inquiry within a self-regulatory resource framework and deploying a sophisticated random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) across three measurement waves, this research successfully disentangled stable between-person covariations from dynamic within-person fluctuations. The findings revealed that at the trait level, physical activity, self-control, and bedtime procrastination operate as an intertwined lifestyle phenotype. More critically, at the intra-individual state level, the results unveiled a prospective longitudinal mediation pattern: temporal increases in physical activity were prospectively associated with enhanced self-control resources, which subsequently predicted lower bedtime procrastination. These associations should be interpreted as prospective within-person associations rather than causal effects, given the observational design. These findings transcend traditional cross-sectional correlations, offering longitudinal empirical insights into the temporal mechanisms governing behavioral covariation and directly responding to the critical need for understanding how disparate health behaviors interact dynamically over time [11].

At the between-person level, the correlational patterns firmly substantiated our initial hypotheses and corroborated extensive existing literature [17, 24]. The data demonstrated that individuals who generally maintain higher average volumes of physical activity inherently possess higher trait self-control and exhibit lower overall tendencies toward bedtime procrastination. This stable, trait-like association robustly aligns with the conceptualization of a “healthy lifestyle cluster.” It suggests that the propensity to actively engage in health-promoting behaviors and the capability to successfully resist health risk behaviors are structurally anchored within an individual’s enduring psychological disposition. From a neurobiological and physiological standpoint, individuals engaged in long-term, regular physical activity consistently display enhanced structural integrity and functional connectivity in the prefrontal cortex—the exact neuroanatomical substrate responsible for superior executive functioning, inhibitory control, and baseline self-regulatory capacity [14, 20]. Consequently, active individuals operate with a biologically optimized cognitive architecture that inherently reduces their susceptibility to evening behavioral dysregulation. However, these between-person associations could also reflect a common underlying trait—individuals with higher self-control may be more likely both to exercise regularly and to go to bed on time, without any causal relationship between the two behaviors. The RI-CLPM’s separation of trait-level variance from state-level fluctuations is specifically designed to address this confound. While these between-person associations are invaluable for identifying generalized risk profiles, relying solely on these static traits severely limits the theoretical capacity to understand the temporal mechanisms necessary for designing behavioral interventions [13].

The most theoretically informative contributions of this study emerge from the within-person analyses. By strictly partialling out time-invariant trait confounders, the RI-CLPM successfully isolated authentic intra-individual temporal associations. A pivotal finding was that a positive deviation in physical activity at a specific time point was prospectively associated with a subsequent positive deviation in self-control. This provides longitudinal within-person evidence consistent with the “spillover effect” or “cross-domain transfer” of self-regulation posited by the strength model of self-control [3]. Engaging in physical activity demands substantial self-discipline to overcome sedentary inertia, endure physical discomfort, and delay immediate gratification. According to the strength model, while such acute exertion may cause immediate, localized ego depletion in the hours immediately following a workout, repeated and sustained engagement acts akin to muscular hypertrophy. Over a longer temporal lag—such as the one-month intervals utilized in this study—this repeated exertion may expand the individual’s overall global reservoir of self-control resources [24, 28]. This temporal specificity is a key interpretive point: the one-month measurement interval was intentionally selected to capture cumulative habit-level gains rather than acute depletion effects, which would be expected to resolve within hours to days. The significant cross-lagged effect of PA on SC at this interval therefore reflects sustained-practice processes rather than acute fatigue, and is consistent with prior longitudinal work demonstrating self-regulatory improvements over multi-week practice periods [24]. Our within-person data are consistent with this restorative mechanism: when individuals exercised more than their own historical average during a given month, their state-level self-control was elevated during the subsequent measurement period.

A related question concerns the temporal dynamics between self-control and bedtime procrastination. One might query why the effect of SC on BP also manifests with a one-month lag rather than concurrently within the RI-CLPM. We propose two complementary explanations. First, the self-control latent variable, as assessed by the 13-item BSCS, captures a dispositional state-level elevation in perceived regulatory capacity—not a single evening’s willpower reserve. A sustained, above-average period of self-control during month T may shift an individual’s behavioral routines, social commitments, and environmental arrangements (e.g., digital device usage norms) in ways that only translate into measurable reductions in bedtime procrastination in the following month T + 1 [2]. Second, given the monthly measurement granularity, concurrent effects at the same wave are statistically absorbed into the RI-CLPM’s time-invariant covariance structure; the cross-lagged paths capture only the prospective unique variance [13].

Crucially, this elevated state-level self-control was prospectively associated with the targeted health risk behavior. The data demonstrated that elevated self-control, relative to one’s own baseline, predicted an intra-individual reduction in bedtime procrastination. Bedtime procrastination is widely recognized in contemporary sleep psychology not merely as a time-management deficit, but as an explicit failure of self-regulation. Individuals succumb to the immediate gratification of late-night digital consumption specifically because their cognitive resources have been cumulatively depleted by the navigational demands, academic pressures, and emotional regulations required throughout the day [15, 23]. When an individual’s self-control is temporarily elevated—associated with their preceding state-level increase in physical activity—they may possess the sufficient cognitive bandwidth necessary to close the intention-behavior gap late at night. Therefore, the significant longitudinal mediation effect structurally supports that health-promoting behaviors do not merely coexist passively alongside diminished risk behaviors, but that their prospective temporal co-occurrence is consistent with a shared self-regulatory mechanism.

Regarding potential alternative explanations, the model comparison results in Sect. Alternative model comparison provide evidence favoring the proposed PA → SC → BP directionality over the reversed model in which SC drives PA. The reversed-path model yielded inferior fit and non-significant SC → PA cross-lagged paths. This finding argues against the simplest alternative explanation—that higher self-control simply leads to more exercise and, independently, to less bedtime procrastination. However, we acknowledge that the present design cannot rule out all possible confounders. One important alternative explanation warranting discussion is that time-varying third factors—such as changes in academic workload across the semester—may have driven the observed lagged associations. It is plausible that students with higher self-control at the beginning of the semester engaged in more PA and showed lower BP simultaneously; as the semester progressed and academic demands increased, these same students may have reduced their PA while maintaining relatively stable SC and low BP. Such a pattern could produce lagged associations in which higher PA at T1 predicts higher SC and lower BP at T2, even if PA per se is not mechanistically involved. The inclusion of time-varying perceived stress as a covariate partially addresses this concern; however, it cannot fully rule out unmeasured time-varying confounds. Future studies should include direct measures of academic workload across waves to evaluate this alternative explanation.

It is also worth noting that the framing of findings within the ego depletion framework requires additional caution. The ego depletion hypothesis, as a theoretical foundation for the strength model of self-control, has faced substantial empirical challenges. A large-scale multilab preregistered replication [12] did not replicate the effect, though the debate regarding the appropriate operationalization of depletion remains active. The present findings are therefore interpreted within a broader self-regulatory resource framework consistent with—but not exclusively dependent upon—the ego depletion hypothesis. The observed within-person associations could be explained by several complementary mechanisms, including physiological fatigue effects, improvements in executive function via aerobic exercise, or reinforcement of health-promoting behavioral routines.

Multiple behavioral mechanisms may account for how elevated self-control translates into reduced bedtime procrastination. These include enhanced resistance to digital media temptations [15], more effective implementation of pre-sleep intentions, and greater adherence to established sleep schedules. Future research explicitly measuring evening technology use, post-exercise fatigue, and executive functioning alongside physical activity and self-control assessments would be better positioned to disentangle the relative contributions of these pathways.

Interestingly, the temporal interaction between these behaviors appeared largely asymmetric within the specific temporal parameters of this study. While state-level fluctuations in physical activity prospectively predicted downstream changes in bedtime procrastination via self-control, there were no significant reverse cross-lagged effects indicating that prior fluctuations in bedtime procrastination undermined future physical activity at the within-person level. This lack of reciprocal association may suggest that while sleep deprivation undoubtedly causes acute fatigue, the intention to engage in physical activity over a broader one-month period might be governed by different motivational constructs, such as autonomous motivation or scheduled behavioral routines, which can override temporary lethargy [7]. Furthermore, the non-significant autoregressive paths for PA—indicating that above-average exercise in one month did not predict above-average exercise in the following month—are consistent with this interpretation and suggest that, in this undergraduate sample, exercise behavior may be governed more by external scheduling and social norms than by stable internal self-regulatory states.

From a practical perspective, the insights derived from this state-level mediation model are pertinent for public health practitioners and cognitive-behavioral therapists. Traditional interventions targeting sleep hygiene and bedtime procrastination frequently adopt a direct, symptom-focused approach. Counselors often advise students to establish strict digital curfews or rely on sheer willpower to put their devices away at night. However, because self-control may be at its nadir during the late evening, such direct mandates often suffer from low compliance and high relapse rates [10]. The present findings suggest that interventions can also strategically target the daytime environment by promoting physical activity. Furthermore, because the prospective association operates at the within-person level, small, relative behavioral increases above the individual’s own baseline—rather than absolute transformations in physical activity level—may be sufficient to initiate this protective cascade. By leveraging the potential cross-domain transfer of self-control from the physical exertion domain to the sleep hygiene domain, practitioners may be able to indirectly support health risk behavior reduction. These clinical implications, however, must be tempered by the observational nature of the study, and should ultimately be evaluated in randomized controlled trial designs.

Despite the rigorous methodological framework, several limitations must be explicitly acknowledged. First, the reliance on self-report questionnaires to assess physical activity duration, self-control capacity, and bedtime procrastination inevitably introduces potential methodological artifacts, including retrospective recall biases and social desirability effects. Future studies should incorporate objective, ambulatory physiological assessments—such as wrist actigraphy to measure sleep onset latency and accelerometer-based tracking to quantify physical activity—to validate these associations against objective data [31]. Second, while the one-month measurement interval was strategically selected to capture the sustained, practice-driven enhancement of self-regulatory capacity [24], the optimal measurement interval remains an open empirical question. The strength model of self-control posits two temporally distinct processes: acute depletion following isolated exertion (operating on a scale of hours) and longer-term hypertrophy following sustained practice (operating over weeks to months; [3]). The cross-lagged effects observed in this study are theoretically consistent with the latter mechanism; however, future investigations employing intensive longitudinal methods—such as ecological momentary assessment (EMA) paired with dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM)—would be needed to precisely map how a specific bout of daytime exercise influences that evening’s procrastination behavior [27]. Third, the data were collected across a single academic semester in China, which may introduce time-varying academic demand as a potential confound (see discussion above). Fourth, the current sample was restricted to undergraduate students, which limits the broader generalizability of the findings. Fifth, the present design—even with the RI-CLPM framework—cannot fully establish causal directionality. The model comparison results favor the proposed directionality, but observational longitudinal designs remain susceptible to unmeasured confounding. Randomized experimental designs are needed to confirm the causal interpretation. Replicating this RI-CLPM framework across diverse cultural contexts, older working adults, and clinical populations remains a vital imperative for future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study advances the longitudinal empirical landscape of health and behavioral psychology by mapping the dynamic, prospective interplay between physical activity and bedtime procrastination. By deploying a sophisticated RI-CLPM approach, the research successfully partitioned the confounding influence of stable between-person trait differences from authentic intra-individual psychological processes occurring over time. The findings provide prospective within-person evidence that state-level elevations in physical activity were associated with subsequent enhancement in self-control capacity, which in turn predicted lower bedtime procrastination. These associations were supported by model comparisons favoring the proposed directionality over competing alternatives, though causal conclusions remain premature given the observational design. By conceptualizing health behaviors not as isolated habits, but as an interconnected temporal network linked by shared cognitive resources, this study offers a theoretically grounded and empirically supported new perspective for intervention design: utilizing upstream, daytime health promotion as a potential psychological lever to address downstream, nighttime health risks.
