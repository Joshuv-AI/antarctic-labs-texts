Glycosylated graphene-nylon fiber: A new material platform to balance the nanotoxicity and bioactivity for anti-infective therapy
Authors: Gefei Li, Juan Mo, Jie Jing, Lucen Li, Yuhao Ju, Wenxiao Ma, Ziyao Zhuang, Xuanxuan Yu, Jie Deng, Jing Lin, Wei Li, Zhenyang Luo, Yan Jiang, Shin-ichiro Shoda, Xin-Shan Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanomaterials show great potential for biomedical applications, particularly in addressing challenges such as drug-resistant bacterial infections. However, their clinical use is hindered by considerable obstacles, mainly due to the potential risks associated with nanotoxicity. One effective strategy to mitigate nanotoxicity is the use of nanocomposites, which involve dispersing and immobilizing nanomaterials within polymer matrices. Unfortunately, it often results in a significant reduction in bioactivity. To overcome the challenge of balancing nanotoxicity and bioactivity, novel glycosylated graphene-nylon fibers were designed and synthesized by using an in situ polymerization technique combined with the surface glycan-engineering. By covalently immobilizing graphene nanosheets within the polyamide matrix, the potential health risks associated with graphene migration in vivo were prevented. The glycan coatings on the surface of the graphene-nylon fiber enhanced the antibacterial and antiviral properties through a synergistic effect of pathogen enrichment and oxidative stress. This novel material platform demonstrated outstanding therapeutic efficacy in both healthy mouse wound models and diabetic mouse wound models with drug-resistant bacterial infections, significantly accelerating the wound healing process. Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms were comprehensively explored. The glycosylated graphene-nylon fibers exemplify an innovative approach to balancing nanotoxicity and bioactivity, and will greatly facilitate the biomedical applications of nanomaterials.Keywords: Graphene, Nylon fiber, In situ polymerization, Interfacial glycosylation, Anti-infective therapy

Introduction

The repeated emergence of antibiotic resistance is recognized as one of the greatest threats to global public health [1]. To address this issue, nanotechnology and nanomaterials have been developed to create entirely new antibacterial mechanisms [2]. Currently, graphene and other two-dimensional (2D) layered nanomaterials are acknowledged as effective therapeutic choice for treating bacterial infections through non-antibiotic mechanisms, including the nano-knives effect and the generation of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH) [[3], [4], [5]], which lead to the destruction of cell walls and membranes [6]. In vitro and in vivo studies of graphene-based nanomaterials have demonstrated their abilities to prevent pathogenic bacterial, fungal, and viral infections [7]. In addition, graphene and its derivatives have demonstrated significant potential in the treatment of neural repair and stem cell differentiation [8]. Liu et al. reported the application of graphene films in regulating the differentiation of adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) into neurons through electromagnetic effects [9]. The use of reduced graphene oxide incorporated into polycaprolactone (PCL) scaffolds was also found to enhance ADSCs differentiation by providing both electrical and topological cues [10]. More recently, the magnetic graphene oxide nanoparticles were employed in promoting the differentiation of human neural progenitor cells into neurons [11]. However, along with the increasing applications of nanomaterials, concerns about their potential nanotoxicity have also grown [12]. Recent studies have shown that nanoparticles (NPs) can cause serious health effects due to their high penetrability and reactivity with various biological tissues [13]. For instance, clinically-used silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) show strong antibacterial properties, but they can also lead to negative effects like argyria [14]. To mitigate these health risks, it has been found that incorporating nanostructures into polymers may significantly reduce their nanotoxicity and improve their safety profiles [15].

At present, there are two primary methods for preparing polymer nanocomposites: one is physical blending, and the other is in situ polymerization [16]. In a traditional physical process, polymer is melted at high temperature and mixed with NPs using strong shearing to homogenize the filler dispersion within the molten polymer matrix. This mechanically noncovalent blending of polymers with NPs is challenging due to the high surface energy of nanofillers and their inherent incompatibility with polymers, often leading to aggregates, agglomerations or clusters [17]. In situ polymerization involves directly mixing functionalized NPs with polymer monomers. During the polymerization process, the NPs act as cross-linking points and directly participate in the reaction, forming covalent bonds with the polymer matrix [18]. This method effectively addresses the issues of nanoparticle dispersion and interfacial compatibility within the polymer matrix [19]. In this work, we employed the in situ polymerization to produce graphene-nylon fibers (GNFs) for the first time, resulting in a fibrous textile material with good interfacial antibacterial activity and high biocompatibility. The covalent immobilization of graphene within a macroscopic polymer matrix can effectively limit their penetration and accumulation in tissues, which are critical factors for biomedical applications. However, compared with homogeneously-dispersed graphene, the antimicrobial capacity of immobilized nanocomposites is often diminished due to the contact-killing mechanism [20]. The effective antibacterial range is limited to the fiber surface, making it insufficient to meet the clinical demand for treating wounds with deep infections. Therefore, achieving a balance between addressing toxic challenges and enhancing antibacterial activities is crucial for the successful and safe integration of GNFs into clinical applications.

Interfacial glycoengineering could open a new avenue to balance material features [21]. The surface-immobilized glycans can interact with lectins and pathogens in a multivalent manner [22], leading to a synergistic glycocluster effect [23]. Several antibacterial applications based on glycosylated interfaces have been reported [24]. For instance, glycan-coated particles showed substantial adhesion to bacteria, as evidenced by their ability to induce bacterial aggregation [25]. A non-antibiotic approach to combat drug-resistant infections was developed using nanorods decorated with glycomimetic polymers to specifically bind against P. aeruginosa [26]. In addition to these glycosylated nanoparticles, modified glycan-layers on the surfaces of polymer materials were discovered for the pathogen-selective separation. Glycopolymer-functionalized porous gels were prepared and applied for affinity chromatography, allowing for the selective capture and pre-enrichment of bacteria [27]. Furthermore, glycan-conjugated chitosan fibers were reported as effective filter materials for controlling the transmission of the influenza virus [28]. Despite the significant microbial capture and aggregation abilities demonstrated by glycosylated coatings on various material surfaces, the potential of glycan-coated nanocomposites is still largely unexplored, particularly for the synergistic antimicrobial effects of nanocomposites.

Based on the consideration of balancing nanotoxicity and antibacterial activity, we attempt to synthesize a graphene-nylon nanocomposite fiber to reduce the nanotoxicity, and subsequently try to enhance its antibacterial activity through the glycosylation modification on the fiber material's surface (Fig. 1a). To that end, utilizing in situ polymerization of amine-functionalized graphene and caprolactam monomer, we first prepared a graphene-polyamide nanocomposite, in which the graphene was uniformly dispersed within the nylon matrix and covalently linked to the polymer chains via amide bonds (Fig. 1b). Subsequent glycosylation modification of the fiber surface endowed the GNF with the enhanced anti-infection capacity through the synergistic effect of glycan-mediated capture/enrichment of pathogenic microorganisms and graphene-based interfacial antibacterial activity (Fig. 1c). It should be noted that traditional immobilized strategies are unable to functionalize fiber material surfaces that lack reactive sites [29]. Therefore, we developed a bioinspired adhesion strategy based on a three-step one-pot process for surface glycan modification of GNFs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to fabricate glycosylated GNFs and explore their biomedical applications in anti-infective therapy. We systematically investigated the in vitro antibacterial and antiviral activities, as well as the biosafety of the glycosylated GNF materials. Subsequently, using both healthy mouse and diabetic mouse models, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of this fabric material on wound infections caused by drug-resistant P. aeruginosa infections. The histological evaluation during the treatment process and exploration of the synergistic antibacterial mechanism were also conducted.

Results and discussion

Development of a one-pot three-step interfacial glycosylation strategy

Currently, there are two main strategies for the fabrication of glycosylated surfaces: polymerization from the surface or grafting onto the surface. Typically, the initiator is first immobilized on the substrates, which then allows for the subsequent polymerization of glycomonomers [30]. However, these methods are not applicable for the modification of nylon fibers with a chemically inert surface. In contrast to traditional surface-grafting methods, mussel-inspired surface chemistry offers a versatile approach for multifunctional coatings through the self-polymerization of dopamine (Dopa) [31]. Previous studies have reported the synthesis of glycopolymers with surface-adhering properties by copolymerizing sugar- and Dopa-containing monomers [32]. A special initiating system is required for this process to prevent the inhibition effects caused by the catechol structure [33], which may result in low Dopa unit content and poor adhesive strength [34,35]. During the standard radical polymerization, the catechol group is often protected with triethylsilyl or acetal groups [36]. After the reaction, deprotection is carried out to produce the final glycopolymers for surface coating. These multi-step protection-deprotection procedures can be inefficient and time-consuming, highlighting the need for a simple and versatile method for interfacial glycosylation to advance the synthesis and bioactivity studies of glycosylated nanocomposites.

Glycosylamines (sugar-NH2) are essential building blocks for synthesizing N-linked glycoconjugates [37]. They are found in many natural and synthetic compounds with important biological and pharmaceutical properties [38]. The efficient synthesis of glycosylamines is a crucial step for the one-pot preparation of functional glycopolymers in our current work. Traditionally, glycosylamines are synthesized through a direct reaction between sugars and ammonia in an alcoholic solution [39]. However, this classical method suffers from the long reaction time and the poor solubility of oligosaccharides in methanol, limiting its application. Therefore, developing a green and straightforward methodology for producing glycosylamines remains a key objective in green glyco-process chemistry [40]. Our previous research demonstrated a direct synthesis of β-glycosyl azides from unprotected sugars using 2-chloro-1,3-dimethylimidazolinium chloride (DMC) in aqueous media [41]. This inspired us to combine this simple reaction with an organophosphorus-catalyzed Staudinger reduction [42], thereby establishing a general and practical route for large-scale glycosylamine synthesis. Under the reported conditions, good conversion rates of various unprotected monosaccharides and oligosaccharides into their corresponding glycosylamines were achieved (Fig. 2a). Notably, this method provides high β-selectivity in most cases and can be applied to complex sialyloligosaccharides.

With various sugar-NH2 compounds in hand, we next explored the synthesis of glycopolymers and glycan-coated surfaces. To create an easy-to-use glycan coating, we developed a three-step one-pot protocol using functional glycopolymers. The glycopolymer was synthesized through NHS-ester-mediated conjugation between reactive polymer, sugar-NH2, and dopamine. Subsequently, without isolation, this glycopolymer was further coated onto the material surfaces in a one-pot manner (Fig. 2b). Dopamine is well-known for its ability to adhere to surfaces, primarily due to the role of its catechol group [43]. The glycopolymer containing catechol blocks could be modified on different surfaces under alkaline conditions, providing a straightforward method for creating surface-glycan coatings. The synthesis of glycopolymers was analyzed using NMR spectroscopy and size exclusion chromatography (GPC). Both the NMR signals and the observed increase in molecular weight confirmed the successful coupling of sugar moieties and catechol groups onto the polymers (Fig. 2c). With these catechol-containing glycopolymers in hand, various materials including plastic, metal, and silicone rubber were treated with a pH 9.0 buffer solution of glycopolymers bearing glucose pendant groups. This process resulted in the creation of glycosylated surfaces. To verify the effectiveness of the glycan coating, the static water contact angles were measured after washing these surfaces three times and drying them with a nitrogen blow under ambient conditions. As shown in Fig. 2d, all of the glycosylated substrates exhibited lower contact angles compared to the native surfaces, indicating that the wettability of diverse material surfaces can be improved through effective glycopolymer coating. In the subsequent selective binding test using fluorescence microscopy, two glycan-coated substrates (pendant sugar: lactose) made from glass and polyurethane were immersed in a PBS solution containing a fluorescent protein (FITC-ConA, 0.01 mg/mL). After incubating for 10 min, the substrates were washed three times with PBS buffer to remove any unabsorbed fluorescent lectin. The samples were then analyzed using fluorescence microscopy, revealing a significant fluorescent signal on the glycan-coated materials (as shown in Fig. 2e). In contrast, the mean fluorescent intensity on the native material surfaces was very weak, indicating a lack of specific binding between carbohydrates and lectins. These results demonstrated that a glycan coating can be easily achieved using dopamine-containing glycopolymers through a simple three-step one-pot process.

Fabrication and structural characterization of glycosylated GNFs

The facile glycan coating process was next applied to the surface modification of graphene-based nanocomposites. GNF is a macroscopic carbonaceous fiber made up of microscopic graphene sheets, giving it properties similar to those of graphene [44]. Due to its unique structure and appealing functionality, significant progress has been made in the development of graphene-based nanocomposite fibers [45]. However, several technical challenges remain, including the distribution of graphene within polymer matrices, the interaction at the interface between graphene and the polymer, and the need for high-quality mass production of the controlled structures [46]. Currently, the graphene-based nanocomposites that are in use are primarily prepared through melt blending, where the polymers and graphene are directly combined via physical processing [47]. This mechanical noncovalent mixing consistently leads to poor interactions between fillers and polymer matrix, resulting in agglomeration and poor dispersion due to the high melt viscosity [48]. In situ polymerization is an effective alternative technique for achieving highly dispersed graphene within a polymer matrix [49]. This process involves the copolymerization of monomers in the presence of reactive graphene. In our work, the covalently conjugated nanostructures achieved by in situ polymerization enable nanoscale dispersion of graphene within the polyamide matrix, thereby eliminating risks of in vivo migration and associated nanotoxicity. Herein, nylon is used due to its notable hydrophilicity and skin-friendliness compared with polyester matrix. Biocompatible properties of the nylon stem from the presence of the amide groups in its chemical structure. The amide group shares a similar chemical structure to that found in natural peptides, which can deceive the immune system, preventing a host foreign body response from occurring [50]. Specifically, amino-functionalized graphene (Fig. S1) was used as a crosslinking agent and reacted with caprolactam to generate the nanocomposite products. Following the processes of extrusion, granulation, and melt spinning [51], the final GNFs were fabricated (Fig. S2). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and Raman spectroscopy were used to demonstrate that in situ polymerization technique can form covalent chemical bonds between graphene and nylon molecular chains, leading to good dispersion of graphene within the polymer matrix (Fig. S3 and S4). Next, we polymerized an N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester-containing acrylate to create the reactive polymer backbone. Subsequently, the reactive polymer reacted with glycosylamines and dopamine under alkaline conditions, resulting in adhesive glycopolymers. Finally, the naked GNFs were treated with this one-pot surface glycosylation protocol to form glycosylated layers on their surface (Fig. 3a).

To confirm the unique 2D planar structure of the graphene present in the hybrid fiber, we treated 2.0 kg of the fiber with 98% sulfuric acid to dissolve the nylon matrix [[52], [53], [54]]. The resulting precipitate was then centrifuged and washed with water and ethanol. Finally, the isolated graphene particles were analyzed using atomic force microscopy (AFM) [55]. The analysis revealed representative nanosheet structures with lateral sizes reaching up to 200 nm and a thickness of 1.0 nm for the graphene (Fig. 3b). The typical structure of the graphene in the fibers was evident in the Raman spectra (Fig. 3c), which showed a G peak at 1612 cm−1 and a D band at 1288 cm−1. Herein, the G peak (Graphitic peak) and D peak (Disordered peak) in Raman spectroscopy are key characteristic peaks for analyzing the structure of graphene, reflecting the degree of carbon atom ordering and defects in the material. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images with EDS elemental mapping revealed a spotty distribution of graphene structures (Fig. 3d). The presence of glycan coatings was confirmed through FT-IR, XPS characterization and the EDS elemental mapping which indicates a significant increase in oxygen content from glycans on the fiber surface (Fig. S6 and S7). Fig. 3e illustrated the distinctive nanostructure of graphene at the interface of the nylon matrices. It is important to note that the adhesion strength of the glycan coatings on the fiber surfaces is a crucial factor for effective pathogen capture and subsequent wound therapy. We assessed the elution performance of the glycan-coated textiles by subjecting them to continuous water washing. The samples collected after different washing times were dried and analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 3f, the XPS spectra revealed a nitrogen signal (N 1s peak at 399 eV) from the nylon fiber as well as another nitrogen signal (at 402 eV) from the attached glycopolymers [56]. The signal intensity associated with N-H from the glycan layers decreased with prolonged washing, indicating a 14% coating loss after 12 h and a 45% loss after 24 h. Considering that wound treatment typically occurs in stationary and wet environments, the current adhesion strength of the glycan coatings is adequate for use as a wound dressing. To further investigate the stability of the glycan coatings in real-life environment, we labeled the glycopolymer with a fluorescent tag (Cy5.5-NH2) and then coated the fluorescently labeled glycopolymer on the surface of graphene-nylon fabrics. Finally, this fabric was used in a mouse model for treatment. As shown in Fig. S10, during the treatment period, the fluorescently labeled glycopolymers coated on the dressing did not detach or migrate into the mouse skin and wound tissue, demonstrating the stability of the glycan coating in a static, moist wound environment.

In vitro anti-microbial abilities and the cytocompatibility investigation for glycosylated GNFs

After characterizing the structures, we systematically studied the antimicrobial properties of the native fibers (GNFs) and glycosylated GNFs. Many important interactions between pathogens and their hosts are highly specific binding events that involve glycan-receptor interactions [57,58]. Previous research has shown that glycan molecules can selectively recognize and bind to different pathogens through specific glycan-receptor interactions, such as lactose binding to certain bacteria [[6], [59]]′-sialyllactose binding to influenza viruses [60]. During the glycan-coating process, a glycopolymer bearing lactose pendant was applied to the fiber to specifically interact with bacteria. Furthermore, the 6′-sialyllactose-type glycan coating was modified on the surface for antiviral applications due to its specific binding ability to the influenza virus. To evaluate its antibacterial properties, we used three representative resistant strains as model bacteria: amoxicillin-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP), tetracycline-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA), and polymyxin-resistant Proteus vulgaris (PV). Prior to in vitro antibacterial testing, the fibers were exposed to UV irradiation for 10 min to enhance their antibacterial properties, as graphene nanocomposites exhibits significantly improved antimicrobial activity under UV-induced oxidative stress [61]. As shown in Fig. 4a, compared with the native fiber, the glyco-GNF demonstrated the enhanced antibacterial activity against KP, PA, and PV. To be more specific, the native fiber killed approximately 65% of KP, 70% of PA, and 50% of PV. In contrast, the bactericidal ability of glyco-GNF was much stronger, killing about 90% of KP, 93% of PA, and 70% of PV. This increased effectiveness is likely due to the pathogen-target capture and enrichment associated with the modified carbohydrate layers. It is well-known that UV light can trigger a cascade of electron delocalization in the various energy bands of graphene. This process leads to energetic delocalization, resulting in a charged or excited state that induces oxygen loss [62]. This mechanism helps to explain the generation of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH) [63], which contributes to highly effective antibacterial outcomes. We found that longer irradiation time exhibits improved antibacterial capacity against PA, indicating that the generated •OH is critical for the antibacterial properties of GNF (Fig. 4b). In the group treated with glyco-GNF for P. aeruginosa, we further evaluated the live/dead bacterial stanning assay [64,65]. Fig. S11 showed the fluorescence microscope photographs of P. aeruginosa after DMAO and PI treatment. The images of the control group exhibited faint red fluorescence. Both the native fiber and glyco-fiber groups showed noticeable red fluorescence, with the glyco-fiber group exhibiting a more pronounced fluorescence, which meant that the bacteria were seriously damaged and lost their vitality. We also employed SEM to observe the morphological alterations in bacteria treated with various materials (Fig. S12). After the glyco-fiber treatment, the bacteria exhibited extensive wrinkling and even the rupture of bacterial cell membranes. These results further confirmed the UV-induced antibacterial performance of GNF.

Moreover, in accordance with our previous report [66], antiviral activity can be assessed using a cytopathic effect (CPE) inhibition assay to evaluate the inhibition of viral infectivity when treated with glyco-GNF. CPE occurs when the infecting virus causes the lysis of the host cell, leading to a reduction in cell titer. No obvious inhibition was observed when using the native fiber or glyco-GNF without UV irradiation. However, treatment with UV-excited glyco-GNF showed a considerable inhibition of the virus (Fig. 4c). These results indicated that the modified glycan layer on nanocomposite materials enhances the antimicrobial effect, and UV excitation is essential for achieving strong pathogen-killing results.

To our knowledge, systematic comparison of nanotoxicity between graphene particles and their nanocomposites is scarce [67]. Therefore, before conducting animal testing, we assessed the cytotoxicity of both graphene and graphene nanocomposites using an in vitro CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 4d, pronounced cytotoxicity was observed when the concentration of graphene exceeded 50 μg/mL. We further examined the cytotoxicity of the synthetic graphene-nylon fibers and their glycan-coated derivatives. As illustrated in Fig. 4e, cells co-incubated with fibers at concentrations of 1.5 mg/mL and 3.0 mg/mL for 24 h showed good growth, and the cell morphology at 3.0 mg/mL appeared normal. The viability of cells co-incubated with different concentrations of the native fibers or glyco-GNF was comparable to the control group. These findings indicate that in situ polymerized graphene-nylon and glyco-GNF exhibit good cytocompatibility with mammalian cells.

In vivo anti-bacterial properties and the biocompatibility investigation for glyco-GNFs

The major bacterial pathogen P. aeruginosa (PA) causes multi-drug resistant infections, especially in chronic wounds [68]. In this study, we evaluated the in vivo therapeutic effectiveness of the GNF-based dressing against PA infections. We used drug-resistant PA to create a subcutaneous abscess infection model in healthy mice (BALB/c) and diabetic mice (BSK.DB/DB). To induce wounds, we cut the skin of the infected mice. Then, the wounds were treated with different dressings fabricated from native GNFs and glycosylated GNFs, respectively. The control group was treated with tetracycline-impregnated gauze (20 mg/mL). All the dressings were replaced every 48 h after UV sterilization (10 min, 254 nm) (Fig. 5a). During the treatment, we monitored the blood glucose levels of the different mouse models. The BSK.DB/DB mice remained in a state of hyperglycemia throughout the study, making them an ideal model for diabetes (Fig. 5b).

When addressing drug-resistant bacterial infections in wounds, the main challenge is to eliminate bacteria, especially in wounds infected with biofilms, as these can hinder the healing process. A GNF-based wound dressing was activated under UV light for 10 min to generate •OH at the wound site, facilitating effective biofilm eradication (Fig. S13) [69]. It is important to note that, unlike photothermal therapy, the wound itself was not directly exposed to UV light. Instead, UV irradiation was used to activate the graphene nanocomposites prior to applying them to the wound. To visually assess the therapeutic effect, the wound healing process was monitored over a period of 10 days. As shown in Fig. 5c–f, there was no significant difference in wound size among the groups on Day 0. However, after treatment, the wounds in the glyco-fiber group showed the fastest healing rate and a more intact appearance when compared with other treatment groups. In our comparison of two types of model mice, we observed that, in the tetracycline-treated group (control), the wound healing rate in diabetic mice was significantly slower than that of healthy mice, particularly during the early stages of healing. On the 6th day, the healing rate for healthy mice was 33.07 ± 1.89%, while that of diabetic mice was only 17.78 ± 1.54%. By the 10th day, the rate was 71.69 ± 5.46% for healthy mice, whereas the rate was 62.61 ± 4.77% for diabetic mice (Fig. 5d–f). However, when treated with the glyco-fiber group, the wound healing rates in both groups of mice were obviously improved. On the 6th day, healthy mice exhibited a healing rate of 68.74 ± 2.76%, while diabetic mice showed a rate of 73.41 ± 0.66%. By the 10th day, healthy mice reached 96.59 ± 0.64%, and diabetic mice achieved 94.73 ± 0.56% (Fig. 5d–f). These results suggest that glyco-GNF materials have the potential to promote wound healing in diabetic patients, and this effect is even more evident in refractory wounds. Furthermore, the pristine GNF dressing also demonstrated superior wound healing outcomes compared to the antibiotic-treated group. Although its mid-term therapeutic efficacy slightly lagged behind the glyco-GNF group, the significant advantage over conventional antibiotics highlighted the potential of nanomaterial-based approaches. These findings collectively underscore the critical role of non-antibiotic antimicrobial modalities against drug-resistant biofilms. To evaluate the safety of the materials in vivo, we conducted further assessments. The findings indicated that there was no damage to the organ morphology of the model mice (Fig. 5g), and there was no significant difference in body weight among the groups during the treatment period (Fig. 5h). This suggests that glyco-GNF materials do not exhibit systemic toxicity. To further demonstrate whether the GNF could generate leachables such as toxic nanoparticles, we analyzed the leachates from GNF using a Total Organic Carbon (TOC) analyzer (Fig. S14). The results showed that GNF have stability similar to nylon fibers and do not release toxic substances (such as monomers or graphene), while nylon materials have already been used in clinical treatments including wound dressings.

Histological evaluation in the wound healing

Wound healing is a complex process that typically involves the proliferation of granulation tissue, resolution of inflammation, formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis), and collagen deposition [70]. Graphene composite materials have been reported to synergistically promotes chronic wound healing in diabetes with its antibacterial, angiogenesis-promoting, anti-inflammatory, and biophysical cues like topographical and electroactive properties [71]. We conducted histological analysis to assess changes in the wound tissue. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that both healthy and diabetic mouse models treated with glyco-GNF materials showed a significant reduction in wound edge length and an increase in granulation tissue thickness compared to the control group (Fig. 6a and b). Collagen is an essential protein in the wound healing process. It not only facilitates the formation of new tissues but also provides structural support for healing. From a biomimetic perspective, the design of graphene-contained fiber scaffolds integrates both micro- and nanoscale topographies to mimic the extracellular matrix. The combination of these topological features with the conductive properties of graphene creates an ideal niche for cells, guiding their migration and alignment, which is crucial for tissue regeneration [10]. Masson's trichrome staining indicated that collagen deposition in the glyco-fiber group was greater than that in the other groups. Additionally, skin appendages, such as hair follicles and sebaceous glands, were observed in the newly formed tissues of the glyco-GNF group (Fig. 6c and d). The results of immunohistochemical staining indicated an increase in Collagen I levels in the wound tissue of the glyco-GNF group, which is consistent with the findings from Masson staining (Fig. 6e). Inflammation plays an important role in wound healing, particularly in diabetic cases. In previous reports, graphene has been shown to influence immune cell behavior, particularly modulate macrophage polarization [8]. This immune modulation plays a crucial role in tissue remodeling by reducing the negative effects of inflammation and fostering a regenerative environment. We assessed the expression of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α in the wound tissue using immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that compared with the other two groups, the glyco-GNF group significantly reduced the infiltration of IL-6 and TNF-α in the wound tissue of both healthy and diabetic mouse models (Fig. 6f). Neovascularization is crucial for delivering oxygen and nutrients, which facilitates the regeneration of skin tissue at the wound site. To evaluate the impact of glyco-GNF materials on angiogenesis, we conducted immunofluorescence staining for CD31 (a marker of endothelial cells) and α-SMA (a marker of vascular smooth muscle). We observed that the CD31 and α-SMA co-located areas, which represented the neovascularization, had significantly increased in the glyco-GNF group (Fig. 6g). In fact, graphene can enhance angiogenesis by activating several key signaling pathways [71]. One of the most prominent is the stimulation of the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, initiating a cascade of events that leads to the increased secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). The quantitative analysis results also indicated that the vessel density in the wound tissue of the glyco-GNF group was significantly higher than that in the other groups, across both healthy and diabetic models (Fig. 6h). These findings suggest that glyco-GNF materials can reduce inflammation, promote collagen deposition, and enhance neovascularization, thereby accelerating the wound healing process.

Mechanistic studies

The glycan-coated GNF materials demonstrated notable anti-pathogen properties and effective wound healing results. However, direct evidence of the generation of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH) is still lacking, and the underlying mechanisms are not yet well understood. To assess the pathogen-target enrichment effect, we conducted direct co-incubation of the fibers with three pathogenic bacteria. Microscopic observations of the co-incubated samples revealed that more bacteria adhered to the glycan-coated fibers compared to the native fibers (Fig. 7a–c). Furthermore, we measured the optical density (OD) of PA over time after adherence to different fiber materials using a NanoDrop Onec device. The results indicated that the glyco-fibers can adsorb bacteria more quickly and efficiently than the native fibers, leading to a rapid decrease in the OD value of the bacterial solution (Fig. 7d). Overall, these findings illustrate that Glyco-GNF materials can effectively capture pathogens.

Exploration into the mechanism of synergistic antibacterial effect. a The adhesion effects of different fiber materials on fluorescent E.coli. b SEM images showing the enrichment of S. aureus on different fiber surfaces. c The capture efficiency of different fibers against P. aeruginosa. d The OD value of P. aeruginosa changing over time after being adhered by different fiber materials. e ESR spectra of the generated DMPO-•OH adduct with different graphene contents (experimental conditions: UV irradiation time of 2 min, and DMPO concentration of 400 mM). f SEM morphology of P. aeruginosa and images of P. aeruginosa stained with DCFH-DA under various treatment conditions. ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Electron spin resonance (ESR) is the most direct, specific, and reliable method for identifying radical species among the various techniques available. To probe the hydroxyl radicals (•OH), 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) was used as a trapping agent. This agent can capture the short-lived •OH radical to create a relatively long-lived paramagnetic spin adduct (DMPO−•OH) within the time scales of ESR spectroscopy [72]. In this study, the fiber samples (20 mg) were first activated in a 400 mM DMPO solution under UV irradiation for 2 min to produce the DMPO−•OH adduct for ESR measurements. The characteristic peaks of DMPO−•OH, with an intensity ratio of 1:2:2:1, were observed in the GNF samples (Fig. 7e). These peaks were nearly identical to those found in the classic Fenton reaction. Additionally, a significantly increased signal was detected in samples made from GNF with higher graphene content. In contrast, the unmodified nylon fiber failed to generate •OH upon UV irradiation. The presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in bacteria was measured by 2′,7′-dichlor-odihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) to further confirm ROS generation from GNF under UV irradiation (Fig. 7f). The glyco-fibers, which capture and concentrate more bacteria, allow ROS to penetrate the bacteria more effectively, as indicated by the stronger fluorescence compared to native fibers. The ROS also caused significant membrane wrinkling and even rupture of the bacterial cell membranes. These experiments confirm a synergistic antibacterial mechanism, as the specially designed glycan coatings effectively capture pathogens, while the generated highly reactive •OH from the graphene-nylon fibers enhances this effect.

Conclusions

In summary, a novel glycosylated nanocomposite has been developed, which demonstrates high antimicrobial performance and low nanotoxicity. The fabrication process involves the in situ polymerization of amino-graphene and caprolactam, creating the native GNF. A unique three-step one-pot glycan-coating technology has been introduced, which can easily apply glycan layers to various material surfaces. This interfacial glycosylation stands out due to its simplicity, the use of readily available ingredients, mild reaction conditions, adaptability to various materials, and suitability for a range of end-users. The glycan-coated GNFs and fabrics were specifically utilized in wound dressings to combat drug-resistant bacterial infections. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that glycan coatings enhance antimicrobial properties through a pathogen-target enrichment effect, effectively balancing the antimicrobial activity and potential nanotoxicity. Additionally, the synergistic antimicrobial mechanism based on UV-induced hydroxyl radical generation and bacteria capture was elucidated. These combined features significantly accelerated the healing process of infectious wounds including diabetic wounds. The resulting glycosylated nanocomposites, which strike a good balance between low toxicity and high activity, may serve as powerful tools against pathogenic microorganisms and antibiotic resistance. Although the progress has been made, there are still some challenges to be addressed. For instance, UV excitation and anti-infective therapy are performed separately, causing a rapid decline in antibacterial activity. To sustain effective anti-infection properties during treatment, next, we plan to incorporate infrared light exposure and leverage NIR-induced photothermal effects to enhance the antibacterial activity of GNF dressings (Fig. S16). Overall, this work offers glycoscience a practical avenue for the application of nanocomposite materials.

Experimental section

Synthesis of glycosyl amines

To a stirred solution of glycosyl azide (0.1 mmol) in methanol (2.0 mL) at room temperature was added PBu3 (28.0 μL, 0.11 mmol). The reduction of glycosyl azides was also carried out in aqueous media by using a water-soluble phosphine derivative--tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP). After TLC indicated the formation of a major product and consumption of the starting material, the reaction mixture was concentrated in vacuo. The residue was dissolved in water and washed with chloroform. The desired product was obtained as white solids after lyophilization.

Synthesis of adhesive glycopolymers

In a typical process, poly(acrylic acid N-hydroxysuccinimide ester) (102 mg, 0.6 mmol of repeat units) and glycosyl amine (sugar-NH2, 0.42 mmol, 70 mol% relative to NAS repeat units) were dissolved in DMSO/carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (0.5 M, pH 9.0) (1.6 mL/0.4 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred for 1 h under N2 atmosphere. Next, the dopamine hydrochloride (0.45 mmol, 85 mg, 45 mol% relative to NAS repeat units) solution in 0.5 mL of carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (0.5 M, pH 9.0) was added to the reaction mixture and stirred for another 2 h under N2 atmosphere. Excess ethanolamine (200 μL) was added to ensure the complete conversion of the remaining NHS-activated ester group. The prepared glycopolymer can be isolated by dialysis and lyophilization for the MMR and GPC analysis. About 110 mg of the functional glycopolymer was obtained after drying.

Preparation of graphene-nylon fibers (GNFs)

According to our previous patent (US 20180312658A1), caprolactam (5 kg), water (25 g), and animo-graphene (10 g) were added to a flask on a temperature-controlled hot plate with a stirrer, and under an inert nitrogen atmosphere. The temperature was raised to 150 °C for 1 h until caprolactam was completely molten. Then, the reaction mixture was heated to 260 °C for 2 h, then to 280 °C for 3 h for in situ polymerization of polyamide 6 (PA6) monomers. After cooling to room temperature, the crude products were washed in boiling water for several times to remove the monomers and oligomers. The resultant nanocomposites were dried at 100 °C under a vacuum overnight. After extrusion, granulation, and melt spinning protocol, the final GNFs were fabricated.

Preparation of glycosylated GNFs (glycan-coatings)

The reaction mixture of the prepared adhesive glycopolymers was directly diluted with carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (25 mL, 0.1 M, pH 9.0) into a 5 mg/mL concentration. The target materials (2 cm × 2 cm for GNF dressing) were immersed in the above solution (10 mL) and reacted for 24 h in a 40 °C oven. After the glycan-modification, the materials were cleaned three times with deionized water to remove the physically adsorbed glycopolymers, and dried under reduced pressure for later use.

Cells and animals

HEK293T and COS-7 cells cultured in DMEM were supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin (all from Gibco), and maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College, which is the headquarter of National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (NSTI). The cell lines were checked free of mycoplasma contamination by PCR and culture. Their species origins were confirmed with PCR. The identities of the cell lines were authenticated with STR profiling (FBI, CODIS). All the results can be viewed on the website (http://cellresource.cn). Female BALB/c and BSK.DB/DB mice aged 8-9 weeks were obtained from the Department of Laboratory Animal Science, Peking University Health Science Center. Animals were caged with food and water available ad libitum with 12 h light-dark cycle. Ambient housing conditions were 22 ± 1 °C with 56%-60% humidity.

In vitro antibacterial assay

The drug-resistant K. Pneumoniae (KP), P. aeruginosa (PA), and P. vulgaris (PV) were obtained from National Metrology and Testing Center for Biopharmaceutical Industry. The bacteria were cultured in a Luria–Bertani (LB) medium for 24 h at 37 °C, 150 r/min. For antimicrobial test, the bacterial suspension after gradient dilution was co-incubated for 0.5 h at 37 °C with 3.8 mg of fiber sample (ultraviolet irradiation for 10 m), 20% sodium hypochlorite solution as the positive control, and normal saline as the negative control. Then, the bacterial suspension was cultured for 24 h, and the CFU count was performed. In order to detect the influence of different UV irradiation time on the anti-bacterial effect of the fiber, the fibers irradiated for 0 min, 1 min, 2 min, 5 min, 10 min were added to the bacterial suspension respectively, the normal saline as the negative control. Then, the bacterial suspension was cultured for 24 h, and the CFU count was performed.

In vivo anti-bacterial experiments

To form subcutaneous abscesses, mice were anesthetized using 0.3% pentobarbital sodium (i.p., 50 mg/kg). The drug-resistant P. aeruginosa (s.c., 107 CFU/mL, 100 μL) was inoculated on the shaved and disinfected back of each test mouse. After a subcutaneous abscess had formed at the infected site, the epidermis of the focal site was cut to create an open skin wound. The wound was treated with the dressing and then covered with sterile gauze. The wound covered with tetracycline-impregnated sterile gauze only was used as a control. Change the dressing every 2 days and record the healing of the wound for 10 days. The wound size was measured using ImageJ software.

Histological analysis, immunohistochemical staining and immunofluorescence staining

For histological analysis, organ and wound tissues from model mice were collected on day 10, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde immediately, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 5 μm thick sections and attached to the slides. Then, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome (Solarbio, G1340). For immunohistochemical staining and immunofluorescence staining, antigen retrieval of the sections was carried out with citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 98 °C for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with goat serum for 30 min at room temperature after permeation for 10 min. Sections were incubated in a humidifying box with the primary antibodies (rabbit anti-TNF-α, CST, 11948, 1:100; rabbit anti-IL-6, CST, 12912, 1:100; rabbit anti-CD31, CST, 15585, 1:100; mouse anti-α-SMA, CST, 48938, 1:100; mouse anti-Collagen I, Epizyme, M012544, 1:200) at 4 °C overnight, and then the corresponding secondary antibodies were used. For immunohistochemistry, sections were stained with DAB and washed with water to stop the reaction. For immunofluorescence staining, sections were mounted with mounting medium containing DAPI and captured by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, LSM880, Zeiss). The thickness of granulation tissue, the collagen deposition ratio and the vessel density were analyzed and quantified using ImageJ software.

ESR measurement

The fiber samples (20 mg) were activated in 400 mM 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) solution under UV irradiation for 2 min. Then, the ESR (EPR200-Plus, Chinainstru& Quantumtech (Hefei) Co., Ltd.) spectra were carried out to detect the possible radical adducts with DMPO. The spectrometer parameters were as follows: sweep width of 100 G, center field of 3505 G, sweep time of 10 s, modulation amplitude of 2 G, modulation frequency of 100 kHz, and microwave attention of 23 dB.

Statistical analysis

The results were represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The differences between groups were compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA). Tukey's test and Dunnett's test were used for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was carried out using Prism (GraphPad) software.
