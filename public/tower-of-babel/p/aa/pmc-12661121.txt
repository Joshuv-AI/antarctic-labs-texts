A scoping review of population‐based dementia registries: advancing research, care, and policy
Authors: Haley Garland, Mireille Jacobson, Shengjia Xu, Julie Zissimopoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite their value for public health, research, and care, population‐based registries for Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) remain limited.We conducted a scoping review of dementia registry studies through December 2023 and identified population‐based dementia registries in Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development countries. We characterized their structure and scope, assessed key themes, and developed recommendations for registry development.We identified 21 population‐based dementia registries from a review of 235 publications. These registries help fill gaps in dementia research by providing longitudinal data, improving case identification, and standardizing outcomes for clinical and policy use. However, many registries lack data on healthcare use and caregiving and have limited geographical coverage, thereby reducing their ability to inform research and public health efforts to address dementia burden in an era of rapidly evolving dementia diagnostics and treatments.As dementia cases rise and advancements in prevention, detection, and treatment accelerate, population‐based registries are essential for generating real‐world evidence to improve dementia care, policy, and outcomes.Highlights This scoping review identified 21 population‐based dementia registries across OECD countries, highlighting the current landscape and structural gaps.Registries provide critical longitudinal data and standardization for research and policy but often lack information on healthcare use, caregiving, and broad geographic representation.With rising dementia rates and evolving treatments, population‐based registries are essential for producing real‐world evidence to strengthen care, research, and public health planning.

BACKGROUND

For decades, patient registries, which systematically collect and store uniform data on patients with a specific disease, condition, or procedure, have informed public health priorities, programs, and spending, as well as basic science, clinical, and social science research. 1 In the United States, cancer and kidney disease registries, and more recently vaccination registries, have been particularly impactful along these dimensions. 1 While some dementia registries – such as the South Carolina registry established in 1988 – have existed for many years, most Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD)‐focused registries have been created more recently. The dementia registries that do exist differ in their goals, the populations represented, the types of data collected and/or linked to, their geographical coverage, and how they are funded and sustained. Many existing dementia registries serve relatively narrow patient populations (e.g., those in a single healthcare system) or are designed for targeted purposes (e.g., recruitment to clinical trials) and thus may have more limited impact.

The scope and utility of dementia registries are shaped by governance structures, privacy and consent policies, and societal attitudes and norms toward dementia. These factors help explain why some registries capture more detailed information than others. In the United States, regulations at both the federal Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) and state levels often require explicit opt‐in consent, which can limit both participation and the breadth of data collected. 1 By contrast, many Nordic countries operate under national legislation with opt‐out models, enabling broader linkage across health and social datasets. 2 Such differences in governance and consent structures contribute to variation in the comprehensiveness and utility of dementia registries across countries. Nevertheless, national registries provide a key opportunity to collect data on large numbers of persons with dementia for use in addressing the significant health, economic, and social challenges of ADRD. Valuable insights from current registries can help inform the use and further development of existing registries and design of new regional or national ADRD registries.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We conducted a scoping review of population‐based dementia registries in OECD countries to inform the development and improvement of registries. Our review included peer‐reviewed studies, registry reports, and government documents to assess registry structure, recruitment methods, and contributions to research, clinical care, and policy. Interpretation of findings: Dementia registries are essential for monitoring disease trends, improving care, and supporting policy and research. Globally, the value of a dementia registry is increasing as numbers of persons with dementia continue to grow. As of 2019, an estimated 55 million people worldwide were living with dementia. 3 This number is expected to double almost every 20 years, reaching 78 million in 2030 and 139 million in 2050. 3 The annual cost of dementia globally was estimated to be around USD 1.3 trillion in 2019. 3 The development of dementia registries around the world can lead to better informed estimates of and measured progress on dementia detection and diagnosis, care, and treatment, as well as support the assessment of physical and economic burdens of dementia on care partners.

Recent and ongoing advances in ADRD prevention, diagnosis, and treatment further underscore the value of comprehensive dementia registries. The introduction of the first disease‐modifying Alzheimer's treatments marks a significant milestone, making it imperative to monitor their real‐world impact on access, safety, efficacy, and long‐term health and economic outcomes. 4 Additionally, blood‐based biomarkers are now approved to aid in AD diagnosis, yet their optimal use at the population level remains uncertain. 5 A well‐structured dementia registry can serve as a crucial tool in tracking and analyzing the impact of these developments, informing evidence‐based policy decisions, and ensuring equitable access to emerging diagnostics and therapies.

We update and expand on a review of registries through 2016. 6 Since that time, three new surveillance‐focused, population‐based registries have been established, with three more in development. The number of publications based on these registries has doubled. This study is a targeted examination of population‐based dementia registries designed for epidemiological surveillance, analysis of quality of dementia treatment, and population‐level research. We defined a population‐based dementia registry as a registry that systematically captures data on all or nearly all individuals with dementia within a defined population. 6 We characterized each registry's structure and scope and identified themes in published studies to assess how insights from registries may inform strategies to address dementia now and in the future.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a comprehensive review of existing population‐based dementia registries in Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) countries, following best practices for scoping reviews outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist. We limited our review to OECD countries to ensure the relevance of findings to comparable regulatory, economic, and political contexts, to facilitate cross‐country comparisons within similar systems, and to manage the scope and feasibility of the review. We consulted the University of Southern California Libraries in developing our scoping review protocol, search techniques, and screening and selection strategies. Using these data, we analyze the characteristics and themes in the dementia registry literature. We assessed registry coverage, recruitment strategies, participant characteristics, data collection practices, consent requirements, data linkages, and funding sources. Additionally, we reported trends in registry utilization for research, key thematic areas explored in published studies, and gaps that future registries could address.

Databases and search terms

We used PubMed, Scopus, and Embase as the primary databases for our search. Additionally, we utilized Google search engine to identify dementia registry websites and relevant gray literature. Search terms used both dementia and Alzheimer's disease (AD) for comprehensive disease identification and in combination with registry terms. We included studies up to December 4, 2023. Studies included in our review had to be in English, cover a dementia registry that was population‐based and focused on epidemiological surveillance, analysis of quality of dementia treatment, or other population‐level research, and capture data at the local, state/provincial, or national level. We excluded from analysis studies of dementia registries in countries other than the USA, Sweden, Australia, or other OECD countries, as well as registries with restricted local coverage, such as hospital/clinic‐based and university‐based case registries. Also excluded were prevention‐ or recruitment‐focused registries (e.g., preclinical dementia registries that aim to optimize conduct of clinical trials for interventions in the predementia phase of the disease and dementia research volunteer registries). Exact search terms and study inclusion and exclusion criteria for study replication are provided in Supporting Information.

Two researchers independently reviewed each article at each screening step. A list of dementia registries and relevant publications was extracted using the standardized search terms and predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria. For process rigor, the two researchers reviewed the first several articles together to ensure a common understanding of how to apply inclusion/exclusion criteria in each step. When information was insufficient based on abstracts, the study was included for full‐text screening. The extracted lists from each step were cross‐checked to ensure consistency, and any discrepancies between the two researchers were discussed and resolved.

Data charting

We created shared data charting documents, organized by dementia registry names, to capture all relevant data extracted. Two researchers divided the included registries in half and reviewed each article for relevant information and characteristics. We created two data charting documents: one for included dementia registries and one for publications related to identified dementia registries.

For the list of included dementia registries, the following information was extracted: registry name, country, coverage (e.g., national, state/provincial, regional), registry type (e.g., epidemiological, quality of care, and dementia research), year established, ongoing or year ended, data frequency, most recent sample size, dementia type (e.g., dementia, AD), diagnostic criteria (e.g., Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, International Classification of Diseases [ICD], other clinical evaluations), recruitment setting (e.g., community setting, hospital setting), recruitment method (e.g., opt‐in, opt‐out), population covered, data collected, data linkages available, funding entities, and factors (e.g., political, social, economic) contributing to the establishment of the registry.

For the list of publications of each dementia registry, the following information was extracted: registry name, author/year, citation, study design, study sample, outcome(s), main results, and data linkages.

The data were analyzed for patterns, common themes, and significant findings. The results were synthesized to facilitate the identification of effective practices in the development and management of dementia registries for the formulation of recommendations for the potential establishment of a dementia registry.

RESULTS

Search methods resulted in 12,304 English language articles that, after removing duplicates, resulted in 6504 unique studies. We conducted three rounds of screening for the 6504 unique publications: initial screening based on title and abstract to assess adherence to inclusion criteria, abstract screening to further evaluate eligibility, and full‐text screening. Across these stages, we excluded 5619 studies in the first round, 560 in the second, and 108 in the final round. We identified 21 additional relevant publications from other sources (e.g., references of included publications and Google searches) for inclusion. Additionally, as we were drafting the reports, we excluded three publications for relevance, resulting in 235 total publications for final review. The results are illustrated in Figure 1.

Excluded dementia registries

Through three rounds of screening, we excluded registries if they did not meet both criteria: (1) alignment with the population‐based definition and (2) a design purpose consistent with surveillance, quality monitoring, or population‐level research. We excluded 15 hospital/clinic‐based or university‐based dementia case registries due to their restricted local coverage, a cohort‐based study, and one operated by a private insurance company. We also excluded six preclinical registries that focus on dementia prevention and recruitment of research volunteers among high‐risk or healthy individuals. This included the Alzheimer's Prevention Registry, the Arizona Alzheimer's Registry, and the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer Prevention (WRAP) in the United States, the Organized Registration for the Assessment of Dementia by the Nationwide General Consortium Toward Effective Treatment (ORANGE) Registry in Japan, and two international registries: the European Prevention of Alzheimer's Dementia (EPAD) and the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network (DIAN). Additionally, we excluded the Health and Memory Study (HMS) registry (Norway) and the Maccabi Registry (Israel) because HMS functioned as a small cohort study and Maccabi was operated by a private insurance company. A full list of excluded dementia registries is provided in Table S1.

Population‐based dementia registries

We identified 21 dementia registries from 235 papers included in the final review. The final list of 21 included registries, sorted by study frequency and country, is provided in Table S2. Five of the registries are no longer operational, while three are not yet operational. The three planned dementia registries – the National Dementia Care and Support Registry of the Netherlands, the National Dementia Registry of Ireland, and the local Digital Dementia Registry Bavaria (digiDEM Bayern) of Bavaria, Germany – provide detailed protocols and thus valuable insights into the process of establishing a new dementia registry. 7 , 8 , 9 Consequently, these registries have the potential to inform the development of a dementia registry.

The 21 identified registries spanned 15 countries, with different levels of geographical coverage (national, statewide, and regional/local). Several national dementia registries were established in Sweden, the United States of America, Finland, South Korea, Austria, Norway, Australia, France, and Israel. Registries operational on state/provincial and local levels were also established in the United States, Spain, the UK, Italy, and Germany.

Among the 21 registries, the Swedish Dementia Registry (SveDem) is by far the most featured in research, with 93 publications based on its data. Some of the other dementia registries with high research output include the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) (n = 31) and the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) database (n = 15) in the United States, the Registry of Dementia of Girona (ReDeGi) of Girona, Spain (n = 17), the Medication use and Alzheimer's disease (MEDALZ) registry of Finland (n = 17), the Clinical Research Center for Dementia of South Korea (CREDOS) (n = 11), the Prospective Dementia Registry (PRODEM) of Austria (n = 10), and the Norwegian Registry of Persons Assessed for Cognitive Symptoms (NorCog) of Norway (n = 6).

Types of population‐based dementia registries

We included three types of dementia registries in our final list of registries: (1) epidemiological dementia registry, (2) quality of dementia care registry, and (3) dementia research registry (Table 1).

Epidemiological dementia registries collect information on the prevalence, incidence, and risk of dementia. The three statewide dementia registries in the United States and four internationally based registries in Spain (ReDeGi), France (National Alzheimer Database [BNA]), Italy (Regional Registry for Alzheimer's Disease and Other Dementias Tuscany), and Israel (Israeli National Dementia Dataset) are identified as having primarily epidemiological aims. 15 , 16 , 17

Quality of dementia care registries focus on monitoring the quality of dementia care on the regional or national level according to clinical guidelines, quality indicators, or established best practices for management of dementia symptoms. Additionally, quality of dementia care registries often provide information on utilization and cost of care and caregiver burden to assist with the planning and development of dementia care services. We identified four quality of dementia care registries worldwide: two in Sweden (SveDem, Swedish BPSD Registry), one in Norway (NorCog), and one in Australia (ADNet). 10 , 18 , 19

Dementia research registries are the most common type of dementia registries. They are often initially established to support individual studies covering a wide range of topics, including causes, risk factors, disease progression, and treatment for dementia. Depending on the individual studies that the registries support, dementia research registries can also provide data on the natural history of dementia and assist with the development of effective interventions to reduce the risk and incidence of dementia as well as to improve disease treatment and management. Despite the large number of dementia research registries across the globe, we focused only on dementia research registries with wide geographical coverage, excluding registries based on single hospital/clinic or university. We included a total of seven dementia research registries in the final review: two in the United States (CERAD and NACC) and one each in Finland (MEDALZ), South Korea (CREDOS), Austria (PRODEM), the UK (Camberwell), and Germany (Bavarian Dementia Registry‐BayDem). 11 , 12 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24

Recruitment and populations covered

Recruitment across operational registries occurs primarily through healthcare settings. However, seven registries identify dementia cases through data linkages to other sources, such as national electronic medical records. The South Carolina Alzheimer's Disease Registry identifies dementia cases from multiple data sources to ensure that the registry captures as many cases as possible, including private practices, vital records, state health plans, emergency departments, long‐term care evaluations, inpatient hospitalizations, home health, and Medicaid. 14

Most registries include dementia broadly, with only two focused specifically on AD. The inclusion of all dementias is appropriate given the mixed pathology of most persons living with dementia. The majority of registries rely on ICD codes for diagnostic information, which is easy to obtain from healthcare system records. For example, in SveDem, dementia is clinically diagnosed using ICD‐10, and dementia diagnoses are further classified as AD, vascular dementia, mixed dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson's disease dementia, unspecified dementia (where specific dementia diagnosis is not ascertained), and other dementia types (which combines miscellaneous dementia disorders such as corticobasal degeneration or alcohol‐related dementias). 10 Similarly, as one of the few dementia registries with primarily epidemiological aims, the South Carolina Alzheimer's Disease Registry relies on ICD‐10 codes from individuals’ medical records to define dementia cases and classifies individuals into four categories: AD, vascular dementia, mixed dementia, and dementia in other medical conditions. 14 , 25

Most registries capture thousands of patients, although the French BNA captures over 1.5 million dementia patients. 26 The large number of participants for many registries facilitates studies of dementia in the population. Only four registries include care partners in the registry population, despite the importance of care partners for the well‐being of persons living with dementia and their inclusion as a common eligibility requirement for clinical trials of AD/ADRD therapeutics. 18 , 22 , 24 , 27 The lack of inclusion of care partners is consistent with the topics covered in the registries, which are clinically focused.

Dementia registry consent, data collection, data linkages, and funding support

As shown in Table 2, participant consent to be entered into a registry occurs via both opt‐in and opt‐out processes. Evidence from behavioral economics would suggest that opt‐out will improve completeness but may entail additional ethical and legal considerations. 28

Many registries collect information on cognition and functioning of the persons living with dementia, which is useful for understanding the stage of dementia and/or progression. Other types of data collected include demographic characteristics, imaging and other biomarker measures, pharmacological intervention, and medication use, and some registries include data on quality of life and behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia. For example, SveDem includes basic dementia diagnostic work‐up information, including data from the structured clinical interview, interview with a person close to the patient, physical and psychological evaluation, cognitive assessments using Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score and clock test at minimum, cerebral imaging, and blood tests (calcium, homocysteine, and thyroid function). 10 Similarly, as one of the newest clinical quality of dementia care registries, ADNet in Australia records baseline scores of completed cognitive assessments (MMSE, Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA], Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale [RUDAS]), structural brain imaging (computed tomography [CT] and magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), functional brain imaging (positron emission tomography and single‐photon emission computed tomography) completed within 12 months prior to diagnosis, and blood tests. 29 Another example in this category is Norway's NorCog, which was established to improve the quality of diagnostic practices of dementia. NorCog includes a comprehensive collection of neurocognitive tests, including MMSE, Clock Drawing Test, and Clinical Dementia Rating Scale to assess global cognition, 10‐word recall test to assess memory, Trail Making Test A and B for attention, Controlled Oral Word Association Test for fluency, CERAD figure copying for constructional praxis, Boston Naming Test for word retrieval, and Reed Scale for evaluation of anosognosia for insight. 18 Additionally, standardized dementia assessment in NorCog also includes a psychiatric evaluation with an emphasis on comorbid depressive disorder (Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia and Montgomery and Aasberg Depression Rating Scale), physical and neurological examination, blood tests, cerebrospinal fluid tests, and structural brain imaging (CT or MRI). 18

Data linkages are important for broadening the scope of registries, enabling them to address key topics such as mortality, comorbid conditions, and costs. Ten registries include data linkages. The most extensive is SveDem, which links to eight different datasets, including the Swedish Prescribed Drug Register, the Longitudinal Integrated Database for Health Insurance and Labour Market Studies, and the Swedish National Stroke Registry. 30 The South Carolina Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias Registry provides another unique linkage example by integrating law enforcement data, including records from the South Carolina Corrections dataset, offering a distinctive perspective on dementia and interactions with the justice system. 14 These diverse linkages enhance public health surveillance by providing a more comprehensive understanding of dementia's impact across healthcare, social, and economic systems, ultimately informing policy and intervention strategies.

Key themes in use of operational dementia registries

We identified eight key themes or topics captured by and used in research on the 18 operational dementia registries. These include (1) registry design, process, and experience; (2) incidence, prevalence, and patient characteristics; (3) survival, mortality, and causes of death; (4) diagnostics and risk factors (including genetic biomarkers and non‐modifiable risk factors, modifiable risk factors, and electro psychological markers); (5) drug treatments and medication usage; (6) caregiver burden; (7) healthcare utilization and costs; and (8) association with other diseases. These themes demonstrate the diverse applications of dementia registries in advancing dementia research and understanding its multifaceted aspects. SveDem is the only registry that collects information across all eight topic areas and features published studies in each area, either through data collection from the registry or linkages to other national registries and datasets. NorCog also collects information across all topic areas, although some of the areas have not been explored in published research yet. “Incidence, prevalence, and patient characteristics” is the only topic area with data available for all registries. The second common topic area is “diagnostics and risk factors,” with 12 out of 18 registries reporting data on it. Over half of registries reported on “survival, mortality, and causes of death” (11 of 18), “drug treatment and medication use” (11 of 18), and “registry design, progress, and experience” (10 of 18). Just under half of registries included information on “healthcare use and cost” (eight of 18), “association with other diseases” (eight of 18), and “Caregiver Burden” (seven of 18).

DISCUSSION

A well‐designed and executed dementia registry can generate real‐world evidence in support of strategies to prevent dementia, advance therapies and find a cure, and improve the quality of life of people living with dementia and their care partners. Recent and ongoing developments in data availability and analytics, along with innovations in dementia detection, care, and treatment, make this an opportune time for population‐based dementia registries.

Most countries either lack a dementia registry or operate registries with limited scope and geographic reach. Expanding the number of national registries is vital for providing representative, population‐based data that can be used to advance knowledge about dementia risk factors and treatment‐related outcomes in populations of persons with heterogeneous genetic profiles, economic, social, policy, healthcare, and physical environments. The presence of national registries in countries such as Sweden, South Korea, and Australia demonstrates feasibility. Coupled with evidence from cancer and vaccine registries in the United States and beyond, they further demonstrate that sustained investment in a dementia registry can yield valuable epidemiological insights and support national prevention, treatment, and care goals.

Data linkages can greatly enhance the effectiveness of registries. Seven registries identify dementia cases through linkages with national electronic health records, allowing for a more comprehensive and cost‐effective approach to case identification and data collection. For example, the South Carolina Alzheimer's Disease Registry utilizes multiple data sources, including hospital records, Medicaid claims, and long‐term care evaluations, to capture a broad and representative sample of dementia cases. 25 This model of data integration should be considered in future registry development, as it minimizes duplication of effort and maximizes the utility of existing health data. The experience of other registries, most notably the linkage between the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program cancer registry data and Medicare claims data in the United States, further demonstrates the potential for data linkages to advance research, improve clinical care, and inform policy decisions. 1 The type of dementia registry adopted, however, will depend on the intended goals, as each type serves distinct but complementary roles. Epidemiological registries, such as ReDeGi in Spain and MEDALZ in Finland, provide critical insights into disease prevalence and risk factors, enabling targeted public health interventions. 21 , 31 Quality of dementia care registries, such as SveDem and NorCog, help evaluate treatment effectiveness and adherence to clinical guidelines, ultimately improving patient outcomes. 10 , 18 Dementia research registries facilitate studies on disease mechanisms, treatment responses, and long‐term disease progression. The coexistence of these different registry types can contribute to a comprehensive understanding of ADRD and improve patient care worldwide.

A notable gap across registry data was information on costs of establishing and maintaining the registry and frequency of data collection. While information regarding the costs associated with establishing and sustaining a registry remains limited within the literature, funding primarily originates from public government entities. For instance, the Australian Dementia Network Registry (ADNeT) was established with support from the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) National Institute for Dementia Research program. NHMRC, a government agency, fosters and promotes health and medical research by allocating grants to projects closely aligned with public health concerns. ADNeT received USD 18 million in funding spanning from 2018 to 2023. However, in 2022, the ADNeT registry secured additional funding from private biotechnology companies Biogen and Roche to bolster project initiatives, such as the development of a tailored clinical research platform. 19 The availability of information on funding for dementia registries reflects broader societal and political contexts. Countries with expansive social safety nets and publicly funded healthcare systems are often better positioned to support and track the financing of comprehensive registries, whereas in other settings, funding and the data on such funding tend to be more fragmented. 6

Additionally, notable gaps remain in areas critical to advancing dementia research and informing policy. Advances in data collection for drug treatments and medication usage for AD are beginning, but long‐term real‐world effectiveness and outcome data remain limited, particularly outside newly established registries. This underscores the ongoing need for comprehensive, population‐level registries to monitor treatment uptake, safety, and effectiveness over time. 32 Similarly, cost and healthcare utilization data are underrepresented, limiting the ability to assess the financial burden of dementia and the cost‐effectiveness of interventions. 33 , 34 Additionally, caregiver burden data are collected by fewer than half of registries, despite the central role caregivers play in dementia care, the high costs of caregiving, and the significant variation in caregiver support policies across countries. 35 , 36 , 37 Expanding data collection in these areas will be essential for evaluating the impact of new treatments, understanding disparities in care, and shaping policies to better support patients and their families.

Despite their benefits, registries remain underutilized in many regions due to challenges in funding, governance, and data‐sharing policies. Greater international collaboration and harmonization efforts, potentially led by organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and Alzheimer's Disease International (ADI), are necessary to address these challenges.

Supporting the development of population‐based dementia registries should be a priority for researchers, policymakers, and funding agencies. The insights gained from registry data can inform healthcare policies, optimize resource allocation, and facilitate multinational research collaborations. Furthermore, as disease‐modifying therapies and novel diagnostic tools emerge, registries will be instrumental in tracking their real‐world effectiveness and safety. To support these efforts, we synthesized findings from the literature into a set of practical recommendations for designing and implementing successful dementia registries, available in Table S3.

CONCLUSION

A comprehensive dementia registry is essential for advancing research, improving patient outcomes, and supporting advances in prevention and treatment. By systematically collecting and harmonizing data, registries can enable more accurate diagnoses, facilitate equitable access to treatments, and strengthen global collaboration in Alzheimer's and dementia research. This review illustrates the diversity of registries and their importance in dementia research, clinical practice, and health policy. As new diagnostic methods and therapies emerge, a well‐structured registry has the potential to not only enhance clinical decision‐making but also inform policy, ensuring that healthcare systems are prepared to address the growing burden of dementia in an effective and patient‐centered manner.
