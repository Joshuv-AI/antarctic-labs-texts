Regional amyloid accumulation, post‐traumatic distress, and intrusive symptoms in cognitively unimpaired older adults at risk for Alzheimer's
Authors: Eleni Palpatzis, Carmen M. Colceriu, Mahnaz Shekari, Muge Akinci, Marc Suárez‐Calvet, Oriol Grau‐Rivera, Gonzalo Sánchez‐Benavides, Juan Domingo Gispert, Eider M. Arenaza‐Urquijo, for the ALFA study
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIn preclinical Alzheimer's disease (AD), early pathology may heighten vulnerability to psychiatric symptoms. We examined whether Aβ accumulation in cognitive‐emotional regions was associated with post‐pandemic psychiatric symptoms.METHODSThis prospective cohort study included 115 cognitively unimpaired ALzheimer's and FAmilies participants (69 [60%] women, mean [SD] age 64.9 [4.8] years). Amyloid beta (Aβ) change was examined in anterior cingulate, posterior cingulate, superior parietal, medial orbitofrontal gyri, and insula using two [18F]flutemetamol positron emission tomography (PET) scans. Outcomes were pandemic‐related post‐traumatic distress (Impact of Events Scale Revised [IES‐R]; total, intrusive, avoidance, and hyperarousal symptoms) and anxious‐depressive symptoms (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [HADS]; mean follow‐up: 3.62 years [SD = 0.68]). Linear regression and voxel‐wise analyses were performed.RESULTSAβ accumulation across anterior cingulate (β = 40.29, p = 0.036), posterior cingulate (β = 29.52, p = 0.012), superior parietal (β = 35.58, p = 0.036), and medial orbitofrontal gyri (β = 35.08, p = 0.036) was associated with post‐pandemic intrusive symptoms only, independent of baseline anxious‐depressive symptoms.DISCUSSIONAβ accumulation in cognitive‐emotional regions may increase vulnerability to intrusive symptoms, highlighting their potential as early clinical markers in AD.

BACKGROUND

Psychiatric symptoms are increasingly recognized as early manifestations of Alzheimer's disease (AD), emerging even during its preclinical stages. 1 , 2 Previous research linked post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptom severity with accelerated cognitive decline and PTSD diagnosis with an increased dementia risk. 3 , 4 Similarly, anxiety and depression symptoms have been associated with greater risk of disease progression and cognitive decline. 5 While these relationships are complex, recent evidence points to shared neurobiological substrates, where early amyloid beta (Aβ) pathology may disrupt neural circuits involved in emotional processing and stress response. 6

Among cognitively unimpaired individuals, psychiatric symptoms have been associated with higher Aβ and tau burden in most, 7 , 8 but not all, studies. 9 However, current understanding is limited by methodological constraints highlighted by meta‐analyses. 7 , 9 Most studies in cognitively unimpaired individuals have relied on cross‐sectional approaches that do not capture disease dynamics and/or focus on overall Aβ biomarkers—such as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), plasma, or global standardized uptake value ratio (SUVr)—that lack regional specificity.

Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests a convergence of emotional circuits and early pathological changes providing a neurobiological basis for psychiatric symptoms in AD. Evidence comes from studies focusing on specific brain regions in preclinical AD suggesting that psychiatric symptoms correlate with pathology and metabolic decline in regions fundamental to emotional regulation. 10 Specifically, among cognitively unimpaired individuals, PTSD symptoms are associated cross‐sectionally with higher Aβ burden in the anterior cingulate, posterior cingulate, precuneus, temporal cortex, and frontal cortex, 11 and psychiatric symptoms are associated with metabolic decline in the posterior cingulate and prefrontal cortices. 12

Additionally, research across the AD continuum with imaging biomarker data reveals that neuropsychiatric symptoms correlate with AD‐related biomarkers in regions critical for emotional regulation, including the anterior and posterior cingulate and prefrontal cortex. 13 , 14 Similarly, across the continuum, neuropsychiatric symptoms have been linked to tau in behavioral circuit regions vulnerable to early AD pathology. 15

The COVID‐19 pandemic constitutes a global traumatic event that significantly increased psychiatric symptoms worldwide. 16 Our previous research showed that the presence of AD pathology before the pandemic predicted a subsequent increase in anxious‐depressive symptoms, indicating heightened vulnerability in response to traumatic events. 17 Building on this foundation, the current study explores whether early AD‐related changes may act as a mechanistic pathway underlying the development or worsening of psychiatric symptoms following a traumatic event.

Our primary objective was to investigate whether regional Aβ accumulation was associated with greater post‐traumatic distress—total, intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal—and anxious‐depressive symptoms after the pandemic in cognitively unimpaired individuals at increased risk of AD. We hypothesized that accelerated Aβ accumulation in regions involved in emotional processing affected early in AD would correlate with worse post‐pandemic psychiatric outcomes.

METHODS

Participants and study design

This prospective cohort study drew participants from the ALzheimer's and FAmilies (ALFA) parent cohort, which recruited 2743 cognitively unimpaired individuals aged 45 to 75 years between 2013 and 2014. The majority (86%) had at least one parent diagnosed with AD (excluding autosomal‐dominant AD). 18 Participants were excluded if they reported an active psychiatric disease at baseline (according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, Text Revision, including current major depression or general anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and dementia); a disorder that could affect cognition, evaluated by an interdisciplinary committee of physicians and a neuropsychologist; severe auditory and/or visual disorder, neurodevelopmental and/or psychomotor disorder; or neurological disorders or brain injuries (Table S1). However, participants with a prior diagnosis of a psychiatric disorder were eligible for inclusion.

The nested prospective ALFA+ study included 451 cognitively unimpaired participants undergoing longitudinal evaluations (approximately every 3 years) including clinical, lifestyle, cognitive testing, genotyping, lumbar puncture, magnetic resonance imaging, and Aβ positron emission tomography (PET).

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. The ALFA+ and COVID‐19 protocols were approved by the relevant ethics committees and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02485730, 2020/9255). All participants provided written informed consent, and all methods were carried out in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

Mental health assessments

Post‐traumatic distress symptoms

During the post‐pandemic period, participants completed the Impact of Events Scale Revised (IES‐R), 19 which is a self‐reported questionnaire used to measure distress related to a traumatic event. Participants reported distress related to the COVID‐19 pandemic. The 22‐item scale includes three subscales (Intrusion, Avoidance, Hyperarousal), with total scores ranging from 0 to 88. A cut‐off score of ≥25 has been suggested for identifying patients who may have PTSD. 20 As a screening instrument, IES‐R measures self‐reported distress symptoms rather than clinical diagnoses.

Anxiety and depressive symptoms

The HADS was self‐administered at three time points: before the pandemic (between July 2016 and February 2020), during the pandemic, and after the pandemic. This 14‐item scale comprises separate 7‐item anxiety and depression subscales (total range 0 to 21 [≤7 normal, 8 to 10 borderline, ≥11 probable anxiety or depression]), which were considered separately. 21 The HADS is a screening tool for anxiety and depression rather than a diagnostic tool.

Aβ PET data acquisition and pre‐processing

[18F] Flutemetamol PET acquisitions were performed before the pandemic (March 2017 to July 2019) and after the pandemic (March 2020 to June 2022) on a Siemens Biograph mCT (Munich, Germany) following a cranial computed tomography scan for attenuation correction; 185 MBq (range 166.5 to 203.5 MBq) of [18F] flutemetamol was injected into the participants, and four frames of 5 min each were acquired 90 min after the injection.

Images were reconstructed using an 3D‐OSEM algorithm and pre‐processed in SPM12 (http://www.fl.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). The averaged PET images were co‐registered to the corresponding T1‐weighted images. Then, T1‐weighted images were segmented and normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space alongside the PET images. The SUVr was calculated in MNI space from the bilateral frontal and parietotemporal areas using the whole cerebellum as reference region. Additionally, Centiloid scale was used to quantify all Aβ PET scans using an in‐house validated pipeline (further details are described elsewhere). 22

We focused on Aβ accumulation in regions of interest (ROIs) commonly implicated in early Aβ pathology 23 and with primary or secondary roles in emotional processing. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 The ROIs identified a priori included the anterior and posterior cingulate, superior parietal, and medial orbitofrontal gyrus, as well as the insula, while the lingual gyrus was selected as a control area because it is not implicated in the earliest Aβ accumulation and has limited involvement in emotional processing, only in specific top‐down visual‐related emotional responses. 23 This control allows us to distinguish region‐specific associations in emotional processing areas from non‐specific effects of global Aβ accumulation. Annualized change rates were calculated between time points for ROI SUVrs based on the raw averaged values from the left and right hemispheres—annual rate = (Δabsolute)/(t2 ‐ t1) – and were considered continuous.

Demographics

Demographic data were collected through self‐reported questionnaires between 2016 and 2019 and included age (at the time of outcome measurements) and years of education (continuous). Sex/gender was self‐reported as a binary variable (woman/man).

APOE ε4 status

Participants were genotyped for two single nucleotide variants, rs429358 and rs7412, which determine the apolipoprotein E (apoE) protein isoform produced from the APOE gene. APOE ε4 carrier status was included as a binary covariate.

Pandemic‐related stressors

In the during‐ and post‐pandemic periods, participants reported exposure to 11 pandemic‐related stressors (e.g., bereavement, job loss, living conditions; see Table S2 for the full list). These were summed into a continuous pandemic‐stressor score to account for objective stressful events in all analyses.

Cognition

Participants’ cognition was measured between July 29, 2016, and March 25, 2019, using a modified version of the Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (mPACC), a validated composite score designed to detect subtle cognitive changes in preclinical AD populations. 30 The mPACC was computed using z‐transformed scores from the Total Paired Recall and Total Delayed Free Recall of the Memory Binding Test, the Coding subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale–Fourth Edition, and semantic fluency, the latter included in place of the Mini‐Mental State Examination to enhance sensitivity to early cognitive changes. 31 All neuropsychological tests were administered using their validated Spanish versions. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Cognitive assessments were conducted at a mean of 0.54 (SD = 0.54) years before first PET and 4.14 (SD = 0.62) years before post‐pandemic mental health assessments.

Statistical analyses

Participant characteristics were summarized as means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for binary variables. We conducted regression analyses examining associations between annualized Aβ accumulation rate and post‐pandemic PTSD‐like total and subscale scores, as well as depression and anxiety symptoms, adjusting for sex/gender, years of education, age, APOE ε4 status, pandemic‐related stressors, and pre‐pandemic depression and anxiety scores.

Sensitivity analyses examined during‐pandemic HADS scores as covariates, as prior work from our group demonstrated that stress perception during the pandemic predicted post‐pandemic anxious‐depressive symptoms. 36 Additional sensitivity analyses examined whether associations remained significant after adjusting for global cognitive function (mPACC) and history of psychiatric diagnosis status separately, to account for stable, pre‐existing differences in cognitive performance and potential influence of pre‐existing psychiatric conditions on ROI‐level associations, respectively.

Analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 and R version 4.2.2. Significance was set at p < 0.05 and corrected for multiple comparisons (false discovery rate [FDR]) to account for multiple ROIs. 37

Voxel‐wise statistical analyses

Confirmatory voxel‐based analyses were performed using multiple regression in SPM12 to test for associations between annualized Aβ change maps and post‐pandemic psychiatric outcomes, adjusting for the same covariates as in ROI analyses. We considered significant results surviving an uncorrected p value of 0.005 at the voxel level and a cluster‐level p < 0.05 family‐wise error (FWE) correction.

RESULTS

Table 1 shows the demographic, clinical, and imaging data of the participants (n = 115). The sample consisted of 60.0% (n = 69) women, all participants were White, and the mean age at post‐pandemic outcome measurements was 64.93 (SD = 4.80). The mean post‐pandemic IES‐R total score was 15.68 (SD = 11.37, range 0 to 51), with 27 (23.5%) participants scoring ≥25, indicating a potential clinical concern. 22 The mean HADS‐Depression score was 2.15 (SD = 2.69, range 0 to 13), with three (2.6%) participants scoring ≥11, indicating probable depression. The mean HADS‐Anxiety was 4.23 (SD = 3.73, range 0 to 20), with nine (7.8%) participants scoring ≥11, indicating probable anxiety. At baseline, the sample exhibited characteristics typical of early preclinical AD, including low Aβ burden (mean Centiloids 3.85, SD = 16.80, range −19.44 to 64.07), with 84.3% of participants being Aβ‐negative, as well as low baseline mPACC scores (mean 0.04, SD = 0.65, range −1.98 to 1.34). Consistent with a preclinical status, participants remained cognitively unimpaired throughout the study (mean mPACC follow‐up 0.15, SD = 0.64, range −1.39 to 1.5). While Aβ burden progressed, the sample average at follow‐up (mean Centiloids 8.72, SD = 21.98, range −17.88 to 89.62) remained substantially below the proposed cut‐offs for AD pathology. 38

Sensitivity and post hoc analyses

Models adjusted for during‐pandemic HADS‐Depression and HADS‐Anxiety, instead of pre‐pandemic scores (n = 112; three participants were missing during‐pandemic HADS) for analyses that were significant in the main models revealed that the association of annualized Aβ change in ROIs with IES‐R intrusion scores attenuated to significant only at the FDR‐uncorrected level (Table S3).

Given that sensitivity analyses showed attenuation when adjusting for during‐pandemic mood symptoms, we conducted post hoc mediation analyses to examine potential pathways linking Aβ accumulation to intrusive symptoms. This analysis was motivated by our previous findings where Aβ burden predicted during‐pandemic anxious‐depressive symptoms in this cohort 17 and by evidence that depression and intrusive memories showed complex interrelationships, with dysphoric individuals demonstrating heightened intrusion‐based reasoning. 39 We performed mediation analyses using bootstrap procedures (computed for N = 1000 bootstrapped samples) to investigate whether the observed associations between Aβ change and post‐pandemic PTSD intrusion scores were mediated by during‐pandemic depression and anxiety symptoms.

These analyses (n = 112) showed a partial mediation effect of HADS‐Depression scores during the pandemic, but not HADS‐Anxiety scores, restricted to the annualized Aβ change in the posterior cingulate, explaining 38% of the association (Figures 3, S3).

In sensitivity analyses, the association of IES‐R intrusion with annualized Aβ change in all ROIs remained unchanged with adjustment to global cognition (mPACC) and history of psychiatric diagnosis status (Tables S4, S5), except for the association with orbitofrontal gyrus Aβ accumulation, which attenuated after adjustment for the history of psychiatric diagnosis status.

DISCUSSION

In cognitively unimpaired older adults at risk for AD dementia, this study investigated whether accelerated Aβ accumulation in regions vulnerable to early Aβ and involved in cognitive‐emotional processing was associated with post‐traumatic distress and anxious‐depressive symptoms approximately 1.5 years after the COVID‐19 pandemic. Using longitudinal PET, we found that higher rates of Aβ accumulation across several regions were associated with greater self‐reported intrusive symptoms. These novel findings suggest that intrusive symptoms may represent a distinct psychological pathway in preclinical AD, linked to Aβ accumulation in regions critical for emotional regulation, suggesting that these symptoms may be associated with early neurobiological changes.

We found Aβ accumulation to be associated with post‐pandemic intrusive symptoms, but not with total post‐pandemic distress symptoms or anxious‐depressive symptoms. The specificity of our findings to intrusive symptoms is noteworthy. In clinical PTSD, intrusive symptoms primarily reflect involuntary re‐experiencing of autobiographical memories rather than non‐specific intrusive thoughts. 40 The posterior cingulate, which showed the strongest association with intrusive symptoms, is particularly relevant given its high metabolic activity and widespread connectivity 25 and being one of the first regions to show Aβ buildup. 39 As a core hub of the default‐mode network (DMN), the posterior cingulate plays an important role in autobiographical memory retrieval, self‐referential processing and introspection. 25 , 41

Notably, the association between Aβ accumulation and intrusive symptoms was consistent across multiple ROIs and confirmed by voxel‐wise analyses. This widespread pattern aligns with the complex nature of intrusive symptoms, which engage distributed neural networks beyond the DMN, 42 including the salience network (emotional processing and threat detection), 10 executive control network (attention control and working memory), 26 and temporal network (semantic processing and internal speech). The multi‐network involvement may explain why associations were observed across several regions and highlights a potential relationship between early Aβ accumulation and the coordination of neural activity necessary for the effective regulation of intrusive memories, a process potentially not affected by global cognitive function, as evidenced by sensitivity analyses.

Further, sensitivity analyses controlling for during‐pandemic anxious‐depressive symptoms showed that the associations between Aβ accumulation in ROIs and intrusive symptoms remained significant at an uncorrected level, suggesting a relationship that operates at least partially independently of mood symptoms. In mediation analyses, during‐pandemic depressive symptoms partially mediated (38%) the association between Aβ accumulation in the posterior cingulate and intrusive symptoms, with no mediation for other ROIs. Building on our previous work showing that pre‐pandemic Aβ predicted during‐pandemic anxious‐depressive symptoms in this cohort, 17 one interpretation is that Aβ accumulation in the posterior cingulate is associated with a heightened vulnerability to depressive symptoms during acute stress, which subsequently contributes to intrusive thoughts. However, several factors limit this interpretation. First, definitive temporal ordering cannot be established; second, depressive and intrusive symptoms likely have bidirectional relationships 43 ; and third, the specificity to the posterior cingulate with attenuation to uncorrected significance in sensitivity analyses suggests that these exploratory findings require replication in larger, prospective studies with multiple assessments.

While Aβ accumulation had not been previously linked specifically to intrusive symptoms, negative thinking has been connected to higher Aβ and tau burden and cognitive decline in non‐demented adults, 44 providing additional support for the link between pathology and thought‐control difficulties. Further, previous research showed that higher metabolic activity in the posterior cingulate, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and right anterior insula correlated with neuropsychiatric symptoms in preclinical AD 45 and that elevated posterior cingulate activity has been linked with rumination in depression, 46 a process potentially mechanistically similar to intrusive symptoms.

These findings may have important implications for understanding psychiatric vulnerability in preclinical AD. Clinically, our findings suggest that intrusive symptoms following stressful events may be related to underlying neurobiological vulnerability in at‐risk individuals. Intrusive symptoms may identify vulnerable individuals regardless of apparent exposure to objective stressors and general cognitive functioning, potentially informing risk identification approaches. However, the cross‐sectional nature of symptom assessments and reliance on self‐report measures limit definitive conclusions about clinical utility.

Our findings did not support the hypothesis that accelerated Aβ accumulation would be associated with post‐pandemic total post‐traumatic distress symptoms when analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons. The specificity to intrusive symptoms suggests a particular vulnerability in thought regulation rather than broader post‐traumatic distress symptomatology. Our results differ from a previous study among cognitively normal older adults, where PTSD symptoms were associated cross‐sectionally with higher Aβ burden in the anterior cingulate, posterior cingulate, precuneus, temporal cortex, and frontal cortex. 13 The discrepancy likely reflects methodological differences: uncorrected versus FDR‐corrected p values, different sample sizes, our focus on intrusive symptoms specifically rather than total PTSD scores, and our longitudinal assessment of Aβ accumulation rather than cross‐sectional burden.

Similarly, our findings did not support the hypothesis that accelerated Aβ accumulation would be associated with post‐pandemic anxiety and depressive symptoms. Previous research yielded mixed findings, with some studies reporting an association between depressive or anxiety symptoms and Aβ in cognitively unimpaired individuals cross‐sectionally 47 , 48 and longitudinally, 49 but others reporting no associations. 50 A recent meta‐analysis on cross‐sectional studies showed no associations of Aβ with depressive symptoms in CSF, PET, or plasma studies, but it did report an association among individuals with cognitive impairment. 11 These findings suggest the relationship between Aβ pathology and depression may develop later in the disease process or require a higher threshold of symptoms. Our predominantly (84%) Aβ‐negative cohort with low anxious‐depressive symptom levels aligns with this possibility.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small and consisted primarily of highly educated White participants, limiting generalizability. Potential selection bias, with respondents having lower baseline symptoms, requires validation in larger, more diverse cohorts. While our study benefited from a well‐characterized at‐risk sample followed up longitudinally for a mean of 3.6 years with unique data collected during the pandemic, this relatively short interval and the cross‐sectional assessment of post‐traumatic distress likely limited our power to observe associations with slowly evolving symptoms. Moreover, the possibility of reverse causation or that pre‐existing psychological vulnerabilities (e.g., neuroticism) contributed to both Aβ accumulation and intrusive symptoms remains. Further, we relied exclusively on self‐report measures without clinical diagnostic interviews, limiting our ability to make definitive statements about PTSD or clinical depression/anxiety. Indeed, participants showed relatively low symptom levels with limited variability, potentially limiting statistical power to detect associations with broader PTSD symptomatology or depression/anxiety symptoms. Additionally, the IES‐R in this study assessed distress related to the pandemic, which may not qualify as a traumatic event. Future research utilizing longitudinal symptom trajectories may offer greater sensitivity to detect pathology‐driven shifts in subclinical samples. Finally, future work should examine associations relative to other biopsychosocial risk factors for both mental health and AD, such as social determinants of health.

CONCLUSION

This study reveals a novel and specific association of accelerated Aβ accumulation with greater intrusive symptoms in cognitively unimpaired adults at risk for AD, independent of general cognitive function and objective stressor exposure and partly independent of anxious‐depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that intrusive symptoms may represent a distinct and early psychological manifestation of AD‐related neurobiological vulnerability, observed in conjunction with Aβ accumulation in regions critical for emotional processing and thought control. While these findings require replication in larger, more diverse samples with prospective designs and clinical assessments, they highlight intrusive symptoms as a potential marker of early AD pathology that may inform risk assessment and early intervention strategies in at‐risk populations.
