Heqi San alleviates diabetic atherosclerosis and alters serum metabolic profiles in rats
Authors: Shufang Chu, Hongli Wang, Deliang Liu, Hengxia Zhao, Juntong Li, Xuemei Liu, Huilin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeqi San has shown therapeutic potential in the treatment of diabetes. This study aimed to explore the mechanism underlying the effects of Heqi San in treating rats with diabetic atherosclerosis (AS).MethodsWe established a model of atherosclerosis in diabetic rats and treated them with Heqi San. Body weight, food intake, and water intake were measured every two weeks. Subsequently, we examined the effects of Heqi San on serum biochemical markers, aortic structure, fat accumulation, and serum levels of inflammatory factors. The levels of p-AMPK and AMPK in thoracic aorta tissue were evaluated by western blotting. Mass spectrometry was used to identify differential serum metabolites after Heqi San treatment.ResultsIntragastric administration of Heqi San enhanced food intake and reduced the excessive water intake caused by diabetic atherosclerosis. Both low and high doses of Heqi San decreased elevated levels of TG, TC, LDL, GLU, and insulin, while increasing HDL levels. Additionally, the AS group showed increased expression of CD68 and CCL-2 in thoracic aorta tissue, along with elevated serum levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. Treatment with Heqi San reduced these inflammatory markers. Heqi San also increased p-AMPK expression in thoracic aorta tissue. Mass spectrometry analysis revealed that the AS group had significantly elevated serum levels of L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin, whereas high-dose Heqi San treatment decreased the levels of these metabolites.ConclusionsHeqi San could alleviate atherosclerosis in diabetic rats, and this effect may be associated with changes in serum metabolites such as L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin.Keywords: Heqi San, Fat accumulation, Metabolism, Diabetic atherosclerosis, AMPK

Introduction

Diabetes is well-known for its complications, especially vascular complications, and has become a severe global social issue. Atherosclerosis is a key contributor to cardiovascular complications in diabetes [1], and is characterized by chronic vascular inflammation [2]. Among them, the diabetes-related dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism has been proven to play a central role in atherosclerosis occurrence and progression [3]. Elevated blood glucose levels, abnormal lipid profiles, and other metabolic changes accompanying the progression of the disease are closely associated with every step of atherosclerosis development [4]. Establishing the pathological connection between diabetes and atherosclerosis is essential, but a comprehensive comprehension of its underlying mechanisms is crucial to identify potential molecular targets for further exploration [5]. Furthermore, there is still a lack of clarity regarding the appropriate strategies for preventing and treating diabetic atherosclerosis (AS). Therefore, there is an urgent need to discover new therapeutic approaches.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) possesses the distinguishing qualities of targeting multiple pathways, making it advantageous in diagnosing and treating atherosclerosis [6–8]. Studies have shown that some Chinese herbal medicines treat atherosclerosis by targeting and improving lipid metabolism disorders and inhibiting inflammatory reactions [9]. According to reports, Chinese herbal medicines like Astragali Radix, Glycyrrhizae Radix et Rhizoma, Fructus Lycii, and Coptidis Rhizoma have therapeutic effects on atherosclerosis [10]. A randomized controlled multicenter trial study protocol showed that Shenqi compound, a Chinese herbal medicine, improves lower limb atherosclerosis in patients with type 2 diabetes by affecting blood glucose fluctuations [11]. In addition, Chinese herbal medicine could treat diabetic atherosclerosis by regulating the NLRP3 inflammasome [12]. Studies have shown that Heqi San has the effect of regulating hormone levels in patients with metabolic diseases and has potential clinical application value [13]. Moreover, Heqi San has a significant therapeutic effect in treating diabetes. However, the mechanism of Heqi San in diabetic atherosclerosis is still unclear.

Given the provided background, we hypothesise that Heqi San may have a beneficial effect on diabetic atherosclerosis. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the impact of different doses of Heqi San on body weight, lipid metabolism, inflammatory markers, and vascular pathology in rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. In addition, mass spectrometry was employed to detect changes in serum metabolic profiles before and after treatment. These data may help to reveal metabolic features associated with the intervention. This study may provide experimental support for the potential application of traditional Chinese medicine in the prevention and management of diabetic atherosclerosis.

Methods

Preparation of Heqi San

Heqi San consists of eight traditional Chinese medicinal herbs: Nelumbinis Folium (lotus leaf), Astragali Radix (astragalus root), Atractylodis Rhizoma (atractylodes), Herba Lycopi (ze lan), Cassiae Semen (cassia seed), stir-fried Benincasae Exocarpium (winter melon peel), Dioscoreae Rhizoma (yam), and Glycyrrhizae Radix et Rhizoma (licorice root). All herbal materials were authenticated and weighed according to the standardized prescription proportions. The combined herbs were extracted by traditional decoction, involving two rounds of boiling with ten times the volume of water, each for 60 min. The decoctions were then combined and concentrated under reduced pressure to obtain a semi-solid extract. Dosages for animal administration were calculated based on the crude herb equivalents, corresponding to 1.7325 g/kg for the low-dose group and 6.93 g/kg for the high-dose group. The formula and extraction method were developed according to traditional practices and previously reported quality control studies. Notably, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) fingerprinting and multi-component quantification strategies such as QAMS have been established for this preparation in prior literature, providing references for its chemical standardization and consistency [14–16].

Animals

An atherosclerosis model in diabetic rats was conducted by Huateng BioScience (Guangzhou, China). SD rats were kept in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light-dark cycle, ensuring room temperature between 22 and 24 °C and humidity levels between 30 and 70%. They had adequate access to food and water before the start of the experiment. The sample size of six rats per group (n = 24 in total) was determined based on standard experimental design considerations and previous study utilizing similar animal models and outcome measures [17]. The rats were randomly divided into a control group (NC group, six rats) and a model group (18 rats) with 24 rats. In the control group, the rats were fed a regular diet for four weeks. An equal volume of sodium citrate buffer was injected into the rats. After six weeks of the injection, all rats underwent a 12-hour fasting period. Blood samples were then collected from the tail to measure blood glucose levels. The model group (type 2 diabetes) was given high-fat feeding (HFF, 18% lard, 20% sucrose, 2.5% cholesterol, 1% bile salt, and 58.5% basal diet) for four weeks. At the end of the fourth week, all rats underwent a 12-hour fasting period without water. The following day, rats in the model group were injected intraperitoneally with 35 mg/kg of streptozotocin (STZ), prepared in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer to achieve a 1% STZ solution concentration. After two weeks of injection, all rats underwent another 12-hour fasting period, and blood samples were collected from the tail to measure blood glucose levels. If the blood glucose level exceeded 11.1 mmol/L, it indicated the successful establishment of the diabetes model.

After the successful establishment of the diabetes model, the rats were randomly divided into the following groups based on their blood glucose levels: control group (NC group), model group (AS group), model + low-dose Heqi San group (AS + L-HQS group), and model + high-dose Heqi San group (AS + H-HQS group). Each group consisted of 6 rats, making a total of 24 rats. Group allocation was performed using a computer-generated random number table to minimize selection bias. To reduce dietary interference during treatment, all rats were switched to a standard chow diet following model confirmation. In the AS group, rats received an equal volume of saline by gavage once daily for 28 consecutive days. In the AS + L-HQS group, rats were intragastrically administered 1.7325 g/kg/d of Heqi San once a day for 28 days. In the AS + H-HQS group, rats were intragastrically administered 6.93 g/kg/d of Heqi San once a day for 28 days. During the 28-day treatment period, all groups, including the NC group, were maintained on the same standard chow to ensure dietary consistency. The general clinical condition of the rats was observed and recorded daily until the experiment’s conclusion.

The animals’ body weight was measured at the beginning and end of the experiment, with additional measurements taken every two weeks during the experiment. Throughout the study, the food and water intake of the rats were measured at two-week intervals. Furthermore, whole blood samples were collected from the rats, and the serum was separated from these samples. Each group took 100 µL of serum, stored separately at −20℃ for subsequent biochemical tests. Another 200 µL of each group’s serum was stored separately at −20℃ for subsequent enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) tests. The remaining serum was stored at −20℃ for mass spectrometry identification. The thoracic aortas of the rats were dissected, with three rats from each group being fixed in formalin for subsequent pathological and immunohistochemistry (IHC) testing. Three additional rats from each group were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Blinded procedures were implemented during sample processing and analysis; personnel conducting histological and biochemical assessments were not informed of the group identities to reduce observational bias. All animal experiments were conducted with prior approval from Guangzhou Huateng Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd (Approval number HTSW211205). The animal experiments were performed according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Biochemical detection

Serum levels of triglyceride (TG), Total cholesterol (TC), High-density lipoprotein (HDL), Low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and glucose (GLU) were measured using the corresponding kits. TG (S03027), TC (S03042), HDL (S03025), LDL (S03029), and GLU (S03039) assay kits are all from Rayto Life, and Analytical Sciences Co., Ltd., TG, TC, HDL, LDL, and GLU were detected according to the instructions. All experiments were conducted a minimum of three times.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining was employed to evaluate the structural changes in the rat thoracic aorta tissue. Sections were baked at 60℃ for 1–2 h and then dewaxed to water. Hematoxylin (BL735A, Biosharp) was applied for 5–10 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water and subsequent blueing with PBS. Eosin staining was performed for 3–5 min. Each stage of the dehydration process involved 5 min of gradual alcohol (95–100%) treatment. After removal, sections were immersed in xylene for 10 min, repeated twice, and then sealed with neutral gum before being observed using a fluorescence microscope (BA210T, Motic).

Oil-red O staining

Oil-red O staining was conducted to detect fat accumulation in rat thoracic aortic tissue. Sections were retrieved from − 20 °C and allowed to reach room temperature for 5–10 min. Once the frozen sections had fully thawed, they were gently immersed in Oil Red O working solution (G1015, Servicebio) for 8–10 min, ensuring they were covered and protected from light. Subsequently, sections were removed and left for 3 s before being briefly immersed in 60% isopropanol for 3–5 s. Following this, sections were sequentially immersed in purified water for 10 s. Hematoxylin staining was performed for 1–3 min, and sections were rinsed with distilled water before being returned to blue using PBS. Buffered glycerol was used to seal the sections, which were subsequently observed under a microscope.

IHC

IHC was applied to determine CD68 and CCL-2 expressions in rat thoracic aortic tissues. Before deparaffinisation, tissue sections were placed at room temperature for 60 min or baked in a thermostat at 60 °C for 20 min. Tissue sections were sequentially placed in xylene (10 min), xylene (10 min), anhydrous ethanol (5 min), 95% ethanol (5 min), 70% ethanol (5 min), PBS (5 min), and PBS (5 min). A concentration of 3% H2O2/PB was used and closed for 5–10 min, followed by washing with PBS three times for 2 min each. A concentration of 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer solution (pH 6.0) was used to heat and boil the hot fixation for 10–15 min, and the heating was completed by cooling to room temperature using tap water. After washing with PBS for 5 min, a blocking solution of 5% BSA was added dropwise and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. A drop of CD68 (28058-1-AP, Proteintech) or CCL-2 (25542-1-AP, Proteintech) primary antibody was added overnight at 4℃. Then, a sheep anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (BL003A, Biosharp) secondary antibody was added dropwise, and incubation was performed for 1 h. DAB method was used to develop the color; then hematoxylin re-staining was performed for 20 s. Sections were washed twice with distilled water for 2 min each time, followed by dehydration in a series of ethanol concentrations including 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%, with each step taking 2 min. Afterward, sections were immersed in 100% xylene for 10 min to achieve transparency. Finally, sections were sealed with a neutral resin and observed under a microscope.

ELISA

Serum Interleukin 6 (IL-6), IL-1β, and Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) levels were measured from rat serum. Inflammatory factors (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) and insulin (INS) were measured using IL-6 ELISA kit (RX302856R, RUIXIN Biotech), IL-1β ELISA kit (RX302869R, RUIXIN Biotech), TNF-α ELISA kit (RX302058R, RUIXIN Biotech), and Rat INS ELISA kit (RX302147R, RUIXIN Biotech) according to the instructions.

Western blot

The thoracic aorta tissue of rats was first ground on ice. Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing a 1% protease inhibitor (KeyGEN BioTECH; Nanjing, China). After protein concentration was quantified with a Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH), equal amounts of protein were separated using 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated with anti-p-AMPK (1: 1000; Abcam), anti-AMPK (1: 1000; Abcam), and anti-GAPDH (1: 1000; Abcam) overnight at 4℃ and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000; Abcam). Protein bands were then detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, UK).

Mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry was utilized to identify differential metabolites in the serum of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis treated with Heqi San. 100 µL of rat serum was taken, and 1000 µL of extraction solution, consisting of methanol, acetonitrile, and water in a ratio of 2:2:1 (v/v), was added to the serum sample and then shaken and mixed well. The sample was ultrasonicated in an ice water bath for 10 min and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1 min. This process was repeated three times. Next, the sample was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then evaporated using a nitrogen blower. 100 µL of acetonitrile water (1:1, v/v) was added to reconstitute the sample. The mixture was shaken for 30 s, followed by another round of sonication in an ice water bath for 10 min. Then, the sample was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The separation was performed on a UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters, USA) with an injection volume of 5 µL and a column temperature of 55℃, using a mobile phase of A-100% H2O 25 mM CH3COONH4 + 25 mM NH4OH, and B-100% ACN, with a gradient elution condition of 0–1 min. 85% B; 1–12 min, 65% B; 12–12.1 min, 40% B; 12.1–15 min, 40% B; 15–15.1 min, 85% B; 15.1–20 min, 85% B; and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. For mass spectrometry, an electrospray ionization source was employed. Depending on the experiment, the ion source temperature was set to 600 °C, while the ion source voltage was either − 4500–5500 V. The air curtain gas was maintained at 20 psi, while the nebulizing and auxiliary gases were set at 60 psi each. Scanning was carried out using multiple reaction monitoring modes. ProteoWizard software was used to convert the raw files into mzxml format for data analysis. Following this, peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction were performed using the XCMS program. Metabolite structures were accurately matched (< 25 ppm) using primary and secondary spectra, employing self-built databases (VGDB) as well as public databases. Data were normalized and then statistically analyzed and plotted using R software. Differential metabolites were identified based on fold change and uncorrected P-values (P < 0.05), reflecting the exploratory nature of the analysis and the limited sample size (n = 6 per group). False discovery rate (FDR) correction was also applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and the resulting q-values are reported in Tables 1 and 2. Each table includes the compound name, group means, fold change, log2 fold change, p-value, q-value, and regulation direction (up/down). Metabolites with q < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Standard deviations were not shown due to output limitations of the metabolomics analysis software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. The measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between multiple groups were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For metabolomics data, statistical comparisons were based on six biological replicates per group (n = 6).

Results

The effect of Heqi San on the behaviour of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis

First, a diabetic rat model of atherosclerosis was established and treated with Heqi San. After HFF, the body weight of the AS groups significantly increased. After two weeks of STZ injection, the body weight of the AS group started to decrease. However, after intragastric administration of a high dose of Heqi San, the weight loss caused by the AS group was restored (Fig. 1A). Additionally, after HFF, the food and water intake of the AS group significantly increased. After two weeks of STZ injection, the food and water intake of the AS group was higher than the NC group. Following Heqi San treatment, food intake remained slightly elevated in AS groups compared to NC but stayed within a physiologically tolerable range. Water intake showed a decreasing trend (Fig. 1B and C). During treatment, all rats were maintained on the same standard diet, eliminating nutritional differences as a source of bias.

The effect of Heqi San on serum biochemical indicators in rats with diabetic atherosclerosis

Next, we examined the effect of Heqi San on the serum biochemical indicators of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. Compared to the NC group, the AS group of rats showed elevated serum TG, TC, LDL, GLU, and INS levels and decreased HDL levels; low or high doses of Heqi San could decrease TG, TC, LDL, GLU, and INS levels and increase the level of HDL in AS model rats (Fig. 2A and F).

The effect of Heqi San on serum biochemical indicators in rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. A-F. The content of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), glucose (GLU), and insulin (INS) levels in rat serum. Data are presented as mean ± SD, n = 6. *P < 0.05, ***P < 0.001

The effects of Heqi San on the arterial structure and fat accumulation in rats with diabetic atherosclerosis

Next, we assessed the effects of Heqi San on arterial structure and fat accumulation in rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. H&E staining showed that the NC group presented with an intact vascular structure and no obvious proliferation of smooth muscle cells, while the AS group exhibited structural disorganization, lipid plaque formation, and increased smooth muscle cell proliferation. In contrast, the AS + L-HQS and AS + H-HQS groups showed partial restoration of vascular wall integrity, with alleviated intimal thickening and more regular smooth muscle arrangement (Fig. 3A). Oil Red O staining revealed that fat accumulation in the thoracic aorta was minimal in the NC group, but markedly increased in the AS group, with lipid deposits mainly distributed along the intimal surface. This distribution pattern was further demonstrated by the inclusion of both low- and high-magnification images, highlighting the location and extent of lipid deposition. Treatment with Heqi San, at either low or high dose, significantly reduced lipid accumulation compared with the AS group (Fig. 3B). Besides, immunohistochemical analysis showed increased expression of the macrophage marker CD68 and the inflammatory chemokine CCL-2 in the AS group relative to the NC group. After treatment with Heqi San, both markers were notably downregulated in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3C and D).

The effects of Heqi San on the arterial structure and fat accumulation in rats with diabetic atherosclerosis

Next, we assessed the effects of Heqi San on arterial structure and fat accumulation in rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. H&E staining showed that the NC group presented with intact arterial structure and no evident smooth muscle cell proliferation, whereas the AS group exhibited disorganized vascular architecture, accompanied by lipid plaque formation and increased smooth muscle cell proliferation. In contrast, the AS + L-HQS and AS + H-HQS groups showed partial restoration of vascular wall integrity, with alleviation of intimal thickening and improved smooth muscle cell arrangement (Fig. 3A and B). Oil Red O staining revealed that the NC group had minimal fat accumulation in the thoracic aorta, while the AS group showed substantial lipid deposition, primarily located in the intimal layer. This localization and extent of lipid accumulation were further confirmed by higher magnification images. Following treatment with low or high doses of Heqi San, lipid deposition was markedly reduced compared with the AS group (Fig. 3C and D). Additionally, compared to the NC group, the AS group displayed increased expression of the macrophage marker CD68 and the inflammatory chemokine CCL-2 in the thoracic aorta. Treatment with Heqi San significantly decreased the levels of both CD68 and CCL-2 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3E and F).

The effects of Heqi San on inflammatory factor levels in the serum of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis

Additionally, we examined the effects of Heqi San on inflammatory factor levels in the serum of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. Compared to the NC group, the AS group showed elevated IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels in the serum. However, after treatment with low or high doses of Heqi San, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels decreased (Fig. 4A and C).

Heqi San upregulated p-AMPK in the thoracic aorta tissue of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis

Next, a western blot assay was conducted to confirm the effects of Heqi San on p-AMPK and AMPK expressions in the serum of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. The results denoted that relative to the NC group, the protein level of p-AMPK was reduced in the AS group; low or high doses of Heqi San could upregulate p-AMPK in the thoracic aorta tissue of AS model rats (Fig. 5).

Identification of serum metabolic differences associated with Heqi San treatment in diabetic atherosclerosis rats

Finally, we used mass spectrometry to identify the differences in metabolic components in the serum of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis before and after treatment with Heqi San. As shown in Fig. 6A, the Venn diagram displayed 29 differential metabolites shared between the AS group and the NC group (Table 1) and seven differential metabolites shared between the AS group and the AS + H-HQS group (Table 2), with three common differential metabolites (L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin) among them. The mass spectrometry results showed that L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin were significantly elevated in the AS group compared to the NC group. Treatment with high-dose Heqi San was associated with a reduction in their serum levels (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Coronary atherosclerosis is a major complication in patients with diabetes, and both type 1 and type 2 diabetes significantly increase the risk of cardiovascular disease [18, 19]. Exploring new therapeutic strategies and clarifying underlying mechanisms are therefore crucial. In this study, we established a rat model of diabetic atherosclerosis and evaluated the effects of Heqi San. Our results showed that Heqi San treatment improved lipid profiles, reduced vascular inflammation, and mitigated pathological changes in the aorta. In addition, we observed significant changes in several serum metabolites, including L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin, following Heqi San administration. Although the specific roles of these metabolites remain to be elucidated, their altered levels suggest a potential association with the therapeutic response. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the metabolic effects of Heqi San in a diabetic atherosclerosis model. Notably, all rats were maintained on a standardized diet throughout the intervention, minimizing the impact of nutritional differences on metabolic outcomes. In this study, histological and biochemical features of aortic lesions, such as lipid accumulation, inflammatory infiltration, and blood lipid profiles, were designated as the primary endpoints to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of Heqi San.

With the development of TCM, more research and exploration have been conducted on the mechanisms of Chinese herbal monomers, Chinese patent medicines, and compound formulas. Tong W et al. reported that Buyang huanwu decoction could inhibit diabetes-induced atherosclerosis by reducing the AMPK-DRP1-mitochondrial fission axis [20]. Ji L et al. demonstrated that Scutellariae Radix-Coptidis Rhizoma polysaccharides could suppress inflammation, improve lipid levels, and reduce plaque area in atherosclerotic mice, playing a preventive and therapeutic role in atherosclerosis [21]. Wang L et al. revealed that the Fufang-Zhenzhu-Tiaozhi capsule could improve coronary artery atherosclerosis by regulating coronary artery inflammation, reducing cell apoptosis, and inhibiting endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition [22]. These studies demonstrate the positive effects of TCM in the treatment of diabetic atherosclerosis. Heqi San was prepared using a consistent decoction method and underwent HPLC fingerprint analysis to verify batch-to-batch uniformity, thereby enhancing the reproducibility and reliability of the intervention. In this study, we also aim to investigate the therapeutic effects of Heqi San and its underlying mechanisms in diabetic atherosclerosis in rats.

The characteristics of dyslipidemia in diabetes are elevated fasting and postprandial TG levels, low levels of HDL, and a slight increase in LDL. These are not independent components of atherosclerotic dyslipidemia, but they are closely related to each other [23]. LDL is a known lipoprotein that contributes to atherosclerosis [24]. Insulin resistance promotes LDL production and reduces HDL production [25]. Furthermore, visceral fat accumulation significantly influences atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, diabetes with hyperlipidemia, and hypertension [26]. In this study, we established a rat model of diabetic atherosclerosis and treated it with Heqi San. Although rats treated with Heqi San exhibited a modest increase in food consumption, the intake levels remained within a physiological range. Notably, all groups were maintained on the same standard chow during the intervention period, reducing the likelihood that dietary differences accounted for the lipid-lowering effects observed. Furthermore, Heqi San downregulated the elevated blood lipid levels and fat deposition caused by diabetic atherosclerosis. Inflammation associated with atherosclerosis is commonly mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines, inflammatory signaling pathways, bioactive lipids, and adhesion molecules [27]. Recent clinical trials have shown that inhibiting inflammation could prevent adverse clinical manifestations of atherosclerosis [28]. This study confirmed that Heqi San could alleviate inflammation in diabetic atherosclerosis rats. These findings support the therapeutic potential of Heqi San in diabetic atherosclerosis, warranting further mechanistic investigation.

AMPK is a cellular energy sensor that plays a central role in maintaining energy homeostasis [29]. It also regulates key physiological processes such as lipid and glucose metabolism [30]. Several studies have confirmed the involvement of AMPK in the development of atherosclerosis [31, 32]. For instance, AMPK phosphorylation has been shown to attenuate atherosclerosis in insulin-resistant mice [33], whereas impaired AMPK signaling may accelerate plaque formation by disrupting cholesterol metabolism [34]. Currently, no study has reported the regulatory effect of Heqi San on the AMPK pathway. Our findings provide the first evidence that Heqi San can enhance AMPK phosphorylation in the thoracic aorta of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis, suggesting that AMPK may serve as a downstream effector in its anti-atherosclerotic action. In addition to its established metabolic roles in the liver and macrophages, AMPK is known to exert protective effects in the vascular system [35]. In endothelial cells, AMPK activation promotes nitric oxide (NO) synthesis by upregulating endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), thereby improving vascular tone and endothelial function [36, 37]. Furthermore, AMPK inhibits NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling and reduces oxidative stress by enhancing antioxidant defenses [38]. These actions help preserve vascular homeostasis and limit the early progression of atherosclerotic lesions. Previous studies have shown that disrupted AMPK activity in the vasculature may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and atherogenesis [39, 40]. Therefore, exploring AMPK phosphorylation in aortic tissue offers valuable mechanistic insight into the vascular protective role of Heqi San in diabetic atherosclerosis. However, whether Heqi San alleviates atherosclerosis primarily through AMPK regulation remains to be confirmed and warrants further investigation. In addition, a previous study indicated that Heqi San may exert therapeutic effects via the PI3K/AKT pathway in polycystic ovary syndrome. This raises the possibility that the PI3K/AKT pathway may also be involved in the vascular effects of Heqi San, which we plan to explore in future studies. Although our findings suggest that AMPK phosphorylation may contribute to the vascular protective effects of Heqi San, we did not employ AMPK-specific inhibitors or gene-silencing techniques to establish a causal relationship. Therefore, further studies using pharmacological or genetic approaches will be essential to validate the mechanistic role of AMPK in mediating the therapeutic effects of Heqi San.

Metabolomics has emerged as a valuable tool for investigating the intricate pathophysiology of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases [41–43]. It is increasingly used for disease diagnosis, understanding disease mechanisms, identifying new drug targets, personalized drug therapy, and monitoring treatment outcomes [44]. In this study, we used mass spectrometry to identify metabolic differences in the serum of diabetic atherosclerosis rats before and after treatment with Heqi San, indicating the potential mechanism of Heqi San treatment. Behr-Roussel D et al. demonstrated that long-term administration of an inhibitor of iNOS, known as L-NIL, could effectively restrict the advancement of pre-existing atherosclerosis in hypercholesterolemic rabbits [45]. Previous research has indicated that charged or polar residues on the surface of insulin, such as substituting A4 (Asp) and A5 (Gln), consistently lead to a decrease in monoclonal antibody binding. On the other hand, substituting a single alanine for A11 (Cys) is predicted to modify the core structure or folding of the chain, resulting in variable impacts on antibody binding [46]. Furthermore, Geshoidin inhibited all three glutathione transferases and exhibited non-competitive inhibition against glutathione and 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene [47]. However, the role of Gln_Cys_Asp and Geshoidin in diabetic atherosclerosis remains poorly understood or investigated. This study found that Heqi San treatment was associated with a reduction in the levels of three common differential metabolites (L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin) in the serum of rats with diabetic atherosclerosis. Although these three metabolites (L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin) showed significant changes after Heqi San treatment, their direct involvement in mediating the therapeutic effects has not been established, representing a limitation of the present study. Given the exploratory design and the high cost and technical constraints of metabolomics, a relatively small sample size (n = 6) was employed to initially identify potential mechanistic candidates. Future studies with larger cohorts and targeted functional validations are planned to verify these findings.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that Heqi San may help alleviate atherosclerosis in diabetic rats. In this study, we established a diabetic atherosclerosis model and administered Heqi San to assess its therapeutic potential. Serum metabolomic analysis identified several differential metabolites, including L-NIL, Gln_Cys_Asp, and Geshoidin, which showed notable changes after treatment. While these metabolites may be associated with the observed improvements, their specific functional roles remain to be clarified. Further experimental validation is required to determine whether these metabolic changes contribute directly to the therapeutic effects. Future clinical and mechanistic studies will be essential to better understand the underlying pathways and to support the broader application of traditional Chinese medicine in diabetic atherosclerosis.
