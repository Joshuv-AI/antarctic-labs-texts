Interindividual differences in acidogenic potential of the salivary microbiome after mouthwash exposure
Authors: Fabian Mermans, Britt Poelman, Mehraveh Saghi, Wim Teughels, Nico Boon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFluoride mouthwashes are commonly used for prevention of dental caries. The most common forms of fluoride are stannous fluoride (SnF2), amine fluoride (AmF) and sodium fluoride (NaF), each with differing activities against oral bacteria. Since the microbiome has been linked to interindividual differences in responses to treatment, the phenotype of the microbiome may provide information on potential treatment responses. Flow cytometry successfully tracks microbial phenotypic heterogeneity and may prove useful for capturing interindividual treatment responses.MethodsWe compared the effect of fluoride-containing mouthwashes, Elmex Anti Caries (AmF/NaF), Listerine Anti Caries (NaF) and Meridol (AmF/SnF2) on the salivary microbiome in vitro. We determined the effect on the microbial community structure using 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, assessed the acidogenic potential by measuring organic acids after incubation, and constructed flow cytometric fingerprints of the microbial community to evaluate if it could be used to capture the response to treatment and to assess if it has potential for precision medicine.ResultsWe observed mouthwash- and donor-dependent changes in the salivary microbiome composition and acidogenic potential. Meridol affected more bacterial taxa and showed the strongest reduction in organic acids, followed by Elmex. The response to treatment could be captured in the flow cytometric fingerprint of the initial intact salivary microbiota and a random forest classifier predicting the optimal mouthwash for each donor was successfully trained (MCC = 0.95).ConclusionsTreatment with fluoride mouthwashes induces a shift in the salivary microbiome in vitro and alter its potential to produce organic acids, contributing to the prevention of dental caries. Moreover, flow cytometry shows promise for tailoring treatments to patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05207-0.

Background

The highly diverse microbial community in the oral cavity is structured in polymicrobial biofilms on the teeth and tongue, and has a wide range of metabolic capabilities [1]. These metabolic capabilities are influenced by the community composition, availability of oxygen, and availability of nutrients supplied from saliva, the crevicular fluid and nutrition [2]. Because of the short transit time of food in the mouth, oral bacteria use easily fermentable carbohydrates as their primary carbon source [3, 4]. When there is overexposure to dietary carbohydrates, in combination with host factors, production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and organic acids from carbohydrate metabolism is promoted, and accumulation of acidogenic and aciduric microorganisms occurs. The increased concentration of organic acids in the tooth-associated biofilm can lower the pH below 5.5, which leads to tooth demineralization and eventually to tooth decay [4–9]. This pathology, also known as dental caries, is estimated to affect 2.5 billion people globally [10]. In a symbiotic state at near neutral pH, the oral microbial communities are dominated by Actinomyces, Bifidobacteria, Fusobacteria, Neisseria, Porphyromonas, non-mutans streptococci and Veillonella [6, 11, 12]. When caries develops and the acidogenic and aciduric species are promoted, the dominance of Veillonella, Neisseria, Streptococcus, Propionibacterium, Prevotella, and Lactobacillus increases [6, 11, 13–15].

Fluoride mouthwashes are commonly used to prevent dental caries [16–20]. Topical administration of fluoride provides its anticariogenic effects by inhibiting demineralization, promoting remineralization and inhibiting plaque bacteria [21]. Inhibition of demineralization is accomplished through adsorption of fluoride into the surface enamel crystal structure, forming fluorapatite, which is less prone to acid dissolution than normal tooth minerals [21, 22]. Remineralization is promoted by acceleration of fluorapatite crystal growth in carious lesions. When fluoride adsorbs into the surface enamel, it attracts calcium ions present in saliva, which in turn attract more fluoride [21]. The inhibition of plaque bacteria is achieved by cellular uptake of fluoride ions under acidic conditions.

This results in inhibition of growth, metabolic activity and enzymatic activity, thereby hindering the bacteria’s ability to form plaque [23–26]. The use of fluoride compounds in oral care products has been reported to induce a shift in microbial community composition, leading to a proportional decrease of disease-associated bacteria such as members of the Streptococcus and Prevotella genera.

Thus, these compounds put a selective pressure on the oral microbiota in favor of the health-associated taxa [27–33]. Different fluoride-containing ingredients are used in mouthwashes of which the most common forms are stannous fluoride (SnF2), amine fluoride (AmF) and sodium fluoride (NaF). Multiple reports indicate that SnF2 and AmF are more effective in reducing erosive enamel loss and have more favorable effects on other clinical health parameters such as the approximal plaque index compared to NaF [34–37].

Additionally, higher reduction of opportunistic pathogenic bacteria and better inhibition of oral microbial biofilm formation have been reported for SnF2 and AmF compared to NaF [32, 35, 38, 39].

These findings suggest that the antimicrobial effect of the oral care products is mostly attributable to the fluoride counter ions and additional ingredients in the products instead of the fluoride ions themselves [40]. Indeed, the in vivo importance of the antimicrobial activity of fluoride in caries inhibition is questioned [24, 40].

Individual differences in oral microbiome composition are significant, and previous research has demonstrated that treatment responses are associated with these interindividual variations in microbiome composition [41–43]. As such, the oral microbiome serves as a valuable source of information for predicting a patient’s response to treatment. Typically, the community composition derived from next-generation sequencing (NGS) is used as a “microbial signature” for patients [44, 45].

However, this genotypic fingerprint only identifies the microorganisms present and does not provide insights into the functional phenotype of the community. Because the phenotype of the microbial community can also influence treatment responsiveness, it is crucial to include phenotypic data when evaluating treatment outcomes [46, 47]. Flow cytometry has proven particularly relevant when assessing phenotypic heterogeneity in microbial populations and has already been used to track the salivary microbiome, making it a fitting tool to study the phenotype of oral microbial communities [48–53].

Interestingly, no studies have directly compared the effects of different fluoride-containing ingredients on the oral microbiome. To address this gap, we investigate the impact of various fluoride-containing mouthwashes on the salivary microbiome. Given the selective pressures fluoride exerts on oral bacteria, we hypothesize that these treatments will induce shifts in microbiome composition.

Furthermore, we anticipate that both the changes in microbiome composition and the anticariogenic effects of the mouthwashes will depend on the specific fluoride formulation. Additionally, we explore whether the anticariogenic effects and microbial responses to treatment are donor-dependent. To this end, we examined whether phenotypic and genotypic fingerprints of the salivary microbiome could capture treatment responses. Saliva samples from different donors were treated with three distinct fluoride-containing mouthwashes. Following treatment, we analyzed the composition of the intact microbial cell population using 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and constructed phenotypic fingerprints using flow cytometry. Finally, we assessed the organic acid production of the treated microbial populations by incubating the samples in minimal medium with sucrose as the carbon source.

Saliva sampling and clinical evaluation of oral health status

A volume of 5 mL of saliva was collected by passive drooling from 13 individuals that did not undergo any antibiotic treatment three months prior to donation (Supplementary 1). Three individuals indicated that they use mouthwashes on a regular basis (donor 2, 6, and 13). Donors were asked to refrain from brushing their teeth, eating and drinking for two hours before donation. Moreover, donors were instructed not to use mouthwash on the day of donation. After donation, a dentist performed a clinical evaluation of their oral cavity. Teeth were examined for decay, restorations, crowns, fixed orthodontic retainers, bridges, extracted teeth and implants. Probing pocket depth (PPD) of the groove between gums and teeth was measured, as well as bleeding on probing (BOP) of the gums, gum recession, and clinical attachment loss (CAL). Additionally, donors were asked to chew on plaque-disclosing tablets for 30 s and rinse with water afterwards to evaluate plaque level (Supplementary 1). Prior to donation and clinical evaluation, donors signed an informed consent form. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Ghent (B6702022000406).

Treatment and incubation of saliva

Before treatment, 500 µL of saliva was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube and samples were centrifuged at 20,238 x g for 10 min. Supernatant was discarded and the remaining pellet was stored at -20 °C up until DNA extraction. The remaining saliva was used for treatment with three different mouthwashes for 1 min in triplicate: Listerine Anti Caries (NaF, 220 ppm F−) (Johnson & Johnson, Maidenhead, United Kingdom), Elmex Anti Caries (AmF and NaF, 250 ppm F−) (Colgate-Palmolive, Świdnica, Poland) and Meridol (AmF and SnF2, 250 ppm F−) (Colgate-Palmolive, Świdnica, Poland). A list of the ingredients of the mouthwashes is provided in Supplementary 2. For donors 1–5 a mouthwash concentration of 9% v/v was used. For donor 6–13 a concentration of 7% v/v was used so that a larger proportion of the microbial community would survive the treatment (Supplementary 10; Figure S9). Control samples were included in triplicate by treating saliva with sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (PBS tablet, Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) in the same concentration as the mouthwashes. Treatment was stopped by diluting 100-fold in unbuffered minimal medium before putting the samples on ice. The unbuffered minimal medium (Table 1) was prepared by adding 20 mL/L vitamin mix and 20 mL/L sucrose-vitamin C mix to the base medium (pH7) [11].

After treatment, the pH of the samples was determined (Supplementary 3) and samples were taken for ion chromatography (IC), flow cytometry and FACS. Additionally, 3 mL of each replicate was transferred to a sterile 12-well plate for each donor. 3 mL of unbuffered minimal medium was added to a 12-well plate as an abiotic control. Consecutively, 12-well plates were incubated under microaerophilic conditions (5–10% O2) at 37 °C for 19 h. A preliminary test assessing growth dynamics of saliva in minimal medium supplemented with glucose was performed on a limited number of donors (n = 2) to confirm that the microbial community reached stationary phase before the end of the incubation period (Supplementary 4). After incubation, the pH was determined (Supplementary 3), and samples were taken for ion chromatography and flow cytometry. Furthermore, 500 µL of sample was centrifuged at 20,238 x g for 10 min; supernatant was discarded, and the remaining pellet was stored at -20 °C up until DNA extraction.

Flow cytometry and FACS

Sample preparation

After treatment, an aliquot of each sample was diluted 10-fold in sterile PBS (PBS tablet, Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany). Next, samples were stained with SYBR® Green I/propidium iodide (SGPI) to assess membrane integrity [54]. A stock solution of SGPI was formulated by diluting SYBR® Green I (10 000x concentrate in DMSO; Invitrogen, Eugene, USA) 100-fold and propidium iodide (20mM; Invitrogen, Eugene, USA) 50-fold in 0.2 μm filtered DMSO (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The SGPI stock solution was used to stain the samples (1% v/v SGPI stock solution), after which they were incubated for 20 min in the dark at 37 °C [55].

Sample measurement

An Attune NxT (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) flow cytometer fitted with a blue (488 nm) and red (638 nm) laser was used to measure the samples. Performance of the machine was checked by employing Attune Performance tracking beads (Invitrogen, Eugene, USA). Excitation of the stains was achieved using the blue laser. Green fluorescence (BL1) was detected using a 530/30 nm band-pass filter, red fluorescence (BL3) using a 695/40 nm band-pass filter, and forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) using a 488/10 band-pass filter. A volume of 100 µL of sample was analyzed using a flow rate of 100 µL/min. The PMT-voltages and threshold (determined on green fluorescence, BL1) were established using appropriate controls: an untreated sample, a heat-killed sample (microwaved at 700 W for 1 min), a filter-sterilized sample, sterile unbuffered minimal medium and sterile PBS.

FACS

The intact cell population was sorted using a FACS Melody (BD, Erembodegem, Belgium) cell sorter equipped with a blue (488 nm) and yellow-green (561 nm) laser and a 100 μm sort nozzle. The performance was checked using CS&T RUO Beads (BD, Erembodegem, Belgium) and drop delay was checked using FACS Accudrop RUO Beads (BD, Erembodegem, Belgium). Excitation of the stains was accomplished using the blue laser. Green fluorescence (PMT1) was detected using a 527/32 nm band-pass filter, red fluorescence (PMT2) using a 700/54 nm band-pass filter, and side scatter (SSC) using a 488/15 band-pass filter. The threshold (set on green fluorescence, PMT1) and PMT-voltages were determined using the same control samples as with flow cytometry. The intact cell population was gated based on the primary fluorescent channels (PMT1 and PMT2) (Supplementary 5; Figure S3). The number of events to sort was set to 250 000 (Supplementary 5; Table S4). After sorting, samples were stored at -20 °C up until DNA extraction.

Determination of organic acids

Ion chromatography was used for the determination of organic acids (C1-C5). Samples were filtered (0.2 μm) and diluted 10-fold in dH2O and stored at -20 °C up until actual chromatographic analysis. After thawing at room temperature until no more ice was visible, samples were analyzed according to Roume et al. using a 930 Compact IC Flex (Metrohm AG, Herisau, Switzerland) system with in-line bicarbonate removal (MCS) [56].

DNA extraction

Sorted samples were thawed at room temperature until no more ice was visible. DNA extractions were performed using the DNAeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands). 500 µL of sorted sample was transferred to PowerBead Pro Tubes containing 250 µL of Solution CD1, while cell pellets (raw saliva and post-incubation samples) were mixed with 800 µL of Solution CD1 and transferred to PowerBead Pro Tubes. Samples were disrupted in a PowerLyzer (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands) for 5 consecutive times at 4000 rpm for 15 s with 45-second cooldown intervals. All subsequent steps were performed following the standard manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were then stored at -20 °C.

Amplicon sequencing

For each sample, 15 µL DNA extract (Supplementary 6) was shipped to LGC genomics GmbH (Berlin, Germany) for library preparation and sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform with v3 chemistry using primers 341F (5’ – CCT ACG GGN GGC WGC AG – 3’) and 785Rmod (5’ – GAC TAC HVG GGT ATC TAA KCC – 3’) to target the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16 S rRNA gene [57].

Data analysis

Analysis of flow cytometry data

Flow cytometry data were analyzed in R (version 4.2.0) [58]. Data were imported by means of the flowCore package (version 2.8.0) [59] and arcsine hyperbolic transformed [48]. Next, gating to remove background and to distinguish between intact and damaged cells was performed using the primary fluorescent channels (BL1 and BL3) (Supplementary 7). Total, intact and damaged cell concentrations were calculated based on the events in the cell gates and the volumetric measurements of the instrument.

Phenotypic fingerprints (i.e. flow cytometric fingerprints) were generated as previously described by Mermans et al. [60]. In short, a Gaussian mixture mask was applied to the data to detect clusters within the data using the ‘PhenoGMM’ function of the Phenoflow package (version 1.1.2) [61]. To generate the mask for each timepoint (before incubation and after incubation), samples were subsampled to an equal number of cells so that every mouthwash treatment and every donor would be represented equally. This way, biased model training towards a specific donor or mouthwash treatment was avoided. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was used for optimization of the GMM [62, 63]. The resulting GMM leads to a one-dimensional (1D) vector representing the number of cells allotted to each cluster in the model for each sample and is considered the flow cytometric fingerprint (Supplementary 8). The FSC-H, FSC-A, SSC-H, SSC-A, BL1-H, BL1-A, BL3-H, and BL3-A were used for model building because these are anticipated to provide most information [64]. Model output was transformed to relative abundances prior to subsequent analysis. Consecutively, transformed model output was utilized to perform Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (vegan package version 2.6-2) [65]. Statistical differences between mouthwash treatments were assessed by distribution-independent analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) (vegan package version 2.6-2) [65]. Benjamini and Hochberg adjusted p-values were generated for pairwise comparisons (stats package version 4.5.0) [58].

A random forest classifier to predict the most effective mouthwash (i.e. recommended mouthwash) for each donor was trained on the PhemoGMM output of the intact microbial population of the control (PBS) before incubation employing the caret package (version 6.0–92) [66]. The Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) was used for classifier optimization to account for class imbalance and implementation was done using the mltools package (version 0.3.5) [67]. The model was trained using a three-times repeated five-fold cross-validation scheme, with the number of threes in the model set to 500. In this cross-validation scheme, the dataset was randomly divided into five subsets, of which four were used for training and one for validation in each iteration; this process was repeated so that each subset served once as validation, and the entire procedure was performed three times using different random partitions to obtain more robust performance estimates.

Analysis of organic acids

Organic acids were analyzed on the level of the molar concentration, and on the level of the cell concentration-normalized molar concentration. The normalized concentrations were calculated by dividing the molar concentration by the total cell concentration obtained through flow cytometry.

Statistical differences between treatment groups were assessed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Benjamini and Hochberg correction for multiple testing in the case of the molar concentration and using mixed-effects models for the normalized concentrations. The mixed-effects models were implemented using the lmerTest package (version 3.1-3) and pairwise comparisons with Benjamini and Hochberg correction were made using the emmeans package (version 1.9.0) [68, 69].

Response to treatment was defined by assessing the relative reduction of organic acid concentration compared to the control (PBS). Strong responders were defined by a reduction larger than 60%, intermediate responders by a reduction between 20 and 60% and weak responders by a reduction lower than 20%. Additionally, the mouthwash that resulted in the highest reduction of organic acids was defined as the recommended mouthwash for the donor.

Processing of amplicon reads

Amplicon sequences were analyzed using the DADA2 package (version 1.30.0) in R as described by Callahan et al. [70]. After filtering, the average number of reads per sample was 30,144 (Supplementary 6). ASVs were classified using the SILVA v138 training set [71]. The resulting read count table with taxonomic annotation was further processed in R (version 4.2.0). Rarefaction curves were used to assess if sufficient sequencing depth was obtained (vegan package version 2.6-2) [65] (Supplementary 9). Samples with a read count lower than 1300 were removed from further analysis, based on the read count values obtained for the negative controls. Quantitative microbial community composition was determined by multiplying the proportional read count data with microbial cell concentrations (flowCore package version 2.8.0) [59, 72].

Alpha diversity was calculated using the iNEXT package (version 3.0.0) [73]. PCoA based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was performed on both the proportional and quantitative community composition using the vegan package (version 2.6-2) [65] and statistical differences between groups were determined through permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing or through distribution-independent analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing (vegan package version 2.6-2) [65]. Distance-based Redundancy Analysis (db-RDA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was achieved using the vegan package (version 2.6-2) [65]. Results were visualized in a type 2 scaling correlation triplot and statistical significance of treatment, donor and timepoint (i.e. before and after incubation) were tested using permutation-based ANOVA for each term in the model. Due to multicollinearity between treatment and timepoint, a new model was built combining the two factors as one (further referred to as ‘Treatment’).

Differential abundance analysis on proportional data was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.42.0) [74]. For the quantitative community data mixed-effects models were used to account for repeated-measures for some donors. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was used for correcting for multiple testing. The mixed-effects models were implemented using the lmerTest package (version 3.1-3) [68].

Integrative analysis of the organic acid concentrations and the quantitative community composition was achieved by sparse Partial Least Squares (sPLS) analysis using the mixOmics package (6.26.0) [75].

First, a basic model was created in regression mode where the organic acid concentration was attempted to be explained by the quantitative community structure on the genus level. This was followed by model tuning, where the optimal number of variables and components were extracted in regression mode with correlation evaluation metrics (comp1 = 5, comp2 = 40 and comp3 = 25 for the quantitative community structure; comp1 = 1, comp2 = 1 and comp3 = 4 for the organic acid concentration; n = 3 for the number of components). These parameters were used to construct a final sPLS model, which was then used to construct relevance network graphs to visualize structure associations.

Results

Changes in salivary microbiome composition after mouthwash treatment

To assess the impact of fluoride-containing mouthwashes on the salivary microbiome composition, saliva samples of 13 different donors were treated with three distinct mouthwashes, namely Listerine Anti Caries, Elmex Anti Caries and Meridol. Samples obtained directly after mouthwash treatment are missing for donor 3 due to failed DNA extractions for this donor. Analysis of cell concentrations revealed that Elmex and Meridol led to a higher reduction in intact cell concentration compared to Listerine and that the magnitude of the effect was donor dependent (Supplementary 10).

The intact microbial population after treatment was studied using 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Differences in community composition were observed between treatments in both the proportional and quantitative community composition (Fig. 2, Supplementary 11). No significant differences in alpha diversity could be observed between the treatments (Supplementary 12). There was a reduction in species richness (H0), Shannon diversity (H1) and inverse Simpson diversity (H2) for some donors (Donor 1, Donor 2, Donor 4, Donor 8, Donor 11 and Donor 12) after treatment with mouthwashes, however, this was not observed for all donors (Supplementary 12). PCoA based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity showed grouping according to treatment for the proportional and quantitative community data (Fig. 3). Statistical analysis indicated significant differences in both proportional and quantitative community composition between PBS and Elmex (proportional (ANOSIM): R = 0.58, P < 0.001; quantitative (PERMANOVA): F = 4.95, P < 0.001), PBS and Meridol (proportional (ANOSIM): R = 0.50, P < 0.001; quantitative (PERMANOVA): F = 5.20, P < 0.001), Listerine and Elmex (proportional (ANOSIM): R = 0.51, P < 0.001; quantitative (PERMANOVA): F = 3.76, P < 0.001), and Listerine and Meridol (proportional (ANOSIM): R = 0.45, P < 0.001; quantitative (PERMANOVA): F = 3.95, P < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed in the flow cytometric fingerprint of the intact cell population after mouthwash treatment (Supplementary 13). Additionally, db-RDA revealed that all model factors (i.e. ‘Treatment’ and ‘Donor’) contributed significantly to the model for the proportional and quantitative community composition (Supplementary 14). Specifically, for the proportional microbiome composition, ‘Treatment’ contributed for 36.7% of the variation, while ‘Donor’ contributed for 31.9%. For the quantitative microbiome composition this was 37.2% for ‘Treatment’ and 23.4% for ‘Donor’.

To determine how bacterial taxa were affected by the different mouthwashes, differentially abundant genera were identified for the proportional (Fig. 4) and quantitative (Supplementary 15) community composition. Overall, Elmex and Meridol significantly reduced the abundance of more different bacterial genera compared to Listerine. For Elmex, ten genera were significantly reduced in the proportional community data, with an additional seven genera reduced in the quantitative data, totaling 17 genera. For Meridol, 19 genera were significantly reduced in the proportional community composition, of which 17 were also significantly reduced in the quantitative composition. In contrast, Listerine reduced only five genera in the proportional data, accompanied by nine additional genera in the quantitative data. Notably, no genera were uniquely reduced by Listerine or Elmex across both datasets, while Meridol uniquely reduced eight genera (Megasphaera, Actinomyces, Pseudomonas, Pseudopropionibacterium, Prevotella, Alcaligenes, Sphingomonas and Atopobium) in the proportional data, though no unique reductions were observed in the quantitative data. Six genera (Streptococcus, Prevotella 7, Porphyromonas, TM7x, Butyrivibrio and Oribacterium) in the proportional data and three genera (Megasphaera, Oribacterium and TM7x) in the quantitative data were reduced by both Elmex and Meridol, but not by Listerine. Additionally, Leptotrichia was reduced in the proportional community composition by both Listerine and Meridol, but not by Elmex. In terms of significantly increased genera, there were six genera in the proportional community composition and three genera in the quantitative community composition that were increased for Elmex, and one genus in the quantitative community composition that was increased for Meridol. This last genus (Campylobacter) was shared with Elmex.

Anticariogenic potential of the mouthwashes

To determine the anticariogenic potential of the mouthwashes, treated saliva was incubated in minimal medium with sucrose as carbon source to assess the production of organic acids by the remaining microbial population. Furthermore, the community composition was studied to link specific bacterial genera to the production of organic acids.

Elmex and Meridol led to a significant decrease in total organic acid concentration (Fig. 5A) and pH (Fig. 6) compared to the control, but not for Listerine. Lactate was the dominant organic acid for all treatments. When the organic acid concentration normalized on the total cell concentration was considered, only a significant decrease for Meridol was found compared to the control (Fig. 5C). For the individual acids, Elmex and Meridol led to a reduction of lactate in the molar and normalized concentration compared to the control (Fig. 5B and D). In the normalized concentrations, there was also a reduction of propionate for Elmex and Meridol and an increase in acetate for Elmex (Fig. 5D). Moreover, large differences were observed between donors in both the molar and normalized acid concentrations (Supplementary 16).

Response to treatment by assessment of the genotypic and phenotypic fingerprint

Since high interindividual variability was observed in the anticariogenic potential of the different mouthwashes (Supplementary 16), we assessed if the response to treatment of a donor could be captured in the phenotypic (i.e. flow cytometric) and genotypic (i.e. proportional and quantitative community composition) fingerprint of the initial intact salivary microbiota. Therefore, the donors were labelled based on which mouthwash resulted in the highest reduction of molar concentration of organic acids (i.e. the recommended mouthwash). All donors were assigned to either Elmex or Meridol, as treatment with Listerine never resulted in the highest reduction in organic acids. PCoA based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities revealed that only separate groups could be distinguished in the phenotypic fingerprint and not in the genotypic fingerprints of the microbial community (Fig. 8). This was confirmed by statistical analysis where no differences between recommended mouthwash groups were found for the genotypic fingerprints, while significant differences were found in the phenotypic fingerprints (ANOSIM: R = 0.20, P = 0.01).

Discussion

Different formulations of fluoride to prevent dental caries are reported to have differing efficacy against oral bacteria [16, 19, 32, 35, 38]. Additionally, large interindividual differences in oral microbiome composition exist and differing responses to treatment are to be expected [41–43].

Because there is no existing literature that compares changes in oral microbiome composition between the different formulations of fluoride, we studied changes in salivary microbiome composition after in vitro treatment with different fluoride-containing mouthwashes. Moreover, we investigated the acidogenic potential of the microbial community after mouthwash treatment by assessing organic acids produced by the remaining microbial community and evaluated interindividual differences in response to treatment between saliva donors. Our results showed a mouthwash-dependent shift in salivary microbiome composition. Furthermore, we found that the acidogenic potential was both mouthwash-dependent and donor-dependent. Finally, we could capture the response to treatment in the flow cytometric fingerprint of the initial intact cell population of the donor and successfully trained a random forest classifier predicting the most effective mouthwash.

Change in salivary microbiome composition is mouthwash-dependent

We found that the mouthwashes induced a taxonomic shift in the intact salivary microbiota (Fig. 3) and found differences in differentially abundant genera for the mouthwashes (Fig. 4, Supplementary 15). The effect of Elmex and Meridol was comparable, however, treatment with Listerine resulted in a community that was more like the control (PBS). Meridol and Elmex effectively reduced Oribacterium and TM7x in both the proportional and quantitative composition, Megasphaera in the quantitative composition, and Streptococcus, Prevotella 7, Porphyromonas and Butyrivibrio in the proportional composition, whereas Listerine did not. All these genera have been implicated in dental caries, except for Porphyromonas of which certain members, such as P. gingivalis, play an pivotal role in periodontal disease [6, 11, 13, 14, 76–79]. Importantly, it should be noted that the 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing employed in this study provides taxonomic resolution only at the genus level. Finer resolution (e.g., species-level identification) would be required to robustly demonstrate a selective reduction of cariogenic species. Additionally, Elmex and Meridol damaged a larger proportion of the salivary microbiota (Supplementary 10). Thus, these mouthwashes are more effective in reducing oral bacteria, and by extension opportunistic oral pathogens. This could be explained by the different (active) ingredients in the mouthwashes. Listerine only contains NaF as an active fluoride ingredient, while Elmex contains both NaF and AmF, and Meridol contains AmF and SnF2. Simultaneously, Listerine contains essential oils with demonstrated antimicrobial properties, and other ingredients with antimicrobial effects (e.g. sodium benzoate) are present in the different mouthwashes as well [80, 81]. Previous research showed that NaF was less effective in reducing opportunistic pathogenic oral bacteria compared to AmF and SnF2, illustrating the importance of the antimicrobial properties of the fluoride counter ions, rather than the antimicrobial effect of fluoride [32, 35, 38]. Furthermore, the antimicrobial effect of fluoride is expected to be limited, as the final concentration in our experiment is low and the importance of the antimicrobial properties of fluoride in caries prevention is questioned [24, 40]. In our data, the added effect of SnF2 in the mouthwash seemed to have a limited effect when AmF was already present.

Only in the proportional microbiome composition more taxa for Meridol than for Elmex were found to be reduced (Fig. 4). In addition, the community composition between Elmex and Meridol was similar (Fig. 3). Literature reports that the antimicrobial effects of SnF2 are comparable with AmF [39]. Simultaneously, Sn2+ has been suggested to exhibit selective binding towards specific pathogenic species [31]. Since the total concentration of fluorides in the two mouthwashes (Elmex and Meridol) was the same and because AmF was shared between the two mouthwashes, combined with the presence of other antimicrobial ingredients in the different mouthwashes, we argue that the overall antimicrobial effect of the mouthwash is the most important driver for the change in community structure. Thus, also the other (active) ingredients in the mouthwashes should be considered. Comparing pure NaF, AmF and SnF2 to each other may shed more light on the specificity of the fluoride compounds towards certain bacterial strains and will avoid confounding effects of the other components of the mouthwashes.

Remarkably, there were genera that were increased after mouthwash treatment. Since the mouthwashes contained antimicrobial compounds and no incubation was performed before analysis, these increased genera should be considered biased. There were more increased genera in the proportional community data, which illustrates the possible bias that can be introduced when resorting to differential abundance analysis methods for estimating differentially abundant taxa based on proportional data [82]. Additionally, the use of DESeq2 for differential abundance analysis on 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing data has been shown to be suboptimal [83]. All increased genera in both the proportional and quantitative microbiome composition are all normal constituents of the oral microbiota and therefore should not be seen as being increased due to contamination. However, bias introduced in the methodology could be the cause of this phenomenon. For example, it could be that staining efficiencies of these specific taxa were different under the different chemical conditions induced by the different mouthwashes when using FACS for retaining the intact cell population for further analysis [84].

Acidogenic potential is mouthwash-dependent

By assessing organic acids after incubation in sucrose-rich medium, greater anti-acidogenic effect was found for Elmex and Meridol compared to Listerine. Therefore, their anticariogenic potential was deemed greater as well. More specifically, Elmex and Meridol led to a reduction of total molar organic acid concentration compared to the control, while Listerine did not. For Meridol, a significant reduction was found when the organic acid concentration was normalized on the total cell concentration in the sample, while the observed reduction for Elmex was not significant. The better anticariogenic potential of Elmex and Meridol was supported by the observed pH-drop over the incubation, where Elmex and Meridol had a significantly smaller pH-drop compared to both the control and Listerine. We argue that differences between the mouthwashes are the result of a different microbial community composition at the start of the incubation between the mouthwashes.

We showed that the mouthwashes exerted a different effect on the intact microbiome composition, thus different species were present for regrowth after mouthwash treatment. Given Meridol was the only mouthwash that resulted in a significant reduction in the total normalized acid concentration, it led to the most favorable overall microbiological shift for treatment of dental caries. Moreover, the molar concentration and normalized concentration of lactate were significantly reduced for both Elmex and Meridol, but not for Listerine.

Since lactate was the most abundant organic acid produced in our experiment, we argue that the difference in total acid concentration is mostly driven by the difference in concentration of lactate.

Network analysis revealed a positive correlation (0.61) between Streptococcus and lactate (Fig. 7). This finding is consistent with literature, where lactate has been reported to be the predominant end metabolite of oral Streptococci at low pH under anaerobic conditions [85, 86]. Differential abundance analysis after incubation showed that Streptococcus was reduced for Elmex and Meridol in the proportional community composition and not for Listerine (Supplementary 18). Thus, the reduction in lactate for Elmex and Meridol is likely the result of the reduction in Streptococcus. Propionate was found to be reduced for Elmex and Meridol in the normalized acid concentrations (Fig. 5). The relevance network showed that propionate was positively correlated with Prevotella 7 (0.67), Veillonella (0.77) and Prevotella (0.51) (Fig. 7).

Indeed, members of Prevotella and Veillonella are known to produce propionate [87–89]. Differential abundance analysis after incubation showed that Prevotella 7 was reduced for Elmex and Meridol, although this was not the case for Veillonella and Prevotella (Supplementary 18). However, the relative abundance of Prevotella 7 in the community was greater than that of Prevotella and Veillonella (Supplementary 17). Thus, the reduction in propionate may be driven by the reduction in Prevotella 7 for Elmex and Meridol. Last, we found that acetate was increased for Elmex in the normalized acid concentrations and that acetate was positively correlated with Prevotella 7 (0.57) and Veillonella (0.65) (Figs. 5 and 7). Members of these genera are known to produce acetate [87, 88]. Nevertheless, these genera were not found to be significantly more prevalent for Elmex, and Prevotella 7 was even found to be reduced in the proportional community data (Supplementary 18).

It is important to note that metabolic interactions in the oral cavity are common and that organic acids can be used as carbon sources by different oral bacteria [90]. Thus, the differential abundance of single species may not be enough to fully explain differences in specific organic acids.

Finally, the study design faces some limitations when assessing the acidogenic potential of the oral microbiota. First, saliva was used as inoculum, rather than supragingival plaque. Supragingival plaque represents the tooth-associated microbial community better and would therefore have potentially resulted in an in vitro microbial community that is more appropriate for studying anticariogenic effects of the mouthwashes. However, saliva is considered as a reservoir that collects shed microorganisms from various oral niches and is seen as a representative for the overall oral microbiome [91]. Saliva was selected as the sampling matrix due to its non-invasive, reproducible, and easily collectible characteristics. Second, liquid cultures were used, rather than biofilm communities. Future research should consider using hydroxyapatite disks as carriers for biofilm formation to mimic the in vivo situation more closely. Third, the use of minimal medium with sucrose as sole carbon source steers the microbial community in a specific direction and does not represent common dietary behavior very accurately. Nevertheless, the purpose of the study was to simulate an extreme condition that would maximize the production of organic acids so that differences between mouthwashes could be observed clearly. Last, host-factors modulating the development of the oral microbial community were not considered.

The response to treatment can be captured in the flow cytometric fingerprint

Based on the high interindividual differences between donors regarding response to treatment, a recommended mouthwash for each saliva donor was defined by choosing the mouthwash that led to the highest reduction in molar organic acid concentration compared to the control. This resulted in either Elmex or Meridol as the recommended mouthwash for our study cohort. We chose the molar organic acid concentration for our recommendation because it is more relevant than the normalized organic acid concentration in a clinical context. It is the absolute concentration of acids that drives tooth demineralization [6, 92]. Groups between donors could be distinguished depending on the recommended mouthwash (i.e. response to treatment) in the phenotypic fingerprint but not in the genotypic fingerprints of the initial intact microbial community (Fig. 8). Hence, the phenotypic fingerprint provided information on the response to treatment that could not be captured in the genotypic fingerprints. Previous research showed that the phenotype of genetically identical microbial populations can play an important role in response to environmental changes [93, 94].

Therefore, we argue that the phenotype of the salivary microbiome also influences the response to mouthwash treatment. Additionally, as the shifts in community composition of Elmex and Meridol were similar for our data, it may be that higher taxonomic resolution would lead to better clustering of the recommended mouthwash groups in the genotypic fingerprints.

We successfully trained a random forest classifier predicting the recommended mouthwash for each donor based on the flow cytometric fingerprint (Fig. 9). These results suggest that flow cytometry may have potential for predictive modelling in tailoring treatments to patients. To increase accuracy in determining the most suitable treatment for patients, a combination of an in vitro assay, a genotypic fingerprint and a flow cytometric fingerprint could be considered as well. However, it should be noted that we determined the anticariogenic potential of the mouthwashes by an in vitro incubation. To confirm these findings, in vivo tests should be performed as well. Furthermore, the number of participants in this study is relatively small, limiting the generalizability of the predictive approach. If the workflow is to be applied in clinical practice, a more extensive study population must be considered to confirm general applicability of the method. Finally, our results hint the use of the flow cytometric fingerprint in personalized preventive care. When combined with risk assessment models, the fingerprint could help predict the risk of specific oral pathologies and support the use of tailored preventive strategies.

Conclusion

We found that a shift in the salivary microbiome composition occurred after in vitro treatment with fluoride-containing mouthwashes and that this shift was mouthwash-dependent. The shift was least outspoken for Listerine (NaF), while the more prominent effects of Elmex (AmF and NaF) and Meridol (AmF and SnF2) were similar. Meridol seemed to affect more bacterial taxa and had the best anticariogenic potential on the level of the study population. On the level of the individual saliva donor, either Meridol or Elmex showed the best anticariogenic potential. Last, we provided proof of concept that the flow cytometric fingerprint of the initial intact salivary microbiome has the potential to be used as a marker for identifying the most appropriate mouthwash treatment for an individual.
