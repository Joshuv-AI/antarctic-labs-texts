Achieving torsional stability of distal femur fractures through a single approach using 90–90 construct
Authors: Sanjay R Agarwala, Mayank Vijayvargiya, Avinashdev Devmani Upadhyay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryDistal femoral fractures (DFFs) are frequently characterised by significant comminution, osteoporosis, high implant failure, insufficient fixation because of a shortened distal part, non-union, malunion, and poor outcome. Our report presents two cases treated with dual plating in a 90–90 construct to achieve torsional stability. DFF fixation by dual plating in a 90–90 configuration proved to be a superior fixation similar to 90–90 plating for distal humerus fracture, since 90–90 plate fixation had significantly greater torque to failure load and has more resistance to torsional loading. Two cases of distal femur non-union with implant failure were treated utilising a double plating. These non-union cases exhibited radiological healing and knee range of motion at a mean follow-up of 6 months. This technique offers a modified way of treating these difficult non-union cases through a single incision, providing mechanical stability conducive to fracture healing.

Background

Distal femoral fractures (DFFs) are frequently characterised by significant comminution, osteoporosis, insufficient fixation because of a shortened distal part, high implant failure, non-union, malunion, and poor outcome.14 Thus, the effective management of distal femoral fractures continues to pose a significant challenge despite notable advancements in methods and implants.

The ideal implant of choice for distal femur fracture with severe osteoporosis, severe comminution, medial wall in-continuity, non-union, peri-prosthetic fractures, and implant failure has long been debated. The use of intramedullary nails, dual plates, and nail plus plates has been tried to salvage such scenarios.

The utilisation of intramedullary nails (IMN) is correlated with a reduction in infection and wound problems compared with the plating. However, IMN will not result in anatomic reduction and has been shown to be less rigid.5 Dual plating in the form of lateral and medial plates is the gold standard, but it poses challenges such as using two separate surgical incisions, lack of specific medial side implants, and complications associated with using long medial plates6 7

Our report presents two cases treated with dual plating in a 90–90 construct (lateral and anterior plate) to achieve torsional stability. DFF fixation by dual plating in a 90–90 configuration proved to be a superior fixation similar to 90–90 plating for distal humerus fracture, since 90–90 plate fixation has significantly greater torque to failure load and 90–90 plating has greater resistance to torsional loading. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report about using 90–90 plating to treat complex distal femur fracture scenarios.

Case presentation

Two cases of distal femur non-union with implant failure were treated utilising a double plating. The modified swashbuckler approach was used, followed by implant removal, fracture reduction, and lateral plate fixation. A locking compression plate was applied on the lateral aspect. Additionally, the anterior locking plate was used to give torsional stability (stability in two planes).

Case 1

A woman in her 50s presented to us in the outpatient department with a right distal femur fracture non-union with implant failure. Index surgery was done 1 year ago. CT scan was done to assess fracture healing (non-union) and bone loss pattern. Infection was ruled out through clinical examination and blood workup. We treated the patient surgically by implant removal, fracture reduction, and lateral plate fixation with a locking compression plate. Additionally, anterior locking plate fixation was done. A cancellous bone graft from the proximal tibia was harvested and applied at the fracture site along with Chronos granules (bone graft substitute) (Synthes Corp) (figure 1). Postoperatively the patient was advised to follow non-weight bearing mobilisation until signs of callus formation were seen on the radiograph at 2 months follow-up. Protected weight bearing started after that. Union time for radiological healing was 4 months. At 6 months the patient was walking using full weight bearing with a flexion knee range of motion of 0–120° (figure 2). There was no evidence of infection, non-union or malunion at the end of 1 year follow-up.

Case 2

A woman in her 70s presented to us in the outpatient department with a left distal femur fracture non-union with implant failure which was not associated with trauma. Index surgery was done 1.5 years ago. The patient presented to us with pain and difficulty walking. CT scan was performed to evaluate the progress of fracture healing (non-union) and observe any patterns of bone loss. Clinical examination and blood tests were used to eliminate the possibility of infection. We treated the patient surgically by implant removal, fracture reduction, and lateral plate fixation with a locking compression plate. Additionally, anterior locking plate fixation was done while concurrently introducing a bone graft composed of Chronos granules (bone graft substitute) (figure 3). Postoperatively the patient was advised of non-weight bearing mobilisation until signs of callus formation were seen on the radiograph at 2 months follow-up. Protected weight bearing started after that. Union time for radiological healing was 6 months. At 6 months the patient was walking using full weight bearing with a flexion knee range of motion of 0–110° (figure 4). There was no evidence of infection, non-union or malunion at end of 1 year follow-up.

Postoperatively, knee range of motion was started immediately. The patient was advised of non-weight-bearing mobilisation until signs of callus formation were seen on the radiograph. Follow-up was done at 2 weeks, 1 month, 2 months, 4 months, 6 months and 1 year. The mean union time for radiological healing was 5 months (range 4–6 months). The mean knee flexion range of motion was 0–115° (range 110° to 120°).

Outcome and follow-up

Both patients achieved a full range of movement and satisfactory radiological healing. There was no evidence of infection, non-union, or malunion at 1 year follow-up.

Discussion

DFFs with significant comminution, osteoporosis, and medial wall discontinuity have been associated with high rates of implant failure and non-union. Recent efforts have focused on mitigating these complications by offering more stable constructs, either through dual plating (medial and lateral), intramedullary nailing combined with plating, or the use of an Ilizarov external fixator. However, none of these approaches has emerged as the preferred choice due to their respective sets of challenges and issues.

Nails have limitations in addressing these complex fractures, especially those with articular comminution or low fracture line patterns, leading to inadequate fixation with screws. These findings have been reported by Kim et al, who documented higher implant failure rates in DFFs associated with low fracture lines and severe comminution.8 Probstel and Borner also stated that retrograde nailing can be challenging in comminuted fractures due to increased instability and malalignment.14

The use of Ilizarov external fixators with minimal internal fixation for distal femur fractures is associated with several drawbacks. Complications in this context can arise from factors such as restricted knee motion, inadequate reduction, susceptibility to septic arthritis and osteomyelitis, pin site infections, loss of reduction, and delayed or non-union necessitating bone grafting.913

Recent focus has shifted toward dual plating with lateral and medial plates to address DFFs with significant medial comminution or osteoporosis. However, the use of an additional medial plate requires a separate incision and surgical expertise, and this technique has limitations, as a large medial plate cannot be used if substantial metaphyseal comminution is present. In contrast to parallel plating, positioning the plates orthogonally and employing a smaller secondary plate can minimise soft tissue stripping and bone devascularisation while still retaining the mechanical benefits of dual plating.14

In our approach, we have introduced a dual 90–90 plating technique, where we can achieve torsional stability with an additional implant through a single incision which leads to decreased surgical time. After lateral plate fixation, an additional 4.5 mm limited-contact dynamic compression plate was used on the anterior surface of the femur to achieve torsional stability in a 90–90 configuration. In a non-union scenario, we feel the described technique accords additional and angular stability, facilitating fracture healing.

A similar principle has been employed for distal humerus fractures, where two plates are applied in a 90–90 construct, providing torsional stability. Biomechanical and clinical studies have proven that the 90–90 construct has improved chances of union and leads to increased ability to withstand deformation in both the sagittal and coronal planes.15

A limitation of this method is that it cannot be employed for acute DFFs since visualisation of the medial articular surface and indirect reduction can be difficult. Additionally, if the fixation of the DFF extends distal to the trochlear surface, the anterior plate may impinge on the patellofemoral joint during articulation. However, in our cases, the fracture pattern allowed us to position the anterior plate proximal to the trochlear surface of the distal femur, ensuring that it did not interfere with patellofemoral articulation. This was confirmed intraoperatively by direct visualisation throughout the range of motion.
