Effectiveness of a Behavioural Management Training on Knowledge and Self-efficacy of Speech Therapists in Managing Children with Neurodevelopmental Disorders in Lagos State, Nigeria.
Authors: Deborah Tope Duruchukwu, Yetunde Celia Adeniyi, Oluwabunmi Funmilola Fola-Bolumole, Cornelius Ani, Olayinka Olusola Omigbodun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Challenging behaviours are highly prevalent among children with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), particularly those with speech impairments. Speech and language therapists in low- and middle-income countries, including Nigeria, frequently encounter these behaviours during service delivery but often report limited training in effective behavioural management approaches such as Functional Behavioural Analysis (FBA). This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a behavioural management training programme on the knowledge and self-efficacy of speech therapists managing children with NDDs.Methodology:A quasi-experimental one-group pre-post intervention design was employed, conducted in three phases: baseline, intervention, and post-intervention. Sixteen participants were recruited using purposive sampling. Pre- and post-intervention knowledge and self-efficacy related to challenging behaviour management were assessed. Data was analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics.Results:The intervention resulted in significant improvements in both knowledge and self-efficacy. Mean knowledge scores increased from 10.69 (± 1.40) at baseline to 11.50 (± 0.73) post-intervention. Self-efficacy scores also improved significantly, rising from 5.06 (± 2.08) to 7.50 (± 1.21). The effect size (Cohen's d = 1.03) indicates a large effect, suggesting that the intervention is potentially effective in improving participants' skills and confidence in managing challenging behaviours.Conclusion:Behavioural management training based on FBA appears effective in improving knowledge and self-efficacy among speech and language therapists working with children with NDDs. Integrating such training into professional development programmes may strengthen service delivery, improve access to care, and optimise outcomes for children in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a diverse group of conditions arising during the developmental period that affect intellectual, motor, language, and social functioning. They include Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Intellectual Disability, Cerebral Palsy, and other communication and motor impairments. These disorders typically emerge in early childhood and are associated with genetic, biological, environmental, and psychosocial factors, and are increasingly recognized as leading causes of morbidity in children worldwide. NDDs impose a heavy burden on families and caregivers, reduce productivity in communities and contribute to significant societal costs.

Speech and language impairments are among the most common comorbidities of NDDs [1], presenting as delayed, impaired, or absent communication skills, affecting both receptive and expressive language. A study in Nigeria reported that nearly 40% of children with NDDs between six months and fifteen years had speech and language disorders [2]. Globally, speech sound disorders such as articulation disorders, dysarthria, and childhood apraxia of speech are frequently observed in children with NDDs, further complicating developmental outcomes.

Challenging behaviours are also prevalent among children with NDDs, including aggression, self-injury, hyperactivity, withdrawal, and disruptive conduct [3,4]. These behaviours may serve specific functions, such as sensory stimulation, avoidance of demands, or attempts to communicate unmet needs. Evidence suggests that challenging behaviours are highly prevalent among children with NDDs, including in low- and middle-income countries [5]. Such behaviours can impede learning, limit social participation, and place significant emotional and financial strain on families. If unaddressed, they may lead to poor academic attainments, social maladjustment, and long-term mental health difficulties [6].

The relationship between communication difficulties and challenging behaviours is well established. Children with speech impairments often express frustration through disruptive behaviours, which can impair emotional regulation, peer interaction, and social functioning. Studies have consistently reported higher levels of disruptive and antisocial behaviour among children with speech impairments compared to their typically developing peers [7]. These behaviours can reduce the effectiveness of speech therapy interventions and hinder therapeutic engagement.

Speech and language therapists (SLTs) play a critical role in supporting children with NDDs, however many report limited training in behavioural management strategies [8]. Research indicates that therapists frequently encounter challenging behaviours during service delivery but lack exposure to structured behavioural interventions such as Functional Behavioural Analysis (FBA) [9]. FBA is a systematic approach that identifies the underlying causes of challenging behaviours and informs targeted interventions. Evidence suggests that FBA can improve behavioural outcomes and enhance therapy effectiveness [10]. However, in Nigeria and other low- and middle-income countries, FBA remains underutilized due to resource constraints and gaps in professional training.

This gap in behavioural management capacity among SLTs poses a significant barrier to effective service delivery. Without adequate skills, therapists may struggle to implement interventions effectively, resulting in suboptimal outcomes for children with NDDs. In Lagos State, Nigeria's most populous city, where the demand for speech therapy services is high, the need for effective behavioural management strategies is particularly urgent.

Therefore, this study sought to evaluate the effectiveness of FBA training in enhancing the knowledge, self-efficacy, and satisfaction of SLTs working with children with NDDs in Lagos. Addressing this gap has the potential to strengthen therapist capacity, improve intervention delivery and enhance developmental outcomes for affected children.

Methods

This study employed a quasi-experimental one-group pre–post intervention design to evaluate the effect of Functional Behavioural Analysis (FBA) training on therapists' knowledge, self-efficacy, and satisfaction. A quasi-experimental approach was selected due to feasibility constraints and the limited pool of trained speech therapists in the study setting, making randomisation impractical. The pre–post design allowed for within-subject comparison, enabling assessment of change attributable to the intervention.

The study was conducted in Lagos State, Nigeria, at two key service delivery points: the Child and Adolescent Mental Health Service Unit of the Federal Neuro-Psychiatric Hospital (FNPH), Yaba, and Amtowa Speech and Education Consult (ASET), Ikorodu. These sites were purposively selected to capture therapists working across both clinical and community-based settings, thereby enhancing the relevance and applicability of findings. FNPH provides specialised psychiatric services, while ASET coordinates therapists working in schools, homes, and community contexts, ensuring representation of diverse practice environments.

Participants were speech and language therapists and assistant therapists working with children aged 2–18 years diagnosed with NDDs. A minimum of three months' work experience was required to ensure participants had sufficient clinical exposure to challenging behaviours. Therapists working exclusively with speech disorders without NDDs, or those working with adults, were excluded to maintain focus on the target population.

The minimum sample size was calculated using the formula n = F(σ/d)2,

n=F(σ/d)2

where:

n is the minimum sample size for the group.

F = 12.99, which is a factor based on 95% power and 0.05% level of significance (Wade, 1997)

σ = is the standard deviation for the primary outcome (Knowledge of Challenging Behaviour Management Questionnaire)

d = is the difference expected to be found between the pre and post-intervention level of the participants' knowledge of challenging behaviour management.

Assuming that the behavioural intervention will result in one standard deviation improvement in the participants' knowledge of challenging behaviour management pre and post intervention, then the sample size (n) will be:

n=F(σ/d)2

n=12.99(1/1)2

n=12.99×1=12.99

n≈13

This sample size (n) was increased by 50% and rounded up to 20 in order to account for potential losses during follow-up or attrition in the course of intervention. Due to attrition, only 16 participants took part in the study.

An effect size equivalent to one standard deviation improvement was assumed, reflecting expected change following a structured training intervention. The calculated minimum sample size was 13 and was increased to 20 to account for potential attrition. Sixteen participants completed the study. Purposive sampling was used to recruit information-rich participants from a finite pool of 48 therapists, ensuring inclusion of those actively engaged in relevant clinical practice.

Data was collected using four instruments. A socio-demographic questionnaire captured participants' background characteristics and prior exposure to behavioural interventions. The Knowledge of Challenging Behaviour Management Questionnaire (KCBMQ), a 12-item adapted tool, assessed knowledge before and after training. The instrument has been found to be reliable for use in Nigeria with a Cronbach alpha of 0.81 [21]. Some minor challenges were made to the instrument during our study to ensure contextual relevance while maintaining alignment with established constructs. These changes were verified through construct validity by two experts. A self-efficacy scale, informed by Bandura's social cognitive theory, measured confidence in managing challenging behaviours. A client satisfaction questionnaire, from Greenfield and Attkisson [11], was administered post-intervention to evaluate acceptability and perceived usefulness.

The intervention consisted of a structured, manualised FBA training programme delivered virtually via Zoom over three weeks. Virtual delivery was chosen to enhance accessibility, minimise logistical barriers, and accommodate participants' work schedules. Four one-hour sessions were conducted using interactive methods, including lectures, group discussions, and case-based problem-solving, to promote active learning. The training content was adapted from an existing manual [12] and covered understanding challenging behaviours, principles of FBA, contingency management strategies, and practical application. Between-session support via phone calls, WhatsApp, and SMS was incorporated to reinforce learning, improve engagement, and reduce attrition.

Pre-intervention assessments included the socio-demographic questionnaire, KCBMQ, and self-efficacy scale, while post-intervention assessments included the same instruments alongside the satisfaction questionnaire. Data were analysed using SPSS version 21. Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics and satisfaction ratings. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-intervention scores, as the design involved repeated measures on the same participants. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d to quantify the magnitude of change, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research and Ethics Committee of Federal Neuropsychiatry Hospital, Yaba, Lagos, on 23/12/2024, with assigned number FNPHY/HREC/2024/001/12/282. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality was ensured through anonymisation and secure data storage, and participation was voluntary, with the right to withdraw at any stage. The intervention posed minimal risk and was expected to provide direct professional benefit by enhancing participants' behavioural management skills.

Results

A total of sixteen (16) SLTs who work with children diagnosed with NDDs participated in this study. The respondents had an average age of 38.7 years (± 8.6), indicating that most were middle-aged professionals. Females constituted a slight majority (56.3%), and most participants identified as Christian (93.8%). Over half held a Bachelor's degree (56.3%), while smaller proportions had postgraduate qualifications or other certifications. The majority were practising SLTs (68.8%), with others in special education (18.8%) or trainee roles (12.5%). Professional experience varied, with half (50.0%) having 0-4 years of experience and only a small proportion (12.5%) having more than 10 years of practice.

Effect of Intervention on Knowledge

Knowledge of challenging behaviour management improved following the intervention. The mean knowledge score increased from 10.69 (SD = 1.40) at baseline to 11.50 (SD = 0.73) post-intervention. This improvement was statistically significant (t(15) = -2.45, p = 0.03), with a moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.61), indicating a meaningful increase in participants' knowledge levels. Effect of Intervention on Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy scores demonstrated a substantial increase after the training. The mean score rose from 5.06 (SD = 2.08) pre-intervention to 7.50 (SD = 1.21) post-intervention. This difference was highly statistically significant (t(15) = -4.12, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen's d = 1.03), suggesting a strong impact of the intervention on participants' confidence in managing challenging behaviours.

Participants' Satisfaction

Participant satisfaction with the training was high. The majority rated the quality of training as excellent (81.3%), while 12.5% rated it as good and only 6.3% as poor. Most participants (68.8%) reported the training as definitely relevant to their practice, with the remainder (31.3%) indicating it was generally relevant.

More than half (56.3%) reported that the programme met almost all their needs, while the remainder indicated that it met most of their needs. Overall satisfaction was high, with 68.8% reporting being very satisfied and 31.3% mostly satisfied. No participants reported dissatisfaction.

Qualitative Feedback

Qualitative feedback highlighted several strengths of the intervention. Participants valued the practical relevance of the training, particularly its direct application to real-world clinical challenges. The interactive format, use of case examples, and clarity of explanations were frequently cited as beneficial. The emphasis on positive reinforcement and discouragement of punitive approaches (e.g., flogging) was also noted as an important takeaway.

In terms of areas for improvement, half of the participants reported no suggested changes. However, some participants highlighted technical challenges related to unstable internet connectivity during virtual sessions. Additional suggestions included making the programme ongoing and reducing mobile data requirements.

Participants also recommended scaling up the training, including involving teachers and parents, increasing the frequency of sessions, and expanding access to a wider audience. Some suggested scheduling sessions at fixed times to improve preparedness, while others expressed satisfaction with the current format.

Discussion

This study examined the effect of a structured Functional Behavioural Analysis (FBA)-based training on knowledge and self-efficacy among SLTs managing children with NDDs. The findings highlight a clear mismatch between clinical exposure and formal training. Although all participants reported frequent encounters with challenging behaviours, the majority had no prior training in behavioural management. This gap in professional preparation mirrors findings from other studies demonstrating insufficient behavioural training among therapists and limited access to integrated services addressing both language and behavioural needs [13,14]. It also reflects a broader structural gap in service delivery, where behavioural and communication needs are insufficiently integrated within therapeutic practice.

The intervention resulted in a statistically significant improvement in knowledge of challenging behaviour management. This improvement likely reflects a shift from implicit, experience-based responses to a more explicit, function-oriented understanding of behaviour. Such conceptual reorganisation is consistent with the theoretical basis of FBA, which emphasises the identification of antecedents, behaviours, and consequences as a framework for intervention. Similar improvements in knowledge and competence following behavioural training have been reported in previous studies [15,16]. In addition, the range of behaviours reported in this study, including aggression, tantrums, and stereotypies, aligns with established evidence on behavioural presentations in children with NDDs [4, 17], underscoring the relevance of equipping therapists with appropriate behavioural frameworks. The observed improvement also supports evidence that training on the relationship between communication difficulties and behaviour can enhance therapeutic effectiveness.

The increase in self-efficacy was more pronounced, with a large effect size, indicating a substantial improvement in participants' confidence in managing challenging behaviours. This finding is consistent with Bandura's social cognitive theory, which posits that skill acquisition enhances perceived competence and expectations of success [18]. Higher self-efficacy has been associated with improved coping, persistence, and overall service delivery [16, 19]. In low-resource settings such as Nigeria, where specialist supervision is limited, self-efficacy becomes particularly important for sustaining effective practice. The magnitude of change observed suggests that the training provided participants with a practical and transferable framework for managing behavioural challenges.

The findings further highlight the close functional relationship between communication impairments and challenging behaviours in children with NDDs. The high frequency of behaviours reported indicates that behavioural dysregulation is central to everyday clinical practice rather than incidental. Without appropriate behavioural management strategies, such behaviours can disrupt therapy sessions and reduce intervention fidelity. FBA offers a structured approach to understanding behaviour as goal-directed and context-dependent, which aligns with the communication-focused orientation of speech therapy. This alignment likely contributed to the intervention's relevance and uptake.

Participant feedback supports the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention. The emphasis on practical, case-based learning and interactive delivery facilitated knowledge translation into clinical contexts. The focus on reinforcement-based strategies, alongside the discouragement of punitive approaches, is particularly important, suggesting that training can influence both clinical practice and underlying norms, especially in settings where non-evidence-based disciplinary practices may persist. High levels of satisfaction with the training are consistent with previous findings that structured, interactive, and contextually relevant training enhances engagement and perceived usefulness [20].

The virtual delivery format was generally effective, despite some reported challenges with internet connectivity. This has important implications for scalability, particularly in settings with limited access to in-person training and a dispersed workforce. Digital platforms may provide a cost-effective approach to expanding training coverage while maintaining participant engagement.

Several limitations should be considered. The quasi-experimental one-group pre–post design limits causal inference, as improvements may be influenced by testing effects or other unmeasured factors. The small sample size reduces statistical power and limits generalisability. In addition, outcomes were assessed immediately post-intervention, and the sustainability of these improvements over time is unknown. In addition, the observed outcome might be due to the Hawthorne effect, where people change their behavior because they know they're being watched/studied, not because of the actual impact of the intervention. The absence of objective measures of clinical practice or child-level outcomes further limits conclusions about the broader impact of the intervention. The study focused on a single urban area, which might limit the broader applicability of its findings. Furthermore, outcomes are limited to provider-level changes, and no inference can be made about child behavioural improvement.

Despite these limitations, the study provides preliminary evidence that targeted FBA-based training can strengthen the capacity of SLTs in low-resource settings. The findings support the integration of behavioural management into both pre-service training and continuing professional development. Future research should incorporate controlled designs, larger samples, and longitudinal follow-up, and should examine whether improvements in therapist knowledge and self-efficacy translate into sustained changes in practice and improved child outcomes.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a brief, structured FBA-based training can produce measurable improvements in knowledge and self-efficacy among SLTs. By strengthening both conceptual understanding and clinical confidence, such training represents a feasible and scalable strategy for improving the management of challenging behaviours in children with NDDs.
