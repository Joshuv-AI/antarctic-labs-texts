The potential of x‐ray virtual histology in the diagnosis of skin tumors
Authors: Giulia Saccomano, Maurizio Pinamonti, Elena Longo, Thomas Marcuzzo, Giuliana Tromba, Diego Dreossi, Francesco Brun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHistopathological analysis represents the gold standard in clinical practice for diagnosing skin neoplasms. While the current diagnostic workflow has specialized in producing robust and accurate results, interpreting tissue architecture and malignant cellular morphology correctly remains one of the greatest challenges for pathologists. This paper aims to explore the prospect of applying x‐ray virtual histology to human skin tumor excisions and correlating it with the histological validation.Materials and MethodsSeven skin biopsies containing intriguing melanoma types and pigmented skin lesions were scanned using x‐ray Computed micro‐Tomography (μCT) and then sectioned for conventional histology assessment.ResultsThe tissue microarchitecture reconstructed by μCT offers detailed insights into diagnosing the malignancy or benignity of the skin lesions. Three‐dimensional reconstruction via x‐ray virtual histology reveals infiltrative patterns in basal cell carcinoma and evaluated invasiveness in melanoma. The technology enables the identification of pagetoid distributions of neoplastic cells and the assessment of melanoma depth in three dimensions.ConclusionAlthough the proposed approach is not intended to replace conventional histology, the non‐destructive nature of the sample and the clarity provided by virtual inspection demonstrate the promising impact of μCT as a valid support method prior to conventional histological sectioning. Indeed, μCT images can suggest the optimal sectioning position before using a microtome, as is commonly performed in histological practice. Moreover, the three‐dimensional nature of the proposed approach paves the way for a more accurate assessment of significant prognostic factors in melanoma, such as Breslow thickness, by considering the whole micro‐volume rather than a two‐dimensional observation.Keywords: histology, melanoma, skin tumors, x‐ray phase‐contrast micro‐tomography, x‐ray virtual histology

INTRODUCTION

Skin cancer is an increasingly common and concerning medical condition that results from the uncontrolled growth of skin cells. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Due to their different characteristics, behaviors, and treatments, cutaneous tumors can be classified into two main groups: non‐melanoma and melanoma. The first category comprises many different lesions, including basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, which are the most frequently occurring skin malignancies, while melanoma is less common, although more serious.

Different in‐vivo approaches have been studied to assist the dermatological diagnosis, from for example, ultrasound‐based 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 to optical‐based methods. 9 Unfortunately, these systems are limited by inadequate resolution and power of penetration, still leading to a preference for an accurate laboratory analysis of the biopsy specimen. Currently, the histopathological diagnosis of melanocytic lesions is mainly based on cancer cells recognition and their diffusion in the excised tissue which can occur also at higher penetration depths. However, the correct identification of malignant lesions from benign tumors remains one of the greatest challenges for pathologists. 10 A significant limitation is that the histological examination still remains a bi‐dimensional approach which might be unable to appreciate the expansion and arrangement of elements of interest in the surrounding volume. To retrieve three‐dimensional information, novel technologies have been experimented on biological tissues by, for example, merging consecutive histological slides 11 , 12 , 13 or by combining tissue clearing and fluorescence microscopy. 14 Unfortunately, these techniques lead to time‐consuming sample preparation and data processing.

X‐ray virtual histology based on phase‐contrast Computed micro‐Tomography (μCT) represents a powerful and non‐destructive imaging technique for biological samples that offers several advantages over traditional histology methods. 15 One key strength is that μCT provides a comprehensive imaging of the intact specimen, resulting in high‐resolution, high‐contrast 3D datasets of fine anatomical details without the need for staining. This 3D reconstruction could accurately guide the pathologist in the selection of the most appropriate slicing for further analysis via conventional histology. This advanced technique has been applied to study several pathological tissues both in mice models and in human excision, such as brain, 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 human pancreatic cancer, 20 breast carcinomas, 21 , 22 , 23 intestine, 24 and lungs. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 To date, skin tumors have not been considered through μCT investigation, and only a few studies applied this imaging technique to melanoma. 29 , 30 , 31

This article aims to explore the application of x‐ray virtual histology to human skin lesion excision specimens. By utilizing high‐contrast high‐resolution synchrotron radiation μCT, we were able not only to reconstruct the complete 3D microarchitecture of the entire sample but also to derive diagnostic information primarily based on architectural features. The objective of this work is to provide an atlas of the most common benign and malignant skin lesions imaged by μCT and compare this virtual 3D model against conventional histology. The imaged data here presented are discussed considering the basics of the histopathology discipline. Although the proposed approach is not intended to replace conventional histology at its current stage of development, it is believed that its potential is considerable. Virtual histology offers supplementary insights and a comprehensive visual representation of an excised specimen that could significantly support the histopathology practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Seven paraffin blocks containing skin biopsies of Italian human patients with strong suspicion of skin cancer were selected by the Department of Pathology of the University Hospital of Trieste (Italy) and investigated by means of synchrotron radiation phase‐contrast μCT at the SYRMEP beamline of the Elettra synchrotron (Trieste, Italy). Table 1 reports a list of the samples considered for this work. To avoid interfering with the clinical practice, histological examination was already performed on these tissue specimens for diagnostic purposes. Tissues embedded in paraffin blocks were further sliced to obtain the corresponding histology section according to μCT results.

Conventional histology

Tissue specimens were formalin‐fixed for 24–48 h depending on their size to preserve tissue integrity and prevent degradation. After the fixation period, samples were rinsed to remove excess formalin and paraffin‐embedded in standard paraffin blocks (2.5 × 3.5 × 1.0 cm3). Paraffin‐fixed tissue samples were subsequently sectioned into 3–5 μm thick slices using a microtome for subsequent histological analysis. Tissue sections were subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining. Finally, histological images were acquired using a D‐Sight 2.0 digital microscope at a 200× magnification.

X‐Ray virtual histology, that is, phase contrast x‐ray μCT

Paraffin blocks within the common plastic cassette used for the embedding of the sample were scanned with a μCT setup at the SYRMEP beamline 32 , 33 , 34 at Elettra Synchrotron (Trieste, Italy). The resulting isotropic voxel size is 2.0×2.0×2.0 μm3. Multiple adjacent acquisitions were considered to cover all the tissue‐of‐interest. All the acquisition details can be found in the supplementary information section. Datasets were stitched to match the field‐of‐view of conventional histology. Paraffin‐based background was digitally removed for visualization purposes. All μCT volumes were virtually explored along different planes and processed to extract the most interesting views.

RESULTS

Virtual histology results are presented together with the corresponding histological sections as validation. The virtual cutting plane within the μCT volume was manually aligned according to the relevant histological image, with particular attention to the recognition of common patterns and features. The following subsections are organized according to the description column of Table 1.

Control skin tissue

As visible in Figure 1, the epidermis — which is the outermost layer of the skin — is mainly visible by its components: the stratum corneum (represented as a white line above the overall skin tissue) and the stratum basale (marked as a thin edge line between the constant light gray of epidermis layers and dermal tissue). The dermis is characterized by a thick layer of connective tissues (e.g., elastic fibers and collagen) visible in the images as a dense light gray pattern beneath the epidermis. A closer look allows to differentiate papillary and reticular dermis. The dermal tissue can also contain other structures such as hair follicles (Figure 1A), sebaceous glands, and sweat glands (Figure 1A, B). The hypodermis mainly consists of adipose fat tissue and loose connective tissue: in μCT they are visible as the gray blobs underneath the dermis (Figure 1C). Finally, blood and lymphatic vessels can pass all layers of the skin and they can also appear in virtual histology images (Figure 1C).

μCT sections of control tissues with skin layers and some characteristic elements highlighted: panel (A) hair follicle structure, (B) sweat glands, and (C) blood vessels inserted in connective tissue. All scalebars are 100 μm.

Nevus and seborrheic keratosis

Figure 2 is a composition of two benign conditions of skin: top panels of Figure 2 display a nevus, while bottom panels show a seborrheic keratosis.

Correlative imaging of μCT and histopathological images of a nevus (top panels) and seborrheic keratosis (bottom panels) at different levels of magnification. All scalebars are 100 μm.

In the nevus, nests of melanocytes are distributed regularly at the tips of the rete ridges and form a central raised area (top panels of Figure 2). This aspect can be observed through μCT as a uniform cellular density throughout the same level of the lesion. Due to their dimensions, μCT is not able to distinguish melanocytes, but it is able to appreciate junctional nests formed by clusters of melanocytes at the epidermal‐dermal junction.

Seborrheic keratosis, and especially the pigmented variant, may raise clinical suspicion and be confused with malignant melanoma due to some morphologic similarities (e.g., irregular borders, variable coloration, and raised surfaces) causing false alarms and unnecessary biopsies. 35 In addition, a particular type of melanoma, also known as seborrheic‐like melanoma, mimics the clinical appearance of benign seborrheic keratoses, leading to a potential delay in proper evaluation and diagnosis. 36

On the contrary, the histological diagnosis of seborrheic keratosis generally does not pose major problems thanks to the easy identification of its main microscopic characteristics, which can be detected also by μCT. As reported in the bottom panel of Figure 2, keratin‐based cysts maintain the basal layer of the epidermis intact, growing slowly and irregularly inside the epidermal layer. From the μCT perspective, seborrheic keratoses are represented as compact proliferations of epidermal keratinocytes as highlighted by the green arrow, which form irregular and thickened structures with defined boundaries.

Basal cell carcinoma

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common form of non‐melanoma skin cancer that has an extremely low metastatic potential. However, untreated BCCs can become locally invasive and spread destroying skin, tissue, and bone.

Among the many morphological subtypes of existing basal cell carcinoma, we report here as an example the nodular type (top panel of Figure 3). BCCs in μCT images appear as compact structures made by clusters of basaloid cells surrounded by peripheral borderline cell layers. The internal arrangement of undifferentiated tumor cells — basal cell‐like — is rather chaotic in both images with a central necrosis highlighted by blue circles in Figure 3A–C and B–D. As visible from Figure 3B–D, BBCs are often connected to epidermis as they originate from the uncontrolled proliferation of basal cells. In larger tumor nodules, necrotic areas are often present and can achieve a cystic appearance. An accumulation of mucin usually occurs near the necrosis of centrally located cells.

Melanoma in situ

Also known as Stage 0 melanoma, melanoma in situ is defined as a proliferation of neoplastic melanocytes confined into epidermis without penetrating deeper layers of tissues. However, nests of malignant or dysplastic melanocytes can be apparent in the papillary dermis, still remaining connected with the epidermis and not breaching out of the basal layer (Figure 4).

Visual correspondence of μCT and histology of a Melanoma in situ at different level of magnification. Panels (A,C) highlight the presence of nested melanocytes and lymphocytes, while panels (B,D) illustrate the superimposition of μCT and histological images for visualizing nested melanocytes. The most relevant pathological elements are summarized in the table of legends. All scalebars are 100 μm.

Lymphocytic infiltrate is also appreciable in μCT imaging but not as single cells due to their dimension. However, they can be seen as organized clusters in different regions of the dermal tissue, as highlighted in yellow in Figure 4A–C. Clusters of lymphocytes do not form a distinct physical layer. On the contrary, their organization and distribution within the tumor microenvironment can vary due to lymphocyte migration in response to cancer cells presence. This because they play a significant role in the immune response against melanoma and their quantification in terms of density and activity is a good prognostic indicator of immune system response to tumor.

Invasive melanoma—Superficial spreading melanoma

Superficial spreading melanoma (SSM) is the most common invasive melanoma variant. While melanoma in situ displays atypia and architectural disorder but do not breach the basement membrane, the SSM lesion is characterized by the presence of atypical melanocytes that have passed the epidermal‐dermal junction, invading the dermis. The distinction between invasive SSM and melanoma in situ is a critical phase for understanding the potential for progression. These melanocytes exhibit various histological features such as irregularities in both nuclear shape and size, also known as pleomorphism, and irregular distribution as visible in Figure 5C–F. As shown in top panels of Figure 5, SSM is identified by the infiltration of neoplastic cells with the tendency of spreading horizontally rather than vertically into the dermal tissue. Due to its gradual invasion that advances into the dermis, pagetoid infiltration of the epidermis by melanocytes is frequently observable in SSM. Pagetoid cells are often larger and more atypical than normal cells, with a tendency to diffuse along the basal layer of the epidermis. Figure 5C–F demonstrate how μCT imaging can uncover the distinctive pleomorphism of such neoplastic cells, enabling the precise detection of their spatial distribution in the tissue. Then, lymphocytes localized in clusters nearby tumor regions in Figure 5B–E are outlined in yellow.

The most characteristic elements have been represented at high zoom factors: panels (B,E) highlight the pagetoid growth pattern as well as nested melanocytes, and (C,F) show tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes. Panels (A,C) compare benign clusters of melanocytes in the nearby nevus with nested neoplastic cells in panels (B,E). The most relevant pathological elements are summarized in the table of legends. In the case here reported, melanoma developed near an existing melanocytic nevus, possibly originating from it. Despite the identification of genetic factors that contribute to it, the reasons for the progression of a nevus to melanoma are still uncertain. 37 Histology enables the examination of the nature of each individual cell, while μCT cannot achieve the same level of resolution. However, μCT is able to acknowledge differences in overall organization of the two cellular groups. In fact, there is a clear demarcation between the cluster of melanocytes and their environment, whereas nests of neoplastic melanocytes have less delimited boundaries.

The two important aspects to consider when evaluating the invasiveness of a melanoma are the Breslow thickness and the presence of ulceration. While Breslow thickness describes the extension of melanoma in term of depth from the surface of the skin to the deepest point of the tumor, the presence of ulceration is a determinant negative prognostic factor that states that melanoma has broken the outer layer of skin. In this specific case, a Breslow thickness of more than a 1 mm and the absence of ulceration define a pathological T2a stage according to TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) classification.

Invasive melanoma—Nodular malignant melanoma

Nodular Malignant Melanoma (NMM) is the most aggressive form of melanoma. Compared to other melanoma subtypes, it tends to grow more rapidly in thickness than in diameter. NMM can occur spontaneously or can develop from a pre‐existing benign or dysplastic nevus with a significant potential for invasion and metastasis. Therefore, it is crucial to consider tumor Breslow depth and the presence of ulceration as significant prognostic factors.

As appreciable in the top panel of Figure 6A,B, NMM appears as a compact mass of dysplastic tumor cells with upward epidermal destruction. Its tumor cells are often characterized by a high mitotic activity, which indicates a high level of cellular proliferation seeking for expansion. NMMs tend to diffuse in clusters or nests of highly atypical melanocytes infiltrating the dermis in a vertical growth pattern, but have minimal adjacent epidermal spread. As a result, the tumor mass appears distinct and confined in its shell with respect to other skin layers (e.g., epidermis and dermis). Both Figure 6E,F also show the presence of ulceration and the tumor below provoking it. This particular case presents also a SSM formed nearby the NMM: it is recognizable by both the pagetoid spreading cells within the epidermis and dermis and the nested atypical melanocytes in Figure 6C,D.

DISCUSSION

The non‐destructive approach proposed in this work enables the creation of a detailed three‐dimensional perspective of the internal tissue structure aimed at supporting the traditional histological processing. Due to the considerable diversity of skin tumors and pseudo‐tumors, this research concentrated on the most intriguing melanoma types and selected skin lesions based on pigmentation, with the objective of providing a differential diagnosis. It was here shown that the main aspects of common benign lesions such as seborrheic keratosis and nevus can be recognized. Three‐dimensional reconstruction of BCCs allows for the understanding of infiltrative patterns, but more interestingly, pagetoid distributions of neoplastic cells as well as nested melanoma volumes can be investigated and analyzed. Of particular interest is the potential of x‐ray virtual histology to perform a three‐dimensional assessment of melanoma depth, leading to the so‐called Breslow thickness. In conventional histology, this measurement is derived from a single two‐dimensional observation, thus limiting its accuracy.

The state‐of‐the‐art technology of x‐ray virtual histology is unable to assess subcellular resolution in the same way as conventional histology. Moreover, the pathological diagnosis in a clinical context is typically carried out by applying molecular staining to assess the precise nature of each singular feature. For these reasons, x‐ray virtual histology is considered a supporting tool for conventional histology, providing a three‐dimensional perspective in a non‐destructive manner that histology cannot achieve.

Finally, this work leverages synchrotron radiation x‐ray imaging. Despite the remarkable capabilities for elucidating microstructural information comparable to conventional histology, it is imperative to recognize the inherent limitations that preclude the widespread application of this imaging modality in medical practice. Synchrotron facilities are sophisticated and expensive installations mainly used only for research purposes. The scarcity of such facilities limits their accessibility for routine medical applications. Nevertheless, intense research is going on aimed at the development of table‐top phase contrast x‐ray devices capable to replicate the high‐resolution/high‐contrast imaging available at synchrotron facilities. 38 , 39 These advancements improve spatial resolution, contrast, and dynamic range useful to get accurate virtual histology images.

CONCLUSION

This study aims to demonstrate the successful application of phase‐contrast μCT based x‐ray virtual histology in assessing human skin excisions. The main objective is to demonstrate the feasibility and potential application of this technique to support the histopathology practice and, eventually, the diagnosis of skin tumors. The study confirms that x‐ray virtual histology can support the differential diagnosis between benign and malignant lesions and identify the high‐resolution cellular composition inside skin tissues. While conventional histology remains essential for cellular and molecular analysis, its 3D nature makes μCT an attractive value. Being also non‐destructive, μCT could be considered as a preliminary stage where the derived images suggest the optimal sectioning position prior using a microtome as commonly performed in the conventional histological practice.
