Differential biventricular responses to VA-ECMO flow ramping after cardiac arrest: establishment of a preclinical ECPR model with PV loop monitoring
Authors: Clark G Owyang, Felipe Teran, Aaron Landau, Caleb Suh, Joshua M Satalin, Daniel Lopez, Bruce Searles, Maryam Nejatollahi, Supaporn Kulthinee, Andrea King, Robert Finkelstein, David Berlin, Manuel Martin-Flores, Paul M Heerdt, Joaquin Araos
Published: 2025

Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) is increasingly used for cardiac arrest (CA), yet its effects on biventricular function remain poorly defined.1, 2, 3, 4 Using a swine model, we established feasibility of direct biventricular pressure–volume (PV) loop monitoring pre-arrest and during ECMO initiation after cardiac arrest. We observed divergent responses across graded ECMO flows: persistent right ventricular (RV) uncoupling and inefficiency versus partial left ventricular (LV) preservation. These findings highlight the potential of this preclinical platform for future mechanistic study of ventricular adaptation to ECMO. This ongoing animal study has been approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Cornell University, College of Veterinary Medicine protocol #2024-0083.

In a 55-kg anesthetized swine, femoral ECMO cannulation was performed prior to induction of ventricular fibrillation. After 20 min of arrest (10 min no-flow, 10 min CPR), VA-ECMO was initiated as previously described.5 Conductance PV catheters were positioned in both ventricles to acquire continuous PV loops during graded ECMO support (15–60 cc/kg/min). PV data were collected during expiratory pauses at pre-arrest baseline and across the ECMO flow (Q) rates of 15, 30, 45, and 60 cc/kg/min each maintained for 15 min. Ventilation, vasopressor dosing, and gases were maintained stable across stages. Functional indices including end-systolic elastance (Ees), arterial elastance (Ea), coupling (Ees/Ea), stroke work (SW), potential energy (PE), pressure–volume area (PVA), and mechanical efficiency (MEE = SW/PVA) were calculated. Elastance and coupling metrics were quantified using the single-beat method,6 which estimates contractility and afterload from a single PV loop beat using regression-based extrapolation of end-systolic pressure and volume points.

Distinct biventricular patterns emerged (Fig. 1). Following arrest, RV ventriculo-arterial (VA) coupling (Ees/Ea) declined from 1.4 to <0.5 across increasing flows, reflecting profound uncoupling. This was driven by rising afterload (i.e., higher Ea in relation to the reduced Ees) and reduced stroke volume. SW dropped markedly while PE remained elevated, reducing efficiency from ∼80% to ∼40%. In contrast, LV contractility partially recovered with ECMO ramping; although afterload increased, SW and PE declined in parallel, preserving efficiency. Despite increased afterload (Ea), LV Ees/Ea also dropped but less so than the RV and exhibited a higher trend (∼0.7) across ECMO flows.

These observations, while limited to a single-animal feasibility preparation, demonstrate the ability to capture divergent ventricular responses to ECMO after cardiac arrest. In this case, the RV exhibited persistent uncoupling and inefficiency, while the LV showed partial recovery of contractility with preserved efficiency despite rising afterload. Building on the clinical observations of Kalra et al.,3 who relied on separately acquired 3D echocardiographic volumes and pressure catheter data, our swine model employs simultaneous conductance PV loop catheters—the reference standard—to directly quantify both LV and RV function before and after arrest across graded ECMO flows. Establishing this model highlights the feasibility of direct, high-fidelity PV loop monitoring during ECMO and provides a novel platform for systematic study of biventricular physiology under controlled conditions.
