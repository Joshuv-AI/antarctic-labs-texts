Juvenile Mitral Stenosis Presenting as Acute Pulmonary Edema Treated with Balloon Mitral Valvotomy
Authors: Newton Ghosh, Priyavardhan Mishra, Kshitij Balwaik, Anant Patil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Juvenile mitral stenosis is defined by poor left atrial emptying due to limited opening of the mitral-valve orifice in patients under the age of 20 years. Balloon mitral valvotomy is the current treatment of choice for juvenile mitral stenosis. Here we report a case of a 14-year-old female presenting with signs of acute pulmonary oedema. Later on, underlying mitral stenosis was revealed on echocardiography. After evaluation, she was successfully managed by percutaneous balloon mitral valvotomy. Screening young patients with pulmonary oedema by two-dimensional echocardiography is necessary to evaluate the underlying cardiac cause.

INTRODUCTION

Mitral stenosis (MS) is uncommon in children and adolescents, except in underdeveloped nations where rheumatic fever is still prevalent with up to 25% of patients under the age of 20 years, which is referred to as juvenile MS (JMS).[1] Rheumatic heart disease is a major health burden to all age groups with an overall prevalence of 1.5–2 per 1000, and in India, it accounts for 2–2.5 million patients.[2] MS is characterized by poor left atrial emptying due to limited opening of the mitral valve (MV) orifice.[3] JMS is marked by a significant occurrence of severe pulmonary arterial hypertension and pulmonary venous hypertension, accompanied by critical MV stenosis.[4]

We present a case of successfully managed JMS by percutaneous balloon mitral valvotomy (BMV) which presented primarily as acute pulmonary edema.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 14-year-old female of low socioeconomic class with no previous significant history presented with a history of acute-onset dyspnea of New York Heart Association Class II with cough and hemoptysis. It progressed rapidly to dyspnea at rest. There was no history of chest pain or recent febrile illness. She had no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or asthma and had not come across contact with any patient suffering from tuberculosis.

On arrival, the general examination revealed tachypnea with an oxygen saturation of 80% in room air. Her blood pressure and heart rate were 100/70 mmHg and 128 beats/min, respectively. She was profusely perspiring with cold extremities. On auscultation, bilateral crackles and rhonchi were heard all over the lung fields anteriorly and posteriorly, so the cardiovascular examination was not performed in detail. She was admitted immediately.

Within a few hours, her oxygen saturation dropped to 75% in room air. Her dyspnea worsened. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed hypoxemia with PaO, PCO, and pH of 34 mmHg, 36 mmHg, and 7.32, respectively. Given these conditions, she was immediately intubated and connected to a mechanical ventilator with positive pressure ventilation. FiO2 of 100% and positive end-expiratory pressure of 8 mmHg were given, and a bolus of injection furosemide 40 mg was given. She was started on injection nitroglycerin as per the intra-arterial blood pressure recording to curb the situation. She was shifted to the intensive care unit on a mechanical ventilator. After 8 h, her oxygen saturation and heart rate improved to 94% in room air and 102 bpm, respectively. Chest X-ray showed evidence of pulmonary edema with bilateral perihilar shadows (bat wing appearance). There was evidence of high pulmonary venous pressure with cephalization of veins and Kerley B lines with mild cardiomegaly. A two-dimensional echocardiography (2D-ECHO) was done which surprisingly revealed severe MS [Figure 1] with no mitral regurgitation (MR), peak/mean gradient across MV 34/18 mmHg, MV area (MVA) by planimetry 1.9 cm2, dilated left atrium (LA), and moderate pulmonary hypertension present. The Wilkins score was 7, and the diagnosis of JMS was established.

Two-dimensional echocardiography showing mitral stenosis

She underwent a BMV procedure. Femoral arterial access was taken to insert the pigtail catheter into the aorta. Hardware needed for transseptal puncture was accessed through the femoral vein puncture, and 260 cm wire was placed in the superior vena cava. Mullins sheath was passed over the wire and was slightly pulled near the right atrium side of the interatrial septum. The transseptal puncture was done by a Brockenbrough needle. LA pressure was measured. A 26 mm Inoue balloon passed over the wire and was crossed across the MV. The distal half of the balloon is in the left ventricle and the other in LA. The balloon was inflated at MV and consequently, after the single inflation significant MR was visualized on 2D-ECHO. Post-BMV 2D-ECHO findings [Figures 2 and 3] were moderate MS with moderate MR, peak/mean gradient across MV 8/4 mmHg, and MVA by pressure half-time (PHT) 1.2 cm2. After she was stabilized, she was discharged with a tablet of metoprolol 25 mg daily, a tablet of furosemide 20 mg daily, and a tablet of penicillin V 400 mg twice daily. She was followed up after 3 months, her 2D-ECHO findings were peak/mean gradient across MV, MVA by PHT, and pulmonary artery systolic pressure was 10/5 mmHg, 1.4 cm2, and 26 mmHg, respectively.

DISCUSSION

JMS refers to the extensive involvement of the subvalvular apparatus with serious stenosis of the MV.[45] There is a significant association between severe pulmonary arterial hypertension with congestive heart failure in JMS patients.[45] The most likely causes are ongoing smouldering rheumatic activity, tissue response to multiple attacks of rheumatic fever, and hypersensitivity of the pulmonary vasculature to the fulminating rheumatic process.[5] JMS and pediatric MS often require surgical intervention due to their severe presentation.[23] BMV has replaced the standard open or closed surgical commissurotomy approach for valvular diseases since it was introduced in 1985, yielding good short- and long-term results in young patients.[2] Few reports of successful BMV in pediatric patients under 10 years of age have been proven safe and effective.[267] BMV has been the current modality of choice in pediatric and JMS patients ages under 12 and 20 years old, respectively.[24] Calculating Wilkins’s score is necessary to evaluate the result of balloon dilatation before planning BMV.[8] This echocardiographic scoring system demonstrates that short-term outcome is related to the degree of valve leaflet thickening and subvalvular apparatus.[8] Although primarily used in adult rheumatic MS patients, the Wilkins score has been reported to be used in a 4-year-old patient undergoing BMV.[4]

Our presented report also illustrates the efficacy of BMV with successful outcomes. BMV is also known to have better immediate hemodynamic results, larger final MVA, and a higher percentage of increase in MVA in younger patients than adults,[4] thus requiring proper follow-up and evaluation postsurgery. The valve area almost doubles and the transmitral gradient reduces to less than half with significant benefits as was noticed in our case post-BMV.

MS is often associated with severe pulmonary hypertension; however, it can also be the cause of intractable pulmonary edema.[48] In our presented case, the patient primarily presented with pulmonary edema and after a 2D-ECHO assessment, the underlying cause of MS was revealed. Hence, this establishes the importance of 2D-ECHO for screening in patients with pulmonary edema. Mechanical ventilation was effective in maintaining the stability and oxygenation of the patient. At the same time, encountering cases of pulmonary edema due to MS beta-blockers are also known to be effective, as was also seen in our case.[9] Beta-blockers slow the adrenergic drive and concurrently the heart rate, reducing the left atrial pressure and following immediate improvement in similar cases.[9] JMS in comparison to adult MS can have some different presentations. Most frequently, stunted growth is observed in patients with JMS.[10] JMS can present with signs of congestive heart failure such as raised jugular venous pressure, hepatomegaly, and pedal edema.[10] Sinus tachycardia is frequently observed rather than atrial fibrillation which is commonly seen in adult MS.[10] However, the management of JMS is the same as that of adult MS.[10]

CONCLUSION

JMS is rarely encountered in clinical practice and can present as acute pulmonary edema. Screening young patients with pulmonary edema by 2D-ECHO is necessary to evaluate the underlying cardiac cause. BMV is the modality of choice in cases of JMS. Evaluation of the patient’s Wilkin’s score is necessary while planning BMV. Postoperative assessment and follow-up after discharge are empirical to monitor the patient.
