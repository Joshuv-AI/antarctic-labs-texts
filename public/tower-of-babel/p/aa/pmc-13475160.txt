Years of Life Lost in Incidental Prostate Cancer
Authors: Fabian Falkenbach, Mattia Longoni, Andrea Marmiroli, Quynh Chi Le, Calogero Catanzaro, Michele Nicolazzini, Federico Polverino, Jordan A. Goyal, Riccardo Schiavina, Alessandro Volpe, Gennaro Musi, Felix K. H. Chun, Alberto Briganti, Francesco Montorsi, Fred Saad, Shahrokh F. Shariat, Lars Budäus, Markus Graefen, Pierre I. Karakiewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIt is unknown how survival is prematurely curtailed by incidental prostate cancer (IPCa). We aimed to quantify the average years of life lost (YLL) due to IPCa.MethodsWithin the SEER database 2004–2021, IPCa patients aged 45–75 were identified. Stratification was based on the Gleason sum, clinical stage, and active treatment (radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy) status. Monte Carlo simulated individual survival according to the Social Security Administration life tables. Subsequently, YLL was compared between IPCa patients versus controls using the Kaplan‐Meier method.ResultsOf 7324 IPCa patients, 1926 (26.7%) and 5398 (73.7%) received active treatment or no active treatment, respectively. Compared to population controls, IPCa patients exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.23 YLL (p < 0.001). Patients without active treatment exhibited a larger survival disadvantage of 0.25 YLL, compared to patients with active treatment (0.18 YLL). In subgroup analyses, the largest YLL disadvantage was observed in patients not receiving active treatment with Gleason sum 8–10 (2.67 YLL), clinical stage T1b (0.83 YLL), and both (Gleason sum 8–10, T1b: 3.02 YLL). Conversely, in active treatment patients with Gleason sum ≤ 7 and/or T1a, no YLL disadvantage was observed.ConclusionsIPCa patients with advanced Gleason sum or clinical stage exhibited substantial YLL disadvantages, when they did not benefit from active treatment. Therefore, these patients should receive active treatment. Conversely, omitting active treatment in low‐risk IPCa patients did not impact premature death.

Introduction

The role of active treatment in incidental prostate cancer (IPCa) remains under debate [1, 2, 3, 4]. Due to its often indolent nature, no active treatment strategies such as active surveillance or watchful waiting are frequently applied to IPCa patients [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Conversely, high‐grade IPCa patients exhibited substantial survival disadvantages compared to population controls [12, 13]. However, these analyses relied primarily on elderly patients, in whom the effect of active treatment on life expectancy and premature death may be less pronounced. While active treatment is effective in delaying cancer progression, treatment toxicity may sometimes outweigh the oncological benefits, especially in elderly patients [2, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. In consequence, clinical decision‐making requires unbiased outcome measures that account for both the frequency of preventable death and the age at which it would have otherwise occurred. Years of life lost (YLL) represents an established measure for assessing how survival is curtailed prematurely [19].

We hypothesized that active treatment in IPCa patients does not exert a substantial advantage on YLL. We tested this hypothesis in a contemporary, large‐scale cohort of IPCa patients within the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (2004–2021), compared to population controls relying on SSA life tables methodology.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Within the SEER database 2004–2021, we identified individuals aged 45–75 with IPCa. According to previously established methodology, IPCa was defined as clinical stage T1a‐b prostate cancer diagnosed after transurethral resection of the prostate (TUR‐P) [10, 12, 13]. Patients who underwent TUR‐P for known cancer and autopsy‐/death‐certificate‐only cases were excluded. Moreover, patients were excluded if relevant data were missing: age at diagnosis, year of diagnosis, clinical stage, and vital status. The patients were stratified according to active treatment (radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy) status.

Statistical Analyses

First, we identified IPCa patients in the SEER database (2004–2021). Subsequently, a Monte Carlo simulation was used to generate simulated controls in a one‐to‐one fashion, which accurately reflected the age, sex, and inclusion year of each PCa patient. A Markov chain representing natural age progression was constructed for each simulated control based on SSA life tables (2004–2021) [20], as previously described [21, 22] and as endorsed by the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology (NCCN Guidelines) [1]. The follow‐up of the simulated controls reflected the actual follow‐up of IPCa patients. Specifically, patients diagnosed in 2011 had a median follow‐up period of 124 months. Therefore, the corresponding simulated controls for IPCa patients diagnosed in 2011 could either survive or die within each of the 124 simulated month intervals, according to the SSA life tables probabilities. Thus, derived life expectancy of simulated controls and the actual survival of IPCa patients were graphically depicted using Kaplan–Meier plots. The log‐rank test was used to compare survival between patients with IPCa and controls. Based on Kaplan–Meier estimates, the average YLL were estimated as the difference between the age at death of IPCa patients and the remaining life expectancy of population‐based controls until the age of 75 years (for illustration, see Figure S1). An age cut‐off of 75 years was used in accordance with the definition of premature death by the National Cancer Institute [23]. Subgroup analyses were performed according to Gleason sum (≤ 7 vs. 8–10) and clinical stage (T1a vs. T1b). The R software environment was used for statistical computing and graphics (R version 4.4.1 (2024‐06‐14), The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two‐sided with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Overall Characteristics

Of 7324 IPCa patients, 1926 (26.7%) and 5398 (73.7%) received active treatment or not, respectively. Actively treated patients were more frequently diagnosed at younger age (median, 66 vs. 68) and at a higher PSA (median, 4.5 vs. 3.2 ng/mL), compared to no active treatment counterparts. Accordingly, actively treated IPCa patients exhibited more frequently a Gleason sum of 8–10 (17.9% vs. 6.0%) and clinical stage T1b (58.4% vs. 27.0%) compared to patients without active treatment (Table 1, all p < 0.001).

Overall YLL in IPCa Patients

The estimation of YLL in IPCa patients rested on 7324 actual patients and 7324 population controls. A premature death event was recorded in 991 (13.5%) IPCa patients and 798 (10.9%) population controls (Figure 1, p < 0.001). Overall, IPCa patients exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.23 YLL (Table 2).

In subgroup analyses according to Gleason sum, IPCa patients with Gleason sum of ≤ 7 and 8–10 exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.04 and 2.00 YLL, respectively. In subgroup analyses of clinical stage, T1a patients did not exhibit a YLL disadvantage, whereas T1b patients exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.67 YLL. In the final subgroup analysis combining Gleason sum and clinical stage, a gradual increase in the YLL disadvantage was observed. Specifically, Gleason sum ≤ 7—cT1b, Gleason sum 8–10—cT1a, and Gleason sum 8–10—cT1b patients exhibited survival disadvantages of 0.23, 1.18, and 2.19 YLL, respectively. Similarly, IPCa patients with an elevated PSA > 4 ng/mL exhibited a larger survival disadvantage than their low PSA counterparts (0.36 vs. 0.13 YLL). The YLL in IPCa patients diagnosed at age ≤ 65 were higher than in older patients (0.34 vs. 0.12).

YLL in IPCa Patients With Active Treatment

The estimation of YLL in IPCa patients with active treatment rested on 1926 actual patients and 1926 population controls. A premature death event was recorded in 308 (16.0%) IPCa patients with active treatment and 282 (14.6%) population controls (p < 0.001). Overall, IPCa patients with active treatment exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.18 YLL (Table 2).

In subgroup analyses according to Gleason sum, IPCa patients with Gleason sum of ≤ 7 and active treatment did not exhibit a YLL disadvantage. Conversely, IPCa patients with Gleason sum of 8–10 and active treatment exhibited a survival disadvantage of 1.38 YLL. In subgroup analyses of clinical stage, T1a patients did not exhibit a YLL disadvantage, whereas T1b patients exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.47 YLL. In the final subgroup analysis combining Gleason sum and clinical stage, a gradual increase in the YLL disadvantage was observed. Specifically, Gleason sum ≤ 7—cT1b, Gleason sum 8–10—cT1a, and Gleason sum 8–10—cT1b IPCa patients with active treatment exhibited survival disadvantages of 0.14, 1.25, and 1.41 YLL, respectively. Similarly, the survival disadvantage was larger in actively treated IPCa patient with an increased PSA > 4 ng/mL (0.25 vs. 0.08 YLL) and patients diagnosed at age ≤ 65 (0.21 vs. 0.08 YLL).

YLL in IPCa Patients Without Active Treatment

The estimation of YLL in IPCa patients without active treatment rested on 5398 actual patients and 5398 population controls. A premature death event was recorded in 683 (12.7%) IPCa patients and 516 (9.6%) population controls (p < 0.001). Overall, IPCa patients without local treatment exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.25 YLL (Table 2).

In subgroup analyses according to Gleason sum, IPCa patients with Gleason sum of ≤ 7 and 8–10 exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.08 and 2.67 YLL, respectively. In subgroup analyses of clinical stage, IPCa patients with cT1a and cT1b exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.03 and 0.83 YLL, respectively. In the final subgroup analysis combining Gleason sum and clinical stage, a gradual increase in the YLL disadvantage was observed. Specifically, patients with Gleason sum ≤ 7—cT1a, Gleason sum ≤ 7—cT1b, Gleason sum 8–10—cT1a, and Gleason sum 8–10—cT1b IPCa exhibited survival disadvantages of 0.02, 0.31, 1.11, and 3.02 YLL, respectively. Similarly, the survival disadvantage was larger in not actively treated IPCa patient with an increased PSA > 4 ng/mL (0.41 vs. 0.14 YLL) and diagnosed at age ≤ 65 (0.46 vs. 0.13 YLL).

Discussion

The role of active treatment in IPCa remains under debate. We hypothesized that we would not capture YLL differences within controlled comparisons of IPCa patients with and without active treatment. The current analysis revealed several noteworthy findings.

First, the total number of deaths or survival months after diagnosis does not provide a good metric for quantifying differences in disease burden because such measures assign the same weight to death at age 80 as they do at age 50. This consideration is especially pertinent when patients are most frequently diagnosed at an advanced age. For example, Di Bello et al. reported a median age of 78 for high‐risk IPCa patients [12]. In fact, previous reports suggested that the risk of meaningful cancer progression of IPCa is virtually zero, if the patient's life expectancy is < 10 years and competing risk are accounted for [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 24]. To overcome this limitation of previous reports, we relied on the established framework of YLL. The YLL metric has repeatedly been used in official documentation by health agencies such as the International Agency for Research on Cancer of the World Health Organization [25, 26] and the National Cancer Institute [27]. Moreover, YLL has been applied to quantify the numerical extent to which prostate cancer had prematurely curtailed survival within entire populations [28, 29, 30]. However, no report has assessed the effects of IPCa and active treatment on YLL. Therefore, the current analysis provided novel insights into the prognosis and treatment management of IPCa in an unbiased fashion.

Second, this novel methodology allowed us to ascertain specific YLL estimates for IPCa patients. IPCa patients exhibited an overall survival disadvantage of 0.23 YLL. In subgroup analyses addressing clinical stage, Gleason sum, and its combination, larger survival disadvantages were observed for patients with more adverse cancer characteristics. Specifically, IPCa patients with Gleason sum 8–10 and cT1b exhibited a survival disadvantage of 2.00 and 0.67 YLL, respectively. These observations, which are based on the YLL methodology for the first time, are in perfect agreement with previous reports that have relied on other metrics [10, 12, 13]. For example, Scheipner et al. reported the highest cancer‐specific mortality in the subgroup of patients with a Gleason sum 8–10 and cT1b [10]. This represents an internal validation of the methodology and data used in the current study.

Third, our methodology provided specific YLL estimates and differences between patients with and without active treatment. Active treatment was associated with lower detrimental effects on the YLL in all subgroup comparisons. The YLL differences between patients with and without active treatment were largest in patients with adverse cancer characteristics. Specifically, patients with Gleason sum 8–10 exhibited a 1.9‐fold larger survival disadvantage measured in YLL, when no active treatment was administered (1.38 vs. 2.67 YLL). Similarly, patients with clinical stage T1b exhibited a 1.8‐fold larger survival disadvantage measured in YLL, when no active treatment was administered (0.47 vs. 0.83 YLL). Finally, patients with Gleason sum of 8–10 and clinical stage T1b exhibited a 2.1‐fold larger survival disadvantage measured in YLL, when no active treatment was administered (1.41 vs. 3.02 YLL). Similiar observations were made regarding elevated PSA and younger age at diagnosis. These differences agree with previous reports and clinical practice that high‐grade patients benefit from active treatment for IPCa [10, 12, 13]. For example, Di Bello et al. reported an absolute overall survival disadvantage of 26% at 9 years between high‐grade IPCa patients with and without active treatment [12]. Because long‐term survival of actively treated PCa patients is often superior to the general population [31], our analysis might underestimate the true detrimental effect of active treatment omission.

Fourth, IPCa patients with favorable cancer characteristics, such as Gleason sum ≤ 7 and cT1a, did not exhibit clinically meaningful YLL disadvantages. Specifically, IPCa patients with Gleason sum ≤ 7 exhibited a survival disadvantage of 0.04 YLL and IPCa patients with cT1a outlived their population controls by 0.01 years. After stratification by active treatment status, no meaningful YLL disadvantages were seen in favorable IPCa patients. Therefore, risk grouping by Gleason sum and clinical stage displayed a strong ability to discriminate survival differences in IPCa patients measured in YLL. These findings align well with previous reports validating cT1a versus cT1b substages as well as high‐grade Gleason grouping in IPCa [10, 12]. This represents another internal validation of our used data and methodology.

Despite its novelty several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, the SEER database does capture information regarding confirmatory prostate biopsy after TUR‐P, prostate volume, PSA dynamics, or PSA‐density. Moreover, information on disease extent and staging is also not available. This information is critical for clinical IPCa management, and its absence should be critically appraised, when our data are applied in a clinical context. Second, the current analysis shares the limitations of all similar studies that were based on SEER databases and used a retrospective data design. Third, information on comorbidities and exact disease extent were not available, which might have influenced treatment decisions and life expectancy as well. While SSA controls provide robust averages for health economics and outcomes research, clinical interpretations should be drawn with some caution. In fact, the provided YLL estimates underestimate the disease burden in very fit elderly individuals, who might otherwise outlive the premature death definition by far. Fourth, further research is necessary to identify IPCa patients, such as non‐Hispanic Black individuals or rare histological subtypes [32, 33], that are at increased risk for adverse survival outcomes. Fifth, the SSA and SEER are limited to individuals in the United States of America. The displayed effect may vary in other healthcare systems.

Conclusions

The current study used a novel framework to estimate YLL differences in IPCa patients. Overall, IPCa was associated with a survival disadvantage of 0.23 YLL. No clinical meaningful YLL disadvantage was observed in IPCa patients with Gleason sum ≤ 7 or clinical stage T1. Conversely, the most substantial YLL disadvantage was observed in not actively treated IPCa patients with Gleason sum 8–10 (2.67 YLL), cT1b (0.83 YLL), or both (3.02 YLL). Therefore, IPCa patients with adverse cancer characteristics represent targets for which no active treatment strategies should be avoided.
