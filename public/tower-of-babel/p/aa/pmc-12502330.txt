Development and optimization of disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards: a Delphi study
Authors: Tingting Li, Huilan Zhang, Hongxia Luo, Kunmei Li, Niyan Huang, Sha Yang, Jianping You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNegative pressure wards are used to admit patients with respiratory infectious diseases. Most of the negative pressure wards are currently reconstructed, and the standard disinfection protocol has not yet been established.MethodsIn this study, we adopted the Delphi method to develop a disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards. First, we constructed a draft disinfection protocol based on literature analysis, theoretical analysis and investigation of the status quo. Then, we conducted two rounds of consultation through letters with fifteen experts in the management and disinfection of negative pressure wards from 6 provinces and cities around China.ResultsThe final disinfection protocol of negative pressure wards contains 8 primary indicators, including air disinfection, surface disinfection, disinfection management of special hardware facilities, fabric disinfection, disinfection of secretions/excretions/vomitus, monitoring of disinfection effectiveness, management of cleaning and disinfection tools, and management of medical waste, along with 20 secondary indicators and 46 tertiary indicators.ConclusionsThe disinfection protocol for the negative pressure wards developed in this study is scientifically sound and highly operable, providing a reference for medical institutions equipped with negative pressure wards.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11659-y.Keywords: Delphi method, Infectious diseases, Negative pressure wards, Disinfection, Infection control

Background

In recent years, emerging infectious diseases with unknown nature and complexity, along with the general susceptibility of the population, have been threatening human health [1, 2]. In autumn and winter, particularly during the Corona Virus Disease 2019(COVID-19) pandemic in 2019, respiratory infectious diseases have placed immense pressure on healthcare systems [3, 4]. Negative pressure wards, as essential infrastructures for isolating patients with respiratory infections and emerging infectious diseases, play a crucial role in responding to these outbreaks. They are also indispensable basic facilities in various comprehensive (specialized) medical institutions.

A negative pressure ward utilizes a ventilation system that directs the airflow from clean areas to contaminated areas, ensuring that the air pressure within the ward is lower than that in adjacent connected regions [5, 6]. This design prevents the outward spread of pathogenic microorganisms. The primary differences between negative pressure wards and standard infectious disease wards lie in the specific direction of airflow, differing ward facilities, and the types of patients treated. It is precisely due to these differences that the disinfection and infection control measures in negative pressure wards differ from those in standard infectious disease wards [7–10]. Implementing standardized and precise disinfection protocol in negative pressure wards can significantly enhance the infection control capabilities of medical institutions in response to emerging infectious diseases, thus effectively protecting HCWs (healthcare workers) and patients by interrupting transmission routes, and preventing the spread of contagious diseases.

Currently, the development levels of negative pressure isolation wards vary globally and few studies have reported on their disinfection in negative pressure wards. With the advancement of computational fluid dynamics (CFD), the study of the relationship between direction of airflow and aerosol concentration in negative pressure wards has become a hot topic in the field of architecture, providing an objective reference for disinfection efforts in these wards [11–14]. Studies have emphasized the importance of optimizing the design of ventilation systems in negative pressure wards, where appropriate air exchange rates and the positioning of exhaust outlets can dilute the concentration of contaminants and reduce the risk of infection [15–17]. Following the global impact of various emerging respiratory infectious diseases, some scholars have conducted preliminary discussions on negative pressure wards within the context of disinfection and infection control. Most studies have focused on the disinfection of the surfaces and the overall management of negative pressure wards, lacking targeted and systematically complete disinfection protocol [7, 18–20]. A comprehensive and standardized disinfection protocol is yet to be established [7, 18].

The current study shows that the research on the disinfection of negative pressure wards is single, only the disinfection measures for air or surface, and there is no disinfection measure for all elements of negative pressure wards, also did not involve the negative pressure ward special hardware facilities, especially the maintenance of filtration system disinfection management. Therefore, this study seeks to address this gap by employing the Delphi method to establish a scientific and systematic disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards. By doing so, it could offer evidence-based infection control strategies to bolster the emergency response capabilities of healthcare facilities and effectively manage emerging infectious disease outbreaks.

Methods

Study design

The Delphi method refers to a process in which researchers draft a questionnaire, send it to experts, and facilitate communication among the experts through anonymous, back-to-back exchanges of opinions. After several rounds of feedback, the experts’ opinions gradually converge, ultimately yielding statistically significant results. Because of its advantages of anonymity, strong professionalism, and ease of statistical analysis, this method has been widely applied in fields such as education, transportation and economic budgeting, and its development in the nursing field has also been recognized [21–23]. This research explored the indicator system for disinfection protocol in negative pressure wards through the use of the Delphi expert consultation method, with a research report on the Delphi method serving to guide the entire research process.

The research team consisted of one chief nurse, two associate chief nurses and two supervisor nurses, and its main tasks included literature search, status survey, questionnaire preparation and distribution, data collection and collation and statistical analysis.

Inclusion criteria

This study included experts in the fields of infection control, infectious disease nursing management, and preventive medicine from the CNKI and Wanfang databases (Through research, we found that certain nursing staff were involved in or hold part-time positions in hospital infection control. They had rich learning experiences and infection prevention and control expertise in dealing with emerging infectious diseases) [24]. The specific inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Work experience: a minimum of 10 years of experience in the fields of infection control, infectious disease nursing management, or preventive medicine; or multiple experiences in executing major infection control tasks; (b) Professional title: associate senior or above; (c) Education: bachelor’s degree or above; (d) Familiarity with the topic of inquiry: disinfection of negative pressure wards; (e) Willingness to participate in two rounds of expert inquiry research.

Draft of the expert consultation questionnaire

We searched the literature and reviewed national standards on disinfection in medical institutions and the use of negative pressure wards. Additionally, a survey was conducted on the current status quo of medical institutions with negative pressure wards. The survey results indicated certain commonalities in the disinfection hardware configuration and management across various medical institutions. The consultation questionnaire mainly consists of three parts: Part one was an introduction that includes the research background, significance, methodology, and instructions for filling out the form. Part two was the contents of the consultation questionnaire, which covers various aspects such as air/surface/fabric/waste disinfection, management of special hardware facilities, disinfection precautions, management of disinfection tools, and monitoring of disinfection effectiveness, employing a Likert 5-point scale ranging from “not important at all” to “very important”:1 = completely unimportant, 2 = unimportant, 3 = general, 4 = important, 5 = very important, assigning values from 1 to 5, with additional space for experts to provide professional opinions [25, 26]. The last part was the basic information about the experts, including age, education, specialty, years of work experience, and familiarity with the research and basis for judgment.

Delphi consulting and feedback cycle

We distributed two rounds of expert consultation questionnaires from August to November 2023, using WeChat or email. The first round of questionnaires was constructed based on literature research and expert consultation. After the first round of inquiries, the team members discussed and analyzed the collected data, and made corresponding modifications based on the experts’ feedback to create the second round of questionnaires(See Questionnaire 1 and Questionnaire 2 in the supplementary file for details). After two rounds of inquiries, the index was included when mean value of importance score > 4.0 and coefficient of variation < 0.25. If the importance score was between 3.5 and 4.0, we needed to discuss and decide whether the indicator items should be retained or deleted [27, 28]. Finally, we further discussed and refined the disinfection protocol of negative pressure wards.

Quality control

The experts were selected strictly according to the research protocol established for this study, taking into account their experience in infection Control, authority, and academic level. We primarily included 15 experts from six cities: Beijing, Guangzhou, Chongqing, Fuzhou, Xi’an, and Zunyi. After the inquiry, two individuals simultaneously coded and entered the data, promptly contacting the experts to confirm and correct any questionable or missing data. We discussed the textual opinions provided by the expert item by item, aiming to quickly formulate the second round of inquiry forms, and ultimately conducted a comprehensive analysis of the data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was conducted using SPSS 26.0. Categorical data were described using frequency and proportion, while continuous data using x ± s. The coefficient of variation (CV) and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W) were used to test the degree of consensus among experts. The expert engagement coefficient was calculated based on the effective response rate of the questionnaire. In general, the coefficient of experts greater than 85% means that the experts have positive feedback on this study. The authority coefficient was calculated based on the experts’ judgment criteria and familiarity [29, 30].

In the first round, we merged 5 indexes, revised 18 indexes, deleted 7 indexes. In the second round, we the research team added 1 index, revised 8 indexes, deleted 1 index. Discussion

In recent years, research on infection control in the environment of negative pressure wards has garnered increasing attention. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, studies on negative pressure wards primarily focused on aspects of airflow organization. During the pandemic, environmental disinfection in negative pressure wards also became a focus of scholarly research, often concentrating on specific parts of the ward environment. While these studies provide valuable references, they have not yet established a comprehensive and systematic disinfection framework [7, 14, 31, 32].It is worth emphasizing that air quality control in negative pressure wards is a critical aspect. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) in the United States has developed a ventilation headboard, which enhances hospitals’ isolation capabilities in response to new and emerging infectious diseases. This innovation not only isolates patients but also protects healthcare workers from airborne infectious diseases [33].In the post-pandemic era of the COVID-19, Lidia Morawska and others have emphasized the critical importance of effectively ensuring indoor air quality to combat various infectious diseases. One key insight is that mechanical designs equipped with filtration systems and ultraviolet air disinfection units can, to a certain extent, guarantee the quality of indoor air. This has provided inspiration for the air quality control in negative pressure wards [34].

Therefore, based on the current status of infection Control in negative pressure ward environments, we completed two rounds of online expert consultations and developed a scientifically reliable and clinically applicable disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards. For details, please refer to Appendices 1–3.

The disinfection protocol is scientific and reliable

The initial draft of the disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards was developed by reviewing literature and national standards, as well as conducting a cross-sectional investigation. Subsequently, a comprehensive and standardized disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards was constructed by combining the results of two rounds of expert letters. The entire research process was rigorous and true, which indicates that the disinfection protocol developed here is scientific. The 15 experts who participated in this study all had solid theoretical knowledge, rich experience of clinical practice in emerging infectious diseases Control. The positive coefficient of the experts in the two rounds of inquiry was 100%, which indicates that the experts’ enthusiasm was high. The authoritative coefficient of the experts in the two rounds of inquiry was 0.91, which was higher than 0.70, indicating that the experts’ authority was high and the results of the inquiry were reliable [35]. Moreover, the Kendall’s W of the experts in the second round (0.178) was higher than that in the first round (0.157), indicating that the opinions of the experts after two rounds of consultation were more unified, and the results of the expert letters obtained were more credible [36].

The disinfection protocol is closely aligned with clinical practice and has strong practical applicability

The World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines emphasizes that a clean ward environment is central to the Control of infections in hospitals. Studies have shown that despite the current control measures, the burden of health-care-related infections remains high in Europe, causing about 37,000 deaths each year [37, 38]. Therefore, as an important role in the hospital infection prevention and control, the disinfection of negative pressure wards requires a standard guiding protocol. At present, the disinfection standards of various countries can provide a certain reference for the disinfection of negative pressure wards. However, due to the differences between the patients admitted and the special hardware facilities, as well as the lack of systematic and comprehensive studies, a closed-loop protocol for the disinfection of negative pressure wards has not been formed, and the clinical disinfection work cannot be effectively guided [5, 6, 39, 40]. Based on the results of the present investigation, we sorted out the misunderstandings and loopholes of disinfection operations of medical staff. In conjunction with the current national standards and, a comprehensive disinfection management strategy has been clearly established, which includes disinfection measures, effect evaluation methods, cleaning and disinfection tool management methods and disinfection precautions. The content involved is thorough and complete, providing significant practical reference for the disinfection work in negative pressure wards.

The disinfection protocol highlights the importance in standard guidance

Dealing with emerging infectious diseases is always a challenge faced by all human beings. It is reported that the establishment of a negative pressure ward is an effective method to improve the first-aid ability of the hospital. However, the risk of nosocomial infection in negative pressure wards is higher than in general wards. Therefore, the quality of disinfection, as the primary influencing factor, plays an important role in this process, and the protocol of disinfection control needs to be updated and perfected constantly [41–46]. Most of the negative pressure wards at home and abroad were hastily established during the outbreak of various emerging infectious diseases, and were left in idle. In addition, we conducted a field study at a number of medical institutions in Chongqing that have negative pressure wards. The heads of these institutions stated that since there was no standard instruction, most of the staff were inexperienced in using and managing the negative pressure wards, usually leading to an increased concentration of disinfectant in the process of disinfection [47, 48]. Therefore, it is necessary to establish a standard disinfection protocol for the HCWs working in negative pressure wards.

Implementation and reflection

Countries like the United States and the United Kingdom initiated the establishment of negative pressure wards in the 1940 s to curb tuberculosis transmission. In China, the development of Such wards commenced post the 2003 SARS outbreak, with significant expansion and upgrades occurring in response to the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 [49, 50]. In the post-pandemic era, respiratory infectious diseases are prevalent in autumn and winter, coupled with the frequent outbreaks of diseases with unknown modes of transmission. How to effectively utilize existing resources for the treatment of various respiratory infectious diseases is a key focus for actively responding to emerging infectious diseases in the future, highlighting the importance of negative pressure wards. We need to update our disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards, so as to prepare in advance, strengthen the defenses of disinfection and infection control, actively prevent the spread of respiratory infectious diseases, and strictly guard against hospital-acquired infections. At the same time, based on the results of the current status survey, the main personnel for the implementation of this plan are the nursing staff. Therefore, systematic training for nursing staff is required to ensure the effectiveness of disinfection, and to achieve a perfect alignment between training, implementation, and effect evaluation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small during the current situation survey phase. Patients should be included in the scope of the current situation survey, and the user-friendliness of disinfection measures should be incorporated into the plan. Second, due to time constraints, the effectiveness of the disinfection protocol developed here has not yet been proved through empirical studies. The next step for us is to validate the disinfection protocol in healthcare facilities with negative pressure wards, further evaluating the effectiveness of the protocol.

Conclusion

A disinfection protocol for negative pressure wards was developed by means of Delphi expert consultation. The protocol includes air disinfection, surface disinfection, special hardware facilities disinfection, fabric disinfection, disinfection tools management and other aspects of the infection control measures, which can provide reference for medical staff in medical institutions with negative pressure wards.
