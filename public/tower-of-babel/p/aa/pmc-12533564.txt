Palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC19 regulates histone-to-protamine exchange during spermiogenesis in mice
Authors: Jiaoyang Li, Zhizhou Xia, Bo Jiao, Zhitong Li, Donghe Li, Pengfei Xu, Yi Huang, Jiawei Nie, Yuqing Dan, Xu Huang, Lei Yan, Rui Zhang, Wei Huang, Xinru Wang, Shiyu Ji, Mofang Liu, Ping Liu, Ruibao Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spermiogenesis, the final phrase of spermatogenesis, involves replacing histones by protamines, a process known as histone-to-protamine exchange, which is crucial for chromatin condensation. While this exchange is essential, the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In this study, we discovered that the palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC19 functions as a key regulator of mouse spermiogenesis. Loss of Zdhhc19 leads to male infertility and abnormal sperm morphology. Zdhhc19-deficient sperm exhibit impaired histone-to-protamine exchange, leading to retention of histones and misdistribution of protamines. Similarly, the palmitoyltransferase catalytic site mutant Zdhhc19 C142S knock-in mice show reduced fertility, sperm abnormalities, and histone retention. Mechanistically, ZDHHC19 mediates histone H3 palmitoylation at cysteine-110, weakening H3-H4 interactions and increasing chromatin accessibility. Palmitoylation of H3 facilitates histone-to-protamine exchange. This study highlights the essential role of ZDHHC19’s palmitoyl-transferase activity in histone-to-protamine exchange during spermiogenesis and underscores the broader role of histone palmitoylation in chromatin remodeling.

INTRODUCTION

Spermatogenesis is a precisely regulated process that includes mitosis, meiosis, and spermiogenesis. During spermiogenesis, postmeiotic round spermatids undergo chromatin condensation, acrosome formation, flagellum extension, and cytoplasm exclusion, ultimately maturing into spermatozoa (1–4). Chromatin condensation, driven by histone-to-protamine (PRM) exchange, represents the highest level of chromatin compaction in eukaryotes. Defects in this exchange disrupt chromatin condensation and sperm motility, often resulting in male infertility (5). Recent studies have revealed that histone-to-PRM exchange involves a dynamic process where transition proteins (TNPs) and PRMs simultaneously access the DNA along with certain histone variants. During this process, TNPs facilitate PRM packaging before being eventually removed (6–10). However, the precise mechanisms regulating this exchange remain unclear (11). Through this exchange, PRMs substitute for 90 to 95% histones to protect the genome, while specific regions, like promoters and repetitive regions, retain histones nucleosomes (12).

Posttranslational modifications (PTMs) of core histones have been shown to contribute to chromatin decondensation in spermatids, weakening histone-DNA interactions and allowing for TNP and PRM incorporation (6, 13). Gene-editing studies in mice have identified several proteins involved in spermatids chromatin compaction through histone PTMs. For instance, chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein 5 (CHD5) and nuclear protein in testis (NUT) facilitate histone-to-PRM exchange by promoting H4 hyperacetylation (14–16). The MIWI-RNF8 complex dissociates in cytoplasm of elongating spermatids, allowing RNF8 to enter the nucleus and ubiquitylate histone H2A and H2AX, triggering histone removal. Mutations in Miwi or deficiency in Rnf8 disrupt histone ubiquitination, leading to incomplete histone removal and male infertility (17–19). PHD finger protein 7 (PHF7) functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase for histones H2A and H3, playing an essential role in sperm chromatin condensation (20, 21). Acetylation of H4 and ubiquitination of H2A, H2B, and H3 are early events in histone removal, while phosphorylation and acetylation of PRMs are required for PRM-DNA binding in sperm (22, 23).

Reversible palmitoylation, another type of PTMs, is catalyzed by enzymes from the zinc-finger DHHC domain-containing protein (ZDHHC) family and is known to regulate protein localization, stability, and function (24–26). While most research works on the palmitoylation have focused on its roles in cancer, immunity, and neurology, its potential involvement in reproduction and development has only recently garnered attention. For instance, a study identified 4883 palmitoylated proteins in the mouse testis, revealing their involvement in various biological processes such as protein transport and cell adhesion (27). Among the ZDHHC family members, ZDHHC19 has been showed to mediate the palmitoylation of R-ras, Smad3, and Flotillin-1 in several human tumor cell lines (28–30). We found that male mice lacking Zdhhc19 are infertile, which were also reported by other two groups (31, 32). But the exact role of Zdhhc19 in spermatogenesis was unclear.

In this study, we confirmed that Zdhhc19 deficiency in germ cells but not in Sertoli cells leads to male infertility. Zdhhc19−/− sperm exhibit distinct morphological defects, including a characteristic “head-base bending,” along with impaired histone-to-PRM exchange. We observed similar defects in the palmitoyltransferase catalytic site mutant Zdhhc19 C142S knock-in mice. We identified histone H3 as a substrate of ZDHHC19 and confirm that palmitoylation occurs at cysteine-110. The palmitoylation reduces H3-H4 interaction and increase chromatin accessibility, facilitating histone removal and the subsequent incorporation of PRMs. These findings reveal a crucial role for ZDHHC19-mediated H3 palmitoylation in regulating histone-to-PRM exchange during spermiogenesis, providing previously unknown insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying male fertility.

RESULTS

Zdhhc19 knockout in germ cell impairs spermatogenesis

In adult mice, we observed that Zdhhc19 is predominantly expressed in the testis (Fig. 1A), whereas its expression is negligible in other tissues. In human, a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of testicular cells revealed that ZDHHC19 gene is specifically expressed in haploid spermatid but absent in spermatogonia, spermatocyte, and Sertoli cell (33). Through fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) of mouse testicular cells, we further demonstrated that Zdhhc19 is predominantly expressed in haploid spermatids compared to that in spermatocytes (fig. S1A). Additionally, Sertoli cell isolation assays confirmed no expression of Zdhhc19 in isolated Sertoli cell populations (fig. S1B). This prompted us to explore the role of Zdhhc19 in spermatogenesis and determine whether its function is specific to germ cells. To this end, we generated a Zdhhc19 conditional knockout mouse model using Cas9 embryo gene editing (fig. S1C). We then crossed these mice with EIIA-cre [constitutive knockout (−/−)], Amh-cre [Sertoli cell–specific knockout (SerKO)] (34), and Tnap-cre [spermatogenic cell–specific knockout (SpKO)] (35), producing three distinct genotypes: Zdhhc19−/−, Zdhhc19SerKO, and Zdhhc19SpKO, respectively (fig. S1D). In Zdhhc19SerKO mice, the protein level of ZDHHC19 in the testis was comparable to that of Zdhhc19+/+, while in Zdhhc19SpKO mice, ZDHHC19 protein was absent, as in Zdhhc19−/− mice (Fig. 1B).

To access the role of Zdhhc19 in male fertility, we bred male mice of different genotypes with wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J females and tracked the presence of mating plugs and the conception rate. Consistent with the protein expression data, Zdhhc19SerKO males remained fertile, while both Zdhhc19−/− and Zdhhc19SpKO males were completely infertile (Fig. 1C). To determine the developmental stage Zdhhc19 deficiency impacts upon, we performed zona-intact and zona-free in vitro fertilization (IVF) tests and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). The IVF results showed that, regardless of the integrity of the egg’s zona pellucida, sperm from Zdhhc19−/− male mice were unable to fertilize the eggs through IVF (fig. S1E). In ICSI, the blastocyst rate from Zdhhc19−/− sperm (71.1%, 32 of 45) is significantly lower than that from Zdhhc19+/+ sperm (92.9%, 39 of 42) (fig. S1, F and G). Despite the defect in blastocyst rate, a separate live birth analysis transferring Zdhhc19−/− sperm derived two-cell embryos produced viable heterozygous offspring (fig. S1H). These results confirm that, despite the defects, sperm from Zdhhc19−/− male mice can support fertilization and development via ICSI.

Upon examination of sperm morphology using Diff-Quick staining, we found that sperm from Zdhhc19−/− and Zdhhc19SpKO displayed a “bent” deformity (Fig. 1D), with more than 95% of sperm being abnormal, compared to only 2% in Zdhhc19+/+ and 15% in Zdhhc19SerKO (Fig. 1E). This deformity was absent in Zdhhc19+/− mice, indicating that a single functional copy of Zdhhc19 is sufficient for normal spermatogenesis. These results demonstrate that loss of Zdhhc19 in germ cell leads to male infertility and severe sperm abnormality, while Zdhhc19 function in Sertoli cell is not essential for fertility.

Loss of Zdhhc19 impairs spermiogenesis by disrupting chromatin condensation

We examined the overall testis phenotypes and found no obvious morphology differences between Zdhhc19−/− mice and Zdhhc19+/+ mice, despite a slight reduction in the testis/body weight ratio in Zdhhc19−/− mice (fig. S2A). Hematoxylin and eosin staining and comparative examination of seminiferous tubule stages between Zdhhc19+/+ and Zdhhc19−/− testes revealed no significant difference in the proportion of tubules in different stages (fig. S2, B and C). Within the testis, analysis of different stage spermatogenic cell populations during FACS showed comparable distribution of these cells between the two groups (fig. S2D). These findings demonstrated that Zdhhc19 deficiency does not cause spermatogenic arrest or sperm developmental delay. Later, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining detected increased apoptotic signal in Zdhhc19−/− late-stage spermatids compared to that in Zdhhc19+/+ (fig. S2E). Consistent with these observations, histological analysis of epididymis sections and computer-assisted semen analysis (CASA) revealed a slight reduced sperm count in the epididymis cauda of Zdhhc19−/− mice compared to that of Zdhhc19+/+ controls (fig. S2, F and G). Furthermore, Zdhhc19−/− sperm exhibited reduced motility, particularly in progressive motility, as measured by CASA (fig. S2G). These findings suggest that loss of Zdhhc19 impairs spermiogenesis after miosis, rather than disrupting the earlier stages of spermatogenesis.

Previous studies have identified two closely similar defects of such bent sperm: head-base bending, which is related to incomplete chromatin condensation, and disjointed sperm, associated with abnormalities in the neck region (36). Notably, both male infertility and the head-base bending phenotypes have been reported in mice with knocked out genes involved in chromatin condensation such as Prm1 (9), Prm2 (37), Tnp1 (38), and Tnp2 (39), as well as genes that that regulate the chromatin condensation process, including Chd5 (15), Phf7 (20, 21), Rnf8 (19), and Nut (16). To clarify the specific defect in Zdhhc19−/− sperm, we tracked the midpiece by staining mitochondrion sheaths. Our analysis revealed that most of the Zdhhc19−/− sperm exhibited a 180° bend at the base of the nucleus (Fig. 2A), causing the head and midpiece to be closely positioned. The continuity of the midpiece, without disconnection between head and tail, indicated the head-base bending deformity in Zdhhc19−/− sperm.

To assess the chromatin condensation status in Zdhhc19−/− sperm, we performed both scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Scanning electron microscopy analysis revealed the head-base bending deformity in Zdhhc19−/− sperm (Fig. 2B). TEM showed that, while the nuclei of Zdhhc19+/+ sperm were uniformly dense, the nuclei of Zdhhc19−/− sperm were less condensed, displaying uneven density and the presence of vacuoles. Additionally, microtubules and mitochondrion sheath were observed surrounding the nuclei in Zdhhc19−/− sperm (Fig. 2C). Consistent with these findings, a sperm chromatin structure assay (SCSA), which measures chromatin condensation, revealed that the DNA fragmentation index (DFI) was ~5 times higher in Zdhhc19−/− sperm compared to that in Zdhhc19+/+ sperm (Fig. 2D). These results demonstrate that Zdhhc19 knockout impairs chromatin condensation during spermiogenesis.

Zdhhc19 deletion disrupts histone-to-PRM exchange in sperm

In elongating spermatids, histones are replaced by smaller PRMs with the help of TNPs to facilitate chromatin condensation (6, 40). Because less condensed chromatin has been associated with abnormal levels of histones and PRMs in sperm (36), we examined these proteins in Zdhhc19−/− mature sperm. Immunocytochemistry analysis of mature sperm revealed obviously higher signals for histone H2B and H3, as well as elevated TNP2 signal in the nuclei of Zdhhc19−/− mature sperm compared to that of Zdhhc19+/+ (Fig. 3A). Lower-magnification views of H2B and PRM2 staining with quantitative analysis showed the malformed sperm were about 80% in Zdhhc19−/− mice (fig. S3A).

(B) Representative confocal histological section images of Zdhhc19+/+ and Zdhhc19−/− epididymis cauda using anti-PRM1 and anti-PRM2 antibodies (red). (C) Immunoblot of core histones, TNPs, and PRMs from Zdhhc19+/+ and Zdhhc19−/− mature sperm.

Furthermore, immunostaining showed disordered localization of PRM1 and PRM2 in Zdhhc19−/− sperm (Fig. 3B). In Zdhhc19+/+ sperm, PRM1 and PRM2 were uniformly and compactly distributed within the nucleus. However, in Zdhhc19−/− sperm, PRM1 and PRM2 were either distribution around the nucleus or showed excessive aggregation outside the nucleus. These findings indicate abnormal histone retention and improper PRM incorporation in Zdhhc19−/− sperm.

Consistent with the results of immunocytochemistry, immunoblotting revealed significantly higher levels of core histones, PRM1 and PRM2 in Zdhhc19−/− mature sperm, along with the retention of TNP1 and TNP2 (Fig. 3C and fig. S3B). Notably, we also detected a pre-PRM2 band in Zdhhc19−/− sperm, which was absent in Zdhhc19+/+ mature sperm. PRM2 is initially synthesized as a precursor protein, which undergoes cleavage to form the mature PRM2 protein (8, 41). These findings suggest that PRM2 processing is also disrupted in Zdhhc19−/− sperm. Later, cell fractionation revealed that, in Zdhhc19−/− sperm, PRM1 was present in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, PRM2 was primarily localized in the nucleus, while pre-PRM2 was mainly detected in the cytoplasm (fig. S3C). Together, these results demonstrated that loss of Zdhhc19 impairs histone-to-PRM exchange during spermiogenesis, affecting chromatin condensation.

The PAT activity of ZDHHC19 is crucial for spermiogenesis

Palmitoyl-acyltransferase (PAT) family members require the cysteine residue within the DHHC domain for autopalmitoylation, which creates a palmitoylated intermediate that transfers the palmitate to the substrate’s cysteine (42, 43). Mutation of this cysteine in the DHHC motif completely blocks autopalmitoylation, effectively impairing the activity of human ZDHHC20 and zebrafish ZDHHC15 in an in vitro assay (42). To investigate whether the PAT activity of ZDHHC19 is necessary for normal spermiogenesis, we generated a knock-in mouse model carrying a PAT-impaired mutant protein, ZDHHC19C142S (Fig. 4A). We observed a slight reduction of Zdhhc19 transcription but no obvious change of ZDHHC19 protein levels in Zdhhc19C142S/C142S testes compared to those in Zdhhc19+/+ (Fig. 4B). However, palmitoylation level of ZDHHC19 detected in Zdhhc19C142S/C142S testes was much lower than that in Zdhhc19+/+, confirming the impaired PAT activity (Fig. 4C).

This C142S mutation severely impaired male fertility in mice, as Zdhhc19C142S/C142S males exhibited a lower pregnancy rate and a significantly reduced number of pups per litter when mated with WT C57BL/6J females (Fig. 4D). Through further examination of sperm from the epididymis cauda of Zdhhc19C142S/C142S mice, we observed that about 56% of the sperm displayed the characteristic head-base bending deformity (Fig. 4E), partially mimicking the phenotype displayed in Zdhhc19 knockout mice. Additionally, CASA revealed reduced total and progressive motility, along with decreased velocity in Zdhhc19C142S/C142S sperm (Fig. 4F). Further, immunoblotting revealed that the protein levels of core histones, TNP1, and PRMs in Zdhhc19C142S/C142S sperm were significantly higher than Zdhhc19+/+ (Fig. 4G and fig. S3D). Immunostaining displayed the retention of H2B and localization defect of PRM1 in Zdhhc19C142S/C142S sperm (Fig. 4H). These results indicate that ZDHHC19’s PAT activity is crucial for normal spermiogenesis and male fertility.

ZDHHC19 specifically catalyzes palmitoylation of histone H3 at cysteine-110

Because ZDHHC19 regulates histone-to-PRM exchange during spermiogenesis through its PAT activity in vivo, we sought to identify its potential substrates by analyzing palmitoylated proteins in haploid spermatids, where the exchange process occurs, from Zdhhc19+/+ and Zdhhc19−/− testes. Using mass spectrometry, we conducted three experiments and identified 18 proteins with significantly reduced palmitoylation levels in Zdhhc19−/− testes (fig. S4A and table S1). Among these proteins, H3.3, a variant of histone H3 encoded by H3f3a and H3f3c, stood out because of previous studies reporting that loss of H3f3a or H3f3c leads to incomplete chromatin condensation and male infertility (44–46).

Previous studies have reported that H3 variants (H3.1, H3.2, and H3.3) undergo palmitoylation at the conserved residue cysteine-110 (47), with H3.3 being the most abundant H3 variant in the testis (48). Immunoblotting confirmed that the palmitoylation levels of H3 were significantly reduced in Zdhhc19−/− and Zdhhc19C142S/C142S testes, compared to those in Zdhhc19+/+ mice (Fig. 5, A and B). This indicates that ZDHHC19 mediates H3 palmitoylation. Additionally, we observed no differences in the palmitoylation levels of other histones, TNPs, and PRMs (H2A, H2B, TNP2, PRM1, or PRM2) between Zdhhc19+/+ and Zdhhc19−/− testes (Fig. 5C).

To investigate whether ZDHHC19 catalyzes H3 palmitoylation in other systems, we introduced ZDHHC19WT or ZDHHC19C142S proteins to GC1 cells (a cell line derived from mouse spermatogonia) and 3T3 cells (a cell line derived from mouse embryonic fibroblast), neither of which naturally express Zdhhc19. We found that ectopic overexpression of ZDHHC19WT but not ZDHHC19C142S, obviously increased the palmitoylation levels of endogenous H3 in both cell lines (Fig. 5, D and E).

Next, we aimed to validate the site of ZDHHC19-mediated H3 palmitoylation. Given that H3.1 contains two cysteines, while H3.2 and H3.3 contains only one (fig. S4B), we introduced C96S and C110S mutations into Flag-tagged H3.1. Coexpressing WT and mutant Flag-H3.1 with ZDHHC19WT in 3T3 cells, we found that the palmitoylation of Flag-H3.1 was not affected by C96S but was completely abolished by C110S or C96/110S mutations (Fig. 5F). These findings demonstrate that ZDHHC19 specifically catalyzes the palmitoylation of histone H3 at cysteine-110, without affecting the palmitoylation of other histones, TNPs, or PRMs.

ZDHHC19-mediated H3 palmitoylation reduces H3-H4 interaction

To explore how H3 palmitoylation affects histone removal, we first monitored the localization of H3 in the elongating spermatids from Zdhhc19+/+, Zdhhc19−/−, and Zdhhc19C142S/C142S mice. Immunofluorescence showed colocalization of H3 and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole in elongating spermatids across all three groups, indicating that neither knockout nor mutation of Zdhhc19 altered the subcellular localization of H3 (Fig. 6A). Later, we examined whether ZDHHC19 affect the protein stability of H3 using cycloheximide treatment assay. We found that H3 remained stable, showing no changes within 16 hours of cycloheximide treatment, regardless of ZDHHC19 overexpression in GC1 cells (fig. S4C).

The palmitoylated C110 site of H3 resides within its structured globular domain (fig. S4B), which was thought to destabilize histone tetramer (49). Therefore, we investigated whether palmitoylation could modulate the interaction strength between H3 and H4.

We examined the interaction between H3 and H4 in haploid spermatids from Zdhhc19+/+, Zdhhc19−/−, and Zdhhc19C142S/C142S testes using a coimmunoprecipitation (coIP) assay. In Zdhhc19−/− and Zdhhc19C142S/C142S haploid spermatids, H3 was found to bind more strongly to H4, compared to that in Zdhhc19+/+ spermatids (Fig. 6B), which correlates with the reduced palmitoylation level of H3 in Zdhhc19−/− and Zdhhc19C142S/C142S mice. This result was consistent across four independent experiments, and quantitative analysis displayed significant differences (Fig. 6C). To precisely quantify the immunoprecipitation (IP)–H4 level, we performed mass spectrometry after coIP and found a twofold increase in H4 recruitment by H3 in Zdhhc19−/− and Zdhhc19C142S/C142S haploid spermatids compared to that in Zdhhc19+/+ (Fig. 6D).

To determine whether the elevated binding affinity between H3 and H4 was due to the reduced H3 palmitoylation, we performed coIP assay using WT and palmitoylation-deficient mutant Flag-tagged H3 in 3T3 cells. The C110S and C96/110S H3 mutants exhibited significantly higher H4 binding than WT H3 in immunoblot and corresponding quantitative plot (Fig. 6, E and F). Further mass spectrometry of coIP also revealed increased IP-H4 level in C110S group compared to that in the WT (Fig. 6G).

ZDHHC19-mediated H3 palmitoylation facilitates chromatin remodeling during spermiogenesis

Previous studies have shown that modifications in globular region of H3 (including H3K115ac and H3K122ac) reduce H3-DNA interactions and thereby facilitate nucleosome disassembly (50, 51). To evaluate alterations in chromatin architecture resulting from H3 palmitoylation, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay followed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) in haploid spermatids. The results showed that H3 bound significantly more DNA in Zdhhc19−/− spermatids compared to Zdhhc19+/+ (Fig. 7A). Correspondingly, the cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) assay and qPCR demonstrated consistent trends with the ChIP data (fig. S4D). Additionally, ChIP and CUT&Tag followed qPCR also revealed that, in 3T3 cells, Flag-tagged H3 with C110S and C96/110S mutation also bound significantly more DNA than WT H3 (Fig. 7B and fig. S4E).

To further validate these findings at the chromatin level, we used transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq) in mouse haploid spermatids to interrogate the chromatin accessibility. The gene profile plots around transcription start sites (TSSs) displayed decreased signal in Zdhhc19−/− spermatids (Fig. 7C). In GC1 cells, overexpression of WT-ZDHHC19 protein also raised signal intensity around TSS (Fig. 7D). All these data illustrated that loss of Zdhhc19 decreased and overexpression of WT-ZDHHC19 protein could increase chromatin accessibility.

Last, to detect the expression level of other Zdhhc family members after Zdhhc19 knockout, we carried out reverse transcription (RT)–qPCR and found no compensatory increase of other Zdhhcs in spermatocytes or haploid spermatids after Zdhhc19 knockout (fig. S5A). We also detected the palmitoylation levels of proteins whose knockout mouse models exhibit phenotypes similar to Zdhhc19−/− mice, characterized by disrupted histone-to-PRM exchange and dysfunctional sperm, such as Rnf8, Phf7, and Brdt. These genes are primarily involved in histone removal through ubiquitination or acetylation. However, we found no obvious changes in either their protein levels or palmitoylation levels in Zdhhc19−/− testes compared to those in Zdhhc19+/+ (fig. S5B), suggesting that ZDHHC19 functions independently of these molecules during spermiogenesis. Meanwhile, in other cell line like BaF3 cells (bone marrow–derived factor-dependent pro-B cell line 3), we observed that a small fraction of H3 was palmitoylated (fig. S5C). These findings suggest that palmitoylation is a novel universal PTM of histones, potentially regulating nucleosome dynamics and chromatin accessibility in somatic cells.

In conclusion, we hypothesized that, under physiological conditions, histone H3 maintains low-level palmitoylation. During spermatogenesis, ZDHHC19 mediates robust H3 palmitoylation in elongating spermatids, which enhances nucleosome accessibility by reducing H3-H4 interaction and increasing chromatin accessibility. This process facilitates histone-to-PRM exchange during spermiogenesis (Fig. 8). These findings suggest that H3 palmitoylation plays a critical role in chromatin remodeling, promoting the transition from histones to PRMs and ensuring proper chromatin condensation during sperm development.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that ZDHHC19 is essential for spermiogenesis and male fertility, with its enzymatic activity playing a crucial role in proper sperm development and chromatin condensation. Zdhhc19 deficiency disrupts sperm chromatin condensation by impairing the histone-to-PRM exchange during spermiogenesis. We identify histone H3 as a substrate of ZDHHC19 and observed reduced H3 palmitoylation in testicular cells from Zdhhc19−/− and Zdhhc19C142S/C142S mice. Palmitoylated H3 at cysteine-110 exhibited decreased binding affinity to H4 and resulted in increased chromatin accessibility in haploid spermatids. These findings demonstrate that ZDHHC19-mediated H3 palmitoylation promotes histone removal in the histone-to-PRM exchange, ensuring proper chromatin remodeling during sperm development.

While our analysis did not detect the expression of Zdhhc19 in Sertoli cell (fig. S1B), we observed significantly higher rates of bent head sperm in Zdhhc19SerKO compared to those in Zdhhc19+/+ (Fig. 1E). This phenotype may potentially arise from the following: (i) Amh-cre recombinase may transport to spermatogenic cell through the cytoplasmic connection between Sertoli cell and spermatogenic cell, and (ii) off-target effects result in Amh-cre-mediated recombination in germ cell.

The ZDHHC family, which consists of 23 members in mice, is well-documented for compensatory effects among its members (52, 53). For instance, PSD-95 can be palmitoylated by several ZDHHC proteins, including ZDHHC2, ZDHHC3, ZDHHC7, ZDHHC15, and ZDHHC17 (54). Many single Zdhhc gene knockout mouse models do not exhibit significant phenotypes (e.g., Zdhhc1, Zdhhc3, Zdhhc4, and Zdhhc11) (32, 55–58). However, Zdhhc19−/− male mice showed severe infertile, which cannot be rescued by IVF. Moreover, no expression increase of other Zdhhcs was detected in Zdhhc19−/− spermatids (fig. S5A), indicating that Zdhhc19 has a unique and indispensable role in spermiogenesis.

The DHHC catalytic domain is highly conserved across species. In the ZDHHC family, the conserved cysteine within the DHHC domain serves as the core active site in in vitro systems (42). However, few investigations of mutant animal models were conducted to validate this hypothesis in vivo. To address this, we generated a Zdhhc19 C142S knock-in mouse model and confirmed that the palmitoylation level of the ZDHHC19C142S protein was obviously reduced (Fig. 4C), indicating its impaired function in vivo. However, Zdhhc19C142S/C142S male mice were not totally sterile. This suggests that C142S mutant protein retains partial function through following potential mechanisms: (i) The mutation reduces but does not completely abolish enzymatic activity. A study demonstrated that weak HRAS palmitoylation signal persist following GCP16-ZDHHS9 (cysteine mutant ZDHHC9) complex (59). (ii) Previous studies have demonstrated that the cascade catalysis exists among PATs (60). Furthermore, the PATs and accessory proteins can form functional complex that cooperatively catalyze palmitoylation of substrates (61, 62). Unlike complete knockout, the mutant ZDHHC19 protein still exists. We cannot exclude the possibility that mutant ZDHHC19 may act as a scaffold, recruiting other PATs or accessory proteins to facilitate downstream substrate palmitoylation. This residual function may explain the weaker phenotype observed in the knock-in mice compared to that in the Zdhhc19−/− mice.

Gao et al. (27) identified histone H2B and TNP2 in the palmitoyl-proteome of mouse testis. We further validated that, in addition to H2B and TNP2, H2A, H3, PRM1, and PRM2 are also palmitoylated in the testis (Fig. 5C). Given the presence of testis-specific histone variants and the cysteine residues in their sequences, we speculate that it is H2A.L.2 and TH2B, rather than the canonical H2A or H2B, undergo palmitoylation in the testis.

In this study, we demonstrated that ZDHHC19 regulates spermiogenesis by mediating the palmitoylation of histone H3. Additionally, our palmitoylation proteome analysis identified other potential ZDHHC19 substrates in the testis. As shown in table S1, adenylate kinase 7 may play a role in shaping sperm flagella (63, 64), while mutations in spermatogenesis-associated protein 20 have been linked to human acephalic spermatozoa syndrome (65). Further research is needed to uncover the detailed mechanisms underlying the relationship between palmitoylation and spermatogenesis, which will enhance our understanding of this process.

Additionally, we observed low levels of H3 palmitoylation in the absence of ZDHHC19, both in Zdhhc19−/− testis and in somatic 3T3 cells, suggesting that other PATs may contribute to maintaining a low degree of H3 palmitoylation. The palmitoylation site on H3 is located at the nucleosome dyad, within the globular domain of H3, adjacent to the H3/H4 tetramer interface. Compared to smaller groups like acetyl, palmitoyl is a bulky hydrophobic moiety that may affect H3-H4 tetramer through three distinct mechanisms: (i) steric hindrance (due to its large size), (ii) competitive occupation of the cysteine thiol group (66), and (iii) hydrophobic effects (altering local protein dynamics) (51, 67). Because histone-to-PRM exchange does not occur in somatic cells, it is likely that H3 palmitoylation plays a role in regulating nucleosome dynamics and modifying chromatin accessibility. Our findings provide valuable insights into the role of histone palmitoylation in modulating nucleosome structure, extending the implications of this modification beyond germ cells to somatic cells as well.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

All mouse models were bred under C57BL/6 background. The Zdhhc19flox/+ mice were produced by Shanghai Model Organisms Center using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. Later, knockout mice were raised by crossing Zdhhc19flox/+ to EIIA-cre mice; two conditional knockout mice were raised by crossing Zdhhc19flox/+ to Amh-cre and Tnap-cre mice as a gift from M. Liu. The Zdhhc19C142S/+ knock-in mice were produced by GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China) using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. Age-matched littermate mice were used as controls. All mice were housed in standard housing and specific pathogen–free conditions in individually ventilated cages and maintained with ad libitum access to feed and water. All murine experiments were monitored by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International and were approved by Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (number JUMC-2023-046-B).

Cell lines and cell culturing

GC1 cell line was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; BasalMedia, L110KJ) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, A5669701). 3T3 cell line was cultured in DMEM with 10% bovine serum (Gibco, 16010142). BaF3 cell line was cultured in 1640 (BasalMedia, L210KJ) with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured in a 37°C humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Antibodies

Rabbit polyclonal anti-ZDHHC19 antibody was generated by immunizing two rabbits with purified mouse ZDHHC19 (215 to 347 amino acids) expressed in pronucleus and collecting rabbit serum. Other antibodies are listed in table S2.

Histology and immunostaining

Mouse tissues were separated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde to make paraffin sections. Following deparaffinized and hydration, the sections were performed standard hematoxylin and eosin staining, immunostaining, or TUNEL staining. For immunostaining, the sections were blocked and incubated with indicated antibodies and corresponding secondary antibodies. The nuclei were stained by Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, H1399). For TUNEL staining, the sections were stained using the TUNEL BrightRed Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, A113), following provided protocol.

Mature sperm staining and immunofluorescence

The epididymal mature sperm was immunostained as described (68). Isolated sperm was fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 min and washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then suspended in PBS, and smeared on glass slide to dry overnight. For Diff-Quick staining, the sperm smear was stained using a Diff-Quick staining kit (BRED Life Science, BRED-015) guided by the product manual. For immunofluorescence, the smear was permeabilized by 0.5% NP-40 in PBS for 10 min following wash and then subjected to boiled sodium citrate antigen retrieval. The chromatin of sperm was decondensed by 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) for 1 hour at room temperature. Subsequently, standard immunostaining was performed.

Immunoblot

Mouse tissues or cells were resuspended in lysis buffer containing detergent [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 0.1 M DTT, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, and 1× proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 4693159001)]. After boiling for 10 min, protein samples were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred to nitrocellulose filter membrane. Following blocking by 5% nonfat milk, primary antibody was incubated at 4°C overnight, and secondary antibodies was incubated at room temperature for 1 hour.

Cell fractionation

Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions of sperm were isolated with the EpiQuik Total Histone Extraction Kit (EpigenTek, OP-0006-100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RT-qPCR analysis

For isolated mouse tissues or sorted spermatogenetic cells, RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596026), and cDNA was synthetized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with guide DNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A). qPCR was performed with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q331) on a ViiA7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences used in this study are provided in table S3.

Sperm isolation and CASA analysis

After euthanizing adult male mice, the cauda epididymis was separated and immediately transferred to 37°C preheated human tubal fluid (HTF) medium (Millipore, MR-070-D) with one cut by scissor. After incubation in 37°C for 5 min, the supernatant containing sperm was dropped into special slide for computer analysis.

IVF and ICSI

IVF and ICSI were performed with the protocols described previously (69). In brief, ovarian stimulation was performed by injecting pregnant mare serum gonadotropin followed by human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). Oocytes were collected 14 hours after hCG administration and incubated in HTF medium. For IVF, sperm from cauda epididymis were added into oocytes and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C. Insemination and culture were performed by standard “microdrop” techniques. ICSI was performed on superovulated oocytes from WT females. For blastocyst development analysis, sperm was injected into oocytes and incubated for 120 hours at 37°C. For live birth analysis, sperm-injected embryos were transferred to pseudopregnant females at the two-cell stage.

Click chemistry

Click chemistry assay was performed using the Click-iT Protein Enrichment Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10416), following provided protocol. Briefly, haploid spermatids were collected and metabolically labeled with 50 μM palmitic acid–azide probe or palmitic acid (negative control) for 6 hours. Cells were harvested and lysed by Urea Lysis Buffer. After determining protein concentration using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225), lysis was incubated with Alkyne Resin and Cu2+ to set up click reaction. Then, resin-bound protein was reduced and washed by 3d H2O, SDS-DTT Wash Buffer, SDS Wash Buffer, UT Buffer, and 20% acetonitrile successively, followed by resuspended by Digestion buffer and digested by trypsin.

Acyl-RAC assay

The protein palmitoylation level was examined by acyl–resin-assisted capture (RAC) assay (CAPTUREome S-Palmitoylated Protein Kit, Badrilla, K010-311) following provided protocol. In brief, free thiols of protein were blocked, and then palmitoyls was cleaved to expose the modified thiols. The exposed thiols of palmitoylated proteins were captured by resin following elution and later analyzed by SDS-PAGE or mass spectrometry.

Mating assay

Adult male mice were fed alone and mated with WT female at 1:1 ratio. The mated females were separated next morning and checked for vaginal plug. We repeated the procedure every 2 days; later, the number of pregnant females and litters was recorded for analysis.

Scanning electron microscopy and TEM

TEM and scanning electron microscopy imaging were carried out by Laboratory of Electron Microscopy, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, as previously described (70). Briefly, sperm from epididymis cauda were fixed in glutaraldehyde. After dehydrating and embedding, sections were imaged by scanning electron microscopy and TEM.

Spermatogenic cell sorting

Haploid spermatids and spermatocytes were sorted by FACS as described previously (71). In brief, adult mouse testes were put in PBS in 33°C for collagenase and trypsin digestion. After filter, single germ cells were stained by Hoechst 33342 (5 μg/ml) in 33°C for 40 min. Then, the two populations separated and sorted by FACS (BD, AriaII, Influx) according to signals in 630/30 and 460/50 excited by 355 nm. 630/30high 460/50high population represents spermatocytes, and 630/30low 460/50low population represents haploid spermatids. In haploid spermatids, FSChigh population represents round spermatids, and FSClow populations represent elongated spermatids (fig. S2D).

Sertoli cell isolation

Sertoli cell was isolated and cultured as described previously (72). In brief, 6-week-old mouse testes were digested into single-cell suspension and cultured in DMEM with 20% FBS. After 6 hours of culture, the culture medium was replaced to eliminate most of spermatogenic cells and to continue incubation for 24 hours. Cells were treated with 20 mM tris solution for 5 min, which was replaced with fresh culture medium to continue incubation for 24 hours. The remaining cells are purified Sertoli cells.

Sperm chromatin structure assay

SCSA was carried out as previously described (73). Briefly, fresh separated sperm was suspended in 0.2 ml of buffer [0.1 M tris, 0.15 M NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA (pH 7.4)], and then 0.4 ml of acid denaturation buffer [0.15 M NaCl, 0.08 N HCl, and 0.1% Triton X-100 (pH 1.4)] was added. After 30 s, the sperm was stained by 1.2 ml of AO staining solution [acridine orange (6 mg/ml), 0.1 M citric acid, 0.2 M Na2HPO4, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.15 M NaCl (pH 6.0)] for 3 min. Samples were detected for green and red fluorescence by flow cytometry in 488-nm excitation. After acid treatment, sperm with uncondensed chromatin will produce more single-strand DNA that will be stained as red by acridine orange, while intact sperm with double-strand DNA that will be stained as green. The DFI was calculated by proportion of red/red plus green fluorescence.

Coimmunoprecipitation

An equal number of cells were collected and suspended in radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime, China) premixed with proteinase inhibitor cocktail. After incubation on ice for 30 min, the lysates were ultrasonicated by 10 cycles of 30 s on followed by 30 s off. Primary antibody was added into the lysates and incubated overnight at 4°C, and then protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated for 4 hours at room temperature. After washing beads for three times, the coIP pellets were used for MS or eluted by SDS loading buffer and analyzed by standard immunoblot assay.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation

About 10,000 cells were collected, and the CUT&Tag assay was performed following the manufacturer’s instruction (Vazyme, TD904). After DNA extraction step, 5 μl of STOP buffer was added into samples and incubated at 95°C for 5 min to release DNA. Last, the DNA concentration was quantified by Qubit3.1 instrument.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

About 5 × 106 cells were collected, and the ChIP assay was performed following the manufacturer’s instruction (Active Motif, 53040).

ATAC-seq analysis

ATAC-seq assay and data analysis were performed according to ATAC-seq protocol by Shanghai Jiayin Biotechnology Ltd. Raw reads were quality filtered using Trimmomatic with the default parameters as described previously (74). ATAC-seq paired-end data were aligned to the reference mouse genome (GRCm38/mm10) using BWA program. The peak calling profiles were analyzed by MACS2 software, and the heatmaps were drawn by deeptools tool.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) and were displayed by means ± SEM. P value was obtained by two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t test. n.s., not significant; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001, respectively.
