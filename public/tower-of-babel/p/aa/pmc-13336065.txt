Longitudinal changes in DTI‐ALPS and choroid plexus volume relative to CSF biomarkers during lecanemab treatment in mild cognitive impairment: A pilot study
Authors: Toshiya Takahashi, Kaku Kimura, Shingo Takenaka, Sayaka Ono, Maya Fukui, Yasushi Okada, Tomohiko Asada, Kan Niimi, Akio Ikeda, Ryosuke Takahashi, Riki Matsumoto, Hidenao Fukuyama

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDLecanemab improves cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, but whether magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) proxies of CSF–interstitial fluid (ISF) exchange improve is unknown.METHODSThis was a single‐center prospective pilot (n = 8). BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition and tau pathology, and these are associated with network dysfunction and progressive cognitive decline. 1 , 2 , 3 Anti‐amyloid antibodies are increasingly used in real‐world clinical practice. Among them, lecanemab, which preferentially binds soluble Aβ protofibrils, has been reported to reduce amyloid burden in the brain and to improve core cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, including Aβ42 and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau), while cognitive decline still continues, although more slowly, under treatment. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Recent large‐scale prospective magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have used indices such as diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI‐ALPS) and choroid plexus (ChP) volume as proxies of CSF–interstitial fluid (ISF) exchange and clearance pathways involving the glymphatic system and the ChP and shown that these indices are independently associated with the future risk of developing dementia, suggesting that impairment of these pathways may contribute to disease progression. 8

The glymphatic system relies on arterial CSF influx, flow along perivascular spaces (PVSs), mixing with ISF, and efflux along venous pathways, whereas the ChP produces CSF, forms the blood–CSF barrier, and serves immune functions. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Because these processes are complex and multilayered, a single measure is insufficient to capture their full dynamics. Therefore, a multimodal approach that combines MRI and CSF biomarkers is appropriate. In this study, we focus on two MRI‐derived markers with complementary physiology: the ALPS index, derived from diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI‐ALPS), and total ChP volume. 9 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

Cross‐sectional studies have reported that the ALPS index is lower in AD/MCI and that lower ALPS index values are associated with lower CSF Aβ42. 8 , 14 , 18 , 19 , 20 ChP volume has been reported to be larger across the AD continuum and to be associated with lower CSF Aβ42 and p‐tau. 8 , 16 , 17 , 20 To our knowledge, there has been no study under anti‐Aβ antibody therapy that has tracked longitudinal changes in both the ALPS index and ChP volume over a 12‐month period and examined their relationship with CSF Aβ42, the Aβ42/40 ratio, and p‐tau181.

Here, we describe 12‐month trajectories of the ALPS index and ChP volume and compare them with changes in CSF Aβ42, Aβ42/40, and p‐tau181, evaluating concordance or discordance relative to prior cross‐sectional associations and to directions expected from the natural history of AD. Our aim was to clarify longitudinal changes in the glymphatic system and the CSF interface under lecanemab treatment.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This was a single‐center, prospective observational study at Yasu City Hospital. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yasu City Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. We enrolled patients with amyloid pathology confirmed by CSF biomarkers. Cognitive inclusion criteria were Mini‐Mental State Examination–Japanese version (MMSE‐J) 22 to 28, Montreal Cognitive Assessment–Japanese version (MoCA‐J) 18 to 25, Clinical Dementia Rating–Global score (CDR‐Global) 0.5 to 1, and Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) ≤ 8. CSF biomarker thresholds were defined as Aβ42/40 ≤ 0.67 and p‐tau181 ≥ 59.0 pg/mL. In addition to these cognitive assessments, we also administered the CDR–Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) and the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS‐J Cog) (Table 1).

All candidates underwent MRI screening, including T1‐weighted imaging, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and time‐of‐flight magnetic resonance angiography to exclude individuals with a history of large territorial infarction, prior intracerebral hemorrhage, intracranial mass lesions, or marked stenosis or asymmetry of the internal carotid arteries.

Eight participants (six women, two men; aged 60 to 79 years) were included. Lecanemab was administered intravenously every 2 weeks as part of routine clinical practice. Assessments were conducted at baseline and at 6, 9, and 12 months.

Assessments and outcomes

Primary imaging outcomes were the ALPS index and ChP volume. ChP volume was assessed as absolute total volume and as intracranial volume (ICV)‐normalized ChP volume (ChP/ICV × 1000) at each time point.

Cognitive measures included MMSE‐J, MoCA‐J, and CDR‐SB at baseline and 6, 9, and 12 months; ADAS‐J Cog was administered at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months.

CSF biomarkers (Aβ42, Aβ42/40 ratio, and p‐tau181) were measured at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. CSF samples were obtained at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months as part of routine clinical care. Aβ42, Aβ40, and p‐tau181 were measured by enzyme immunoassays (EIAs) through an external clinical laboratory service. The assays were subcontracted through LSI Medience and performed at TOKIWA CHEMICAL INDUSTRIES CO. The Aβ42/40 ratio was calculated from the measured Aβ42 and Aβ40 values.

MRI acquisition

All MRI examinations were performed on a 3T MAGNETOM Lumina (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32‐channel head coil. T1‐weighted images were obtained with an MPRAGE sequence (TR/TE/TI = 2000/2.88/900 ms, flip angle 10°, field of view 256 × 256 mm, voxel size 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3).

Diffusion MRI was acquired using a single‐shot echo‐planar imaging (voxel size 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 mm3, TR/TE = 10200/106 ms). The protocol included 64 diffusion‐encoding directions at b = 1500 s/mm2 and b = 0 images with phase encoding in the anterior‐to‐posterior (A→P) direction. Further, an additional b = 0 (b0) image with reversed phase‐encoding direction (P→A) was obtained for susceptibility‐induced distortion correction with topup.

All MRI sequences were acquired using Siemens Brain Dot Engine protocols with AutoAlign Head, which automatically aligned the imaging volume to a consistent orientation and minimized variability in slice positioning and angulation and head position between sessions and participants.

As part of routine clinical care during lecanemab treatment, follow‐up brain MRI including FLAIR and susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) was also performed at approximately 6, 9, and 12 months for monitoring of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs). QSM has also been used to evaluate ARIAs under anti‐amyloid therapy, but ARIA monitoring in the present study was performed using routine FLAIR and SWI. 21

Measurement of ChP volume

Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) data were converted to Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) format with dcm2niix. 22 For structural analysis, the original T1‐weighted images were used as input to the longitudinal FreeSurfer (version 8.0.0) processing pipeline and Sequence Adaptive Multimodal SEGmentation (SAMSEG)‐based ChP segmentation. 23 ChP was segmented with longitudinal SAMSEG in FreeSurfer, and total ChP volume was defined as the sum of the left and right ChP. 24 , 25 Estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) was obtained from the FreeSurfer longitudinal within‐subject template (base) created with recon‐all‐base.

The primary volumetric outcome for the ChP was the ICV‐normalized volume, calculated as ChP volume divided by eTIV and multiplied by 1000 (ChP/ICV × 1000). Because eTIV was constant within each participant, changes in ICV‐normalized ChP volume primarily reflected changes in ChP volume itself. All SAMSEG‐derived ChP labels were visually inspected in each subject and at each time point, and no manual editing was performed. A representative example of the SAMSEG‐based ChP segmentation used for quality control is shown in Figure S1. As an additional exploratory structural measure to aid interpretation of the longitudinal ChP findings and to assess whether other global structural changes were also observed over time, ICV‐normalized ventricular volume (Ventricle/ICV × 1000) was also derived from the same longitudinal SAMSEG outputs.

Measurement of ALPS index

We calculated the ALPS index from diffusion tensor images processed with FSL (version 6.0.7.13). DICOM diffusion data were converted to NIfTI format with dcm2niix. 22 For diffusion MRI preprocessing, susceptibility‐induced distortions were corrected with FSL topup using the main A > P series and a reversed phase‐encoded b = 0 image (P > A), and eddy‐current and motion correction were performed with FSL eddy, with rotation of b‐vectors accordingly. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 T1‐weighted images underwent bias‐field correction, skull stripping, and tissue segmentation with FSL fsl_anat to provide structural reference images for diffusion preprocessing and registration.

For each subject, diffusion tensors were fitted with dtifit in FSL to obtain directional diffusivity maps (Dxx, Dyy, Dzz) in native diffusion space. These diffusivity maps were transformed to the MNI152_T1_2 mm standard space using subject‐specific transformations that combined an initial linear coregistration to each subject's T1‐weighted image and a subsequent non‐linear normalization of the T1 image to the MNI152_T1_2 mm standard space (MNI) template. 31 Following the original DTI‐ALPS framework, we identified the axial slice at the level of the lateral ventricles in standard space, where medullary veins run radially, projection fibers run predominantly in the superior–inferior (z) direction, and association fibers run predominantly in the anterior–posterior (y) direction. 9 , 32

Four small, symmetric, spherical regions of interest (ROIs; diameter 4 mm) were placed bilaterally in the projection and association fiber regions. These ALPS ROIs were implemented as template‐based spherical ROIs on the MNI152_T1_2 mm standard space and were applied identically across all subjects and time points using a custom ImageJ/Fiji macro implemented in Fiji (version 2.16.0 / ImageJ 1.54p). ROI locations were visually inspected on color‐coded diffusion‐encoded composite images for every subject and time point, and no manual corrections were required. A representative example of the automated ROI placement is provided in Figure S2. Mean directional diffusivities (x, y, z) were extracted from each ROI, and the ALPS index was calculated as

ALPS = mean (Dxx, proj, Dxx, assoc) / mean (Dyy, proj, Dzz, assoc).

Values from the left and right ROIs were averaged to yield a single ALPS value per participant and time point.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2). Changes from baseline were tested with two‐sided Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests. Assessment windows were as follows: MRI measures (ALPS and ICV‐normalized ChP volume) and MMSE‐J/MoCA‐J at 6/9/12 months; ADAS‐J Cog and CDR‐SB at 6/12 months; CSF biomarkers (Aβ42, Aβ42/40, p‐tau181) at 6/12 months. Absolute total ChP volume was additionally evaluated in a sensitivity analysis. Multiplicity was controlled at false discovery rate (FDR) = 0.05 within prespecified outcome families (MRI, CSF, cognition) using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and FDR‐adjusted q values were reported. For each contrast, we reported the unadjusted p, the FDR‐adjusted q, and the effect size r; tables also included the Hodges–Lehmann median difference (Δmedian) with 95% confidence intervals, and box plots were annotated with p, q, and r. Given the pilot and exploratory nature of this study and the limited precision of estimates, we interpreted p and q values together with effect sizes r, rather than relying solely on statistical significance.

To further evaluate cross‐modal longitudinal relationships, we performed repeated‐measures correlation analyses using the shared time points available across modalities (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months). These analyses examined associations between CSF biomarkers (Aβ42, Aβ42/40, and p‐tau181) and MRI measures (ALPS index and ICV‐normalized ChP volume), as well as associations between the same CSF biomarkers and cognitive measures (MMSE‐J, MoCA‐J, CDR‐SB, and ADAS‐J Cog). FDR was controlled within the MRI–CSF and cognition‐CSF analysis families using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method. In addition, time‐adjusted linear mixed‐effects models were fitted for the MRI–CSF relationships, with MRI measures as outcomes, CSF biomarkers as predictors, time point as a fixed effect, and subject as a random intercept.

MMSE‐J did not change significantly (6/9/12 months: p = 0.890/0.204/0.667; r = 0/−0.449/−0.152; q = 0.890/0.464/0.890). MoCA‐J was also non‐significant (6/9/12 months: p = 0.824/0.714/0.386; r = 0/0.129/−0.307; q = 0.890/0.890/0.643). CDR‐SB showed a trend toward worsening at 6 months that attenuated by 12 months (6/12 months: p = 0.089/0.221; r = 0.601/0.433; q = 0.445/0.464) (Table 2).

ChP volume

Within the MRI family, ICV‐normalized ChP volume increased at each time point and remained significant after FDR correction, with consistently large effects (6/9/12 months: p = 0.008/0.008/0.008; r = 0.940/0.940/0.940; q = 0.010/0.010/0.010).

Repeating the analysis with absolute ChP volume instead of ICV‐normalized ChP volume yielded exactly the same statistical results (identical p values, effect sizes r, and FDR‐adjusted q values), because eTIV derived from the FreeSurfer longitudinal base template was constant within each participant (Figure 1, Table 4). In an additional exploratory structural analysis, ICV‐normalized ventricular volume (ventricle/ICV × 1000) also increased significantly at 6, 9, and 12 months compared with baseline. Detailed numerical results are shown in Table S1.

Cross‐modal longitudinal association analyses

To quantify cross‐modal longitudinal relationships, we performed repeated‐measures correlation analyses using the shared time points available across modalities (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months). For MRI–CSF relationships, ICV‐normalized ChP volume showed positive correlations with Aβ42 (r = 0.564, q = 0.037) and Aβ42/40 (r = 0.721, q = 0.007) and a negative correlation with p‐tau181 (r = −0.572, q = 0.037). In contrast, the ALPS index did not show significant repeated‐measures correlations with Aβ42 (r = −0.259, q = 0.329), Aβ42/40 (r = −0.388, q = 0.186), or p‐tau181 (r = 0.252, q = 0.329).

For cognition–CSF relationships, no association remained significant after FDR correction. The strongest associations were observed for CDR‐SB with Aβ42 (r = 0.607, q = 0.083) and with p‐tau181 (r = −0.584, q = 0.083), but these did not meet the FDR threshold.

We also fitted time‐adjusted linear mixed‐effects models for the MRI–CSF relationships. In these models, no MRI–CSF association remained significant after FDR correction, including the associations between ICV‐normalized ChP volume and Aβ42 (standardized β = −0.046, q = 0.792), Aβ42/40 (standardized β = −0.003, q = 0.939), and p‐tau181 (standardized β = −0.053, q = 0.792). The association between the ALPS index and CSF biomarkers was likewise not significant in the mixed‐effects models. Detailed results are shown in Tables S2 and S3.

No ARIA‐E/H or other clinically evident intracranial adverse events were observed during the 12‐month observation period.

DISCUSSION

Summary of main findings: alignment with natural history and prior cross‐sectional associations

Over 12 months, CSF biomarkers moved in the expected treatment direction, with Aβ42 and the Aβ42/40 ratio increasing and p‐tau181 decreasing, while MRI showed an overall downward trend in the ALPS index and an increase in ICV‐normalized ChP volume (Figure 1, Tables 3 and 4). According to previous natural history studies, declining ALPS index and increasing ChP volume are associated with disease progression. 10 , 14 , 15 , 32 , 33 Thus, in our cohort, MRI changes were in the same directions as those reported in natural history cohorts, even while CSF biomarkers indicated improvement. Furthermore, the patterns observed in this longitudinal study partially diverged from previously reported cross‐sectional associations, in which the ALPS index was positively correlated with CSF Aβ42 and ChP volume was negatively correlated with Aβ42 and p‐tau. 8 , 14 , 18 , 19 , 20

DTI‐ALPS: divergence from reported CSF Aβ42 associations and consistency with natural history

Previous studies linked lower ALPS index values to lower CSF Aβ42 and worse cognitive function and demonstrated that the ALPS index declined with disease progression. 9 , 14 , 32 In contrast, in our cohort undergoing lecanemab treatment, the ALPS index decreased despite improvement in CSF Aβ42 and related biomarkers. This suggests that, over a 12‐month treatment window, the ALPS index may not necessarily change in parallel with soluble CSF biomarkers.

CSF biomarkers and DTI‐ALPS reflect different aspects of the CSF–brain interstitial interface. Therefore, even within the same observation window, improvement in soluble CSF biomarkers may not necessarily be accompanied by parallel changes in DTI‐ALPS.

ChP volume: mixed alignment with CSF biomarkers and consistency with natural history

Cross‐sectional studies have related larger ChP volume to lower Aβ42 and lower p‐tau. 16 , 17 In the present study, however, ICV‐normalized ChP volume increased while Aβ42 increased and p‐tau decreased. Thus, the increase in ChP volume diverged from the reported cross‐sectional association with Aβ42, whereas the direction of change in p‐tau was broadly consistent. Taken together, this mixed pattern suggests that ChP volume should not be interpreted as a simple proxy for amyloid clearance or tau dynamics during treatment.

Anti‐amyloid antibodies have been associated with transient inflammatory and vascular changes in the brain. 34 , 35 Although data on the ChP are lacking, we cannot exclude the possibility that treatment‐related vascular or inflammatory effects might influence ChP volume independently of amyloid or tau burden. Additional exploratory structural analysis showed that ventricular volume also increased over time in this cohort. However, at present, increased ChP volume itself has not been established as a recognized pseudoatrophy finding, 36 and the present data do not support explaining the observed ChP enlargement solely by pseudoatrophy. This issue may be clarified by future studies with larger samples and appropriate controls.

Cross‐modal interpretation

Lecanemab has been reported to improve core CSF biomarkers, including Aβ42 and p‐tau, while cognitive decline still continues, although more slowly, under treatment. 4 , 5 In a recent large‐scale prospective MRI study, 8 MRI measures intended to reflect CSF–ISF exchange and clearance pathways, including the glymphatic system and the ChP, were examined. That study showed that indices such as DTI‐ALPS and ChP volume were independently associated with future dementia risk, suggesting that impairment of these pathways may contribute to disease progression.

In this study, we tracked how these MRI‐based proxies of CSF–ISF exchange and clearance changed over time under lecanemab treatment, in parallel with changes in CSF Aβ42 and p‐tau181. Although only the 9‐month comparison remained significant after correction, DTI‐ALPS showed a consistent downward tendency from baseline, with large effect sizes. In addition, ChP volume showed a significant increase from baseline.

Taken together, these findings suggest that, over at least 12 months of lecanemab treatment, DTI‐ALPS and ChP volume may show changes in directions that are broadly consistent with those reported in the natural course of AD and that these trajectories do not necessarily parallel improvements in CSF biomarkers. Because our observations were made under treatment, the present data do not allow us to determine whether these MRI changes reflect ongoing disease‐related processes or treatment‐related vascular or inflammatory effects. However, at least within the 12‐month observation period, we did not observe changes in the opposite direction to those reported in the natural course of the AD continuum, namely, an increase in DTI‐ALPS or a decrease in ChP volume.

We also observed that previously reported cross‐sectional relationships between CSF biomarkers and DTI‐ALPS/ChP volume may not necessarily be reproduced under treatment in a longitudinal setting. Our findings therefore suggest that the ALPS index and ChP volume may be insufficient when used alone as surrogate markers of treatment effect over 12 months. At the same time, their non‐recovery under treatment may itself be informative, because it suggests that CSF–ISF exchange and clearance‐related pathways may not be fully normalized by anti‐amyloid therapy alone within this time frame, and this is consistent with the observation that cognitive decline may continue at a slower rate during anti‐amyloid therapy.

Additional cross‐modal analyses further refined this interpretation. ICV‐normalized ChP volume showed longitudinal associations with Aβ42, Aβ42/40, and p‐tau181 in repeated‐measures correlation analyses, whereas the ALPS index did not show clear longitudinal associations with these CSF biomarkers. However, when time point was included in mixed‐effects models, the MRI‐CSF associations were no longer significant. Although longer follow‐up will be important to clarify how these MRI‐based proxies behave over time under treatment, the cross‐modal analyses based on the present 12‐month data did not support a simple pattern of delayed parallel recovery between MRI‐based clearance proxies and soluble CSF biomarkers. Rather, they support the view that MRI and CSF measures may capture partly different aspects of disease‐related biology during longitudinal observation under lecanemab treatment.

This was an observational study without a control group, and these hypotheses should be tested in larger controlled longitudinal cohorts. Nevertheless, our findings suggest the potential value of exploring therapeutic strategies that directly target CSF–ISF exchange and clearance pathways as a potential complement to anti‐amyloid therapy, although this possibility needs to be examined in future studies.

Limitations

This was a single‐center, small cohort (n = 8) without a control arm. Because of the uncontrolled design, we were not able to clearly separate natural disease progression from treatment‐related effects or directly compare our MRI changes with those reported in natural history cohorts. Comparison with external longitudinal control cohorts could provide important additional context for interpreting whether the observed changes in DTI‐ALPS and ChP volume are more consistent with untreated disease progression or treatment‐related effects. However, a rigorous comparison of this kind would require substantial harmonization of imaging acquisition, preprocessing pipelines, and longitudinal analytic methods across datasets and was therefore beyond the scope of the present single‐center pilot study. Future studies incorporating such external longitudinal control cohorts may help clarify this issue. Multiplicity was controlled within endpoint families, but residual risks of both type I and type II error remain, given the small sample size. Diffusion imaging used a single‐shell acquisition, and the ALPS index depends on acquisition and modeling. The ChP is a small structure prone to partial‐volume effects from adjacent CSF, and mask geometry can influence estimates. Moreover, both the ALPS index and ChP volume are indirect MRI‐based proxies of CSF dynamics and can be influenced by factors unrelated to glymphatic or ChP function; interpretation at the level of specific biological mechanisms should therefore be made with caution.

CONCLUSION

Over 12 months of lecanemab treatment, CSF biomarkers changed in the expected therapeutic direction, whereas MRI measures showed an overall downward trend in the ALPS index and an increase in ICV‐normalized ChP volume. These MRI changes were in the same direction as those reported in natural history studies of AD, even though CSF biomarkers improved. Furthermore, the cross‐sectional associations among ALPS, ChP volume, and CSF biomarkers reported in previous studies were not necessarily reproduced in longitudinal observation under treatment.

Considering that cognitive decline continues, although more slowly, even under lecanemab treatment and that the ALPS index and ChP volume have been linked to CSF–ISF exchange and clearance pathways, as well as to future dementia risk, our findings indicate that, even when CSF biomarkers show a therapeutic response, within the 12‐month observation window we did not observe a directional increase in DTI‐ALPS or a directional decrease in ChP volume. Our findings suggest that the ALPS index and ChP volume may be insufficient if used alone as surrogate markers of treatment effect. In addition, they suggest the potential value of exploring therapeutic strategies that directly target CSF–ISF exchange and clearance pathways as a potential complement to anti‐amyloid therapy. To validate and extend these observations, larger multicenter longitudinal studies with appropriate controls are needed.
