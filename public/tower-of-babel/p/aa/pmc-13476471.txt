Weaning infants with short bowel syndrome associated intestinal failure from parenteral nutrition: An ERNICA multicentre survey
Authors: Susan Hill, Rebecca Pulvirenti, Dominique Guimber, Sarah Macdonald, Cora Jonkers, Emmanuelle Echochard-Dugelay, Merit Tabbers, Giovanna Verlato, for the European Reference Network for rare Inherited and Congenital Anomalies (ERNICA)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore and compare practices of European Intestinal Rehabilitation Centres participating in the European Reference Network for rare Inherited and Congenital Anomalies (ERNICA) intestinal failure (IF) working group when weaning short bowel syndrome (SBS)-IF infants from parenteral nutrition (PN).Material and methodsQuestionnaires were distributed by email to 49 ERNICA centres. Details regarding oral/nasogastric (NG) feeding when weaning PN, use of protocols/guidelines, criteria for PN cycling/reducing infusion hours, dietary recommendations, use of synthetic glucagon-like peptide-2 (teduglutide), antibiotics for intestinal bacterial overgrowth and long-term follow-up practices were requested in children with neonatal onset SBS-IF. Descriptive statistical analysis was conducted.ResultsThirty-five/49centers from 15 countries participated. In non-thriving infants aged < 6months, 74.3% centres supplemented oral intake with NG feeding and 94.3% continued it for > 6months if needed. Local/national guidelines were used by 63% when weaning PN. Criteria for cycling PN included age (range: term-3 months), weight (3–4.5 kg), and enteral feed tolerance with 95% centres reducing PN infusion rate before disconnecting. In 88.6% centres patients were monitored until transitioned to adult care (even if weaned from PN). Dietary recommendations and antibiotic usage varied widely. Teduglutide was used in 23/25(92%) centres from 11 countries where it was approved and funded.ConclusionA variety of nutritional strategies were used to wean PN in SBS-IF infants even in specialist centres using guidelines. NG-tube feeding was used alongside oral intake in most centres, but other practices differed widely. Multicentre studies investigating which practices are associated with the best outcome for infants with SBS-IF are needed to develop evidence-based guidelines.

Introduction

Short bowel syndrome–associated intestinal failure (SBS-IF) is a malabsorptive condition requiring parenteral nutrition (PN) and represents the leading cause of IF in the pediatric population [1], [2]. SBS-IF may be congenital or, more commonly, develops after small intestinal (SI) resection, when the infant is left with insufficient SI length or mass to absorb adequate nutrients for normal growth [3]. The most common paediatric aetiology is surgical resection of necrotising enterocolitis (NEC).

Clinically, SBS-IF can be defined as the need for PN for > 42 or > 60 days after bowel resection, or as a short bowel length below a critical value for nutritional supply for adequate growth [4], [5], [6].

SBS-IF prevalence increased 100-fold from 1992 to 2012 and is probably increasing further with improved survival of premature neonates [7]. The incidence is 24.5/100,000 live births [8], and 700/100,000 in newborns < 1500 g who have the greatest risk of NEC [9]. SBS-IF survival rate has increased up to 88–95% at 9.1 and 8.9 years, respectively [10], [11].

The main challenge in SBS-IF remains achieving enteral autonomy while preserving metabolic stability and growth, even though there have been recent improvements in PN composition and SBS-IF management [12], [13].

Whilst there are established guidelines for PN management [14], oral/enteral nutrition (EN) strategies vary in different European centres. It is well established that EN use limits complications [15] and oral/enteral feeding (EF) is commenced as soon as possible after surgery with human milk (HM) prioritised [5], [6]. Our previously published results from surveying European Reference Network for rare Inherited and Congenital Anomalies (ERNICA) intestinal rehabilitation (IR) centres found HM was prioritised and Complementary Feeding (CF) introduced early. However other practices when introducing EN varied.

To further understand practices in European IR centres participating in the ERNICA network, we aimed to explore and compare strategies used when weaning (cycling/reducing) PN, introducing EN, use of guidelines, medications used including antibiotics and glucagon-like peptide−2 (GLP−2) analogues, and length of follow-up.

Material and methods

In January 2019, the first ERNICA-IF workshop was held in Amsterdam, the Netherlands. A research agenda was developed to investigate several aspects of SBS management for which there was little or no published evidence. Our group was tasked with investigating PN “weaning strategies”. The group was composed of specialist physicians and dietitians from 5 centres in 4 countries. We started to work on this follow up questionnaire in 2022 communicating via e-mail and virtual meetings. The topics included: enteral feeding modalities, PN cycling and weaning, medications to facilitate intestinal adaptation, and long-term follow-up for SBS-IF patients even after they had gained enteral autonomy.

The group decided to select enteral weaning practices for the questionnaire for which there was little or no evidence in the literature. In this study we separated infants into two groups: younger infants aged under 6 months and older infants from the age of 6 months. The questionnaire was composed of 49 questions with 37 multiple choice-questions, 9 questions with both multiple choice and open questions and 3 open questions. It was sent to 49 ERNICA centres (see appendix 1) from 21 European Countries: Austria, Belgium, Croatia, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxemburg, Norway, Poland, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, The Netherlands, and the United Kingdom.

We used the following definitions:−Cycling PN was reducing the hours that PN was given in a 24-hour period so that the infant was receiving PN over less than the full 24 h i.e. the PN was infused over 23 h or less−Enteral automomy was the ability of the infant to ingest and absorb sufficient oral/enteral nutrition to maintain weight gain and growth without any further PN infusions−feeding tolerance was the ability to ingest and absorb a certain amount of oral food/enteral feed whilst on partial PN or after gaining enteral autonomy−Weaning PN was the reduction in the volume and/or nutrients given as PN whilst increasing oral/enteral nutrition intake.

We did not use stricter definitions for ‘weaning’ or ‘tolerance ’ since we wanted to collect data on possible differences in practice. However, this was an ERNICA study and participating centres would be familiar with these terms and shared a common understanding based on previous collaboration.

Each IF rehabilitation team was asked to fill out the questionnaire with two or more professionals, preferably a doctor and dietitian completing it together in each centre. Reminders were sent to non-responding centres over a three-month period. Since the questionnaire was addressed to professionals and referred to practices and not individual patient data, ethical permission was not required.

The responses were collated in an Excel spreadsheet and analysed using descriptive statistics. For the purposes of this study we defined EN as any nutrition via the enteral route and EF as liquid enteral feed given via an artificial feeding device such as a NG tube. Comparisons between centres with > 10 long-term PN patients on home PN centres with < 10 patients on longer term PN at home were performed using Chi-square test.

Discussion

We analyzed European practices when attempting to gain intestinal adaptation. Our survey built on results of our previous one, focusing on strategies for introducing postoperative EN and CF in ERNICA associated SBS-IF rehabilitation centres.13 Most centres were small and managed < 20patients/year.

Commonly used practices included NG feeding to supplement oral intake in 74.3% centres that was continued for > 6months in 94.3% if needed. Also, PN was cycled from 40 weeks/term equivalent corrected age and 3 kg weight in some centres. Ideally infants tolerated some oral/enteral intake when cycling PN commenced. Virtually all, 95% centres, reduced PN infusion rate before disconnecting (Table 1). The majority, 88.6% centres, monitored patients until transitioned to adult care, even after weaning from PN. However, dietary recommendations and antibiotic use varied widely. GLP−2 was used in 23/25 (92%) approved and funded centres from 11 countries.

Although adult studies have demonstrated that EF with/without oral feeding improved absorption [17], evidence in children is lacking. Whilst EN is probably beneficial in children [18], artificially feeding children may exceed gastrointestinal absorptive potential with abdominal distension, bacterial overgrowth, mucosal injury [13], feeding problems and food refusal ensuing [19].

Most centres prioritized oral feeding over NG feeds in infants < 6 months when capable of sucking, which probably reduced the risk of food aversion [19], as recently reported [5]. When growth faltered, despite oral feeding, 66% centers gave additional NG feed and preferred bolus to continuous/intermittent feeds. Lack of consensus in feeding methods is consistent with existing literature. However, a common feature was to start EF early, especially when oral feeding was unfeasible [20].

In contrast to the previous questionnaire on practices in younger infants < 6 months, infants in this study aged > 6months were preferentially given formula EN in 46% of centres; only 25% prioritised HM with/without formula. Milk choice was probably influenced by lack of availability of HM, even when HM might be the best feed for infants with malabsorption [21]. Twelve centres considered blended diet, that might minimise feeding difficulties and improve intestinal function and gut microbiome [22].

Most centres used hydrolyzed protein feed as first choice of EF when HM was not available, in keeping with the findings in our previous survey [20]. Centres that started with polymeric feed, changed to hydrolyzed feed when polymeric feed was poorly tolerated.

Despite most centres (63%) claiming to follow a guideline when reducing, cycling and/or stopping PN; management varied [5]. The 2023 ESPGHAN position paper on SBS-IF management should help standardise treatment [5].

Cycling PN can be important in limiting hyperinsulinism with associated fat deposition in the liver [15] and improved lipid oxidation. A trial is feasible “once the patient is clinically and biochemically stable” or “can manage any enteral intake or can tolerate time off the PN infusion” [23], [24]. The optimal time to start cycling is unknown. Guidelines suggest that “cycling PN is safe even in clinically stable newborns” [25] and should be considered as early as possible, particularly in stable infants > 4 kg since risk of hypoglycemia is less in smaller infants [5]. In our study, PN cycling was initiated at a weight of 3 kg in 14 centres, suggesting that this practice may be feasible at lower weights than previously reported. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of this approach.

A night off PN could be trialed when about 50% of nutritional requirements are tolerated enterally [23], with the infusion rate reduced over 1–2 h before stopping [23]. Indication for withdrawing PN varied with most centres considering ceasing treatment when growth was adequate.

Dietary recommendations varied widely between centres, probably due to cultural differences and lack of literature [6].

The variability observed across centres likely reflects differences in institutional profiles, including caseload, clinical experience, and access to specific treatments. Although all ERNICA centres are recognized as expert units, those caring for a smaller number of patients on home PN may have more limited exposure to complex cases, which could influence management approaches. In addition, this variation is likely influenced by the limited availability of high-quality paediatric evidence, as well as differences in patient case-mix and the organization of healthcare delivery across countries, all of which may contribute to the lack of uniformity in clinical practice.

Variability between centres could also be explained by differing interpretations of terms such as 'weaning from PN' or 'tolerance', for which no definitions were provided in the questionnaire. Definitions of these concepts are also not always consistent in the literature, which may contribute to variability in their interpretation across centres [26]. Although these concepts were extensively discussed within the ERNICA IF Working Group, differences in interpretation may still have contributed to heterogeneity in the responses.

Similarly, although no significant differences were observed between high- and low-volume centres (>10 vs <10 patients/year), patient volume alone is unlikely to account for the variability observed across centres. Differences in expertise, multidisciplinary team composition, local guidelines, access to therapies, and healthcare organization may also contribute to heterogeneous clinical practices.

GLP−2 was not funded by some European governments despite European Medicines Agency, EMA approving teduglutide in 2016. It had not been used in about a third of centres. Smaller centres with fewer patients (7 had 0–5 home patients), were less likely to have had suitable patients for treatment. Many centres had only recently started using teduglutide; only 48% had used it for > 24months.

Small Intestinal bacterial overgrowth, SIBO is poorly understood and can be difficult to diagnose. SIBO appears to be associated with a wide variety of bacteria and some cases may be colonic dysbiosis rather than excessive SI bacteria [27]. The gut microbiota comprises the aggregate of microorganisms within the human digestive system and plays a key role in maintaining host health. Alterations in the composition or abundance of these microorganisms, known as dysbiosis, can disrupt homeostasis and have been implicated in the development of inflammatory bowel disease and SIBO [28].

In the absence of a reliable gold standard for diagnosing SIBO in this population, both the clinical definition and management of SIBO vary widely, likely reflecting the limited available evidence [29], [30]. Clinical findings attributed to SIBO included gaseous abdominal distension, bloating, weight loss, faltering growth and exacerbation of diarrhoea/malodorous stool possibly associated with nutrient malabsorption triggering an inflammatory cascade [31]. Antibiotics were not used routinely in 80% of centres but only when SIBO was suspected. Publications on antibiotic use in SIBO have been limited to small clinical studies with differing treatment approaches. Although ciprofloxacin and metronidazole have been successfully used to eradicate SIBO in Crohn’s disease [32], there is no conclusive evidence on the best therapy in SBS associated SIBO. In our survey, rifaximin was the most used antibiotic while amoxicillin and metronidazole was the commonest combination. Poorly absorbed antibiotics, such as Rifaximin and neomycin are less likely to lead to side effects than systemic antibiotics [33], [34], [35].

Given the challenges associated with diagnosing SIBO, careful clinical evaluation and assessment are essential when SIBO is suspected, to support judicious antibiotic use and minimize the risks of antimicrobial resistance, adverse reactions, and opportunistic infections. Antibiotics should be tailored to minimise risk of both SIBO recurrence and bacterial resistance, particularly when used long-term.

This study had several limitations. Firstly, our study was descriptive and not designed to evaluate clinical outcomes and therefore no conclusions can be drawn regarding the relative effectiveness of the reported strategies. Secondly, the questionnaire was not piloted, however the multi-institution collaboration with 5 centres from four countries participating in drafting it may mitigate this limitation. Thirdly, the study was limited to European ERNICA centres; in the future, it would be valuable to broaden participation to include centres from diverse geographical and socioeconomic settings, enabling evaluation of clinical practices in relation to cultural backgrounds, national healthcare systems, and available rhesources.

Fourthly, no comparison between responding and non-responding centres was performed. However, all IF ERNICA centres, regardless of volume, are designated expert centres in IF according to European Commission criteria [36]. As such, participating centres are likely to share a broadly similar level of expertise and organizational structure. Nevertheless, the possibility of selection bias cannot be fully excluded, as more engaged centres may have been more likely to participate.

We investigated NG tube use, but not other artificial feeding devices such as gastrostomy. Patients in the study age group would not usually have a gastrostomy; they are usually inserted in infants > 6 months old and often not until 9–10 months. Furthermore, the questionnaire was drafted and disseminated before the ESPGHAN SBS position paper was published [5]. Management of SBS in infancy maybe less heterogenous now the position paper is available.

Conclusion

Despite the differences between centres in nutritional strategies when introducing EN in infants with SBS-IF, early introduction of EF and support with artificial/NG feeding were frequently used. Prospective multicentre studies are needed to identify the practices that lead to the best outcome. An ERNICA prospective multicenter study is currently ongoing with the aim of developing an SBS-IF management guideline.
