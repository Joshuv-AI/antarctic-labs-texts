Refining grapevine vegetation status by utilizing grassland and remote sensing data
Authors: Tibor Zsigmond, Zsófia Bakacsi, Ágota Horel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The present study aimed to investigate specific vegetation indices (VI) at three research sites - one grassland and two vineyards - and evaluate the potential of grassland remote sensing (RS) data to refine Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) values in grass-covered inter-row vineyards. The vineyards differed in soil texture, with silt loam at BCS and clay at GB, located on 8–15% slopes. Field monitoring included NDVI, Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI), and Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR) data at different slope positions. We also downloaded spectral data from Sentinel-2 (S2; n = 124) to see how well the NDVI field and S2 data correlate. Afterward, different machine learning techniques were used to refine the accuracy of the measurements, such as linear regression (LR), random forest (RF), and XGBoost. Significant differences in VI were observed between the research sites, mainly correlating with soil chemistry. While NDVI is an indicator of overall canopy vigor, PRI was more responsive to short-term physiological changes, showing higher sensitivity under stress conditions. Ground truth and RS NDVI were well correlated (r = 0.68), with RF providing the best accuracy when trained with day of year and the grassland data (r = 0.787). Each model moderately predicted grapevine VI using basic inputs (r > 0.61), however, all three models performed well when grassland NDVI included in the training.

Introduction

Monitoring vegetation parameters can provide us with crucial information on the decline in plant development, biomass productivity, or stress-induced changes as a result of shifts in environmental parameters1. Plant monitoring-derived vegetation indices (VI) can help achieve more accurate crop yield or biomass production estimates. Vineyards are especially valuable for spectral monitoring because of their spatially heterogeneous canopy structure and plant vigor. In contrast to uniform cropping systems, vine rows are often discontinuous, with different vine densities, inter-row vegetation and trellis systems that create complex reflectance patterns. The VI can further help determine mitigating actions to lessen any negative effects arising from changes in meteorological, or soil physical and chemical conditions. Due to climate change, crops are becoming more sensible for longer drought conditions and changes in precipitation patterns and intensities2,3hence, proactive mitigation measures are essential for preparedness.

Implementing VI monitoring can help detect any adverse changes in plant health, canopy density, or coverage. Stress-related changes can be caused by drought, heat, insufficient amount of fertilizer in the soil, diseases, or pests, all of which can adversely impact plant growth and crop yield. The inhomogeneity in vegetation cover, resulting from grass and bare or cultivated inter-rows in vineyards, introduces variability in the canopy’s spectral response. This variability directly influences the interpretation of VI4,5making accurate monitoring both more challenging and more necessary. Incorporating this relationship into consideration, it helps to better justify the use of VI for assessing vineyard conditions under varying inter-row management practices.

Various types of VI can be used to detect changes in plants, such as the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). NDVI is one of the most studied VI as it can effectively detect many key vegetation characteristics (e.g., greenness, biomass), often associated with spectral shifts like increased red-edge reflectance or a depression in red reflectance due to changes in chlorophyll content6. For example, sparsely vegetated or tilled inter-rows increase soil background signal, reducing NIR reflectance and potentially resulting in a lower NDVI. While remote sensing NDVI such as S2 assumes relatively uniform plant canopies and averages of all data over a larger area (i.e., 100 m2, small sensors and UAVs have the advantages of measuring areas of grapevine leaves and canopies only7depending on the distance of plant canopy and the sensors. Hence, smaller sensors can provide data on grapevines at the same time when the inter-row vegetation might reflect changes in environmental conditions resulting in shifts in spectral responses. Drought-related stress response might have a longer lag time in VI, such as NDVI8or a lag time of a few days, e.g., photochemical reflectance index (PRI)9. PRI can also be used for heat-related stress response in plants10 due to a reaction to changes in xanthophyll cycle pigment dynamics11. The photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) or the fraction of absorbed PAR (fAPAR) calculated from above and below canopy PAR values is another plant parameters that can indicate plant health based on plant density12. However, PAR/fAPAR are radiometric measures related to plant productivity rather than spectral-ratio-derived metrics, and therefore are not typically categorized as vegetation indices. However, the rate of fractional vegetation cover is crucial, as some indices are sensitive to higher plant density or sparse vegetation cover13. Plant NDVI and fAPAR values can be highly related, however, the type of plants, leaf area indices (LAI), measurement times, leaf orientation, and the environmental background of the plants can greatly influence the level of this relationship12,14,15. However, some VI can lose reliability in contexts with a low fraction of vegetation cover due to a combination of background effects (e.g., soil spectral signatures), weak signal strength (e.g., increased noise) or pixel conditions (e.g., mixed vegetation, soil, and shadows). Within a LAI range of 0.5 to 3, NDVI values can vary widely, leading to significant errors when derived from satellite data, which can be reduced if enough background information on soil types are available16. In grapevines with dense foliage, NIR reflectance can be higher than that of the grass vegetation in the inter-rows; therefore, wide, grass-covered inter-rows can reduce the overall reflectance. Outside the phenological stages between flowering and leaf drop, NIR reflectance from grass-covered areas can exceed that of grapevines with underdeveloped canopies. Across both Continental and Mediterranean climate zones, VI exhibit the highest variability between flowering and veraison; however, heat stress and water deficiency can also cause substantial variability during the interval between veraison and ripening. Hence, site-specific measurements of VI under local meteorological and environmental conditions (e.g., soil chemistry) can further refine measurement accuracies that can be used to enhance plant health and crop yield estimations.

Vineyards are similar to orchards in that the plants, once established, can remain in the soil and be productive for several decades. Various inter-row management strategies can be used in vineyards. Under the grapevine plants, soil management events can be performed frequently (e.g., weed control) to ensure optimal plant growth. Inter-row soil management can experience different methods, such as cover crops, grasses, or tilled rows17 with varying levels of soil management techniques. One of the most common practices is the grassed inter-rows, where perennial grass is grown between rows. There are several benefits of having grassed inter-rows in vineyards, it can be relatively low maintenance (e.g., requiring mowing a few times a year), reduce soil erosion18can support larger machinery for grapevine care19or keeps microbial diversity and density less affected20and lowers carbon emissions21. After the grapevines have developed a deep root structure, the plants can retrieve soil moisture from deeper layers for growth22hence, less affected by shorter drought periods.

The time of the year when measurements are taken is crucial to be able to predict plant stress, such as nutrient deficiency or soil water extremes, in a timely manner. Some areas might experience stress from different origins, such as abiotic e.g., light deficiency or water and heat stress23which are the most important types of stress that should be monitored for fast mitigation purposes. Other soil management related stress such as soil compaction can change soil hydrological parameters24,25affecting water movement toward deeper vine roots. Most of these stresses can have distinct temporal dynamics and spectral responses, hence, its studies can help with mitigating measures. Plant phenological stages are distinct times when the plant requirements are shifting. At early stages of plant growth (i.e., for grapevines after budburst to flowering), VIs such as NDVI, LAI, or chlorophyll content can be lower, and when advancing into maturity, these number can reach their maxima26. Hence, excessive data from different seasons or plant phenological stages and the effects of different years are recommended to draw a reliable picture of plant development or its response to environmental changes.

However, as grapevines are planted in rows, leaf or plant measurements are needed to capture their changes, while more homogeneously covered areas (e.g., high fractional coverages) such as grasslands, meadows, or forests, can have larger scale above-canopy measurements using proximal sensors. Techniques, such as spectral reflectance sensors to monitor vegetation can enable farmers and researchers to acquire fast data on plant health12,27,28. Excessive development in remote sensing (RS) improved spatial resolution of plant VI, such as Sentinel 2 (S2) offers 10 m resolution with 5 days overpass time. Higher spatial resolution options, such as UAVs, are available but can be expensive to attain the required data. These tools allow for the evaluation of large areas and the identification of areas of concern, which can be crucial in agricultural settings. Data gathering from RS can require further ground truth measurements, as heterogeneous areas can only provide average vegetation index values. This is especially relevant for orchards or vineyards, as between woody vegetation ground cover can vary from bare soils to fully covered with vegetation. As a result, spectral signals from specific plant species may represent other plant species, complicating interpretation. S2 VI were successfully used for vineyards without inter-row vegetation or bare soil inter-row29hence the type of inter-row management is also a crucial factor for RS data interpretation4. It has also been noted that S2 imagery can only be used to a limited extent in vineyards due to the influence of inter-row vegetation, but it can be effective in identifying spatial patterns29. Inter-row soil covers in vineyards can take up a large area of the RS pixel, therefore, it is very important to calibrate the method used with field measurements such as handheld devices or UAVs, for RS values to be reliable.

Natural and semi-natural grasslands and pastures are important land use types with both environmental and economic importance. Compared to croplands or vineyards, grasslands and pastures experience lower levels of anthropogenic interference, resulting in ecosystems that are less affected by soil disturbances2. We can theorize that data from grassland adjacent to the vineyard, characterized by similar environmental conditions, may provide a useful reference point for spectral analysis and can be incorporated with grapevine data for more precise grapevine health indices. To do so, we need to take field measurements to gather the necessary information by making well-supported connections. A recent study demonstrated that the spatial variability of vegetative vigor of cover crops can significantly correlate with that of vines as a function of soil parameters measured in the field30. Combining VI with soil analysis can enhance our understanding of vine vigor by linking spectral signals from ground vegetation to underlying soil conditions. Hence, incorporating data from nearby grasslands may improve the interpretation of VI in vineyards, especially with grassed inter-rows. RS data can offer important information, some shortcomings still can complicate its use. The time of the satellite pass is a crucial factor, as it might occur during cloudy days31,32hence no usable data (e.g., S2) can be derived and no information will be available about plant health. The frequency of the satellite passes is another important factor (e.g., Landsat series are less frequent than S2), or the resolution is too low for farm-scale studies (e.g., MODIS). While paid data might be a solution, often freely available data is preferred. Previous information retrieved from earlier satellite passings, or field measurements can be used to estimate vineyard areas of concern. With large amounts of field measurements, gathered over large grid sizes (spatial) or several years (temporal) might provide a more recent possibility of being used as training data for machine learning (ML) techniques. ML algorithms such as linear or non-linear models have gained momentum in being used to analyze large numbers of collected data while identifying complex relationships between VI from field measurements and RS, or environmental factors. A variety of ML models such as linear regression (LR), or more advanced ones such as random forest (RF) or extreme gradient boost (XGBoost) are available and frequently used for evaluating complex connections, and predicting plant growth outcomes from varying soil environmental and meteorological factors33–36. Despite the growing application of these techniques, their use in vineyard-specific data remains scarce in the current literature.

The primary aim of the present study was to improve the interpretation of vineyard vegetation indices by integrating environmental parameters and auxiliary grassland data. Our focus was on field VI data for grapevines, assuming that including grassland data extracted from satellite imagery can be a useful tool to better estimate plant development and yield in vineyards with grassed inter-row. Therefore, our objectives were.

(i)to analyze vegetation data gathered from both field measurements and RS;(ii)to determine the relationship between the vegetation data and environmental variables such as soil physical and chemical properties; and.(iii)to investigate the potential of including grassland-derived VI data from a nearby site to enhance grapevine VI analysis, particularly in vineyards with grassed inter-row soil management.

Materials and methods

Site description

The study areas are located in the Csorsza catchment in the Balaton Uplands region of Hungary (Fig. 1). The population of the study catchment is highly dependent on viticulture. The experimental locations included two vineyard sites (Balatoncsicsó Vineyard – BCS and Gergely Vineyard – GB) and a grassland. The investigated vineyards and the grassland sites have a continental climate, presenting hot summers and cold winters. The present study was implemented in 2020 and 2021, focusing on the growing or vegetation period (May to October). The sites have an annual precipitation total of 545 mm (2016–2024), while in 2021 the area experienced a substantial precipitation decrease (399 mm), the lowest among data from recent years12. All three research sites rely solely on rainfall, with no irrigation applied.

Vegetation indices and soil water content (SWC) measurements

In 2020 and 2021, field measurements were performed from fixed monitoring stations at both vineyard sites. Each vineyard site was equipped with three monitoring sensor sets of PRI, NDVI, Photochemically Active Radiation (PAR; METER Group), and soil water and temperature sensors (5TM or TEROS 12; METER Group). The sensor sets were positioned at the upper, middle, and lower sections of the slope to better account for elevation-related variability. This placement strategy aimed to capture key spatial differences in microclimate and vine vigor along the vertical gradient. One hemispherical PRI, NDVI, and PAR sensors per research site were also implemented. The field sensors were installed in a nadir view, so the distortion and shadow effects were reduced and the reflectance accuracy from the plants enhanced40. The sensors were placed 2.5 m above ground or 1 m above plant canopy. It represented 0.65 m diameter of plant canopy area measurement with fully developed vines, where up to 0.40–0.45 m radius could be measured for vine side distance from the midpoint, hence during later vegetation growth (normally from June) the sensors presented vine spectral values only with no data from inter-row vegetation. These sensors were connected to an Em50 datalogger (METER Group), which gathered the data at 10-minute intervals. We used midday spectral reflectance data, calculated from 10:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m., and averaged it prior to analysis as midday data provides the most reliable information about plant physiology.

The grassland site got a similar spectral sensor setup in 2021 measuring data at 10-minute intervals, therefore, we collected data from fieldwork using a handheld instrument equipped with the same sensors as the monitoring stations. These field measurements were taken on a bi-weekly basis, with 6 replicates per measurement point.

Volumetric soil moisture or water content and soil temperature monitoring were conducted at 15 and 40 cm below the soil surface at each study site. SWC and temperature data were logged every 10 min (EM50 datalogger; Decagon Devices Inc., USA). For further analysis, we averaged the collected data for each day (144 measurements per day) and included those days’ data when we also had available field measurements.

Soil sampling and analyses

Soil samples for chemical and physical analyses were collected in triplicates, from the upper 0–20 cm of the soil layer, representing the biologically most active topsoil highly influenced by vineyard management practice. Samples were homogenized, sieved (< 2 mm), and analyzed for total nitrogen, NH4+-N, NO3--N, K2O (ammonium lactate soluble), P2O5 (ammonium lactate soluble), soil organic carbon (SOC) content, and pHH2O. The amount of total nitrogen was determined using the modified Kjeldahl method with accuracy of 0.01 to 0.1% N (ISO 11261:1995). The amount of organic carbon was measured by wet digestion based on the Tyurin method41 with calibration curve over 0.99. K2O and P2O5 values were measured using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (Quotation ICP-OES, Ultima 2, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) after ammonium lactate extraction, following general quality assurance protocol. The soil pH was measured in 1:2.5 soil-to-water suspensions. The particle size distribution of the soil samples was measured by the sieve-pipette method. The soil-water suspension was mixed in a sedimentation cylinder, and a pipette was used to sample particles of a specific size. Soil element concentrations are expressed in mg kg[- [1 dry weight soil.

Remote sensing data

We retrieved the remote sensing data from the European Space Agency’s Sentinel-2 satellite, which provides images of an area with 10-meter resolution every five days. Four images per satellite pass covered our study sites, and the pixel values were averaged. This paper uses Level-2 A (MSIL2A) images, which have been corrected for atmospheric distortions, orthorectified, and transformed to surface reflectance. All S2 satellite overpasses were examined using the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. Cloud masking was implemented using the QA60 band, and the collected images were further inspected manually to remove thin cloud covered images that could not be reliably filtered out. A total of 74 satellite overpasses were analyzed with 37 passes in each year. Vegetation indices were calculated using the web-based GEE interface. For each usable satellite image, pixel values representing the monitoring points across all study areas were then extracted. These pixels were selected to ensure that they did not include areas bordering the vineyards. NDVI values were calculated using the NIR (B8) and red (B4) bands. Sentinel-2 images from the 2020 and 2021 growing seasons were collected, and the corresponding midday values from the monitoring system for each respective day were then assigned to these pixels. In total, data from 38 different field measurements with satellite overpass occasions could be used for the analyses presented in this study (19 overpasses in 2020 and 19 overpasses in 2021; Table 1).

Data analyses

We chose three main machine learning algorithms based on a literature review of agricultural research studies, especially studying water stress signals in vineyards42. LR, RF, and XGBoost are often used and already well-accepted ML methods for predicting VI from proximal data36,43,44. LR can be defined as a linear computational model in which two or more characteristic variables are employed to explain the target variable. RF regression is a decision-tree-based integrated learning algorithm for the analysis of regression of continuous variables that can efficiently analyze the relationships between nonlinear data. XGBoost is a gradient boosting decision tree model that incorporates a regularization term into the objective function with a view to preventing overfitting45–47. XGBoost and RF are decision tree based learning algorithms that can efficiently analyze the relationships between nonlinear data. They also perform well on small datasets because they are robust to noise and less sensitive to overfitting, especially in the presence of limited datasets. While other ML models, such as support vector machines or K-nearest neighbors, could also be applied to similar problems, they were not included in this study in order to focus on a limited and interpretable set of models with demonstrated effectiveness in remote sensing and agronomic applications. The 124 available Sentinel-2 concurrent with field measurement data were randomly split 70/30 for cross-validation and iterated 1000 times. The root mean squared error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R2 were calculated for training and validation to compare the different ML techniques. Several rounds of optimization of the hyper-parameters were performed to evaluate the model performance, until further fine-tuning of the learning rate and the number of trees did not lead to any performance improvement. After the hyperparameters were set, the learning rate of the XGBoost model was set to 0.05, the maximum depth of trees to 3, and the number of decision trees to 100, and a tree node was allowed to split only if the sum of instance weights in that node reached at least 10. For the RF algorithm, the number of decision trees was set to 10000 and the nodesize to 10. The performance of each method was tested with three different input datasets. In the first step, the satellite NDVI value of the vineyards was used, in the second step this was supplemented with the variable DOY to reveal the specificities between different phenological phases, and in the last step, NDVI data from a grassland study area were included in the learning process to correct for the influence of the grassed inter-row. All ML model runs were performed using the software package R (R Core Team, Version 4.0.2).

Statistical analyses

The effects of land use types (vineyards and grassland) on soil physical and chemical properties and plant parameters (spectral reflectance and PAR values) were analyzed using nonparametric statistical analyses of the Wilcoxon test and Kruskal–Wallis test for the non-normally distributed datasets. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. The Durbin–Watson test was used to detect autocorrelation in the residuals of a regression models. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to evaluate the linear correlation between the soils’ physical and chemical characteristics and plant parameters. The descriptive multivariate statistical analyses of hierarchical cluster analysis (CA, Euclidean distance) and the principal component analysis (PCA) were applied to explore the factor patterns of the selected environmental parameters from field measurements and RS. All statistical calculations were performed using the software package R (R Core Team, Version 4.0.2). The statistical significance of the data sets was determined at p < 0.05.

Environmental parameters of the study sites

Soil physical and chemical parameters

We found significant differences in the physical and chemical characteristics of the study sites in some of the investigated parameters. The highest total N and SOC contents were measured for the grassland sites, while the highest K2O and P2O5 values were observed for the Vineyard GB (Table 2). Notable differences were also observed between the vineyards; e.g., total N and SOC were significantly higher for GB than for BCS. Regarding soil physical properties, the GB site had significantly higher clay content and significantly lower sand content compared to the other study sites. Additionally, we found significantly higher SWC for the GB site, despite the highest soil temperature data at a soil depth of 15 cm. At the depth of 40 cm, these differences were less distinguishable (Table 2).

Correlations between field and satellite retrieved plant parameters and environmental factors

The PCA analysis revealed that the first principal component (PC1) accounted for 34.42% of the variation caused by the interaction, while PC2 accounted for 25.17%, together explaining nearly 60% of the total variance of the dataset (Fig. 4). The data shows a clear partitioning of the different research sites, suggesting site-specific responses in plant and soil conditions. Based on the PCA and Pearson correlation method, we found a moderate positive correlation between leaf area and S2 NDVI (r = 0.65; p < 0.001). A significant but weaker correlation was observed between NDVIFM and PRIFM (r = 0.49; p = 0.003). The strong loading of NDVIFM and LAIFM on PC1 also supports the correlation results where a strong correlation between NDVIFM and LAIFM (r = 0.80; p < 0.001) was observed. Strong negative correlations were noted between NDVIFM and total N (r = -0.76; p < 0.001) and SOC contents (r = -0.78; p < 0.001). We found a very strong correlation between SOC and Total N contents (r = 0.97 p < 0.001). A notable negative connection between LAIFM and SOC content was also observed (r = -0.75; p < 0.001). From the VI NDVIFM and PRIFM were positively correlated to soil pH (r = 0.63 and 0.54; p < 0.001; respectively). NDVIS2 was moderately correlated to LAIFM (r = 0.56; p < 0.001) and soil silt content (r = 0.43; p = 0.008). In this study, PCA was performed for different research sites, and the first six principal components are displayed in Table 3 for the 14 investigated variables. The first component of the PCA is characterized by an eigenvalue of 5.51, the second has an eigenvalue of 4.03, PC3, PC4, and PC5 still have eigenvalues larger than 1, while the other nine components have eigenvalues below 1 and are not considered to explain the model.

Grapevine phenological phases and cluster analysis for NDVI

Our primary focus among the VI was on NDVI, particularly for method development, where we aimed to validate our hypothesis that grassland RS data can significantly enhance our understanding of grapevine status by improving data reliability. We considered three sources of NDVI data in the analysis: (i) measured in situ at the vineyard sites (vineyard FM), (ii) retrieved from S2 for all measurement points at the vineyard sites (vineyard S2), and (iii) retrieved from S2 for the grassland site (grassland S2). The NDVI data were analyzed based on plant phenological phases (Fig. 5) and by cluster analysis (Fig. 6) to see any distinguishable trends or clusters. The collected NDVI data revealed the largest differences among vineyard sites and grassland during the budbreak to flowering stages of grapevines, which is expected, as grassland begins to get greener earlier than grapevines. However, toward the veraison stage, grapevines grow leaves at an accelerated rate, causing their greenness level to exceed that of the grassland. We also found that from flowering until harvest, both the field and S2 grapevine data were significantly higher compared to the grassland (p < 0.05; Kruskall-Wallis test), with field measures having not significantly, but higher NDVI values compared to S2 (Fig. 5). The Durbin–Watson test indicated strong positive autocorrelation in the NDVI values across all three study sites, suggesting that subsequent values in the time series are significantly dependent on preceding ones. These data further indicate that S2 resolution captures values of both grapevines and grasses from the inter-row, resulting in lower overall NDVI values compared to grapevines only. However, with the line spacing of 250 cm, and the maximum of 90 cm of grapevine canopy overlapping the inter-row area, the satellite data should underrepresent the ratio of grapevine NDVI values to the inter-row vegetation.

Discussion

In the present study, we analyzed two vineyard sites with grassed inter-row soil management and investigated the associated soil and plant parameters. As a subsequent step, we incorporated data from a grassland site within the same catchment to improve our understanding of vegetation indices retrieved in vineyards. We employed remote sensing vegetation data to integrate the grassland data into the grapevine-focused analysis, which allowed for validation against field measurements and enabled us to explore the proficiency of RS as a proxy for assessing grapevine health, especially in vegetated inter-row vineyards.

The soil chemical and physical characteristics varied among research sites, which could influence vegetation growth and development. The highest total N and SOC contents were observed in the grassland; however, the lowest field measured NDVI was also associated with this research site. While the higher nitrogen and carbon content in the soil represents better environmental conditions for the plants, plant types are also highly influential factors. Some plants, such as C4 and legumes can aid in the accumulation of carbon and nitrogen in the soil48while other plants can take up high levels of nutrients during growth49. The tall grasses at our research site develop long internodal portions that dry out quickly as a part of their phenology, thus decreasing overall NDVI values. If the smaller vegetation is still green, but partially or even fully covered by the taller plants with low NDVI values, RS methods would not be able to distinguish among them as only the highest plants’ NDVI can be retrieved. Hence, the importance of the timing of VI measurements should be further emphasized, especially in species rich areas. Wang et al.50 found that NDVI was highly influenced by biodiversity along with vegetation percent cover, canopy growth stage, and changes in phenological phases, which is similar to our grassland site. Smaller, more drought-tolerant plants will have higher NDVI values for longer duration; however, overall vegetation indices can change sharply after land management practices, such as cutting tall grass where satellite data can help us to detect these changes occurring from mowing or grass cutting51,52. The instruments used also play a crucial role in determining the final VI values. For instance, small handheld instruments measure areas of less than 1 cm2 while spectral reflectance sensors can assess larger areas depending on the sensor’s height above the canopy (i.e., our instruments measure 1.3 m2 when placed 2 m above the plants). Therefore, specific plant leaves may exhibit higher VI values, while the overall area yields lower numbers, due to the mixture of plant species that can produce different spectral reflectance responses at different wavelengths53. Grapevines are less sensitive to drought conditions if deeper soil layers have available moisture to retrieve due to their root structure and length54. Upper soil layers can dry out faster due to high temperatures, low precipitations, and competition from all vegetation with shallower root structures (e.g., certain inter-row grass types versus the upper root structure of grapevine). This can potentially increase water and heat-related plant stress and result in lowered VI55.

Soil textures varied among the research sites. The GB site had the highest clay content and the lowest sand content. Higher clay and silt content aids in soil water retention56which is supported by our results, as the GB site had the highest SWC content, despite the same meteorological conditions at all sites. All three sites were susceptible to soil erosion; however, the grassland was particularly eroded, with topsoil layers occasionally measuring less than 30 cm. This erosion allowed fractured bedrock pieces to comprise a significant portion of the overall landscape, which could influence soil water movement through preferential flow and restrict plant root development to the top few centimeters of soil. The BCS research site has a very thick (over 2 m) homogeneous soil layer without rock fragments present, enabling deep root development of plants. Thus, the highest NDVI and LAI measurements were observed at the BCS site, highlighting the influence of soil texture and classification on grapevine growth57. Soil moisture or soil water content is an important indicator of highly dynamic soil health conditions for plant development17. Land use types can affect soil properties and water retention2,58. Soil chemical properties can significantly influence soil physical parameters, such as higher SOC content can enhance soil moisture2 and present essential nutrient for plant growth. A recent study found that spatial variability in vine vegetative vigor is strongly associated with soil physicochemical parameters, including SOC30. In particular, that study highlights how areas with deep and structurally homogeneous soils show greater biomass accumulation and higher NDVI, consistent with what was observed in the BCS site of our study. Optimal moisture conditions can help maintain plant health and crop yield. A mixture of grass and smaller plants along with the lowest SWC in the grassland, resulted in significantly lower field-measured plant parameters (i.e., NDVI, fAPAR, and LAI) compared to the grapevines. The SWC can be a major factor resulting in changes to VI. However, NDVI might not be the best indicator of water stress, because it may approach normal values while yield decreases59. For woody plants, it might take longer to detect water stress from NDVI values, although newer techniques can provide better solutions for faster plant observations60–62. Other indices can be used to effectively show water stress, such as the normalized difference water index (NDWI) or the ratio of the water index to the NDVI63,64. However, most of these indices still require ground truth comparisons and we would only have derived data from satellite systems. Our grassland site is highly vulnerable to drought conditions due to the eroded topsoil and the high runoff generation from precipitation. The site has plants with dense root structures that further inhibit water infiltration into the deeper layers, and exacerbate both evaporation and transpiration. However, these changes can also help predict grapevine stress by carefully estimating water deficit hysteresis from this more uniform research location.

Our results explored various vegetation indices for monitoring plants, as well as the usefulness of satellite data in vineyards, employing different ML methods and vegetation types, to develop customized approaches for grapevine growth. We found that NDVI data from nearby grassland can greatly improve the predictability of vineyard NDVI when using ML techniques. This enhancement is likely due to ecological covariation between the different land covers, such as similar environmental conditions of soil characteristics, microclimate, or water availability. These factors influence plant vigor in vineyards and grasslands, hence, spectral signals from grassland can serve as a proxy for assessing variability in vineyard vegetative functioning. While many studies on VI such as NDVI measurements in vineyards can be found in the literature4,12,65separate sites are not usually included to improve VI estimates. The R2 value of 0.464 showed some correlations between measured and satellite-retrieved data; however, it cannot provide with reliable estimates from S2 data alone. While this level of correlation might be insufficient for operational applications, it reflects known challenges when comparing satellite-based NDVI to field measurements in heterogeneous canopies. The higher R2 value indicates better model performance, R2 values around 0.5 can be considered weakly or moderately sufficient for NDVI predictions when mixed plant species in agricultural fields are used for a comparison with more homogeneous plant types. This discrepancy can be further amplified when different phenological phases of grapevines are used as ground truth measures, as phenology of grassland plant types might differ from that of grapevines, as we found in our study. The associated RMSE of 0.11 ± 0.1 reflects a relatively high level of uncertainty, particularly considering that NDVI values typically range from 0.2 to 0.9 in vineyards, further underscoring model limitations. Using different ML algorithms, we found that model predictability increased when we added the day-of-the-year data, increasing model accuracy between 4 (LM) and 67% (RF; R2 = 0.47–0.62; RMSE = 0.12 ± 0.1). Further enhancement was observed when grassland NDVI data were included, resulting in up to 21% (XGBoost) additional increase in model accuracy (R2 = 0.57); however, model uncertainty remained similar (RMSE = 0.11 ± 0.1). Consistent with previous findings, non-linear regression models have been shown to outperform linear regression models when using ML66. However, when considering XGBoost, we observed lower R2 values for the general training sets and the DOY data compared to the general data comparisons. Consequently, we noted a large increase of 43% when including the grassland data. It was noted that RF and XGBoost are great options for various grapevine-related indices such as soil-adjusted VI or normalized green-blue difference index67. While we obtained similar results with RF, XGBoost was less accurate. This is similar to Thanh et al.68where the authors pointed out that RF performed better with less complex models and noisy data. In our study, RF underperformed relative to LR when only the collected data were used. However, significantly better accuracy was observed across all ML techniques when DOY was included. Therefore, we found that LR can serve as a basic estimate of plant status when information is limited. Nonetheless, including DOY can significantly improve the accuracy of RF models, and this information is typically highly accessible with S2 data. Additionally, we conclude that using grassland is highly effective when VI are calculated for various plant-related parameters, especially for vineyards with vegetation-covered inter-row soil management. Future studies should extend the input parameters by using extensive environmental variables, such as PAR data or climatic indices, to mitigate model performance differences resulting from research site characteristics.

Future research directions

In this study we investigated two vineyards and a grassland site to determine possible connections and to identify similarities and differences among the examined set of environmental parameters (soil physical and chemical characteristics, along with VI). Although this study demonstrates the potential of combining VI and ML models to assess vineyard vigor, some limitations must be considered. We found that DOY data is very important in terms of model predictivity; however, grassland plants and grapevine’s phenology have a timely shift resulting in lower model accuracy compared to fully developed plants. Hence, some more robust data needs to be used for ML techniques from grapevine budbreak phenology to flowering. Additional years of study could provide a higher model predictability during these phases. Another enhancement of the present study could include soil chemical and physical data from deeper soil layers, so the lower root structures and soil water retrieval of the grapevine plants could be further investigated. Future research should involve higher resolution UAV imagery, so we can further validate the measured spectral indices.
