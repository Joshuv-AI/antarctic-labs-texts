Elevated plasma matrix metalloproteinase 9 in schizophrenia patients associated with poor antipsychotic treatment response and white matter density deficits
Authors: Xiaojing Li, Xiujuan Wang, Yongfeng Yang, Jiahui Zhou, Xufei Wu, Jingyuan Zhao, Jianhong Zhang, Xiaoge Guo, Minglong Shao, Meng Song, Xi Su, Yong Han, Qing Liu, Tengfei Chen, Luwen Zhang, Bing Liu, Weihua Yue, Luxian Lv, Wenqiang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Oxidative stress and neuroinflammation contribute to schizophrenia (SCZ) pathology and may influence treatment efficacy. Matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) is a critical molecular node mediating the interaction between oxidative stress and inflammation, and so may influence treatment efficacy. Here we examined the associations of plasma MMP9 concentration with antipsychotic drug responses, clinical symptoms, and brain structure. A total of 129 healthy controls and 124 patients with SCZ were included in this study. Patients were monitored clinically during 8 weeks of antipsychotic treatment and classified as poor responders (n = 49) or good responders (n = 75). We then compared plasma MMP9 concentrations in healthy controls at baseline and both SCZ responder groups at baseline and after the 8-week antipsychotic treatment regimen. Cognitive function was also examined using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery. In addition, we extracted regional white matter density from magnetic resonance images of patients. Compared to healthy controls, plasma MMP9 levels were significantly elevated in poor responders at baseline and negatively correlated with both white matter density in the right superior temporal gyrus and the change in cognitive symptoms after treatment. Conversely, there was no significant difference in plasma MMP9 between good responders and healthy controls, and no associations of plasma MMP9 with cognitive symptoms or regional white matter density among good responders. Elevated plasma MMP9 is associated with poor antipsychotic drug efficacy and white matter deficits in SCZ patients, and so may be a useful biomarker to guide personalized treatment.

Introduction

Pharmacotherapy is the cornerstone of treatment for schizophrenia (SCZ), and antipsychotic medications are the primary pharmacological treatments1,2. However, it is known that a sizeable minority of SCZ patients do not respond or respond poorly to antipsychotic medications3. Therefore, there is a clinical need for a biomarker to predict drug efficacy before or early in the course of treatment. Early differentiation between good responders and poor responders could assist in the formulation of individualized treatments to reduce disability, lower health care costs, and ultimately improve long-term outcomes4,5.

Biomarkers of oxidative stress and inflammation hold great potential as clinical predictors of treatment response6,7 as redox imbalance and immune dysregulation are strongly implicated in SCZ etiopathology8–12. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are proteolytic enzymes secreted by glial and neuronal cells into the brain extracellular space that contribute to tissue remodeling during development and the progression of neuroinflammation13. Matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) is the most prevalent MMP in the central nervous system14. It is expressed in neural and glial cells in multiple brain regions, including the superior temporal gyrus (STG) and there is mounting evidence that MMP9 activity reciprocally links brain oxidative stress and neuroinflammation6,15,16. Furthermore, MMP9 can bind to various substrates, including dendrite and myelin basic protein. At the physiological level, it controls dendritic and myelin sheath development which are vital for synaptic plasticity, learning, memory and white matter development17,18. Elevated MMP9 is also associated with cognitive impairment19 and white matter injury20,21. MMP9 plays a deleterious role in the development of white matter injury after focal ischemia22. Several studies have also reported abnormal peripheral gene expression23,24 and DNA methylation of the MMP9 gene in SCZ patients25, while numerous clinical studies have reported higher blood MMP9 concentrations in patients with SCZ than healthy matched controls19,26,27. However, most such studies were cross-sectional, so there is little evidence that MMP9 contributes directly to SCZ symptoms or influences clinical responses to drug treatment.

In this study, we compared plasma MMP9 levels among healthy controls, SCZ patients showing good clinical responses to 8 weeks of antipsychotic treatment, and patients demonstrating poor clinical responses to this same treatment regimen28. In addition, we measured the levels of receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE), a downstream substrate of MMP9 associated with oxidative stress, the cognitive function and regional white matter density in patients. The aims of this study were to examine if plasma MMP9 at baseline can predict the antipsychotic treatment responses of patients with SCZ, and to investigate the relationship between plasma MMP9 and white matter density defects in SCZ patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study included an initial screening visit followed by one assessment visit for inpatients with SCZ. The initial visitation (baseline) was conducted within 1 week (±7 days) of admission to the hospital, and the follow-up period was 8 weeks (±7 days) after initiation of antipsychotic medication. The assessment visit included blood sample collection, MRI acquisition, and clinical evaluation. Demographic information and blood samples were also collected from healthy controls at baseline. Throughout the study, patients with SCZ continued their normal antipsychotic medication regimens. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University. Participant recruitment occurred from March 2021 to March 2022. All participants provided written informed consent. The trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (ChiCTR2200057153).

Subjects

A total of 129 healthy controls and 124 inpatients with SCZ were enrolled in this study. Healthy controls were recruited from the local community through advertisements and the 124 inpatients were recruited from the Department of Psychiatry of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University. The inclusion criteria for patients were (1) Han Chinese ethnicity, (2) age 18‒55 years, (3) meeting the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria for schizophrenia based on a structured clinical interview for DSM-IV-TR disorders (SCID), (4) Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score ≥ 60, and (5) providing written informed consent. Healthy controls were required to meet criteria (1), (2), (5) and did not have any history of psychiatric illness themselves, first or second-degree relatives (assessed with Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview). The exclusion criteria for all participants were as follows: (1) meeting the DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for schizoaffective disorder, mental retardation, generalized developmental disorder, delirium, dementia, memory impairment, or other cognitive impairment; (2) mental disorders due to substance abuse, serious and unstable physical disorders, confirmed diabetes, thyroid disease, hypertension, heart disease, narrow-angle glaucoma, or epilepsy; (3) meeting DSM-IV-TR diagnostic criteria for alcohol or drug dependence; (4) drug-induced malignant symptoms, severe extra pyramidal symptoms (EPSs) such as tardive dyskinesia; (5) receiving electroconvulsive shock treatment without convulsions (MECT) within one month prior of enrollment; (6) planning to become pregnant, currently pregnant, or breastfeeding. The exit criteria were (1) withdrawal of informed consent and (2) deemed to be in the best interest of the participant for any reason according to the investigator (such as intolerance to blood collection).

Determination of plasma MMP9 and RAGE levels

We collected 5 mL of fasting blood by venipuncture on the first day of the study and at week 8. The samples were centrifuged, and the plasma was preserved at −80 °C until further testing. Plasma levels of MMP9 were measured using Quantification human ELISA kits (KE00164, Proteintech, USA). The minimum detectable concentration was 10.0 pg/mL, and all values obtained from plasma samples were above this value. We randomly selected 22 samples from the good responders and 22 from the poor responders for OLINK assay of plasma RAGE concentration (SINOTECH GENOMICS, Shanghai, China).

Image data acquisition and preprocessing

We collected MRI data from 65 patients at baseline and at week 8 using a 3.0-T MR scanner (Siemens, Verio, Germany). During data collection, participants were required to stay awake, relax, close their eyes, and remain as still as possible. Acquisition parameters for T1-weighted scans were as follows: repetition time = 2530 ms, echo time = 2.43 ms, inversion time = 1100 ms, flip angle = 7°, matrix size = 256 × 256 × 192, and voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm. Foam pads and earplugs were used to reduce head motion and scanner noise.

White matter density maps were obtained from T1 images of each subject using the computational anatomy toolbox (CAT12, http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/) of the Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) package. White matter density were then extracted based on the 246 partitions of the Brainnetome atlas29.

Assessment of psychiatric scales

Disease severity was measured using the 30-item PANSS at baseline and again on week 8. The scale includes three subscales30: two seven-item subscales for positive and negative symptoms and a 16-item subscale covering general psychopathology. Clinical outcome was quantified as the percentage reduction in PANSS total score after the 8-week antipsychotic drug treatment program (the endpoint) [(PANSS baseline—PANSS endpoint) / (PANSS baseline–30) × 100%]. Patients were categorized as good responders if the decline in PANSS score was ≥ 50% and as poor responders if the decline was <50% according to previous studies31,32. The MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) was used to assess cognitive status. The MCCB assesses seven cognitive domains33,34: speed of processing, attention, working memory, verbal learning, visual learning, reasoning and problem solving, and social cognition (untested). This study used the published and approved translation of the MCCB for China and the Chinese normative and standardized data correction. Raw scores were converted to eight domain T-scores using the MCCB scoring program. The patients were reassessed at baseline and after treatment.

All evaluators received uniform training prior to the study and had extensive experience testing patients with SCZ. All evaluators and the plasma sample tester worked independently to ensure objectivity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Science version 26.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and statistical graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). All continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Distributions were tested for normality of distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Except for the white matter density, the remaining data did not follow normal distribution. Based on these results, age and years of education were compared among the 3 groups using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, and sex ratio using the chi-square test. White matter density in the two SCZ groups was compared by independent samples t-test, and the rest of the results from the two SCZ groups were compared by Mann–Whitney U-test. Post-treatment values were compared to baseline values within groups using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlations were examined with Spearman’s rank correlation. The baseline characteristics age, sex, education, duration of illness, CPZE, and plasma MMP9 levels were included in a multivariate binary logistic regression model for prediction of poor antipsychotic treatment response. All P values of multiple comparisons were corrected for false discovery rate (FDR). For all analyses, a P < 0.05 (2-tailed) was deemed statistically significant.

Plasma RAGE in good versus poor responders and associations with MMP9

In contrast to MMP9, baseline plasma RAGE did not differ between good responders and poor responders according to OLINK assays (n = 22 samples per group, Supplementary Fig. 1). Among poor responders, however, baseline plasma RAGE was positively correlated with baseline plasma MMP9 (r = 0.729, P < 0.001) and the change in plasma MMP9 after treatment was negatively correlated with the change in RAGE (r = 0.523, P = 0.012). Correlations of plasma MMP9 with white matter density in good and poor responders

A total of 48 brain regions white matter density were extracted based on the Brainnetome atlas. Each brain region contains different subregions, and the 48 brain regions contain 246 subregions in total29. We extracted regional white matter density based on the Brainnetome atlas from magnetic resonance images of 38 good responders and 27 poor responders. Firstly, among poor responders, we found that baseline plasma MMP9 was negatively correlated with white matter density in right STG and this correlation persisted after FDR correction (r = –0.607, P = 0.001, P_FDR = 0.048) (Supplementary Table 1). The right STG contains six subregions (Brainnetome atlas lable 70,72,74,76,78,80). To further explore the relationship between baseline MMP9 and white matter density in the right STG in poor responders, we performed correlations between baseline MMP9 and the six subregions of the right STG. We found that only two subregions, 74(r = –0.617, P = 0.001, P_FDR = 0.003) and 80(r = –0.614, P = 0.001, P_FDR = 0.003), showed the correlation. While there were no significant correlations among good responders (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Table 2). Discussion

This is a longitudinal study demonstrating that the elevated plasma MMP9 concentration observed in patients with SCZ is associated with poor antipsychotic drug response. Compared to healthy controls, baseline plasma MMP9 concentration was significantly elevated in SCZ patients showing a poor antipsychotic drug response but not in patients exhibiting a good response. Baseline plasma MMP9 was negatively correlated with the percentage reduction in PANSS total score, and the adjusted binary logistic regression model also indicated that elevated plasma MMP9 increased the risk of poor antipsychotic drug response among SCZ patients. Further, baseline plasma MMP9 concentration was negatively correlated with white matter volume in the right STG and with the change in cognitive function among the poor responders. Thus, high plasma MMP9 may be a useful predictive biomarker for antipsychotic drug response and a contributor to the structural brain abnormalities observed in SCZ.

Baseline plasma MMP9 concentration was numerically higher in our SCZ patient cohort (p = 0.096), consistent with previous studies examining MMP9 in SCZ patients19,26,35,36 and notably also with a Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database entry showing higher MMP9 mRNA expression in cadaveric brain samples of SCZ patients compared to healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 3). While the cause of elevated plasma MMP9 in SCZ is still uncertain, it is known that MMP9 can promote oxidative stress, inflammation, synaptic plasticity and involved in blood-brain barrier damage, all of which are implicated in disease pathogenesis18,35,37,38. For instance, MMP9 can both modulate neuroinflammation pathways directly and act as a neuroinflammatory signaling molecule39,40. In addition, it has been shown that MMP9 promotes neuroinflammation by regulating the extracellular shedding of RAGE immunoglobulin superfamily type I membrane glycoproteins and mediates the deleterious effects of oxidative stress on development6,41. This higher plasma MMP9 concentration in poor responders to antipsychotic medication was predictive of higher plasma RAGE, also suggesting that upregulation of MMP9 may contribute to the pathogenesis of SCZ via oxidative stress and neuroinflammation15,42. Glutathione acts as an important reducing agent in many redox reactions and the phenotypes of glutathione synthesis-deficient animals suggest that insufficient GSH, oxidative stress, and ensuing MMP9 elevation are involved in the SCZ pathogenesis, potentially via RAGE shedding, NF-kB activation, secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, microglia activation, and neuroinflammatory damage. Indeed, inhibition of MMP9 at early developmental stages can prevent SCZ-like defects in adulthood6, while several clinical trials have found that the MMP9 inhibitor minocycline can improve the negative and cognitive symptoms of SCZ43,44.

Oxidative stress and neuroinflammation can influence the effectiveness of antipsychotic treatment6,7,45. Indeed, one such study found an inverse association between systemic inflammation as evidenced by elevated peripheral proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TFN-γ) and poor treatment response among patients with first-episode psychosis46. Similarly, baseline expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α mRNAs were found to be higher in patients with treatment-resistant depression47,48. MMP9 is a key molecular node in the molecular pathway linking oxidative stress and inflammation, and therefore may have broad effects on therapeutic efficacy. We found that higher plasma MMP9 levels at baseline were associated with lower PANSS reduction, indicating a poorer response to antipsychotic medication. In addition, univariate binary logistic regression and adjusted binary logistic regression models showed that elevated plasma MMP9 levels increased the risk of poor response to antipsychotic medication. One study found that plasma MMP9 significantly increased in treatment-resistant SCZ patients treated with clozapine49. Based on these results, we suggest that high MMP9 is a useful biomarker for predicting the efficacy of antipsychotic drug treatment.

Moreover, these patients continued to respond poorly even though elevated MMP9 levels returned to normal after 8 weeks of antipsychotic treatment, consistent with previous studies50–52. We suggest that this 8-week treatment period may be too brief to reverse redox dysregulation and neuroinflammation, thereby maintaining SCZ symptoms. These patients may therefore require longer drug therapy.

In poor responders, we found that elevated MMP9 levels were negatively correlated with white matter density in the right STG at baseline, suggesting that elevated MMP9 may remodel white matter in the right STG. It is well known that the components of white matter, such as axons and oligodendrocytes, are highly sensitive to oxidative stress. Therefore, white matter is vulnerable in diseases associated with inflammation and oxidative stress53. MMP9 is a critical molecular node mediating the interaction between oxidative stress and inflammation6,41. Redox imbalance and immune dysregulation are strongly implicated in cerebral stroke54 and SCZ8 etiopathology. Several studies have found that MMP9 plays a deleterious role in the development of white matter injury after focal ischemia22, MMP9 could be a therapeutic target for subarachnoid hemorrhage induced white matter injury53 and MMP9 deletion effectively attenuated white matter injury in mouse models of cerebral ischemia55. Similarly, our findings revealed that MMP9 may play a role in white matter defects in the right STG region of SCZ patients. We speculate that the proteolytic effect of MMP9 on extracellular matrix structures will accelerate the assembly and disassembly of synaptic structures and disrupt the stabislity of neural networks56,57. In addition, pathological overexpression of MMP9 in neuroinflammatory states may lead to excessive synaptic pruning, leading to white matter damage in the STG region. Several structural MRI studies have reported that STG volumes are reduced in patients with SCZ58. Dysfunction of the STG, which encompasses the auditory cortex and Wernicke’s area, is strongly implicated in SCZ pathophysiology. The STG is critical for auditory information processing and integration59 as well as language comprehension60, functions impaired in SCZ.

MMP9 is pro-inflammatory. Its upregulation can cause damage to neurons and result in aberrant synaptic pruning. These impairments can lead to abnormal development of cortical circuits which can cause impaired cognitive function37. Cognitive impairment is a core characteristic of SCZ and among the best predictors of poor lifetime outcome61–63. Cognitive impairment is also a predictor of antipsychotic drug efficacy64,65.In this study, we use the MCCB to examine cognitive function in SCZ patients. MCCB is a non-invasive, relatively objective, and quantifiable method of rating the behavioral performance of brain function66, Impaired visual learning has been reported among SCZ patients and high-risk groups67. The BVMT-R component of the MCCB was used to assess visual learning function in SCZ patients68 and the MAZES component of the MCCB was used to assess the reasoning and problem-solving skills in SCZ patients. We found that baseline plasma MMP9 levels in poor responders were negatively correlated with the change in visual learning and reasoning and problem-solving skills before and after treatment, no such correlations were found in good responders. These suggested that MMP9 may affect cognitive improvement differently in different SCZ subgroups, but there were no statistical differences in cognitive scores across subgroups yet. Therefore, these data can explain very few issues. We presented these data with the hope of raising awareness about these issues, and we also hope that there will be more sample studies in the future to better explain these results.

The study has certain limitations, including the observational design, which limits causal inferences. We did not measure the levels of relevant inflammatory factors and molecules associated with oxidative stress in plasma. Besides, the patients were not medication free at baseline. Future studies must address the specific mechanisms underlying these associations among high plasma MMP9, poor antipsychotic drug response, cognitive dysfunction, and regional white matter atrophy in a subpopulation of SCZ patients.

Conclusion

Plasma MMP9 was elevated in SCZ patients showing poor antipsychotic drug responses. Moreover, this elevated plasma MMP9 was associated with a decrease in white matter volume within the right STG. We suggest that MMP9 is a potential predictive biomarker for short-term antipsychotic drug response and that identifying these patients before treatment could help guide individualized treatment to improve outcome.
