Machine learning–based preoperative classification of colorectal cancer stage using systemic inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and constitutes a significant cause of cancer-related deaths. Preoperative staging, which involves accurately distinguishing between early-stage (TNM I–II) and advanced-stage (TNM III–IV) disease, is vital for determining prognosis and planning treatment strategies, including neoadjuvant therapy or more extensive surgical interventions [1].

Traditional staging approaches, which include imaging methods and histopathological evaluation, may show limited sensitivity and specificity, particularly for detecting lymph node involvement and micrometastatic disease. In recent years, biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status, such as the hemoglobin–albumin–lymphocyte–platelet (HALP) score, platelet–lymphocyte ratio (PLR), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), and lymphocyte–monocyte ratio (LMR), have emerged as important prognostic indicators in CRC. These biomarkers reflect the host immunonutritional status, which is becoming increasingly important in determining tumor progression and treatment response [2, 3]. Machine learning (ML) methods have created a new paradigm in clinical decision-making processes by enabling the analysis of complex and multidimensional datasets. By integrating hematological and biochemical parameters, ML models have the potential to improve the accuracy of preoperative risk classification beyond traditional statistical approaches [4, 5].

This study leverages machine learning to investigate the predictive synergy between host systemic inflammatory status and nutritional biomarkers in preoperative CRC staging. Our objective was twofold: to rigorously compare the performance of diverse classifiers in identifying disease progression and to elucidate the most critical predictors that may refine personalized management strategies for patients with CRC.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This observational study examined patients with confirmed colorectal cancer (CRC) treated between January 2021 and December 2025. The aim was to create and validate machine learning models for preoperative staging. A total of 204 patients participated and were grouped according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system: early-stage (TNM I–II, n = 103) and advanced-stage (TNM III–IV, n = 101). TNM staging was determined based on postoperative histopathological findings for T and N components, while the presence of distant metastasis (M stage) was established based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings. This combined approach was used as the reference standard for model development. None of the included patients received neoadjuvant therapy before surgery. Tumor size was measured before surgery using contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT). The maximum tumor diameter from axial images was recorded as a continuous variable for analysis. To qualify for the study, patients needed definitive histopathological confirmation and complete preoperative clinical and laboratory data. We excluded patients with synchronous cancers, active systemic infections, or known blood disorders and autoimmune diseases to reduce potential confounding effects on inflammatory and nutritional markers. This study was approved by the Karabük University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2647), and all experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Calculation of ınflammatory and nutritional biomarkers

Several composite indices were calculated to assess the systemic inflammatory and nutritional status of patients. The hemoglobin–albumin–lymphocyte–platelet (HALP) index was calculated as hemoglobin (g/L) × albumin (g/L) × lymphocyte count (/L) divided by platelet count (/L). The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was calculated as follows: 10 × albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3). The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) was calculated as platelet count divided by lymphocyte count, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count, and the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) as lymphocyte count divided by monocyte count.

The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count. The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) was calculated as neutrophil count × platelet count × monocyte count divided by lymphocyte count.

The systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) was calculated as neutrophil count × monocyte count divided by lymphocyte count. The platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR) was calculated as platelet count divided by albumin level. The red cell distribution width–to–albumin ratio (RAR) was calculated as red cell distribution width divided by albumin level.

By dividing CRP (mg/L) by albumin (g/L), the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) was calculated. Patients were assigned a score of 0 for CRP ≤ 10 mg/L and albumin ≥ 35 g/L on the modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS), 1 for CRP > 10 mg/L or albumin < 35 g/L, and 2 for both CRP > 10 mg/L and albumin < 35 g/L.

The systemic inflammation score (SIS) was determined based on serum albumin and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), where patients were categorized according to previously described criteria. All formulas and parameters were used in compliance with the institutional laboratory standards.

Variables included in the machine learning models were selected based on their routine preoperative availability and previously reported associations with colorectal cancer prognosis, systemic inflammatory response, nutritional status, or tumor progression.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JASP (version 0.18.2). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because continuous variables were not normally distributed (p < 0.01), data are presented as medians (interquartile range, IQR). Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Machine learning analysis

Machine learning analyses were performed using Orange Data Mining software (version 3.40.0) following a structured workflow consistent with TRIPOD-AI reporting principles. Five supervised algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Logistic Regression (LR), Neural Network (NN), and Naive Bayes (NB), were developed to classify patients with colorectal cancer into early-stage (TNM I–II) and advanced-stage (TNM III–IV) groups. For the Neural Network model, a multi-layer perceptron architecture with a single hidden layer containing 100 neurons was used, together with the ReLU activation function and Adam optimizer. The Random Forest model was configured with 500 trees and 5 attributes considered at each split, whereas Logistic Regression used Ridge (L2) regularization. Model parameters were predefined within the Orange Data Mining environment prior to analysis.

Model performance was evaluated using stratified tenfold cross-validation to maximize data utilization and reduce sampling bias. To prevent information leakage, all preprocessing procedures, including feature scaling and selection, as well as model fitting steps, were performed exclusively within the training folds and subsequently applied to the corresponding validation fold during each cross-validation iteration. Performance was assessed using accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), precision, recall, F1 score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). Model interpretability and feature importance were evaluated using SHAP integrated into the Orange analytical workflow. A classification threshold of 0.5 was used for binary classification.

Results

We included 204 patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) in this study. Based on TNM staging, 103 patients (50.5%) were classified as early-stage (Stage I–II) and 101 patients (49.5%) as advanced-stage (Stage III–IV). The median age was 68.00 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 62.00–76.50) in the early-stage group and 70.00 years (IQR: 62.00–77.00) in the advanced-stage group (p = 0.66). The sex distribution was comparable between the groups, with 61 men (59.22%) and 42 women (40.77%) in the early-stage group versus 58 men (57.42%) and 43 women (42.57%) in the advanced-stage group (p = 0.79).

The confusion matrix analysis demonstrated balanced classification performance across early- and advanced-stage groups for all evaluated models (Fig. 2). The RF model achieved the highest classification consistency, correctly identifying most early- and advanced-stage cases with minimal misclassification rates. The GB and LR models showed comparable classification patterns, whereas the NN and NB models exhibited relatively higher rates of misclassification, particularly near the decision boundary between stages.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further confirmed strong discriminative ability (Fig. 2). Ensemble-based algorithms demonstrated the largest area under the curve, indicating a good sensitivity–specificity balance across varying classification thresholds. The RF model achieved the highest AUC, followed closely by GB and LR, reflecting stable predictive performance across the validation samples. An importantly steep initial receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve ascent, followed by early plateau formation, indicates high sensitivity without substantial loss of specificity. This pattern supports the clinical applicability of biomarker-based ML models for distinguishing disease stage while minimizing false-positive classifications.

Model explainability was evaluated using SHAP, with feature importance distributions presented in Fig. 3. Across the Random Forest and Gradient Boosting models, systemic inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers consistently demonstrated the strongest contributions to model predictions; HALP emerged as the dominant predictor in these ensemble models, exhibiting the widest SHAP value dispersion and the greatest overall impact on model output. In contrast, in the Logistic Regression model PLR was the single largest contributor to predictive output, with PIV and the systemic inflammation index (SII) showing notable secondary importance in that model. SHAP summary plots provided a detailed explanation of the model behavior, consistent with the clinical distributions observed in Table 2 and Fig. 1.

SHAP-based feature importance and impact plots for machine learning models used in colorectal cancer stage classification

In the SHAP analysis, positive SHAP values indicate contributions toward advanced-stage classification (TNM III–IV), whereas negative values favor early-stage predictions (TNM I–II).

High values of HALP, LMR, and PNI were associated with predictions favoring early-stage (TNM I–II), whereas high values of PLR, SII, PIV, and tumor markers (CEA and CA 19–9) produced SHAP contributions that pushed predictions toward advanced-stage (TNM III–IV).

Notably, while tumor size was significant in conventional group comparisons, it exhibited relatively lower importance in the SHAP ranking, suggesting that systemic host response and nutritional status may offer superior discriminative value than tumor diameter alone in ML-driven staging. Preoperative CEA showed moderate but directionally consistent effects across models, whereas PAR demonstrated minimal impact in the ensemble models and PIV contributed a stronger secondary influence particularly within the logistic regression model. These patterns were most evident in ensemble-based models, where biomarker gradients demonstrated a clearer separation between predicted disease stages. Although tumor size demonstrated statistically significant differences between groups in conventional analysis (Table 2, Fig. 1A), it was not among the top-ranked variables in SHAP importance plots, indicating that systemic inflammatory and host nutritional responses contributed more strongly to model discrimination than tumor burden alone.

Discussion

Accurate preoperative staging remains a cornerstone of therapeutic decision-making and prognostic assessment in colorectal cancer (CRC). In our cohort, machine learning (ML) models integrating systemic inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers demonstrated clinically meaningful discrimination between early-stage (TNM I–II) and advanced-stage (TNM III–IV) diseases, underscoring the biological relevance of host-related parameters in tumor stratification. Notably, ensemble-based algorithms, particularly random forest (RF) and gradient boosting (GB), consistently outperformed conventional approaches, suggesting that nonlinear learning frameworks are better suited to capture complex interactions between inflammatory status, metabolic reserve, and tumor progression. Importantly, feature importance patterns differed between model families: while HALP was the dominant predictor in the ensemble models, the Logistic Regression model assigned greatest weight to PLR (with PIV/SII contributing more strongly in that model), indicating that different algorithms may prioritize complementary biological signals.

Differences in SHAP-derived feature importance across models likely reflect the distinct mathematical structures of the algorithms [6]. Ensemble-based methods can capture nonlinear interactions and higher-order relationships among biomarkers, whereas logistic regression primarily reflects linear additive associations [7]. Accordingly, HALP emerged as the dominant predictor in tree-based models, while PLR demonstrated greater relative influence in logistic regression. The clinical plausibility of these findings is supported by prior evidence linking HALP and PLR to prognosis in colorectal cancer [8, 9]. These biomarkers may reflect complementary biological dimensions of tumor progression, including systemic inflammation, nutritional depletion, immune dysregulation, and cancer-associated thrombocytosis [10–12].

These findings reinforce the accumulating evidence that biomarker-driven ML models can enhance preoperative staging accuracy and may serve as complementary decision-support tools within precision oncology workflows for CRC management [13, 14].

Systemic inflammation and nutritional status have increasingly been recognized as key contributors to tumor progression, immune evasion, and oncologic outcomes [10]. Our findings showed that HALP, PLR, LMR, PNI, and PIV were among the strongest predictors of tumor stage; however, the relative ranking of these features was model-dependent. Specifically, HALP consistently emerged as the leading feature in Random Forest and Gradient Boosting models, whereas PLR exhibited the largest contribution in the Logistic Regression model (with PIV and the systemic inflammation index showing notable secondary influence in that model) [15]. These results are consistent with those of prior studies demonstrating that elevated platelet-driven inflammatory responses and impaired nutritional reserves correlate with aggressive tumor biology and nodal or distant spread [11].

HALP and PNI, which integrate albumin and lymphocyte-related parameters, likely reflect both systemic inflammation and host nutritional status [8]. Lower values observed in patients with advanced-stage cancer may indicate cancer-related cachexia, immunosuppression, or chronic inflammatory burden [12]. Similarly, PLR and PIV represent proinflammatory hematologic indices linked to tumor-induced thrombocytosis and altered immune surveillance [16]. The prominence of these biomarkers in the SHAP analysis suggests that ML models prioritize biologically meaningful features rather than purely anatomical metrics [6].

Given that several biomarkers are derived from overlapping hematologic parameters, multicollinearity may be present. However, tree-based algorithms such as Random Forest and Gradient Boosting are relatively robust to correlated features, and therefore, the impact on model performance may be limited [17]. In contrast, multicollinearity may have influenced coefficient estimation and relative feature contribution in linear models such as Logistic Regression, and therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Among the evaluated models, RF and GB achieved higher AUC and accuracy than logistic regression and Naïve Bayes [18]. Ensemble algorithms are inherently capable of modeling nonlinear relationships and complex feature interactions, which may explain their enhanced performance in this multidimensional dataset [7]. Importantly, a confusion matrix analysis indicated that ensemble models demonstrated particularly strong performance in early-stage prediction, with direct clinical implications [19]. Accurate identification of early-stage disease may influence surgical planning, the extent of lymphadenectomy, and the selection of patients who may avoid unnecessary neoadjuvant therapies [20].

Interestingly, tumor size, although significant in conventional group comparisons, did not emerge as a leading predictor in the SHAP analysis across models; this finding persisted even when examining model-specific SHAP rankings, further suggesting that host inflammatory/nutritional state contributed more strongly than diameter alone to the algorithms' stage discrimination [21].

Recent studies have highlighted the utility of inflammation-based indices, such as NLR, PLR, and SII, in predicting survival and recurrence in CRC [15]. However, most prior investigations relied on traditional regression-based approaches [22]. By applying ML algorithms, our study extends this body of evidence and demonstrates that integrated biomarker profiles can enhance preoperative risk stratification [23]. The high AUC values observed in the RF and GB models are comparable to or exceed those reported in previous ML-based CRC staging studies, underscoring the potential of non-invasive hematologic markers as adjuncts to imaging [24].

Furthermore, the use of SHAP analysis enhances the interpretability of the ML framework, addressing a common criticism regarding the “black-box” nature of artificial intelligence models [25]. The identification of HALP, PLR, LMR, and PNI as dominant features across models strengthens the biological plausibility of our findings; yet the SHAP plots also reveal model-specific preferences. Ensemble models emphasize HALP-driven signals, while the linear model (LR) emphasizes PLR/PIV, underscoring the value of reporting feature importance stratified by algorithm when presenting ML results to clinicians [9].

In line with current recommendations for transparent reporting of artificial intelligence–based prediction models, this study was designed with an emphasis on methodological transparency, reproducibility, and explainability. Aligning with the TRIPOD + AI reporting principles may enhance the interpretability of machine learning findings and support their integration into clinically meaningful decision-support frameworks [26].

The integration of ML-based decision support tools into preoperative evaluation may enhance individualized treatment planning [27]. As the biomarkers used in this study are inexpensive and routinely available in clinical practice, this approach may be readily translated into real-world settings without additional patient burden [28]. Such tools may complement radiologic staging by helping to identify patients with biologically aggressive diseases who could benefit from intensified treatment strategies or closer surveillance.

The aim of the present study was not to replace pathological staging, but to investigate whether routinely available preoperative biomarkers could predict the final pathological stage. It is important to note that the objective of this study was not to compare ML-based predictions with conventional imaging-based preoperative TNM staging directly. In routine clinical practice, preoperative staging primarily relies on imaging modalities that reflect anatomical tumor characteristics. In contrast, the present ML approach leverages systemic inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers, capturing host-related biological responses that may represent a complementary dimension of disease progression.

Tumor size derived from preoperative CT and radiologic assessment of distant metastasis were incorporated into the clinical evaluation process; however, comprehensive standardized imaging-based TNM staging variables were not uniformly available in a structured format for direct comparative modeling in this retrospective cohort. Therefore, a direct statistical comparison was not feasible.

A baseline model based solely on conventional clinical variables was not constructed, which limits the ability to assess the incremental benefit of biomarker-driven ML models over traditional approaches.

Future prospective studies integrating both imaging-based staging and biomarker-driven ML models may help to clarify their complementary roles in clinical decision-making.

Although tumor size demonstrated a significant difference between the early- and advanced-stage groups, it did not emerge as a dominant feature in the SHAP analysis, highlighting the divergence between traditional statistical associations and ML-derived predictive importance [29].

Given the relatively high number of variables compared to the sample size, there is a potential risk of overfitting despite the use of cross-validation.

Although internal validation using stratified cross-validation was performed, external validation using independent multicenter datasets is essential to confirm the robustness and generalizability of these models before clinical implementation. This finding suggests that systemic inflammatory and nutritional markers may better reflect underlying tumor biology and classification boundaries than size alone, underscoring the value of explainability tools when interpreting model behavior. Future investigations should prioritize external multicenter validation and the integration of radiologic, radiomics-based, and molecular variables to enhance generalizability and biological depth. Prospective studies are also warranted to determine whether biomarker-driven ML models can meaningfully influence surgical decision-making, neoadjuvant treatment selection, and long-term oncologic outcomes, while advanced ensemble strategies and calibration-focused analyses may further refine clinical applicability beyond conventional performance metrics [30].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Although internal validation was performed using stratified cross-validation, external validation in independent multicenter datasets is still necessary to confirm model robustness and generalizability. Therefore, the reported model performance should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of clinical utility. Second, advanced radiomic and molecular tumor features were not incorporated and may further enhance predictive accuracy in future investigations. Finally, despite strict exclusion criteria, residual confounding related to systemic inflammatory variability cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that machine learning models incorporating systemic inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers show potential as a decision-support tool pending external validation. These models may help differentiate early-stage from advanced-stage colorectal cancer in the preoperative setting. Ensemble algorithms showed the highest predictive performance, while explainable AI analyses highlighted potentially relevant predictors such as HALP, PLR, LMR, and PNI. However, larger multicenter studies with external validation are needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings.
