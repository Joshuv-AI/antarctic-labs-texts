Evaluation of planetary health in family medicine specialization theses
Authors: Zeynep Ö. Erinç, Özden Gökdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction and Objective:The effects of climate change and environmental degradation on human health are becoming increasingly apparent. In this context, the role and trends of family medicine, which is central to primary health care, are of paramount importance. This study aimed to evaluate the degree to which the concepts of planetary health and One Health are addressed in family medicine dissertations in Turkey.Method:This is a retrospective review study designed in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. Data were obtained by scanning Family Medicine theses published between 2001 and 2025 from the Turkish National Thesis Center database. Theses using the terms “planetary health” and “one health” in their title or abstract were incorporated into the search method. Data were categorized by publication year, institution, advisor name, and thematic content and analyzed using descriptive statistical methods.Findings:A total of 579 theses were reviewed, and two duplicate theses were excluded. Only 5 theses (0.86%) were identified that included the specified terms in their abstract or title. All these theses were published in 2024, and it was observed that the advisors were concentrated among certain individuals. The examined theses revealed that although family physicians have positive attitudes toward planetary health, significant knowledge gaps exist, the concept of One Health is largely unheard of (78.3%), a family health center’s carbon footprint is largely driven by transportation (76.35%), and there is a strong correlation between ecological intelligence and healthy living awareness.Conclusion:The research findings indicate that planetary health themes are very limited in family medicine specialty theses. This contrasts with the World Health Organization’s emphasis on this topic. Integrating planetary health into primary care requires updating medical curricula (climate change modules, One Health principles), expanding practical measures such as social prescribing, and promoting sustainable healthcare practices.

Introduction

Planetary health is a field that examines the connections between human health and environmental health from a holistic perspective.[1] The impacts of rising global temperatures on pandemics and public health demonstrate why planetary health is a vital issue.[2]

Family medicine is the physician community most likely to contribute to planetary health through its preventive medicine and community-oriented approach.[3] When community-based practices are combined with interventions addressing income-related health issues, the relationship between the family medicine model and planetary health becomes clearer.[4]

In Turkey, family medicine residency training focuses on preventive medicine, which is a branch of the WONCA Tree. While residency training programs primarily focus clinical practice and individual patient care, the concept of planetary health is reported to be weakly emphasized in teaching. A study conducted in Ankara indicated that family medicine residents have limited opportunities to practice in areas such as environmentally induced diseases and occupational illnesses.[5] In our study, we plan to examine family physicians’ interest in planetary health within family medicine specialization theses. By exploring this interest through research, effective plans can be made to develop the One Health concept.

Method

This bibliometric analysis study aims to systematically assess planetary health and One Health topics within family medicine specialization theses in Turkey. The research is a retrospective screening study conducted based on the PRISMA guidelines.[6] The PRISMA flow diagram is provided in Table 1.

The National Thesis Center database provided the study’s data. As a search strategy, theses containing the words “gezegen sağlığı” (planetary health), “planetary health,’ “tek sağlık” (One Health), and “one health” in their titles or abstracts were included. Specialization theses in family medicine published between 2001 and 2025 were included in the study. Animal studies, review articles, and theses whose full text could not be accessed were excluded.

Following data extraction performed by two independent researchers, the authors resolved discrepancies by reaching a consensus among themselves. The data were classified according to publication year, institution, supervisor’s name, and thematic content. Descriptive statistical methods were used for the analyses, performed using Microsoft Excel 2016.

Results

The National Thesis Center’s official website was used to assess family medicine specialization theses published between 2001 and 2025 for the study. The study included theses with the terms “Planetary Health” or “One Health” in their abstract or title.

Two duplicate theses were eliminated from the 579 theses that were reviewed. Five theses (0.86%) were found to have the designated terms in their abstract or title. All these theses were published in 2024.

The thesis titled Investigation of Family Physicians’ Level of Knowledge and Awareness Regarding One Health[8] was analyzed. This cross-sectional study, planned by the Department of Family Medicine at Akdeniz University Faculty of Medicine, evaluated Turkish family physicians’ knowledge and awareness of the One Health approach in the context of global health threats emerging from the COVID-19 pandemic. A 20-item Likert-type questionnaire assessing sociodemographic data and One Health knowledge was administered to 355 family physicians across Turkey. Results revealed that the vast majority of participants (78.3%) had never heard of the One Health concept, though most demonstrated cautious antibiotic prescribing practices reflecting awareness of antimicrobial resistance. Neither age nor professional experience significantly influenced willingness to learn about One Health (P > 0.05). These findings underscore the necessity to integrate core One Health principles into family medicine curricula and implement continuous professional development programs. The study emphasizes that strengthening primary healthcare workers’ capacity in integrated human-animal-environment health governance is critical for pandemic preparedness, emphasizing the significance of a coordinated strategy to reduce potential health risks.

The thesis titled Evaluation of the Relationship Between Ecological Intelligence and Healthy Living Awareness Among First- and Final-Year Students in the Faculties of Veterinary Medicine and Environmental Engineering[9] was evaluated. This study examined the correlation between ecological intelligence and healthy living awareness among medical, veterinary, and environmental engineering students (n = 350) at Bursa Uludağ University through the lens of One Health and planetary health perspectives. Results from the Ecological Intelligence Scale for Adults and Healthy Lifestyle Awareness Scale revealed that female gender, smoking cessation, and eco-friendly product usage showed significant positive correlations with ecological intelligence (P < 0.05), while marital status, smoking cessation, positive health perception, and eco-friendly product preference were associated with healthy living awareness. The most important result was that ecological intelligence and awareness of healthy living were strongly positively correlated (P < 0.05). These results underscore the importance of the interdisciplinary One Health concept, indicating that environmental awareness interventions could serve as tools for improving individual and community health outcomes.

Concurrently, the thesis Investigation of Family Physicians’ Perspectives, Knowledge, and Sensitivity Levels Regarding Climate Change and Its Health Impacts[10] was analyzed. This cross-sectional study by Akdeniz University Faculty of Medicine assessed climate-health knowledge among 401 Turkish family physicians using a 43-item five-point Likert scale. While 82% of participants demonstrated climate change knowledge, this understanding primarily focused on direct health impacts (68%). Notably, awareness of environmental/global effects significantly increased with age (r = 0.42), professional experience (r = 0.39), and academic rank (P < 0.05). Although 73% accurately identified climate impacts on respiratory/infectious diseases, knowledge gaps emerged regarding indirect effects like food security (41%) and water scarcity (38%). These findings necessitate integrating climate change modules covering multidimensional health effects into medical curricula and implementing professional development programs to strengthen family physicians’ community leadership roles. Discussion

This study is the pioneering study in the literature evaluating the speciality theses of family medicine residents in Turkey in terms of planetary health. The findings show that the areas directly related to planetary health are addressed in a limited number of theses of family medicine residents. This contradicts the WHO’s increasing emphasis on planetary health.[1112] On the other hand, the recent increase in publications is promising.

Izmir University of Economics, Faculty of Medicine, for example, has developed a planetary health curriculum for both students and family medicine residents; this approach should be implemented in medical schools.[13] As a result, physicians who are not familiar with the concept of planetary health are trained. Planetary health awareness is slowly emerging in family medicine. In this case, the biggest role is played by the interest of educators.[4] In the research conducted, it was observed that the supervisors of theses related to planetary health were similar people. One may argue that educators’ interests are closely linked to the idea of planetary health.

In addition to educational interest, there are positive developments in the recognition of planetary health. Additionally, freshly qualified family doctors are becoming more interested in planetary health. The European Young Family Physicians Movement (EYFDM), formerly the Vasco-da-Gama Movement, has a Special Research Group on Planetary Health[14] and WONCA Working Party on Planetary Health. This may increase the interest of newly qualified residents in the topic.

Efforts to incorporate planetary health into family medicine curricula are growing, but progress is uneven. In Europe and Canada, initiatives are underway to embed planetary health concepts in postgraduate and continuing professional development, yet many faculty and practitioners lack knowledge and confidence in this area. Barriers include curriculum overload, faculty burnout, and limited resources. Calls for evidence-based, socially accountable curricula and faculty development are common, with frameworks emphasizing systems thinking, social justice, and interconnection with nature.[111516] The curriculum should be revised, if necessary, to increase the knowledge of family medicine residents about planetary health.[5] Encouraging theses addressing topics such as planetary health during the residency period can make significant contributions to the adoption of this understanding at both academic and practical levels.[1] Various strategies can be disseminated to increase planetary health in primary care. One of these is the opportunity for preventive medicine. Family physicians can use social prescribing (suggesting nature walks, encouraging plant-based nutrition). This is a cost-effective method and will contribute to planetary health. Another strategy may be to reduce the carbon footprint of family medicine (dissemination of sustainable practices such as energy efficiency and waste management). Studies on this subject have already been carried out in specialization theses.[17]

Although climate change is acknowledged as the largest health danger of the twenty-first century, existing medical education curricula fail to represent the gravity of the climate catastrophe.[18] According to the research, planetary health issues are not well integrated into medical education. The publication of the Rockefeller Foundation-Lancet Commission on Planetary Health in 2015 marked the beginning of efforts to integrate environmental education into medical curricula.[1] For instance, just 15% of medical schools address climate change and health in their curricula, according to a survey that covered 112 nations. Furthermore, core competences such as eco-medical literacy, environmental inequality, and advocacy are reported to be insufficient.[19] However, existing postgraduate medical education frameworks need more knowledge base in the current scenario.[20] It lists the barriers to planetary health education as time constraints, curriculum overload, lack of faculty, and lack of administrative support. On the other hand, solutions such as longitudinal and interdisciplinary education models, increasing student participation, and integration with clinical practices are offered. In particular, Asaduzzaman et al.[20] emphasize the need to reduce global inequalities and develop standardized curriculum modules.

The qualitative research conducted by Bates et al.[21] in Ireland reveals the main barriers to the adoption of planetary health issues in medical education. The study findings address the challenges faced in this integration process in a multidimensional manner. According to the results, there is a perceptual problem regarding planetary health among both students and educators. A significant number of participants stated that these issues are outside of clinical medical education and are not seen as a priority. In particular, it was observed that educators with a traditional medical education approach consider planetary health issues outside the main curriculum. The report also highlighted the educators’ lack of subject-matter expertise as a barrier. Participants stated that not having sufficient expertise in the field of planetary health makes it difficult to teach these topics. In addition, the practical difficulty of making room for these topics in the already intensive medical curriculum was emphasized. Among the systemic problems identified in the research, lack of senior management support and inadequacy of evidence-based training materials stand out. Participants stated that these deficiencies at the institutional level prevented the dissemination of planetary health education. One of the biggest obstacles to implementation was found to be the lack of prioritization of these issues, particularly in curriculum creation procedures. The study’s conclusions demonstrate that before planetary health issues can be incorporated into medical education, perceptual and informational barriers must be removed. Capacity building of educators, reconsideration of curriculum design, and strengthening institutional support mechanisms can be considered as important steps in this process.[2122]

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

The most striking strength of the study is that it adopted a pioneering approach to systematically examine the concepts of ‘planetary health’ and ‘One Health’ in family medicine speciality theses. The complete search of all family medicine theses in the period 2001–2025 from the National Thesis Centre database provided a comprehensive bibliographic search that minimized selection bias. A flow diagram created in compliance with the PRISMA protocol strengthened the study’s methodological rigor and improved the transparency of the thesis selection and exclusion procedures. Quantitative findings revealed that only five out of 579 theses (0.86%) were focused on planetary health, dramatically highlighting the knowledge gap in the field.

Since the study was limited to a title/summary search based on the keywords ‘Planetary Health’ and ‘One Health,’ there is a possibility that theses that do not use these terms but deal with related topics may be overlooked. Because just five theses were analyzed, the sample size was extremely small, and the results’ generalizability was constrained.

Conclusıon

The findings of the study indicate that themes such as environmental sustainability, climate change, carbon footprint, social prescribing, and social health should be made more visible in family medicine education and research. In particular, integrating planetary health-oriented content into training curricula will both increase the level of awareness of physicians receiving speciality training and enable a more holistic approach to protecting public health. Programs encouraging planetary health for family medicine residents should be increased. In order to increase awareness, environmental history should be added to the daily anamnesis practice of physicians as emphasized in the climate change adaptation strategy and action plan 2024–2030[23] published by the Climate Change Presidency.

In conclusion, this study serves as a warning: When the health of the planet is under threat, it is unthinkable for primary health care services to remain exempt from this responsibility. Family medicine should assume a leading role in the protection of planetary health.
