Surveillance After Treatment of Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma of the Head and Neck: Review of Institutional Outcomes and Proposed Surveillance Imaging Protocol
Authors: Esther Duruchukwu, Anna Lawless, Dasantha Jayamanne, David Veivers, Andrew Wignall, Leo Pang, Joseph Chan, Mark Stevens, Thomas Eade, Adrian Lee, Alexander Guminski, Sarah Bergamin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionLong term survival for adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) arising from the head and neck declines for many decades after initial treatment due to late disease relapse. Follow up and surveillance imaging practices vary due to limited evidence as well as a lack of international guidelines (until 2021).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted for patients treated curatively for ACC between 2007 and 2024 at a single institution. Clinicopathologic information, treatment details, patterns of follow‐up (including radiologic investigations) and oncological outcomes are reported.Results51 patients comprised the study cohort. Median age was 50 years; 96% of patients underwent surgery and adjuvant radiation therapy. Median follow‐up was 7.65 years. 5‐year overall survival (OS) and disease‐free survival (DFS) were 88% and 73% respectively. 18% and 61% of patients underwent surveillance imaging within 3‐ and 12‐months of treatment completion respectively. 80% of first recurrences were detected on surveillance imaging. Half the episodes of first disease recurrence occurred within 5 years of initial treatment, a further 25% within 10 years and the remaining 25% occurred > 10 years post treatment.ConclusionThis cohort of patients curatively treated for ACC at a single institution highlights the propensity for late distant relapse as well as heterogeneity of surveillance imaging. A local institutional protocol is proposed based upon the 2021 ASCO guidelines to standardise clinical and radiologic follow‐up for this group.

Introduction

Adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) is a rare malignancy arising from the salivary glands, most commonly in the head and neck region [1]. Prognosis is variable depending upon disease location and stage at presentation. Estimated 10‐year overall survival rates are 70% for patients with localised disease and 50% for patients with distant metastases [2]. However, cancer‐specific survival continues to decline up to 30 years after diagnosis, due to propensity for late disease relapse [2, 3].

Historically, surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy have been the mainstay of treatment for localised ACC [4]. Systemic therapies are offered for metastatic disease, predominantly in the setting of high disease burden and/or symptomatic disease, but with low response rates [5]. Patterns of practice regarding surveillance imaging and follow‐up after treatment for ACC are highly variable between institutions, due to rarity, lack of evidence for intensive surveillance imaging (i.e., Detecting salvageable relapses), as well as imaging accessibility and affordability depending upon patient location. In addition, until the 2021 publication of the ASCO Guidelines on Management of Salivary Gland Malignancy [6], there was little in the way of comprehensive international recommendations. The NCCN Guidelines do not provide surveillance imaging guidelines for salivary gland malignancies, apart from those recommended for mucosal carcinomas of the head and neck [7]. The current ASCO guidelines provide some guidance on the clinical and imaging surveillance recommended for salivary gland carcinomas in general; however, gaps remain and require adaptation for ACC specifically, as well as for local institutions.

This single centre retrospective series from an Australian cancer centre aims to report the oncological outcomes and existing clinical and radiologic surveillance patterns for patients treated for ACC. The authors then propose a detailed surveillance imaging protocol, adapted from the ASCO guidelines, for ongoing use within our unit. The aim of this protocol is to improve detection of salvageable recurrence and potentially improve overall survival. Planned prospective evaluation of this new protocol will determine feasibility of this approach, as well as whether detection of treatable recurrences can be improved and unnecessary and/or costly scans (and associated patient anxiety) can be minimised.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was undertaken in an Australian tertiary cancer centre. All patients who underwent treatment for adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) between January 2007 and February 2024 were identified and extracted from a prospectively maintained database. Patients were excluded if insufficient treatment details were available. Clinical and pathologic information, treatment details (including radiologic imaging) and oncological outcomes were extracted.

Overall survival (OS) and disease‐free survival (DFS) were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Univariate Cox‐regression analyses were performed to assess the relationship between clinicopathologic features and survival. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient, treatment and follow‐up outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Ethics was approved by the Northern Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/ETH00356).

Results

76 patients were identified who were treated for ACC between 2007 and 2024. After exclusion of patients with ACC arising from a primary site outside the head or neck (n = 13, of which 11 were breast), treatment with palliative intent (n = 5), or treatment at external sites with insufficient details accessible (n = 7), there were 51 patients who comprised the study cohort.

Clinical and pathologic details are summarised in Table 1. The median age of diagnosis was 50 years, and the majority of patients were female (59%). Tumours arose from the major (53%) or minor salivary glands (47%) of the head and neck. 65% of tumours had perineural invasion. Post‐operatively, margins were confirmed as involved in 59% or close (≤ 5 mm) in 29% of patients. 49 patients (96%) underwent surgical resection and adjuvant radiation therapy (RT), with the remaining two patients undergoing definitive radiotherapy due to unresectable disease, one with the addition of chemotherapy. The majority of patients received 60 Gy in 30 fractions, with many receiving a simultaneous integrated boost to 63 Gy in the presence of involved margins (see details in Table 2).

Median follow up was 7.65 years. 36 patients were alive at the time of censoring. 1‐, 5‐ and 10‐year overall survival (OS) was 98%, 88% and 73% respectively (Figure 1). On univariate analysis, factors associated with shorter OS included worse performance status at diagnosis (hazard ratio (HR) 4.83 for ECOG ≥ 1 compared to ECOG 0 p = 0.013), higher tumour grade (HR 6.36 for poorly differentiated compared to well‐differentiated, p = 0.013) and histopathologic subtype (HR 5.22 for solid and 1.20 for tubular subtype compared to cribriform, p = 0.02) (Table 1).

61% of patients underwent routine surveillance imaging within 12 months of initial treatment. Modalities included MRI or CT of the primary site, x‐ray or CT of the lungs, or full body FDG‐PET/CT. Nine patients (18%) underwent FDG‐PET/CT scan 3 months after completion of treatment. Within the first 2 years of treatment completion, 41% underwent imaging of the primary site and 26% underwent dedicated chest imaging (in addition to those patients who underwent 3‐month FDG‐PET/CT). Half the episodes of (first) disease recurrence occurred within 5 years of initial treatment; a further 25% occurred within 10 years, with the remaining 25% occurring > 10 years post treatment (Figure 2).

Locoregional Recurrence

Six patients had first recurrence as local or locoregional only; the 1‐, 5‐ and 10‐year local recurrence free survival (LRFS) was 100%, 87% and 78% respectively (Figure 3). Of the nine patients with local recurrence (with or without distant metastases), all recurrences were within the area that received high dose RT.

Local recurrence free survival.

Disease Free Survival

1‐, 5‐ and 10‐year disease free survival (DFS) was 96%, 73% and 50% respectively (Figure 4). Overall, 20 patients (39%) experienced disease recurrence during the study period. 55% (11) of these patients had distant metastases alone as the first site of recurrence, with a further three patients having distant recurrence in combination with locoregional recurrence (Table 3). 80% of recurrences were detected on surveillance imaging, with the remaining 20% detected after patient‐reported symptoms or on routine clinical examination. Of these 20 patients with disease recurrence, nine patients had lung‐only metastases as the site of first recurrence. These were detected on surveillance imaging in eight patients, and on imaging prompted by patient‐reported symptoms in the 9th patient.

Eight of 20 patients with disease recurrence underwent upfront salvage therapy for locoregionally recurrent or oligometastatic disease. Four of eight had local or locoregional only disease and were managed with surgery (n = 2), surgery combined with adjuvant re‐irradiation and chemotherapy (n = 1) and induction chemotherapy and re‐irradiation (n = 1). The other four had lung only distant metastases and were managed with stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) (n = 3, survival 19, 44 and 56 months from time of SBRT) or pulmonary metastatectomy (n = 1, survival 94 months from metastectomy). A further two patients (of the 20 with disease recurrence) had metastatic disease and were initially observed then later received salvage treatment at oligoprogression (lung SBRT 4 years and pulmonary metastatectomy 5 years after detection, respectively). The remaining 12 patients were treated palliatively.

Discussion

This study reports 5‐ and 10‐year overall survival (OS) rates of 88% and 73%, respectively, for patients with adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) of the head and neck treated curatively, the majority with surgery and adjuvant radiation therapy (RT). Surveillance imaging was inconsistent in its application, timing and modality. Half the episodes of first disease recurrence occurred more than 5 years post‐treatment completion, and 80% were detected on surveillance imaging. The majority of relapses were distant and occurred in the lungs. These results are in keeping with the known biologic behaviour of ACC, with comparatively long survival and propensity for late distant relapse compared to other head and neck cancers. The majority of recurrences were detected on surveillance imaging, which emphasises the importance of a standardised follow‐up protocol.

Survival and Patterns of Recurrence

The 5‐ and 10‐year overall survival estimates (OS) in this study (88% and 73%) are similar to those reported by Gao et al. in their series (86% and 67%) [2]. The estimated rate of distant metastases from ACC is up to 50%, with a median time of approximately 4 years to first metastasis [2]. The current cohort had a disease recurrence rate of 39%, with half the episodes occurring in the first 5 years and 25% occurring after 10 years. Of the patients with recurrence after 10 years, three had lung‐only metastases and one patient had isolated local recurrence. Albeit small numbers, these results are comparable to previous series. Worse performance status, higher tumour grade and histopathologic subtype (solid or tubular compared to cribriform) were both associated with shorter OS. Although limited conclusions can be drawn with such small numbers, this would be of interest in future larger studies, as it may help identify subgroups of patients warranting more or less intensive surveillance.

Treatment of Oligometastatic Disease

Early detection of disease recurrence may allow patients with ACC to undergo salvage locoregional treatment and/or aggressive treatment of oligometastatic disease, which may confer a survival benefit, improve quality of life, or delay time to systemic treatment. In particular, selected patients with lung‐only metastases, which portend a more favourable prognosis than metastases at other and/or multiple sites [2, 8], may be suitable for pulmonary metastatectomy and/or stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT). In a series of 109 patients undergoing pulmonary metastatectomy for ACC, 5‐ and 10‐year survival was 67% and 41% respectively [9]. In a smaller series from Saudi Arabia of 56 patients undergoing pulmonary metastatectomy for head and neck cancer, 5‐year OS for the 11 patients with ACC was 100% [10]. Three patients in the current cohort underwent salvage SBRT, with one alive after 5 years. It is possible that more rigorous surveillance chest imaging (as is proposed below) may have detected more recurrences at an earlier stage amenable to salvage treatment.

SBRT improves OS for selected patients with oligometastatic cancers of various primary sites [11]. The currently recruiting SOLAR trial will provide further information on the role of SBRT in oligometastatic ACC, by randomising participants with oligometastatic ACC to standard of care with or without the addition of SBRT to all sites of disease [12]. Early detection of lung metastases may facilitate delivery of effective locoregional treatments, which may improve disease‐free survival or defer time to initiation of systemic therapy. This is an important consideration in a young patient cohort with potentially long survival, even after presentation with distant metastases from ACC.

Chemotherapy has traditionally been the only option for systemic treatment of metastatic ACC; although the optimal regime is not clear, with limited evidence for any regime meaningfully altering survival [13]. There are an increasing number of rare, but potentially actionable molecular targets, including RET‐fusion and BRAF V600E mutation [14, 15]. Although these hold promise, the evidence remains limited, and the role of palliative systemic therapy is still typically reserved for rapidly progressive and/or symptomatic metastatic disease where surgery and RT are not possible, thus underpinning the importance of early detection of metastatic disease amenable to local treatments.

Nature and Timing of Surveillance Imaging

Timing and modality of surveillance imaging within the study cohort was heterogeneous and markedly lower than those recommended in the recent ASCO guidelines. Specifically, only 61% of patients underwent routine surveillance imaging within the first year of treatment. This reflects real‐world practice over two decades and highlights the need for institutional review of outcomes and formalised protocols. 57% of patients underwent dedicated primary site and 26% dedicated chest imaging. Factors that may have contributed include a lack of understanding of recommended surveillance imaging, a perception that recurrences (particularly distantly) were not salvageable (or that salvage treatment would not meaningful prolong survival), or loss to follow‐up (which may be multifactorial and requiring better clinician‐directed patient education or systemic level changes to reduce this). Additionally, the absence of clear, international guidelines (prior to 2021) may have also contributed.

The 2021 ASCO guidelines recommend all patients with salivary gland malignancies undergo post‐treatment baseline imaging with contrast CT or MRI of the primary site and/or PET/CT 3 months after completion of all treatment. Following this, surveillance imaging of the primary site (contrast CT or MRI) and chest (CT) may be obtained every 6–12 months for 2 years, then from years 3 to 5 offered annually in cases of high‐grade histology or poor prognostic clinicopathologic features (which would include all ACC) [6]. Caution is made in recording the use of FDG/PET, as ACC may not always take up FDG [6]. The strength of these recommendations is considered moderate, based upon low quality evidence and obtained by informal consensus [6].

In Table 4, we summarise the current ASCO guidelines for recommended surveillance after treatment of salivary gland malignancies (in general), and alongside this, proposed guidelines for surveillance after treatment of ACC at our institution. In line with the ASCO guidelines, all patients are recommended to undergo post‐treatment imaging after 3 months. Clinical follow‐up will continue as per our current practice for mucosal head and neck cancers in general (3‐monthly for 2 years, then 6‐monthly to 5‐years), with the addition of ongoing clinical follow‐up annually from years 5 to 10, due to the propensity for late relapse in ACC (whereas patients with mucosal head and neck cancers are routinely discharged if in remission after 5 years). Our proposed protocol recommends imaging of the chest (CT) on an annual basis for 10 years and considered annually thereafter, as well as imaging of the primary site (contrast CT or MRI) recommended for the first 5 years, and to be considered on an annual basis thereafter. Yearly follow‐up including physical examination is recommended with the treating specialist(s) for at least the first 5 years after treatment and thereafter may be with the patient's general practitioner (GP). Although outside the scope of this study, we strongly recommend adherence to local and international guidelines regarding survivorship care for patients with head and neck cancers [16, 17, 18].

Prospective evaluation of this proposed institutional surveillance schedule will allow for further refinements as data becomes available. For example, the institutional results reported suggest the majority of local recurrences (LR) occur between 2 and 7 years after treatment, which might be the optimal time for intensive locoregional surveillance rather than the current recommendations for the first 5 years. Additionally, further information regarding the relationship between local recurrence and salvageable distant metastases would be useful to determine whether this group also requires more intensive distant surveillance during this period. The proposed protocol attempts to adapt rigorous international guidelines into a local context. The goal is that this will standardise current procedures and possibly facilitate early detection of recurrences and improve survival.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study of 51 patients with ACC of the head and neck reports contemporary oncological outcomes in an Australian setting. The propensity for late relapse, heterogeneity of current surveillance imaging, and high proportion (80%) of relapses detected on such imaging highlights the need for the introduction of a surveillance imaging protocol. A local adaptation of the ASCO guidelines is proposed, which will be prospectively evaluated.
