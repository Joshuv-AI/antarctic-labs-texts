A case of persistent left superior vena cava and coronary sinus ostium in the lateral right atrium
Authors: Hikaru Hagiwara, Rui Katano, Hirokazu Komoriyama, Yoshiya Kato, Toshihisa Anzai
Published: 2024

Introduction

Persistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC) is the most common thoracic venous anomaly and a component of complex cardiac pathologies. PLSVC has serious implications in patients undergoing cardiac device implantation. In addition, when implanting cardiac resynchronization therapy with a defibrillator, the coronary sinus (CS) is also a critical structure. CS anomalies have been reported in patients with atresia of the CS ostium, unroofed CS, connection with the left atrium, and even hypoplasia of the CS.1 Herein, we report a case of a patient with PLSVC and CS ostial closure with drainage to the posterolateral right atrium (RA), via courses along the RA.

Case report

A 71-year-old man with nonischemic cardiomyopathy underwent permanent pacemaker implantation 2 years ago for symptomatic sick sinus syndrome. The right superior vena cava and persistent left superior vena cava were connected by an innominate vein (Figure 1A–1E). The patient was pacemaker dependent, had decreased left ventricle (LV) function, and was hospitalized multiple times owing to worsening heart failure. Therefore, we decided to upgrade the conventional pacemaker to a cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator (CRT-D). The patient refused to undergo right ventricle (RV) lead extraction; therefore, we decided to abandon the RV lead and upgrade to CRT-D. Preoperative echocardiography showed that the CS was approximately 1 cm in caliber (Figure 1F).

First, we prepared the device and leads and then performed left subclavian vein puncture, after which a new RV lead was placed in the apical septum. We attempted cannulation of the CS ostium in the lower portion of the posteroseptal aspect of the right atrium; however, the CS ostium could not be identified. Nine months before CRT-D implantation, coronary angiography was performed to rule out coronary artery disease as the cause of the LV dysfunction progression. The coronary veins and PLSVC were contrasted in the venous phase of the coronary angiogram; there was no drain into the right atrium, and the CS ostium presented as a blind edge (Figure 2A). Contrast-enhanced imaging of the PLSVC revealed that the PLSVC did not drain into the RA through the CS; the CS ostium was atretic, and blood vessels passed through the RA floor and drained into the lateral RA (Figure 2B and 2C). The angle between the PLSVC and CS was steep, and the LV lead could not follow this angle. Since there was a high possibility that the lead length was insufficient for approaching the lateral vein through the RA lateral blood vessels, the LV lead was placed in the middle cardiac vein. Discussion

We report the case of a patient with a rare PLSVC and CS ostial closure with drainage into the lateral RA. Knowledge of the abnormal anatomy of the large vessels of the neck and thorax is particularly important for electrophysiologists. Most congenital venous abnormalities are asymptomatic; therefore, the exact prevalence in the general population is unknown.2 Among patients with vena cava anatomical abnormalities, 80%–90% have a right superior vena cava and a left superior vena cava, and in approximately 35% of these patients, the two vessels connect via an innominate left brachiocephalic vein.3 This innominate vein allowed us to place the pacemaker on the left side of the chest via the left subclavian vein. However, in this patient, there was an abnormality in the CS, the integrity of which is important for CRT implantation. Notably, the CS is a critical structure for electrophysiologists, as it provides information about epicardial activation along the mitral annulus and left ventricular leads when placed in the CS venous system. CS has five general categories of abnormalities: enlargement, complete atresia, hypoplasia, unroofing, and CS ostium atresia.1 In this patient, CS ostium atresia prevented venous blood from entering the lower portion of the posteroseptal aspect of the right atrium. Interestingly, a pathway that coursed along the RA floor and ultimately drained into the lateral RA was observed. The CS venous blood entered the RA via two routes: (1) the PLSVC and innominate vein and the right superior vena cava and (2) the blood vessels passing through the RA floor, ultimately draining into the lateral RA.

There are multiple explanations for this anomaly. The CS develops from the primordial sinus venous, and by 8 weeks of age, both left and right horns are formed.4 The left horn develops into the CS, while the right horn becomes the “smooth” part of the RA (venous sinus). In our patient, CS ostium atresia and the CS persisted from the floor of the RA to the lateral RA, and a PLSVC was observed. Abnormal signaling between the two horns of the sinus venosus may have caused CS ostium atresia, continuation of the CS into the RA, and the lack of regression in the left common cardinal veins and caudal part of the left superior cardinal vein. Another explanation for the pathway that courses along the RA floor may involve the drainage of the anterior cardiac veins. It has been reported that in approximately 60% of patients, the anterior cardiac veins drain into intramural channels in the right atrium parallel to the tricuspid annulus, and these sinuses, which measure 2–12 cm in length and 1–4 mm in diameter, are termed the “right atrial coronary sinuses.”5 The second hypothesis is that the CS incorporates into this channel and ultimately drains into the RA. Finally, this patient may have a CS ostium atresia variant in which the collateral circulation was well formed and eventually drained the lateral RA.

Computed tomography (CT) to evaluate the CS before surgery may have made LV lead insertion easier; however, evaluating CS ostium atresia would have been difficult with CT. A previous coronary angiography confirmed CS ostium atresia and enabled us to switch to LV lead insertion via the PLSVC. We believe that it is important to evaluate the coronary veins using CT, coronary angiography, or both, before surgery.

Conclusions

The abnormal anatomy of the great vessels in the neck and chest and the CS sometimes complicates the implantation of cardiac electrical devices. It is important to confirm the anatomy using computed tomography or coronary angiography before surgery in patients with complex anatomy, such as the one reported here.
