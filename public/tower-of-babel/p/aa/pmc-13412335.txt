Medical documentation using artificial intelligence (AI) for battlefield injury simulations: A comparative study
Authors: Daniel Gelman, Danielle Akler, Gil Shimon, Sharon Ogen, Itay Ketko, Michael Leibovitz, Ofer Almog, Avi Benov, Zivan Aviad Beer, Roy Nadler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Incomplete prehospital documentation remains a major challenge, compromising continuity of care and data quality. Manual documentation is time-consuming and cognitively demanding, particularly under operational stress. Speech-based artificial intelligence (AI) systems may enable real-time documentation without interrupting clinical workflow.METHODS:We conducted a feasibility study comparing AI-assisted speech-based documentation with conventional manual documentation during simulated prehospital trauma care. Eight teams completed trauma scenarios using both documentation methods in randomized order. AI outputs were structured into 40 predefined documentation fields derived from the standard combat casualty card. Documentation completeness and user-reported usability were compared between methods. Analyses were performed on paired team-scenario observations.RESULTS:Forty-four simulations were completed (22 per method), with successful AI output in 91% of cases. Overall documentation completeness was similar between AI-assisted and manual documentation [73.9% (interquartile range, 64.1–83.7) vs. 64.8% (interquartile range, 58.7–79.2); p=0.31], with no differences across scenarios or documentation categories (all p>0.05). Usability ratings favored the AI-assisted approach for overall ease of use (p=0.024) and data entry (p=0.015).CONCLUSIONS:In this simulated prehospital battlefield setting, AI-assisted speech-based documentation was feasible and achieved documentation completeness comparable to manual documentation while improving perceived usability. Further development and real-world evaluation are needed for operational implementation. (J Trauma Acute Care Surg. 2026;101: S89–S94. Copyright © 2026 The Author(s). Medical documentation holds critical importance in the treatment of casualties on the battlefield. Accurate documentation is required for high-quality treatment, communication and continuity during transfers between different levels of care.1,2 In mass casualty incidents, it enables data-based resource allocation. Furthermore, documentation is the basis for learning processes to improve treatment methods.3

Field documentation systems typically rely on paper forms or on portable digital monitors.4,5 However, manual entry of medical information using these methods is time-consuming and requires considerable attention from caregivers in the field.4,6 Consequently, gaps in medical documentation are often observed during prehospital care, particularly in disasters and wartime scenarios.3

Recently, advances in artificial intelligence (AI) technology, particularly in audio-based systems, paved the way for innovative tools in medical documentation. As a result, AI-powered systems are increasingly utilized across various medical fields to assist in documentation processes.7 As with other medical fields, this technology has significant potential for prehospital and battlefield emergency care settings.

This study aims to evaluate the capability of an AI-powered model to document medical data during simulated trauma casualty care, assessing its potential to enhance documentation capabilities on the battlefield.

METHODS

Study Overview

This study was designed to evaluate the feasibility of AI-assisted clinical documentation compared with manual documentation during simulated prehospital trauma care. The experiment was conducted in a military medical simulation facility using battlefield injury scenarios.

Study Population

All participants were reserve service physicians during a medical officers course.

Study Design

Eight medical teams participated in the study. Each team consisted of four participants assigned to one of four roles: medical team leader, advanced life support provider, and two basic life support providers. Three trauma scenarios were used (extremity injury, chest injury, and head injury). In every scenario, teams completed two paired simulations, one using conventional manual documentation and one using AI-assisted documentation. Each team completed four to six simulations, each lasting between 5 and 7 minutes. The order of documentation methods was randomized and alternated across scenarios for each team. Documentation in both methods was performed continuously throughout the simulation.

Documentation Methods

Manual documentation was performed using a standard combat casualty card.

For the AI-assisted method, ChatGPT-4o (Plus version, OpenAI, CA) was used. As this was a feasibility study, a general-purpose large language model (LLM) was selected rather than a dedicated documentation system. Verbal communication during each simulation was continuously recorded using a smartphone, bounded by predefined verbal trigger words marking the beginning and end of documentation. Voice mode was not employed. The recorded speech was processed into a tabular format, based on the documentation framework of a standard manual combat casualty card, and decomposed into 40 individual data fields (Table 1). During documentation, the medical team leader was provided with a blank standard casualty card to guide the structuring of information.

For each simulation, an observer with medical training equivalent to that of the participants documented in real time all clinically relevant information verbally stated during the simulation. This reference record served solely to identify which data elements were communicated and therefore eligible for documentation.

Documentation Completeness Assessment

Documentation completeness was evaluated for each simulation using a predefined framework. For each documentation method, each field was evaluated as fully completed, partially completed, or not completed (scored 0–2). Fields that were not verbally communicated during the simulation were excluded from completeness scoring. This approach was selected to isolate documentation performance from communication behavior, given the structure of the manual form, in which an uncompleted field could indicate either missing information or a negative/nonapplicable response to the assessed item. This allowed documentation completeness to reflect the ability of each method to capture verbally expressed clinical information rather than all predefined documentation fields. Accordingly, for each simulation, a completeness score was calculated as the ratio of the achieved score to the maximum possible score based on verbally communicated fields.

During analysis, the data fields were grouped into two high-level categories based on the nature of the information captured. The first category described physiological status and therapeutic actions (physiologic and treatment data), while the second described patient identity, injury characteristics, differential diagnosis, and evacuation planning (clinical context and evacuation). This grouping was used to distinguish between data elements that are typically structured and protocol-driven and those that require contextual judgment.

System Usability

At the end of each simulation, the medical team leader completed a usability questionnaire developed for the study, assessing the ease of use of each method. The questionnaire was developed by the study team based on commonly used usability domains. Responses were recorded on a scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The questionnaire’s internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha (α=0.9). To maintain internal consistency, two questions related to perceptions of documentation timing were excluded.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated assuming a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80. A 15% difference in documentation completeness between AI-assisted and manual documentation was defined as the minimum clinically meaningful effect size based on expert consensus among the study investigators. This estimate was informed by prior studies reporting incomplete documentation of key clinical variables in prehospital settings3 and improvements in documentation completeness following the implementation of novel documentation approaches.8 Based on previously reported variability in field completion rates, an expected standard deviation of 15% was assumed.9 Under these assumptions, a conservative sample size of 16 applications per documentation method was determined.

Statistical Analysis

Documentation completeness and expression rates (the proportion of predefined documentation fields that were communicated) were compared between manual and AI-assisted documentation using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with each team-scenario pair serving as its own control. Documentation completeness analyses were performed separately by injury scenario, by documentation category, and as an overall aggregated comparison across scenarios. Only team-scenario pairs with usable output data for both documentation methods were included in the paired analyses. Completeness levels were reported using median and interquartile range (IQR). A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Usability scores were also compared using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and the results were reported as median and IQR. As not all participants completed all items, the number of paired observations varied by question (range, 15–22). Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons in the usability questionnaire, with a corrected p value <0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

Ethical Considerations

The study was reviewed and granted an exemption by the institutional review board.

The CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility studies guideline was used to ensure proper reporting of methods, results, and discussion (SDC 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/TA/F451).

RESULTS

System Output Performance

Eight teams participated in the experiment, completing a total of 44 simulations, 22 for each documentation method. Overall, the teams completed 16 simulations in the limb injury and chest injury scenarios, and 12 simulations in the head injury scenario.

The AI-assisted documentation approach produced usable output in 20 of 22 simulations (91%). The two failures were attributable to audio recording errors. Of the 20 successful simulations, 15 (75%) generated the required structured documentation on the first attempt. In five cases, minor prompt adjustments were required. Among them, in two cases, the model was explicitly instructed to convert the narrative output into a tabular format, and in three cases, the model initially generated an incomplete table and was prompted once more to regenerate the output, including all predefined data fields.

Documentation Completeness

The AI-assisted documentation approach demonstrated similar completeness levels compared with the manual form (Fig. 1). The median score achieved by the AI-assisted approach was 73.9% (IQR, 64.1–83.7), compared with 64.8% (IQR, 58.7–79.2) using the manual forms (p=0.31). Notably, no instances of data hallucination (inclusion of nonexistent data) were identified.

In addition, no differences were observed between the methods in subgroup analysis according to simulation types (Fig. 1A). Topic-level completeness was also comparable between documentation methods (Fig. 1B), with no statistically significant differences observed between categories (all p>0.05). For clinical context and evacuation data, completeness was 83.5% (IQR, 69.8–89.6) for AI-assisted documentation and 81.7% (IQR, 70–92.1) for manual documentation. For physiologic and treatment data, completeness was 64.8% (IQR, 57.7–83.3) for AI-assisted documentation and 58.6% (IQR, 52.7–72.4) for manual documentation.

The documentation method was not associated with a significant difference in the expression rates of communicated fields during simulations, with 65% (IQR, 60–70) for manual documentation versus 57.5% (IQR, 54.4–67.5) for AI-assisted documentation (p=0.16).

Usability

User-reported usability differed between documentation methods across several domains (Table 2). The AI-assisted documentation approach was rated higher for overall ease of use, with a median score of 4 (IQR, 3–5), compared with a median of 2 (IQR, 2–3) for manual documentation (p=0.024). Ease of data entry was also rated higher for the AI-assisted approach, with a median of 4 (IQR, 3–5), compared with a median of 3 (IQR, 2–3) for manual documentation (p=0.015). No statistically significant differences were observed between the methods for ease of reading the completed documentation, ease of data correction, or perceived documentation quality.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates the feasibility of an AI-assisted approach to clinical documentation in the prehospital setting and its potential to reduce the manual charting burden. Developed on a general-purpose LLM platform, the AI-assisted approach demonstrated documentation completeness comparable to manual forms across all scenarios and across both structured physiologic and treatment fields, as well as more narrative clinical context and evacuation fields. Although documentation completeness was comparable between methods, the AI-assisted approach received higher usability ratings. This difference likely reflects subjective aspects of the documentation experience that are not fully captured by completeness metrics, including perceived effort, convenience, and overall workflow experience during the simulation. Importantly, the documentation method did not affect the proportion of fields verbally communicated during the simulations, indicating that the AI-assisted approach did not alter clinical communication.

Prehospital documentation is widely recognized as essential, yet it remains a persistent challenge across multiple systems and organizations.5,9,10 This challenge is intensified by time-critical decision making under high cognitive load,10 underscoring the need for intuitive documentation workflows. Dictation-based interfaces have therefore been proposed as a promising approach for prehospital documentation.11 In this study, an AI component mapped free speech into predefined documentation fields, enabling continuous clinical discourse while producing structured documentation. Unlike conventional dictation platforms that require fixed response formats, AI-assisted structuring may improve the efficiency and completeness of documentation in time-critical settings.12 This is particularly relevant in combat settings, where communication often relies on domain-specific expressions, contextual understanding, and integrates operational directives alongside medical information. This feasibility study also lays the groundwork for systems capable of real-time automated documentation, even in mass-casualty situations.

Previous reports have evaluated AI-based documentation methods in medical contexts.7,13,14 However, despite significant advancements in the field, concerns have been raised regarding the risk of less-accurate records that may lack the critical human reasoning essential for quality care.15 Recent work demonstrated that an integrated LLM framework outperformed single models; however, it still required corrections in specific fields, especially for medications and treatments.16 In the current study, we observed no loss of critical contextual details compared with manual documentation and, most importantly, no cases of “hallucinations.” Moreover, previous studies implementing AI models in health care documentation have highlighted the need for refined prompting techniques to optimize accuracy and reliability,14,17 suggesting that a more suitable prompting process and domain-specific training could further enhance the results. As this feasibility study utilized a general-purpose LLM without domain-specific fine-tuning, the observed performance may represent a conservative estimate of the potential effectiveness of dedicated AI documentation systems optimized for prehospital care.

When considering future operational use, AI-assisted documentation systems must address operational security requirements in military prehospital environments. While the system evaluated in this study was developed on a general-purpose LLM to serve as a proof of concept, future implementation may be feasible through small, locally deployed AI models operating offline. Such models will enable real-time structuring of dictated clinical information while maintaining compliance with data security requirements.18 Moreover, although this study focused on a military prehospital context, similar documentation challenges have been described in civilian emergency medical services.10 In civilian settings, where security and connectivity constraints are often less restrictive, implementation of such systems may be even more readily achievable.

This study has several limitations. First, the experiments were conducted without environmental constraints, such as communication disruptions, background noise, or operational pressures on caregivers. Documentation recording relied on predefined verbal trigger words to mark the beginning and end of documentation capture, which may not be consistently used in real-world settings and could affect system performance and workflow integration. The study also did not directly evaluate the speech recognition process. In addition, excluding noncommunicated fields from the completeness scoring may have resulted in higher completeness estimates compared with an approach in which all predefined fields are included regardless of verbalization. Because each team completed paired simulations using both documentation methods, learning effects and carryover between runs cannot be excluded, despite randomization of method order. Variation between teams and team leaders may have also influenced the results, although all had similar training and professional backgrounds. Finally, because documentation was performed continuously within predefined simulation timeframes, we could not isolate and measure the time dedicated to the documentation process itself. Evaluating time efficiency differences between AI-assisted and manual documentation remains an important objective for future studies.

In conclusion, this study underscores the potential of AI to improve battlefield documentation and address long-standing challenges in medical documentation. As this feasibility study employed a general-purpose LLM and was conducted under simulated conditions, further validation and development are needed to tailor these systems to the unique demands of prehospital and battlefield settings before broader operational implementation.
