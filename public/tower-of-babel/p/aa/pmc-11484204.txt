Oral microbiome dysbiosis may be associated with intra cranial aneurysms
Authors: Jing Ma, Fangyu Wang, Yang Zhu, Yu Tian, Chengzhong Du, Lingjun Yan, Chenyu Ding, Dengliang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the etiology of aneurysms remains elusive, recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technology and ongoing human microbiome investigations suggest a potential link between microbiome composition and the onset of various human diseases.ObjectiveThis study aimed to utilize high-throughput 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to analyze the oral flora bacterial profiles of individuals, comparing patients with intracranial aneurysms to a healthy control group. Importantly, we sought to identify differences in the oral microbiota and offer novel insights and methods for early diagnosis and identification of intracranial aneurysms.MethodSaliva samples were collected from 60 patients with cerebral aneurysms (case group) and 130 healthy individuals (control group). The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced using the HiSeq high-throughput sequencing platform to establish the bacterial profile. Sequencing data were analyzed using QIIME2 and Metastats software to compare composition differences and relative abundance at the phylum and genus levels in the oral microbiota of the two groups.ResultsSignificant differences in oral microbiota composition were observed between patients in the case and control groups (P < 0.05). Genus-level identification highlighted key positions occupied by Eubacterium, Saccharimonadaceae, Rothia, Gemella, Streptococcus, Lactobacillales, Phocaeicola, Bacteroides, Saccharimonadales, and Abiotrophia.ConclusionThis study revealed noteworthy distinctions in the composition, abundance, and diversity of oral microbiota between intracranial aneurysm patients and healthy controls. These disparities suggest a potential correlation between oral microbiota and the development of intracranial aneurysms, offering new avenues for early diagnosis and intervention. However, limitations such as a small sample size, lack of prospective design, and absence of causal inference warrant further validation and exploration.

Introduction

Intracranial aneurysms (IAs) represent localized dilatations of intracranial arteries caused by the weakening of the vascular endothelium in the brain. They account for approximately 3.2% of the global population, with 85% situated in the anterior circulation of the Circle of Willis, posing a significant risk of death or disability from stroke [1, 2]. IAs predominantly affect adults and are more common in women [3, 4]. While IAs often remain asymptomatic and undetected, they can become life-threatening upon rupture, underscoring the critical importance of early diagnosis and detection.

The pathogenesis of IAs remains elusive, and various factors contribute to arterial wall weakness, elevating the risk of IA development or rupture. Etiologically, intracranial aneurysm formation can be categorized into congenital and acquired factors [5]. Congenital factors involve arterial dysplasia, while acquired factors, a multifactorial manifestation, are linked to arterial wall atherosclerosis, craniocerebral injury, and meningitis, among others. IAs commonly occur at the bifurcation of the basilar arterial ring and are mostly located anterior of the Circle of Willis, such as internal carotid artery aneurysm, posterior communicating artery aneurysm, and anterior cerebral artery aneurysm. Symptomatically, unruptured aneurysms may exhibit subtle symptoms or remain asymptomatic, while larger ones can cause local compression-related symptoms. Ruptured aneurysms manifest as severe headache, vomiting, and impaired consciousness, among other hemorrhagic symptoms [6]. Currently, various technical methods, including Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA), Transcranial Doppler (TCD), Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA), and Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA), are available for diagnosing intracranial aneurysms. In the acute phase of hemorrhage, a rapid diagnosis can be achieved using CTA, and DSA is widely acknowledged as the gold standard for intracranial aneurysm diagnosis [7]. However, these techniques possess inherent limitations and drawbacks, such as high invasiveness, elevated costs, intricate procedures, and the potential for radiation damage. Consequently, there is a pressing need to identify a straightforward, effective, and non-invasive approach to the diagnosis of intracranial aneurysms.

The advancement of molecular biology has experienced the widespread application of gene sequence-based techniques. Utilizing 16 S rRNA gene sequencing provides a direct and comprehensive approach to analyzing the genetic sequences of microorganisms, enabling the determination of bacterial composition and diversity in any given environment [8]. In a healthy population, the oral cavity harbors more than 700 species of microorganisms that colonize different parts of the body, forming a complex microbial environment. This stable microbial system maintains a dynamic equilibrium with the host, collectively contributing to the host’s health [9, 10]. Drawing a parallel to the “brain-gut axis”, Tonomura et al. [11] introduced the concept of the “brain-oral axis” to explore the connection between oral flora and cerebral microangiopathy. This concept underscores the profound influence of oral flora on cerebral microvascular degeneration. The “brain-oral axis” explores the relationship between oral flora and cerebral small vessel disease, highlighting the substantial impact of oral flora on cerebral small vessel degeneration and illustrating the influence of oral microbiota on brain health [11, 12]. Imbalances in the oral microbiota can lead to oral diseases like dental caries and periodontitis, subsequently affecting systemic health, including conditions like cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and obesity [11, 12]. Studies have also indicated that oral dysbiosis may pose a risk factor for lacunar infarction [13]. Therefore, oral microbiota has emerged as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis and prevention of intracranial aneurysms.

To investigate the connection between oral microbiota and intracranial aneurysms, we employed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing-an approach directly analyzing microbial gene sequences to determine bacterial composition and diversity in various V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was sequenced [12, 14]. Saliva samples were collected from 60 patients with intracranial aneurysms and 130 healthy controls. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced, establishing a bacterial profile that was analyzed using data. Functional prediction of metabolic pathways was conducted using PICRUSt2, and the resulting plots were generated using STAMP2.1.3. Our analysis revealed significant disparities in the composition, abundance, and diversity of oral microbiota between individuals with intracranial aneurysms and healthy controls. Key genera differentiating the two groups included Eubacterium, Saccharimonadaceae, Rothia, Gemella, Streptococcus, Lactobacillales, Phocaeicola, Bacteroides, Saccharimonadales, Abiotrophia, F0058, Tannerella, Selenomonas, Stomatobaculum, Clostridia, Dialister, Leptotrichia, and Porphyromonas at the genus level. These observed differences may indicate a correlation or reciprocal relationship between oral microbiota and the development of intracranial aneurysms, offering novel insights and methods for early diagnosis and intervention. The study’s primary objective was to ascertain disparities in the oral microbiota of patients with intracranial aneurysms compared to healthy controls. Additionally, we aimed to explore potential mechanisms and significance by examining the bacterial profiles of the subjects’ oral flora using high-throughput 16 S rRNA gene sequencing technology.

Methods

Saliva sample and inclusion/exclusion criteria

Oral saliva specimens ranging from 2 to 5 mL were collected from two groups: 60 patients diagnosed with cerebral aneurysms at the First Hospital of Fujian Medical University (case group) and 130 participants from the community health check-up population (control group). We recruited the control group participants from a community health checkup population. These individuals were undergoing routine checkups and did not present with any illness at the time of sample collection. Additionally, participants were excluded if they were accompanying patients or had any underlying health issues that could affect the oral microbiome. Saliva samples were chosen for this study due to their ease of collection, non-invasive nature, and their ability to reflect the microbial communities from various oral sites, including the tongue, cheeks, and gums. This makes saliva particularly suitable for large-scale clinical studies where non-invasive sampling is preferred. While oral rinse specimens may capture a broader spectrum of oral flora, particularly from hard-to-reach areas like the dental plaque and gingival crevices, we opted for saliva samples to ensure participant compliance and repeatability in future diagnostic applications. Samples collection took place between March 2019 and September 2022. Participants refrained from eating, drinking, smoking, or chewing gum for 30 min prior to sampling. Saliva was retained in the mouth for at least 1 min after rinsing with water. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, and participants provided informed consent. For this study, 16 S rRNA gene libraries were created using individual saliva samples from each participant. No pooled samples or PCR products were used to create the libraries, ensuring that each sample’s microbial composition was independently assessed.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were required to meet the diagnostic criteria for intracranial aneurysm as outlined in the European Stroke Organisation Guidelines for the Management of Intracranial Aneurysms and Subarachnoid Haemorrhage [15]. The confirmation of an intracranial aneurysm diagnosis was mandatory and was established through digital subtraction angiography. Additionally, individuals were excluded if they had undergone any treatment affecting the oral microbiota (such as taking anti-inflammatory drugs or receiving immunosuppressive therapy) within the preceding two weeks. Inclusion criteria also stipulated no history of oncological diseases, Han nationality, an age range between 20 and 80 years, and the absence of immunodeficiency or systemic diseases.

Exclusion criteria

Subjects with documented records of other neurological disorders, craniocerebral injuries, a history of gastrointestinal disorders, systemic diseases, distant metastases, blood disorders, or any other infectious diseases were excluded from participation in the study. Control subjects were excluded if they had a history of oral diseases such as periodontitis, or if they had received any treatment that could alter the oral microbiota, such as recent use of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory medications. This ensures that the observed differences in microbiota composition, including the enrichment of Eubacterium, were not influenced by underlying oral infections. To minimize the confounding effects of pre-existing oral or gingival diseases, participants with a history of oral health issues such as periodontitis or dental caries were excluded from the study. Additionally, individuals who had received recent dental treatments, including the use of dentures, were also excluded. However, due to limitations in the study design, data on participants’ dietary habits (such as sugar and fat intake) and the presence of dentures were not comprehensively collected. To minimize transient changes in the oral microbiome, participants were instructed to refrain from smoking, eating, and drinking for at least 30 min before saliva collection. While these measures help reduce immediate disturbances in the oral flora, we recognize that transient microbial fluctuations may still occur. Future studies could address this issue by obtaining multiple samples from each participant and focusing the analysis on genera or species consistently present across samples.

Data collection

General information, including age, gender, BMI, and history of smoking and drinking, was collected for all subjects. For patients with intracranial aneurysms, additional clinical information, such as the location, size, morphology, and rupture status of the aneurysm, was also gathered. Medical professionals meticulously entered and verified all collected data.

Gene sequencing analysis of 16SrRNA

The collected saliva samples underwent a rigorous process: after being repetitively collected into sterilized centrifuge tubes, they were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the remaining saliva was quick-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃. For saliva microbial gene extraction, Genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB or SDS method, followed by quality assessment using spectrophotometry. The V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was then amplified using specific primers. The V3-V4 region of the 16SrDNA gene was specifically targeted for PCR amplification, utilizing primer pairs 341 F (5’-CCTACGGGRSGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACVVGGGGTATCTAATC-3’). The V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced using paired-end reads (150 bp) on the Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina Incorporate, CA, United States). Subsequently, the PCR amplification products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads, dissolved in an Elution Buffer, and labeled to complete the library construction. The fragment range and concentration of the libraries were examined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Based on the size of the inserted fragments, the library was selected for sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina Incorporate, CA, United States). Quality filtering was performed using the DADA2 plugin within QIIME2, which removed low-quality reads, adapter sequences, and chimeras. Approximately 97% of the reads survived the filtering process. After quality filtering, ASVs (Amplicon Sequence Variants) were generated using DADA2 in QIIME2. Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were generated using DADA2 in QIIME2. Taxonomic classification was performed using the SILVA database [16]. We limited our analysis to the genus level due to the constraints of V3-V4 amplicon sequencing. Raw sequencing data underwent a meticulous processing protocol to obtain Clean Data:

(1) The method employed for removing low quality involved a window of 25 bp. If the average quality value within the window was lower than 20, the back-end bases were truncated. If the length of the truncated reads was less than 75% of the original read length, the entire sequence was removed. (2) Connector-contaminated reads were eliminated, with the default adapter sequence having a 15 bp overlap with the read sequence. The set overlap was 15 bp, and the allowed number of mismatches was 3; (3) Reads containing N were removed; (4) Low-complexity reads, defined as consecutive bases ≥ 10 in the default read, were excluded. Samples were distinguished based on barcodes and primers, allowing zero mismatches for barcode sequence comparison with sequencing reads.

Statistical analysis

The Shannon index, Pielou index, and Faith’s phylogenetic index serve as indicators of flora richness in a sample. A higher index value suggests greater species variety within the sample. On the other hand, the Coverage index reflects the sequencing depth of the samples. The closer this index is to 1, the more comprehensive the sequencing has been, covering a significant portion of the species present in the sample—indicating high species coverage. Additionally, β Diversity method analysis is employed for a comparative examination of microbial community composition. This analysis allows for the comparison of different samples and samples between different groups, providing insights into the variations and differences in microbial communities across diverse conditions or groups. Significant taxa were identified using LEfSe analysis (LDA score > 3.5), and network plots were generated to visualize the connections between discriminant taxa.

All clinical data underwent statistical analysis utilizing SPSS20.0 software. For experimental data and comparisons of means among two or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA) was employed. In cases where measured data did not conform to normal distribution and for identical items in other groups, as well as for count data in each group, the χ2 test was utilized.

Furthermore, the data were subjected to analysis using QIIME2, encompassing species annotation, Alpha diversity analysis, and Beta diversity analysis. Metastats software was then utilized to scrutinize differences between the two groups of samples, identifying OTUs with significant differences and annotating the species accordingly.

Discussion

Intracranial aneurysm, a prevalent neurovascular disease, has an unclear pathogenesis potentially linked to various factors, including genetics, environment, and lifestyle. The current diagnostic and treatment approaches for intracranial aneurysms heavily depend on imaging techniques like CTA, MRA, DSA, etc. Despite their utility, these methods pose inherent limitations and drawbacks, such as invasiveness, elevated costs, procedural complexities, and the risk of radiation damage. Consequently, the quest for a straightforward, efficient, and non-invasive approach to diagnose and prevent intracranial aneurysms assumes paramount clinical significance.

In recent years, the advancement of high-throughput sequencing (NGS) technology has unveiled a significant association between the microbiome and human health and disease. Within this context, the oral cavity emerges as a crucial microecosystem within the human body, where the oral microbiota plays a pivotal role in both oral and systemic health. Imbalances in the oral microbiota can precipitate oral diseases, including dental caries and periodontitis, subsequently impacting systemic health with repercussions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and obesity. Moreover, the oral microbiota may engage in interactions with distant organs or tissues through the bloodstream or nervous system, influencing their function and overall status. Given these intricate relationships, the oral microbiota holds promise as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis and prevention of intracranial aneurysms. Metadata such as age, gender, BMI, smoking, and drinking history were included in the microbiome analysis to account for potential confounding factors that could explain microbiota variations.

In this study, we systematically analyzed the oral microbiota of intracranial aneurysm patients and healthy controls using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, revealing significant differences in composition, abundance, and diversity between the two groups. While saliva samples provide a practical and non-invasive method for assessing the oral microbiome, we acknowledge that oral rinse specimens may offer a more comprehensive view of the oral flora by including microorganisms from dental surfaces and gingival crevices. Future studies should consider comparing the two sampling methods to determine the extent to which oral rinse samples might better represent the complete oral microbiome. Specifically, at the genus level, Eubacterium, Saccharimonadaceae, Rothia, Gemella, Streptococcus, Lactobacillales, Phocaeicola, Bacteroides, Saccharimonadales, and Abiotrophia were pivotal in the intracranial aneurysm patient group, where fluctuations in their content were associated with changes in other flora, potentially influencing intracranial aneurysm development. At the species level, Eubacterium, methanogenic, Prevotell, Dialister_invisus, Clostridiale, Selenomonas, Bacteroidetes, Saccharimonadales, Actinomyces, and Phocaeicol played a significant role, with alterations in their levels impacting other flora and potentially contributing to intracranial aneurysm development. The observed differences suggest a correlation or reciprocal influence between oral microbiota and intracranial aneurysm development. Further Picrust2 pathway analysis highlighted variations in predicted functional changes in human metabolic pathways, including D-fructuronate degradation, superpathway of β-D-glucuronide and D-glucuronate degradation, superpathway of hexuronide and hexuronate degradation, enterobacterial D-glucuronide and D-glucuronate degradation, superpathway of hexuronide and hexuronate degradation, enterobacterial common antigen biosynthesis, sucrose degradation IV (sucrose phosphorylase), superpathway of chorismate metabolism, superpathway of menaquinol-6 biosynthesis I, superpathway of menaquinol-10 biosynthesis, superpathway of menaquinol-9 biosynthesis, and L-rhamnose degradation I. This suggests the potential involvement of oral flora metabolic pathways in intracranial aneurysm pathogenesis. At the genus level, Eubacterium emerged as the bacterial genus exhibiting significant differences between the two sample groups. This genus plays a crucial role in nutrient metabolism and maintaining intestinal homeostasis [17]. It is well-established that various species within the genus Eubacterium produce short-chain fatty acids, which serve as essential components for the intestinal epithelium, contribute to the protection of the intestinal mucosal barrier, reduce inflammation levels in the body, and enhance gastrointestinal motility machinery [18]. Eubacterium is integral to cholesterol conversion, oxalate catabolism, and insulin degradation. Recent studies have suggested that oral microbiota may interact with gut microbiota through shared metabolic pathways, influencing systemic conditions such as cholesterol levels and inflammation, which are risk factors for IA [19–21]. Consequently, a reduction or deficiency in Eubacterium may lead to a spectrum of health issues, including depression, obesity, diabetes, colorectal cancer, autism, and cardiovascular diseases [17]. Our analysis showed that the genus Eubacterium was significantly enriched in the control group, indicating its possible role in maintaining oral health. Previous studies have found that Eubacterium is often isolated in individuals suffering from oral diseases like periodontitis, but in this study, the control group was composed of healthy individuals without reported periodontal infections. Therefore, the elevated levels of Eubacterium in controls may reflect its protective role in oral health rather than contributing to the pathogenesis of aneurysms. These statements are based on findings from previous studies, referenced accordingly, and correlated with the current study’s observations. Reduced levels of Eubacterium may lead to dysbiosis that impacts cholesterol metabolism, oxalate catabolism, and insulin degradation, potentially contributing to aneurysm development through systemic metabolic disturbances.

Streptococcus, which causes oxidation of iron in hemoglobin molecules within red blood cells or results in complete rupture of red blood cells, has been shown to increase the risk of intracranial aneurysms [22]. When bacteria like Streptococcus invade the circulatory system, they may induce inflammation and damage to the vessel wall, thereby elevating the risk of intracranial aneurysm formation and rupture. The study found a significantly higher relative abundance of Streptococcus in the group of patients with intracranial aneurysms compared to the healthy control group. This increase in Streptococcus levels may lead to inflammatory damage to the vessel wall, potentially weakening the arterial wall and increasing the risk of developing intracranial aneurysms or experiencing an aneurysm rupture.

At the species level, Prevotella was identified as the bacterial genus that significantly differed between the two sample groups. Prevotella is known for its role in breaking down proteins and carbohydrates [23–25]. Several studies have indicated the effectiveness of Prevotella in treating multiple sclerosis (MS) by attenuating the pro-inflammatory response associated with the disease [23, 24]. Additionally, low Prevotella abundance has been detected in children with autism, and behavioral problems in two-year-olds have been linked to decreased Prevotella levels during infancy. Varying levels of Prevotella in the gut have been observed in children with cerebral palsy and epilepsy [7, 26]. Hea Ree Park et al. [27] revealed the association of Prevotella intermedia with the development of the first intracranial fungal aneurysm, providing evidence of Prevotella’s potential involvement in aneurysm development. Existing research has shown the role of bacterial DNA in the pathogenesis of cerebral aneurysms [28], with factors like an elevated fibronectin-to-laminin ratio in the wall of unruptured aneurysms possibly leading to rupture [29]. Decreased expression of transforming growth factor-α in the vessel wall has also been associated with aneurysm formation [29]. Moreover, a study by Lu Jia et al. found a correlation between hyperglycemia and mortality on admission for aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage [30]. In light of these findings, the study hypothesized that changes in Prevotella abundance affecting the breakdown and synthesis of carbohydrates and proteins may be correlated or reciprocal with the development of intracranial aneurysms. The study observed a significantly lower relative abundance of Prevotella in the intracranial aneurysm patient group compared to the healthy control group. The decrease in Prevotella may contribute to an imbalance in the oral microbiota, increasing the risk of oral inflammation and infection. This imbalance could potentially affect the integrity and stability of the blood vessel wall. Additionally, the decrease in Prevotella may influence the metabolism of carbohydrates and proteins, leading to abnormalities in blood glucose and lipid levels, ultimately increasing the risk of developing intracranial aneurysms and lipid abnormalities.

Simultaneously, Rothia has been associated with potential benefits such as improving intestinal biodiversity, increasing glucose tolerance, aiding in weight loss, and rejuvenating colon cells. Dysregulation or excess of Rothia spp. may impact multiple metabolic pathways and is linked to various diseases [31]. Reduced abundance of Rothia has been observed in conditions like Parkinson’s, myalgic encephalomyelitis, hypertension, atherosclerosis, and others. Rothia can serve as a biomarker for symptomatic pathologies, such as gallstone formation, or function as a probiotic to restore beneficial flora [32]. Notably, Rothia dentocariosa has been identified as a rare cause of endocarditis, and variations in Rothia abundance have been detected in neurological disorders like Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [33] and Parkinson’s [34]. At the genus level, rRNA amplicon sequencing in Parkinson’s disease patients revealed significantly fewer putative “anti-inflammatory butyrate” bacteria, such as Rochesteria spp., in their feces compared to controls [35]. Some studies have reported cases of subarachnoid hemorrhage due to Rothia dentocariosa endocarditis and stroke associated with intracranial aneurysm [36, 37]. In the present study, the relative abundance of Rothia was notably higher in the group of patients with intracranial aneurysms than in the healthy control group. This increase in Rothia may indicate an imbalance in the oral microbiota, potentially leading to heightened oral inflammation and infection. Such oral microbiota imbalance could impact the integrity and stability of the vessel wall. Moreover, the increase in Rothia may influence the synthesis of vitamins and amino acids, resulting in a decrease in the repair and regeneration of the vascular wall, ultimately elevating the risk of intracranial aneurysms.

The exact mechanisms by which the oral microbiota may contribute to the development of intracranial aneurysms are not fully understood. However, several potential pathways can be hypothesized based on existing research:

Inflammation and Immune Response: Oral bacteria, such as Streptococcus and Prevotella, can translocate into the bloodstream and trigger systemic inflammatory responses. Chronic inflammation has been identified as a key factor in weakening the arterial wall, making it more susceptible to aneurysm formation [21]. Bacterial invasion of the vascular endothelium can also stimulate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may further compromise vessel integrity.

Metabolic Pathways: Changes in the oral microbiome, particularly in genera like Eubacterium and Rothia, are known to affect systemic metabolism. For example, Eubacterium is involved in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, which plays a role in maintaining vascular health [38]. Dysbiosis in the oral microbiome could lead to altered SCFA levels, contributing to vascular dysfunction and increasing the risk of aneurysm.

Microbial Metabolites: Oral microbiota can produce various metabolites, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), that have been implicated in endothelial dysfunction and vascular inflammation. LPS may induce oxidative stress and damage to the arterial wall, promoting aneurysm development. Future studies should explore the specific metabolic pathways involved in this process [20, 21, 39].

These mechanisms suggest that oral dysbiosis may have both local and systemic effects that influence the pathogenesis of intracranial aneurysms. Further research is needed to validate these hypotheses through mechanistic studies.

The study results revealed distinctions in the oral microbiota between patients with intracranial aneurysms and healthy controls. Furthermore, the identification of specific bacterial genera associated with intracranial aneurysms offers novel insights and potential avenues for early diagnosis and preventive strategies for intracranial aneurysms. While Metastats was chosen for its simplicity and its past applications, we acknowledge that newer tools like metagenomeSeq may provide more robust analyses and will consider these for future studies. This study used 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to analyze the microbial community structure, a method that has been widely employed in microbiome research to establish links between microbial dysbiosis and various diseases. However, as other researchers globally have also noted, this approach has limitations, particularly in the ability to resolve taxa at the species level and the lack of functional information. These limitations underscore the need for future studies to integrate more comprehensive techniques such as metagenomics or metatranscriptomics, which could provide a more complete picture of microbial roles in intracranial aneurysms.

Limitation

While this study identified differences in oral microbiota between patients with intracranial aneurysms and healthy controls and identified specific bacterial genera associated with intracranial aneurysms, there are noteworthy limitations. Firstly, the small sample size, comprising saliva samples from only 60 patients with intracranial aneurysms and 130 healthy controls, may compromise the stability and reliability of the results. Although we implemented pre-sampling restrictions such as abstaining from smoking, eating, and drinking, the oral microbiota is known to exhibit transient changes independent of these activities. This is a limitation of our current study design. Collecting a second sample and analyzing only the taxa consistently present in both samples would help mitigate the effects of such transient variations. This approach could provide a more stable representation of the core oral microbiome and will be considered in future research. We acknowledge that various confounding factors, such as pre-existing oral conditions, dietary habits, and the use of dentures, could influence the composition of the oral microbiome. While participants with known oral diseases were excluded, we did not collect comprehensive data on dietary intake or denture usage, which may have impacted the observed microbial differences. Future studies should consider a more detailed assessment of these factors, including objective oral health assessments and dietary surveys, to better control for their potential confounding effects. One limitation of this study is the use of saliva samples instead of oral rinse specimens. Although saliva samples offer a non-invasive and easy-to-collect option, oral rinse specimens might capture a broader range of microbes, especially from the gingival crevices and dental plaque. Future research should explore the potential advantages of using oral rinse samples to better represent the oral microbiome. A limitation of this study is the lack of detailed data on participants’ diet, such as sugar and fat intake, as well as the presence of dentures, both of which could influence the oral microbiome. Future research should include these variables to better control for their potential confounding effects. To enhance representativeness, future research should involve an expanded sample size encompassing diverse aspects of intracranial aneurysms, including different locations, sizes, morphologies, and whether they are ruptured or not. Secondly, the lack of a prospective design limits this study to retrospective observations, preventing the establishment of a causal relationship between oral microbiota and intracranial aneurysms. Future endeavors should prioritize prospective randomized controlled trials to validate whether oral microbiota acts as a risk or protective factor for intracranial aneurysms and to assess the potential of modulating the oral microbiota for prevention or treatment. Thirdly, this study focused solely on analyzing the composition and diversity of oral microbiota using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, neglecting an in-depth exploration of its function, metabolism, and molecular mechanisms linking it to intracranial aneurysms. To address this, future investigations should employ more comprehensive analyses, incorporating macrogenomic, macrotranscriptomic, and macrometabolomic technologies. Additionally, direct and effective validation of the interactions between oral microbiota and intracranial aneurysms should be pursued using in vitro and in vivo experimental models. A key limitation of our study is the use of 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, which while effective for taxonomic profiling, does not allow for accurate species-level identification or provide functional insights into microbial activities. Future research should consider employing metagenomic or metatranscriptomic techniques to address these gaps and provide a more detailed understanding of the microbial functions related to intracranial aneurysms. While our study identified significant associations between oral microbiota and intracranial aneurysms, it did not directly assess the functional consequences of these microbial changes. Future studies should focus on elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms by which oral bacteria contribute to aneurysm pathogenesis, including their effects on systemic inflammation, endothelial health, and metabolic pathways. One limitation of the study is the reliance on a single sequencing run per sample. Although the sequencing depth was adequate, future studies could consider replicating sequencing runs or using pooled samples to verify the robustness of the observed microbiome differences. A limitation of this study is the use of a single saliva sample, which may not fully account for the transient nature of the oral microbiome. In future studies, obtaining multiple samples from each participant and analyzing only the genera or species consistently present across samples could reduce the impact of transient fluctuations in microbial composition.

Conclusion

In this investigation, we systematically compared the oral microbiota of intracranial aneurysm patients with that of healthy controls using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Our analysis revealed significant differences in the composition, abundance, and diversity of oral microbiota between these two groups. Specifically, within the intracranial aneurysm patient group, key genera such as Eubacterium, Saccharimonadaceae, Rothia, Gemella, Streptococcus, Lactobacillales, Phocaeicola, Bacteroides, Saccharimonadales, and Abiotrophia exhibited notable variations at the genus level. Changes in the levels of these genera were found to influence other flora, potentially contributing to the development of intracranial aneurysms. The fluctuations in their content may trigger changes in other flora, further influencing the progression of intracranial aneurysms. These identified differences suggest a correlation or mutual influence between oral microbiota and the development of intracranial aneurysms, offering novel insights and potential methods for early diagnosis and prevention of this condition. However, it is essential to acknowledge certain limitations in our study, including a relatively small sample size, the absence of a prospective design, and a lack of causal inference. These limitations underscore the need for further validation and exploration. While our findings hint at a possible relationship between intracranial aneurysms and oral microbiota dysbiosis, proposing a new avenue for diagnosis and treatment, comprehensive studies are imperative to substantiate these initial observations.
