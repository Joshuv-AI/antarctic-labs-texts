Comparison of unilateral biportal endoscopic decompression and percutaneous endoscopic lumbar decompression for single-level degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yu Zhang, Jidong Ju, Jinchun Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, both unilateral biportal endoscopic decompression (UBED) and percutaneous endoscopic lumbar decompression (PELD) recognized as effective treatments for degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS). Nevertheless, there remains a divergence of opinion regarding the preferable operative procedure. Consequently, the meta-analysis aimed to evaluate and compare the effectiveness and adverse events associated with UBED and PELD in the treatment of DLSS.MethodsMultiple databases were thoroughly screened on the internet to identify studies focusing on clinical endpoints and complications of UBED versus PELD in the management of single-level DLSS. Visual analogue scale (VAS) scores and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) were recorded to assess clinical outcomes. Additionally, perioperative findings and complications were documented for analysis. Meta-analyses were conducted using Stata MP 17.0 software.ResultsA total of 14 studies involving 1222 participants were included in this analysis. The results indicated that UBED significantly reduced operative time, VAS scores for leg pain at three months postoperatively and ODI at the final follow-up (p < 0.05). Furthermore, UBED was associated with a significant increase in postoperative dural sac cross-sectional area compared to PELD (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between UBED and PELD concerning intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay, VAS scores for back pain, overall complication rates, or dural tear rates (p > 0.05).ConclusionOur evidence suggests that there is no significant difference between UBED and PELD regarding most outcomes. However, UBED may reduce operative time while providing superior decompression efficacy compared to PELD. Therefore, these findings indicate that UBED appears more effective than PELD in treating single-level DLSS. Further multi-center randomized controlled trials are necessary before definitive conclusions can be drawn.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06338-2.Keywords: Unilateral biportal endoscopy, Degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis, Percutaneous endoscopic lumbar decompression, Clinical efficacy, Complication, Meta-analysis

Introduction

The incidence of degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS) among the elderly population has significantly increased over recent decades [1]. DLSS is a multifactorial disorder characterized by fibrous tissue proliferations, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy or osteophytes, which reduces the sagittal diameters of the spinal canal or neural root canals, ultimately leading to nerve root compression. Common clinical manifestations include back and leg discomfort, intermittent claudication, and severe limitations in ambulation, causing significant distress among elderly populations. For patients who fail conservative medical management, operative therapy should be considered to alleviate symptoms [2, 3].

Historically, open laminectomy was the standard surgical treatment for DLSS, with the primary goals being nerve decompression and improvement in daily activities. However, extensive dissection during this procedure often led to paravertebral muscle atrophy due to ischemia and denervation. Additionally, removal of posterior ligaments and bone structures resulted in prolonged hospitalization, increased blood loss, and higher risks of postoperative back pain and surgical site infections. Furthermore, open decompressive laminectomy might lead to spinal instability [4, 5]. Although posterior fusion via screw placement provided acceptable short-term outcomes and mitigated destabilization concerns [6], it became associated with adjacent segment degeneration over time [7, 8].

To address these limitations, various minimally invasive procedures emerged as alternatives to traditional open laminectomy. Bilateral decompression through unilateral laminectomy using minimally invasive microscopic techniques preserved more intact spinal structures, avoided postoperative instability, and limited soft tissue damage, demonstrating proven efficacy in treating DLSS compared to conventional open decompression surgery [9, 10]. The use of endoscopes or microscopes through small incisions allows surgeons to achieve clear visualization while minimizing iatrogenic damage to lumbar spinal structures [11]. These minimally invasive approaches aim to provide adequate neural decompression while reducing tissue trauma and postoperative complications.

Recently, following the fast advancement of spinal endoscopic techniques, clinicians and patients embraced percutaneous endoscopic lumbar decompression (PELD) due to its advantages of minimal invasiveness, low blood loss, excellent visualization, and robust decompression capabilities. Numerous studies reported favorable clinical outcomes of PELD in treating DLSS [12–15]. Despite its benefits, PELD had certain limitations, including single-channel viewing and operating systems that restrict instrument movement, narrow visual fields, and incomplete three-dimensional visualization. Moreover, the small diameter of the working tube necessitated specialized and potentially costly equipment, limiting its efficiency. The limited intraoperative surgical space might result in incomplete decompression, and the steep learning curve posed challenges for young clinicians.

To overcome the limitations of PELD, unilateral biportal endoscopic decompression (UBED), initially proposed by Kambin and Sampson in 1986, re-emerged as a promising alternative with the refinement of Korean scholars in recent years [16, 17]. UBED offered an exceptional learning curve alongside the benefits of outstanding magnification, illumination and visualization capability. It featured independent endoscopic observation and operation channels, enabling one channel for visualization and the other for instrumentation, thereby enhancing outcomes in lumbar decompression laminectomies and foraminotomies. UBED combined the advantages of both open procedures and minimally invasive surgeries, contributing to improved treatment of DLSS [18–20]. Nonetheless, the most effective technique for minimally invasive surgery remained undetermined. Many investigations reported better clinical outcomes with endoscopic techniques compared to microscopic approaches [21]. Regrettably, the distinction between UBED and PELD in treating DLSS remained ambiguous. The purpose of this review was to comprehensively evaluate the outcomes of UBED and PELD in treating DLSS, thereby informing clinical decision-making.

Methods

Literature search

This research was registered at PROSPERO (CRD42024531084). This study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (additional file 1) [22]. The authors of this review conducted a comprehensive and systematic literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, CNKI and Wanfang databases from the start of the databases to 8 January 2025. In addition, manually searched reference lists, systematic reviews and meta-analyses of each retrieved article were used to find relevant literature. The complete search syntax for each database was recorded in additional file 2.

Quality Assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the quality of the observational study. The total score was 9, with ≥ 6 considered high quality.

Statistical analyses

Meta-analyses were performed using Stata MP 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). Heterogeneity of included studies was assessed using the I2 statistic. I2 > 50% indicated heterogeneity between included studies and a random effects model was used. Otherwise, meta-analysis was performed using a fixed-effects model. For continuous outcomes, effect sizes were expressed as weighted mean difference (WMD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). For dichotomous outcomes, effect sizes were estimated as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Subgroup analyses were performed according to postoperative follow-up time. Publication bias was assessed using the Egger test. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Intraoperative blood loss

Five reports analyzed intraoperative blood loss and showed there was no significant difference between UBED group and PELD group [WMD = 17.49, 95% CI (−2.44, 37.42), p = 0.09] (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of intraoperative blood loss

Hospital stay

Eleven articles analyzed length of hospital stay, including 1009 cases. These findings proved there was no significant difference in hospitalization time between UBED and PELD groups [WMD = 0.36, 95% CI (−0.06, 0.78), p = 0.09] (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of hospital stay

VAS scores for back pain

Six, four and twelve publications recorded VAS scores for back pain at three months postoperatively, six months postoperatively and at final follow-up, separately. There was no significant difference between both groups regarding back VAS scores at three months postoperatively [WMD = −0.06, 95% CI (−0.15, 0.04)], at six months postoperatively [WMD = −0.05, 95% CI (−0.20, 0.11)] and at final follow-up [WMD = 0.00, 95% CI (−0.05, 0.05)] (Fig. 5).

Forest plot of visual analogue scale scores for back pain

VAS scores for leg pain

Six, four, and twelve articles reported VAS scores for leg pain at three months postoperatively, six months postoperatively, and at the final follow-up, respectively. Figure 6 showed UBED significantly reduced VAS scores for leg pain at three months postoperatively compared to PELD [WMD = −0.10, 95% CI (−0.18, −0.02)]. However, there was no significant difference between both groups regarding leg VAS scores at six months postoperatively [WMD = 0.04, 95% CI (−0.09, 0.17)] and at final follow-up [WMD = 0.03, 95% CI (−0.03, 0.08)] (Fig. 6).

Forest plot of visual analogue scale scores for leg pain

ODI

ODI at three months postoperatively, six months postoperatively and final follow-up was documented in six, four and twelve studies, respectively. The results showed there was no significant difference between both groups regarding ODI at 3 months postoperatively [WMD = 0.42, 95% CI (−0.06, 0.90)] and at six months postoperatively [WMD = −0.18, 95% CI (−0.63, 0.27)]. However, Fig. 7 showed UBED significantly reduced ODI at final follow-up compared to PELD [WMD = −0.42, 95% CI (−0.71, −0.13)] (Fig. 7).

Dural sac cross-sectional area postoperatively

Three reports described postoperative dural sac cross-sectional area. Figure 8 showed UBED significantly enlarged the postoperative dural sac cross-sectional area compared to PELD [WMD = 15.04, 95% CI (11.56, 18.52), p < 0.01] (Fig. 8).

Total complication rates

We collected total complication rates, based on data from 13 studies. The total complication rate in the UBED group was similar to that of PELD group [OR = 0.66, 95% CI (0.40, 1.10), p = 0.11] (Fig. 9).

Forest plot of total complication rates

Dural tear

Thirteen articles reported the incidence of dural tears. Our result indicated the incidences of dural tear in UBED group were not significantly different from PELD group [OR = 0.76, 95% CI (0.39, 1.46), p = 0.40] (Fig. 10).

Forest plot of dural tear rate

Publication bias

The Egger test was carried out to determine publication bias. The findings revealed that there was no significant publication bias in any outcomes (p > 0.05) (additional file 3).

Discussion

Among elderly populations suffering from spinal disorders, DLSS was recognized as a significant indication for operative intervention [1]. A critical consideration in this demographic was the heightened prevalence of secondary comorbidities. Therefore, the paramount challenge lied in delivering effective treatments while simultaneously minimizing tissue damage. Open laminotomy, often referred to as a traditional surgical approach, had been established as a classical and effective method for addressing DLSS. However, this technique might result in considerable soft tissue trauma and bleeding, which underscored the necessity for minimally invasive spinal surgery. The evolution of minimally invasive procedures introduced numerous advantages, including reduced muscular injury, shorter rehabilitation periods, diminished risks of complications, and an overall improvement in quality of life [37–39]. With the rising adoption of minimally invasive techniques, clinical documentation regarding single and double portal endoscopic operations had proliferated [40, 41]. PELD provided complete decompression while preserving spinal stability with minimal zygapophysial joint injury. Nonetheless, PELD presented certain limitations such as an extended learning curve and restricted indications. In recent years, various reports had highlighted biportal endoscopic spinal surgeries that achieved satisfactory clinical and radiological outcomes through two distinct channels. Early studies indicated that both operative techniques were efficient and safe [42–44].

UBED provided the benefits of an extensive operating space and a broad field of vision, rendering it suitable for a broad spectrum of indications. It demonstrated particular efficacy in treating complex lumbar degenerative diseases, including severe or multi-segmental lumbar spinal stenosis, calcified intervertebral disc protrusion, and mild to moderate lumbar spondylolisthesis (grades I–II). In contrast, PELD allowed for direct treatment of lesions through a single channel, thereby minimizing patients’ trauma. This technique was applicable to cases of single-segment simple lumbar disc herniation, encompassing central, paracentral, lateral, and far lateral types. Moreover, PELD was also indicated for addressing unilateral symptoms caused by spinal stenosis. Neither approach exhibited absolute superiority or inferiority. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment should be performed based on the patient’s specific condition and the physician’s expertise.

In recent years, several meta-analyses highlighted differences between endoscopic and microsurgical procedures in patients with DLSS who required surgical intervention for symptom relief [45–47]. Unfortunately, few meta-analyses demonstrated any significant differences between these two primary endoscopic techniques. The objective of our study was to systematically evaluate the endoscopic outcomes of UBED and PELD in DLSS patients, aiming to inform clinical practice. Our findings confirmed that there were no significant differences between UBED and PELD concerning intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay, VAS scores for back pain, overall complication rates and dural tear rates. However, it was noteworthy that UBED group exhibited a shorter operative time, lower VAS scores for leg pain at three months postoperatively, lower ODI at the final follow-up, and larger postoperative dural sac cross-sectional area compared to PELD group.

Given that the intraoperative space for UBED was not naturally physiologic, careful dissection of the bone interface using a radiofrequency tip was necessary. This procedure also involved applying high-pressure irrigation fluid to create a surgical cavity while considering hemostasis and flushing to maintain clarity in the surgical field. Currently, the learning curve associated with UBED remained steep [48]. Jiang et al. indicated that establishing an appropriate work area as well as identifying spinal layer boundaries posed challenges during the initial stages of performing UBED [49]. In their study, they concluded that compared to PELD, UBED presented several drawbacks including increased bleeding risk, longer surgical duration, extended hospital stays, and higher total hospital costs. However, our meta-analysis results revealed no significant difference in intraoperative blood loss and length of hospital stay between both groups. Conversely, we found that PELD required a longer operation time than UBED. The anatomy observed in the unilateral double-channel endoscope was consistent with that seen in conventional open procedures. The visual and surgical access were separate, allowing for the use of larger instruments during open procedures to enhance decompression efficacy. Once the ligamentum flavum was excised, sufficient space around the nerve roots was created, thereby eliminating the necessity for disc removal. However, PELD required additional time for both ligamentum flavum and nucleus pulposus removal. Furthermore, in older patients, the nucleus pulposus exhibited reduced elasticity, which could lead to detachment of disc fragments from the annulus fibrosus. Hemostasis might also take longer under these circumstances. To enlarge the lateral recess of bone effectively, it was necessary to remove bone from the posterior edge of the vertebrae as well, often resulting in significant bone bleeding. Consequently, PELD appeared to consume longer haemostatic time and overall operating duration than UBED, suggesting that UBED was superior to PELD in the decompression of nerve roots. As UBED gained popularity, it evolved such that its procedural duration became shorter than that of PELD.

Our research demonstrated that, in comparison to PELD, UBED significantly reduced VAS scores for leg pain at three months postoperatively and improved ODI at the final follow-up. These findings suggested that UBED was more effective in alleviating early postoperative neurological symptoms in lower extremities and offered greater advantages over PELD regarding long-term functional recovery. In terms of imaging outcomes, UBED significantly increased postoperative dural sac cross-sectional area when compared with PELD. This finding suggested that UBED provided superior spinal canal decompression effects relative to PELD. Overall, UBED represented a novel technique combining benefits from interlaminar endoscopy and microscopic procedures through an endoscopic platform akin to those utilized in knee arthroscopy. By integrating the observing tube and the operating tube, these instruments could be maneuvered with greater freedom, thereby enhancing both decompression capabilities and exploration potential. A significant advantage of UBED over PELD was its utilization of dual channels instead of a single tube, which facilitated more versatile operations. Consequently, there was increasing evidence that the learning curve for UBED appeared to be relatively brief. In fact, it had been suggested that approximately 54 procedures might suffice for surgeons to master this technique safely [48]. Moreover, UBED bore similarities to open decompression techniques and tended to be more familiar to spine surgeons compared to PELD. Each surgeon possessed unique operational habits and varying ranges of decompression. Historically, percutaneous endoscopic transforaminal discectomy typically necessitated ventral decompression of the dural sac along with local excision of the ligamentum flavum. In contrast, percutaneous endoscopic interlaminar discectomy required dorsal decompression of the dural sac and removal of hyperplastic ligamentum flavum. Notably, UBED effectively decompressed the dural sac across an extensive range of 270°.

Previous evidence suggested dural tears represented one of the most prevalent complications associated with UBED, with reported incidences ranging from 1.5 to 5.8% [50]. The frequency of dural tear was strongly associated with the pathology and anatomy in DLSS. Furthermore, operators who lacked experience with this surgical technique or possessed limited field visibility might encounter challenges such as turbid fluid conditions or blurred vision—factors that could contribute to dural tears due to inadequate hemorrhage control or continuous washout efforts. In cases involving unilateral laminectomy for bilateral decompression, the primary dural tear would be typically located dorsally relative to the dural sac, which occurred during ligamentum flavum excision. The primary assessment of tissue characteristics on the basis of intraoperative VAS score in recipients given local anaesthesia might aid the surgeon in avoiding operative complications like neurological damage and dural sac rupture. Nevertheless, there remained a pressing need for advancements in endoscopic dural reconstruction technology to mitigate the risk of complications. Park et al. reported the existence of calcification was significantly linked to the onset of dural tears, thereby necessitating more gentle manipulation in the presence of calcification [51]. Additionally, factors such as poor microscopic visibility, dural adhesions, and inadequate maneuvers also contributed to dural injuries. The key to preventing damage lied in maintaining open saline drainage to manage bleeding from small epidural vessels and bony surfaces, which provided a clearer field of vision and aided in identifying structural edges. Prior assessment of epidural adhesion before surgery was essential, as meticulous dissection could reduce the incidence of dural tears [52]. Furthermore, the ligamentum flavum served as an excellent protective barrier for surrounding neural structures, effectively safeguarding the dura from injury until laminectomy was completed. For novices performing UBED, employing blunt neurodissectors and high-speed diamond burrs proved considerably safer than utilizing sharp spatulas or osteotomes in cases where experience or anatomical clarity might be lacking. Some studies reported that a confined workspace coupled with challenges in handling endoscopic instruments could lead to an increased incidence of complications such as dural tears or neurological injuries [53, 54]. Nonetheless, our meta-analysis revealed that the overall complication rates were comparable between UBED and PELD. Throughout the procedure, continuous saline irrigation facilitated greater epidural workspace around neural structures and adjacent soft tissues, thus enhancing recognition and management of relevant anatomy within a limited surgical area. Consequently, continuous saline irrigation contributed to reduced complication rates.

Hospitalization expenses represented a critical consideration for both patients and surgeons alike. Several studies demonstrated that hospitalization costs associated with UBED were higher than those of PELD [55–58]. This discrepancy might be attributed to factors such as the use of general anesthesia, expensive endoscopic instruments, and the complexity of postoperative care. Yang et al. reported that UBED incurred substantial care costs, potentially due to its more intricate postoperative care requirements [58]. UBED was characterized by greater muscle trauma and necessitated the placement of intraoperative drainage tubes, which contributed to an increased workload and associated costs in postoperative care. In contrast, PELD involved less intraoperative trauma and bleeding, resulting in relatively straightforward postoperative management and thus lowered care costs. Moreover, Yang et al. found that the incremental cost-utility ratio for UBED was lower, indicating superior cost-effectiveness for UBED despite its higher initial expenses. This advantage might stem from UBED’s ability to provide a broader surgical perspective and larger working space during surgery, facilitating more comprehensive decompression. Additionally, while PELD presented a steeper learning curve [59], the operational process of UBED more closely resembled traditional surgical techniques, resulting in a gentler learning curve that was easier for practitioners to master [48, 50]. Given these advantages, UBED could achieve better cost-effectiveness even with a modest increase in overall expenditure. Consequently, UBED might be prioritized for younger patients who generally possessed good health conditions. Conversely, for elderly patients with multiple comorbidities, PELD might represent a preferable option due to its reduced trauma levels, minimized bleeding risk, and expedited recovery time.

This systematic review represented the first comprehensive summary and meta-analysis of published studies comparing the efficacy and side effects of UBED and PELD in treating DLSS. There were several limitations in our meta-analysis. First, the limited sample size constrained the robustness and external validity of the findings. Second, the follow-up duration was insufficient to thoroughly assess the safety and effectiveness of UBED. Third, this study only included observational studies, which inherently suffered from selection bias. Fourth, significant heterogeneity was observed in the data regarding operative time, hospital stay, and intraoperative blood loss, which might relate to factors dependent on the surgeon’s experience, patient characteristics, or surgical equipment. Furthermore, we were not able to explore the sources of heterogeneity through subgroup analyses or meta-regression analyses. Therefore, high heterogeneity might affect the reliability of the meta-analysis results. Fifth, differences in the type and severity of spinal stenosis and surgeon preferences might influence the results. Additionally, our study did not conduct a comparison of the learning curve and cost-utility. Therefore, the comparison between UBED and PELD was not sufficiently comprehensive. Finally, the inclusion of studies only in English or Chinese languages might introduce language bias. Hence, comprehensive large-scale multicenter controlled clinical studies, particularly high-quality RCTs and long-term follow-up outcomes, are essential to compare the long-term efficacy and safety of UBED and PELD in treating DLSS.

Conclusion

Our evidence demonstrated there was no significant difference between UBED and PELD with regard to intraoperative blood loss, hospital stay, VAS scores for back pain, overall complication rates and dural tear rates. However, it was noteworthy that UBED exhibited shorter operative time, lower VAS scores for leg pain at three months postoperatively, lower ODI at the final follow-up, and a larger postoperative dural sac cross-sectional area compared to PELD. In conclusion, these findings suggest that UBED appears more effective in treating single-level DLSS compared to PELD. Further multi-center randomized controlled trials are necessary before definitive conclusions can be drawn.
