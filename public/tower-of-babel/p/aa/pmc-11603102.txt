Prediction of biochemical recurrence after radical prostatectomy from primary tumour characteristics
Authors: Matthew J Roberts, Nathan Papa, Hans Veerman, Katelijne de Bie, Andrew Morton, Anthony Franklin, Sheliyan Raveenthiran, William J Yaxley, Maarten L Donswijk, Henk G van der Poel, Hemamali Samaratunga, David Wong, Nicholas Brown, Robert Parkinson, Troy Gianduzzo, Boon Kua, Geoffrey D Coughlin, Daniela E Oprea‐Lager, Louise Emmett, Pim J van Leeuwen, John W Yaxley, André N Vis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo construct and externally calibrate a predictive model for early biochemical recurrence (BCR) after radical prostatectomy (RP) incorporating clinical and modern imaging characteristics of the primary tumour.Patients and MethodsPatients who underwent RP following multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging, prostate biopsy and prostate‐specific membrane antigen‐positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA‐PET/CT), from two centres in Australia and the Netherlands. The primary outcome was biochemical recurrence‐free survival (BRFS), where BCR was defined as a rising PSA level of ≥0.2 ng/mL or initiation of postoperative treatment per clinician discretion. Proportional hazards models to predict time to event were developed in the Australian sample using relevant pre‐ and post‐surgical parameters and primary tumour maximum standardised uptake value (SUVmax) on diagnostic PSMA‐PET/CT. Calibration was assessed in an external dataset from the Netherlands with the same inclusion criteria.ResultsData from 846 patients were used to develop the models. Tumour SUVmax was associated with worse predicted 3‐year BRFS for both pre‐ and post‐surgical models. SUVmax change from 4 to 16 lessened the predicted 3‐year BRFS from 66% to 42% for a patient aged 65 years with typical pre‐surgical parameters (PSA level 8 ng/mL, Prostate Imaging‐Reporting and Data System score 4/5 and biopsy Gleason score ≥4 + 5). Considering post‐surgical variables, a patient with the same age and PSA level but pathological stage pT3a, RP Gleason score ≥4 + 5 and negative margins, SUVmax change from 4 to 16 lessened the predicted 3‐year BRFS from 76% to 61%. Calibration on an external sample (n = 464) showed reasonable performance; however, a tendency to overestimate survival in patients with good prognostic factors was observed.ConclusionTumour SUVmax on diagnostic PSMA‐PET/CT has utility additional to commonly recognised variables for prediction of BRFS after RP.Keywords: Prostate‐specific membrane antigen, PSMA, PET/CT, biochemical failure, radical prostatectomy, Gleason score

Introduction

Radical prostatectomy (RP) for localised prostate cancer is often performed with curative intent; however, a significant proportion of patients experience postoperative biochemical recurrence (BCR). Subsequent management of BCR includes local, potentially curative treatments (e.g., prostatic fossa with/without pelvic radiotherapy) as well as systemic, non‐curative treatments (e.g., androgen‐deprivation therapy) [1]. Quality of life for patients receiving single or multiple treatments is reported to be less than that of those who receive surgery alone [2, 3]. Furthermore, difficulty with prediction or location of distant metastases may render local treatments ineffective but still cause morbidity.

Prediction of BCR after RP, with ensuing treatment and potential morbidity, has been attempted using various clinical and molecular classifiers [4, 5]. In recent years, the use of prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET) has changed the way that BCR after RP is defined and assessed. PSMA PET is now recommended for re‐staging of patients with BCR to detect metastases and invoke management change [1], while a negative PSMA PET may be predictive of a favourable outcome after local salvage treatment [6, 7]. Preliminary data has suggested that preoperative staging PSMA PET characteristics, such as the primary tumour PSMA intensity (or ligand uptake), may be prognostic [8, 9] and may assist with the identification of patients who are at risk of BCR, and thus require further treatments, after RP.

The aim of this study was to develop a predictive model for BCR after RP with incorporation of imaging characteristics, together with clinical and pathological variables. External validation of this predictive model was performed using an international dataset. The hypothesis is that PSMA PET characteristics, specifically intraprostatic uptake, will improve the prediction of biochemical failure after RP.

Patients and Methods

Setting

In this multicentre, international, retrospective study, patients who underwent multiparametric MRI (mpMRI), transperineal prostate biopsy and PSMA PET prior to robot‐assisted RP (RARP) were selected. From all patients, clinical data were collected (i.e., age, serum PSA level). The Australian cohort from Brisbane was sourced from an institutional database across the time period of August 2014 to June 2018. The Netherlands cohort from Amsterdam included patients who underwent RARP between August 2016 and June 2022 at the Amsterdam University Medical Centres (VUmc), and Netherlands Cancer Institute (NCI). Institutional ethics approval was obtained (approval numbers 2019.25.303 [Brisbane], 2017.543 [VUmc] and IRBdm19‐348 [NCI]).

Imaging

The MRI was performed prior to prostate biopsy according to a standard institutional protocol, including T2‐weighted, diffusion‐weighted imaging and apparent diffusion coefficient with/without dynamic contrast enhancement, using a 3‐T Siemens Skyra machine, without use of endorectal coil. Images were reported by experienced radiologists according to the Prostate Imaging‐Reporting and Data System (PI‐RADS) version (1 or 2) applicable to the time that the scan was performed.

The PSMA PET/CT was performed for primary staging according to institutional protocols. In the Australian cohort, 68Ga‐PSMA‐11 PET/CT was performed using two scanners (Phillips Ingenuity, General Electric [GE] Discovery Molecular Imaging [MI]) after 45–60 min minimum uptake time following injection of 68Ga‐PSMA‐11. Anatomical localisation and attenuation correction was aided by low‐dose, 128‐slice CT scan. PET images were reported by experienced nuclear physicians using either Phillips IntelliSpace or Siemens syngo.via, including inclusion of intraprostatic maximum standard uptake value (SUVmax) values routinely. The highest SUVmax for intraprostatic regions of interest was considered for this analysis, due to our prior work showing highest SUVmax to best associate with the index lesion and most oncological relevance [10].

In the cohort from the Netherlands, PSMA‐PET was performed with either 18F‐DCFPyL (PSMA) or 68Ga‐PSMA‐11, after 60–120 min and 44–60 min tracer incubation time, respectively, using multiple scanners (Philips Ingenuity, Philips Gemini TF‐II or Vereos Digital PET/CT; Philips®, the Netherlands/USA) in line with European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) Research Ltd. (EARL) harmonisation standards. Anatomical localisation and attenuation correction was aided by either low‐dose or diagnostic CT scan. Volumes‐of‐interest (VOIs) were manually drawn (by one expert) to the size of the prostate area with care taken to omit physiological tracer activity (bladder, urethra). The SUVmax was automatically calculated in those VOIs, using available clinical software: Sectra IDS7 (Sectra AB, Linköping, Sweden) and Osirix MD (Pixmeo SARL, Bernex, Switzerland), which have both been cross‐validated to have 100% agreement. The VOI with highest SUVmax was dominant intraprostatic lesion, thus lesion of highest suspicion to be clinically significant, and included in the analysis.

Imaging protocols from both cohorts have previously been described [9, 11, 12].

Histopathology

Histopathological reporting for both prostate biopsy and RP specimen were performed according to International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) protocol [13].

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was BCR‐free survival (BRFS), defined as freedom of BCR (defined as a serum PSA level >0.2 ng/mL and rising on two separate occasions) or salvage/adjuvant treatment [14], according to clinician discretion, or death due to other causes. Patients were censored at the date of the last recorded PSA test if an event was not recorded. Horizon time point of 36 months was used, in line with international guidelines.

Statistical Analysis

Proportional hazards models for BRFS were developed using pre‐surgical covariates (age; log2PSA; biopsy Gleason score categories: 6 and 7 (3 + 4), 7 (4 + 3), and 8, ≥4 + 5; PI‐RADS categories: 2/3, 4/5) or with post‐surgical covariates (age; log2PSA; RP Gleason score categories: 6 and 7 (3 + 4), 7 (4 + 3), and 8, ≥4 + 5; pathological stage: pT2, pT3a, pT3b/4; margin status) that were defined a priori based on published data and nomograms [15, 16]. The PI‐RADS scores of 2 and 3 were combined, due to similar risk (less than or ~20%) of significant cancer (Gleason score ≥7) when MRI triage is performed (most PI‐RADS score 2 patients avoid biopsy) based on published data [17, 18].

The log2SUVmax was then entered along with interaction terms between it and all other covariates except for age. Differences and relative fit between models were assessed with the likelihood ratio test and Akaike information criteria (AIC). Proportional hazards assumption was evaluated by examining Schoenfeld residuals. Check for the calibration of the model in an external validation cohort (Amsterdam) was performed using the method described by Royston [19], defining three prognostic groups with cut‐points at the 25th and 75th centile of the linear predictor from the Cox model. Predictive accuracy was evaluated with the ‘Index of Prediction Accuracy’ (IPA) measure [20] at the 36‐month time horizon for the full model, one without SUVmax, and one using only the Cancer of the Prostate Risk Assessment‐Postsurgical (CAPRA‐S) score [21] as a continuous variable but omitting seminal vesicle invasion. Analysis was undertaken with Stata, version 17.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA) with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Sample Description

The data of 846 patients from the Brisbane cohort were used to develop the models, the clinical and imaging characteristics are outlined in Table 1. The median PSA level was 6.0 ng/mL, SUVmax was 5.6, PI‐RADS score of 4 or 5 present in 86%, with most common Gleason score of 3 + 4 for both biopsy (35%) and RP (37%), as well as organ‐confined pathological stage pT2 (48%). A total of 219 patients experienced biochemical failure on follow‐up. The median follow‐up in patients without an event was 46 months.

Survival Outcomes

In the models incorporating pre‐surgical variables, inclusion of SUVmax improved the AIC (2639 vs 2650) and was statistically significantly different to a model without this parameter (P < 0.001). The predicted BRFS at various levels of PSA, biopsy grade and SUVmax were plotted as a function of time (Fig. 1) and at 1‐ and 3‐years after RP (Fig. S1). Considerable differences in 3‐year survival estimates were noted as SUVmax increased. As an example, for a patient aged 65 years, with a PSA level of 8 ng/mL, PI‐RADS 4 or 5 and biopsy Gleason score ≥4 + 5, the predicted BRFS rate at 1 year was 82% for SUVmax 4 vs 66% for SUVmax 16, and 3‐year BRFS was 66% vs 42%.

Predicted BRFS up to 36 months after RP according to pre‐surgical parameter model at three levels of PSMA PET SUVmax (4 = blue, 8 = green or 16 = red). PSA level set at 8 or 16 ng/mL; biopsy Grade Group 1/2, 3/4 or 5; age fixed at 65 years and PI‐RADS at 4 or 5.

For models with post‐surgical variables, the value of including SUVmax was less certain with AIC marginally superior (2527 vs 2530) though the models with and without SUVmax were still significantly different (P = 0.019). Plots of the predicted survival curves showed appreciable differences according to magnitude of SUVmax for surgical margin negative disease (Fig. 2). For example, a patient aged 65 years with a PSA level of 8 ng/mL, pT3a, Gleason score ≥4 + 5 disease with negative surgical margins, the predicted 3‐year BRFS proportion varied from 76% if the SUVmax was 4 to 61% if the SUVmax was 16. However, this variation was less pronounced in surgical margin positive disease (Fig. 2).

Calibration in an External Sample

An external sample from Amsterdam comprised 464 patients whose details are listed in Table S1. Significant differences from the Australian sample were noted in most baseline characteristics, particularly margin positivity (36% vs 20%). A total of 141 events were observed and the median follow‐up time in those without the event was 21 months.

Substantial differences in the survival curves were noted between the two sites (Fig. S2) with worse BRFS in the Netherlands sample. Splitting the sample into ‘good’ (76th–100th percentile), ‘intermediate’ (26–75th percentile) and ‘poor’ (1st–25th percentile) prognostic groups, the pre‐surgical parameter model tended to overestimate survival for patients in the ‘good’ prognostic group, with slight overestimations for the ‘intermediate’ group (Fig. 3). Survival predictions were better for the ‘poor’ group and for all three groups in the post‐surgical parameter model. Calibration plots of the models without SUVmax showed notably poorer performance in the ‘intermediate’ prognostic group, especially in the post‐surgical model where survival was considerably overestimated (Fig. S3). The IPA for the full model (27.9%) was clearly superior to one including only CAPRA‐S (18.6%), but not higher than a model without SUVmax (27.8%).

Calibration in an external validation (Amsterdam) sample. Black denotes ‘good’ prognostic group, green = ‘intermediate’ prognostic group, orange = ‘poor’ prognostic group. Thick lines are the predicted BRFS after RP over time, connected dots and caps denote the actual survival.

Discussion

Despite advances in disease characterisation with mpMRI, targeted biopsy and PSMA PET prior to RP, prediction of postoperative BCR and potential subsequent treatment remains an unmet need for clinicians and patients. Here, incorporation of quantitative PSMA PET information (primary tumour SUVmax) indicated that further delineation of BCR profiles was possible when combined with existing clinical information (histopathology, PSA value). However, generalisability of the predictive models may be limited, as suggested by the clinical differences for the validation cohort resulting in variation in predicted from actual outcome data. Overall, these findings are an important advance in understanding the contribution of preoperative PSMA PET to prediction of BRFS outcomes, as well as the multifactorial nature of BCR.

First, the additive contribution of clinical variables (histopathology, PSA value) with quantitative PSMA PET information (primary tumour SUVmax) was determined in two clinically applicable measures. In the overall BRFS estimates (Fig. S1), an overall survival percentage at 1 and 3 years can be determined for the given variables. This is visual estimation of the natural course of the disease, similar to the widely used estimates from Albertsen et al. [22], illustrating the additive value of included variables and is simple to use. Furthermore, the representative survival curves for different histopathology categories and SUVmax levels (Figs 1 and 2) clearly indicate the additive value of PSMA‐ligand uptake, mostly due to overexpression within primary tumours (but also tumour size, tracer uptake time, PET acquisition and other technical factors) as a marker for cancer biology [23]. These visual aids are similar to historical data from Pound et al. [24], but provide contemporary, multifactorial estimations. Prior clinical data has suggested additive value of primary tumour SUVmax to predict clinical outcomes [8, 9, 10, 12, 25], supported by basic science studies implicating PSMA in mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)‐regulated gene transcription via protein kinase B (AKT)–mTOR pathway upregulation [26] and higher PSMA expression in men with castration‐resistant than hormone‐sensitive disease translating to worse overall survival [23, 27, 28].

Second, the multiple contributors to BCR survival outcomes were demonstrated. The utility of SUVmax was similar in the preoperative model to the postoperative model for surgical margin negative patients (Figs 1 and 2). However, the additive benefit of PSMA PET information (SUVmax) was attenuated in surgical margin positive patients (Fig. 2B), who expectedly showed lower BRFS compared to their surgical margin negative contemporaries. An advantage of RP is the additional information garnered from whole‐mount histopathology, where Gleason score is the most significant predictor of progression outcomes to guide postoperative treatments. However, prediction of change in Gleason score from biopsy to RP, even with MRI‐targeted biopsies [29], can occur in about one third of patients but prediction of upward change is challenging. Therefore, incorporation of clinical and PSMA PET characteristics may improve preoperative prediction of survival outcomes. Positive surgical margins are known to be an independent predictor of BRFS from other variables (Gleason score, stage, PSA) [30], as well as previously shown in the Brisbane cohort here [8]. Therefore, it is likely that the contributory effect to BRFS by positive surgical margins negates that of PSMA PET information overall; however, it may have additional value in Gleason score 7 (3 + 4 and 4 + 3) tumours, a biologically heterogeneous cohort where sub‐classification has been limited to molecular classifiers [31] that are associated with adverse PSMA PET outcomes [28]. PSMA PET has been consistently shown to help identify worse biology in this heterogeneous cohort [23].

External validation of the models showed reasonable performance, particularly for patients in the poor prognostic category (Fig. 3). While BRFS was over‐estimated particularly in the good‐risk group, and adding SUVmax did not confer substantial predictive accuracy, it is likely that differences within the patient cohorts are the main driver, in particular higher surgical margin positivity (36% vs 20%). Reproducibility of predictive models is a known challenge seen for many other nomograms [5], such as the Kattan, John's Hopkins (Han), Walz, CAPRA‐S and Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) nomograms. Known technical limitations in direct comparison of SUVmax between centres [32], such as difference in tracer [33] (68Ga‐PSMA‐11 in Brisbane versus 68Ga‐PSMA‐11 and 18F‐DCFPyL in Amsterdam), PET camera calibration and potentially acquisition parameters (timing of scan after tracer injection) may account for the differences in SUVmax between the centres (median 5.6 vs 8.1; P < 0.001) and generally limited value of SUVmax in the Amsterdam cohort.

Despite the strengths of the study, including data from high‐volume, tertiary facilities resulting in large training cohort and external, international validation cohort using the same outcome definitions, consideration of the limitations is important for translation to clinical practice. First, the study was a retrospective analysis of existing databases, hence harmonisation of data collection in terms of exposure and outcome variables between the two sites was limited. This likely had effects characterised by the disparate survival curves and prevented optimisation of the external calibration. We also only considered primary tumour, or intraprostatic, characteristics so the contribution of clinical and pathological nodal staging to the models was not assessed (but is already established [34, 35]). Second, the trade‐off for the large datasets is variability in surgeon practice, such as different biopsy templates (number of cores), use of pelvic lymph node dissection and clinical staging in a PSA detected [36], mpMRI‐triaged cohort, which also is a strength in being broadly generalisable to clinical practice. Similarly, variation in imaging acquisition and reporting may limit the accuracy in direct comparison between sites, but also reflects the inherent differences in clinical practice between imaging providers that clinicians encounter. Comparison to existing nomograms was not feasible, due to differences in horizon time points for various nomograms.

In conclusion, this large, international study has shown that PSMA‐PET SUVmax has an additive contribution for the preoperative and postoperative prediction of BRFS after RP. Greatest benefits seemed to be present for intermediate‐ and high‐risk patients. The data presented here provide valuable insights for clinicians when counselling patients on BRFS after RP and potential requirement for salvage therapies. Further validation in prospective studies, with consideration of change in management based on pre‐surgical factors and their subsequent oncological outcomes, as well as investigation to accurately adjust for PET parameters between institutions is warranted.
