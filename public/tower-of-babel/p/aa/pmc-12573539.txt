Inuit youths’ explanations of avoidance of formal mental health services and what should be done: results from interviews in two Nunavut communities
Authors: Léa Plourde-Léveillé, Brian L. Mishara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Nunavut, Inuit territory in Canada, young people have suicide rates nine times higher than the overall Canadian rates. However, mental health services struggle to reach young people. Regional suicide prevention organizations call for improved services and a better continuum of care. Yet little research has explored young people's experiences with the services currently available. As part of a larger study on resilience in Inuit youth, we asked them to explain their lack of use of mental health and suicide prevention services. The study was conducted in Arviat and Pangnirtung and followed the Inuit methodology Aajiiqatigiingniq Research Methodology (ARM), a cultural process for consensus building and solution-seeking compatible with a qualitative research approach. Advisory boards were created in each community. Interviews were conducted with thirty-one Inuit youths age 15 to 24. Data were analyzed by a thematic analysis. Young Inuit expressed a general discomfort with available services, including uneasiness with the health center and the way services are provided, they lacked information about services, lamented inadequate outreach methods and expressed a feeling of mistrust. Our findings support the value of several ongoing initiatives based upon cultural traditions, and may inform the continuum of care for suicide prevention .

Introduction

There are significant differences in suicide rates and trends between and within countries. In recent decades, particular attention has been paid to the alarming and disproportionate incidence of death by suicide in indigenous communities around the world. In Canada, suicide rates among indigenous populations are three to nine times higher than the Canadian national average [1]. Although some communities have rates similar to the general population, others have extremely high suicide rates that mandate an urgent and effective response. In Nunavut, the most recent statistics for 2000 to 2019 show a suicide rate nearly nine times higher than the Canadian national average and Inuit youth aged 15 to 29 are the most at risk of engaging in suicidal behaviour [2,3]. This situation is of particular concern since the Inuit population is the youngest and fastest growing in Canada [4].

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), differences in the incidence of health statistics often reflect an unequal access to healthcare resources [5]. Research has shown that in contexts where several cultures meet, the biases of the dominant culture are expressed in health systems through the nature of services provided, resulting in barriers to accessing quality care for members of cultural minorities [6]. The main barriers to access to care in indigenous populations have been described as: 1) lack of cultural adaptation, particularly in terms of language and inclusion of belief systems including traditional healing methods; 2) systemic racism; 3) lack of acknowledgement of the specificities of Aboriginal cultures: and 4) experiences of discrimination and exclusion that stem from the historical colonial context [7–9]. In remote communities, geographical barriers limiting access to services, high staff turnover and lack of supervision of professionals are often additional barriers [10].

The National Collaborating Centre for Aboriginal Health [11] encourages the recognition of the diversity among Canadian Indigenous populations and the acknowledgement of the local realities in terms of strengths, needs and challenges. A recent systematic literature review on mental health interventions among Canada's aboriginal populations highlighted the paucity of research on the specific reality of the Inuit. The authors concluded by calling for the development of research projects focusing on the needs of subpopulations, including Inuit, to produce more context-specific findings, in order to better understand mental health resource needs [12]. In a context where health needs, including mental health, are high and services are limited, such research is essential to identify recommendations for enhancing the use of available services, and to develop new resources that meet users' needs.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada produced a call to action to work in consultation with Indigenous peoples to close the health gaps between Indigenous and non-Indigenous communities [13]. Recently, Canada's Minister of Health and Nunavut's Minister of Health, announced two bilateral agreements to invest a total of more than $35.6 million to improve health care access and services in Nunavut [14]. In this context, we conducted a participatory research project co-developed with Inuit community members, to better understand factors that Inuit youth consider to be associated with their limited use of mental health services available in their communities, their perceptions of those services, what they feel inhibits their use of services, and potential ways to facilitate better use of necessary services that are adapted to their needs.

Context

Nunavut, which means “our land” in Inuktitut, is Canada's northernmost region and home of Inuit. Today, Nunavut's population is divided into 25 communities, but this wasn't always the case. Until the 1950s, Inuit lived in small groups across the territory, moving with the seasons and animal migrations. Emotional and spiritual wellbeing was relational and promoted through Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ), Inuit ways of knowing, and the Maligait (Inuit Big Laws) that governed how to live a good life. Key principles including Pilimmaksarniq – developing skills through practice, Piliriqatigiinniq – Working together for the common good and Tunnganarniq – open heartness. These were core expectations of all Inuit and were maintained through kinship [15,16].

Since 1945, Canadian sovereignty was first gradually and then rapidly imposed through a series of coercive measures, including forced sedentarization, slaughter of sled dogs, residential schools and the relocation of part of the population to inhospitable regions further north. In the book Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, Tester argues that “it is hard to imagine a more rapid and, for many, devastating exercise of social change than that experienced by Inuit in the period after the Second World War” [16]. This colonisation is recognised as having caused a significant erasure of traditional Inuit knowledge, values and practices, a disintegration of family and community ties, a decline in self-esteem, and historical and intergenerational trauma [3,16].

The 1950s marked the beginning of movements to resist the erosion of Inuit socio-cultural identity and to protest against living conditions in these new communities. After years of fierce demands, Nunavut was separated from the Northwest Territories in 1999 by the Nunavut Act, which provides Inuit with self-government. Over the years, the federal government of Canada has gradually transferred responsibility to the territorial government for health, education, social services and housing, enabling Nunavummiut (people of Nunavut) to have greater control over decisions on their land. Today, Nunavut's health care system is funded by Health Canada and the Public Health Agency of Canada, with funds transferred to the Government of Nunavut and then distributed to communities through regional boards, which coordinate and administer health services in Nunavut's three main regions (Kivalliq, Qikiqtaalik and Kitikmeot). Nunavut currently has one hospital, the Qikiqtani General Hospital in Iqaluit, and two large medical clinics in the regional centres of Rankin Inlet and Cambridge Bay. Other communities have community nurses, annual physician visits, and sometimes a mental health nurse working at the health centre.

The high incidence of suicide in Nunavut has raised concerns regarding the state of mental health services. Under the current service structure, residents with severe mental health difficulties must travel to receive care in tertiary care facilities located outside the territory in Yellowknife, Edmonton, Winnipeg or Ottawa, far from their homes and families. Those with more moderate symptoms are usually cared for in the community by a nurse or mental health nurse while waiting for a visit from a doctor, possibly with telehealth psychiatric consultation). Almost half of all positions in the Department of Health are vacant and less than half of the communities have a mental health nurse or mental health worker, although all communities have a position for one [17,18]. Furthermore, according to recent published data, 92% of the Department of Health's professional workforce is non-Inuit, with little prior working knowledge of life in the arctic, and lacking knowledge of Inuit culture. More than half of the physicians working in Nunavut are hired on short-term contracts for fewer than 20 days at a time [19,20]. This situation gives rise to significant concerns about the cultural appropriateness and transitory nature of the mental health services available in the communities. We have not identified any research on the impact of turnover and recruitment difficulties on service in Nunavut. However, research reporting on data collected from First Nations and other aboriginal communities around the world suggest that this situation leads to low patient satisfaction increasing the likelihood of their disengagement from the care process, wasted staff time, lack of cultural safety, poorer health outcomes, and negative effects on the relationships between patients and health care providers due to a lack of trust toward professionals [21–23]. Recent research conducted in Nunavik (the part of northern Quebec Province mostly inhabited by Inuit) highlighted a negative perception of mental health services, evidence of systemic racism, and significant concerns about trust in services and confidentiality that hindered access to care. Several articles on Nunavik conclude that since many Inuit find that health and mental health services do not reflect their values, knowledge and ways of interacting, many Inuit choose not to go to the services until a crisis erupts [24,25]. These findings from Nunavik may not be generalisable to Nunavut, which is distinguished by a territorial government that directly decides about provision of mental health care. The Government of Nunavut manages its own health system, in accordance with Inuit societal values [26]. This contrasts Nunavik, which is governed by the Quebec provincial legislature.

Over the years, several efforts have been made to improve access and the quality of services offered to Nunavut youth. Several incentive-based employee recruitment and retention strategies have been put in place (including wage increases, retention bonuses, assistance in paying back student loans, and tuition forgiveness). Arctic College, in Iqaluit, has developed a Health and Wellness programme to promote learning opportunities for adult Nunavummiut and to increase their employability in health and social services. Despite these efforts, mental health and social service positions are often unfilled, and mental health programmes and services vary greatly across the territory [27]. Since 1990, Nunavut has had a regional helpline, called Kamatsiaqtut, offering mental health and suicide prevention support services in Inuktitut and English. Nunavut youth also have access to national hotlines such as: Hope for Wellness, that offers counselling and crisis intervention for all indigenous people in Canada, the Indian Residential School Crisis Line, that provides former residential school students and their families with free and confidential counselling, and the Kids Help Phone for counselling geared to young people. According to a review on Nunavut youth mental health services, use for these helplines in Nunavut is very low [27]. In the Qikiqtani Region, the Northern Psychiatric Outreach Programme of the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (NPOP) provide community-relevant services and education. Their services include monthly consultation visits by psychiatrists in Iqaluit, Arctic Bay, Kingaait, Clyde River, Sanirajak, Igloolik, Kimmirut, Pangnirtung, Pond Inlet, and Qikiqtarjuaq, as well as mental health rounds by teleconference [28]. In 2018−2019 they provided 149 days of clinical service in 24 visits to the region, including 21 trips by psychiatry residents affiliated with the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Toronto. Since 2014, the TeleLink Mental Health programme (TeleLink), a partnership between the Ontario Department of Health’s Mental Health and Addictions Team and The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids), offers psychiatric services for children and youth by telephone or video calls. In addition to remote assistance, Crisis services are usually offered by various regional and national organisation when traumatic events occur. These include debriefing sessions, mobile trauma response services, and additional psychosocial supports [27]. However, despite all these efforts, concerns persist. Findings from a 2019 review of available mental health services found that 82% of service providers working mainly in youth mental health and 90% of the general population felt that mental health services for young people in Nunavut were not meeting their needs. Among service providers, 72% questioned the quality of the services offered, and this opinion was shared by 82% of the general population. This report on children and youth mental health services concluded that, while it is important to recognise that there are people working hard to provide mental health services for young people in the territory, a tremendous amount of systemic change must occur for services to be perceived as helpful, used by young people and have services available that meet the mental health needs of the population [27].

In 2016, the Inuit Tapiiriit Kanatami (ITK) National Suicide Prevention Strategy called for ensuring access to a continuum of Mental wellness services for Inuit to help individuals experiencing mental distress and mental illness, including depression and substance misuse [3]. In keeping with these priorities, and in order to inform decisions regarding planned investments to improve access to health services in Nunavut, we explored the experiences and perceptions of Inuit youth regarding the mental health services currently offered in their community, as well as their perceived needs and their suggestions concerning how to promote their use of mental health services.

Methods

This paper reports the finding of a community based participatory research project, conducted between 2017 and 2022 in Arviat and in Pangnirtung Nunavut. Arviat is a community in the Kivalliq region, located on the western shores of Hudson Bay, with a population of 3170. The community of Pangnirtung is located on the Baffin Island in the Qikiqtaaluk Region and is composed of 1673 inhabitants. In both communities, approximately 50% of the population is aged 25 or younger [17]. The objective was to better understand the factors promoting resiliency of Inuit youth in Nunavut as well as their experiences with mental health services (Plourde-Leveille, Mishara, 202611Submited, currently being reviewed.). Formal mental health services were defined as those provided at the health centre, as well as telephone helplines, which the local advisory board members indicated to be the only mental health services currently available. The project received approval from the Nunavut Research Institute (NRI), an organisation designated by the Government of Nunavut to review and authorise research in the territory to ensure their ethics, inclusivity, relevance, and cultural sensitivity.

Data gathering approach

The research process was designed under the guidance of an advisory board in each of the participating communities and followed the Aajiiqatigiingniq Research Methodology (ARM), an Inuit methodology described as a cultural process for consensus building and solution-seeking that is compatible with a qualitative research approach [29]. ARM is characterised by: 1) a focus on wellness; 2) a process of successively adding members to the discussion; 3) respectful and trusted communications; 4) reliance on narrative discourse; 5) personal engagement with the consensus process; and 6) meeting styles that appear structurally informal and unrestricted by time limits [29]. In line with the approach, the first author spent time in the two communities to develop and maintain trusting reciprocal relationships with community members throughout the research process. All phases of the project, from developing the first drafts of the research questions, were developed based upon one-on-one and small group discussions with members of both communities guided by local community advisory boards to ensure coherence with the community’s vision and priorities. Concerns related to language, interview locations, possible risks and confidentiality issues were discussed. The interview questions were reviewed by members of the advisory boards to ensure that they were not triggering. Special attention was paid to the wording of the interview questions to highlight the strengths of the communities and their youth and explore their needs in order to build a better future. For example « Tell me about a time when you successfully overcame a challenge or dealt with a stressful situation » or « We sometimes hear that young people do not tend to use formal services. What do you think about that? » . Opportunities for informal discussion were also offered to all community members who wished to share their knowledge on suicide prevention, in to inform the research process, as well as to provide feedback on the interpretation of the results and their implications.

Participant engagement

In the fall and winter of 2019, semi-structured interviews lasting from 30 minutes to one and a half hours were conducted and recorded by the first author, with 31 youths (19 women and 12 men) age 15−24 (mean age 19). In accordance with the ARM, at the time of data collection the interviewer had already spent several weeks in the communities, resulting in a natural snowball effect of increasing involvement of young people in the research, corroborating Healey and Tagak's [30] advice on the importance of relationship building to conduct culturally sensitive research. Considering the sensitivity of the topics that may be discussed during interviews, key community members (mental health nurses, elders, teachers, youth leaders) identified by members of the advisory boards were informed of the time and place of each interview to ensure the availability of mental health support during and after the interview, if needed. The interviewer had suicide prevention training and cultural training to provide temporary support if needed. The second author, who has extensive experience in training and supervision of suicide prevention interventions in diverse cultures, who works in collaboration with the Nunavut Kamatsiaqtut Help Line, was available to consult and support the interviewer, if needed. Informed consent was obtained from each participant prior the interview and a list of resources including local and regional formal services and the names of key people in the community who could help them, such as elders and youth leaders identified by members of the advisory boards, was given to all participants. None of the participants disclosed information during the interviews indicating the presence of a risk of suicidal behaviours.

Data analysis

Due to Covid-19 restrictions on visits to Nunavut, it was decided, based upon advice given by members of the advisory boards, that an initial analysis of the results would be conducted by the PI. The relationship established over the years allowed the members of the advisory committee to trust the lead author to carry out this initial stage of analysis. A thematic data analysis based upon a predominantly inductive method was chosen, using Nvivo12 [31]. Broad themes related to the research questions were identified to provide a general overview of the content. These themes were organised in light of the generous teachings shared by community members regarding the relational worldview that characterises Inuit epistemology [16,30]. As a first step, the coping strategies used and considered effective by Inuit youth were identified and discussed with the members of the advisory committees. The results of these discussions were the subject of a previous article (submitted, currently being reviewed). The interview responses concerning respondents’ knowledge, use and perceptions of formal mental health services in Nunavut were subsequently analysed, along with the participants’ recommendations to improve services. In ARM, the equal weight of voices is seen as critical to ensure that the consensus reflect the « truth » [29]. Therefore, the results of this second analysis were discussed with community members who wished to share their knowledge and opinions, including the local advisory board members in each community. Building on that principle of respectful and trusted communication, the subjectivity of all partners involved, including that of the researchers, was recognised as a resource for interpretation and the creation of meaning. In that sense, the article integrates knowledge shared by local partners who provided advice and guidance for the study since its inception, as understood by the authors; the principal investigator (PI), a non-Inuit Ph.D. student supervised by a non-Inuit university professor, with much of his practical and research activities conducted in various cultures around the world, and with active consultation and collaborative research projects on suicide prevention in Nunavut, including participating in the development of the ITK Inuit Suicide Prevention Strategy, and working with the Nunavut Kamatsiaqtut Help Line in a collaborative culturally adapted applied research programme to prevent repetition of suicide attempts in Nunavut using lay community resources.

Results

In the initial analysis of the data conducted in the first phase of this research project, aimed at identifying the coping strategies used by young people to overcome difficulties and distress, the use of formal services was not mentioned as a coping strategy by any of the 31 young people interviewed. Many mentioned that they would not consider using formal services even in the case of great distress. The few who had used formal services in the past all reported significant discomfort and said they would not use them again. When questioned about this, the youths spoke at length about the reasons why they and their friends do not use formal mental health services. Their responses portrayed a generally critical attitude towards the services currently available. In this section we first, report the views of all participants, and then we summarise the experiences of youths who had direct contact with mental health services in the past.

The scary health centre and its resources

The interviews indicate a general discomfort with the local health centre and considerable reluctance to use the mental health services offered. Many described the health centre as “scary”, explaining that the mere idea of going there generated “a bad anxiety”. Some youths reported feeling uncomfortable about consulting the services, due to a lack of knowledge about the services available. There was a sense of fear associated with not knowing what they would be talking about, or what they would have to tell the professionals they consulted. The general impression was that they might be forced to talk about painful subjects.

“I hate going to the health centre, it’s always scary”

Talking face to face is hard, especially in a second language

The face-to-face modality was also cited as a barrier to using the mental health services at the health centre. The embarrassment of recounting one's emotions in person seems to make many people prefer not to consult.

“​​​​Face to face is hard, Like It's hard to explain feeling and how you feel. We feel shy, and our voice getting shaky when we talk and sometimes our voice breaks. Easier for me to text”.

Some also talked about a language barrier, the need for services in Inuktitut. Recalling that “Some people cannot even speak English, especially the younger kids”, participants elaborated on the feeling of discomfort in having to express themselves in English to strangers, on sensitive themes such as mental health difficulties. They agreed that “it would be helpful if nurses spoke Inuktitut, at least basic”.

For most of the youths interviewed, access to care in their mother tongue is essential, but not sufficient. Formal services was said to be incompatible with their needs when going through a difficult period.

“I would rather talk to someone I know, not talk but just have someone just be there for me, not talk but just be there”.

Staff turnover hinders the development of trusting relationships

The temporary presence of mental health professionals was identified as a factor contributing to the fear of the health centre, as well as the discomfort of the face-to-face meetings. Several young people mentioned that they were uncomfortable with the idea of consulting mental health resources, even when they experienced great distress, since they do not know the professionals providing the services, and expect that they will not be there for long. Elaborating on “not opening up to people like that”, several young people reported feeling that the current context prevents them from developing relationships of trust, leading them to turn to peers or family.

“I don't usually talk with those people because I just trust my parents”.

Some expressed major doubts about the potential benefits of consulting temporary mental health service providers, if ever they decide to contact them. They spoke of the constant need to repeat their difficult story to each new professional, without the person staying long enough to offer the necessary follow-up.

“So, a mental health nurse comes, I talk, tell my grievances and stuff. They work but they leave, one more worker comes and I have to repeat so its repeating”

Information about helplines do not reach youth

Helplines are formal mental health services available outside the health centre. They overcome some of the barriers mentioned above: services are provided by phone, text or chat rather than face-to-face. Still, young people said they did not feel any more comfortable calling them. In fact, only one participant mentioned having contacted a youth helpline in the past. When asked about the reasons for their discomfort, many mentioned the language barrier.

“Youth hardly know how to speak English. If I ever became suicidal and there was a toll free number, I would want to speak to somebody who could also speak, Inuktitut”.

None of the 31 participants we met were aware that the Kamatsiaqtut Nunavut Helpline also offered services in Inuktitut. When informed of this option, some expressed an interest in the resource, while others mentioned that they still had no intention of using the service, citing the telephone modality as the main barrier to use Kamatsiaqtut. Kamatsiaqtut is now offering help by chat and text as part of their affiliation with the national 988 helpline network, but this has not been publicised and appears to have limited availability at this time.

“I'm not comfortable using help lines because I can't see the person so I'm not comfortable, even if the person speaks Inuktitut”.

The misalignment between encouraging spoken expression and the need for silent accompaniment in times of distress was also cited to explain why many are reluctant to use helplines.

“I just don't feel like talking”.

It is hard to know who to talk to

Participants mentioned finding it particularly difficult to know who to talk to and when. Above all, they mentioned finding themselves feeling confused as to whether it's best to reach out to someone in the community or to a mental health professional who is usually an outsider working in their community for a short period of time. While they all agreed that it would be easier for them to discuss sensitive issues with members of their community, major concerns about confidentiality and the risk of spreading rumours were raised. These preoccupations led some to then speak of the advantages of talking to someone outside of their community. However, other barriers, notably lack of trust, aversion to face-to-face contact and the language barrier were often mentioned. On this topic, the members of the advisory committees emphasised the difficulty of follow-up in a context of temporary workers, pointing out that while the first contact may sometimes be facilitated by the temporary presence of a mental health professional, the support is limited because temporary workers do not stay long enough to ensure necessary follow-up.

“You can't really decide whether to talk to your friends or to a stranger. It gets really confusing and at the end you're like I'll handle this, no one cares. Maybe that's why the suicide rates are so high, because they're confused about who to talk to, because no matter what you say you're always going to end up being judge, you just got to go with the flow even though you got no one to talk to. You either get used to it or you just give up”.

Experiences with mental health services

All of the young participants interviewed mentioned experiencing significant challenges on a daily basis, and going through emotionally difficult times. However, only five of them had ever interacted with people working at mental health services in their community. Four of the five had been approached directly by personnel who offered them support during a difficult period. Far from appreciating this outreach, the participants reported feeling pressure to talk that seemed to confirm their negative perceptions of formal mental health resources.

“Social services did ask me to go talk to them because. they think I have been through too much, but I'm always doing fine, so I don't talk to them. I don't want to go and I don't go”.

The only participant who deliberately contacted mental health resources mentioned that she was unable to obtain services when she felt she needed them because of a lack of staff. She reported being asked to wait more than a month, and in the meantime, she found “her way” on her own. She mentioned having eventually been contacted by mental health services. Although she had previously contacted the services, her experience of the service reaching out to her echoes those of her peers.

“Then they [mental health professional] asked me do you have anything to talk about, and I didn't. It was so weird because I really, really needed them before, and then they were forcing me to go there four weeks later and I didn't want to go anymore.”

Describing mental health services as “not helpful” and “boring”, young people who had direct contact with mental health services mentioned that they would not use these services again in the future.

"Talking with our best friend is very helpful, not [talking] with mental health”.

At least, we have each other

Faced with these barriers, many mentioned preferring to rely on those around them for help. Formal mental health services are perceived as only a last resort alternative to use when the social network is no longer sufficient.

“I don't go to the health centre because I have friends I go to and who can help me, we help each other a lot”.

Mutual assistance is seen as crucial in the absence of quality mental health care. They turn to those around them in times of difficulty, and they also often find themselves in a position to support others who are experiencing difficult times.

“Some of the younger come up to me or message me whenever they’re feeling down. They don’t like being helped by those people, like nurses, or mental health”.

Understanding that others are no longer comfortable calling on formal services, youths accept this role and remind their peers “let's keep helping each other, that's the only strength we can get”.

Suggestions to improve services

When asked for their suggestions for improving current services, there was no consensus on the topic. Differences in opinions were illustrated in the previous section in the youths' pros and cons they expressed concerning the types of services and regarding the proximity the service providers within the community. This variety of recommendations seems to indicate young people's inability to imagine an adaptation that meets their needs. However, many discussed the importance of improving relations with service providers on a human level, emphasising the link between improved relationships with service providers and the likelihood of increased use of services. Participants expressed the wish for mental health professionals to be more respectful, open to the culture and the Inuit way of life, to adopt a non-judgmental attitude and to be more sensitive to the pressure that can quickly be felt when they come into contact with young people.

“For the helpers, they need to be approachable, but not approaching, basically.”

Discussion

The interviews reveal a general discomfort with the available services. Youths generally felt uneasy about going to the health centre and the way services are provided there. They lack information about which services are available, when one should use the services and what to expect, and they feel that culturally appropriate services are not available online and by chat and text. Overall, there is a general feeling of mistrust that appears to prevent young people from using formal mental health resources.

The government's efforts to recruit and maintain employees does not yet seem to have had the effect on improving trust in available services. An audit found that outside health care professionals are motivated to come to the territory to have a new experience, to broaden the scope of their practice and to discover the land and the culture of Nunavut [18]. The report also mentioned that some service providers choose to work in Nunavut to make a “quick buck” to pay their mortgage, pay off student debt or stabilise themselves financially. The high turnover rate of workers has been also been explained by the difficulty of the work environment, remoteness, culture shock, the high cost of living, and limited job opportunities for spouses [18]. Although research on the motivations of employees is scarce, providing additional financial incentives does not address the “tourist” appeal of the job, and could even entice more financially motivated professionals, confirming beliefs about the monetary motivations of service providers.

According to participants, improving the relationship between healthcare providers and the community would increase the likelihood of young people using the services. This understanding of the situation refers directly to relational epistemology that characterises the Inuit worldview [16]. The ecocentric perspective on which relational epistemology is based, understands the individual as part of an ecosystem consisting of a network of relationships in constant interaction [32]. The Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) are the foundation of relationships. They specify the type of relationship to be developed and how to maintain it. For example, the principle of Piliriqatigiinniq encourages collaboration, while that of Tunnganarniq commands kindness and Inuuqatgiitsiarniq, respect and goodness [16]. The difficulties experienced are thus understood as resulting from a disruption in the harmony of the ecosystem [33,34]. From this perspective, services and service providers must integrate with the community ecosystem and follow the IQ principles to maintain harmony. Local understanding of issues such as suicide, and the services offered to address them, requires taking into account this relational worldview to ensure the cultural safety of the services provided. According to a growing number of researchers, the disciplines of the health and social sciences, which frames the development of current services and the training of fly-in fly-out healthcare providers working in Nunavut, fails to offer a useful paradigm that can serve the interests of local Inuit population [6,35,36].

Recent publications in the health and social services sector in Nunavut, suggest that providing adequate orientation, ongoing training, interpreters and on-site support could have the potential to reduce turnover rates, improve the cultural safety of services and their provision and consequently improve relations with the community [18,19]. No information was found regarding whether these recommendations have been applied in recent years. However, since 2017, the HealthNU mobile app designed in collaboration with community members, health care professionals, and Pinnguaq, has been available on Apple and Androïd devices, and is being promoted for use throughout Nunavut. This online resource, developed specifically for new and short-term health care providers, has information on Inuit communication patterns and health care expectations; Inuit history, settlement and societal values, the health care model, maps and community information and includes a bibliography and list of Inuktitut expressions, as well as referral pathways for tertiary care and mental health. In 2019, the application was the subject of an initial evaluation (n = 18) with promising results [37]. However, it would be relevant to carry out a larger-scale evaluation to determine the usage rate in communities and its impact on the employee and client experiences.

An overview of initiatives implemented in Nunavik found that that for nearly 10 years, the Nunavik Regional Board of Health and Social Services has been offering a comprehensive 3-day pre-assignment training programme for all employees in the health and social services network. During this training, new employees receive information about the process of adaptation and communication in an intercultural context, Inuit culture, and the health and social services network. The aim of the training is to improve cultural safety of services and employee retention. Although the impact of this training programme has not yet been evaluated, comments received indicate that new employees are very appreciative of the training and feel better equipped to start their new jobs (Nunavik Regional Board of Health and Social Services, personal communication, 2024). Initiatives such as the HealthNU application in Nunavut and the three-day pre-departure training developed in Nunavik are promising. It would be helpful to set up an exchange space to share ideas developed and implemented in these different Inuit contexts and other Canadian Inuit lands, while respecting the particularities of each region, to draw inspiration, rather than constantly reinventing the wheel.

Training and hiring Inuit and Northern nurses is often indicated as one of the best strategies for strengthening community health services in Nunavut [19,38]. However, despite the many recommendations to promote employment and reduce barriers to hiring, the percentage of Inuit nurses remains very low. This has been explained by difficulties in accessing the education needed to obtain jobs and meet difficult job qualifications [38]. Access to post-secondary education in communities remains limited despite the creation of social work training programmes by Nunavut Arctic College.

Another important issue is the professionalisation of mental health services. In Western cultures, the predominant medical view is that suicide expresses underlying psychological problems that is have to be addressed by mental health professionals within formal mental health service delivery systems [6,36]. Drawing on many years' of research and clinical work with American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN) populations, Wexler et Gone [36] concluded that suicide prevention is best undertaken by someone who has relationships with the key people, but is not directly involved in their interpersonal issues. Since mental health professionals are often temporary, community members friends, and family must often take on this role [36]. This observation is congruent with the findings reported in this article: young people are more likely to seek help from their peers than from mental health professionals. In this context, Gatekeepers programmes that aim to train and support community members to identify individuals at risk of suicide, refer them to appropriate resources and encourage them to obtain the help they need seem particularly promising [39,40]. Since 2009, Uqaqatigiilluk! (Talk About It!), the Nunavut version of ASIST suicide prevention training, is easily accessible and widely offered by different organisations. With material fully translated into Inuktitut, this version of the programme addresses concerns of cultural appropriateness. According to information available on the Government of Canada website dating from 2015, The Nunavut version of ASIST has been evaluated at the local level and is assessed on a workshop-by-workshop basis by both participants and trainer. However, no published research nor evaluation of the programme in Nunavut has been identified.

The limited published research on Gatekeepers training in indigenous contexts around the world has shown mixed results [41]. In Canada, the two studies identified report contradictory data. Cwik [42] has argued that data generally support that gatekeeper (or “caretaker”) training can add important knowledge and skills to a comprehensive suicide prevention programme for First Nations communities. However, Sareen and colleagues [43] found that there was no improvements in the capacity to intervene with suicidal behaviour and even identified a trend toward an increase in suicidal ideation among those who participated in the ASIST training. In light of these results, more research should be conducted before integrating gatekeeper programmes into suicide prevention strategies in Inuit contexts [42,43]. While the foundation of the programme, which encourages peer support, seems to be consistent with young people's preferences, it is important to verify its impact by conducting a formal, independent scientific evaluation of the Uqaqatigiilluk! programme. However, their may be an issue with the primary goal of gatekeeper training, which is to teach people to identify individuals at risk and refer and encourage them to use appropriate resources, including professional mental health services. Our study suggests that the multiple barriers to using mental health resources identified in these programmes are likely to interfere with the referral process of those in need, and the use of the gatekeepers themselves. The impact of the Uqaqatigiilluk! Programme may be limited by negative attitudes to professional mental health services and the lack of congruence between the nature of the services available and the needs of Inuit community members.

Conclusions

This study identified significant barriers that limit young people's use of mental health and suicide prevention services in Nunavut. In a context where the ITK (2016) recommends improving a continuum of care to prevent suicides, and the Government of Nunavut is planning to invest heavily in this area, before establishing new programmes, it is essential to review the nature of services currently in place in order to determine what adaptations need to be made to increase their use by young people. Many of the employee training and support initiatives in place seem promising, as do some initiatives involving community members in wellness support and suicide prevention. However, the results from the study reported in this article indicate a pressing need to evaluate these strategies. Recruitment and retention initiatives currently in place need to be complemented by more in-person training, including specific training in suicide prevention. Furthermore, far more Inuit community members need to be trained to work in mental health, and the potential of peer training for suicide prevention needs to be evaluated to determine if it can significantly contribute to suicide prevention in the region.
