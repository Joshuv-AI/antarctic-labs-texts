Bridging the gap between evidence and clinical practice for migraine headaches: a systematic review (2019–2024)
Authors: Eva Chen, Madeline Rowe, Adam Khan, Taylor Gardner, Chance Bratten, Alec Young, Eli Paul, Danya Nees, Alicia Ito Ford, Matt Vassar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine headache (MH) is a prevalent and complex neurological disorder affecting over one billion individuals worldwide, making it one of the leading causes of years lived with disability. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) support evidence-based neurology, but whether recent trials provide practice-ready information remains unclear. Background

Migraine headache (MH) is a highly prevalent neurological disorder that affects over one billion individuals worldwide and remains one of the top leading contenders of years lived with disability. Despite therapeutic advances over the years, patients and clinicians still face a persistent disconnect between available randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence and the realities of clinical decision-making. Many MH RCTs focus on short-term, surrogate endpoints rather than long-term, patient-centered outcomes that reflect functional improvement and quality of life. Existing studies also frequently exclude clinically relevant populations and underreport trial features needed for reproducibility and real-world applicability, such as economic evaluations, protocol transparency, and raw data availability.

To characterize and quantify this gap, we systematically reviewed RCTs of acute and preventive MH interventions published from 2019 to 2024, identified through a structured Embase and MEDLINE search adapted from Cochrane strategies. Our study applied the migraine-adapted, 13-item usefulness criteria developed by van ’t Hooft et al., evaluating each trial for methodology, patient-centeredness, pragmatism, and transparency. The primary aim was to map where recent MH RCTs succeed and where they fall short in features critical to clinical utility and open science compliance.

This analysis addresses an urgent issue for the MH field: aligning trial design and reporting with the priorities of patients, clinicians, and policymakers, especially in the context of evolving international clinical trial registries and U.S. open science mandates. By identifying concrete areas for improvement, such as broader patient inclusion, economic evaluations, and data sharing, our systematic review seeks to inform future trial design and support more effective, patient-centered, and policy-relevant MH research.

Introduction

Migraine headache (MH) is a highly prevalent and complex neurological disorder affecting over one billion individuals worldwide, making it one of the leading causes of years lived with disability, particularly among adults aged 20–59 years [1–3]. Additionally, there has been a notable increase in incidence in adolescents over the past 30 years as well as a more rapid increase in cases in males overall [1]. Over the past two decades, a growing range of pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic therapies (e.g., triptans, gepants, ditans, calcitonin gene-related protein (CGRP) monoclonal antibodies, neuromodulation devices, and behavioral interventions) have expanded treatment options [4–6]. However, despite this therapeutic progress, clinicians and patients often lack high-quality, patient-centered data to guide personalized care decisions [7, 8].

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in MH research have traditionally prioritized short-term, surrogate endpoints, such as pain freedom at 2 h, headache-day reduction over 4–12 weeks, or biomarker changes, rather than long-term functional outcomes that reflect the real-world burden of disease [9, 10]. Such long-term outcomes include sustained reductions in disability, improved work productivity, and patient-reported measures of quality of life [11]. While validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) like the Migraine Disability Assessment (MIDAS), Headache Impact Test (HIT-6), and Migraine-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (MSQ) exist, they are often relegated to secondary or exploratory analyses, limiting their influence on clinical practice [12–15].

Common limitations in trial design and reporting compound this gap in evidence. MH is a heterogeneous condition, with variations in phenotypes (e.g., episodic vs. chronic MH, presence or absence of aura) and comorbidities (e.g., psychiatric, cardiovascular, and atopic conditions) that significantly influence treatment response [6, 16, 17]. Yet many RCTs use narrowly defined or unstratified populations, limiting the generalizability of their findings [18, 19]. Moreover, trials frequently exclude key subpopulations, such as older adults or individuals with psychiatric comorbidities, despite their prevalence in clinical settings [20, 21]. Previous studies concluded that the methodological and reporting qualities of MH RCTs remain suboptimal [22, 23]. Moreover, trials of behavioral interventions often lack detailed descriptions of protocols, including session frequency, therapist training, or fidelity monitoring, further complicating replication and clinical adoption [24, 25].

As a result, clinical decision-making is often guided less by robust evidence and more by physician experience, regional prescribing patterns, and patient trial-and-error. To address this disconnect, criteria that systematically assess the clinical utility of RCTs, such as the usefulness criteria developed by van ’t Hooft et al., can help identify studies that offer real-world relevance [26]. This usefulness framework for appraisal of RCTs is based on 8 criteria: problem base, context placement, information gain, pragmatism, patient centeredness, value for money, feasibility, and transparency [27]. Transparency is further broken down into preregistration, protocol adherence, funding, conflict of interest, and availability of raw data. This new framework was originally developed based upon existing literature on usefulness in clinical research and has been applied to RCTs addressing preterm birth prevention, obesity and metabolic syndrome, and shoulder surgery [26–29].

Following van ’t Hooft et al.’s original commentary, we have applied the developed set of usefulness criteria to assess the clinical utility and transparency of RCTs evaluating acute and preventive MH interventions published from 2019 to 2024 [27]. Our objectives are to identify potential common shortcomings in design and reporting (particularly the underrepresentation of patient-reported and long-term functional outcomes); highlight trial features that enhance value to practicing clinicians; and offer recommendations that improve the alignment between MH research and patient-centered care.

Methods

Reproducibility and study design

This study analyzed the usefulness of published RCTs on MH interventions. Studies published (electronically or in print) from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2024, were evaluated to provide a sufficient sample size and capture the latest advancements in the field. Before conducting this study, the search strategies, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and data extraction form were pilot-tested on a sample of five articles to refine the materials, as needed. To ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting, the study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [30]. To enhance transparency and reproducibility, all raw data, analysis scripts, extraction forms, and related materials were made publicly accessible via Open Science Framework (OSF) upon study conclusion [31, 32]. Additionally, our study protocol was made publicly available on OSF before the onset of the study [31, 32].

Search strategy

On May 14, 2025, a systematic search was conducted using Embase via Elsevier with simultaneous access to MEDLINE. The search targeted RCTs focused on MH interventions. To ensure relevance and recent evidence, filters were applied to limit studies by publication date, language, and study design (RCT). Published RCT search filters were adapted from Cochrane strategies, and the complete strategy is available on OSF [31–33]. No additional sources such as trial registries, citation tracking, gray literature, or author contacts were used. All search results were imported into Rayyan, and duplicates were excluded using the platform’s deduplication tool and confirmed manually by two independent reviewers (E.C., M.R.).[34]

Training

All team members received standardized training to ensure familiarity with the study’s objectives, eligibility criteria, methodology, and data variables. Training was led by faculty investigators (M.V., A.F.), who provided live instruction and written guidance on screening and data extraction procedures. Multiple sessions were conducted by reviewers (E.C., M.R.) for title/abstract screening, full-text screening, and use of the van ’t Hooft criteria for determining trial usefulness. Rayyan was used for screening, and a pilot-tested Google Form was used to collect data. A calibration exercise was completed on five sample studies to ensure consistency in interpretation and approach. Discrepancies during the pilot phase were resolved through group discussion and consensus, and a written data dictionary was used to underpin uniform decision-making during full data extraction.

Eligibility Criteria/Screening

To be included, articles had to be RCTs focused on MH interventions. The inclusion criteria required that RCTs be published in full-text form, in English, and involve human participants receiving an intervention for MH (e.g., pharmacological, behavioral, neuromodulation-based). Articles were excluded if they did not focus primarily on interventions for MH, were not randomized, included pediatric and adolescent populations, were focused solely on drug pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, involved single-group assignment, had medication dosage as the main outcome, were cost-focused, or were solely safety-focused. Furthermore, cohort studies, case series, cross-sectional analyses, case-control studies, pilot studies, longitudinal studies, review articles, commentaries, editorials, letters, errata/corrigenda, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, and unrelated studies were excluded. For trials with multiple publications, we included the earliest report that provided the most comprehensive account of the primary results. Two researchers (E.C., M.R.) independently screened titles and abstracts against these criteria. Disagreements were discussed with a third researcher (A.K.) if needed.

Data extraction

During full-text data extraction, all items–with one exception–were assessed blindly and in a duplicate manner using a standardized, pilot-tested Google form by the two primary data extractors (E.C., M.R.). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with general input from a third reviewer (A.K.) or technical clinical advising (D.N.) available as needed. The extraction method exception was that the “problem base” item was evaluated by a clinical investigator (D.N.) due to the need for clinical and contextual expertise in determining whether these articles addressed conditions with meaningful health impact and broad patient relevance. Extracted variables included: article title, PubMed Identifier (PMID), journal name, journal impact factor, journal specialty, publication date, trial registry number, continent(s) where the trial was conducted, type of intervention, randomized sample size, funding source, and trial phase. Usefulness evaluations were based on the 13-item criteria developed by van ’t Hooft et al., with each criterion assigned a value of 0(criterion absent), 1 (partially fulfilled), or 2 (fully fulfilled), resulting in a maximum possible total of 26[26]. Usefulness criteria are defined in Table 1 and the full operationalized scoring criteria are listed in Supplemental Table 1. To promote consistency and resolve ambiguities, the extraction process was piloted on a sample of five RCTs.

Usefulness criteria item descriptions

Clinical utility

Was defined as the degree to which RCTs provide information that is relevant, generalizable, and actionable in real-world clinical settings. Trials with high clinical utility reflect patient priorities and populations, align with existing evidence, and evaluate outcomes that influence diagnosis, treatment, or patient well-being. Transparency was defined as the openness, completeness, and reproducibility of RCTs. Trials that are transparent permit independent verification, reduce the risk of selective reporting, and contribute to the broader research field by allowing replication, meta-analysis, and critical appraisal. These two concepts together demonstrate overall usefulness.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) in RStudio (version 2023.09.1 + 494). Frequencies and percentages were calculated to determine trial characteristics. Simple linear regression models were used to evaluate the association between publication year and (1) the total usefulness score, as well as (2) the values for the subsets of clinical utility and transparency. A Pearson correlation coefficient (with significance set at p < 0.05) was used to observe the relationship between transparency and clinical utility values. Finally, a multivariable linear regression model was used to identify predictors of clinical utility, transparency, and overall usefulness. Independent variables included in this analysis were sample size, continent(s) of trial conduct, funding type, journal type, and journal impact factor.

Ethical oversight

Our research protocol was submitted to the Oklahoma State University Center for Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB # 2025057) for review and was determined to not meet the regulatory definition of human subjects research in accordance with the Code of Federal Regulations [45 CFR 46.102(d) and (f)] [35].

Protocol deviations

The study had two deviations from the preregistered protocol. First, the planned risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool with ChatGPT support was omitted because ChatGPT was not specifically designed for this task and the alternative, RobotReviewer, is no longer operational. Second, the “problem base” item was determined by a clinical investigator (D.N.) instead of a duplicate fashion due to the need for clinical and contextual expertise. All other procedures followed the original protocol and are documented in the OSF registry [31, 32].

Results

Identification and inclusion of eligible studies

The initial database search identified 653 records. Removal of a single duplicate left 652 unique records. Title and abstract-level screening via Rayaan excluded 399, leaving 253 articles for full-text review. Of these, 84 did not meet the eligibility criteria. Accordingly, 169 RCTs met all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final quantitative analysis. Reasons for exclusion at each stage are detailed in Fig. 1.

Trial demographics and publication characteristics

The median impact factor of the journals in which these articles were published was 3.0 (IQR, 2.0–5.0). The median sample size was 90 participants per RCT (IQR, 56.0-151.0). General medicine journals published the largest share of articles (88/169; 52.1%). In contrast, pain and anesthesiology journals published the fewest (7/169; 4.1%). Geographically, trials were concentrated in Asia (95/169; 56.2%) and North America (30/169; 17.8%), whereas only a single study was undertaken in Africa (0.6%). Funding was most often reported as private/industry (36/169; 21.3%) or self-funded/no external support (35/169; 20.7%); government funding was the least common source (17/169; 10.1%). During the study period, from 2019 to 2024, the highest number of publications appeared in 2021 (34/169; 20.1%) with the least in 2024 (15/169; 8.9%). Evaluation of trial clinical utility and transparency

We assessed seven clinical utility and six transparency domains across 169 articles. Patient-centeredness was nearly universal (167/169; 98.8%), whereas pragmatism (5/169; 3.0%) and value-for-money (1/169; 0.6%) were rarely addressed. Transparency varied: 73 articles (73/169; 43.2%) were prospectively registered, 141 (141/169; 83.4%) disclosed funding sources, and 159 (159/169; 94.1%) provided conflict-of-interest statements. In contrast, 7 articles (7/169; 4.1%) made their protocols publicly available, 13 (13/169; 7.7%) documented adherence to the protocol, and 3 (3/169; 1.8%) provided open raw data. Discussion

Our systematic review of 169 MH RCTs reveals a field marked by slow yet measurable progress: while over half surpassed the midpoint on the usefulness scale, fewer than 2% met the threshold for high utility. This finding underscores a persistent disconnect between methodological rigor and real-world applicability. Notably, patient-centeredness and context placement were commonly satisfied, suggesting increasing attention to outcomes that matter to patients and integration with prior syntheses. However, other domains, particularly transparency and economic evaluation, remain significantly underdeveloped.

One area where MH RCTs are clearly excelling is in the consistent inclusion of patient-centered outcomes. Nearly 99% of the trials that we assessed reported using at least one PROM, underscoring the field’s recognition that MH is not just a biomedical condition but a deeply disruptive lived experience. Tools like the MIDAS, HIT-6, and MSQ have become common components of trial design, reflecting a shift toward valuing how patients feel and function, not just whether their pain scores decline [13–15]. This emphasis is especially important in MH, where symptom severity, treatment tolerability, and functional impairment vary widely across individuals. PROMs allow trials to capture outcomes that matter to patients–such as reduction in missed workdays, improved ability to care for children, or restored participation in social life–which may not always align with traditional endpoints like two-hour pain relief [9, 10, 12]. This patient-centered orientation represents a significant methodological strength of the MH RCT landscape and provides a solid foundation for future efforts to improve trial pragmatism and transparency. However, further subanalyses should be conducted to truly assess the extent of patient centeredness in RCTs, given the various methods of recording PROMs as well as other outcomes.

In the studies that we reviewed, there were over 200 different overall outcome measures used. While there will always be some variability across studies due to unique study foci, inconsistency in how even the most common outcomes are measured limits the opportunity for wider data synthesis and generalized conclusions. For example, how is overall improvement in a migraine prevention trial quantified: reduction in migraine days? (and over what period of time?), reduction of intensity? (quantified how?), or some measure of improvement in functioning? As the field continues to evolve, initiatives like the Core Outcome Set for Migraine (COSMIG)–which recommends a standardized set of outcomes for preventive intervention trials–offer a valuable opportunity to further harmonize outcome reporting and ensure comparability across studies [37]. While our review did not assess adherence to COSMIG (which was published in November 2021–approximately mid-way through our study review period), its increasing use could help align trial outcomes with both patient priorities and evidence synthesis needs.

In contrast, one domain almost entirely overlooked in the RCTs that we reviewed was economic evaluation–an omission with serious implications given the costly and chronic nature of MH care [38]. Despite the rising use of expensive therapeutic classes such as CGRP monoclonal antibodies, gepants, and neuromodulation devices, fewer than 1% of studies conducted any form of economic analysis, including cost-effectiveness, budget impact, or value-of-information modeling. This absence limits the ability of healthcare systems, particularly those operating under constrained budgets, to make informed decisions about which interventions offer the best value for money [39, 40]. For example, CGRP inhibitors can cost up to $600/month out-of-pocket, adding substantial financial strain for individuals already burdened by MH[1, 41]. Moreover, the indirect costs of MH, including caregiver burden and absenteeism, remain substantial and unaddressed in most trial protocols [42]. Woldeamanuel et al. estimated that MH’s global economic burden was 1.9% of the 2025 gross domestic product with indirect costs ($7108 USD) dominating direct costs ($2788 USD), summing up to $9896 USD per person per year [43]. Considering the rising incidence of MH in adolescents, this burden will likely continue to increase over time [1]. Incorporating economic endpoints and modeling strategies into future MH RCTs would improve not only their policy relevance but also their clinical translatability. These models could help prioritize trials for funding, assess feasibility of implementation in public health systems, and guide formulary inclusion decisions [44]. Sacco et al. emphasized the need for funding of pragmatic MH trials to close the gap between trial design and clinical application [45]. Ultimately, value-based design and reporting would ensure that innovations in MH treatment are not only clinically effective but also financially accessible and equitably distributed.

Despite successive U.S. open-science directives–most notably the 2013 Office of Science and Technology Policy memorandum, the 2018 National Academies report Open Science by Design, and the 2023 “Year of Open Science” initiative–policy alone does not appear to have significantly shifted real-world transparency practices in MH research [46–48]. These landmark initiatives laid a strong conceptual foundation by calling for publicly available protocols, prospective trial registration, and data sharing, particularly for federally funded studies. Similarly, the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) and the Clinical Trial Registry of India mandate registration of clinical trials to promote transparency [49, 50]. Yet in our review of MH RCTs published from 2019 to 2024, fewer than half were prospectively registered, and just 4.1% included a publicly accessible protocol. The gap between expectation and implementation is particularly striking given that MH is a high-burden, high-prevalence neurological condition with extensive National Institutes of Health investments and widespread pharmaceutical interest [51]. In theory, this makes MH trials prime candidates for alignment with open science mandates. The May 2025 Executive Order on “Restoring Gold-Standard Science,” which expands requirements for data transparency and research integrity across federal agencies, may help drive more meaningful change, but its effects could not be captured in this review, as our literature search ended in December 2024 [52]. Additionally, although ChiCTR has made progress in documenting elements such as intervention type and registration timing, it continues to face challenges in data management and in evaluating the quality and completeness of registered content [53]. Going forward, proactive steps by journals, funders, and investigators will be crucial. These include not only enforcing transparency requirements at the point of submission and review, but also investing in infrastructure, such as streamlined repositories, data dictionaries, and support for FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles [54–56]. In MH research specifically, enhancing transparency would improve trial reproducibility, enable more reliable meta-analyses, and empower patients, clinicians, and policymakers to make better-informed decisions [57]. Without implementation mechanisms that match the strength of policy vision, the promise of open science will remain largely unrealized.

To strengthen the transparency and clinical utility of future MH RCTs, we recommend a coordinated effort from both trial registries and journals. Registries should require structured outcome definitions, including timing, measurement tools, and analytic metrics, to address the variability that complicates outcome reporting in MH research. Uploading full protocols and statistical analysis plans at the time of registration, along with maintaining protocol adherence logs, would guard against selective outcome switching, a known issue in trials of MH therapies [58, 59]. Registries should also link trial entries to corresponding publications and shared datasets to facilitate evidence synthesis, particularly important in MH where effect sizes are often modest and patient subgroups diverse [60]. Journals can reinforce these efforts by adapting standardized transparency checklists during peer review, requiring prospective registration as a condition for publication, and mandating that authors disclose protocol deviations and upload their trial protocols as supplementary files [61]. For trials investigating high-cost MH interventions, such as CGRP inhibitors or neuromodulation devices, journals and funders should also encourage inclusion of economic analyses and value-based endpoints to ensure findings are both clinically meaningful and policy-relevant [62]. Together, these measures can help realize the open science vision that recent U.S. federal initiatives have set forth, translating it into tangible improvements in the credibility, reproducibility, and real-world impact of MH research.

Clinical implications

The methodological shortcomings that we identified, such as poor raw data availability, limited pragmatism, and the rarity of economic evaluations, can have consequences for clinical care. When raw data or protocols are inaccessible, clinicians cannot fully assess the reliability or applicability of trial results, hindering evidence-based treatment decisions. Similarly, trials that lack pragmatic features do not reflect real-world clinical settings, reducing their usefulness to both clinicians and patients. Furthermore, the omission of economic evaluations limits the ability to assess cost-effectiveness, which is a critical consideration for healthcare systems and patients. Without insights into the value of an intervention relative to its cost, healthcare providers may struggle to recommend treatments that are both clinically beneficial and economically sustainable. Overall, these shortcomings may pose serious ramifications that hinder evidence-based patient care.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. We adhered to a preregistered protocol, performed duplicate blinded screening and data extraction, and have made all analytic code and data openly available. By applying a validated usefulness checklist to a comprehensive five-year body of literature, we provide the first field-wide benchmark of methodological quality in MH research. Our sample of 169 RCTs, spanning diverse specialties and regions, enabled detailed analyses of design features, funding transparency, and geographic patterns.

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation of clinical utility involved a degree of subjectivity that could not be entirely eliminated. Also, though supervised by senior researchers and informed by general neurology (D.N.), the data extraction conducted by medical student researchers (E.C., M.R.) was not independently verified by a headache specialist. Second, our analysis was restricted to English-language publications, and the majority of MH RCTs were conducted in Asia (56.2%), which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other global contexts. Third, a formal risk of bias assessment was not conducted; future studies are encouraged to incorporate this component to enhance methodological rigor. Additionally, this study represents the first application of the van ’t Hooft framework within the MH research landscape. While our systematic review provides an important starting point for evaluating clinical utility and transparency of MH RCTs, there is a need for further validation and methodological refinement of these criteria specifically within MH research. Finally, reliance on published reports may have led to overestimating transparency and clinical utility, as undisclosed protocol deviations or unpublished negative studies could not be accounted for.

Conclusion

In summary, our systematic review shows that MH RCTs published from 2019 to 2024 increasingly capture patient-focused measures yet still fall short on data openness and pragmatic design. Bridging these shortcomings is crucial if novel interventions are to yield tangible benefits for the more than one billion individuals affected by MH worldwide. By providing the first quantitative benchmark of trial clinical utility in this field, we meet our primary aim–identifying where current evidence is robust and where it needs to evolve. Implementing the improvement priorities highlighted here–economic evaluations, prioritization of patient-outcomes, and proactive data sharing–will foster more trustworthy science and, ultimately, more effective and equitable MH management.
