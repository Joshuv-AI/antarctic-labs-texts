When minutes matter: A practical reporting framework for avoidable ischemic time in donation after circulatory death heart transplantation
Authors: Brian Lima, Aaron M. Williams
Published: 2026

Donation after circulatory death (DCD) heart transplantation has moved beyond proof of concept. A randomized trial, growing national registry experience, and an international multicenter series now support its feasibility using both direct procurement and perfusion—involving rapid cardiac recovery followed by ex situ perfusion—and thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion (TA-NRP), involving in situ reperfusion of the thoracic and abdominal organs after death determination and the required observation period.1, 2, 3 Yet as adoption widens, the steps that vary most across—and even within—jurisdictions are precisely those that determine ischemic exposure: where withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) occurs, how cessation of circulation is monitored, when death is declared, and how the no-touch interval—the mandatory observation period after circulatory arrest—is documented. Identical legal rules across countries are neither realistic nor necessary. Identical biologic injury, however, is unavoidable—the donor heart experiences ischemia in minutes, not in jurisdictions.

Our group’s recent single-center analyses should be read in this light: not as definitive global thresholds, but as 1 illustration of why interval-resolved reporting is needed. In TA-NRP-recovered DCD hearts, asystolic warm ischemic time, defined as the interval from asystole to reperfusion beyond 10 min and pulseless electrical activity warm ischemic time (PWIT), defined as the interval from the onset of non-perfusing pulseless electrical activity (PEA) to reperfusion,) beyond 12 min were each associated with severe primary graft dysfunction and early mortality.4, 5 These findings require external validation, but they expose a problem of broad relevance: when clinically meaningful signals appear across intervals of only 10-12 min, even modest logistical delay can consume a disproportionate share of the biologic margin. Because effective reperfusion itself requires several minutes after incision, time lost to transport, repositioning, preparation, or documentation is not administrative trivia—it is ischemic exposure.

That exposure should first be confronted domestically before it is generalized abroad. Within the United States alone, organ procurement organizations differ substantially in DCD heart experience and protocol, and regional analyses have long documented variation in where WLST occurs and how death is determined.6, 7, 8 The variation is not merely procedural: in a high-volume US organ procurement organization, successful DCD donation was more likely when WLST occurred in the operating room than in the intensive care unit.9 We do not conclude that WLST must occur in the operating room everywhere—only that every DCD heart recovery should document the elements that make avoidable delay measurable: WLST location; whether the donor was positioned and prepared before death declaration; transport or transfer time; incision time; and time to effective reperfusion. Such reporting lets centers under different policies identify post-declaration latency without imposing a single mandated workflow.

A second problem, equally domestic in origin, is terminology and data infrastructure. Neither the United Network for Organ Sharing nor the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation registry currently captures the granular DCD variables—PWIT, asystolic warm ischemic time, WLST location, no-touch duration, and recovery strategy—needed to separate donor physiology from process-related ischemia. This limitation is important and often unstated: it constrains both intranational and international comparison and should temper criticism of any single registry or country for failing to report what its data systems were never built to collect. The remedy is shared, standardized definitions rather than blame. Consistent with American Society of Transplant Surgeons recommendations and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation scientific statement—both of which recognize PEA as circulatory arrest once effective perfusion has ceased10, 11—we propose a practical minimum dataset comprising: the time from WLST to each prespecified physiologic threshold; the study-specific definition of functional warm ischemic time, including the oxygen saturation and/or blood pressure threshold used to define its onset; the onset of non-perfusing circulation; asystole where locally required; the start and end of the observation period; death declaration; incision; reperfusion method; and early graft-support endpoints, including severe primary graft dysfunction and post-transplant extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

Recording the onset of non-perfusing circulation is not the same as declaring legal death, and we do not propose changing any jurisdiction’s legal death standard. We propose only that, where measurable—ideally from continuous invasive arterial pressure using a conservative, locally specified threshold—this transition be captured as a physiologic timestamp, so that PWIT can be measured and studied even where death is legally declared only after asystole. Such a timestamp is not a new death standard; it is a quality metric.

International relevance does not imply uniform implementation. Any shared framework must remain adaptable to regional legal, cultural, and operational realities. Controlled DCD heart transplantation and TA-NRP are neither available, legally sanctioned, nor culturally accepted in many parts of the world, and programs across the Asia-Pacific region in particular face substantial legal, operational, financial, and public trust barriers.12 Nothing proposed here should be read as a recommendation to adopt DCD heart transplantation or NRP where they are not already established. The framework is intended only for jurisdictions already performing or actively contemplating controlled DCD heart recovery and as a shared vocabulary for eventual comparison across very different systems.

That same adaptability should govern how we interpret programs built on longer, legally mandated observation periods. European experience—including the Italian national series—has shown that DCD heart transplantation can succeed despite prolonged no-touch intervals, and these results deserve respect rather than dismissal.13 They also illustrate why outcomes beyond survival must be reported alongside it: the same Italian series that established feasibility transparently reported that 21% of recipients required post-transplant extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.13 That transparency is a model, not a fault—an illustration of what beyond-survival reporting reveals: recovery after prolonged ischemia may carry a different physiologic and resource cost, and naming it keeps comparisons fair rather than rhetorical.

None of this comes at the expense of end-of-life care or public trust. Prospective, waveform-based observation after WLST has shown that spontaneous resumption of cardiac activity, termed autoresuscitation, when it occurs at all, occurs within minutes; and an updated systematic review found autoresuscitation beyond 5 minutes to be exceedingly uncommon.14, 15 Such evidence supports—rather than shortcuts—careful monitoring and a transparent observation period. The goal is not to compress comfort-focused care or relax any safeguard, but to eliminate avoidable, system-generated ischemia once the donor has met local criteria for circulatory death.

The next step for DCD heart transplantation is not to make every jurisdiction look the same. It is to make every case speak the same scientific language. A shared reporting framework for avoidable ischemic time would let centers under different constraints compare like with like, recover the minutes that workflow can give back, and test—across populations far broader than any single program—whether those minutes truly change graft function and survival. If minutes matter biologically, they should matter operationally, and they should be reported accordingly.
