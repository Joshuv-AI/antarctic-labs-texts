TTC26 scaffolds MINDY3-mediated RACK1 deubiquitination to drive osteoclastogenesis and pathological bone resorption
Authors: Yuan Gao, Bowen Lai, Ran Yan, Jinnan He, Tingwei Lu, Shiyuan Yang, Jun Chen, Rui Gao, Heng Jiang, Xuhui Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoclast overactivation is a central driver of bone metabolic disorders such as osteoporosis, and novel drug targets for these diseases continue to be actively explored. Scaffold proteins, which enhance the specificity of deubiquitinase (DUB)-substrate interactions, have emerged as promising therapeutic targets for the sustained management of chronic diseases. Intraflagellar transport (IFT) proteins, recognized for their scaffolding roles in cellular signaling, are increasingly implicated in bone remodeling. However, their therapeutic potential for bone metabolic disorders remains to be fully elucidated.MethodsWe systematically investigated the function of the core IFT protein, tetratricopeptide repeat protein 26 (TTC26), in osteoclastogenesis through clinical correlation analysis and mouse models (Ttc26flox/flox, Lysm-Cre). Furthermore, through an integrated approach combining RNA sequencing, Western blotting, co-immunoprecipitation, in vitro osteoclast differentiation assays, and protein structural analysis, we elucidated the mechanistic role of TTC26 in regulating osteoclast differentiation. Ultimately, by osteoclast-targeted inhibition of Ttc26 (shTtc26) in ovariectomized (OVX) mice, we validated its therapeutic potential as a novel target for osteoporosis intervention.ResultsWe identified TTC26 as a previously unrecognized regulator of osteoclast differentiation. Acting as a scaffold protein, TTC26 recruits the DUB ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase-3 (MINDY3) to promote K48-linked deubiquitination of receptor for activated kinase C-1 (RACK1), thereby stabilizing RACK1 and activating nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1), the master transcription factor of osteoclastogenesis. Furthermore, structural analysis reveals that six critical TTC26 residues (N533, T534, E537, R541, K545, and K548) are essential for this scaffold activity. Notably, osteoclast-targeted inhibition of Ttc26 successfully alleviates trabecular bone loss in ovariectomized (OVX) mice.ConclusionOur findings uncover a novel role for TTC26 in bone homeostasis and elucidate its molecular mechanism in driving osteoclastogenesis by scaffolding MINDY3-mediated deubiquitination of RACK1.Translational potentialThis study holds clear clinical translational prospects: 1) TTC26 may serve as a novel biomarker for osteoporosis diagnosis; 2) Its functional residues provide precise targets for developing small-molecule inhibitors; 3) The scaffold protein regulatory paradigm offers a new model that can be referenced for treating other bone metabolic diseases.Keywords: Deubiquitination, Osteoclastogenesis, Osteoporosis, Scaffold protein, Targeted therapy, TTC26

Introduction

Dysregulated osteoclast activation is a key pathological driver of various bone metabolic disorders, most notably postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) [1]. Recent epidemiological data highlight the global osteoporosis burden now exceeds 200 million cases, posing a substantial healthcare challenge worldwide [2]. Osteoclastogenesis commences when bone marrow monocytes (BMMs) or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) undergo differentiation and multinucleation stimulated by receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) [3]. This process is critically regulated by the canonical RANKL signaling pathway, which triggers intracellular cascades that up-regulate the expression of activating nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1), the master transcription factor of osteoclast differentiation [4]. Conventional osteoporosis therapeutics, such as bisphosphonates, carry significant limitations. Although these agents effectively inhibit bone resorption, their excessive suppression of bone remodeling may induce severe complications, including osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures [5]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets that can moderately and selectively regulate osteoclastogenesis.

Deubiquitinases (DUBs) serve as pivotal regulators of the ubiquitin-proteasome system [6], orchestrating key cellular processes, such as protein homeostasis, cell cycle progression, and immune responses regulation [7]. By removing ubiquitin chains from substrate proteins, DUBs stabilize their targets. Compared to other protein degradation-targeting technologies, modulation of deubiquitination offers a gentler approach, making it ideal for maintaining homeostasis or managing chronic disease. Consequently, DUB-targeted therapies have shown considerable promise in treating cancer, immune disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases [8]. However, most DUBs exhibit broad substrate specificity, limiting target selectivity and compromising therapeutic efficacy [9]. Recent studies highlight the role of scaffold proteins in deubiquitination by organizing catalytic components and enhancing DUB-substrate specificity. A deeper understanding of scaffold-mediated DUB-substrate recognition could unveil novel therapeutic targets for modulating ubiquitin signaling pathways. In bone metabolic disorders such as osteoporosis, effective treatments must precisely regulate osteoclast function while ensuring moderate and sustained effects to prevent excessive bone resorption. Targeting scaffold proteins that modulate DUB activity—particularly those regulating the protective deubiquitination of pro-osteoclastogenic factors—could offer a novel approach to inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. However, no scaffold proteins involved in osteoclast-specific DUB regulation have been identified, presenting a gap in current therapeutic strategies.

The intraflagellar transport (IFT) protein family is distinguished by its core scaffolding functions in cellular signaling processes [10]. Recently, growing evidence indicates broader roles of IFT protein family in non-ciliary cellular processes [11]. In particular, several IFT members have been implicated in bone metabolism, with roles both in osteogenesis [12,13] and osteoclastogenesis [14]. Among IFT family members, tetratricopeptide repeat protein 26 (TTC26) has drawn our particular interest, as our bioinformatic analyses and clinical validation have successfully identified TTC26 as a key molecule associated with PMOP. TTC26 is a well-characterized component of the IFT system and functions as a scaffold protein that orchestrates the assembly and trafficking of protein complexes along the microtubule network of primary cilia [15]. TTC26 dysfunction has been linked to various diseases, including hepatocellular carcinogenesis, cholestatic disorders, and pituitary axis abnormalities [10]. Despite these insights, the specific function of TTC26 in bone homeostasis remains unexplored, and its mechanistic contributions and therapeutic potential in skeletal biology have yet to be elucidated.

Here, we establish for the first time that TTC26 functions as a molecular scaffold to positively modulate osteoclast differentiation and pathological bone resorption. Through comparative and correlation analyses using clinical data from PMOP patients versus matched controls, alongside parallel comparisons in OVX versus sham-operated mouse models, we demonstrate a positive correlation between TTC26 expression and osteoclastogenic capacity. Moreover, conditional knockout of Ttc26 specifically in myeloid lineage reveals that TTC26 deficiency significantly impairs osteoclast differentiation. Mechanistically, TTC26 interacts with the receptor for activated kinase C-1 (RACK1), activating the RACK1-p38/AKT-NFATc1 axis to drive osteoclastogenesis. Furthermore, we reveal that TTC26 acts as a molecular scaffold to recruit ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase-3 (MINDY3), promoting K48-linked deubiquitination of RACK1 and thereby stabilizing its expression. Crucially, the scaffold function of TTC26 in assembling the TTC26-RACK1-MINDY3 ternary complex is mediated by six critical amino acid residues (N533, T534, and E537 for TTC26-RACK1 binding; R541, K545, and K548 for TTC26-MINDY3 binding). Mutation of these residues abolishes TTC26's scaffold activity to disrupt MINDY3 recruitment and RACK1 stabilization, ultimately impairing osteoclastogenesis. Finally, targeted knockdown of Ttc26 (shTtc26) in osteoclasts effectively attenuates osteoporosis in ovariectomized (OVX) mice, demonstrating the potential of TTC26 inhibition in treating bone metabolic diseases associated with excessive osteoclast activation. Therefore, these findings reveal a novel role for TTC26 in bone homeostasis and establish its potential as a therapeutic target for regulating osteoclastogenesis.

Methods and results

Identification of TTC26 as a principal regulator to promote bone resorption

To identify regulatory factors implicated in postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP), we analyzed mRNA expression profiles in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from PMOP patients and matched controls (Con) within the GEO dataset (GSE56815), following the methodology established in our previous publication [16]. The analysis revealed significantly up-regulated TTC26 expression in the PMOP group (Fig. 1a). To further validate the bioinformatic finding, we isolated PBMCs from PMOP and Con groups for subsequent clinical comparative and correlation analyses (Fig. 1b), and established an OVX mouse model (Fig. S1a) followed by isolation of BMMs. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis demonstrated significantly elevated TTC26 mRNA expression in the PMOP group compared with the Con group (Fig. 1c), and similarly increased TTC26 expression in OVX mice compared with sham-operated mice (Fig. S1b). Furthermore, after in vitro osteoclast induction, both PMOP patients and OVX mice exhibited increased osteoclast numbers (Oc.N) by TRAP staining and larger osteoclast areas (Oc.Ar) by F-actin staining compared to their respective controls (Fig. 1d, e, and S1c, d), along with markedly up-regulated expression of osteoclast-specific genes (CTSK, TRAP, and NFATc1) (Fig. 1f–S1e). These findings collectively indicate excessive activation of bone resorption activity in both PMOP patients and OVX mice. Notably, Pearson correlation analysis showed significant negative correlations between TTC26 mRNA expression levels and femoral bone mineral density (Fig. 1g), while revealing positive correlations with osteoclast-specific gene expression (Fig. 1h–j). Together, these results uncover a strong association between TTC26 expression and bone resorption activity.

TTC26 deficiency impairs osteoclastogenesis, thus suppressing bone resorption

To elucidate the role of TTC26 in bone resorption, we generated myeloid-specific Ttc26 knockout (Ttc26cKO) mice by crossing LysM-Cre (LysM) mice with Ttc26flox/flox mice (Fig. S2). Micro-CT analysis demonstrated a significant increase in trabecular bone mass in Ttc26cKO mice compared to LysM, with elevated trabecular bone volume (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular number (Tb.N) and thickness (Tb.Th), along with reduced trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) (Fig. 2a). Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining confirmed an elevated trabecular area (Tb.Ar) in Ttc26cKO mice (Fig. 2b). Consistent with these findings, Ttc26 knockout resulted in a marked reduction in both Oc.N (Fig. 2c) and osteoclast activity (Fig. 2d), suggesting that TTC26 deficiency significantly suppresses bone resorption. In vitro differentiation assays using BMMs isolated from Ttc26cKO mice demonstrated compromised osteoclast differentiation, as evidenced by fewer TRAP positive multinucleated osteoclasts (Fig. 2e), defective F-actin ring formation ability (Fig. 2f), decreased resorption pit area (Fig. 2g), and significant down-regulation of osteoclastogenic genes (Fig. 2h). Importantly, myeloid-specific Ttc26 ablation has no effect on either macrophage function or osteoblast function. This conclusion is supported by the absence of differences in inflammatory cytokine expression or polarization capacity of BMMs between the LysM and Ttc26cKO mice (Fig. S3), as well as the lack of differences in body length, von Kossa (VK), osteocalcin (OCN), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining, and alizarin red S (ARS) mineralization between the two groups (Fig. S4). Overall, these results demonstrate that TTC26 deficiency specifically impairs osteoclast-mediated bone resorption without influencing osteoblast-dependent bone formation.

TTC26 deficiency down-regulates p38/AKT-NFATc1 axis in osteoclastogenesis

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which TTC26 deficiency impairs osteoclastogenesis, we performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on BMMs from LysM and Ttc26cKO mice after 48 h of RANKL stimulation. A total of 70 up-regulated and 142 down-regulated differential expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on an adjusted P < 0.05 (Fig. 3a). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed marked down-regulation of several critical signaling cascades, with the MAPK and PI3K-AKT pathways ranking among the top five most significantly affected pathways (Fig. 3b). Since both MAPK (p38) and PI3K-AKT signaling have been established to converge on NFATc1 activation during osteoclast differentiation [17,18], we hypothesized that Ttc26 deficiency attenuates osteoclastogenesis through suppression of the p38/AKT-NFATc1 axis. To validate this, we examined the phosphorylation of p38 and AKT in RANKL-treated BMMs at 0, 30, and 60 min. The results demonstrated that Ttc26 deficiency significantly impaired phosphorylation of both p38 and AKT at 30 and 60 min (Fig. 3c and d). These signaling defects were accompanied by corresponding reductions in NFATc1 protein levels (Fig. 3e), indicating compromised activation of this master transcription regulator in the Ttc26cKO BMMs. These findings establish the p38/AKT-NFATc1 axis as a critical downstream effector of TTC26 in osteoclast differentiation.

To functionally validate this regulatory axis, we employed pharmacological inhibition using the specific p38 inhibitor BIRB796 (BIRB) [19] and AKT inhibitor MK2206 (MK) [20]. Following dose optimization (Figs. S5 and S6), Ttc26cKO or LysM BMMs were treated with BIRB or MK during osteoclast differentiation. Notably, inhibition of either p38 or AKT signaling failed to further suppress osteoclastogenic capacity in Ttc26cKO BMMs, with unchanged NFATc1 expression (Fig. 3f and g), osteoclast numbers (Fig. 3h), and F-actin ring formation (Fig. 3i). Not surprisingly, osteoclast differentiation was reduced to the same levels in both Ttc26cKO and LysM BMMs with BIRB or MK treatments (Fig. 3f–i), suggesting that inhibition of p38 or AKT successfully perturbs TTC26-mediated osteoclast differentiation. Collectively, these data suggest that TTC26 orchestrates osteoclast differentiation through coordinated activation of the p38/AKT-NFATc1 signaling cascade, providing a mechanistic framework for its essential role in bone remodeling.

TTC26 interacts with RACK1 to activate the p38/AKT pathway

To investigate the mechanism by which Ttc26 deficiency suppresses p38/AKT pathway, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) in combination with mass spectrometry (MS) was performed. RACK1 was identified as a major interacting protein of TTC26, ranking among the top three most abundant binding partners (Fig. 4a). RACK1 potentiates the activity of various kinases [21], thereby facilitating the activation of multiple intracellular signaling pathways. The physical interaction between TTC26 and RACK1 was biochemically validated through Co-IP experiments co-transfected with TTC26-HA and RACK1-Myc plasmids (Fig. 4b and c). Furthermore, RACK1 expression showed a significant reduction in the Ttc26cKO BMMs compared to LysM (Fig. 4d). This reduction occurred independently of RANKL stimulation, as RACK1 expression remained stable throughout the course of RANKL treatment in both genotypes. These findings demonstrate the interaction between TTC26 and RACK1 provides a plausible molecular explanation for the impaired p38/AKT signaling observed in Ttc26-deficient cells.

Given that RACK1 has been implicated in MAPK (p38) and PI3K-AKT pathway activation [22,23], to further elucidate the role of RACK1 in TTC26-mediated osteoclastogenesis, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing RACK1 in Ttc26cKO BMMs. Following transfection with RACK1 overexpression (RACK1OE) plasmids, RACK1 protein levels were standardized between LysM and Ttc26cKO BMMs (Fig. S7). Strikingly, RACK1OE fully restored the p38 and AKT phosphorylation in Ttc26cKO BMMs to levels comparable with control cells (Fig. 4e and f). This signaling recovery of RACK1OE was accompanied by a significant up-regulation of NFATc1 expression (Fig. 4g), indicating reactivation of the downstream transcriptional program. Consistent with these molecular changes, RACK1OE rescued the defective osteoclast differentiation phenotype in Ttc26cKO cells, as shown in mature osteoclast numbers (Fig. 4h), and F-actin ring formation (Fig. 4i). These data establish that RACK1 is both necessary and sufficient to mediate TTC26's effects on osteoclastogenesis. The complete restoration of p38/AKT signaling and osteoclast differentiation in Ttc26cKO cells by RACK1 overexpression provides compelling evidence that TTC26 promotes osteoclastogenesis primarily through its interaction with RACK1 (Fig. 4j).

TTC26 acts as a scaffold to recruit MINDY3 for RACK1 deubiquitination

As TTC26 lacks intrinsic catalytic or transcriptional activity, we hypothesized that it may act as a scaffold protein that recruits functional partners to regulate RACK1 protein levels. To identify potential interactors shared by TTC26 and RACK1, Co-IP in combination with MS was performed. Interestingly, MINDY3, a recently identified DUB [24], emerged as the only protein that co-precipitated with both TTC26 and RACK1 among the top 10 interacting partners (Fig. 5a). Subsequent Co-IP experiments confirm that MINDY3 interacts with both TTC26 (Fig. 5b–S8a) and RACK1 (Fig. 5c–S8b), strongly suggesting the formation of a ternary complex containing all three proteins.

To further investigate whether the regulation of RACK1 expression by TTC26 depends on MINDY3, we designed small interfering RNA targeting Mindy3 (siMindy3) or Ttc26 (siTtc26) and confirmed their knockdown efficiency in BMMs (Fig. S9). Our results demonstrated that RACK1 protein levels were significantly lower, while its mRNA levels showed no changes with siMindy3 or siTtc26 treatment compared to control (Fig. 5d), suggesting that MINDY3 and TTC26 regulate RACK1 at the post-translational level. Notably, additional Ttc26 knockdown failed to further reduce RACK1 protein levels after Mindy3 inhibition (Fig. 5d), revealing that MINDY3 is indispensable for TTC26-mediated regulation of RACK1. Moreover, Mindy3 inhibition did not alter TTC26 expression, nor did Ttc26 knockdown affect MINDY3 levels (Fig. 5d–S10), suggesting there is no regulatory relationship between TTC26 and MINDY3. Finally, the inhibition of Mindy3 or Ttc26 also regulates the p38/AKT-NFATc1 axis, as there is a decrease in the phosphorylation of p38 and AKT (Fig. S11), NFATc1 expression (Fig. S12), and osteoclast formation (Fig. S13) under siMindy3 or siTtc26 treatment.

To explore whether TTC26 and MINDY3 regulate RACK1 through proteasomal pathway, we assessed RACK1 protein levels at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 h after cycloheximide (CHX) treatment. The results demonstrated that siMindy3, siTtc26, or combined knockdown treatment reduced RACK1 half-life compared to the control (Fig. 5e), suggesting that the knockdown of Ttc26 or Mindy3 suppressed the degradation of endogenous RACK1. We further treated the knockdown groups with proteasome inhibitor MG132 and observed complete restoration of RACK1 protein levels to the control (Fig. 5f), demonstrating that RACK1 degradation occurs primarily through the proteasomal pathway. Collectively, these data demonstrate that TTC26 protects RACK1 from proteasomal degradation in a MINDY3-dependent manner.

MINDY family is a K48-linked specific DUB [24]. To directly assess the K48-linked ubiquitination status of RACK1, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with the following plasmids: TTC26-HA; RACK1-Myc; MINDY3-His; Ub-Flag (WT, K0, K48, and K48R). We found that neither TTC26 nor MINDY3 alone reduced RACK1 ubiquitination, while combined overexpression of TTC26 and MINDY3 significantly decreased RACK1 ubiquitination (Fig. 5g). Furthermore, the deubiquitination of RACK1 was specifically detected in cells transfected with WT-Ub or K48-Ub plasmids, but not in those expressing K0-Ub and K48R-Ub (Fig. 5g). This suggests that MINDY3 specifically cleaves K48-linked ubiquitin chains to promote RACK1 deubiquitination. Taken together, these data indicate that TTC26 acts as a scaffold to recruit MINDY3 for deubiquitinating K48-linked ubiquitin chains on RACK1, thereby stabilizing RACK1 and suppressing its proteasomal degradation.

TTC26's scaffold function is indispensable to maintain RACK1 stability

Scaffold proteins have been documented to recruit DUBs and facilitate the specific interaction between DUBs and substrates [25,26]. Our study demonstrates that TTC26 deubiquitinates and stabilizes RACK1 in a MINDY3-dependent manner, raising the possibility that TTC26's scaffolding function is indispensable for MINDY3-mediated RACK1 deubiquitination. To establish TTC26's role as a molecular scaffold facilitating the interaction between RACK1 and MINDY3, we performed Co-IP between RACK1 and MINDY3 in TTC26 knockdown condition. The result showed that TTC26 depletion significantly disrupts the RACK1-MINDY3 binding (Fig. 6a), indicating that TTC26 is required for the interaction between RACK1 and MINDY3.

To selectively disrupt TTC26's scaffolding function in mediating RACK1-MINDY3 binding, we performed computational protein docking simulations through H-DOCK technology [27]. The results identified three key residues (Asn533, Thr534, Glu537) in TTC26 mediating its binding with RACK1 and three residues (Arg541, Lys545, Lys548) governing its interaction with MINDY3 (Fig. 6b). Based on the identified interaction residues, we constructed two TTC26 mutants: Mut1-TTC26 (mutations include N533A, T534A, and E537A) selectively disrupting RACK1 binding, and Mut2-TTC26 (mutations include R541A, K545A, and K548A) selectively disrupting MINDY3 binding. Co-IP assays confirmed that RACK1 lost Mut1 binding but retained Mut2 interaction (Fig. 6c–S14a), while MINDY3 failed to bind Mut2 but maintained Mut1 interaction (Fig. 6d–S14b). Overall, we precisely disrupted TTC26's scaffold function in mediating RACK1-MINDY3 binding through mutation of just six amino acid residues, without affecting its overall structure or other potential functions.

To determine whether mutation of these amino acid residues compromises TTC26-dependent stabilization of RACK1, we transfected empty vector, WT-, Mut1-, and Mut2-TTC26 in Ttc26cKO BMMs. Through RACK1 detection and CHX assays, we found that only WT-TTC26 effectively increased RACK1 protein levels (Fig. 6e) and prolonged RACK1 half-life (Fig. 6f and g), while two mutant variants showed no effect (Fig. 6e–g). This demonstrates that the scaffolding function of TTC26 is critical for protecting RACK1 from degradation. Furthermore, ubiquitination assays revealed that only WT-TTC26 reduced K48-linked ubiquitination of RACK1, demonstrating that the scaffold function of TTC26 suppresses the ubiquitin-proteasomal pathway of RACK1 (Fig. 6h–i, and S15). Not surprisingly, disruption of TTC26's scaffolding function impairs its ability to promote osteoclast formation, as TRAP and F-actin staining demonstrated that only WT-TTC26 restored osteoclast numbers and actin ring formation in Ttc26cKO BMMs (Fig. 6j and k). Taken together, these results indicate that the scaffold function of TTC26 is indispensable to maintain RACK1 stability and drive osteoclastogenesis.

Osteoclast-targeting shTtc26 delivery ameliorates estrogen-deficiency-induced bone loss

Clinically, various bone metabolic disorders are driven by pathological RANKL overactivation, with PMOP being the most representative disease [28]. RANKL signaling has been well-established to induce NFATc1 activation, while this study demonstrated that TTC26 deficiency promotes RACK1 degradation to suppress NFATc1 expression, thus inhibiting osteoclast differentiation. Therefore, we propose TTC26 as a promising therapeutic target for the diseases caused by pathological RANKL overactivation.

To evaluate the therapeutic effects of Ttc26 inhibition in PMOP, we synthesized osteoclast-targeted adeno-associated virus (AAV) carrying shTtc26 to treat osteoporosis in OVX mouse models, and compared the therapeutic outcomes between shTtc26 and zoledronate treatment (Fig. 7a). Fluorescence imaging of femoral sections confirmed that the AAV was successfully targeted to bone tissue (Fig. 7b). Micro-CT analysis revealed that Ttc26 inhibition significantly mitigated the OVX-induced impairments in trabecular bone mass, as mice with shTtc26 treatment showed an increase in BV/TV, BMD, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, along with a reduction in Tb.Sp (Fig. 7c and d). HE staining further confirmed this finding, showing elevated Tb.Ar with shTtc26 treatment (Fig. 7e). Moreover, TRAP staining revealed that shTtc26 treatment significantly reduced the elevated osteoclast numbers in the OVX mice (Fig. 7f), suggesting Ttc26 inhibition successfully impairs osteoclast differentiation. However, the therapeutic effects of shTtc26, as quantified by micro-CT parameters (Fig. 7c and d), HE-based histology (Fig. 7e), and TRAP-positive osteoclast counts (Fig. 7f), were statistically indistinguishable from those achieved with zoledronate treatment. Collectively, these results reveal that shTtc26 treatment effectively protects against trabecular bone loss in OVX-induced osteoporosis, highlighting TTC26 as a potential therapeutic target for PMOP.

Discussion

Our study is the first to uncover a novel function of TTC26 as a molecular scaffold regulating deubiquitination during osteoclastogenesis. Previously characterized as an essential IFT-B subunit critical for ciliogenesis, TTC26 localizes at the periphery of the IFT complex [29], enabling interactions with key effectors such as motor proteins and signaling molecules [30,31]. For example, TTC26 mediates Hedgehog signaling via transmembrane receptor interactions and recruits motor proteins during retrograde transport [31]. Recent studies have expanded the recognized distribution of IFT proteins beyond the cilia, demonstrating their presence in non-ciliated cells like osteoclasts and in extra-ciliary compartments of ciliated cells such as BMMs [14]. This emerging view positions IFT proteins as multimodal signaling integrators. Several IFT family members have already been suggested to function as scaffolds in bone remodeling—loss of IFT20 disrupts collagen trafficking and osteoblast polarity [12], while IFT80 deficiency enhances osteoclastogenesis by promoting tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 degradation [14]. However, no IFT protein has previously been shown to function as a scaffold for DUBs in osteoclastogenesis.

Our findings fill this gap by demonstrating that TTC26 serves as a dedicated scaffold for MINDY3, a DUB, enhancing its substrate recognition and catalytic efficiency toward RACK1. This facilitates K48-linked deubiquitination and stabilization of RACK1, a pivotal regulator of NFATc1 signaling and osteoclast differentiation. This mechanism adds a new layer of specificity to DUB-mediated regulation, which has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for metabolic bone diseases. While several DUBs, including OTUB1 and USP7, have been associated with bone homeostasis [32,33], their broad substrate specificity limits direct therapeutic utility. In contrast, scaffold protein TTC26 refines DUB-substrate interactions and offer a more targeted approach. Our study thus not only expands the functional repertoire of IFT proteins but also introduces a previously unrecognized regulatory axis in which DUB scaffold proteins modulate bone remodeling and may serve as potential therapeutic targets.

As a substrate of the deubiquitination process mediated by TTC26, we found that RACK1 amplifies the canonical RANKL signaling pathway. Originally identified as an anchoring protein for protein kinase C [34], RACK1 utilizes its seven-bladed β-propeller structure to mediate various dynamic interactions [35]. This architecture enables RACK1 to regulate osteoclast differentiation. For example, RACK1 has been demonstrated as a positive regulator for MAPK(p38) pathway activation and subsequent NFATc1 induction in osteoclastogenesis [22]. Similarly, a study has established that the interaction between RACK1 and c-Src, critical for PI3K-AKT pathway activation, is essential for osteoclast function [23]. In our study, we discovered that when TTC26 maintains RACK1 expression, RACK1 enhances the activation of both the PI3K-AKT and MAPK pathways—two crucial signaling cascades in the RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis. This amplification culminates in the activation of NFATc1, a master regulator of osteoclastogenesis. Conversely, when TTC26 is deficient and RACK1 expression is down-regulated, this amplifying effect is abolished, leading to impaired NFATc1 expression and compromised osteoclast differentiation. These findings collectively demonstrate that RACK1 plays a vital regulatory role in the RANKL signaling cascade by amplifying the p38/AKT-NFATc1 pathway, which acts as a master regulatory circuit governing osteoclast differentiation.

RACK1, a non-druggable protein, is a target for intervention primarily through the regulation of its ubiquitination process. Previous studies have shown that modulating RACK1 deubiquitination can serve as a therapeutic strategy. For instance, the inhibition of deubiquitination has demonstrated potential in suppressing metastatic progression in hepatocellular carcinoma [36]. In this study, we identify MINDY3—a recently characterized K48-linked specific DUB of the MINDY family—as the principal DUB responsible for RACK1 stabilization in osteoclastogenesis. However, as a newly discovered member of the DUB family, the functional significance of MINDY3 in cellular homeostasis and disease contexts remains to be fully explored. Building on structural insights that identify residues C51 and H287 within MINDY3's DUF4250 domain as critical catalytic determinants (Abdul Rehman et al.) [24], we demonstrate that MINDY3 specifically hydrolyzes K48-linked ubiquitin chains on RACK1. Crucially, we establish that scaffold protein TTC26 functions as a molecular chaperone facilitating this substrate-specific recognition of RACK1. Our results reveal that TTC26, MINDY3, and RACK1 form a stable ternary complex. Knockout of TTC26 disrupts the interaction between MINDY3 and RACK1, leading to the suppression of osteoclast differentiation signaling. In the absence of MINDY3, TTC26 fails to regulate RACK1 expression, indicating that TTC26 itself lacks catalytic activity and functions solely as a scaffold protein for the DUB. These findings suggest that modulating RACK1 expression through the TTC26-mediated deubiquitination pathway could effectively regulate osteoclast differentiation.

We further define the scaffold function of TTC26 within the TTC26-MINDY3-RACK1 ternary complex. Through H-DOCK modeling, we mapped the molecular interfaces that govern TTC26's interactions with both MINDY3 and RACK1. This scaffold activity is mediated by six critical residues: N533, T534, and E537 for TTC26-RACK1 binding; R541, K545, and K548 for TTC26-MINDY3 binding. These residues could serve as blueprints for the rational design of targeted inhibitors. Collectively, our findings elucidate a TTC26-mediated deubiquitination process that enables precise substrate selection by MINDY3, advancing our understanding of chaperone-dependent recognition in DUB regulation. Moreover, these results uncover novel principles of post-translational control in osteoclast signaling.

Current targeted protein degradation strategies primarily rely on either promoting ubiquitination or inhibiting deubiquitination to induce protein degradation. In our study, we propose that modulating RACK1 degradation through deubiquitination represents a viable and promising alternative. First, the E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for RACK1 degradation in osteoclasts remains unidentified, rendering ubiquitination-based strategies less tractable due to their dependence on E3 ligase specificity. Second, compared to the often aggressive nature of ubiquitination, deubiquitination offers a more nuanced and tunable mechanism of protein regulation—an advantage particularly relevant to chronic diseases such as osteoporosis. In such long-duration conditions, current pharmacological agents often lead to adverse outcomes associated with excessive suppression of bone remodeling [37,38]. Thus, targeting deubiquitination may offer a safer and more sustainable therapeutic avenue.

However, direct DUB inhibition is often limited by low substrate specificity and off-target effects [9]. Emerging evidence suggests that scaffold proteins play a crucial role in guiding DUB-substrate recognition, offering spatial precision and regulatory fidelity. This has positioned scaffold proteins as high-value therapeutic targets for indirectly modulating disease-relevant substrates. For instance, disrupting DCAF7 [25] or S100A9 [26] scaffolds selectively interferes with oncogenic signaling in cisplatin-resistant nasopharyngeal carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, respectively. In this study, we identify TTC26 as a dedicated scaffold protein that enhances the substrate specificity of MINDY3 for RACK1, thereby amplifying RACK1-mediated osteoclastogenic signaling. While therapeutic strategies targeting deubiquitination in osteoclast biology are scarce, current approaches such as UCHL1 knockdown fail to address the specificity issues inherent to direct DUB inhibition [39]. In contrast, our findings underscore the therapeutic advantage of targeting scaffold proteins. Specifically, we achieved a bone-marrow-specific delivery of shTtc26 in OVX mice utilizing AAV-9 vectors conjugated with Asp8 peptide. This AAV serotype has been demonstrated to possess bone-targeting capability, although its relative transduction efficiency between BMMs and BMSCs requires further quantification [40]. Our results showed that shTtc26 effectively preserved trabecular bone mass and microarchitecture without disrupting physiological bone remodeling, highlighting the translational potential of this approach. By targeting TTC26's scaffold function, we selectively disrupted RACK1's activation of PI3K-AKT and MAPK pathways. This scaffold-targeting strategy thus offers a mechanistically refined and pathway-selective therapeutic framework for PMOP and potentially other osteoclast-driven metabolic bone disorders.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that TTC26 functions as a scaffold protein facilitating the recruitment of MINDY3 for the deubiquitination of RACK1, thereby promoting osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption via p38/AKT-NFATc1 axis (Fig. 8). This functional characterization of TTC26 in bone remodeling addresses a significant gap, enriching our understanding of the multifaceted roles of IFT machinery. Furthermore, we validate the translational potential of the scaffold-targeted therapy, showing that TTC26 inhibition selectively silences the pathological osteoclastogenesis in osteoporosis. Collectively, these findings provide a precision therapeutic framework for conditions characterized by dysregulated osteoclast activation, highlighting the potential of scaffold-mediated control of protein stability as a novel therapeutic strategy.
