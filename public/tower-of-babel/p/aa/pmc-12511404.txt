Characterization of geothermal resources and estimation of power potential of Badrinath geothermal field northwest Himalaya India
Authors: Sameer K. Tiwari, Árni Ragnarsson, Gudni Axelsson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Geothermal energy is a recognized renewable resource in the present scenario, with over 30 nations generating electricity and 88 using it for heating and heat pumps. Despite its substantial geothermal potential, India has yet to develop this resource because of limited geological exploration, reservoir characterization, and technological constraints. The Indian Himalayas host over 300 geothermal fields, offering a promising avenue for sustainable energy deployment in remote regions. This study evaluates the geothermal potential of the Badrinath geothermal field in the northwest Himalayas using a volumetric heat estimation method integrated with Monte Carlo simulations, addressing uncertainties in subsurface temperature distribution, heat capacity, and fluid recharge. Results indicate a maximum thermal power potential of 39 MWt and an electrical generation potential of 3.0 MWe, demonstrating its viability for small-scale power production. Furthermore, the design of a district heating system for the guesthouse is investigated, with particular attention to optimizing thermal energy transfer efficiency from the geothermal spring to the radiators. Despite extreme winter temperatures reaching − 10 °C, our findings confirm that geothermal water can be effectively utilized for heating applications without endangering the structural integrity of heat exchangers and distribution pipelines. This study represents the first systematic assessment of the Badrinath geothermal field, providing insights for future geothermal energy utilization in the Indian Himalayas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19120-5.

Introduction

The transition to a carbon-free society depends on the prompt adoption of renewable energy sources (RES) to replace fossil fuels1. While wind and solar energy are cost-competitive, their intermittency limits their capacity to provide reliable baseload power2. Geothermal energy (GTE) emerges as a strong candidate, providing the reliability needed for a sustainable energy future2. Geothermal energy cuts greenhouse gas emissions, supporting United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) goals, and is a sustainable and eco-friendly source3,4. Geothermal energy offers a distinct advantage over other renewable sources because it provides baseload electricity and enables direct utilization5.

These unique characteristics have contributed to a significant global increase in geothermal electricity generation and direct applications over the past few decades6–8. Geothermal systems are primarily formed because of exothermally heated groundwater driven by convection through fractures, high geothermal gradients, and heat sources such as active tectonic zones (e.g., the Himalayas), volcanic regions, radioactive decay in the Earth’s crust, plate boundaries (continental or divergent), and sedimentary basins9–14. India ranks first in energy consumption among developing nations15, and the financial burden of importing fossil fuels has become increasingly unsustainable16. Given the global urgency to address climate change and reduce fossil fuel dependency, the international community is rapidly transitioning to cleaner energy alternatives.

The geothermal potential of the Himalayan region, particularly the Garhwal Himalaya, is closely associated with the Main Central Thrust (MCT), which functions as a deep-seated regional heat source9,11,17–19. This tectonic structure enables meteoric water to infiltrate to considerable depths, where it is heated and subsequently re-emerges as geothermal activity19. In the study area, geothermal manifestations result from convective water movement in the upper crust within a confined zone, where heat is transferred from the source to the surface under free-surface conditions8,20,21. The structural setup extends approximately 150 km northwest, hosting multiple geothermal resources19,22. These observations are consistent with the high heat-flow anomalies recorded along the MCT in the Garhwal Himalaya, which are associated with numerous hot springs and geothermal fields occurrences11,22.

During electricity production, hot water gradually releases its heat, leading to a progressive decline in temperature6. However, the remaining thermal energy can be harnessed for various purposes, such as geothermal cooling, greenhouse farming, space heating, snow melting, geothermal heat pumps, district heating, industrial applications, gas and mineral extraction, and balneotherapy (e.g., lagoon tourism and spa facilities) through a cascade utilization approach. Geothermal resource assessment integrates surface manifestation observations with geoscientific data, including geophysical, geochemical, and geological analyses, to evaluate such potential23.

Geothermal resource assessment techniques can be classified into four primary categories: (1) surface thermal flux, (2) volume, (3) planar fracture, and (4) magmatic heat budget. The volume method proves particularly effective due to its broad applicability, the capacity to accurately measure or estimate parameters, manageable error assessment, and the potential to address uncertainties related to recoverability and recharge through future modeling and engineering techniques24. This approach enhances the reliability of geothermal assessments and facilitates sustainable resource management.

Estimating geothermal reservoir resources is primarily based on stored heat and volumetric methods, with Monte Carlo simulation widely regarded as the most reliable resource estimation25. The volumetric method evaluates the thermal energy of the system by treating the reservoir as a unified body or dividing it into layers and smaller blocks, each assigned unique values for essential parameters. Key parameters include temperature, porosity, area, thickness, density, and the heat capacity of the fluid and rock matrix26. By applying Monte Carlo simulation, uncertainties in these parameters can be quantified, resulting in more accurate and dependable estimates27. This integration of probabilistic modeling enhances the reliability of resource evaluation and improves decision-making in geothermal energy development.

The Monte Carlo method demonstrates how randomness can inform decisions in the face of uncertainty. By repeatedly simulating outcomes for unknown variables, it generates statistically robust results through numerous iterations. For example, rolling a die produces possible outcomes ranging from 1 to 6, but the exact result remains unpredictable, highlighting the role of chance. Similarly, geothermal reservoir parameters such as area, thickness, porosity, and temperature also exist within a range of possible values, each carrying inherent uncertainty27.

To achieve accurate results, these uncertainties are modeled using suitable probability distributions (e.g., normal, triangular, uniform, or log-normal) based on the specific characteristics of the geothermal field under investigation. The simulation then generates many random values for each parameter, which are processed iteratively until the results converge into a reliable estimate28. By combining sampling theory with numerical analysis, Monte Carlo simulation has evolved into a powerful tool in computational science, enabling detailed quantification of complex geothermal systems with high precision29.

The widespread adoption of Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS) in geothermal research is primarily because of advancements in computing, which enable complex simulations that were previously labor-intensive30,31. This approach allows known geothermal resource characteristics to be projected into potential distributions within the same area30,32. A similar approach, the volumetric heat-in-place method, was initially developed by the United States Geological Survey (USGS)24,33 and later refined by34–36.

In this study, the volumetric heat-in-place method is combined with Monte Carlo simulation to evaluate uncertainties in estimating the geothermal power potential of the Badrinath geothermal field in the Garhwal Himalaya, India. The assessment incorporates surface manifestations, geochemical and geophysical analyses, geological surveys, and dissolved silica thermometry data. The findings indicate a substantial geothermal potential, suggesting that Badrinath is well-suited for developing a pilot geothermal heating system to meet local energy demands.

Geological setup, data, and methods

Study area

Regions with high geothermal gradients, such as tectonically active areas or plate boundaries, commonly display geothermal anomalies13,19,21,37,38. A geothermal system consists of three key components: a heat source, a reservoir, and a transfer medium, usually a fluid that transports heat to the surface39,40. In India, notable geothermal regions are found in the Himalayas, primarily in deeply incised river valleys and along major geological structures, including the Indus-Tsangpo Suture Zone (ITSZ) and Main Central Thrust (MCT)19. The rapid uplift and erosion of the Himalayan range have resulted in a steep geothermal gradient, mainly driven by hydrothermal activity41,42. Magnetotelluric surveys in the Garhwal region of the northwest Himalayas have identified low-resistivity layers at an average depth of 5 kilometers22. Across India, approximately 300 geothermal springs or fields have been identified, with nearly half (about 120) located in the Himalayan region13,19,37,38.

The ITSZ defines the northern boundary of the Himalayan orogeny, while the Himalayan Frontal Thrust (HFT) marks its southern limit43. This region is divided into four primary litho-tectonic units, separated by south-dipping intercontinental thrusts, including the South Tibet Detachment (STD), (MCT), and the Main Boundary Thrust (MBT)44. Notable geothermal activity in the Garhwal Himalaya is concentrated around the MCT zone, which is characterized by high heat flow (130 ± 30 mW/m2) and steep temperature gradients (60° ± 20 °C/km) in the northwest Himalaya45. In the Alaknanda Valley, geothermal features are associated with rock types such as gneiss, schist, and quartzite, all belonging to the Central Himalayan Crystallines46. This site contains gneiss, schist interbedded with calc-silicate, calc-schist, marble, and quartzite, all part of the central crystalline group47. The Garhwal region of the northwest Himalayas is influenced by the Indian summer monsoon (I.S.M.) and westerly winds, which impact the rivers and streams in the area. The two major proglacial tributaries of the Ganga River system, Alaknanda and Bhagirathi, drain an area of approximately 30,000 km2.

Badrinath is home to a single geothermal spring, strategically located on the right bank of the Alaknanda River at a remarkable elevation of 3089 m above sea level. This spring emerges from morainic deposits and is elegantly enclosed by a dome-like structure, along with other permanent installations37. The water, consistently heated to 55.6 °C, flows into various Kunds, serving as a vital resource for pilgrims. It is characteristically tasteless, possesses a distinct sulfurous odor, and occasionally contains gas bubbles, adding to its unique appeal. With an impressive flow rate of approximately 400 L per minute19, this spring not only fulfills spiritual needs but also showcases striking geological features, including visible carbonate deposits near the Tapta Kund, while displaying no ferruginous staining (Fig. 1). Located just 50 m downstream, a second spring with a gentle temperature of 20 °C offers its unique attributes. Its exact origin remains unclear, obscured by surrounding permanent structures. This colorless, slightly sour water is used for practical purposes such as bathing and laundry, with a notable discharge rate of around 190 L per minute. Collectively, these springs enhance Badrinath’s spiritual significance and highlight the region’s fascinating natural phenomena. The study area has a cold-temperate climate, with snowfall occurring in the upper valleys during December and January, leading to the emergence of springs19,48,49. The study area lies within the inner Himalayan Range, characterized by rugged terrain with steep hills and deep valleys, with elevations ranging from 1400 to 6596 m above sea level. The region has a cold-temperate climate, and snowfall occurs in the upper reaches of the valleys during December and January. The monsoon season, spanning June to September, brings the heaviest rainfall, with an annual average of 1000 mm and 1200 mm48. Additionally, the region is considered highly promising for geothermal energy exploration37.

The primary objective of geothermal resource assessments is to determine geothermal resources’ long-term power generation potential while ensuring sustainable utilization for at least 50 years. Accordingly, resource management strategies are designed to support long-term production. These assessments are generally performed during the reconnaissance and exploration phases before drilling, where data are synthesized to identify potential heat sources and host reservoirs, which are later confirmed through exploratory drilling at both shallow and deep levels.

Geochemical characteristics

Geothermal systems exhibit rock-water interactions at moderate to high temperatures, with noticeable effects on the Earth’s surface. These interactions are often reflected in the chemical composition of spring water, making it a valuable indicator for studying geothermal systems. The water from Badrinath geothermal spring is mildly neutral, with a pH of 7 to 7.2. At 25 °C, its enormous specific conductivity is 2690 micromhos/cm, while the total dissolved solids (TDS) content is 1910 mg/L. This geochemical characteristic suggests its deep circulation has a meteoric origin19. The surface temperature of the geothermal spring water is recorded at 55.5°C19,37,49. The volumetric ‘‘heat in place’’ method for geothermal resource

Evaluating geothermal resources requires analyzing each area’s location, size, and geological characteristics. This information is crucial for estimating the accessible resource base, the thermal energy stored in the reservoir, and the recoverable thermal energy. The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) volumetric “heat in place” method is the most effective way to assess the energy potential of geothermal reservoirs. This reliable approach highlights the substantial resources available for sustainable energy production, underscoring the importance of geothermal energy in renewable solutions50. This study used the volume method to determine the stored energy and produced heat energy of a low-temperature geothermal reservoir in Badrinath. Microsoft Excel was utilized to conduct Monte Carlo simulation studies.

The volume method is a highly effective strategy for calculating the thermal energy contained within a specific volume of rock and water, enabling a robust estimation of energy recovery potential. This method accurately determines the thermal energy in the ground by multiplying the volume of a geothermal reservoir by its mean temperature, porosity, and the specific heats of both rock and water. Alternatively, a more straightforward calculation can be employed using the volume, temperature, and an assumed volumetric specific heat51,52.

While assessing recoverable thermal energy introduces more complexity, requiring an in-depth understanding of critical reservoir properties such as permeability, this challenge is well worth overcoming. In many cases, the recovery factor can only be approximated53, but the insights gained are invaluable. Notably, the volume method has proven highly effective in estimating thermal energy from low-temperature geothermal fields, underscoring its essential role in advancing sustainable energy solutions35.

The volumetric method enhances this by combining MCS with the reservoir volume method24,33–36. Here, the volumetric method is adopted to estimate the geothermal power potential. Monte Carlo simulation (MCS)

The MCS employs stochastic techniques to assess the impact of measurements and uncertainties on petrophysical results. This technique is preferred because it allows the interpretation model to be processed multiple times with randomly varied parameters. It is often used when the model is complex, nonlinear, or contains uncertain parameters. While the method incorporates various approaches, they generally follow a common pattern. MCS is reliable and widely adopted in geothermal resource assessment, power generation, and techno-economic evaluations. It relies on the reservoir volume method and iterative sampling of probability distributions (e.g., uniform, triangular, Gaussian, PERT). It helps to estimate the probability and cumulative distributions of geothermal resources, with P90, P50, and P10 values representing 90%, 50%, and 10% probability outcomes54,55.

Input parameters for MCS

In this study, the collected data comprise reservoir temperature, formation thickness, porosity, formation rock density, specific heat, and the specific heat of formation fluid. These parameters are characterized using either uniform or triangular probability distributions. For the triangular distribution, each parameter is defined by its minimum, most likely, and maximum values, which makes this distribution particularly suitable when available data are limited35,56. Accordingly, triangular distributions are employed for density, specific heat capacity of rock, geothermal manifestation area, reservoir temperature, project lifespan, and reservoir lithology thickness. Uniform distributions are assigned to rock porosity and recovery factor, whereas constant distributions are used for hot water’s specific heat capacity and density, conversion efficiency, accessibility, and reference temperature. The MCS assessed the geothermal resource and power potential over 25, 35, and 50 years. The MCS runs with at least 100,000 iterations in Microsoft Excel. Reservoir area and thickness

The data range of the reservoir area has been selected based on the surface manifestation of geothermal activity according to19,37. In the Badrinath area, the lower order resistivity zone, having a resistivity range of 60 to 250 Ohm-meter with thickness ranging from 20 to 250 m, is indicated. The lowest resistivity, 60 Ohm-m, is recorded nearest to the hot spring manifestation (Table 2). This conductive zone appears to be significant from the point of view of geothermal potential. This zone’s resistivity may be lowered because of the heating effect of the hot fluids or the saturation57. The active geothermal area spreads over ~ 3 km2 (Fig. 1) between Bamni village (south of Badrinath) and Mana village (north of Badrinath Town).

Reservoir and reference temperature

The reservoir temperature was taken from the literature for this study, taken from19,37,49. The maximum temperature may be 150 °C. Here, 130 °C was considered the most likely temperature of the geothermal reservoir, and the reference temperature is adopted as 20 °C for this case Table 3. The reference temperature for district heating in a geothermal field depends on the context (energy calculation, system design, or efficiency evaluation), but generally, it represents the baseline temperature relative to which useful heat is calculated. The reference temperature is usually the ambient or ground temperature of the area, or sometimes the return temperature of the district heating network.

Porosity

This parameter can only be accurately determined through comprehensive testing. Studies indicate that about 90% of thermal energy is stored in the rock, with only 10% in the fluid59,60. Consequently, many researchers overlook porosity when using the volumetric method61. In this study, we adopt a uniform distribution, setting the most probable value at 5%, as supported by research in La Palma62 and standard practices in geothermal heat storage evaluation60. Recovery factor

The geothermal recovery factor is the ratio of heat recovered at the wellhead to the heat stored in the reservoir. This value typically depends on several factors, including the permeability structure, the characteristics of the wells, and the reservoir’s thermal and hydraulic boundary conditions. As a result, the recovery factor is not an intrinsic property used in resource evaluation with the volumetric method; rather, it reflects the actual operational characteristics of a reservoir63.

Currently, there is no information regarding the permeability characteristics of the potential resource, as there is no evidence of permeable reservoirs in the locations being studied19,37. A range of 0–0.20 is considered, including the null value for low productivity and uncertainties, with 0.20 being the maximum credible value from liquid-dominated reservoirs. Input parameters for the House heating system

House heating systems maintain indoor temperatures (18-23°C) during winter. Basic methods use fireplaces or individual units powered by gas, kerosene, boilers, or electricity. Central systems circulate hot water or steam to radiators or floor heating. Electrical resistance heating and air-to-air heat pumps are standard. District heating connects multiple homes to a shared hot water supply64.

Building heat loss calculation

The heat loss of a building is a function of the outdoor weather conditions and indoor temperature, as well as the construction of the building components (insulation). The heat is lost by transfer through the building surface and by the exchange of air between the heated space and the surroundings of the building65. Low outdoor temperature and high wind speed are the most important parameters contributing to high heat losses from buildings.

Sensitivity analysis of MCS input

Among the key input parameters in our Monte Carlo simulations, the reservoir’s volume, temperature, and recovery factor are the most uncertain variables. Their significant influence on the simulated capacity output is reflected in the thermal power, electrical energy capacity, and power capacity described in Eqs. (1, 3, 6, 7) and the minimum–maximum range in Table 3. While we established triangular distributions for the Badrinath reservoir based on credible data, variations in the mode (the most likely value) can significantly affect the final results. To illustrate this, we conducted additional Monte Carlo iterations, adjusting each zone’s most likely recovery factor and volume. In this analysis, we treated volume as an independent variable rather than deriving it from reservoir area and thickness. The most likely recovery factor was adjusted from 0.1 to 0.2, while the minimum and maximum values for the triangular and uniform distributions were kept constant per Table 3. All other distributions remained unchanged, highlighting the critical importance of precise input parameters for reliable simulation outcomes.

District heating potential of the Badrinath geothermal field

This study evaluates the geothermal potential of the Badrinath field using the volumetric method and Monte Carlo simulation to determine its feasibility for space heating in the Garhwal region of the northwest Himalaya. Geothermal water and steam are sustainable energy sources for electricity generation and direct use. Electricity generation utilizes geothermal heat (130 °C to 300 °C) to produce mechanical energy, achieving 10% to 30% efficiencies. Direct uses, such as space heating, attain 50% to 80% of thermal efficiencies68. Co-generation systems optimize energy use by integrating both methods. District heating systems, effective in cold regions, offer cost-efficient heating solutions. This study also assesses the potential for geothermal house heating in Badrinath, comparing it to successful European models, particularly from Iceland. The volumetric assessment shows promising potential, with estimates indicating the field could generate at least 3 MWe of electricity and 39 MWt of thermal power upon successful drilling. This makes it suitable for a geothermal heating system at a government guesthouse managed by the Garhwal Mandal Vikas Nigam in Badrinath town. The geothermal water’s chemical composition, primarily Na–HCO₃, is detailed in Table 1.

Design and heating load assessment of the site

The government guesthouse is the primary commercial building in Badrinath town, comprising 20 rooms and a dormitory with over 300 beds. It is located 300 m from the geothermal resource, with an aerial distance of 200 m from the spring. The guesthouse spans 1000 m2 (50 × 20 m) with a volume of 8000 m3 (50 × 20 × 8 m). The heat demand of the building is primarily influenced by outdoor temperature, though factors like wind speed and solar radiation also play a role. Input parameters indicate that the maximum outdoor temperature reaches 15 °C (the required room temperature for human comfort) for only one month annually.

In contrast, sub-zero temperatures persist for over six months, necessitating space heating. Studies estimate the Badrinath geothermal reservoir temperature between 130 and 150 °C. Heat will be supplied using wall-mounted steel radiators, though their quantity is yet to be determined. Return water temperature sensors will regulate the radiator flow rate. The system will include steel pipes (1 or 1.5 inches in diameter) for internal circulation and pre-insulated plastic pipes (PEX, 52 mm diameter, 300 m long) for outdoor installation. The heating system is designed for a peak load of 320 kWt.

The town has a stable electricity supply, minimizing the risk of power failure affecting pump operation. To enhance reliability, 1 MW solar power units will be installed. Since the hot spring is at a lower elevation (5 m) than the guesthouse, pumping is required to transport the geothermal water. A schematic of the system is shown in Fig. 5, illustrating key components: hot springs, heat exchangers, pumps, transmission pipes, and radiators. The maximum discharge rate of the spring is 430 L per minute, with a natural outflow temperature of 55.5 °C, currently used for tourism-related bathing. Chemical analysis of the geothermal water19 confirms its suitability for direct-use heating without scaling or corrosion risks in pipes or radiators. However, a heat exchanger system will be implemented to prevent freezing in outdoor pipes during low ambient temperatures. In case of pump failure, a closed-loop system will circulate freshwater mixed with 30% ethylene glycol antifreeze through the radiators. Direct through-flow of geothermal water would be more efficient if the spring were higher, but this is not the case. Among various heat exchanger types, the brazed plate heat exchanger is preferred because of:Fig. 5Proposed schematic diagram for space heating for the government guesthouse.

(i) Compact design with a high power-to-volume ratio (m3/kW); (ii) Enhanced heat transfer efficiency because of high turbulence (even at Reynolds numbers as low as 10–400), which also prevents fouling; (iii) Lightweight and easy handling compared to other systems. Conclusions

This paper presents the first evaluation of geothermal resources and power potential estimation of the Badrinath geothermal field. Based on the available published geological and geophysical data, the volumetric heat in place method combined with the Monte Carlo simulation approach was applied in the most promising zones identified in the literature of India. Geo-thermometric analysis indicates subsurface reservoir temperatures between 100 and 140 °C, confirming the field’s suitability for low-temperature geothermal development. Using the volumetric method combined with Monte Carlo simulations to account for uncertainties, the study estimated power potential under 25, 35, and 50-year utilization scenarios. For 25 years, thermal power output (MWt) was estimated at P10: 39.5, P50: 27.3, and P90: 18.0, with corresponding electric power output (MWe) of 3.2, 2.2, and 1.4.

Badrinath geothermal field could initially support a 1.04 MWe plant for 35 years. For 50 years, thermal output was 19.7, 13.7, and 9.0 MWt, with electric output at 1.6, 1.1, and 0.7 MWe. These results demonstrate that the available geothermal resource can provide a consistent and sustainable source of electricity and heating for Badrinath, significantly reducing reliance on fossil fuels. However, the current geothermal spring discharge is adequate to meet the heating needs of the guesthouse. Overall, this study confirms that the geothermal resource in the Garhwal Himalaya presents a viable and sustainable energy solution for high-altitude, remote regions.

Despite considerable geological uncertainties, the techno-economic analysis strongly demonstrates that geothermal energy is a reliable, cost-effective, and cost-competitive solution. It is crucial in propelling India toward its ambitious carbon–neutral and green energy goals.
