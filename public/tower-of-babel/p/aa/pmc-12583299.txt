The impact of tislelizumab immunotherapy on multiple primary lung cancer presenting as ground-glass nodules: preliminary results analysis from a single-arm, phase II trial
Authors: Junfei Wu, Xiangning Fu, Yi Gao, Shenghui Qin, Jing Xiong, Rirong Qu, Yixin Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of multiple primary lung cancer (MPLC) continues to increase, with synchronous multifocal ground-glass nodules (GGNs) representing a characteristic manifestation. In this study evaluated the efficacy and safety of tislelizumab in patients with MPLC who presented with synchronous multifocal GGNs were evaluated.MethodsIn this single-arm, phase II exploratory trial, 12 patients with synchronous multifocal GGNs were enrolled. All patients received at least two doses of tislelizumab (200 mg i.v., Q3W). The primary endpoint was the objective response rate (ORR). Multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF), T-cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq), and whole-exome sequencing (WES) were performed to investigate alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) during immunotherapy.ResultsNone of the 12 patients had lesions meeting the criteria for complete response (CR) or partial response (PR). One patient developed pseudoprogression (PsP), while one patient developed a new GGN during follow-up. The remaining 10 patients manifested stable disease (SD). No patients experienced grade 3–5 adverse events. mIF revealed significant differences in the TIME between PsP lesions and responsive lesions, characterized by increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration concurrent with heightened infiltration of immunosuppressive cells. TCR-seq analysis indicated that PsP lesions exhibited low clonality but high diversity. Furthermore, WES analysis demonstrated high heterogeneity among multiple lesions, with PsP lesions exhibiting a low tumor mutational burden (TMB).ConclusionPatients with synchronous multifocal GGNs exhibited poor therapeutic responses to tislelizumab. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) may induce PsP in pure GGNs, thereby leading to unnecessary extension of surgical resection, hence ICIs should be applied with caution in clinical management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04212-y.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Ground glass nodule, Multiple primary lung cancer, Tislelizumab, Clinical trial

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most prevalent malignancy globally and the primary cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. With the widespread application of low-dose computed tomography (CT)-guided lung cancer screening and high-resolution diagnostic CT scans, the detection rate of ground-glass nodules (GGNs) has increased dramatically. On the basis of the presence or absence of a solid component, pulmonary GGNs can be classified into two types: pure GGNs (pGGNs) and part-solid nodules (PSNs) [2]. GGNs, which are nonspecific imaging findings, can be observed in benign conditions such as infections, focal hemorrhage, and interstitial fibrosis, as well as in malignant tumors [3, 4]. Despite their diverse etiologies, the prevalence of malignancies among persistent GGNs is relatively high [5].

The incidence of multiple primary lung cancer (MPLC) has shown a persistent increase in recent years, especially MPLCs characterized by synchronous multifocal GGNs as the primary manifestation [6]. There are currently no standardized treatment protocols for MPLC in clinical practice, and surgical resection remains the primary therapeutic approach for MPLC patients. The clinical feasibility of radical resection is substantially constrained by restricted pulmonary functional reserve, high comorbidity burden, and multilobar distribution of lesions [7, 8]. Moreover, there remains a lack of consensus regarding effective therapeutic approaches for surgically retained lesions demonstrating high malignant propensity [9].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have gained widespread clinical application across various malignancies because of their favorable toxicity profile and excellent tolerability [10]. Immunotherapy has significantly improved long-term survival outcomes in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) being substantially prolonged [11, 12]. Multiple phase III clinical trials have confirmed the significant efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with resectable stage I-IIIA NSCLC [13, 14]. However, the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs remains unclear in ultraearly stage NSCLC patients who predominantly feature GGNs [15].

Compared with solid nodules, GGN-featured lung carcinomas exhibit a distinct tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [16, 17]. The TIME plays a critical role in tumorigenesis, immune evasion, and therapeutic response [18]. Remodeling the TIME constitutes a fundamental mechanism underlying the antitumor activity of ICIs [19]. However, our understanding of how ICIs specifically modulate changes in individual TIME components remains relatively limited. Previous studies have indicated that specific characteristics of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) (including phenotypic profiles, density metrics, and spatial architecture) could function as potential biomarkers for predicting ICI efficacy in NSCLC patients [20, 21]. GGN-featured lung carcinomas are characterized primarily by a low tumor mutation burden (TMB), reduced infiltration of immune cell subsets, and diminished immune microenvironment activity. These features suggest that GGNs may exhibit poor responsiveness to ICI therapy. Contrary to expectations, recent research from Cheng et al. contests this hypothesis, revealing that PD-1 blockade shows promising clinical activity in patients presenting with multiple GGNs [22]. This discrepancy between theory and clinical observation implies that the response of GGN-featured lung cancers to ICIs may involve more complex mechanisms, and their potential therapeutic efficacy requires further validation in prospective studies.

Therefore, we designed a single-arm, phase II prospective exploratory study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of tislelizumab in MPLC patients who presented with synchronous multifocal GGNs. This study explored the long-term efficacy of immunotherapy in this cohort and analyzed its impact on the TIME.

Materials and methods

Study oversight

This study is a single arm, phase II prospective clinical trial. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04047186. All participants provided written informed consent before treatment, and this trial adhered to all relevant ethical considerations. This study was also conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013).

Patients

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Chest CT scans revealing two or more GGN lesions in the lungs, with at least one lesion pathologically confirmed (surgical resection or biopsy) as NSCLC; (2) at least one unresected GGN lesion with a diameter of 8 mm–3 cm, assessed unanimously by a multidisciplinary team (MDT; the team was made up of oncologists, radiologists, and pathologists) as highly suggestive of malignancy; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1; (4) age ≥ 18 years; and (5) adequate organ function. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) preoperative assessment indicating distant metastasis; (2) immunodeficiency, active autoimmune disease, a history of autoimmune disorders, or current systemic immunosuppressive therapy; (3) treatment with radiotherapy or chemotherapy; (4) a history of malignancy within the past 5 years; (5) pregnancy or lactation; (6) patients deemed ineligible by the investigator.

Treatment and endpoints

After the baseline assessment, the enrolled patients were administered tislelizumab (200 mg i.v., Q3W), with all patients completing at least 2 and up to 4 treatment cycles. During treatment, all patients underwent chest CT scans every 2 cycles. Tumor response was assessed by CT scan at approximately 12 weeks (range, 77–91 days) following initiation of immunotherapy. Treatment will be discontinued if any of the following conditions occur: disease progression confirmed upon Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) review; occurrence of intolerable treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs); clinically significant deterioration of the patient’s general condition; voluntary withdrawal of consent by the patient. Upon treatment completion, investigators comprehensively considered surgical tolerance, patient preference (conservative vs. surgical management), and lesion aggressiveness (nodule size, solid component, etc.) to formulate subsequent therapeutic strategies. Accordingly, a secondary surgery was scheduled to be performed within 4–6 weeks after completion of immunotherapy. All patients underwent CT reexamination regularly from the treatment completion. During follow-up, surveillance chest CT scans were performed at 3 months intervals during the first year, with subsequent evaluations conducted every 6 months. The primary endpoint was the objective response rate (ORR), which included complete response (CR) and partial response (PR). Secondary endpoints included major pathological response (MPR), pathological complete response (pCR), and safety profiles monitored through 3 months after final drug administration. Exploratory analyses included assessments of lesion-specific immunological reactions to tislelizumab and treatment-induced alterations in the TIME.

Study design

We used a Simon’s optimal two-stage design to assess the ORR as the primary endpoint. The null hypothesis was an ORR of 10% versus the alternative ORR of 30%. The null hypothesis ORR of 10% was selected based on historically reported response rates. The alternative hypothesis ORR of 30% was chosen to represent a clinically meaningful improvement threshold. A Simon’s two-stage design, optimal version, was applied. Type I (α) and type II (β) error rates were set at 0.05 and 0.2, respectively. Accordingly, 12 patients were enrolled in the first stage. If none of them responded, the trial was terminated; if one or more patients responded, 23 patients were subsequently enrolled in the second stage.

Radiological and pathological evaluations

All enrolled patients underwent chest CT scans within 1 month before enrollment as a baseline assessment. Changes in tumor size were evaluated by two experienced clinical radiologists. Given the significant inter-lesion heterogeneity in MPLC, where different lesions within the same patient can exhibit divergent treatment responses, a simple patient-level aggregation based on the sum of diameters may obscure important biological phenomena. To accurately capture this complexity, based on RECIST v1.1, we implemented a modified, two-step response evaluation system. Firstly, each target lesion was independently categorized as follows: (1) CR: Disappearance of the lesion; (2) PR: A decrease ≥ 30% in the longest diameter. (3) Stable Disease (SD): A change in the longest diameter between − 30% and + 20%. (4) Progressive Disease (PD): An increase ≥ 20% in the longest diameter with an absolute increase of > 5 mm. Then the overall response for each patient was determined by synthesizing the lesion-level assessments according to the following rules: (1) CR: Disappearance of all target lesions. (2) PR: At least one lesion is rated as PR or CR, with the remaining lesions being SD. (3) SD: All target lesions are rated as SD. (4) PD: Defined by any of the following: The appearance of new lesions. At least one target lesion was rated as PD, provided all other lesions were SD. In the scenario where PR/CR and PD co-existed (with other lesions as SD), the maximum diameters of only the PR/CR and PD lesions were summed. The patient was then rated as PD if this sum increased by ≥ 20% with an absolute increase > 5 mm; as PR if the sum decreased by ≥ 30%; otherwise, the overall response was designated as SD. All patients who underwent primary tumor resection before enrollment had baseline tumor staging performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition criteria, including histological diagnosis and pathological evaluation of mediastinal lymph nodes. Pathological response evaluation was performed by two specialized pathologists experienced in ICI therapeutic assessment. The residual viable tumor percentage in the lesions was quantified through conventional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. MPR was defined as the point when viable tumor cells constituted ≤ 10% of the lesion.

Multiplex immunofluorescence detection

Consecutive 4 μm thick sections were cut from FFPE tissue blocks and placed on glass slides for subsequent experiments. Slides were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated by washes with decreasing concentrations of ethanol, and then processed for antigen retrieval through boiling in citrate antigen retrieval buffer (pH 6.0). The slides were blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. Sections were stained with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and then incubated with species-specific HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 min at room temperature. The corresponding tyramide signal amplification (TSA) reagent (G1256, Servicebio, China) was added for a 10 min reaction at room temperature, protected from light, followed by thorough washing with Tris-buffered saline and tween (TBST). After antigen‒antibody dissociation by boiling in citrate antigen retrieval buffer (pH 6.0), the antibody incubation process was repeated for multiplex labeling. DAPI (G1012; Servicebio, China) was used for nuclear counterstaining for 5 min, followed by washing with PBS and mounting with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting reagent (G1401; Servicebio, China). A total of 11 biomarkers were detected, including CD8, CD4, FOXP3, PD-1, and CD103 (panel 1); CD19, CD68, PD-L1, HLA-DR, and CYTOKERATIN (panel 2); and CD68, CD163, and HLA-DR (panel 3).

Primary antibodies against the following proteins were used: CD8 (GB12068, 1:1000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), CD4 (GB13588, 1:1000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), FOXP3 (GB112325, 1:5000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), PD-1 (GB15338, 1:5000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), CD103 (AB224202, 1:5000 dilution; Abcam, MA, USA), CD19 (GB15061, 1:5000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), CD68 (GB113150, 1:4000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), PD-L1 (HA721176, 1:200 dilution; Hangzhou, China), HLA-DR (AB92511, 1:3000 dilution; Abcam, MA, USA), CYTOKERATIN (GB152051, 1:5000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and CD163 (GB113150, 1:2000 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The secondary antibodies used were goat anti-mouse IgG (HRP) (GB23301, 1:500 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (HRP) (GB23303, 1:500 dilution; Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The slides were scanned with Pannoramic MIDI (3DHISTECH, Hungary), after which two pathologists (S.Q. and J.X.) used 3DHISTECH-PATTERNQUANT software to select three regions of interest (ROIs) for each slide to analyze the proportion of positive cells (number of positive cells/total cell count). All image analyses were performed by a pathologist who was blinded to patient outcomes.

Evaluation of tertiary lymphoid structures

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) were morphologically assessed and quantified using H&E-stained specimens with a thickness of 4 µm. These sections were scanned to generate whole-slide images (WSIs). TLSs were defined as lymphoid aggregates containing vessels with high endothelial venule features that could be present with or without germinal centers. Two independent observers manually evaluated and annotated the abundance of TLSs within the intratumoral regions on the WSIs [21, 23].

T cell receptor sequencing

Genomic DNA from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples was extracted using a Universal Genomic DNA Kit (CWBio, China). The concentration of the genomic DNA was measured using a Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Only samples with a concentration > 20 ng/μl were used for library preparation. The third complementarity-determining region (CDR3) of the TCRβ chain was specifically amplified through multiplex PCR for library construction. Library sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, USA) generating 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads by SEQHealth (China). Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and processed using SOAPnuke (v1.6.0), and the resulting clean reads were used for TCR repertoire analysis. The processed reads were aligned with the IMGT reference database using MiXCR (v3.0.3) for V (D)J gene segment identification and CDR3 region annotation [24]. TCRβ clonotypes were defined as unique combinations of the V gene, J gene, and the nucleotide sequence of the CDR3 region.

Whole exome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE samples and whole blood control samples using the TIANamp FFPE DNA Kit and TIANamp Blood DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China), respectively. Genomic DNA was fragmented into 180–280 bp fragments by Covaris LE220R plus (Covaris, USA). Afterward, the DNA fragments were end repaired and phosphorylated, followed by A-tailing and ligation at the 3′ ends with paired-end adapters. Adapter-ligated fragments were then enriched by PCR amplification. Size selection was carried out by the Agencourt SPRIselect kit (Beckman Coulter, USA) to isolate fragments of the desired size. Libraries were then subjected to exome capture using an Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit (Agilent, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Postcapture amplification was performed to enrich the captured fragments. The final enriched exome libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, USA) with 150 bp paired-end reads, achieving a mean coverage depth of approximately 150 × for all the samples.

After adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed, the remaining high-quality reads were aligned to the reference genome (hg19) using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA, v0.7.17), generating BAM files [25]. Variant calling was performed with GATK (v4.3.0) to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and Insertions/deletions (InDels). The frequency, number, types (including the transition/transversion ratio), and genotype distribution of SNPs and InDels in different genomic regions, particularly coding regions, were analyzed. Somatic variants, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs), InDels, and copy number variations (CNVs), were detected using Mutect2 (v4.3.0) and Control-FREEC (v11.4), respectively [26, 27]. The resulting variant call format (VCF) files were annotated using ANNOVAR (2023Feb1) [28].

Statistical analysis

This study is an exploratory preliminary study that enrolled 12 patients. Descriptive statistical methods were used to analyze the baseline characteristics, efficacy, and safety data of the patients. Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed with SPSS V.25.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Results

Patient characteristics

In this single-arm phase II prospective clinical trial (NCT04047186), the efficacy and safety of tislelizumab in patients with MPLC presenting as synchronous multifocal GGNs was evaluated. From March 2020 to July 2023, 12 eligible patients were ultimately enrolled (Fig. 1a). All patients received at least two doses of tislelizumab. The detailed treatment courses and follow-up status of all the enrolled patients are presented in Fig. 1b. The median age of the enrolled patients was 56.5 years (range 39–69), with 10 (83%) being female. All the patients had no smoking history. Dominant lesions were surgically resected and pathologically confirmed as adenocarcinoma in 11 patients, while biopsy-confirmed adenocarcinoma was confirmed in 1 patient; Thus, the group comprised 2 adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) patients, 3 minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) patients, and 7 invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC) patients. Among the 11 patients who underwent surgical resection for primary lesions, sublobar resection was performed in 9 (81.8%), and lobectomy was performed in 2 (11.8%). Representative radiological and pathological features of the dominant lesions are presented in Fig. 2. EGFR testing was performed on primary lesions in 6 patients, with 5 testing positive for EGFR mutations. Finally, 30 unresected GGNs from 12 patients constituted the ITT lesions, with a mean size of 10.97 ± 2.61 mm, of which 26 (86.7%) were pure GGNs. Efficacy and safety

As of December 31, 2024, all 12 patients were evaluated for treatment efficacy. Radiological assessment demonstrated that none of the 30 ITT GGNs achieved a CR or PR, resulting in an objective response rate (ORR) of 0%. Disease progression (PD) occurred in one GGN of one patient and was characterized by enlargement of the lesion diameter and an increased solid component proportion. Another patient developed a new GGN during follow-up. The remaining 10 patients maintained SD, resulting in a disease control rate (DCR) of 83.3%. As of December 31, 2024, the median follow-up time was 21.5 months (range 14.8–35.5 months). Notably, the newly emerged GGN lesion resolved spontaneously after 4 months of follow-up. No radiological progression was observed in the remaining patients during the posttreatment follow-up period. Secondary surgery was performed on 3 patients, resulting in the resection of 7 ITT lesions. Postoperative pathology revealed 2 cases of IA, 2 cases of MIA, and 3 cases of AIS. The median interval from the last dose of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to secondary surgery was 5 weeks (range, 4–6). Pathological evaluation revealed a MPR rate of 0% and a pCR rate of 0% in these patients. Detailed information regarding the secondary surgeries is presented in Table 2. In the first stage, we successfully accrued 12 evaluable patients. Among these 12 patients, no objective responses were observed (ORR = 0%). As this result (0 responses) fell strictly below the prespecified threshold of ≥ 1 responses required to proceed, the study was halted early for futility as per the statistical plan.

Representative case illustrations

To illustrate the heterogeneity in the response to immunotherapy, three typical cases were identified on the basis of imaging assessment. Patient 1 was a 57-year-old female who, after receiving two doses of ICIs, showed progression of a pGGN lesion in the right lower lobe on CT. The lesion increased in diameter from 16.3 to 58.9 mm and evolved into a solid nodule (Fig. 3a). The patient subsequently underwent secondary surgery. However, pathological analysis revealed > 80% tumor regression with extensive foam cell infiltration and inflammatory cell influx (predominantly lymphocytes). Concurrently, abscess formation was observed. Lymphocytic infiltration was also noted in the tumor stroma. Therefore, this lesion was diagnosed as pseudoprogression (PsP). Patient 2 was a 57-year-old female whose ITT lesions were stable after four doses of ICIs. After 9 months of follow-up, a new 7.4-mm GGN emerged in the left lower lobe. The GGNs regressed spontaneously after an additional 4 months of surveillance (Fig. 3b). Patient 3 was a 67-year-old female whose ITT lesions were stable after two doses of immunotherapy. No radiological progression was observed during the subsequent follow-up (Fig. 3c). In our study, we also performed immunohistochemical staining for PD-L1 on biopsies and resected lesions from 12 patients. According to the PD-L1 score, we described the PD-L1 status of the corresponding tumors of 12 patients. Among all patients, the dominant lesion was PD-L1 negative in 11 patients (11/12, 91.7%). mIF and TLSs

Clarifying the cellular composition is essential for understanding the mechanisms of PsP and its effects on antitumor immunity. Therefore, we performed mIF on one PsP lesion and six SD lesions to quantify the proportions of infiltrating immune cells. The following components were visualized through staining with Panel I: CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, PD-1+ cells, Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), and CD103+ tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) (Fig. 4a). Panel II targeted CD19+ B cells, HLA-DR+ cells, cytokeratin + tumor cells, PD-L1+ cells, and CD68+ macrophages (Fig. 4b). mIF analysis revealed that compared with the SD lesions, the PsP lesions were infiltrated by significantly more CD8+ T cells. This pattern is consistent with previous observations in responsive lesions. In contrast to lesions that responded, this PsP lesion simultaneously demonstrated greater infiltration of Foxp3+ CD4+ Tregs and CD19+ B lymphocytes.

TCR-seq and WES

As critical mediators of antitumor immunity, T cells can be interrogated through TCR-seq to gain a mechanistic understanding of immune responses. TCR-seq analysis of posttreatment lesions revealed a dominant clonotype in the PsP lesion, constituting 8.4% of the total TCR repertoire in this specimen. In comparison, the most abundant clonotype in the SD lesions was significantly less represented (mean ± SD: 3.7% ± 1.5%; Fig. 5a). Intriguingly, the PsP lesion maintained low clonality and high diversity despite possessing this dominant clonotype, indicating potentially divergent T-cell response dynamics in PsP lesions (Fig. 5b, c). Immune repertoire overlap assessment in patients undergoing resection of multiple lesions after ICI therapy revealed that the frequency of shared TCR clonotypes was significantly greater than the ratio of corresponding TCR clonotypes (Fig. 5d).

Discussion

Recent advances in immunotherapy have significantly transformed the therapeutic paradigm for lung cancer [29]. However, our understanding of the applicability of this therapeutic strategy to MPLC, particularly for patients who present with synchronous multifocal GGNs, remains limited. This single-center prospective study was designed to assess the efficacy and safety profile of tislelizumab in this MPLC cohort. These findings revealed a limited therapeutic response in patients with GGN lesions to PD-1 blockade, accompanied by substantial heterogeneity across patients. The majority of patients maintained SD. One patient developed pathologically confirmed PsP, and another patient exhibited new-onset GGN during follow-up. Safety analysis indicated that all adverse events were Grade 1–2. No grade 3–5 TRAEs or novel safety concerns were observed.

Immunotherapy for MPLC is under intensive investigation. A phase I trial by Xu et al. reported controllable toxicity profiles for ICIs in MPLC patients, although tumor size alterations were insignificant [30]. Cheng et al. reported promising clinical activity and disease control with ICIs, with an overall ORR of 13.9% (5/36). No new lesions emerged in any patient, but only two GGNs achieved a response (2/49) [22]. In a prior observational study, Wu et al. reported that patients with synchronous multifocal GGNs exhibit poor sensitivity to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [31]. Zhang et al. described an MPLC patient treated with ICIs whose remaining GGNs showed marked shrinkage of solid nodules but a limited response [32]. In our study, none of the 30 ITT lesions demonstrated significant shrinkage. These findings suggest that multifocal GGNs may be intrinsically insensitive to PD-1 inhibitors.

This study is the first to report the phenomenon of PsP in pGGN lesions following PD-1 inhibitor treatment. Prior research demonstrates that patients exhibiting PsP derive significantly greater survival benefits than those with typical progression in advanced-stage lung cancer [33]. However, the clinical significance of this phenomenon for early-stage lung cancer patients still requires further investigation. Notably, pGGNs predominantly exhibit pathological subtypes of AIS or MIA, both of which are associated with exceptionally favorable prognoses. Owing to their indolent biological behavior, these lesions are typically managed with conservative approaches including active surveillance or sublobar resection [34]. Nevertheless, the challenge in distinguishing PsP from true progression substantially complicates clinical management decisions [35]. The PsP reported in this study caused abnormal enlargement of the lesion volume, ultimately necessitating lobectomy. This treatment escalation caused irreversible damage to the patient’s pulmonary function. For patients with severe underlying diseases, this situation significantly intensifies the pressure of clinical decision-making.

To explore the impact of ICIs on the TIME and further understand the mechanism of PsP, we conducted additional studies on resected lesions posttreatment. mIF analysis revealed significantly greater CD8+ T-cell infiltration in PsP lesions than in SD lesions, mirroring the infiltration pattern observed in responsive lesions [22]. Several studies have demonstrated better OS in patients with PsP than in those with PD or SD, suggesting that elevated CD8+ T-cell density within the tumor microenvironment serves as a robust biomarker for positive immunotherapy outcomes [36]. Additionally, PsP lesions also exhibit a greater TLS distribution. However, PsP lesions appear to have increased infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, such as Foxp3+ CD4+ Treg cells and CD68+ CD163+ M2-like macrophages [37, 38]. Our observations reveal the spatial coexistence of antitumor immune cells and immunosuppressive populations, suggesting that the TIME maintains a highly dynamic balance under treatment-induced selective pressure. This phenomenon may reflect adaptive immune resistance following early immune activation: as ICIs enhance T-cell function and elicit tumor killing, persistent immune activity promotes the release of suppressive factors, including CCL22, IL-10, and TGF-β, which facilitate the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells and ultimately attempt to restore a tumor-promotive microenvironment. This coexistent immune landscape suggests that immunotherapy does not unidirectionally increase antitumor immunity but instead drives the TIME toward a more intricate, newly configured state of dynamic equilibrium [21]. Notably, compared with previously reported findings, PsP lesions demonstrate a strikingly abundant infiltration of M2-like macrophages, suggesting their potentially critical role in PsP development. Furthermore, this coexistent state of immune activation and suppression may be time-dependent. The current detection and surgical resection may have coincided with a particular dynamic temporal phase, so we cannot exclude the possibility that this observation represents a transient event. Nevertheless, the eventual trajectory of this equilibrium state remains unclear and cannot be conclusively determined. Thus, the clinical implications of PsP in patients receiving immunotherapy warrant additional extensive research.

The low tumor mutation burden (TMB) commonly observed in GGN-featured lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) results in markedly diminished neoantigen production and presentation. This impairment in turn inadequately activates antigen-presenting cells and subsequently fails to initiate an effective antitumor immune response. More importantly, GGN lesions exhibit significantly reduced infiltration of key effector cells, such as cytotoxic T cells and helper T cells, coupled with the downregulation of pathways associated with antitumor immunity. Since the primary mechanism of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) involves dismantling the suppression of preexisting yet inhibited T-cell function, this immune status substantially undermines the foundation for ICI efficacy. Additionally, in contrast to that in solid nodules, the Th1/Th2 immune balance in GGN-related lung adenocarcinoma tends to shift toward a Th2-polarized response, indicating a heightened propensity for immunotolerance and adaptive immune resistance [16]. This shift likely constitutes a key mechanism underlying the poor responsiveness of GGNs to immune checkpoint blockade therapy.

T-cell activation critically governs the clinical efficacy of immunotherapy. Clinical evidence has demonstrated that increased T-cell receptor (TCR) clonality strongly correlates with enhanced antitumor immunity and superior therapeutic outcomes [39]. In the field of cancer immunotherapy, high TCR clonality often indicates that the immune system actively recognizes and attacks tumor cells, thus potentially achieving effective tumor control or cure. Conversely, low clonality may suggest insufficient immune response or that tumor cells have undergone immune escape [40]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. demonstrated that responsive lesions displayed reduced T-cell receptor (TCR) diversity. Concurrently, Cheng et al. reported that diminished TCR diversity in peripheral blood is correlated with enhanced clinical benefit from immune checkpoint inhibition [22]. This study revealed that PsP lesions demonstrate low TCR clonality coupled with high diversity. Analysis of the TCR-seq data revealed that although the most predominant clonotypes accounted for a high proportion in this sample, there were also numerous CDR3 clonotypes with extremely small proportions. Although the proportion of each of these clonotypes was low, their vast quantity significantly increased the Shannon entropy of the immune repertoire, reflecting the high complexity of the immune response and potential immune activation status. Notably, a TCR repertoire overlap analysis revealed significant enrichment of shared clonotypes, indicating their critical function in antitumor immunity following immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy [41].

Posttherapeutic histopathological analysis confirmed malignancy in all seven resected ITT lesions. These findings suggest a high likelihood of malignancy in multifocal GGN lesions. Therefore, we recommend adopting more proactive monitoring strategies. Notably, the PsP lesions were EGFR mutation negative, whereas all five SD patients demonstrated EGFR mutation positivity. These findings indicate that patients with EGFR mutation-positive tumors benefit less from immunotherapy, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [42]. Consistent with the findings of Liu et al., PD-L1 negativity was also commonly observed in GGN lesions, suggesting it as a key underlying mechanism for their poor response to immunotherapy [43]. Collectively, our findings suggest that GGN lesions demonstrate a limited therapeutic response to PD-1 inhibitors and carry risks of PsP. Therefore, extra caution is warranted when considering immune checkpoint inhibitor-based therapies for patients with multifocal GGN lesions. Currently, surgical treatment remains the primary therapeutic approach for MPLC, with demonstrated therapeutic efficacy [15, 44]. For patients intolerant to surgery, particularly those with early-stage disease, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) serves as an alternative radical local treatment option because of its favorable disease control and minimally invasive nature [45]. Given the risk of irreversibly compromised pulmonary function associated with extensive or repeated resections, combined surgery and SBRT may be a more advantageous treatment strategy, though this integrated strategy warrants comprehensive clinical validation.

Despite these encouraging findings, our study has several limitations. First, as a single-center, single-arm exploratory study, the sample size was relatively small, which limits the generalizability of our findings to larger, more diverse patient populations. Furthermore, the absence of a randomized controlled design restricts our ability to make definitive causal inferences. Although our study revealed a unique TIME profile in PsP lesions, these exploratory analyses are limited by the limited cohort size. Consequently, while this study offers novel insights into the immunobiology of PsP, these observations necessitate further validation in larger, independent patient cohorts. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up period may have compromised the reliability of the results to some extent. Given these limitations, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution, and further validation through prospective, randomized, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes is needed.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this prospective study revealed that patients with synchronous multifocal GGNs exhibit limited responsiveness to tislelizumab therapy. Furthermore, we observed that ICIs may induce rare instances of PsP in pGGN lesions. Critically, our findings delineate the distinct TIME and TCR repertoire characteristics associated with this PsP phenomenon.
