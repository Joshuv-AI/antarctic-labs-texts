A G3P[3] bat rotavirus can infect cultured human cholangiocytes and cause biliary atresia symptom in suckling mice
Authors: Pengfei Hao, Zhaoxia Pang, Qiaoqiao Qu, Chunmei Cui, Yuhang Jiang, Jing Chen, Zihan Gao, Zhiqiang Xu, Letian Li, Ningyi Jin, Chang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Bat rotavirus strain MSLH14 (G3P[3]) can infect human cholangiocytes in vitro.•MSLH14 can cause suckling mice biliary atresia symptom, indicating potential public health risks.•MSLH14 have the potential for establishing a biliary atresia animal model.

DearEditor,

Rotavirus (RV), a member of the Reoviridae family, is non-enveloped virus with a genome consisting of 11 segments of double-stranded RNA. It possesses a three-layered shell structure, and can be classified into types A–L based on the VP6 serotype, with species A being the most common in mammals. Apart from causing severe diarrhea in infants and young animals, rotavirus is also identified as a potential cause of biliary atresia, a serious condition characterized by bile duct obstruction, leading to major complications such as growth retardation, jaundice, cirrhosis, liver failure, and death (Wang et al., 2020). Several strains, such as rhesus rotavirus (RRV), have been demonstrated to induce biliary atresia symptoms in BALB/c mice (Mohanty et al., 2020). Additionally, orthoreovirus type 3, another member of the Reoviridae family, has been identified as a causative agent of biliary atresia (Averbukh and Wu, 2018).

MSLH14, a species A rotavirus isolated from bat, was found to induce diarrhea symptoms in Kunming mice (He et al., 2013). Similar bat strains (MSLH14-like) have also been found in humans, as the first bat rotavirus induced diarrhea in humans (He et al., 2017). The MSLH14 strain is a G3P[3] rotavirus, sharing the same G/P type as RRV. Intragastric infection experiments showed that this strain can induce diarrhea symptoms in BALB/c mice and lead to pathological changes in intestine (Supplementary Fig. S1A, S1B). Sequence analysis on the attachment protein of rotavirus (VP4) revealed that the VP4 of MSLH14 contains the Serine-Arginine-Leucine (SRL) peptide (Supplementary Fig. S1C), which is essential for rotavirus infection in biliary epithelial cells (Mohanty et al., 2017). Hence, we wondered whether this bat rotavirus could also trigger biliary atresia symptoms in addition to diarrhea.

We first assessed the infectivity of MSLH14 in human intrahepatic biliary epithelial cells (HIBECs), a commonly used in vitro model for bile duct related research (Meng et al., 2012). The results indicated that MSLH14 could infect HIBECs (Fig. 1A and B). In contrast, MYAS33 (Xia et al., 2013), a bat rotavirus sharing similar viral protein classification with MSLH14 except for VP4, was unable to infect HIBECs. Further investigations revealed that Serine-Arginine-Leucine-Tyrosine (SRLY) synthetic peptide could inhibit MSLH14 infection (Fig. 1C and D). Next, we investigated the viral infection characteristics of MSLH14 strain in cholangiocytes in vivo. One-week-old BALB/c mice were intraperitoneally infected with the virus, and their body weight, survival rate and condition were monitored daily. The results showed that the BALB/c mice gained weight slowly or lost weight compared to the non-challenged group (Fig. 1E). All challenged mice died within 13 days post-infection (Fig. 1F). During this period, oily fur, a typical symptom of biliary atresia, were observed (Fig. 1G). Postmortem examination revealed mucosal yellowing (Fig. 1H), indicating potential biliary obstruction in mice. Immunofluorescence assays showed that the infectivity of this virus strain to cholangiocytes (Fig. 1I). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining was used to analyze the pathological changes. Multifocal hepatocyte necrosis and dissolution were observed in the liver tissue, with increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia and disordered arrangement of hepatocytes. Focal necrotic fragments were common, and connective tissue hyperplasia was observed around numerous portal areas, accompanied by extensive lymphocyte infiltration (Fig. 1J). Additionally, shedding of mucosal epithelial cells were observed in the bile duct tissue; many mucosal sinus epithelial cells were degenerated, with diluted and lightly stained cytoplasm; a small amount of lymphocyte infiltration was seen in the lamina propria (Fig. 1K). Collectively, these data suggest that this G3P[3] bat rotavirus can infect mouse biliary epithelial cells in vivo.

To comprehensively assess changes in indicators related to biliary atresia, an enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis of mouse serum was performed. The results revealed that the concentrations of matrix metalloproteinase 7 (MMP7), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), key proteins associated with biliary atresia (Mohanty et al., 2017; Lertudomphonwanit et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2018; Thomas, 2018), were significantly elevated (Fig. 1L–O). Notably, the specific diagnostic marker MMP7 (Yang et al., 2018; Thomas, 2018) exhibited an approximately ten-fold increase in serum, mirroring the fold change observed in human biliary atresia (Jiang et al., 2019). Furthermore, we analyzed other important differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in mouse by qPCR, paralleling human biliary atresia (Mohanty et al., 2020). The results showed a noteworthy upregulation of these genes in infected mice samples, including amphiregulin (AREG), C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), thrombospondin 1 (THBS1), interleukin 6 (IL-6), integrin subunit alpha 2 (ITGA2), laminin subunit gamma 2 (LAMC2), MMP7, serpin family E member 1 (SERINE1), versican (VCAN) (Fig. 1P).

This study reveals that rotavirus strain MSLH14 (G3P[3]) isolated from bats can infect human cholangiocytes in vitro and induce biliary atresia symptoms in BALB/c mice. This comprehensive investigation sheds light on the pathogenicity of MSLH14 in inducing biliary obstruction in a murine model, suggesting the potential for establishing a biliary atresia animal model. In summary, this work demonstrated that a bat rotavirus can induce biliary atresia symptoms in mice, highlighting potential public health risks.
