Sex Differences and Survival Among COPD Patients in France: The Palomb Cohort
Authors: Nolwenn Jestin-Guyon, El Hassane Ouaalaya, Alain Bernady, Emilie Berteaud, Zamira Betancourt-Rial, Jean Moinard, Laurent Falque, Frédéric Le Guillou, Mathieu Molimard, Laurent Nguyen, Cécilia Nocent, Annaig Ozier, Mohammed Staali, Chantal Raherison-Semjen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePrevious studies suggest that the clinical presentation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is different between men and women. The aim of this study was to investigate sex-related differences and survival among COPD patients.Patients and MethodsMulticentric prospective real-life cohort. COPD patients (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.7) of any stages of airway obstruction recruited since January 2014 by pulmonologists. Stratified logistic regression models assessed to identify the determinants of dyspnea, exacerbations, ABE classification and all-cause mortality by sex. Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional-hazards models were also performed.ResultsA total of 3265 patients included, with 36.7% women. Women had an increased risk of ≥2 exacerbations (aOR=1.79; 95%CI[1.46–2.19]), mMRC grade 1 (aOR=1.46; 95%CI[1.14–1.89]) and grade 2 (aOR=1.41; 95%CI[1.07–1.87]) and a higher risk of severity E of the ABE classification (aOR=1.56; 95%CI[1.27–1.93]). In stratified models, the determinants of these outcomes were different. Men had a lower survival rate at GOLD airway obstruction I–II (Log-rank: p<0.0001) and III–IV (Log-rank: p<0.0001) and a higher risk of mortality (HR=1.62; 95%CI[1.40–1.87]). Common factors characterizing mortality were: age, BMI <18.5, mMRC ≥ 2, airway obstruction, diabetes and lung cancer. Additional factors related to mortality in men were asthma, left heart failure and tobacco-related cancers.ConclusionCOPD is more severe in women however men have a higher risk of mortality. Clinical features and mortality in men and women with COPD are different in our cohort. Further studies are needed to elucidate sex-related differences among COPD patients.Keywords: COPD, women, exacerbations, mortality

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the third leading cause of death worldwide, affecting men and women.1 Global projections estimate that by 2050 COPD prevalence will be 10.60% in men and 8.33% in women, with an increase number of women affected in low- and middle-income countries.2

The clinical picture of COPD is different between men and women. For equivalent stages of airway obstruction, women have an earlier onset of symptoms, more dyspnea, exacerbations, a different disease trajectory, adverse quality of life, anxiety, depression and osteoporosis, while men have more cough, sputum production and cardiovascular comorbidities.3 These differences can be explained biologically, such as sex hormones, and by multiple factors affecting lung throughout life.4 Women are also more vulnerable to tobacco and second-hand smoke.5,6 Moreover, the current and expected increasing prevalence of COPD among women is related to the increase in tobacco consumption, biomass exposure and occupational restructuring by gender (which has led women to work in fields traditionally occupied by men, known to be at risk of developing COPD).3,4,7

In France, COPD prevalence for 2025 is estimated around 10.6% and 6.7% respectively among men and women older than 45 years old, which represents nearly 2.8 million individuals.8 From 2000 to 2014, national indicators, as hospitalization for exacerbation and mortality, showed a trend toward an increasing burden of COPD among women thus approaching that of men.9 Furthermore, daily smoking prevalence in metropolitan France has largely declined since the last decades, particularly among men,9 however its current prevalence remains significant. In 2022, 27.4% of men and 21.7% of women aged between 18 and 75 years old smoked tobacco on daily basis.10

To our knowledge, a few studies were conducted in France to explore sex-related differences among COPD patients, as characteristics and mortality in the INITIATIVES BPCO real-life cohort11,12 and quality of life.13 Given the fact that COPD is no longer a disease only affecting men, a better understanding of sex-differences in COPD is essential to improve patients care. In addition, a deeper understanding of men and women profiles according to the recent ABE classification used for treatment prescription is necessary, given that we hypothesize that women experience a more symptomatic expression of COPD.14

The aim of this study was to investigate clinical and mortality differences among men and women diagnosed with COPD in a French real-life cohort. This work consists of describing the characteristics of men and women regarding clinical features, treatment prescription and comorbidities, and identifying factors associated with disease severity and mortality.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Population

The Palomb cohort is a real-life setting prospective multicentered study of COPD patients. Patients are recruited and followed-up since January 2014 by almost 30 pulmonologists (independent or hospital) from two western French regions (Aquitaine and Charentes). Throughout consultation, patients of any stages of airway obstruction with a spirometry test indicating a COPD diagnosis (forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <70% post-bronchodilator) based on the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) recommendations were included.15

Ethics Approval and Informed Consent

Prior to the initiation of The Palomb project, authorizations request to the National Commission on Informatics and Liberty (CNIL) and the Advisory Committee on Information Processing in Material Research in the Field of Health (CCTIRS) were submitted. Approvals were obtained from both the CNIL and the CCTIRS to implement the study. Patients were informed of study purpose and gave their informed consent. Individual data were anonymized.

Data Collection

At baseline, pulmonologists conducted face to face interviews. Inclusion and follow-up data were filled by pulmonologists themselves, associated clinical researchers or medical secretaries through a secure online questionnaire described previously.16 The following information were completed at baseline: sex, smoking status, symptoms, anthropometric features, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), comorbidities (based on patients’ medical data and treatment prescription) and prevention. COPD medication were also recorded: short-acting beta-2 agonists (SABA), short-acting muscarinic antagonists (SAMA), long-acting beta-2 agonists (LABA), long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA) and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS).

Variables Management

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated (BMI = weight (kg)/height2 (m)). Occupational exposure was obtained considering the following categories: agricultural work, foundry and steel industries, building and civil works, mining industry. Of the total 26 comorbidities (pleuro-pulmonary, cardiovascular, metabolic, osteo-articular, neuropsychiatric), tobacco-related cancers were obtained17,18 and lung cancer considered separately. Childhood and adulthood respiratory infections variables were created based on patients self-reported information. Standardized equations were calculated for FEV1% predicted (neutral ethnic group).19 Airway obstruction stages were obtained according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD): stage I (FEV1 ≥ 80%), stage II (50% ≤ FEV1 < 80%), stage III (30% ≤ FEV1 < 50%) and stage IV (FEV1 < 30%).20 The ABE classification was also created using the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale (mMRC) (0–1 vs ≥ 2), the number of exacerbations during the previous year (≥2) and hospitalization for exacerbation (information collected for the reason of the consultation).20 The definition of COPD exacerbation, as described previously, corresponded to an increase of respiratory symptoms, cough and/or sputum and/or dyspnea leading to additional prescription of treatments, antibiotics and/or systemic corticosteroids (data collected at inclusion and during follow-up).21

Vital status was updated on the 23th November 2023 from the national register for the identification of natural persons (RNIPP), as well as dates of death.

Statistical Analyzes

Descriptive analyses were made to compare men and women at baseline (included until the 8th of February 2024) and deceased. Appropriate tests were used: Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney test for quantitative variables and Chi-square or Fischer’s exact tests for qualitative variables.

Multivariable logistic regression models were performed to identify sex determinants of COPD-related outcomes. All variables with a significant p<0.05 in univariate analysis were first included in models. Then, a backward procedure for variables selection with Bonferroni correction for p-values was used.22,23 Exacerbations in the 12 months before inclusion ([reference: 0]: 1; ≥ 2), dyspnea mMRC ([reference: 0–1]: ≥ 2), ABE classification ([reference: A]: B; E) and mortality all-cause determinants were analyzed. Analyses were conducted in two steps: 1) among all patients included, to investigate specifically if sex was a determinant factor of COPD outcomes, 2) among men and women separately in stratified analysis.

Ten-years survival was analyzed between men and women. Kaplan–Meier curves and Log rank test were used to evaluate survival rates difference. Then, stratified Cox-proportional-hazards ratio models were performed. The analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4) and RStudio (version 4.3.1).

Women were younger (66.8 vs 64.2 years old), more current smokers (35.3% vs 45.5%), exposed to passive smoking (1.9% vs 3.3%) and had a lower BMI (<18.5) (3.3% vs 12.1%). Women had more dyspnea (mMRC ≥ 2) (55.0% vs 59.2%), frequent exacerbations (22.9% vs 31.3%) and were more in group E of the ABE classification (25.0% vs 34.0%). They also had more previous attempt to quit smoking (9.5% vs 14.0%) and received more help for smoking cessation (8.8% vs 12.1%).

Men were more ex-smokers (61.0% vs 47.3%), smoked more (40.2 vs 35.8 pack-years) and were more currently or previously (retreated) working in a field at risk of developing COPD (21.0% vs 3.7%). Men had more sputum (43.3% vs 37.6%) and were more likely to be vaccinated against influenza (45.0% vs 41.3%).

LABA + LAMA (p=0.494) and LABA + ICS (p=0.117) prescriptions were not different between sexes. Description of total prescription shows that more men of airway obstruction stages I–II had no treatment prescription when included (34.6% vs 28.6%), while women received more SABA/SAMA + ICS (p=0.012) and LABA/LAMA + ICS (p=0.011), see Supplementary Material 1. At airway obstruction stages III–IV, women received more SABA/SAMA + LABA (p=0.014) and SABA + SAMA + LABA + LAMA + ICS (p=0.012).

Men had more high blood pressure (40.0% vs 29.1%), dyslipidemia (26.1% vs 14.3%), ischemic heart disease (22.0% vs 7.4%), sleep apnea syndrome (21.0% vs 7.7%), diabetes (15.2% vs 7.3%), arrhythmia (13.3% vs 7.7%), lower limb arteriopathy (7.8% vs 4.1%), atheroma (6.8% vs 3.6%), metabolic syndrome (4.2% vs 1.7%) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (0.7% vs 0.2%) (Table 2). Women had more anxiety (10.8% vs 21.0%), depression (8.7% vs 21.1%), asthma (9.2% vs 13.1%), osteoporosis (1.2% vs 6.4%), denutrition (1.2% vs 3.3%) and osteodensitometry (0.4% vs 2.9%).

Exacerbations

Female sex was associated to a higher risk of ≥2 exacerbations (aOR=1.79; 95%CI[1.46–2.19]). However, sex was not observed as a determinant of 1 exacerbation, see Supplementary Material 2.

In stratified analyses (Table 3), men and women had no determinants in common for the risk of 1 exacerbation. Factors related to 1 exacerbation for men were: cough, dyspnea, decreasing FEV1% predicted and be more likely to be vaccinated against influenza. Among women, ex-smokers and smokers, more involved in physical activity and anxiety were factors characterizing 1 exacerbation profile.

All-Cause Mortality

A total of 1061 patients died since inclusion (32.5%). More men died (37.1%) compared to women (24.5%) and at older age (75.2 vs 73.6 years old). Characteristics at inclusion of men and women alive and deceased are presented in Supplementary Materials 5 and 6.

In logistic regression model, men had a higher risk of mortality compared to women (aOR=1.95; 95%CI[1.62–2.35]), see Supplementary Material 7.

Median follow-up time was slightly longer in women (71.6 vs 76.1 months). Men had a lower survival rate at any stages of airway obstruction (52.5% vs 66.1%; Log-rank: p<0.0001), see Supplementary Material 8. At airway obstruction stages I and II (58.0% vs 71.6%; Log-rank: p<0.0001) and stages III and IV at inclusion (36.4% vs 52.7%; Log-rank: p<0.0001) men also had lower survival rates (Figure 1).

Discussion

This study investigates clinical differences among 3265 COPD men and women in a French real-life cohort. Women had a higher risk of frequent exacerbations, mMRC grades 1 and 2 and belonging to group E of the ABE classification. However, sex was not a determinant of 1 exacerbation, mMRC ≥2 and group B. Men had lower survival rates at different airway obstruction stages and a higher risk of mortality. In all stratified models, the determinants associated to the studied outcomes were different between men and women.

As observed in the literature, women were younger, more current smokers or non-smokers and men smoked more.13 Smoking is known to be more intense among COPD men.24–30 Nevertheless, beyond the statistical difference, tobacco consumption was relatively high among women in our study.

Women had a more pronounced airway obstruction, with differences of 3.2%, 3.9% and 6.2% of FEV1% predicted compared to men at inclusion, alive and deceased respectively. Conversely, in the study of Raherison et al, FEV1% predicted was lower in men.13 The smoking habits of women is the main factor explaining this pronounced airway obstruction. Furthermore, sex hormones potentially impact women’s lung, especially during and after menopausal transition,31 with an increased effect among those with a lower BMI.32

Consistent with French literature, we found a lower BMI in women11–13 and a higher proportion of men exposed to an occupation at risk.12 Women more exposed to passive smoking were also found in another study, but only in-home context, while the authors found that men were more exposed to passive smoking in general and at workplace.24

In our study, women had more previous attempt and support to quit smoking, which was also observed in multivariable analyses.33

In accordance with the literature, men had more cardiovascular comorbidities, such as ischemic heart disease,11,13 dyslipidemia13 and women had more anxiety11–13 and depression.13 As in the ARCTIC cohort, no sex-differences were found for lung cancer prevalence,34 despite a trend close to significance for lung cancer in deceased patients in our study.

Younger women had a higher risk of frequent exacerbations in our study. In a previous publication on the Palomb cohort, sex was not contributing to frequent exacerbations among 932 patients.21 This variation can be explained by the on-going recruitment of patients in our cohort. However, this finding was consistent with other studies, regarding a shorter time to first exacerbation in women29,34 of younger age and of different COPD severity stages.35,36 Anxiety was identified as a determinant of frequent exacerbations in both sexes, despite a higher prevalence of it in women. Anxiety is known to trigger and increase the frequency of exacerbations. Otherwise, blood eosinophils counts, air pollution or bacteria can explain frequent exacerbations,37 but these information were not collected in our study.

No sex-differences were observed in our study for dyspnea mMRC ≥2, while female sex was identified in a previous work on the Palomb cohort. The greater risks observed in sensitive analyses for mMRC grades 1 and 2 in women are somehow consistent with this previous publication.38

Women in our cohort had a higher risk of being classified in group E. In another study, women under 65 years old had more risk of belonging to groups B and D, while women older than 65 years old had a higher risk of belonging to group B.35 In our study, older age was associated to group B for both sexes, but was not a factor explaining group E. As observed for exacerbations and dyspnea, anxiety remains a determining risk factor of belonging to groups B and E in men. Depression and sputum in women were associated to group E. Sputum is known to be a more common symptom in men3 and was also found more prevalent among them in the 3CIA study.25 Nevertheless, depression and sputum were associated to altered quality of life in COPD women in the study of Raherison et al, which shows the relation between impaired quality of life and disease severity.13

Consistent with previous studies, men had a lower survival rate25,29,39 and a higher risk of mortality.25,34,39 Lower survival rate11 and higher risk of mortality in men29 were not found respectively in the INITIATIVES BPCO and TORCH studies.

In our cohort, older age, lower BMI, dyspnea and airway obstruction were notable factors associated with mortality in both sexes.40

Women with airway obstruction grade IV had a considerable risk of mortality. In descriptive results (results not shown), women from this severity group were younger than those from airway obstruction grades II and III, were more previous smokers and had the highest tobacco consumption. Mortality among COPD women was also characterized by the presence of diabetes and lung cancer, whose contribution to mortality is substantial. Our results did not suggest a relation between mortality and cardiovascular comorbidities in women, as found previously.28,39,41 Prevention, early COPD diagnosis in women and lung cancer screening are key elements.

Mortality among COPD men was characterized by diabetes, left heart failure, tobacco-related cancers and lung cancer, with an inverse relation observed for asthma. Our results are aligned with other studies for diabetes41 and cardiovascular comorbidities.28,39 The result for cancers in general in our study was not found in the study of Li et al, for cancers or malignancy (men and women).39

Our study presents some strengths. The recruitment in a real-life context provides a real picture of COPD men and women. Furthermore, the information on more than 20 comorbidities was collected. Thus, some of our main findings are consistent with previous studies.

However, our study presents some limitations. Our study does not assess other major risk-factors for developing COPD, such as air pollution, both indoor and outdoor, biomass exposure or socio-economic factors. The information collected for occupation at risk and the variable created for occupational exposure do not allow for assessment and quantification of the risk of exposure to dust, vapors, irritating gases, smoke, or chemical agents such as pesticides. The Bonferroni correction used, mitigating type I error, is known to be more conservative. It is entirely possible that variables excluded in the stepwise selection procedure had a significant effect on the outcomes studied. Our analysis does not consider the time before the occurrence of a new or first exacerbation. Our study focuses on mortality all-cause but not on specific causes of death.

Conclusions

COPD is affecting men and women equally in France, but their clinical presentation of disease shows some differences. Women experienced a more severe COPD and men had a higher risk of mortality. Stratified analysis by sex should be considered for future investigations, in order to thoroughly identify the determinants that impact the disease, such as exacerbations triggers. It would also be essential to consider other risk factors related to lifestyle, previous bacterial or respiratory infections, the environment, the deprivation level and other social determinants of health to identify risk profiles of COPD in men and women. This would contribute to improving prevention, early diagnosis and tailored management strategies for COPD in both sexes. Finally, we recommend measures that aim to raise awareness and understanding of COPD in France, which from now on apply to both men and women.
