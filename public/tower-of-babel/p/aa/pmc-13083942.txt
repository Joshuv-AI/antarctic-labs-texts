Long period quakes linked to Mefite D’Ansanto (Italy) mantellic CO2 emissions
Authors: Paola Cusano, Simona Morabito, Simona Petrosino, Lucia Nardone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMefite D’Ansanto is a remarkable mantle origin CO2 emissions system, and the understanding of the relative source mechanisms is one of the fundamental aspects for the comprehension of the evolution of the southern Apennines active dynamics. Moreover, due to its lively activity in a nearly quiet place, Mefite also represents a natural laboratory for the investigation of hydrothermal system dynamics. The seismic wavefield sourced by these CO2 emissions is complex, characterized by a background stationary hydrothermal tremor, with intermittent superposition wavetrains of variable duration. Here we focus on the transient signals generated by the emissions, which show a spectral content in 0.7–7.0 Hz and a peculiar waveform, described here for the first time. Due to their similarities with Long Period earthquakes occurring in volcanic/hydrothermal systems, we classify them as LP quakes and investigate their propagation and source processes. The Mefite LPs appear to be a mixture of P- and surface waves, generated at a depth slightly greater than that of the hydrothermal tremor and likely associated with bubbling mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42564-2.Keywords: Long Period quakes, CO2 emissions, Mefite D’Ansanto, Crack model, Tomography, Wavefield analysisSubject terms: Natural hazards, Ocean sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The Mefite D’Ansanto gas emission area, in the southern Apennine chain (Italy), releases the largest quantity of low-temperature CO2 ever measured on Earth1,2. It is located in the Valle D’Ansanto, between the Sannio and Irpinia seismogenic regions (Fig. 1) that hosted large historical earthquakes, some of which were proved to be linked to the presence of fluids3. The emission area rests above the structural high of the NW-SE-trending Frigento Antiform and is likely fed by the buried reservoir hosted in this anticline, at the top of the Apulian Platform carbonates and sealed by clayey and calcareous marly sediments of Lagonegrese Units (Monte Forcuso 1 well data4. Nevertheless, fluids emitted at Mefite are of mantle origin, as testified by their isotopic signature2,3. The gas leakage at Mefite is supposed to be promoted by active normal faults that characterize the axial part of the southern Apennine chain2,5. At the surface the emission is characterized by venting and diffuse degassing and by the presence of a small bubbling lake.

In the top right inset a red square delimits the study area on Italian territory. The hydrothermal tremor linked to the Mefite gas emission has been recently studied by several authors to shed light on its source and on the characteristics of the noise wavefield at this site. Pischiutta et al.6 used the polarization technique and found that the seismic noise has a strong horizontal polarization with a N115° trend. Morabito et al.7 studied seismic noise data recorded by the AME (Array MEfite) array and observed stable wavefield features, with high-frequency tremor (> 5 Hz) linked to the emission source and low-frequency tremor (< 5 Hz) made up by both endogenous and exogenous contributions, including meteorological factors (< 1 Hz). The same array data were analyzed by La Rocca et al.8, who identified low-frequency signals (1.5–3.5 Hz) as coming from a stationary source and higher frequencies (3–20 Hz) from an intermittent one. According to the authors, both bands were dominated by surface waves generated by very shallow sources. Panebianco et al.9 tested a machine learning technique in the 6–20 Hz band to detect tremor signals. Morabito et al.10, analyzing the AME array data, determined that the wavefield near the emission vents in the 1–15 Hz frequency band consists of a stationary background component and of an intermittent, higher-energy one. The background component is attributed to shallow hydrothermal activity, such as bubbling, and has the main spectral content in 2–5 Hz frequency band, while the intermittent, higher amplitude, ones are likely caused by bursts of overpressurized gas breaking through shallow subsurface layers after overcoming the internal resistance of the medium.

Often, inside the continuous recordings of AME, different kinds of low energy transients can be detected. Among these, La Rocca et al.8 observed tremor bursts with frequencies in the 25–35 Hz band, likely associated with volume waves generated by micro-fracturing processes occurring at many tens of meters’ depths below the Mefite lake, and low-frequency ones, in the 1.5–3.5 Hz band, composed of surface waves generated at shallower depths. In general, the Mefite transient signals show short duration (2–3 s), peculiar shapes and narrow or nearly monochromatic spectral contents. Based on their amplitude spatial pattern, they seem to be generated in correspondence with the main emission area of Mefite (Mefite lake) and show coherence across all the stations of the array or, at least, at the ones closest to the vents. Among these transients, we detected some events with a spectral content in the band 0.7–7 Hz, which resemble the properties of the Long Period quakes (LPs) commonly observed in volcanic/hydrothermal environments. In general, the LPs are characterized by low-frequency harmonic waveforms, typically containing one or up to several dominant frequencies between 0.5 and 5 Hz or slightly higher11,12. In most cases the LP signals are composed of P-waves, nevertheless in case of shallow propagation and in presence of a medium stratification, the wavefield consists of a mixture of P- and surface waves13,14. Those events may originate from a wider range of processes, including the migration of gas bubbles or slugs, magma accumulation, degassing, cooling, and crystallization, as well as the movement of magmatic or hydrothermal fluids through fractures and conduits. These processes generate pressure fluctuations that can excite resonance in fluid-filled cracks, producing the characteristic low-frequency waveforms11,12,15. Although several models have been proposed to explain their origin, they all rely on the dynamical coupling between subsurface fluids and the surrounding host rocks. The study of those signals can shed light on the inner generating processes. Palo et al.15 by investigating repeating LPs at Shishaldin volcano (Alaska), associated the changes in their characteristics to pressure variations in the feeding system, with a rapid pressure drop at eruption onset. By using a seismic array at Arenal volcano (Costa Rica), Almendros et al.16, analyzed LP events and volcanic tremor and found that both originate from nearby but distinct processes within the shallow volcanic plumbing system. In the literature, the observations of LP seismicity in non-volcanic, cold degassing areas are scarce. Piccinini et al.17, reported the first-ever detection of LPs in a non-volcanic region, specifically in the Northern Apennine, Italy. The characteristics of those LPs, such as the dominant frequencies, match the expected resonance within a fluid-filled crack containing a mixture of water and mantle-derived CO₂.

In this study, we focused on the LP-like events of Mefite D’Ansanto (hereinafter Mefite LPs or, simply, LPs), detected for the first time within the seismicity associated with the local cold degassing system. In the following, we analyze their spectral and spatio-temporal properties to characterize the seismic phases contained in the signals and to demonstrate that the models commonly associated with LPs in volcanic/hydrothermal environments are compatible with the generation process of the Mefite LPs. Much effort was dedicated to the reliability of our results due primarily to the low amplitude of these LPs over a persistent and intermittent hydrothermal tremor typical of the area.

Results

On 24 August, 2021 at 08:22 UTM, a clear LP was recorded by AME (Fig. 2a). This event has a duration of about 2s at AME4 and AME5, the two stations closest to the vents area (Fig. 1), and shows two frequency peaks, in the 2–5 Hz frequency band, the same of the principal spectral content of the background noise linked to the emissions (hydrothermal tremor10), and the other one in the 5–7 Hz band, which attenuates faster moving away from the lake7,10.

Due to the favorable signal-to-noise ratio (2.3 at AME5), we choose the signal associated with this LP, as a template to search for similar transients inside the continuous signal. In Fig. 2 we show the templates extracted for the vertical component of AME5 (Fig. 2c) and for AME4 (Fig. 2d), after filtering the signals in the band 0.7–7 Hz using an acausal filter, with 3 poles.

Following a Template Matching approach17,18, we cross-correlated each of the two template traces against subsequent portions of the continuous signal, filtered in the 0.7–7.0 Hz band, at the relative station, obtaining a catalogue of similar events, detected if the correlation sum peaks exceeded a threshold value of 90% for AME5 and 99% for AME4. These thresholds were calibrated on the basis of random checking of about 50 extracted samples. Moreover, we applied a threshold for the minimum amplitude of the LPs, 360 counts (1count = 1/400 µm/s) calibrated to ensure a reliable signal-to-noise ratio, and maximum one, 2000 counts, to avoid spurious outliers (teleseisms, spikes, etc.). The matching was applied over the entire acquisition period, 9 June-28 Sept 2021. AME4 catalogue contains 976 LPs, while the AME5 one, which is broadly encompassed by the AME4 one, 627 LPs. The differences between the two catalogues are likely due to the different instrument operating times, mainly because AME5 did not acquire from June 16 to July 28 and it was moved on August 24. In a similar way, we constructed the catalogues for the other AME stations. The catalogue for AME2 contains 788 LPs and 801 LPs for AME6. The matching for AME0, AME3 and AME7 was not reliable since a further 50% of random extracted matched events resulted to be noise segments, even in correspondence with a maximum correlation of 99.9%, and the relative catalogues were not taken into account.

Independent component analysis

The ICA was performed on LP event samples recognized through the template matching at AME4 and AME5. Generally, we analyzed two LPs per day, both for AME4 and for AME5 (Fig. S1), occurring in a different time slot, both during nighttime and daytime, on different days. To check if the considered LPs could have a seismic phase above 7.0 Hz, the signals were filtered in the 0.7–10 Hz band. Then, the FastICAg19 algorithm was applied to each considered LP, using mixture matrices containing the vertical component of all the operating stations, with the signals aligned with respect to the LP record of AME4. The PSD (Power Spectral Density) of the retrieved ICs (Independent Components) was estimated, and the corresponding frequency peaks are represented in Fig. 3a, for AME4, as time distribution. In Fig. 3b we represented the distribution of the occurrence of the relative frequency peaks. From the two plots several observations can be made:

(1) A low frequency IC (ICα) can be identified around 3 Hz, in the band 2–4 Hz, which resembles the main IC associated with the persistent hydrothermal tremor by Morabito et al.10. In most cases, the PSD shows resonances at higher frequencies (Fig. 3d);

(2) ICβ has the most populated bin around 7 Hz (6.5–7.5 Hz). This component is almost extracted all over the analyzed time-windows, even if it is less well represented in August;

(3) From the end of July till the end of August, ICA algorithm gives many components, with ICs below 5 Hz (ICα) and above 8.5 Hz extracted more frequently than in other time intervals, while ICβ is less represented. This pattern seems in countertrend with the other time period in which the ICβ is better extracted, almost suggesting the two ICs were mutually exclusive.

From this analysis, we can deduce that the filter 0.7–7.0 Hz, applied to the signals to obtain the LPs’ catalogue, is appropriate to select the most significant frequency content. Moreover, the shared frequency band between the LPs and the stationary hydrothermal tremor could lead to hypothesize a common source and deserves further investigation, as does the altering pattern between ICα and ICβ.

Sompi analysis

The nearly monochromatic aspect of the LP oscillations suggests that these signals could be generated by a resonance process in a fluid-filled crack, similar to that observed in volcanic environments20. Under this hypothesis, the quality factor Q of the resonator and the dominant frequency of the oscillations can provide some constraints on the composition and physical properties of the fluid contained in the crack, as well as on its geometry21. The quality factor and the dominant frequency can be calculated by analyzing the coda decay pattern of the LP events using the Sompi method22. We conducted this analysis for 20 events with a good signal-to-noise ratio, selecting a suitable time window that contained the signal’s decaying part until its amplitude fell below the noise level. We applied the autoregressive (AR) filter with a maximum order of 20; the optimal order of the filter was chosen on the basis of the values of the kurtosis23,24. The cumulative f-g diagram obtained from this analysis allowed us to recover the frequency of the dominating oscillations, their growth rate g and the corresponding quality factor (Fig. 4). Overall, the events presented dominant frequencies between 3.5 and 10 Hz (at AME5 and AME4) and Q values ranging from 5 to 12.

Seismic tomography

To rule out the presence of lateral discontinuities beneath the array, an active tomographic geophysical survey was conducted in the only area with convenient access. The section between AME2, AME3, and AME0 proved most suitable (Fig. 5a), allowing not only unobstructed cable deployment and optimal survey conditions, but also the maximum possible layout length, thereby increasing the achievable investigation depth.

Shot points (S1–S5) and geophones (e.g., G1, G39) are marked along the top of the section, while depth (Z, m) and distance along the profile (m) are shown on the axes. The tomographic section in Fig. 5b shows P-wave velocity distribution along the profile down to ~ 40 m. The subsurface is highly heterogeneous in the first 25 m, becoming largely homogeneous below this depth. In the shallowest layers, P-wave velocities range from approximately 400 m/s in the upper 2–3 m to 2200–2500 m/s at a depth of around 25 m. Within this portion of the subsurface, the seismic layers do not follow the perfectly parallel trend to the surface, but rather display lenticular anomalies and variations in thickness along the entire section. Notable velocity variations occur between shot points S2 and S3, where a lenticular anomaly (white dashed ellipse in Fig. 5b) with a thickness of ~ 10 m and a length of ~ 25 m is present. This lens causes not only a significant lateral velocity variation but also a velocity inversion between 10 and 20 m depth, with P-wave velocities decreasing from ~ 2500 m/s to ~ 1500 m/s. This anomaly likely accounts for a distortion observed between AME2 and AME3 by Morabito et al.10 in the polarization parameters of the hydrothermal tremor. P velocity anomalies reflect the properties of the medium, characterized by Lagonegrese Unit Flysch deposits with alternating deformed rock and clayey layers, with sharp spatial variations in the mechanical properties even within short distances10,25.

Polarization analysis and apparent velocity estimation

We performed a polarization analysis of the LPs’ signals filtered in 2–5 Hz frequency band so that we can track the same seismic phase across the entire array. For each LP, we used a 3-s-long time window, starting from the event onset, and a sliding that ensures 1.5 wave cycles of the minimum frequency considered (2 Hz). In Fig. 6 we report the results obtained over the entire catalogue of AME4 and AME5 as a function of time. The upper plots show high values of rectilinearity; the rectilinearity is also high for AME2 and AME6 (Fig. S2a–d). The azimuth at AME4 is 160° ± 10° and 100° ± 30° at AME5, indicating that the LPs are likely sourced in the area of the vents (see also Fig. S2a–d). The incidence angles have values distributed around 60°–70° at AME4, AME5 and AME2 (Fig. 6 and Fig. S2a–c) and 80°–90° at AME6 (Fig. S2d). The temporal pattern of the azimuth seems quite stable over time, while the incidence angle shows a clear increase from June to September, indicating that the signal becomes more strongly polarized in the horizontal plane. The temporal patterns at AME2 and AME6 are affected by high uncertainties so that clear behaviors are not identifiable (Fig. S3a–b). The polarization at AME0, AME3 and AME7 are not well constrained, likely due to the attenuation combined with significant local effects10.

Temporal pattern of the polarization parameters for the LPs at (a) AME5 and (b) AME4. From top to bottom: rectilinearity, azimuth and incidence angle.

Taking into account the outcomes of polarization analysis together with those from tomography, in the following we will consider and discuss only the results obtained at AME4, AME5 and AME6 stations.

To investigate the nature of the seismic phases (P-, S-, Rayleigh or Love wavetrains), we performed a particle motion analysis. In the calculations we considered that the amplitude of the Mefite LPs is low and, in many cases, is on the same order of the seismic noise, especially in correspondence of the intermittent tremor wavefield9,10, potentially altering the shape of the motion and leading to inaccurate conclusions. To reduce significant contamination from the noise, we selected the most energetic LPs, with a minimum amplitude of 800 counts from AME4 catalogue, extracting 27 events. In addition, we applied the particle motion estimation to 14 LPs with an amplitude below the threshold of 800 counts and randomly extracted from AME4 catalogue, reaching a total of 41 analyzed events.

Regardless of the amplitude, the selected LPs exhibit a main frequency content with peaks mostly above 5 Hz at AME4 in almost all cases, comprised in 3.3–5.5 Hz band at AME5 and less than 4 Hz at AME6, likely indicating that the higher frequency wavefield attenuates rapidly away from the vents, and, consequently, only AME4 has recorded a significant contribution of the LP-waveforms above 5 Hz.

All of the selected LPs show oscillations pointing towards the vents (see the example in Fig. 7j), with the azimuths in agreement with those of the polarization vector (Fig. 7d and h) for the 93% of the considered LPs, while the incidence angles of the particle motion and those of the polarization vector are always compatible (Fig. 7e, f and i). In 82% of the cases the high amplitude LPs show a linear pattern at AME4. Only in a few (3/4) cases the particle motion at AME5 and AME6 is linear, pointing towards the emission vents, while in the other cases the ground motion is elliptical or not resolvable. AME6 shows strong attenuation effects and often the estimation of particle motion is not possible.

The linear pattern of ground motion for higher amplitudes and the agreement between polarization and particle motion results at AME4, the lack of frequency peaks above 5 Hz at AME5 and AME6 and the attenuation effects, let us suppose that the LPs are composed by a mixing of P- and surficial waves, with a main contribution of high frequency P-waves detectable only in proximity of the vents and which attenuate moving away.

The precise location of the LP source is not suitable. The vents lie at the foot of a landslide deposit on the south-western flank of a step-sloping hill. The station’s employment was limited to the north-western side from the emissions, the only nearly flat and sufficiently extensive zone due to the morphology of the area7, resulting in a gap of about 290°. The eastern side is steeply sloping and is covered by a dense woodland and the southern one is interested by the gas lethal flow, channelized at the bottom of a narrow valley2. Moreover, the LP signals are low-amplitude with an emergent onset, which make a classical phase picking procedure difficult to realize. Array techniques are also non-effective for the LP events. AME is characterized by a limited number of recording stations, resulting in inadequate spatial sampling of the local wavefield, as well as by inter-station distances that are too large relative to the upper frequency limit of the signal’s spectral content (i.e., > 5 Hz). La Rocca et al.8 conducted a detailed assessment of the AME performance and concluded that array techniques are reliable only within the 2–5 Hz frequency range. In this study, we attempted to derive a rough estimate of the LP source location by combining polarization analysis, which is a single-station technique and therefore not affected by array-geometry limitations, with estimates of the apparent velocity of the initial wavepackets. We considered the selected 27 high amplitude LPs and calculated the time-lag between the stations closest to the vents, AME4 and AME5, and the farthest one, AME6. The signals recorded by the vertical components of the considered stations were aligned according to the LP first seismic phase and the lags between AME4 and AME6 and between AME5 and AME6 were calculated, when possible. For each LP, the apparent velocity was estimated by dividing the lags, expressed in seconds, by the distance, AME4-AME6 or AME5-AME6, expressed in kilometers. For both the couples, AME4-AME6 and AME5-AME6, the apparent velocity varies between 0.3 km/s and 2.6 km/s. Then, we roughly calculated the LP source depth, using the estimation of the incidence angle for AME4, AME5 and AME6. For each station, the depth was estimated by multiplying the cotangent of the incidence angle (average value) for the distance of the station from the center of the lake. The depth corresponding to the average incidence angle is approximately 40 m below ground level. This implies that, within the volume between the source and the AME6 station, seismic waves propagate on average at depths between about 40 m and the free surface. According to the tomographic model (Fig. 5), P-waves within this volume likely exhibit velocities ranging from 0.4 to 3.0 km/s, which are consistent with the observed apparent velocities of 0.3–2.6 km/s. Considering near-surface wave propagation (with incidence angles mainly in the 60°–90° range for all the stations considered), we infer that the LP signals are predominantly composed of P-waves. Nevertheless, in several cases the particle motion is clearly elliptical, which is typical of Rayleigh waves, whose velocities are expected not to exceed 1.4–1.6 km/s, assuming a Poisson’s ratio of 0.25–0.30 for the Flysch. The most plausible interpretation is that the Mefite LP signals consist of a mixture of P- and Rayleigh waves.

Discussion

The emission area of Mefite D’Ansanto is characterized by the presence of several vents which release CO2, likely generated in the mantle3 and expelled at the surface in different ways. According to the schematic model developed by Morabito et al.10, if during its ascent, the gas encounters one or more shallow aquifers, a few meters below the surface26,27, it drags some water to the surface and the emission manifests as bubbling, otherwise the emissions are dry. Moreover, if the gas encounters layers of higher strength, it accumulates and undergoes overpressure, generating cyclic higher energy emissions throughout ephemeral pathways and producing an intermittent and higher amplitude seismic wavefield. In general, the seismic signal associated with the emissions, i.e. the hydrothermal tremor, consists of a stationary component, with the main spectral content in the 2–5 Hz band, over which the intermittent one, mainly characterized by higher frequencies, superimposes6,8–10. However, several typologies of transient seismic events, associated with certain frequency content and likely linked to the emissions, have been observed.

This study reports, for the first time, the observation of LP-like events within the seismicity associated with the Mefite cold emissions. These signals are generated in the vent area, share a frequency content in the 0.7–7 Hz band, and last a few seconds. On the basis of their waveform and spectral content we classified them as LP events because of the similarities with volcanic Long Period earthquakes. By applying a Template Matching procedure17,18 we identified a catalogue of 976 LPs recorded at AME4, the station closest to the vents (~ 45 m of distance), which is the most complete and reliable compared to the catalogues found for the other stations. The ICA confirmed that the 0.7–7.0 Hz band contains most of the information of the system dynamics and extracted two signals, ICα and ICβ associated with frequencies in the 2–4 Hz and 6.5–7.5 Hz bands, respectively. Over time, ICβ is dominant with respect to ICα and the two ICs seem almost mutually exclusive. Moreover, looking at the spectra (e.g., Fig. 4e and f), ICβ is nearly-monochromatic, while the PSD of ICα shows a peak at 7 Hz alongside the main one at 3 Hz.

La Rocca et al.8 identified low-frequency (1.5–3.5 Hz) transients originating from a stationary source, dominated by surface waves and generated at very shallow depths. Those events, classified as low-frequency tremor bursts, are the sole low-frequency seismic transients related to the Mefite emissions reported in the previous literature. Those bursts exhibit a predominant surface-wave content and an apparent velocity of about 0.5 km/s. The authors linked them to the stationary hydrothermal tremor. This is consistent with Morabito et al.10, who identified the 2–5 Hz range as the most significant frequency band for the stationary hydrothermal tremor. On the basis of ICA results (Fig. 3), we are confident that the Mefite LP quakes are seismic events distinct from tremor bursts. Indeed, the LPs contain an important spectral component peaked in 6.5–7.5 Hz that the ICA extracted as the dominant source signal (Fig. 3b), not contained in the bursts8. Moreover, according to the schema retrieved by Morabito et al.10, the 6.5–7.5 Hz band is not representative of the hydrothermal tremor at Mefite.

The results from Sompi analysis suggest that, as observed in volcanic/hydrothermal areas, the generation mechanism of the Mefite LPs could be interpreted in the framework of Chouet’s fluid-filled crack model20. Indeed, we obtained estimations of the dominant frequencies in the 3.5–10 Hz band and quality factor Q between 5 and 12. In volcanic environments, these low Q values associated with rapidly decaying oscillations are compatible with a variety of fluids with different composition and physical properties (e.g. gas-gas mixture, liquid-gas mixture such as bubbly water and bubbly basalt28–30). However, considering the non-volcanic setting of our study area, we can reasonably hypothesize that the LPs are generated by the resonance of a wet crack filled by a CO2–water mixture at a low gas volume fraction12. This scenario is consistent with the presence of water in the shallow soil26,27 dragged by the uprising CO210. Based on the values of the quality factor and resonance frequency obtained from the Sompi analysis, we estimated the crack length to range from 2 to 6 m and the width from 0.05 to 0.2 cm.

Further constraints come considering the range of the resonance frequency (3–10 Hz) of the oscillations. From numerical and analytical modelling, frequency variations have been found to be related to changes in the crack stiffness (which in turn depends on the fluid and rock properties) and geometry31–33. Specifically, increasing stiffness results in a decrease in resonance frequencies for all modes (independently of the crack geometry), whereas reducing the crack aspect ratio (W/L) increases the frequencies of all the modes. Assuming that at Mefite D’Ansanto physical and chemical properties of fluids2,34 and soil do not change along the short investigated time interval, the increase of the resonance frequency can be attributed to a decrease of the crack aspect ratio. This means, for example, that if the crack width is constant, an increase of the crack length would justify the frequency shift towards higher values.

The range of the LP resonance frequency (3–10 Hz) retrieved by Sompi is consistent with the frequencies associated with the ICα and ICβ components extracted by ICA (see Figs. 3 and 4 for example). Taking into account these results and considering that ~ 3 Hz is a peculiar peak associated with the stationary source of the hydrothermal tremor10, a possible dynamical model is the following: (i) the ICα is related to the resonance of the fundamental mode of the same structure that generates the stationary tremor, with similar dimensions but with a higher energy; (ii) the ICβ is activated when the energy is even greater, so that the system jumps to a higher vibration mode, or when the aspect ratio of the resonating structure decreases (as for example when a longer portion of the crack is involved), or by a combination of both the mechanisms. Finally, transient pressure steps of higher energy compared to that inducing the CO2 uprising associated with the stationary hydrothermal tremor, are a repetitive trigger for the fluid-filled fracture vibrations, which in turn generate the LP waveforms.

Basically, the source mechanism that generates the LP quakes of Mefite D’Ansanto likely consists of self-oscillations induced by the interaction between a hydrodynamic flux (water and CO2) and solid structures (crack/fractures), capable of activating both fundamental mode and overtones35,36 and/or of reducing the aspect ratio of the resonant structure, with a shift towards higher frequencies, as the pressure increases. Considering that we are dealing with a low-temperature non-volcanic environment2,37, we can assume a simple LP generation mechanism, which involves spherical gas bubbles that vibrate and ascend inside a fluid (water)38. If we consider the fundamental mode, f varies mainly in 2–4 Hz band and, hence, R varies between 5 and 10 cm, which are values compatible with the actual dimensions of the vents at the surface (Fig. 8). The estimated bubble radius R, inferred from the resonance frequency, represents an acoustically equivalent dimension of the bubble, associated with the compressibility of gas patches or elongated gas lenses distributed along the fracture, rather than its actual geometry. A more realistic collective behavior of multiple bubbles can be modeled as a single equivalent bubble using the Minnaert formulation, which helps infer the spatial scales and physical behavior of the system. The fact that the estimated Minnaert radius is compatible with the vent dimensions suggests a maximum size that a single bubble cloud can reach. A similar approach was used by Commander at al.41, who developed a theoretical model based on an effective-medium description, in which the presence of many bubbles is treated in an averaged sense rather than by analyzing each individual interaction. In the same way, Falanga et al.42 adopted an effective modeling approach to describe the resonant fluid-filled structure as an equivalent resonator, reflecting the collective behavior of a complex network of fractures, rather than resolving the detailed geometry of the individual fractures that generate the hydrothermal tremor. Following the concepts of bubble resonance and collapse described by Kedar et al.43, the equivalent Minnaert radius can also be considered an effective length in cases where the liquid under a pressure drop oscillates and resonates when driven by an external acoustic or pressure field, leading to an increase in the amplitude of radial oscillations.

Picture (taken by this paper authors) of the Mefite lake during the summer of 2023, when the lake’s water had completely evaporated and the vents were exposed. The length of the vents’ alignment is about 10 m. The size and shape of the vents have not been artificially altered.

The polarization analysis, in conjunction with the particle motions’ estimation and the amplitude attenuation pattern, indicates the generation of the LPs in the vent area. In general, the polarization analysis gives information on how the polarization vector oscillates in space and, to fix constraints on the wave type, further analyses are performed alongside, i.e. particle motion or array techniques44,45. For the most of the higher amplitude LPs recorded by AME4, we found high values of rectilinearity (close to 1, that means linear oscillations, Fig. 6b) and a linear ground motion in space (Fig. 7d–f) for the 82% of them, concordant polarization and ground motion azimuth (pointing towards the vents) and compatible incidence angles, which are all clear indicators that unequivocally identify P-waves. Conversely, all the considered LPs with lower amplitude at AME4 and most of the evaluable ones at AME5 and AME6 display elliptical motion, typical of surface waves. This lack of P-waves can be explained by a faster attenuation of the higher frequency components of the LPs, well distinguishable at AME4 under condition of sufficiently high energy/amplitude of the relative wavetrain. The presence of P-waves within the wavefield of LP events is predicted by the Chouet’s model11,46, where a step drop in pressure induces gas exsolution and bubble expansion causing a volumetric increase of the crack without a shape change and, hence, the source undergoes spherically symmetric oscillations.

The temporal pattern of the azimuth seems quite stable over time, pointing towards the lake, while the incidence angle shows a clear increase from June to September, suggesting a stronger polarization in the horizontal plane and, hence, a more surficial sourcing process. This effect could be ascribed to the seasonality of the local hydrological regime since the shallow aquifers, that mainly fed the Mefite lake, undergoes fluctuations related to rainfalls10,26. The increase of rainfall/soil humidity observed by Morabito et al.10 starting from late August, was responsible for the variations in the overall temporal pattern of the incidence angle of hydrothermal tremor. Evoking a similar mechanism, rainfall/soil humidity could be also responsible for the overall variation of the LP’s incidence angle, corroborating the existence of a link between the LPs and the hydrothermal tremor.

A rough depth of the LP source was estimated based on of the incidence angle values averaged over the entire acquisition interval, resulting in ~ 40 m below the ground level, which is deeper than the source of the intermittent tremor found by Morabito et al.10 at ~ 20 m, which in turn is deeper than the source of the stationary tremor. Subsequently, we estimated the apparent velocity between the two stations closer to the vents, AME4 and AME5, and the farthest, AME6, for the higher amplitude LPs. In both cases the apparent velocities vary in the 0.3–2.6 km/s interval. This implies that, within the volume extending from the source to the AME6 station and bounded between depths of about 40 m and the free surface, seismic waves propagate with apparent velocities consistent with the P-wave velocities estimated from tomography (0.4–3.0 km/s). In near-surface wave propagation, characterized by incidence angles mainly in the 60°–90° range for all the stations, the apparent velocities do not differ significantly from the true velocities. These considerations, together with the evidence of linear particle motions, suggest that the LP signals are predominantly composed of P-waves. Conversely, some LP events exhibit a clear elliptical particle motion, which is typical of Rayleigh waves, whose velocities are expected not to exceed 1.4–1.6 km/s, assuming a Poisson’s ratio of 0.25–0.30 for the Flysch. Therefore, the most plausible interpretation is that the Mefite LP signals consist of a mixture of P- and surface waves, as commonly observed in presence of shallow propagation and of a stratified medium13,14.

Alongside the estimation of P-wave velocities, another intriguing outcome emerged during our investigation. The tomography showed the presence of a velocity anomaly underneath AME3 station, with a linear extension of about 25 m and an elliptical shape (delimited by a white dashed line in Fig. 5), distinguished by higher values. The presence of such anomaly inside the Flysch strata of Lagonegrese Units, which outcrop in the region where Mefite D’Ansanto is located4, is not surprising. Due to their depositional and tectonic history, flysch units present lithological heterogeneity, alternating sandstones and silty-clayey or clayey layers, with a network of discontinuities and variations in mechanical properties even within short distances4. Even though determining the nature of this anomaly requires further investigations, its presence explains the difficulties encountered in the present study in the characterization of the LPs’ behavior at AME2 and AME3, as well as some distortion in the polarization patterns of the hydrothermal tremor in correspondence with the two stations noticed by Morabito et al.10.

Conclusions

The Mefite D’Ansanto seismicity linked to the CO2 emissions, is very complex7,10 being constituted by both, continuous and transient signals, over a wide frequency band. Basing on the data acquired by AME array during the summer 2021, in the intermediate frequency band, 1–15 Hz, the wavefield is composed by (i) a stationary hydrothermal tremor, whose main frequency content is in the 2–5 Hz band and it is sourced at very shallow depths10; (ii) an intermittent hydrothermal tremor, characterized by higher frequencies, mainly in the 7–9 Hz band, a deeper source (~ 20 m beneath the lake) and higher signal amplitude10; (iii) several transients, among which we have focused on the LP quakes, with a main spectral content in the 0.7–7 Hz frequency band, a short duration and a source depth deeper than the hydrothermal tremor (~ 40 m beneath the lake). The Mefite D’Ansanto LP quakes were reported here for the first time and represent one of the rare observations of LP-like seismic events associated with cold fluid emissions. Indeed, the characteristics of the source processes of these signals are linked to the ascent of CO2 along surficial fractures/cracks2,10 coupled with the properties of the soil. In this paper we have proposed and discussed the resonance of gas-water fill fractures/cracks as LP source, while Morabito et al.10 proposed the bubbling as one of the possible source processes for the hydrothermal tremor when the emissions occur in presence of water. In both the cases, a crucial role is played by the inner pressure, that is the common trigger factor. By considering the signal amplitude as a proxy of the radiated seismic energy, we guess that more the gas is pressurized, higher is the seismic amplitude of the generated signal and, hence, higher is the energy involved in the source process. Following a hypothetical escalation, a very large pressure input, induced by a local/regional earthquake, could trigger larger-scale bubbling in the gas-water-fractures’ system and generate high energy surficial phenomena47.

In the future, more precise and effective techniques could be used to improve the source location of the hydrothermal tremor and transients at Mefite D’Ansanto, such as Matched Field Processing analysis48,49 which require a more extensive and denser array employment, that possible give insight in the deeper portion of the plumbing system; as well as, video surveys could be planned49,50 to better link the emissions to seismic recording.

Clear evidences demonstrate that the CO2 emitted at Mefite originated at mantle and many authors highlighted the importance that such deep fluids have in the seismogenesis at local/regional scale (see Di Luccio et al.3 and reference therein). In this framework, our study on the Mefite LP quakes gives an interesting insight on the relation between the local pressured CO2 -water-soil interaction and local/regional seismicity. Continuous monitoring of internal pressure would be preferable. Indeed, in several worldwide cases, the generation of higher amplitude transients inside tremor was recorded in conjunction with pressure pulses within the fluid in the conduit43.

Since Mefite D’Ansanto has revealed a good natural laboratory due to a quiet environment and lively hydrothermal activity, a possible future development could foresee a larger and denser array employment to monitor the emissions, that permits to extend the investigations at very higher depths, and, hence to eventually shade light on the deep processes involving the fluid transfer and the tectonic regime.

Methods

Seismic array

For our analyses we used the seismic signals recorded by array AME (Fig. 1). AME operated in a restricted area close to Mefite main emissions for about four months starting from 9 June 2021, and consisted of 7 three-component stations equipped with 1 Hz Lennartz 3D-Lite sensors. The array was first arranged in a larger inter-station configuration, designed to better exploit the available space (the triangles in Fig. 1). On 24 August 2021 three stations (AME5, AME6 and AME7) were moved to form a second arrangement more suitable to the analysis of a higher frequency band (2.8–8 Hz). We uninstalled the array on 28 September 2021. For more details, see Morabito et al.7.

ICA

The Independent Component Analysis is a decorrelation technique acting in time domain, which allows to decompose a signal (mixtures), acquired by one or more recording devices, in the ‘‘basic’’ signals (ICs, Independent Components) peculiar of the emitting sources and has been successfully applied in a number of different fields51–54. Stating the statistical independence of the sources, the ICs can be estimated on the basis of the Central Limit Theorem, according to which the distribution of the sum of two independent random variables is closer to a Gaussian than the distribution of the two single variables19. In the methodological formulation19 m recordings, x, are considered as a linear combination of n mutually independent unknown sources s, x = As, where A is an unknown constant mixing matrix, which includes each of the contributions due to path, noise, instrumentations, etc. The ICA provides a separating matrix W = A−1, so that the vector IC = Wx is an estimation of the original independent sources, IC ∼ s, by maximizing the non-Gaussianity of the sources.

In our case, the recorded seismic signals are the linear representation, x, of non-Gaussian sources, IC, and we apply the fixed-point FastICA algorithm (https://research.ics.aalto.fi/ica/fastica/) for the estimation of the independent source signals (ICs). The independence of the sources is evaluated by approximate the negentropy matrix (JG) through the equation JG(w) = [E{G (wTx) }-E{G()}]2, where G is a suitable non-quadratic contrast function (i.e. a cubic power law), w is an m-dimensional vector of the weight matrix W, E is the expectation value so that E{(wTx)2} = 1, and υ is a Gaussian random variable. The ICs are obtained from the estimation of weight vectors w1,..., wn, by maximizing JG. The input signals for the algorithm are generally aligned by using the maximum of the cross-correlation function, with respect to the reference station.

Sompi method

Sompi method is a high-resolution spectral technique based on the homogeneous autoregressive model of Kumazawa et al.22. The homogenous part of the seismic signal of an LP event is characterized by an exponential coda decay which can be parameterized in terms of complex frequency fc = f − ig, (f is the frequency and g is the growth rate of the signal) and quality factor Q, defined as Q = − f /2g55. Under the hypothesis that LPs represent the acoustic resonance of a fluid-filled fracture, these two parameters can provide indeed some constraints on the nature of the fluids involved in the generation process and on the crack (resonator) dimension (e.g.28,29).

2D seismic refraction tomography

Two-Dimensional Seismic Refraction Tomography (2D_SRT) is a geophysical imaging technique used to resolve velocity variations in the subsurface along a linear profile. The method records the first-arrival times of seismic waves—both vertical and horizontal components—that are critically refracted at velocity contrasts between subsurface layers. Seismic energy is generated using controlled sources such as sledgehammers, weight drops, or small explosive charges, and the resulting signals are recorded by an array of geophones positioned along the survey line (see Fig. 5b).

Multiple source points are typically deployed along the profile to improve ray coverage, thereby enhancing the resolution of the final velocity model. The recorded data are processed through inversion algorithms that iteratively refine an initial velocity model until the misfit between observed and calculated arrival times is minimized. Common approaches include ray tracing and finite-difference solutions of the Eikonal equation, both of which can represent smooth velocity gradients as well as sharp discontinuities without requiring laterally continuous homogeneous layers. The inversion is performed on a discretized grid, where velocity values are estimated for individual cells along the profile.

The effective investigation depth (defined as the maximum depth reliably resolved) typically corresponds to 30–50% of the total length of the geophone array. It also depends on geological conditions, source strength, and receiver spacing. The resulting tomographic section provides a vertical cross-section of the subsurface, highlighting lateral and vertical velocity variations. This makes 2D_SRT particularly effective for identifying lithological boundaries, heterogeneous zones, and structures associated with strong velocity contrasts.

In this study, we employed ZONDSt2D (http://zond-geo.com/english/zond-software/seismic-tomography/zondst2d/ last access 25/08/2025), which implements a ray-tracing algorithm based on the ray equation derived from the Eikonal equation. In this framework, seismic propagation is modelled as rays that minimize travel time through media with spatially varying velocities, consistent with Fermat’s principle. The software computes ray paths by numerically integrating the system of differential equations that govern ray direction and curvature as functions of the local velocity gradient. This approach, extensively described in geophysical literature56, provides accurate travel-time calculations that form the basis of tomographic inversion.
