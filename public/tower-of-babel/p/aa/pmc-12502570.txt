Gestational diabetes mellitus and risk of type 2 diabetes 4 years after the index pregnancy in Moroccan women—a prospective cohort study
Authors: Sanae Elomrani, Bouchra Assarag, Vincent De Brouwere, Mohamed Khalis, Bouchra Benazzouz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe epidemic of diabetes mellitus is spreading throughout the world. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a transitory form of diabetes first recognized during pregnancy, is a known harbinger of future type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, and cardiac disease. To prevent the future onset of T2DM in mothers and offspring, preventive postpartum care and follow-up are necessary. However, the area remains grossly underprioritized. This study aims to examine long-term metabolic changes, including T2DM, in a cohort of Moroccan women with a known history of GDM.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted in the Marrakech-Safi region in Morocco. Women with GDM (initial cohort, n = 210) and those who did not develop hyperglycemia (non-GDM) during 2016–2017 were examined in 2020–2021. Women were classified by a 2-h, 75-g oral glucose tolerance test according to the World Health Organization criteria. Historical data from the index pregnancy and anthropometrical measurements were collected.ResultsThe median duration of follow-up was 4.00 years. Although our study population was relatively young (mean age 31.1 years), a woman with GDM in the index pregnancy was 3.15 (adjusted OR 3.15; 95% CI 1.25–7.52) times more likely to develop T2DM within 4 years than women with no GDM after controlling for other confounding variables. Women with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) were 4.27 times more likely to develop T2DM than women with normal weight (adjusted OR 4.27; 95% CI 1.32–13.72). Complying with the recommended healthy diet was a protective factor, markedly decreasing the risk of developing diabetes within four years (adjusted OR 0.38; 95% CI 0.16–0.89). GDM and obesity were significant predictors of the development of T2DM.ConclusionGiven the emerging burden of GDM and the steady increase in diabetes-related morbidity and mortality rates, there is a pressing need to transform the current maternal and child health system to address a burgeoning metabolic challenge for this high-risk population in Morocco. Initiating interventions during preconception and continuing into the postpartum period has the potential to confer long-term maternal-child benefits, promoting virtuous cycles of health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08206-7.Keywords: Gestational diabetes, Risk factors, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Diabetes prevention, Cohort study, Postpartum

Background

With more than half a billion people living with diabetes worldwide, making it one of the fastest-growing health emergencies and a leading cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. The prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) is significantly increased in adults, particularly young women [1]. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a transitory form of diabetes first recognized during pregnancy [3, 4]. Women tested positive for GDM if fasting blood glucose was 0.92–1.25 g/l (5.1 − 6.9 mmol/l), 1.8 g/l (10 mmol/l) and above one hour, or between 1.53 and 1.99 g/l (8.5–11.0 mmol/l) two hours after ingestion of 75 g glucose [3]. Globally, GDM affects 14% of pregnancies, with the highest prevalence in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) at 27.6% [5]. GDM is one of the main pathologies encountered by women during pregnancy, with serious short- and long-term consequences on maternal health [6]. Most cases of hyperglycemia during pregnancy occur in low and middle-income countries (87,5%), where access to prenatal care is often limited [7, 8]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the reported prevalence of GDM varies widely [9], ranging from 3.7% in certain rural settings to 13.9–16.7% among high-risk women in Nigeria [10, 11]. In Morocco, diabetes prevalence has risen from 6.6% in 2000 to 10.6% in 2018 [12]. National data on GDM and postpartum screening are limited, with regional studies reporting prevalence rates ranging from 8.2% in 2009 to 18.3% in Al Haouz and 23.7% in Marrakech in 2017 [13].

GDM is a major risk factor for T2DM, hypertension, and heart disease [14–16], with the risk of T2DM peaking 3–6 years postpartum—especially in women under 40—and being nearly ten times higher than in women without GDM [17, 18]. Despite recommendations for screening at 4–12 weeks postpartum and every 1–3 years thereafter [19], participation in post-partum screening remains suboptimal [20, 21]. However, less than half of women receive appropriate screening, with limited data on screening in underserved populations [20, 22, 23].

GDM significantly affects maternal health, a fact well established in developed countries. However, data on its effects in North Africa are limited. Previous studies have utilized varying diagnostic criteria, which may lead to misestimations of adverse outcomes. This study aims to clarify the risk of adverse maternal outcomes, particularly the development of T2DM, in women with gestational diabetes. It is based on a cluster randomized controlled trial conducted in the Marrakech-Safi region, using updated diagnostic criteria for GDM [13].

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in the region of Marrakech-Safi, located in central Morocco and covering part of the Atlas Mountains, from November 2017 to December 2021, four years after the initial research that set up the cohort of women studied (parent study). The region has 4.9 million inhabitants, representing 13.3% of the national population [24]. This region has 443 primary healthcare (PHC) facilities, including 325 health centers providing comprehensive primary health care [25]. The prefecture of Marrakech is essentially an urban prefecture, and the province of El Haouz is a rural and mountainous province with limited geographical access to healthcare services. The two districts were selected for the implementation of the parent study. The health center served as the randomization unit (cluster), with cluster randomization preferred over individual randomization to minimize contamination between study groups. In each district, 10 health centers per district with ≥ 30 monthly prenatal consultations were randomly selected [13, 26]. The 20 health centers selected for contacting the women corresponded to those randomly chosen in the parent study, based on the availability of the baseline study participant database, the competencies previously acquired by the staff, and the centers’ geographical proximity to the target population.

This study was covered by ethical approval for studies from the Ethics Review Committee of the Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy, University Mohammed V of Rabat (Ref. No. 55/20/07/2020). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Participation was entirely voluntary, and women were free to withdraw from the study at any time without any impact on their clinical care. We strictly maintained the confidentiality of personal and medical information by anonymizing data and storing it securely, ensuring access only for authorized research personnel. All procedures, including interviews, physical measurements, and laboratory testing, were conducted with respect for the participants’ privacy and dignity, accommodating their availability. These measures ensured transparency, ethical integrity, and protection of participants’ rights throughout the study.

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study, conducted over a four-year follow-up, included participants from the parent study implemented between November 2016 - November 2017. The parent study provided a rigorously standardized screening and initial-management protocol and an established baseline cohort of screened women—prerequisites for valid prospective assessment of incident type 2 diabetes after pregnancy. The parent study’s demonstration of high GDM prevalence (23.7% in Marrakech and 18.3% in Al Haouz) and the subsequent adoption of its protocol by the Ministry of Health and Social Protection (March 2020) further ensured the study’s public-health relevance. Women were diagnosed with GDM by capillary glucose testing of a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), applying the International Association of Diabetes in Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) and WHO criteria as adopted by FIGO in their latest consensus recommendations for the diagnosis of GDM [27]. During pregnancy, all participants received the same recommendations about a healthy diet and recommendations about physical activity according to the Moroccan maternal health education program (specific guidelines for GDM, 2017). A total of 210 women were diagnosed with GDM and included in the main study analysis.

Maternal health care providers at the health facilities (one provider per site) (called local investigators) received a one-day training on long-term GDM adverse outcomes and the study’s data collection tool and procedures. Local investigators in selected health centers were asked to recruit every woman with GDM from the parent study conducted 3–4 years ago until they reached at least 80% of the 210 GDM patients who consented to participate in the study. And to reach the double for the control sample of women consisted of women who attended antenatal consultation during the same period (November 2016-November 2017), who had a normoglycemic pregnancy and who consented to participate in this study.

The study population consisted of pregnant women receiving antenatal care between November 2016 - November 2017, categorized as follows:

Inclusion criteria: (1) Women diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) during the study period (n = 210) [13], (2) A comparison group of pregnant women with normoglycemic pregnancies followed concurrently, selected based on matching criteria relative to the GDM group, including age, gestational age at enrollment, and presence of family history of diabetes mellitus, and (3) Women who agree (provide written consent) to participate in the follow-up study.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Women diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) during the study period (n = 210) [13], (2) A comparison group of pregnant women with normoglycemic pregnancies followed concurrently, selected based on matching criteria relative to the GDM group, including age, gestational age at enrollment, and presence of family history of diabetes mellitus, and (3) Women who agree (provide written consent) to participate in the follow-up study.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Women who declined to provide written informed consent, (2) Normoglycemic pregnant women who did not meet the eligibility criteria established for the comparison group, (3) women pregnant during the data-collection period, and (4) insufficient documented proof of “exposure status” (GDM vs. non-GDM) or “outcome status” (diabetes vs. non-diabetes).

Antenatal records of the index pregnancy (parent study) were available in 100% (N = 210) of women with hyperglycemia in pregnancy (“exposed” group). All “exposed” women with antenatal records of the index pregnancy were invited to participate in the study. For each “exposed” woman, two “nonexposed” women (420) (who were pregnant during the parent study) in the same health facilities were invited to participate.

Potential participants were requested to attend the data collection sessions with their prenatal and medical records. The local investigators, supervised by the research team, examined the medical and prenatal records to identify participants who met the inclusion criteria. They then administered the questionnaire and carried out clinical examinations of selected participants. Due to the difficulties encountered during the COVID-19 pandemic (containment, infection of health workers with the COVID-19 virus, fear of going to the health center and the COVID-19 vaccination campaign), it was very difficult to recruit participants, especially “unexposed” women. Despite these challenges, a total of 370 women were recruited for the study, 204 out of 210 women who had GDM and 166 out of 240 randomly selected women who did not have GDM during the index pregnancy. Data collection procedure

The data collection was carried out by trained healthcare providers using a questionnaire specifically designed for this study and pretested at two selected health centers (Annex A). Participants provided verbal informed consent at enrollment (parental or guardian consent for girls under 16). Pregnancy-related information and glycemic status during the index pregnancy were extracted from antenatal records, while outcome status (presence or absence of diabetes) was determined from medical records held by the patient. Data on maternal and sociodemographic characteristics (age, gravidity, educational level, type of insurance coverage, place of residence urban or rural, medical history, previous pregnancy complications), childbirth and postpartum complications were extracted. Feeding patterns in the early years of life and postpartum screening for T2D and lifestyle parameters (dietary diversity and physical activity) were collected by trained midwives and nurses in face-to-face questionnaire application and asserted by medical records during the follow-up. Capillary fasting blood sugar (FBS), blood pressure (BP), weight and height were also measured. The WHO criteria for the diagnosis of diabetes in nonpregnant adults were used to determine the outcome status [28].

Participants were asked about their food and physical activity frequency. Considering the education level of the participants, interviewers administered structured questions to assess weekly physical activity frequency [29]. The scoring system was defined as follows:

0 times/week: no recorded physical activity.

0 times/week: no recorded physical activity.

1–4 times/week: moderate activity (e.g., sweating exercises, ≥ 20 min of vigorous, or ≥ 30 min of moderate, or ≥ 75 min walking per session).

1–4 times/week: moderate activity (e.g., sweating exercises, ≥ 20 min of vigorous, or ≥ 30 min of moderate, or ≥ 75 min walking per session).

≥ 5 times/week: high activity frequency, with the sum calculated from the number of weekly exercise episodes across all intensities. In addition, women were asked about their adherence to healthy lifestyle recommendations made during the index pregnancy.

≥ 5 times/week: high activity frequency, with the sum calculated from the number of weekly exercise episodes across all intensities. In addition, women were asked about their adherence to healthy lifestyle recommendations made during the index pregnancy.

Ascertainment of exposure (GDM)

Women who participated in the parent study with documentary evidence of gestational diabetes mellitus in antenatal records during the index pregnancy were classified as the GDM group. Women with normal blood sugar assessments during the index pregnancy were classified as the non-GDM group.

Ascertainment of outcome (T2DM)

Diagnosis of type 2 diabetes is the primary outcome of the study and is defined according to WHO IDF criteria [28]. Women were classified as having diabetes mellitus if they had been diagnosed by a doctor as having diabetes since the index pregnancy and were currently being treated for diabetes or if there was documented evidence in medical records or blood sugar reports having an FBS > 126 mg/dl or 75 g OGTT 2-hour value 200 mg/dl, HbA1c ≥ 6.5% based on WHO criteria [28]. For women who were diagnosed with diabetes by their physician at follow-up visits, their medical records were reviewed to assess diagnostic plasma glucose values, and fasting plasma glucose was tested to confirm diabetes status according to the study criteria.

The 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) remains the screening test of choice; glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) does not have sufficient sensitivity to detect hyperglycemia [30–32]. In our study, progression to T2DM was assessed using a 2-hour, 75-gram OGTT. This test was administered to all participants except those with a prior confirmed diagnosis of diabetes who were already receiving treatment. A private laboratory contracted by the researchers conducted the testing in health centers close to participants’ residences. After a 10-hour fasting period, venous blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein to measure plasma glucose concentrations. The women were classified as normal weight (BMI ˂25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25–29 kg/m2), or obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) [33]. Hypertension (ICD-10 I10-I15) was defined as a persistent elevation in systolic BP ≥ 140 and/or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg [34]. Family history of diabetes mellitus (parents and siblings; “yes” and “no”).

Women presenting abnormal values in the measured parameters during the follow-up period were referred to the referral health centers for appropriate medical care.

Data analysis

Characteristics of women in the GDM and non-GDM groups were described using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical variables, and means (SD) and medians (IQR) were used to summarize continuous variables. Normality was assessed using both graphical methods (histograms and Q-Q plots), and descriptive statistics (means, medians, and standard deviations), and formally tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Appropriate statistical tests were applied according to the type and distribution of variables. Comparisons between the GDM and non-GDM groups were performed using t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, chi-square tests for categorical variables, and non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis) for non-normally distributed variables. Binary logistic regression was used to examine associations between the development of diabetes and potential predictors. All statistical analyses were conducted using jamovi (Version 2.3)1.

Results

A third of women in the non-GDM group were in their first pregnancy, compared to 22% in the GDM group (p = 0.029). Progression to type 2 diabetes

Diabetes occurred in 30 (16.7%) women in the GDM group and 7 (5.6%) in the non-GDM group during the study. The OGTT test during the 4-year postpartum follow-up allowed for the diagnosis of 90% of the women, while only 10 had been previously diagnosed with T2DM. In addition, 9.9% of women in the GDM group had pre-diabetes, compared with 4% of women in the control group (p < 0.001) [34–36].

A woman with GDM in the index pregnancy was 3.15 (adjusted OR 3.15; 95% CI 1.25–7.52) times more Likely to develop diabetes within four years after controlling for other variables compared to women with no GDM. Being obese increased the risk of developing diabetes within four years by 4.27-fold (adjusted OR 4.27; 95% CI 1.32–13.72) compared to women with normal weight. Women who adopted dietary measures markedly decreased the risk of developing diabetes within four years by 2.7-fold (adjusted OR 0.38; 95% CI 0.16–0.89) after controlling for age at delivery, gravidity, GDM in previous pregnancies, postpartum visits, hypertension and moderate physical activity (Table 2). The model correctly classified 88.6% of cases.

We examined sociodemographic and clinical factors predicting type 2 diabetes in women with a history of GDM, using multivariate logistic regression over a four-year postpartum period (Table 3). The multivariate logistic regression model identified obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) as a significant predictor of type 2 diabetes four years after the index pregnancy (adjusted OR 5.44, 95% CI 1.43–20.60, p = 0.013). Other variables, including gravidity, history of stillbirth or macrosomia, and adherence to a low sugar and fat diet, were not significant predictors. The model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.88, indicating that it correctly classified 88% of participants. Discussion

A total of 355 women were included in the study, comprising 191 with GDM and 164 without. Baseline characteristics such as age, education, and health insurance did not differ significantly between groups. Notably, approximately half of women with GDM resided in rural areas compared to one-third of non-GDM women (p = 0.043), reflecting lifestyle changes and persistent health disparities in rural populations. This finding corroborates the ongoing rural-urban and racial/ethnic disparities among pregnant women in Kansas from 2005 to 2019 [35]. With rising maternal obesity trends, there is a need to expand gestational diabetes services in rural areas and implement tailored interventions for at-risk women [37]. A first-degree family history of diabetes was reported in nearly half of the GDM group (p < 0.001). Significant differences were also observed for obstetric outcomes, including prior abortion, stillbirth (p = 0.023), and macrocosmic infants (p < 0.001), consistent with known GDM-related complications [36, 38]. Our findings show that GDM is a significant predictor of developing diabetes mellitus 4 years postpartum. Women with GDM had a 3.15-fold increased risk of developing T2DM during four years of follow-up (adjusted OR 3.15; 95% CI 1.25, 7.52) compared with women without GDM after controlling for other confounding variables. Our estimate is higher than the 1.39 [0.91–2.15] risk reported for a 3–5.9-year duration of follow-up [18]. The risk estimates for T2DM were 19.7% to 29.4% at 10 and 20 years, respectively [15].

The women in our study progressed at an even higher rate, with almost 16.7% (30 diabetic cases) of women with GDM developing T2DM within the 4 years. This finding corroborates the data Supporting that Women with a history of GDM had a significantly increased risk of diabetes, with the risk being highest in the 3–6 years after GDM and appearing to plateau after 10 years [39]. Other studies reported a higher risk, GDM adjusted ORs for diabetes in women ≥ 3 - <6 years post-GDM were 16.55 (16.08–17.04) [40].

Other factors significantly associated with developing diabetes in this study included obesity and nonadherence to healthy diet recommendations after controlling for other variables. Among the predictors of maternal risk factors, BMI before and after delivery is the most commonly examined. Our findings support that postpartum BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 increased the risk of postpartum T2DM 4.27-fold (95% CI, 1.32–13.72). Among women with a history of GDM, obesity further amplified the risk of developing type 2 diabetes, with obese women being 5.44 times more likely to develop the condition compared to those who maintained a normal body weight (adjusted OR = 5.44; 95% CI 1.43–20.60). Postpartum weight gain, as reported in several studies, may be an important modifiable factor influencing progression to DM risk, independent of pre-pregnancy and pregnancy BMI [37, 41]. Our study population is relatively young (mean age 31.1 years), which corroborates the current scientific data attesting that with the increasing prevalence of obesity, type 2 diabetes has become a concern among young people [1, 2, 42].

In terms of behavioral factors that influence progression to diabetes in women with GDM, it is important to note that women who have adopted dietary measures have a significant 2.7-fold (OR 0.38; 95% CI 0.16–0.89) reduction in the risk of developing diabetes within four years after controlling for the other confounding variables. However, moderate physical activity was not significantly associated with the prevention of diabetes, which may be attributed to the lack of precision of physical activity measurement in our study.

Investigating the use of lifestyle changes to prevent or delay the onset of DM in high-risk groups, women with a weight-loss goal were able to reduce their DM risk by adhering to physical activity and a low-fat, low-calorie diet compared to placebo [43, 44]. Women with a history of gestational diabetes who increased their total physical activity level by 150 min per week of moderate-intensity physical activity had a 47% (RR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.38–0.75) lower risk of diabetes when compared with women who maintained their total physical activity levels over 16 years, even when adjusting for BMI [45]. Lifestyle changes adopted within six months of a pregnancy complicated by gestational diabetes have been Linked to a 39% reduction (RR 0.61, 95% CI: 0.40–0.94) in progression to diabetes at any point postpartum [46].

There was no significant association between future risk of diabetes and family history of diabetes, gravidity, breastfeeding, access to postpartum care visits or gestational age at delivery in our study. While a family history of diabetes may not clearly be associated with an increased risk of progression from GDM to DM [47]. It has also been suggested that recurrent pregnancies after a pregnancy complicated by gestational diabetes may increase the risk of progression to diabetes. However, studies evaluating this hypothesis are often confounded by increasing maternal age [48].

With regard to breastfeeding, a retrospective analysis of women with a history of gestational diabetes from the Nurses’ Health Study cohort corroborates our findings and found no association between lifetime breastfeeding and the incidence of diabetes in women with gestational diabetes [49]. Although, there is strong prospective evidence that the intensity and duration of breastfeeding can significantly reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes [50, 51].

There is a strong scientific consensus that primary healthcare helps prevent illness and death [52, 53]. However, access to postpartum care was not significantly associated with a reduced risk of diabetes. This is probably due to the lack of continuous monitoring of women with GDM during the postpartum period, which is a missed screening opportunity to detect diabetes early. Postpartum screening of women with GDM is not a priority in Morocco; 18 (9.4%) women with GDM had no contact after delivery, and 72 (37.7%) women had only a single PP visit. This limited access to postpartum screening, due to women’s role as mother and caregiver is competing with one’s own health priority, resulting in poor postpartum screening and poor management of eating and physical activity behaviors [54]. This finding is consistent with the persistently low postpartum care coverage in Morocco, which has remained around 22% between 2011 and 2018 (DHS) [55, 56]. To address this gap, context-adapted interventions Such as community outreach programs, integrating postpartum services into sexual and reproductive health during primary care visits, mobile health reminders, and culturally tailored educational campaigns are essential. These interventions aim to raise awareness and utilization of postpartum care among Moroccan women, identify those with a history of gestational diabetes who may be unaware of their condition, and ensure they receive timely care. Promoting a healthy diet, regular physical activity, and weight management is vital for preventing and managing type 2 diabetes. The postnatal period presents a key opportunity to enhance access to diabetes prevention, care, and education [57]. Women’s experiences and needs should be considered when designing strategies and interventions to promote healthier lifestyles in this population [54]. The local evidence generated by this study is likely to motivate clinicians and community health workers to follow up with these women more closely to ensure postpartum screening and women centered healthy lifestyle interventions after GDM pregnancy.

We acknowledge some limitations in our cohort study. The recruitment process was significantly affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, especially impacting the control group, which resulted in a smaller sample size and diminished statistical power. While the findings remain consistent with existing evidence, the smaller sample size may limit generalizability, particularly to populations or settings that differ from the study context. Financial constraints also prevented us from repeating oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT). Additionally, our evaluation of physical activity was constrained by the absence of validated measurement instruments, a limitation compounded by the participants’ varying educational levels. Future research should employ standardized instruments adapted to the national context, such as the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), to improve the accuracy and comparability of physical activity measurements [58].Furthermore, there was the potential for recall bias regarding past events. Despite these limitations, we utilized multiple data sources—including health center patient records, personal health records from the women involved, and results from medical tests—which helped to mitigate some of the challenges and enhanced the reliability of the data collected.

Conclusions

Maternal and child health in Morocco is increasingly challenged by rising prevalence of obesity and metabolic disorders, with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) representing a significant risk factor for both mothers and their children. This study assessed the long-term risk of type 2 diabetes (T2D) among women with prior GDM and identified key predictors. Our findings show that a history of GDM and obesity were the strongest predictors of progression to T2D, highlighting the urgent need for enhanced postpartum follow-up and targeted preventive strategies. Such strategies should include preconception and postnatal lifestyle interventions, community-based support, and culturally adapted health education. The study is limited by its relatively small sample size and reliance on self-reported lifestyle data, which constrain the generalizability of the results. Future research should prioritize larger, multi-center cohorts, the use of validated lifestyle measures, and the evaluation of structured interventions to mitigate long-term metabolic risk in women with prior GDM.
