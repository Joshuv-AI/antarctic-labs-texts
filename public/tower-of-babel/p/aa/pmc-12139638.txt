Navigating a Case of Swyer Syndrome with Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: K. Subramanyam, Dilip Johny, Amita Rao, Aditya Ojha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 38-year-old female underwent septal closure for a large ostium secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) as she had progressive dyspnea. She was also found to have primary amenorrhea, for which she was evaluated. The clinical examination and investigations revealed a female phenotype and a male karyotype (46 XY). She was diagnosed with Swyer syndrome and subsequently started on hormone replacement therapy. This case highlights the presence of congenital heart disease and large ostium secundum ASD with left to right shunt in patients with Swyer syndrome, which has not been reported in the literature.

INTRODUCTION

Swyer syndrome is a rare genetic disorder, classified as a disorder of sex development (DSD), which is characterized by the presence of a female phenotype with female external genitalia, uterus, and ovaries but has a male karyotype (46 XY). They are called DSD XY females, previously also termed sex reversal, intersex, or pseudohermaphroditism.[1] The most frequent causes of 46 XY females are androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS) and gonadal dysgenesis. The prevalence of 46 XY females is 6.4/100,000 live female births, whereas the prevalence for AIS is 4.1/100,000 and gonadal dysgenesis is 1.5/100,000.[2] 10%–20% of women with the syndrome have a deletion in the DNA binding region of the sex-determining region of the Y chromosome (SRY) gene, while in the remaining 80%–90% of cases, the SRY gene is normal and mutations in other testis-determining factors are probably implicated.[3] After fertilization, a sequential pattern follows in utero, leading to sexual differentiation. The genotype of the embryo is established first, followed by the differentiation of gonads into testes or ovaries. The presence of a single gene determinant (testis-determining factor) on the short arm of the Y chromosome results in differentiating into testes. In the absence or nonfunctioning of the Y chromosome, the primordial gonads differentiate into ovaries by default. This differentiation of the primordial gonads will lead to an alteration of the hormonal milieu of the fetus, which in turn results in the corresponding differentiation of the internal and external genitalia. In the absence of effective male sex hormones, the external genitalia also develop into female external genitalia by default.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 38-year-old female was evaluated for progressive dyspnea for 6 months. A transthoracic echocardiography and transesophageal echocardiography showed congenital heart disease with 12 mm ostium secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) with left to right shunt with a Pulmonary Artery Systolic Pressure (PASP) of 40 mmHg, with normal biventricular function. Furthermore, transesophageal echocardiography revealed adequate rims. She underwent ASD device closure with 20 mm cocoon septal occluder deployed across the ASD successfully with no residual shunt [Figure 1], the procedure was uneventful without any complications. The patient gave a history of primary amenorrhea, for which she was evaluated further. She was 154 cm tall, weighed 55 kg, and had a webbed neck. Breast development was Tanner Stage 3. The axillary and pubic hairs were sparse. Differential diagnoses for primary amenorrhea in this case included Turner syndrome, AIS, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, anatomical abnormalities of the genital tract, and other causes of gonadal dysgenesis [Figure 2].

Differential diagnosis of swyer syndrome

External genitalia appeared as a normal female with a normal vagina. Abdominal examination was normal. The ultrasound (USG) abdomen showed a hypoplastic uterus with streak gonads. Hormonal analysis revealed elevated levels of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and normal luteinizing hormone, estrogen, and testosterone levels. Blood was sent for genotyping after obtaining consent from the patient, which showed an XY chromosomal pattern. Patient’s conventional karyotyping analysis showed abnormal karyotype – 46, XY,15ps+ [Figure 3]. Following which the fluorescent in situ hybridization analysis was done and showed one signal for chromosome X (green) and one signal for chromosome Y (red) in all the interphase and metaphase cells. The presence of chromosomes X and Y in all the cells examined [Figure 4]. In view of clinical features and laboratory tests, a diagnosis of Swyer syndrome was made. The patient was counseled about her condition, and hormone replacement therapy was initiated. Patient came for follow-up after 1 month and was doing well. For continuation of her HRT, she was referred to an endocrinologist.

DISCUSSION

DSD is a condition in which an individual with one X chromosome and one Y chromosome in each cell, the pattern normally found in males, has atypical or female external genitalia, caused by incomplete intrauterine masculinization with or without the presence of Müllerian structures. With no current scientific evidence to the contrary, 15ps+ is considered to be a normal variant and seen in the general population with no significant clinical manifestation.

As her karyotyping revealed an XY chromosomal pattern, she was diagnosed as XY female due to Swyer syndrome. 45X/46 XY mosaicism was considered and ruled out after karyotyping, as patients having a 45 X cell line are at increased risk for the spectrum of cardiovascular anomalies associated with classic 45 X gonadal dysgenesis (Turner syndrome). An important differentiating feature from Turner syndrome is the height of the patient, which is normal or more in Swyer syndrome, which is postulated to be due to the Y chromosome. It may also be due to low levels of sex steroids allowing for a delayed epiphyseal closure.[4]

The etiology of this 46 XY gonadal dysgenesis is thought to be a short arm Y chromosome deletion involving an SRY that leads to inhibition of SRY function or mutation of SRY function. These inhibit testicular differentiation and inhibit anti-Mullerian hormone production. As a result, the male hormonal milieu is suppressed, resulting in the formation of the female external and internal genital tract. Ovarian tissue develops in the absence of a testis determining factor and also, as a result, the action of anti-testis genes such as DAX1, Rspo1, and WNT4. Children present commonly with delayed puberty and amenorrhea. A high incidence of gonadoblastoma and germ cell malignancies has been reported, and hence gonadectomy is suggested.[5] The management strategies of these patients include the following:

The link seen between 46 XY developmental sexual disorder (DSD) and ASD shows a complicated relationship of genetic factors that affect both sexual development and heart growth. Research points out that changes in the GATA4 gene, which are important for forming the heart and gonads, can appear as both DSD and heart issues. New results suggest that certain harmful changes in GATA4, like c. 487C>T (p. Pro163Ser), are linked to 46 XY DSD patients who have ASD. This indicates lower transcriptional activity, hinting at a negative impact on GATA4 function.[78] Mutations in this gene are recognized to affect how transcription is regulated, leading to different traits in people with GATA4 issues, thus connecting DSD and ASD. Since there is no literature stating a case of congenital heart disease and Swyer syndrome, hence this case report will help in further study to establish any association.

CONCLUSION

The early diagnosis of Swyer syndrome is very challenging in these patients, as they are investigated when they present with primary amenorrhea. Association of congenital heart disease is rare and, if present, needs to be treated accordingly. It requires a multidisciplinary approach for the induction of puberty, prevention of osteopenia, gonadectomy, and management of infertility.
