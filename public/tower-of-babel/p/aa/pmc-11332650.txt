Small RNA-Seq and real time rt-qPCR reveal islet miRNA released under stress conditions
Authors: Bssam Aljani, Annett Lindner, Marc Weigelt, Min Zhao, Virag Sharma, Ezio Bonifacio, Peter Jones, Anne Eugster
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReplacement of beta cells through transplantation is a potential therapeutic approach for individuals with pancreas removal or poorly controllable type 1 diabetes. However, stress and death of beta cells pose significant challenges. Circulating miRNA has emerged as potential biomarkers reflecting early beta cell stress and death, allowing for timely intervention. The aim of this study was to identify miRNAs as potential biomarkers for beta cell health. Literature review combined with small RNA sequencing was employed to select islet-enriched miRNA. The release of those miRNA was assessed by RT-qPCR in vivo, using a streptozotocin induced diabetes mouse model and in vitro, through mouse and human islets exposed to varying degrees of hypoxic and cytokine stressors. Utilizing the streptozotocin induced model, we identified 18 miRNAs out of 39 candidate islet-enriched miRNA to be released upon islet stress in vivo. In vitro analysis of culture supernatants from cytokine and/or hypoxia stressed islets identified the release of 45 miRNAs from mouse and 8 miRNAs from human islets. Investigation into the biological pathways targeted by the cytokine- and/or hypoxia-induced miRNA suggested the involvement of MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways in both mouse and human islets. We have identified miRNAs associated with beta cell health and stress. The findings allowed us to propose a panel of 47 islet-related human miRNA that is potentially valuable for application in clinical contexts of beta cell transplantation and presymptomatic early-stage type 1 diabetes.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic autoimmune disease resulting from T cell- mediated destruction of insulin producing beta cells in the pancreatic islets leading to insulin secretion insufficiency and lifelong exogenous insulin dependency.1 The cornerstones in T1D management are exogenous insulin replacement and close blood glucose monitoring. Alternative management strategies include islet beta cell replacement such as donor islet transplantation or stem cell derived beta cell transplants.2,3 A major challenge impeding the success of beta cell transplants is the early loss of functional beta cells during the immediate post-transplantation period in an inflammatory host environment.4,5 Non-invasive biomarkers revealing islet stress would provide the ability to regularly monitor graft status and potentially allow intervention in a timely manner.6

MicroRNAs (miRNA), single-stranded RNA molecules comprising 21–25 nucleotides, function as post-transcriptional gene silencers.7 They are measurable in the blood and in almost all bodily fluids,8–12 using various technologies, including RT-qPCR and next generation small RNA-seq.13,14 miRNAs have been implicated in T1D pathogenesis and beta cell survival, with reported alterations in circulating miRNA profiles in patients.15–19 In particular, miR-375 has been extensively described as a biomarker in the context of beta cell death and diabetes.19–24 Thus, miRNAs have been proposed as biomarkers for beta cell stress and T1D disease progression25,26 and to monitor beta cell transplantation success.6,19–27–31

Here, the potential of miRNAs to serve as biomarkers for beta cell stress and death during the development of diabetes and after islet transplantation therapy for type 1 diabetes was investigated. Using literature searches and small RNA-seq, islet-enriched miRNAs were identified and their utility as biomarkers in in vivo and in vitro islet stress models was assessed. Through in silico analysis, target genes of potential biomarker miRNAs and pathways regulated by these miRNAs were identified. We suggest that the miRNA panel assembled in this study may be of value for noninvasive monitoring of pre-symptomatic early-stage T1D, or during intervention therapies including islet transplantation.

Methods

In vivo experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Landesdirektion Sachsen or the South West– Central Bristol Research Ethics Committee (Reference 20/SW/0074). Ten-week-old C57BL/6JRj mice were injected intraperitoneally with either 225 mg/kg Streptozotocin (STZ) (n = 57 mice) or PBS (45 mice). Body weight and blood glucose were measured 14 days before-, at the day of- and daily after treatment, until day 4. Blood samples were collected before treatment (−14 day) and at day 1, day 3, and day 4. Blood was left at room temperature for 30 min for full clotting, centrifuged for 10 min and serum collected and stored at −80°C. Diabetes was diagnosed if blood glucose exceeded 300 mg/dl.

Mouse and human islets and serum for in vitro experiments

Islets from 6- to 8-week-old CD-1 or C57BL/6JRj mice were isolated by ficoll density gradient as described.32 Islet equivalents (IEQ) were calculated and islets were either snap-frozen and stored at −80°C until further prsocessing or cultured in RPMI (10% FBS, 1% P/S & 1% L-glut) overnight at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 20% O2.

Human islets were obtained from King’s College London Human Islet Research Tissue Bank. The islets were cultured in CMRL-1066 (PAN-Biotech) and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 or 20% O2.

Human serum was obtained from 6 healthy adults (3 males, 3 females, age range 27–45 years) providing informed consent; the study was approved by the Ethikkommission an der TU Dresden (EK502122018).

miRNA extraction and quantification by RT-qPCR

Extraction of miRNA from islet and pancreas tissue samples or from 200 μl serum or cell culture supernatant was performed with the miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen) or the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen), respectively. Total RNA was quantified and qualitative miRNA analysis was performed by Bioanalyzer (small RNA kit (Agilent). cDNA was synthesized using miScript II RT Kit (Qiagen). 3 μl 1:5 diluted total cDNA was pre-amplified for 16 cycles (1× 94°C for 1 min, 16× (55°C for 40 s and 72°C for 30 s), 1 × 72°C for 6 min) with the TaKaRa Ex Taq® Hot start version kit (Takara Bio) in 20 μl with 0.2 μM universal primer, 0.2 μM corresponding target primers, 1X (2 mM) Ex Taq Buffer (Mg2+plus), 5 U/μl TaKaRa Ex Taq HS and 200 μM of each, dATP, dGTP and dCTP final. qPCR of pre-amplified cDNA was done in 20 μl TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio) with 0.5 μM miScript primer assay (Qiagen) for each miRNA target of interest and 0.5 μM universal primer final. qPCR conditions were adjusted to the LightCylcer480 (Roche) or ViiA 7 (Applied Biosystems). The ID and sequence of mature miRNA targets were retrieved from the microRNA database (miRBase version 22.1). ΔΔCq method was used for RT-qPCR data analysis. Fold change was calculated as: 2^(-ΔΔCq). Endogenous or exogenous reference miRNA were used for normalization (Supplementary Table S1).33

miRNA profiling using small RNA-seq (NGS)

Library preparation was done with the NEXTflex Small RNA-Seq Kit V3 (NOVA-5132-06, Bioo Scientific). 50 ng total RNA was used for ligation of the Bioo Scientific NEXTflex 3’ 4N adenylated adapter and the Bioo Scientific NEXTflex 5’ 4N adapters, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA. Purified 1st strand products were PCR amplified for 25 cycles, adding sample-specific barcode sequence. After size section (8% PAGE) and cleanup (NEXTflex Cleanup) Illumina libraries were equimolarly pooled for 75 bp single read sequencing on Illumina NextSeq 550. Raw reads were trimmed using Cutadapt v1.8.134 and the primer specific for smallRNAseq “TGGAATTCTCGGGTGCCAAGG” was removed. Subsequent analysis was performed with reads >15 bp using the QuickMIRSeq pipeline.35 Briefly, the QuickMIRSeq pipeline first created a database of small RNA molecules for the species of interest (mouse). The second step involved the mapping of the raw reads to the database of interest. Identical reads were collapsed into unique reads within and across samples, thereby reducing computational time. Reads that were mapped to miRNA molecules with mismatches were remapped to the reference genome to remove spurious hits to miRNA molecules and thereby reducing false positive matches. Lastly, the strand information was also taken into account. QuickMIRSeq pipeline was used for expression quantification and the resulting count matrix was then used to identify differentially expressed miRNAs using the DESeq2.36 miRNA with an adjust p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg) of less than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Immunofluorescence staining

Pancreatic sections were stained overnight with anti-insulin antibody (Abcam-ab7842) followed by 30 min incubation with secondary antibody (Goat anti-guinea pig IgG (H+L), alexa fluor 488) followed by a 1 h incubation with anti- glucagon (Dako-A0565) and anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling-9661 L) antibodies and a final 30 min incubation with secondary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L), alexa fluor 568) and DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). The slides were examined using SP5 I laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica microsystems). Five randomly selected fields from 2 slides per mouse, each containing multiple islets, were examined. Immunofluorescence signals were analyzed and quantified using ImageJ to determine the mean intensity of immunostaining for insulin, glucagon, and caspase-3. Percentages (e.g., % insulin) were subsequently calculated to represent the proportion of positive staining events relative to the total counts (e.g., insulin + glucagon). Statistical analysis was conducted using One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD for multiple comparisons.

Apoptosis and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion assays

Islets were cultured in RPMI containing pro-inflammatory cytokines mixes of recombinant murine IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ (mild cytokine mix: 1 ng/ml IL-1β, 5 ng/ml TNF-α and 5 ng/ml IFN-γ; aggressive cytokine mix: 20 ng/ml IL-1β, 100 ng/ml TNF-α and 100 ng/ml IFN-γ) for 3, 6 and 24 hours (h). To induce hypoxia, the cell culture incubators were set to 1% O2, 5% CO2 and 94% N2. Caspase activity was measured with the Caspase-Glo® 3/7 assay (Promega) in the GloMax-96 Promega. Glucose stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) was done by incubating islets treated as described above in 2 mM or 20 mM glucose solution at 37°C for 1 h. After centrifugation (2 min 2000 rpm, 4°C) the supernatant was snap-frozen and stored at − 80°C. Secreted insulin was quantified from a 100 μl sample using a mouse insulin ELISA kit (Thermo Scientific).

Statistical and bioinformatical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in R with the one- or two-way ANOVA method and statistical significance was established at a two-tailed p-value <0.05. Multiple comparisons were tested by Tukey’s honestly significant difference post-hoc test. Student’s t-test was used when analyzing two groups. Pearson correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r) was used to determine the association between miRNA level and blood glucose level in mice and apoptosis activation in cultured islets. For pathway enrichment analysis, all miRNA with significant changes in release upon stress culture were pooled and their target genes were extracted using miRNAtap (1 0.18129/B9.bioc.miRNAtap) and (org.Mm.db1 0.18129/B9.bioc.org.Mm.eg.db) R packages for mouse and mirDIP for human.37 Only target genes that were not shared between miRNA were selected (“unique targets”) and used for KEGG pathway analysis using the clusterProfiler package.38 Pathways with p < 0.05 were considered.

Results

Identification of mouse and human islet- specific miRNA

Targeted- and non- targeted approaches were used to identify miRNAs highly expressed in mouse islets compared to serum. A panel of 70 miRNAs was designed based on literature review (Supplementary Table S2) and tested by RT-qPCR in BL6 mouse islet- and serum samples (N = 3 each). Of these, 29 miRNAs had > 2-fold higher and significant (unadjusted p < 0.05) expression in islets relative to serum (Figure 1(A,B), Supplementary Table S2). Small RNA-seq performed on islet and serum samples from BL6 mice identified 194 upregulated miRNAs in islets as compared to serum (Figure 1(C,D)), including 27 of those identified by RT-qPCR (Figure 1(E)). Literature review also led to a panel of 30 potentially islet enriched human miRNA (Supplementary Table S3).39,40 Of these, 13 miRNAs had significantly higher concentrations in human islets as compared to healthy human serum (Figure 1F,G, Supplementary Table S3). In vivo testing of islet-enriched miRNA

BL6 mice were treated with STZ (n = 57) or PBS (n = 45) and monitored for blood glucose and body weight for 4 days. Mice were categorized as non-diabetic PBS-treated (n = 45), non-diabetic (blood glucose <16.7 mmol/L) STZ-treated (n = 16), and diabetic (blood glucose >16.7 mmol/L) STZ-treated (n = 41). The diabetic STZ-treated group had elevated blood glucose values within one day after STZ treatment with weight loss at day 2 after treatment (Supplementary Figure S1). Diabetic STZ-treated mice also had markedly lower frequencies of insulin-positive cells and higher glucagon-positive cells in islets as compared to non-diabetic mice (Supplementary Figure S2A and B). Significantly higher frequency of caspase induction was measured in diabetic STZ-treated mice compared to controls, suggesting ongoing apoptosis. After 4 days, the serum content of 39 islet-enriched miRNA, selected amongst miRNA found through literature search and Small RNA-seq as, described above, was measured from 30 diabetic-, 11 non-diabetic STZ-treated- and 28 non-diabetic PBS-treated mice using RT-qPCR (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table S4). Increases in 18 miRNAs were observed in the STZ-treated mice, including 13 miRNA that were increased only in the diabetic STZ-treated mice and 6 miRNAs in both diabetic- and non-diabetic STZ-treated mice (Figure 2). In vitro inflammatory- and hypoxic stress induces miRNA release from mouse islets

In vitro cultured mouse islets were exposed to inflammatory- and hypoxic stress and apoptotic death and insulin-secretory function was determined over 24 h. Inflammatory stress caused by mild- or aggressive cytokine mixes equally induced apoptosis as measured by changes in caspase-3 activity after 24 h (Figure 3(A)). This effect was amplified by the addition of hypoxic stress, with significant changes in caspase-3 activity measurable already after 3 h or 6 h, when combined with aggressive- or mild cytokine mix, respectively. Combined, cytokine and hypoxia treatment had marked effects on the insulin secretory capacity of isolated islets, with elevations in basal insulin release and a loss of further glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, which is consistent with beta cell damage (Figure 3(B)). The effects on beta cell function were exacerbated by more prolonged treatment with the stressors. Treatment with the aggressive cytokine mix or with hypoxia alone, increased basal insulin secretion after 6 or 24 h, and prevented further stimulation of insulin release in response to 20 mM glucose. In contrast, exposure to the mild cytokine mix alone had little effect on either basal- or glucose-stimulated insulin release. Together, these observations demonstrate the ability of relatively short-term exposure to hypoxia and inflammatory cytokines, either singly or in combination, to be associated with beta cell dysfunction.

A total of 64 islet-enriched miRNAs, selected from the STZ-model and NGS data (Supplementary Table S5) were quantified in the supernatant of the in vitro cultures using qPCR. Increases associated with inflammatory- or hypoxic stress were found for 45 miRNAs, including 36 that remained significant after Tukey’s correction (Figure 4). The miRNA changes varied by time and stress-type. Increases after 3 h were minor and largely restricted to the aggressive cytokine condition (12 miRNA). After 6 h, increases were observed for 25 miRNAs, including 18 that remained significant after correction. The greatest increases were observed in the mild cytokine plus hypoxia condition. No changes were induced by hypoxia alone after 3 h, and no miRNA was increased exclusively in the hypoxia alone condition after 6 h. After 24 h, increases were observed for 41 miRNAs (34 significant), including 26 of 30 with increases observed at 3 or 6 h. The miRNA increases appeared to follow three time-courses. Some miRNAs reached their maximum increase already after 6 h (e.g., mmu-miR-99b-5p, mmu-miR-7b-5p, mmu-miR-7a-5p), a minority increased at 6 h and increased further at 24 h (e.g., mmu-miR-485-5p), and others were significantly increased only at 24 h (e.g., mmu-miR-7a-2-3p). In vitro human islet stress and miRNA release

Human islets were cultured in vitro under combined inflammatory (aggressive cytokine mix) and hypoxic stress conditions. The release of 9 miRNAs that were identified from the STZ model or in the in vitro islet cultures and with a recorded human homologue (Supplementary Table S6) was quantified by RT-qPCR after 24- and 48 h culture (Figure 5(A)). Eight of the 9 tested miRNA were increased in the culture supernatants after 24 h stress condition. This increase was still measurable for 5 of the miRNAs after 48 h (hsa-let-7e-5, hsa-miR141-3p, hsa-miR148-3p, hsa-miR200-3p, hsa-miR30c-5p). Pathway enrichment analysis of the 6407 genes encoding the mRNAs targeted by the 8 miRNA with a significant increase upon stress conditions revealed highest enrichment in MAPK signaling with 150 genes (p = 1e-7) targeted by 6 mRNAs (hsa-has-375, hsa-mhas200c-3p, hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-miR-129, hsa-miR-141-3p, hsa-miR-148a-3p and PI3K-Akt signaling with 177 genes (p = 1e-7) targeted by 5 miRNAs (hsa-200c-3p, has let-7e-5p, hsa-miR-129-5p, hsa-miR-141-3p, hsa-miR-148a-3p) (Figure 5(B)). From the 52 enriched pathways found, 39 (out of 79) had been found to be enriched in genes targeted by miRNA significant in the mouse counterpart in vitro cytokine and hypoxia stress conditions (Supplementary Table S7). Discussion

The utility of biomarkers in non-invasively monitoring islet cell loss induced by inflammatory and hypoxic stress has long been acknowledged. Here, we present a systematic approach to identify islet-enriched mouse and human miRNAs increased in conditions of islet death, cytokine stress and hypoxia. The genes regulated by these miRNAs are enriched in MAPK and PI3K-Akt dependent pathways. Based on our findings, we propose a panel of miRNA that may potentially be valuable for monitoring islet stress, function or potentially survival.

Several groups have proposed miRNAs as biomarkers to diagnose beta-cell stress and death. Methods to identify suitable miRNAs have included profiling patient sera before and after islet transplantation,27–31 during T1D,15,17,23 or pancreatitis19 and using in vitro islet stress models25,41 or in vivo mouse models.20 Techniques included small RNA-seq, qPCR and microarray. Here, we employed a systematic approach, combining literature review with small RNA sequencing followed by in vivo and in vitro experimental confirmation. We propose a panel of 47 human miRNAs, accompanied by 6 reference miRNAs for normalizing qPCR measurements of islet markers (Table 1). We selected miRNAs that were found to be islet-enriched or to be released under stress and were either found: 1. In more than one experimental setting (including tissue-specificity testing, in vivo STZ-model, in vitro mouse or human islet model); 2. In at least one of the human islet experiments conducted in this study; 3. In one experimental setting and confirmed by NGS to be islet-enriched. Among those proposed are 6 novel islet-enriched miRNAs (miR-487b-3p, miR-325, miR-384, miR-340-3p, miR-485-5p, miR-485-3p) discovered through small RNA sequencing and experimentally confirmed in our studies as being released by islets exposed to experimental stressors. The remaining 41 miRNAs listed have been previously described as islet or stress markers (see references listed, Table 1). Pathway analysis of gene targets for miRNAs species, which were significantly increased in both mouse and human islets in vitro on exposure to inflammatory and hypoxic stress conditions revealed 39 pathways shared between mouse and human. The top 3 pathways (PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and Ras signaling pathway) found in the human experiments were also the most significant in the related mouse experimental setting.

The implication of the MAPK signaling pathway in islet cell stress was reported previously, with stimulation of mitogen activated kinases (MAPKs) in cytokine-stressed beta cells.48 PI3K and mTOR have been shown to be important for beta cell function during stress and interaction between components of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway has been reported to be regulated by miRNAs in the course of islet damage.49–52 In the context of type 2 diabetes (T2D), pathological activation of the Ras pathway has been shown to increase beta cell stress, leading to de-differentiation and impaired insulin secretion.53,54 Stress-associated secretion of miRNAs (miR-29b-3p, miR-216a-5p, miR-148a-3p, miR-375) controlling the PI3K-Akt pathway and mTOR has been described.29 To preserve beta cell function, stress-associated activation of pathways, such as the MAPK, the PI3K-Akt and the Ras signaling pathways, and apoptosis might be accompanied by upregulation and controlled secretion of miRNAs regulating them. Secreted miRNAs may serve as messengers, communicating distress to induce damage repair mechanisms by other cells. Secretion can be nonspecific release of miRNA from damaged islet cells or it can be a controlled process; our in vitro islet-stress experiments suggest that at least some miRNAs tested here follow a controlled release, as variable secretion patterns are observed over time.

ErbB signaling was another significant pathway targeted by miRNAs found in both mouse and human experimental settings. The epidermal growth factor receptors (ErbBs) mediate proliferative activity as well as differentiation of islet cells. Stress may be associated with an upregulation of miRNAs regulating such cellular activities to preserve basal cell function.

The study has several limitations. A major limitation regards the STZ model. STZ is not completely specific to β-cells since STZ enters cells via GLUT2, which is also expressed in the liver, kidney and intestine cells.55 Thus, it remains uncertain to which degree those tissues are affected by STZ in our model. Supplies of cadaveric human islets were limited and this in turn restricted the number of human miRNAs that could be tested and confirmed in vitro (Figure 5). Furthermore, islet and serum samples used for human islet-enriched panel discovery were obtained from differing donors (islets from deceased donors and serum from healthy adults), which is unlike the setting for the discovery of the mouse islet-specific panel and we cannot rule out that the islet and serum miRNomes differ between individuals. Third, the time-course of miRNA release under stress conditions suggested different time-related patterns of release for miRNAs, which requires further investigation. Finally, the value of the panel requires validation in experimental and clinical setting, which was beyond the scope of this study.

We present a bioinformatic and experimental study that has identified a panel of potential biomarker miRNAs for detecting beta cell stress both in vitro and in vivo. We propose that this biomarker panel may be of value for noninvasive monitoring of pre-symptomatic early-stage T1D, during intervention therapies such as islet transplantation to monitor beta cell stress and potentially survival or, taking it even further, to monitor loss of islet cell function in T2D.
