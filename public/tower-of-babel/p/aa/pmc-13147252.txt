Moderate mitral regurgitation: when to prefer clip over medical therapy
Authors: Federica Coletti, Myriam Carpenito, Lorenzo Guarino, Valeria Cammalleri, Elisabetta Ricottini, Rosetta Melfi, Simona Mega, Gian Paolo Ussia, Francesco Grigioni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional mitral regurgitation (FMR) is common in patients with heart failure (HF) and is associated with an adverse prognosis. Therefore, both ventricular and atrial phenotypes of FMR represent relevant therapeutic targets in clinical practice. Although randomized clinical trials have primarily focused on the treatment of severe FMR, the prognostic implications and management of moderate FMR have emerged as a topic of increasing interest. This review aims to provide an overview of epidemiology, prognosis, and management of moderate FMR, with a focus on the role of pharmacological treatments and emerging percutaneous strategies.Keywords: Ventricular functional mitral regurgitation, Atrial functional mitral regurgitation, Heart failure, Optimized medical therapy, Transcatheter therapies

Introduction

Functional mitral regurgitation (FMR) is secondary to alterations in the geometry and function of the mitral valve, the left ventricle, and the left atrium, occurring without significant structural abnormalities of the mitral valve apparatus. Two morpho-functional phenotypes can be identified: the ventricular form of FMR (vFMR), mainly associated with ventricular remodelling and dysfunction, and the atrial form of FMR (aFMR), primarily related to annular dilatation and loss of contractile function in the context of atrial remodelling. Both phenotypes are common in patients with heart failure (HF) and are associated with a significant increase in morbidity and mortality. FMR can also be categorized based on the severity of valve regurgitation. Although randomized clinical trials initially focused on the treatment of severe FMR, the prognostic role, and management of moderate FMR represent a topic of growing interest. This review focuses on the epidemiology, prognosis, quantification, and therapy of moderate FMR.

Epidemiology of moderate functional mitral regurgitation

Data on the prevalence of moderate FMR in patients with HF mainly derive from observational studies (Figure 1). Studies including patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) have reported a prevalence of moderate FMR ranging from 6% to 32%.1 Abel et al.2 reported a prevalence of 10% for moderate FMR in a cohort of patients with heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF). In heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), the prevalence of moderate FMR ranges from 7% to 14%.3 However, to date, no studies have specifically assessed the exact prevalence of moderate FMR—vFMR and aFMR—across the various HF phenotypes.

The clinical spectrum of moderate functional mitral regurgitation (FMR) in heart failure (HF) includes disease prevalence across different HF phenotypes, prognostic impact, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic strategies. HFrEF, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; HFmrEF, heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction; HFpEF, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction; EROA, effective regurgitant orifice area; sPAP, systolic pulmonary artery pressure; FMR, functional mitral regurgitation; OMT, optimized medical therapy; CRT, cardiac resynchronization therapy; M-TEER, transcatheter edge-to-edge mitral valve repair.

Quantification: when is functional mitral regurgitation significant?

Echocardiographic quantification of FMR has posed several challenges over the years, due to the dynamic nature of regurgitation, its dependence on volume status, the geometry of the regurgitant orifice, and the limited applicability of individual quantification methods in routine clinical practice (Figure 1). These challenges are reflected in the historical discordance and frequent revisions of European and American guidelines regarding the definition of severity thresholds. American recommendations initially differed from European ones, which defined severe FMR using a lower effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) compared with degenerative mitral regurgitation (DMR) (20 vs. 40 mm2). Subsequently, European and American guidelines converged, both adopting reduced EROA thresholds (20 mm2) for FMR, before ultimately reverting to higher EROA thresholds (40 mm2) for severe FMR, identical to those used for DMR. However, epidemiological data show that EROA values >40 mm2 are relatively uncommon in FMR. Benfari et al.4 showed that FMR quantified by EROA is associated with excess mortality—even independently of ventricular remodelling—starting from the threshold of 10 mm2.4 Therefore, the excess mortality related to FMR occurs at EROA values well below the guideline-defined threshold of 40 mm2 for severe regurgitation. These findings underscore the importance of both accurate FMR quantification, which is strongly correlated with prognosis, and careful contextual interpretation of EROA and regurgitant volume values. Moreover, although EROA remains the principal severity index for FMR, a multiparametric approach is recommended, including assessment of vena contracta, regurgitant fraction, pulmonary venous flow, left atrial, and left ventricular dimensions.

The dynamic change in valvular regurgitation during exercise is another key consideration. Exercise echocardiography may play a particularly relevant role in patients who are asymptomatic or who present with moderate or moderate-to-severe FMR at rest. Exercise-induced increases in the degree of MR are associated with reduced exercise capacity and represent an independent predictor of adverse long-term prognosis. In addition, an increase in systolic pulmonary artery pressure during exercise is associated with a dynamic increase in EROA and, similarly, with a significant increase in mortality.5,6

Treatment of moderate functional mitral regurgitation

The treatment of severe FMR follows an integrated approach including optimized medical therapy (OMT), cardiac resynchronization therapy, and ablation of the arrhythmic substrate—depending on the specific FMR phenotype— as well as mechanical correction through surgical or transcatheter approaches (Figure 1). To date, there are no standardized recommendations for managing moderate FMR, vFMR, or aFMR, except for standard HF medical therapy.

Non-valvular treatments

Ventricular functional mitral regurgitation

In vFMR, which is generally associated with HFrEF, OMT is based on the use of disease-modifying therapies for HFrEF, including beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE inhibitors) or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2 inhibitors). Loop diuretics may also be used to reduce symptoms and signs of congestion.

To date, the literature provides limited data on the effects of these treatments in patients with moderate FMR. The PRIME trial (Pharmacological Reduction of Functional, Ischemic Mitral Regurgitation) compared valsartan with ARNI in patients with predominantly mild-to-moderate FMR and left ventricular dysfunction, showing a greater MR reduction in the ARNI-treated group, mediated by favourable effects on preload and afterload as well as natriuretic properties.7

More recently, the DEFORM trial (Dapagliflozin Effect on Functional Mitral Regurgitation and Myocardial Remodelling) demonstrated that dapagliflozin use is associated with a significant improvement in MR and positive cardiac remodelling among patients with both severe and moderate FMR.8 Similarly, in the EFFORT trial (Ertugliflozin for Functional Mitral Regurgitation), which included patients with moderate-to-severe FMR, treatment with ertugliflozin was associated with a significant reduction in MR severity.9 In addition, ertugliflozin treatment was associated with a significant improvement in left ventricular global longitudinal strain and a marked reduction in left atrial volume.9

In addition to OMT, cardiac resynchronization therapy should be considered in eligible patients, as it reduces MR by promoting left ventricular reverse remodelling, improving mechanical dyssynchrony, and enhancing mitral leaflet coaptation.

Atrial functional mitral regurgitation

In aFMR, which is generally associated with atrial fibrillation (AF) and HFpEF, medical therapy includes SGLT2 inhibitors to improve prognosis and loop diuretics to relieve symptoms and signs of congestion. In patients with HFpEF, management of cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities (such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus) is also essential. Rhythm and rate control strategies are particularly relevant in patients with AF, as restoration and maintenance of sinus rhythm are associated with a significant improvement in the severity of MR.10 To date, however, no studies have specifically assessed the impact of these strategies in patients with moderate aFMR.

Transcatheter treatment

The optimization of medical therapy and the use of adjunctive procedures such as cardiac resynchronization therapy or AF ablation represent the first and indispensable steps in the management of FMR. Transcatheter treatment of FMR may be considered in patients who remain symptomatic despite OMT, complementing—but not replacing—medical and device interventions. Currently available transcatheter therapies include repair strategies that improve leaflet coaptation or annular remodelling, and valve replacement strategies. Among repair techniques, transcatheter edge-to-edge mitral valve repair (M-TEER) remains the most widely used approach.

Ventricular functional mitral regurgitation

The most recent ESC/EACTS guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease recommend M-TEER in symptomatic patients with severe vFMR who fulfill clinical and echocardiographic criteria predicting a favourable response to treatment (COAPT criteria) (Class I recommendation, Level of Evidence A). This indication is downgraded to a Class IIb recommendation with Level of Evidence B in patients who do not fulfill these criteria, after careful evaluation of alternative therapies such as heart transplantation or ventricular assist devices.

These recommendations are largely based on the results of two major randomized clinical trials—COAPT (Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients with Functional Mitral Regurgitation)11 and MITRA-FR (Percutaneous Repair with the MitraClip Device for Severe Functional/Secondary Mitral Regurgitation)12—which similarly compared M-TEER plus OMT vs. OMT alone, producing contrasting results. While the MITRA-FR trial showed neutral outcomes, the COAPT trial demonstrated a significant prognostic benefit of M-TEER, with reduced HF hospitalizations and mortality.11,12 This discrepancy has been attributed to several factors, including differences in patient selection—patients in COAPT had larger mean EROA values but less dilated ventricles—as well as differences in medical therapy optimization and procedural aspects.

More recently, the MATTERHORN trial (Transcatheter Versus Surgical Mitral Valve Repair in Patients With Heart Failure and Secondary Mitral Regurgitation) demonstrated that, among patients with HF and severe FMR, M-TEER is not inferior to surgery for efficacy outcomes, while offering a more favourable safety profile.13

Nevertheless, the role of M-TEER in patients with moderate or moderate-to-severe FMR remains uncertain. In this context, the recently published RESHAPE-HF2 trial (Randomized Investigation of the MitraClip Device in Heart Failure: 2nd Trial in Patients with Clinically Significant Functional Mitral Regurgitation)14 evaluated the efficacy of M-TEER with the MitraClip device in addition to OMT, compared with OMT alone, in patients with HF and moderate-to-severe FMR. Between March 2015 and October 2023, the trial enrolled 505 patients with left ventricular ejection fractions between 20% and 50%, persistent symptoms despite OMT, and moderate-to-severe FMR.

Compared with OMT alone, M-TEER significantly reduced the composite end-point of HF hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality at 2 years, significantly decreased HF hospitalizations at 2 years, and improved quality of life at 1 year, as assessed by the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ). These findings are consistent with those of the COAPT trial, supporting the efficacy of M-TEER even in patients with moderate and moderate-to-severe FMR. However, in contrast to COAPT, RESHAPE-HF2 did not show a significant reduction in all-cause mortality.

These differences likely reflect variations in study cohorts across the trials. Similar to COAPT, RESHAPE-HF2 excluded patients with right ventricular dysfunction, severe tricuspid regurgitation, severe pulmonary hypertension, and haemodynamic instability, who were instead included in MITRA-FR. Patients enrolled in RESHAPE-HF2 had a mean EROA of 25 mm2—only a minority had an EROA ≥40 mm2 consistent with severe MR—whereas COAPT and MITRA-FR patients had higher mean EROA values (40 and 20 mm2, respectively). Moreover, RESHAPE-HF2 patients had lower levels of N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), better renal function, and received more intensive OMT, despite still limited use of SGLT2 inhibitors.

Overall, these data suggest that patients in RESHAPE-HF2 were at an earlier disease stage than those enrolled in COAPT and MITRA-FR, with less severe MR, lower NT-proBNP levels, improved renal function, and greater medical therapy tolerance. RESHAPE-HF2 therefore confirms the favourable results of COAPT in a population with less advanced HF. However, these characteristics may also explain the lack of a significant mortality benefit. Notably, a subanalysis of the RESHAPE-HF2 trial found that a recent HF hospitalization (within the year before randomization) was associated with a greater prognostic benefit after M-TEER. Thus, the selection of patients with moderate and moderate-to-severe FMR who may benefit from early interventional strategies should prioritize truly symptomatic subjects with a history of recent or recurrent HF hospitalizations, in the absence of right ventricular dysfunction or severe pulmonary hypertension.

Atrial functional mitral regurgitation

M-TEER has also proven to be a safe and effective strategy for reducing the MR severity in patients with atrial phenotype, with durable long-term results.15 A recent post hoc analysis of the MATTERHORN trial demonstrated a similar safety and efficacy profile of M-TEER both in atrial and ventricular phenotypes. However, further studies are needed to clarify the role of TEER in improving prognosis in patients with moderate aFMR.

Conclusions

Moderate FMR is an increasingly recognized clinical entity due to its high prevalence and prognostic significance in HF patients, as well as challenges in accurate quantification and the absence of standardized treatment recommendations. OMT remains the first and most indispensable step in the management of patients with moderate FMR.

More recently, based on robust evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of transcatheter strategies in the treatment of severe FMR, earlier interventional treatment has been explored. In this context, the positive results of the RESHAPE-HF2 trial support transcatheter therapy in patients with HF and moderate to severe FMR, while highlighting the importance of careful patient selection to identify those most likely to benefit.

The management of moderate FMR, therefore, requires a personalized approach considering symptom burden, clinical history, disease stage, exercise response, tolerance to medical therapy, and valvular anatomy. Further studies are needed to confirm the promising findings of the RESHAPE-HF2 trial, assess potential effects on mortality, and guide future updates of guideline recommendations.
