The role and position of palliative care nurses within the Dutch healthcare system: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Ingrid van Zuilekom, Suzanne Metselaar, Fleur Godrie, Myrna Pelgrum-Keurhorst, Berdine Koekoek, Bregje Onwuteaka-Philipsen, Harmieke van Os-Medendorp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGeneralists in palliative care and professionals with specialized expertise, including palliative care nurses, achieve high-quality palliative care. To strengthen their role, it is essential to understand the tasks palliative care nurses perform and their position within the organizations. We aim to accomplish this understanding and explore facilitators and barriers to taking on the role as specialized nurses from both the nurses’ and managers’ perspectives.MethodsA sequential mixed-method design combined an online survey, followed up by five focus groups. Analysis involved descriptive statistics and thematic coding.ResultsA total of 234 palliative care nurses across various care settings answered the survey. They primarily provided physical care (88%), with less focus on psychological (72%), social (68%), and spiritual (68%) domains, and were consulted for fewer than half of their activities. Having no job descriptions often occurred (from 78% in home care and nursing homes to 44% in hospitals). Hospital nurses were more involved in advisory teams (56%) than those in other settings (26% − 28%). Nurses showed leadership by promoting generalists’ expertise, connecting partners, serving as role models, and advocating for palliative care. Focus groups with twelve nurses and nine managers identified facilitators and barriers that palliative care nurses and their managers encounter in embedding their roles and positions. These covered: (1) distinction between generalist and specialist palliative care, (2) collaboration, (3) job descriptions, (4) leadership, (5) visibility, (6) funding and reimbursement.ConclusionsPalliative care nurses fulfill an essential role but experience ambiguity about their role and position. They and their managers emphasized the importance of leadership and advocating for palliative care in their organizations. Visibility, funding, reimbursement, and collaboration are facilitators and barriers to their role and position. There are notable areas for improvement, including joining a palliative care advisory team, having a job description, and finding ways to demonstrate cost-effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03863-7.

Background

Palliative care aims to enhance the quality of life for patients with life-limiting illnesses and vulnerabilities, as well as for their families [1–3]. The demand for palliative care is increasing due to a growing number of individuals affected by dementia, cancer, and other life-threatening or incurable diseases [4, 5]. Palliative care is delivered across various healthcare settings, from hospitals to primary care [6].

Given the widespread need for palliative care, it has been argued that all healthcare professionals (HCPs) should possess the ability to provide basic palliative care. Additionally, some HCPs should have specialized expertise to support their colleagues [7, 8].

One proposed approach to ensuring access to appropriate care is the generalist-plus-specialist palliative care model [7, 9, 10]. As many people now live longer with increased illness burdens and more complex care needs, both generalist and specialist palliative care services are often required [7, 11]. When necessary, patients should have access to HCPs who are specialized in palliative care [12, 13]. These specialists must be equipped to manage complex and challenging cases and collaborate with generalists by providing consultation and assuming care when appropriate [14, 15].

Nurses, at both the vocational and bachelor’s degree levels, can play a significant role in the provision of both generalist and specialized palliative care. They are expected to continue playing a vital role in the foreseeable future [16]. Nurses specialized in palliative care (Palliative care nurses) focus on improving the quality of life for patients and their families by alleviating suffering through early identification, comprehensive assessment, and the treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial, or spiritual issues [17, 18].

Joshi et al. [19] identify key factors that distinguish generalist from specialized palliative home care, including nurses’ availability, time, and competence, the complexity of patients’ symptoms, and organizational financial resources. These factors were associated with differences in perceived security, normalcy of daily life, and symptom management.

In a systematic literature review, Godrie et al. [20] outlined five primary roles for medical and nursing palliative care specialists: care provider, care consultant, educator, researcher, and advocate. Leadership skills were found to be essential across all roles. According to Forbat et al. [21], effective communication and active listening are core competencies for palliative care professionals, including nurses, physicians, and social workers. Hökkä et al. [22] further identified six core competency domains: (1) collaboration with patients, families, and care teams; (2) communication and cultural sensitivity; (3) clinical expertise; (4) psychosocial and spiritual care; (5) ethical and legal understanding; and (6) professional role and leadership. In recent years, considerable attention has been given to the role of nurses in palliative care; however, there remains a lack of clarity and consensus regarding the exact nature of this role.

However, while palliative care is recognized as a formal medical specialty in some countries, this is not the case in the Netherlands [23, 24]. In the Dutch context, palliative care nurses provide generalist and specialist care. The wide range of educational programs available has led to varied job titles and role descriptions, resulting in ambiguity regarding the responsibilities of palliative care nurses. This ambiguity can lead to confusion within healthcare organizations about what to expect from different HCPs specialized in palliative care [25].

In response, the Professional Association of Nurses (V&VN) developed a competency profile for palliative care nurses, within six Entrustable Professional Activities (EPA's) [26, 27] (see Box 1). This profile supplements the existing Dutch Educational Framework for general palliative care [28, 29]. It aims to promote consistency in role definition across different care settings, including home care, hospitals, hospices, and nursing homes, by clearly outlining the responsibilities and expectations for palliative care nurses.

Educational institutions have developed curricula based on these EPA's and defined corresponding competencies or learning outcomes. However, feedback from the field and experiences shared by palliative care nurses suggest their roles remain underutilized in clinical practice. For example, while some nurses are employed full-time in palliative care roles, others can only allocate a small portion of their work hours to such activities. Job titles also vary, with some nurses identified as palliative care nurses and others as consultants. Many nurses report feeling insufficiently empowered after completing their training due to the lack of clarity and consistency in role implementation within their organizations.

To strengthen the role and visibility of palliative care nurses and to further promote the field of palliative care, it is essential to understand the specific tasks these nurses perform, the topics on which generalists and other HCPs consult them, and how both nurses and their managers perceive their position within the organization. This study, therefore, aims to: define the work activities of palliative care nurses, identify the topics with which they are consulted, and explore facilitators and barriers to their roles and professional positioning from both the nurses’ and managers’ perspectives.

Methods

Design

We employed a sequential mixed-methods study design [30, 31] to gain a comprehensive understanding of the role of palliative care nurses. First, we conducted an open online survey with pre-structured, open-ended, and closed questions focused on the work activities of palliative care nurses and the topics on which they were consulted. Based on the survey results, additional questions were posed in the focus groups. Follow-up questions were formulated to address the desired positioning of these nurses and the perceived facilitators and barriers related to embedding their roles [32].

Setting

The online survey was conducted in the Netherlands over two months (May- July 2024). In addition, five focus groups were conducted in September and October 2024. Three focus groups for palliative care nurses were held in the association building in a central area of the Netherlands, and the two focus groups for managers took place online. All focus groups lasted 120 min and were led by the same moderator, the first author of this article, and supported by a researcher of the research group.

Population

The survey participants included vocational and bachelor-level nurses specialized in palliative care who worked in various settings such as hospitals, hospices, nursing homes, and home care environments. Vocational nurses are trained at the Netherlands Qualification Framework (NLQF) [33] level 4, and bachelor nurses at NLQF level 5 (before 2020) or level 6. The NLQF level is based on the European Qualifications Framework (EQF) [34], which comprises eight levels of learning outcomes characterized by knowledge, skills, independence, and responsibilities [34].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants must be palliative care nurses who carry out palliative care in daily practice, and have completed a post-vocational or post-bachelor’s course consisting of at least 21 instructional days at one of the four Dutch Universities of Applied Sciences that offer this postgraduate program.

Participants in the survey could mention their willingness to participate in the focus groups. To ensure a balanced representation in the focus groups, the first and third authors classified these registrations based on the work settings and work experience in palliative care and the University of Applied Science at which their education was completed.

This approach ensured a balanced representation of vocational and bachelor-level nurses from diverse work settings who were educated at different universities in the focus groups. For the focus groups involving managers, participants who were supervising one or more palliative care nurses within their healthcare organizations were included. Efforts have also been made to achieve a balanced distribution of managers, with some closely involved in the care process and others more visible at higher organizational levels, representing diverse work settings.

Recruitment

The survey was shared with alumni of four Universities of Applied Sciences in the Netherlands that provide post-vocational and post-bachelor courses in palliative care. It was also published in the Dutch Nurses Association’s digital newsletters, which disseminated the questionnaire among its members, palliative care nurses. The survey was even published on LinkedIn. After a month, reminders and reposts were sent via LinkedIn. The last question in the survey asked whether respondents wanted to participate in the focus groups and if they would be willing to leave their email addresses so they could be contacted. The managers were approached through the consortium structure of palliative care network coordinators, with the question of whether they wanted to participate in the focus group.

Variables and data collection

The outcomes of this study are how the role of palliative care nurses is fulfilled in care organizations, more specifically, what work activities palliative care nurses perform, the topics on which they were consulted, and the facilitating and barrier factors they and their managers experience regarding their (embedded) role and position in clinical practice. We define a position as the place in the organization where the palliative care nurse is located, or has been placed or arranged. Due to the generalist nature of palliative care, we define the role not as a formal function but as that of a specialized nurse with additional competencies and tasks. Due to the generalist nature of palliative care, we define the role not as a formal function but as that of a specialized nurse with additional competencies and tasks. This conceptualization aligns with Anglin et al.‘s description of a role as a set of expectations, norms, and behaviors associated with a particular position within a social group or organization [35]. We define facilitators as factors that enable embedding, while barriers are factors that obstruct it [36].

Survey

The online questionnaire consisted of 24 questions (Appendix 1), primarily closed-ended, with some open-ended questions. To gain insight into the profile of palliative care nurses, we also collected demographic and work-related characteristics, such as age, sex, current work setting, and the number of patients seen in the last year, as well as whether they combine the role of palliative care nurse with another nursing role (questions 4 to 12). Data regarding the description of their work activities, by whom and for which topics they are consulted about, at what frequency, and how they demonstrate nursing leadership, and finally how satisfied they are about their role was collected using nine closed questions (13 to 22) and two open questions (23 and 24).

Five researchers developed the questionnaire based on their combined practical and academic experiences in palliative care (authors IvZ, SM, FG, BOP, and HOM) and relevant peer-reviewed literature [20, 21], and [37]. The survey was conducted using Qualtrics, which Saxion University of Applied Science provided.

Focus groups

Based on the results of this questionnaire, the study team developed a topic guide (Appendix 2) to explore the experiences of nurses and managers in the embedded role and position of palliative care nurses, including the facilitators and barriers they encounter.

At the outset of the focus group, participants were presented with the questionnaire results. The initial inquiry was whether the participants recognized their work in these results.

The first author led all focus groups, with the third, fifth, and final authors providing alternating support. To mitigate the risk of confirmation bias, peer debriefing, field, and reflexive notes were used following each focus group [38]. The three physical focus groups were audio-recorded, and the two online focus groups were conducted and recorded on Microsoft Teams. The first author carefully reviewed and corrected these recordings.

Analysis

Questionnaire

Respondents who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Quantitative analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and to describe the number and percentages of palliative care nurses’ work and consult activities. A Chi-square test was used to determine whether significant differences existed in work characteristics and satisfaction across different work settings. Due to the large number of tests, p-values less than 0.001 were considered statistically significant.

Questions 23 and 24 (open-ended questions regarding leadership in the role of the palliative care nurse) were analyzed by deductive coding based on the description in the profile of how palliative care nurses demonstrate leadership in their work performance. The first and third authors read and re-read the answers, and the final analyses were discussed with all authors.

Focus group analysis

After each focus group, the first author (IvZ) listened to the audio record and read and reread the transcript to become familiar with the data. The first author transcribed the audio recordings of the focus groups verbatim and analyzed the qualitative data from the focus groups using ATLAS.ti 24 (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development Company). A second researcher (MPK) also independently analyzed and coded the focus groups.

Using an open coding process, codes were assigned to relevant and meaningful statements that reflected the perspectives of palliative care nurses and managers. The analysis focused on identifying facilitators and barriers to embedding the role and position of palliative care nurses. Subsequently, the codes were grouped into themes and sub-themes, reviewed and discussed collaboratively by all authors, and collectively finalized.

Ethics

All respondents were fully informed about the study objectives and provided informed consent. An email with information about consent and confidentiality of the survey data was sent. The email also included a link to the online questionnaire. The mandatory question for consent was the first question in the online questionnaire, which stated: ‘Do you consent to using your answers to the questions in the questionnaire for research purposes?’

If the respondents did not give permission, they could not continue the questionnaire. Informed consent was obtained from all the respondents. All data were collected and analyzed anonymously. We conducted our study following the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki. According to Dutch law (WMO) [39], permission is required from an Ethics Committee [40]. According to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act, our study is exempt from formal review by the ethics committee (METC Office) at Amsterdam UMC, as declared in POS-PZV 2024 − 0157.

Results

Population

A total of 318 nurses initiated the questionnaire, with 315 indicating consent to participate. Nine participants did not complete the questionnaire. Among the 306 remaining respondents, 257 (84%) had completed a palliative care training program, and 243 of them were employed in direct palliative patient care. Of the 243 who met the inclusion criteria, 234 (76%) continued to complete the questionnaire and were included in the analysis.

Table 1 shows general characteristics of these 234 participants. The majority of palliative care nurses were female (95%), primarily worked in home care (44%), and most (65%) had over 20 years of work experience.

Additionally, participants were asked if they combined the role of a palliative care nurse with another role or function. Of all participants, 24% worked solely as palliative care nurses. In contrast, 27% of respondents combined this role with a function of district nurse, 12% with a function of oncology nurse, and 24% with another role or function. In this context, ‘others’ refers to nurses with combined roles, such as consultants in palliative care or nurses specialized in respiratory care or dialysis, who coordinate volunteers or assume additional responsibilities during specific shifts, such as night duty. Approximately 94% of respondents across all work settings were familiar with the profile of the palliative care nurse [26].

Characteristics of the work setting of palliative care nurses

Table 2 reveals that 56% of hospital nurses reported having a job description for a palliative care nurse in their organization. This was significantly lower in hospices (28%), home care (22%), and nursing homes (22%).

The majority of respondents worked throughout the organization and not solely on their teams: in home care (88%), hospitals (54%), nursing homes (82%), and hospices (59%).

Most respondents worked in organizations where an interprofessional consultation team was organized: hospitals (97%), nursing homes (28%), home care (30%), and hospices (35%). This suggests that such teams are more prevalent in hospitals than in nursing homes, home care, or hospices.

Among palliative care nurses, 56% of those employed in hospitals primarily participated in the organization’s consultation team. In contrast, only 31% of palliative care nurses in home care and 28% in nursing homes are involved, while the involvement in hospices is 26%. This highlights the variation in nurses’ participation rates in advisory teams across different types of organizations.

Work activities in direct (palliative) patient care and consultation

Out of the 228 respondents who started by answering the generic characteristics of the work setting, a notable dropout rate was observed once the questions shifted toward their work within the organization and the frequency with which they were consulted about this activity (see Fig. 1). On average, approximately 163 respondents answered each question. No significant differences were found in age, gender, or work setting between those who completed the questionnaire and those who dropped out.

In the inquiry regarding the work activities performed by palliative care nurses and the frequency with which they are consulted about these, a distinction is made between work activities related to the direct (palliative) patient care process and those beyond the direct (palliative) patient care process. Figure 1 shows that palliative care nurses tend to perform the work activities by themselves more frequently than when consulted about these topics. Additionally, palliative care nurses more frequently provided care focused on the physical domain (88%) compared to care within the psychological (72%), social (68%), and spiritual (68%) domains. Respondents described providing adequate care for complex grief as the activity they perform the least often (55%).

Similarly, data show that palliative care nurses were more frequently engaged in work activities closely related to the direct (palliative) care process as opposed to overarching activities across the board. Nurses scored high in maintaining their expertise (98%) and promoting the expertise of generalist healthcare professionals (78%). The percentage of work activities in which they are consulted is more than 50% lower than the percentage of work activities they perform. Interestingly, respondents actively apply guidelines (96%) but do not participate actively in guideline committees (44%).

Satisfaction with the position in the organization

Respondents were also asked about their satisfaction with their professional role within the nursing domain and the extent to which colleagues consult them for palliative care-related inquiries (Table 3). Satisfaction rates varied from low (28%) in nursing homes to moderate (about 50–60%) in other settings, but there were no significant differences between the different organizations.

Leadership

Palliative care nurses’ responses to open-ended questions (Appendix 1, questions 23 and 24) aimed at identifying the leadership elements they exhibit in their professional activities. The analysis revealed four key components fundamental to their leadership. A distinction was made between leadership within generalist palliative care—promoting expertise and building connections with partners—and leadership extending beyond generalist care, encompassing roles such as serving as role models and advocating for patients or the healthcare field.

Promoting the expertise of general nurses involves offering guidance, providing team education, and organizing reflective practice sessions and peer-to-peer support. Bedside teaching was frequently highlighted as a practical and effective approach. A nursing home respondent noted, “Collaborative care provision is the best way to raise awareness of palliative care among colleagues.”Promoting collaboration: promoting interprofessional and regional collaboration, bridging gaps between general practitioners, district nurses, hospitals, and hospices to ensure seamless care transitions.Acting as a role model involves initiating care improvements, enhancing the quality of palliative care, and maintaining a high level of visibility. For example, a district nurse shared, “I guide colleagues in anticipating end-of-life scenarios and make myself visible within the region by attending network meetings and giving guest lectures.”Advocacy of palliative care involves developing and integrating a vision into the organizational agenda. Some respondents emphasized articulating this vision, while others highlighted discussing palliative care with patients and their families as a core responsibility.

Key activities of a palliative care nurse in daily practice

Twelve palliative care nurses participated in three focus groups. Four worked in home care, three in hospice care, one in psychiatry, and four in hospitals (three in outpatient clinics and one in cardiac care). Additionally, nine managers participated in two focus groups, eight working in community and nursing home care, and one working in disability care.

Respondents reflected on the questionnaire results regarding their work activities and consultations in palliative care (Figs. 1 and 2). Respondents indicated they have an idea of how they wish to structure their work activities and responsibilities within the organization. However, managers did not always have a clear picture of the tasks they could expect these nurses to perform. The role of the palliative care nurse is not a “one-size-fits-all” approach; instead, it is adapted to the specific context and needs of the care setting. The patient’s stage of life differs across environments. For instance, hospital nurses primarily identify, diagnose, and mark palliative care needs, whereas hospice nurses are more frequently engaged in end-of-life care. All nurses indicated that supporting colleagues in generalist palliative care with a comprehensive outlook is essential. Nurses as well as managers emphasized the importance of demonstrating leadership, actively facilitating discussions about palliative care at both the patient level and within the organization, maintaining a broad perspective, and identifying and anticipating changes in patients’ conditions at an early stage. Additionally, promoting the expertise of generalist colleagues, primarily through bedside teaching, in these areas is a crucial component of their role as palliative care nurses. They acknowledged that their holistic approach, providing care in the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains, allows for a comprehensive assessment of overall care needs. Additionally, they noted that palliative care nurses perform these activities more frequently independently than they are consulted about these activities.

Barriers and facilitators

Nurses experienced various facilitators and barriers in their roles and in embedding their positions within the organization. The main bottleneck was the lack of acknowledgment and recognition of palliative care nurses’ roles by colleagues, managers, and other HCPs. Nurses mentioned that their role as palliative care nurses is informal, making it unclear what can be expected of the palliative care nurse. They often fulfilled the role alongside other functions, such as oncology nursing. They emphasized that they significantly contribute to exemplary care but are not yet recognized by their colleagues, including those from different healthcare professions (HCPs), such as general practitioners and district nurses. Managers have not yet fully understood the role and position that palliative care nurses should have and subsequently suboptimally facilitate the positioning of palliative care nurses, but were curious about how the role can be fulfilled.

Related to the main bottleneck of unclarity about the palliative care nurses’ role, we distinguished six themes in which barriers and facilitators could influence the role and position of palliative care nurses within an organization.

First, the lack of a clear distinction between generalist and specialist palliative care made it challenging for colleagues to identify which questions are appropriate for palliative care nurses. Second, collaboration; being part of an advisory team within palliative care is effective due to clear role demarcation. However, palliative care nurses also have experience in collaboration and domain conflicts with clinical nurse specialists and district nurses. Third, having a precise and formalized job description facilitated nurses in articulating their roles and responsibilities. Fourth, leadership involves proactively identifying and responding to changes in care needs. Additionally, leadership contributes to the visibility of their position by showing the courage to step into the forefront and drive specific palliative care processes, such as taking a leading role in the process of shared decision-making around critical moments. Fifth, visibility is achieved through organizational presence and education efforts, raising awareness of their role. Lastly, funding and reimbursement impact salary allocation post-training and cost-effectiveness contributions, reducing overall care costs. It is essential to clarify that this funding not only pertains to salary allocation but also to preventing unnecessary care, thus ensuring cost-effectiveness. Nurses indicate that they find cost-effectiveness crucial for their position; if they can demonstrate that they work cost-effectively, it contributes to recognizing their role.

Discussion

Main findings

The results of this study indicate that the work activities of palliative care nurses encompass the provision of holistic palliative care across the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains. Their responsibilities include leading advance care planning and actively promoting patient involvement in decision-making processes regarding their care. In addition to direct (palliative) patient care, palliative care nurses engage in broader professional activities, such as enhancing the palliative care competencies of generalist healthcare professionals and facilitating transmural collaboration.

The palliative care nurses in this study reported that they initiate many of their tasks and activities themselves, rather than being performed in response to consultation requests. When consultations do occur, they are predominantly related to issues within the physical domain. Palliative care nurses working in hospitals, hospices, and home care settings reported higher levels of satisfaction with their professional position within the nursing domain compared to those working in nursing homes.

Barriers and facilitators influencing the role and position of palliative care nurses were grouped into six thematic categories: (1) the distinction between generalist and specialist roles in palliative care, (2) interprofessional collaboration, (3) job description clarity, (4) leadership, (5) professional visibility, and (6) funding and reimbursement structures. Several key findings emerge from these results and warrant further discussion.

Bedside teaching

The quantitative data indicated that the actual work performed by palliative care nurses aligns with the six Essential Professional Activities (EPAs) outlined in their national competency profile [26], a finding that participants in the focus groups corroborated. However, respondents report a lack of (timely) consultation requests from generalist HCPs, which may result in suboptimal patient care [13, 42]. One suggested strategy to improve visibility among generalists is for palliative care nurses to adopt a proactive coaching and supportive role, for example, through bedside teaching or by organizing educational meetings [43, 44]. Bedside teaching emerged as a commonly reported activity among the palliative care nurses participating in this study. The broader, anticipatory perspective that palliative care nurses bring, coaching generalists and including them in discussions about potential end-of-life care scenarios based on patients ‘needs and preferences, serves to illustrate the added value of their expertise. This approach aligns with existing research emphasizing the importance of enhancing generalist competencies in palliative care [21, 45–47].

Registering and monitoring work activities and consultations

Palliative care nurses experienced that they effectively contribute to the quality of care provided to patients with palliative care needs and their relatives. Even though they have good ideas about it, and managers appreciate their role, they still find it challenging to clarify their role in the organization. They are looking for ways to make their work more transparent and less informal. While role ambiguity is often seen as a challenge, it can also enable nurses to act autonomously and to further shape the role of the palliative care nurse in response to the specific context of their care setting. This role for autonomous practice, defined by Mrayyan et al. as “the professional nurse’s competency and innovative performance to determine independent or interdependent nursing activities while conserving accountability for decisions and reflecting advocacy and caring,” further supports their capacity to navigate complex care environments and initiate appropriate referrals (p.1) [48]. This autonomy enhances their role as coordinators and advocates within the broader palliative care system. We recommend a structured system that registers and monitors the work activities of nurses, making it possible to gain insights into the activities of palliative care nurses, including responding to consultations, which should be documented in the Electronic Patient Record (EPR). Linking EPR data to measurable outcomes could provide valuable insights into the care provided and unnecessary interventions avoided. This data could enhance understanding of cost-effectiveness [49]. A regional community of practice (CoP) among palliative care nurses and their managers across diverse work settings could potentially strengthen their role and position [50]. This CoP should aim to create regional collaborative contracts and outline the work activities that palliative care nurses can implement within their organizations [51].

Being part of a (consultation) team

Palliative care nurses in this study confirmed that participation in consultation teams or PaTz groups significantly enhances their visibility and provides support to generalist HCPs in managing complex situations. Involvement in palliative advisory teams further promotes visibility and facilitates interdisciplinary collaboration [13, 52]. It was observed that nurses working in hospitals, hospices, and home care more frequently held roles in these teams compared to those working in nursing homes. To strengthen the role and position of palliative care nurses working in nursing homes, we recommend their inclusion in interprofessional or multidisciplinary teams, modeled on the PaTz framework [53]. To enable timely anticipation of changes in patients’ health status and encourage a proactive rather than reactive approach, vulnerable patients and care recipients should be discussed within the consultation team in a timely manner, thereby ensuring appropriate awareness and documentation of their condition. The leadership and holistic perspective demonstrated by palliative care nurses can serve as key facilitators in introducing patients to relevant consultation teams, supporting timely and appropriate care [13, 54, 55]. This approach aligns with principles of integrative care and is consistent with integrated palliative care initiatives such as case management, palliative care pathways, and multidisciplinary specialist palliative care [56].

Job description aligning with national professional guidelines

Palliative care nurses in hospitals, hospices, and home care settings reported greater job satisfaction. They were more likely to have formal job descriptions as specialists compared to those in nursing homes. Focus group discussions revealed that nurses with a formal job description based on a palliative care nurse’s profile feel valued and recognized. Managers expressed interest in how other organizations position their palliative care nurses and showed a willingness to learn from each other. To further formalize and embed the role and position of palliative care nurses, it is recommended that each palliative care nurse have a job description derived from the established profile, with the competencies outlined in this profile serving as a framework. Palliative care nurses should define and clarify their roles to their managers, colleagues, and other HCPs in their organizations and COPs, emphasizing palliative care nursing as a distinct profession while integrating consultation and case management skills. This enhances colleagues’ recognition and reduces the perceived domain conflict with district nurses and clinical nurse specialists (CNS) [57].

Cost-effectiveness of palliative care

One key facilitator for strengthening the role and position of palliative care nurses is adequate funding. Managers expressed particular interest in reimbursement rates provided by health insurers and the salary scales allocated to this group of specialized nurses. This concern is closely linked to another important facilitator: demonstrating the cost-effectiveness of specialized palliative care. Several studies have already underscored the economic benefits of palliative care interventions. For example, evidence indicates that home-based palliative care can generate substantial cost savings while improving patient outcomes [58]. Providing such data is essential for enhancing the professional standing of palliative care nurses.

A novel approach involves using artificial intelligence to analyze data from patient records to assess cost-effectiveness, optimize resource allocation, and improve access to palliative care services [59]. Finally, we recommend further research focused on cost-effectiveness. Nurses participating in this study reported a perception that their work contributes to preventing unnecessary care. It is therefore important to translate this perception into empirical evidence demonstrating that palliative care nurses indeed contribute to reducing healthcare costs, similar to clinical nurse specialists [60].

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study lies in including data from palliative care nurses working across all care settings in which palliative care is delivered, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Using a sequential mixed-methods design enabled the collection of objective data regarding nurses’ activities and an in-depth exploration of their experiences and perceived opportunities. This approach contributed to the validity of the results and provided a more comprehensive understanding of the roles and positioning of palliative care nurses.

However, participation from organizations supporting individuals with intellectual disabilities or psychiatric conditions was limited, which constrains the applicability of our findings to these particular settings. Given the significant role these sectors play in providing bedside palliative care, further research focusing on these populations is warranted. An additional limitation is that we did not specifically explore the role of the palliative care nurse in contact and collaboration with family and informal caregivers. According to the foundational principles of palliative care, described in diverse national and international studies, and the Dutch competency profile of palliative care nurses, collaboration with family and informal caregivers is essential [15, 22, 26]. This perspective is further supported by conceptualizing the patient and family as a single unit of care, as described by Nebel, which enables a more holistic and relational approach to care delivery [61]. This is especially relevant in in-home-based care settings, where informal caregivers are prominently present. A majority of nurses mentioned their role in the social domain, but collaboration with family and informal caregivers was not explicitly discussed in the focus groups. This could be explained by the emphasis on their position within the organization instead of the discussion of the content of their work. While efforts were made to achieve a balanced representation of managers from various care settings, a hospital-based manager could not be included in the focus groups, which represents a limitation of the study.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive insights into the work activities of palliative care nurses. The findings indicate that less than half of their activities involve consultations that extend beyond direct (palliative) patient care. Palliative care nurses play a vital role in establishing high-quality palliative care, which is acknowledged and valued by managers; however, considerable ambiguity persists regarding their role and professional positioning in organizations. The nurses highlighted the importance of leadership and advocating for palliative care in and beyond their organization, as well as the need for both formal and informal mechanisms to support (the recognition of) their role as specialized nurses. The study identifies several facilitators and barriers to their effective functioning, related to visibility, funding, reimbursement structures, and interprofessional collaboration. Areas for improvement include participation in interprofessional palliative care advisory or consultation teams, the development of clearly defined job descriptions aligned with the national competency profile for nurses specialized in palliative care, and the establishment of strategies to demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of having palliative care nurses among nurses who provide palliative care on a generalist level.
