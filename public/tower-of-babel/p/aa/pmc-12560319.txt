Introduction

A gossypiboma is a rare but serious complication that can occur after a Cesarean section or other surgical procedure when a surgical sponge or gauze is accidentally left in the body. The term comes from the Latin word gossipium (cotton) and the Kiswahili word boma (a place of concealment). It is also called textiloma [1–3].

The exact incidence of gossypiboma is unknown, due to underreporting related to medicolegal issues [4]. The majority of gossypiboma cases are found within a few days of surgery. However, they can go unnoticed for years [5–7].

Several factors contribute to the risk of retained foreign bodies. Complex surgical procedures, high patient turnover, high bod mass index, and emergent surgeries can increase the likelihood of errors during the counting and retrieval of surgical items [2, 7, 8]. Furthermore, human factors, such as lack of experience or appropriate training, distractions or fatigue in the operating room, can play a role in these incidents [2].

Delays in diagnosis and treatment can result in significant morbidity and potentially lead to mortality [3]. In this report, I present a case of chronic gossypiboma following a Cesarean section, which initially presented as a mature cystic teratoma. This case highlights the importance of accurate and timely diagnosis in clinical practice to prevent further complications. It also aptly points out that one has to keep in mind the differential diagnosis of a foreign body in any case of chronic abdominal pain with a mass and prior history of abdominal surgery.

Case presentation

This is a 40-year-old woman from Western Ethiopia, a para III patient, who has been experiencing intermittent lower abdominal pain for the past four years. She has had one vaginal delivery and two Cesarean deliveries at a public general hospital. Her last Cesarean delivery occurred four years prior, on 12/3/2021. Since then, she has suffered from dull, aching lower abdominal pain, which has led her to visit various healthcare facilities. During these visits, she was treated with pain relief medication and antibiotics. Additionally, she has experienced recurrent urinary issues over the last four years. The patient denied any history of abnormal vaginal discharge, genital itching, vulvar ulcers, or multiple sexual partners. There is no personal or family history of breast or gynecological cancers. She reports no fever, headache, or palpitations, and has not experienced any bowel, respiratory, or musculoskeletal complaints. Furthermore, she has no known chronic medical conditions such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, tuberculosis, or heart disease.

Bilateral tubal ligation was performed during her last delivery. She has regular monthly menstrual cycles, with the most recent occurring three weeks ago. Currently, she has been referred to a tertiary hospital with a diagnosis of a mature cystic teratoma.

Upon examination, the patient appeared unwell. Her vital signs were as follows: blood pressure (BP) of 120/70 mmHg, pulse rate (PR) of 88 beats per minute, respiratory rate (RR) of 20 breaths per minute, and a temperature of 36 °C. She exhibited a pink conjunctiva and had a 2 cm by 3 cm anterior neck mass that was movable during swallowing. Additionally, there was a healed transverse suprapubic abdominal scar. During the bimanual pelvic examination, a smooth, mobile, and non-tender right adnexal mass measuring 8 by 10 cm was detected. The respiratory system, cardiovascular system, and musculoskeletal system appeared normal. Neurologically, reflexes were intact, and there were no meningeal signs present.

Investigations included blood grouping, urinalysis, stool examination; complete blood count, organ function tests, electrocardiogram (ECG), and serological tests for hepatitis (see Table 1). An abdominopelvic ultrasound revealed an echogenic complex mass with cystic component (see Fig. 1).

The patient was admitted to the ward for surgery following a preliminary diagnosis of a mature cystic teratoma. During the operation, an 8 by 10 cm well encapsulated fluctuant mass was found on in the pelvic cavity at right broad ligament with adhesion to the uterus, bladder, and anterior abdominal wall. To remove the mass as a whole, the adhesions were sharply dissected but in doing so the wall opened up. The mass was found to contain about 500 ml thick caseous fluid and gauze measuring 4 by 12 cm. The fluid was drained, and the gauze was gently removed without incurring injury to the adjacent structures (see Fig. 2).

Discussion

Gossypiboma is a well-known issue in the surgical field, yet it remains a common problem in many countries [5]. The most frequent causes of gossypiboma include emergency surgeries, unexpected changes during operations, a high body mass index, rushed sponge counts, prolonged procedures, unstable patient conditions, and a lack of experienced staff [6–8]. This particular case occurred following an emergency cesarean section.

Retained surgical items may elicit two distinct types of tissue reactions: an exudative reaction that manifests early in the postoperative period and an aseptic fibrous reaction that develops gradually and can remain asymptomatic for an extended duration [6, 9, 10]. Due to the biochemical inertness of cotton fiber, surgical sponges generally do not provoke specific reactions within the body; nonetheless, they can lead to the formation of adhesions and granulomas, resulting in their aseptic encapsulation [4, 6]. The granulomatous reaction and surrounding tissues caused by foreign body may reach huge volume and, may lead to misdiagnosis as a tumor [3]. This case involved a well-defined mass that was adhered to nearby structures.

Diagnosing gossypiboma is difficult due to varying symptoms, time since surgery, and imprecise imaging [10, 11]. They are usually identified within days of surgery, but some can remain undetected for years. When gossypibomas present late, they may lead to significant diagnostic confusion [5]. In this case, the prolonged duration from previous surgery, made the care providers considers cystic teratoma.

The diagnosis of most cases can be confirmed through imaging techniques such as Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT) scan, radiography, and ultrasound [4, 9]. Newer technologies like radiofrequency chip identification by bar code scanner are being developed [9, 12].

Due to its superior sensitivity, the CT scan is the preferred imaging method for ruling out gossypibomas. It shows a typical spongiform pattern or heterogeneous central areas due to gas, calcification, and radiopaque markers [8, 10]. A calcified “reticulate rind sign” may also be noticed in cases of long-lasting gossypiboma and represents a handy characteristic radiological feature [8, 9, 12].

MRI is another diagnostic alternative and it shows a well-defined hypo-intense cystic lesion on T1 weighed images and hyper-intense on T2 weighed images. However, it does not visualize the radiopaque marker usually lacking radio-magnetic characteristics and giving poor MRI signals [8, 10]. Ultrasound can also be used as a diagnostic tool which shows a poorly defined echogenic area with intense posterior acoustic shadowing [9, 10].

Regular abdomen X-rays usually don’t provide conclusive evidence of the underlying existing gossypiboma [7]. However, tagged surgical gauze can be detected by regular x-ray. This is expected to decrease the rate of error associated with the use of manual counting [6, 7].

The most effective way to manage gossypiboma is through prevention rather than surgical intervention. It requires a multifaceted approach, emphasizing stringent protocols, personal accountability and a collaborative environment [9, 12]. Proper handling of surgical instruments and textiles is crucial during every operation. The World Health Organization (WHO) surgical safety checklist should be used to reduce reliance on memory [10]. Surgical instrument count should be conducted at the beginning of the surgery, during the closure of the peritoneum, and after dressing the wound [10, 13].

Once the diagnosis is made, the immediate removal of any gossypiboma associated is needed [8]. Most patients require exploratory laparotomy under general anesthesia for gauze removal. However, Explorative laparotomy and removal of surgical gauze may sometimes be difficult because of adhesions to the adjacent structures [14]. Therefore; great care is needed during surgery. Retrieval via laparoscopy is also reported to be an alternative, with advances in the field of endoscopy, the incidence of residual gauze can be reduced [4, 8].

Conclusion

In patients who have previously undergone surgery for obstetric or other gynecological procedures and present with vague abdominal complaints, it is important to consider the possibility of a chronic gossypiboma mimicking a mass of unknown origin. Following established surgical protocols and implementing new preventive measures, such as using tagged gauze/ radio-opaque markers, and ongoing staff training could help reduce or prevent the occurrence gossypiboma. Additionally, the author advises performing delicate surgical procedures to remove retained gauze to prevent bleeding and tissue damage. The gauze should be gently lifted, and the wound must be examined for any damage.
