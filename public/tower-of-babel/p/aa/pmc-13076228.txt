Subtle-seq reveals frequent ageing-associated chromosome fragmentation on nucleosome core DNA
Authors: Ryuma Matsubara, Therese Solberg, Mariusz Nowacki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTelomere healing seals the broken ends of DNA double-strand breaks by adding de novo telomeres. In the ciliate Paramecium, telomere healing results in chromosome fragmentation, which ultimately leads to clonal ageing. Despite its importance, where and how chromosomes fragment and undergo telomere healing, and to what extent sequence preferences exist, remain poorly understood. To investigate chromosome fragmentation, we enriched for telomere-containing sequences from the complex, highly polyploid genome of Paramecium by developing Subtle-seq (Subtelomere Enrichment-sequencing). Subtle-seq reads of young Paramecium mapped to chromosome termini and shifted toward proximal sites with increasing divisions, recapitulating chromosome fragmentation during ageing. Transcriptome analysis identified downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in aged cells, and Subtle-seq reads enriched in or around these genes. Moreover, we identified nucleosome-occupied DNA as hotspots of telomere healing. Nucleosome occupancy has been reported to correlate with gene expression in Paramecium, and indeed, the downregulated DEGs were predominantly highly expressed and showed higher nucleosome densities. Hence, the density of nucleosomes needed for high expression of genes likely results in chromosome fragmentation as “collateral damage” and may contribute to the reduced transcription in aged cells. Our method allows the detection of rare telomere-healing events and sheds light on the hitherto unrecognized mechanism of telomere healing and consequent ageing in Paramecium.

Introduction

Ageing is a decline in physiological function associated with a progression in lifecycle, which ultimately determines the lifespan of individuals. While the physiological changes accompanying ageing is caused by systemic and multidimensional alterations of the chromosomal structure, epigenetic marks, transcriptional, and metabolomic features, DNA damage has been suggested as a major driver of these changes [1, 2]. Ageing is also a major risk factor for many diseases, which has led to the concept of “healthy ageing” going hand in hand with longevity research, and a surge in studies aimed at preventing age-related diseases through targeting the consequences of DNA damage. As such, understanding which regions of the genome are more susceptible to DNA damage, and the outcomes of various types of DNA damage, is of outmost importance.

Widely explained as a result of distributing limited resources to reproduction over somatic maintenance (disposable soma theory of ageing) [3], ageing has often been regarded as a consequence of higher complexity in metazoans. However, it is in fact ubiquitously observed in unicellular organisms from bacteria to protists [4–7], suggesting an ancient origin in evolution. Ciliated protists such as Paramecium are keen examples of a primitive ageing phenotype, as they share the key concept of ageing in metazoans: the distinction of soma and germline. The genetic information for sexual and asexual reproduction is compartmentalized in two distinct nuclei, a highly polyploid macronucleus (MAC) and two diploid micronuclei (MICs) in a single cell. Paramecium propagates asexually via binary fission (termed vegetative division) in favorable environments, during which the MAC is responsible for cellular activities, and the MICs are transcriptionally silent and serve as the germline to preserve genetic information. Sexual reproduction is triggered by environmental stress, such as starvation, prompting the cell to either conjugate to a mate or undergo self-fertilization (autogamy) to develop a zygotic MAC from the parental MIC genome (Fig. 1A) [8]. The new vegetative clone relies on the newly developed MAC and will, unless given another opportunity for sexual reproduction, gradually slow down its growth speed and eventually cease to divide, known as clonal ageing [9].

Unlike most organisms, Paramecium maintains telomerase activity throughout vegetative growth and utilizes it to repair DNA damage in the MAC genome. Since the MAC divides without mitotic processes (amitosis), it lacks the homologous recombination pathway to accurately rejoin DSBs. Instead, it protects the exposed DNA ends by adding de novo telomere sequences, known as neotelomeres [10–12]. Such a patchwork strategy will induce progressive fragmentation of chromosomes, and, since amitosis does not require centromeres for chromosome segregation, fragmented chromosomes may be maintained throughout nuclear divisions. The extreme polyploidy (∼800 n) buffers out the negative effects of broken genes until the fragmentation becomes too excessive, which likely shapes the deterioration of the MAC during ageing. While it has been suggested that the determinant of nuclear deterioration is accumulative DNA damage, or more precisely, random chromosome fragmentation of the MAC genome, only limited experimental evidence has been provided to validate this hypothesis [13], and the locations of chromosome fragmentation throughout the clonal lifespan have not been reported. This endeavor is further complicated by the highly polyploid nature of the MAC, which in conventional sequencing methods, masks the much rarer telomere reads.

Somatic DNA damage has also been suggested as a major, if not the major, cause of ageing and age-related diseases [1, 2, 14, 15]. Although there are several kinds of DNA damage, chromosome fragmentation is one of the most catastrophic modes of chromosomal anomalies. It has also been associated with age-related diseases, such as cancer, and leads to mis-segregation, loss of chromosomes, and/or direct damage to genes where the break takes place. For this reason, the formation of neotelomeres on DSBs is strictly controlled in higher organisms [16], and telomerase is kept at an undetectable level in terminally differentiated cells in humans [17, 18]. Despite this strict regulation, neotelomeres have been detected in several cancers and diseases [16, 19, 20]. Given that 90% of cancers have reactivated telomerase, the extent of neotelomere formation may be highly underestimated due to a lack of appropriate tools for their detection. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop tools that can allow the detection of rare neotelomere events to further our understanding of how and where neotelomeres are formed, the mode and characteristics of which are currently virtually unknown.

To accurately identify age-related chromosome fragmentation, we developed Subtle-seq, a method to selectively sequence telomere–subtelomere junctions by exploiting the 3′ protective nature of telomeres. With this method, we successfully mapped age-accumulated chromosome fragmentation across the Paramecium MAC genome throughout the clonal lifespan. We identified nucleosomes as hotspots of ageing-associated neotelomeres, and found that nucleosome-rich, highly expressed genes are preferentially downregulated in older clonal ages. Hence, dense nucleosomal arrays needed for the high expression of genes are likely prone to chromosome fragmentation as “collateral damage” in older clonal ages, which may lead to reduced transcription and contribute to subsequent ageing phenotypes. In sum, our work highlights the unrecognized preference of telomere healing in nucleosomal arrays and identify a possible link to the ageing phenotype. Subtle-seq is a powerful and useful tool for detecting even rare telomere healing events, and is widely transferrable to other systems where neotelomere reads are of low abundance.

Materials and methods

Paramecium culture

Paramecium tetraurelia strain 51, mating type 7, was maintained in wheat grass powder medium (Pines international, Lawrence, KS), bacterized with Klebsiella pneumoniae, and supplemented with β-sitosterol (0.8 mg/ml, Sigma–Aldrich) as previously described [21]. Unless otherwise noted, the cells were maintained at 27°C. Autogamy was induced by starvation, and the onset was confirmed by the observation of old MAC fragmentation under the microscope with DAPI staining.

Collection of aged Paramecium samples

For the collection of aged samples, post-autogamous cells were left in 27°C for 7–10 days to ensure full clearance of the old MAC fragments. This culture, defined as clonal age 0, was then diluted in bacterized medium to start the clonal time course, and subsequently diluted daily to obtain samples of different clonal ages. Cell densities were carefully maintained in the exponential phase (30–600 cells/ml) to prevent the induction of autogamy, and at least 100 cells were observed daily by DAPI staining and microscopy to ensure that no autogamous cells were present. At the desired clonal age, the cells were collected by brief centrifugation at 270 × g for 3 min and washed twice with 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4). The cell pellets were subsequently snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept in −80°C until use. Two independently passaged cell lines, derived from the same post-autogamous culture, were maintained and analyzed as replicates.

Genomic DNA extraction

The frozen cell pellets were lysed in 2 ml of lysis buffer [500 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA); 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS); 1% N-lauryl sarcosine] supplemented with Proteinase K (1 mg/ml final concentration, Promega) overnight at 55°C. RNase A (40 µg/ml final, QIAGEN) was then added to the lysate, and the RNA removed by a 5 min incubation at room temperature. gDNA was extracted with an equal volume of phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol solution (PCI; 25:24:1 mixture, pH 7.9, Invitrogen) for 1 h at 4°C under gentle agitation. After centrifugation at 3200 × g for 30 min at 4°C, the aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, and the extraction was repeated by adding an equal volume of PCI to the aqueous phase. Following extraction and centrifugation, the aqueous phase was dialyzed three times against 1 l of ice-cold TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 1 mM EDTA), for at least 3 h each. The gDNA was concentrated by ethanol precipitation, resuspended in TE buffer, and the quality assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Telomere restriction fragment assay

The TRF assay was performed with the alkali transfer protocol following the instructions of the blotting membrane (Amersham Hybond-XL, GE healthcare). Briefly, 2–3 µg of gDNA, either intact or digested by DpnII (New England Biolabs), was separated by agarose gel electrophoresis in TAE (Tris-acetate-EDTA) buffer. After staining with ethidium bromide to record the migration pattern, the gel was rinsed in ultrapure water and incubated in depurination solution (0.125 M HCl) for 10 min under gentle agitation. The gel was then rinsed in ultrapure water and soaked in denaturing solution (0.5 M sodium hydroxide; 1.5 M sodium chloride) for 30 min under agitation. The DNA was then transferred to a nylon membrane (Amersham Hybond-XL) by capillary blotting overnight, using denaturing solution as the transfer buffer. After the transfer, the membrane was washed twice in 2× saline-sodium citrate buffer (SSC) for 15 min each, prehybridized in Church buffer (1% bovine serum albumin; 0.5 M NaPO4, pH 7.2; 7% SDS; 1 mM EDTA) [22] at 55°C for 4 h or overnight, and hybridized with a [γ-32P]-ATP-labeled single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) probe against telomeres (5′- AACCCCAACCCCAACCCM -3′; M, C/A) in Church buffer at 55°C overnight. Lastly, the membrane was washed three times with 2× SSC and visualized on a Typhoon FLA 7000 (GE Healthcare).

Design of the adapter and PCR primers to target the telomere–chromosome boundary

The sequencing adapter contains four random nucleotides serving as a unique molecular identifier (UMI), and a terminal 5′-GATC extension to be ligated to the DpnII digestion sites. Equimolar (200 µM each) sense (5′-TGTGACTGCGTGTTCACCNNNNGCGGCCGC-3′; N = A/T/G/C) and antisense (5′-GATCGCGGCCGC-3′, underline denotes complementarity between the oligos) oligonucleotides in annealing buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 1 mM EDTA; 50 mM NaCl) were denatured for 5 min at 95°C, followed by slow cooling to facilitate efficient annealing. The annealed adapter was stored at −20°C until use.

The forward polymerase chain reaction (PCR) primer (TGTGACTGCGTGTTCACC) was designed to bind to the adapter and include UMIs in the amplicon. The reverse primers, preferentially binding to telomere–chromosome boundaries, were designed with the following strategy: they contain degenerated oligos to target the ambiguous telomeric repeat (GGG[G/T]TT) of P. tetraurelia [23, 24], and four random nucleotides at the 3′ end to facilitate primer annealing to the boundaries. Since telomeres of P. tetraurelia start with either GGG[G/T]TT or TT[G/T]GGG, two reverse primers (5′-CCCMAACCCMAACCCMAANNNN-3′, 5′-AAMCCCAAMCCCAAMCCCNNNN-3′; M = C/A; N = A/T/G/C) were mixed at the same ratio.

Reference genomes and annotations

The following reference sequences and annotations of the MAC genome were retrieved from the Paramecium DB (https://paramecium.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr/) and used for analyses of Subtle-seq, nucleosome profiling, and the messenger RNA (mRNA) sequencing data.

MAC genome sequence: https://paramecium.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr/files/Paramecium/tetraurelia/51/sequences/ptetraurelia_mac_51.fa

MAC gene annotation: https://paramecium.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr/files/Paramecium/tetraurelia/51/annotations/ptetraurelia_mac_51/ptetraurelia_mac_51_annotation_v2.0.gff3.

MAC ± lncRNA annotation: prepared by combining the MAC gene annotation and Paramecium lncRNA genes annotated in [25].

Vegetative SRC annotation: the vegetative small RNA clusters (SRCs) annotation was obtained from the previous publication [26].

Enrichment of telomere–chromosome boundaries by Subtle-seq

Five micrograms of gDNA was digested with 250 units of DpnII (New England Biolabs) in a 100 µl reaction for 4 h at 37°C, followed by heat inactivation for 15 min at 65°C. After digestion, 5% of the reaction was analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm fragmentation, and the rest was purified by ethanol precipitation and resuspended in 20 µl of ultrapure water. Thirty microliters of pre-annealed 50 µM adapter, 10 µl T4 DNA ligase buffer, and 2000 units of T4 DNA Ligase (New England Biolabs) were added to constitute a 100 µl ligation reaction, and the reaction was carried out overnight at 16°C. After heat inactivation at 65°C for 10 min, the DNA was ethanol-precipitated and resuspended in ultrapure water. To remove unwanted DNA fragments, including chromosomal (non-telomeric) DNA, chromosomal DNA with adapter(s), and adapter monomers and dimers, the samples were digested with 500 units of Exonuclease III (New England Biolabs) at 37°C overnight. After digestion, Exonuclease III was heat inactivated by incubating at 70°C for 20 min. Enriched telomeric DNA was purified by PCI extraction followed by ethanol precipitation with 1 µl of GlycoBlue (Invitrogen). The DNA was resuspended in 5 µl of ultrapure water, and 1 µl was PCR amplified using GoTaq (Promega) and the primers described earlier. Illumina sequencing libraries were constructed from the PCR amplicons with the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (cat. E7645S, New England Biolabs) and the multiplex kit (cat. E6442S, New England Biolabs) without DNA fragmentation or size selection. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 SP flow-cell in 2 × 150 bp paired-end mode with 15% of PhiX spike-in at the Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) Platform of the University of Bern, Switzerland.

Identification of telomere sites

Illumina paired-end reads obtained by Subtle-seq were filtered using bbduk.sh from BBTools v38.90 (Bushnell B. https://github.com/bbushnell/BBTools) in paired-end mode with default adapter-trimming and flag “ktrim=r mink=11 qtrim=rl trimq=15 minlen=50 tpe tbo”. Next, the paired reads were merged using PEAR v0.9.11 with default settings [27]. The merged reads were further filtered to extract reads fulfilling all of the following criteria; (i) carrying a single DpnII site, (ii) containing telomeric repeats, (iii) containing a single adapter sequence. Reads were then aligned by the orientation 5′– Adapter–chromosome–telomere –3′ using seqtk (https://github.com/lh3/seqtk) for convenience of downstream analyses. UMIs were detected and extracted by UMItools v1.1.2 [28] with flag “--extract-method=regex --bc-pattern='^.*GATC(?P.{16})(?P.*)$'”. To accurately identify telomere sites in the genome, we trimmed the telomeric repeats before mapping to increase mappability. The processed reads were mapped to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.1.0 [29] with default parameters. After mapping, primary alignments were selected by Samtools v1.10 [30] with the option “-F 260”, and PCR duplicates were removed using a custom shell script (reads with the same UMI and identical RNAME, POS, CIGAR, and FLAG fields in the SAM file were considered as PCR duplicates). The 3′ end of the reads, which corresponds to the junction of the telomeric sequences, was recorded by their mapped coordinates on the reference genome using pileup.sh from BBTools. The obtained telomere sites were further polished so that only the strongest signals in each 10 bp sliding window were retained (Supplementary Table S1). The workflow of data processing is summarized in (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Detection of unannotated chromosome ends

To identify unannotated chromosome ends, the entire reference MAC genome was divided in 2 kb sliding windows in steps of 100 bp, to which the Subtle-seq reads from four division samples were mapped. The signals in each window were then summed (replicates and each strand were counted separately). Signals of “true” chromosome ends (ends from chromosomes annotated as complete in [31]) were extracted, and the median of the signals was used as a threshold to identify unannotated chromosome ends. A window was defined as a telomere site if both replicates had signals on the same strand stronger than the threshold. Overlapping windows were merged when both passed the threshold.

Neotelomere enrichment on genomic features

Neotelomere counts on genic and intergenic regions were calculated using the MAC gene annotation. To analyze the correlation between neotelomere formation and gene expression levels, the published Paramecium gene expression table was obtained from [32], and the expression scores as well as the statistic test results for differential expression were used. In detail, vegetative genes were defined as satisfying both “Expression_profile” being “none” and a VEG score >100, whereas the autogamy-specific genes satisfied both “Expression_profile” other than “none” and a VEG score below 10. To further investigate the relationship between gene expression and neotelomere formation, all annotated genes were categorized into five groups by quantiles of their VEG scores based on previously published RNA-seq data, where group 1 has the lowest expression [32]. Neotelomere counts on each group were then calculated. Tandem repeats were detected using Tandem Repeats Finder [33] v4.09 with default settings. The distance from the detected repeat was measured using the Bedtools v2.30.0 [34] reldist function. To analyze palindromes near neotelomere sites, we extracted 10 or 125 nucleotides upstream and downstream of the annotated neotelomere sites (21 or 251 nucleotides in total) from the MAC reference, keeping the neotelomere sites at the center. For each extracted sequence, the Levenshtein distance between the original and the reverse-complement sequence was computed using the stringdist package in R (Supplementary Fig. S5A). As a control, random sequences with the same length and number of reads were generated while taking the probabilities of the MAC nucleotide composition into account (A = 0.3636616; T = 0.3632638; G = 0.1452895; C = 0.1449231). For analysis of SRCs, the Subtle-seq signals detected within and outside of the SRC regions were counted. The significance of enrichments was assessed using the Pearson’s chi-square goodness-of-fit test from the stats package in R. To analyze overlaps in neotelomere formation sites, the telomere loci were converted to binary in steps of 100 bp (TRUE if any position within the range has telomere signals) before Dice-Sørensen coefficients were calculated using the R package proxy.

Motif detection

To identify motifs, sequences 50 bp upstream and downstream of the telomere-healing sites were extracted from the reference MAC genome. The sequences were further subsampled to 36 000 reads by seqtk (ver. 1.4-r122; https://github.com/lh3/seqtk) due to restrictions of the MEME Suite web server. Motif enrichments were performed by STREME in MEME Suite 5.5.9 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) [35] with default settings. To analyze sequences specific to neotelomeres, we focused on regions proximal to the chromosome center, excluding the 2 kb flanking each chromosome end. To detect a conserved sequence at the immediate locations of telomere addition, the same sequences used above were analyzed on the WebLogo 3.9.0 server (https://weblogo.threeplusone.com/) [36] with a GC% set to 29.

Nucleosome density analysis

Nucleosome profiling was performed as previously described [37] with a few simplifications. Approximately 1.5 million Paramecium cells were collected and washed as described. Nuclei isolation, MNase digestion, and gDNA extraction were performed using the EZ Nucleosomal DNA Prep Kit (ZYMO Research). The duration of the nuclei isolation step was adjusted each time to ensure the complete release of nuclei, which was assessed by microscopy with DAPI staining. For each experiment, a ladder-like pattern of nucleosomal DNA fragments was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries were prepared using the illumina TruSeq Nano kit without mechanical fragmentation or size selection and sequenced on a Novaseq 6000 with a paired-end 2 × 150 bp flow cell. Library preparation and sequencing were performed at Fasteris (Geneva, Switzerland).

For analyses of nucleosome profiling data prepared in this or our previous study, mapping and extraction of the mononucleosomal reads were performed as described [37]. Briefly, reads were pre-processed by BBduk in paired-end mode with default adapter-trimming and the flags “ktrim=l mink=11 qtrim=rl trimq=15 minlen=50 tpe tbo”, mapped to the MAC reference using Hisat2 with the option “--no-mixed”, and fragment sizes between 125 and 175 bp were selected based on the TLEN field of the SAM file format. The reads were subsequently downsampled so that all samples were comparable in read number. Nucleosome densities were analyzed from the mapped data using DANPOS3 (https://github.com/sklasfeld/DANPOS3) and deepTools v3.5.1 [38], following previously published methods for Paramecium nucleosome analysis [39]. The dpos function from DANPOS3 with the flag “-m 1” was used to detect accumulated nucleosome signals over MNase-undigested controls. The function profile from DANPOS3 was used to visualize nucleosome densities around positions of interest (i.e. neotelomere sites). To further show that the accumulation of nucleosome densities is not only observed in a minor fraction of neotelomere sites, heatmaps of nucleosome signals at each neotelomere site were depicted using deepTools computeMatrix and plotHeatmap functions. For the GC skew shown in Fig. 4, counts of G over C in 10-nucleotide windows (sliding 1-nucleotide over the reference MAC genome) were calculated using the R Biostrings package (https://rdrr.io/bioc/Biostrings/). For heatmaps of nucleosome densities within and outside of SRCs, control regions (genomic intervals not covered by SRCs) were obtained by bedtools complement and subsampled to the number of SRCs (2602 regions) using bedtools sample. Heatmaps were generated using deepTools computeMatrix and plotHeatmap functions.

Transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was extracted with Tri reagent (Sigma–Aldrich) by following the manufacturer’s instructions, and RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA kit. Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the NGS Platform of the University of Bern, Switzerland. Read pre-filtering was performed using BBduk.sh, and the reads were mapped to the MAC genome using Hisat2 with flag “--no-mixed”. Read numbers per gene were counted by HTseq [40] with options “-m intersection-nonempty --nonunique all” using the MAC + lncRNA annotation, and differential gene expression was calculated by DESeq2 [41]. Genes with an adjusted P-value < .05 and a Log2 fold-change of >1 or <-1 were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the R package gprofiler2, and visualized using clusterProfiler [42, 43]. The distance between each DEG and the closest telomere signal upstream of the gene was calculated using Bedtools v2.30.0 [34] closest with flags “-D a -id -t first”. Positions with >10 counts of telomeres (both strands combined) were selected and used for the analyses. The number of genes containing telomere signals within the feature was counted using the same method, by counting the genes with “distance = 0” (Bedtools closest outputs 0 if two features overlap). For both analyses, the same number of randomly selected genes were subjected to the analyses as randomized controls. The random selection and analysis were repeated 100 times for each replicate and analysis. To evaluate the accumulation of mutations in the aged culture, SNPs in RNA-seq of 24 and 93 divisions were counted by bcftools mpileup with flags “--max-depth 50 000 --min-MQ 20 --min-BQ 20 --annotate FORMAT/AD,FORMAT/DP” (ver. 1.19) [30].

Results

Subtle-seq enriches for telomere–chromosome boundaries

To enrich for NGS reads spanning the junction of telomeres and subtelomeres (chromosomal region adjacent to telomeres), we fragmented MAC gDNA, ligated adapters, and PCR-amplified the region of interest with telomere- and adapter-targeting primers. However, this strategy resulted in vast contamination of chromosomal reads with adapters on both ends (data not shown). To remove these contaminants, we digested the library with Exonuclease III (Exo III), a double-stranded DNA-specific exonuclease targeting blunt or 3′-recessed 3′ ends. As telomeres contain ssDNA with 3′ overhangs [44], the sense strand of telomeric reads remains intact, whereas chromosomal reads with adapters, adapter dimers, DNA without adapters, and reads with interstitial telomeric sequences, are all subject to degradation (Fig. 1B). The condition of Exo III digestion was experimentally refined to ensure removal of chromosomal DNA with little change to telomeric fragments (Fig. 1C). Of note, Paramecium telomeres are heterogeneous with random repeats of two telomeric units, G4T2 and G3T3 [23]. The PCR primers were therefore designed to contain partially degenerated sequences to target GGG[G/T]TT repeats, and four random bases at the 3′ end to facilitate annealing to telomere–subtelomere boundaries. By combining Exo III digestion and degenerated oligos, we successfully enriched telomere-harboring reads from below 1% in standard WGS to as high as nearly 70% of the total libraries in our Subtle-seq (Fig. 1D; see “Materials and methods” section for details). Hence, Subtle-seq successfully captures and enriches for sequences containing telomere–subtelomere boundaries.

Neotelomeres form incrementally in chromosomal bodies during ageing

Next, we applied Subtle-seq to Paramecia of different clonal ages to identify age-induced formation of neotelomeres (Fig. 2A). We first selected reads containing both telomeric repeats and the adapter sequence (see “Materials and methods” section). The sequences between telomeres and the adapters were then extracted, which correspond to the chromosome sequences adjacent to telomere-healing sites. At clonal age 4, the reads predominantly mapped to the terminal region of the reference genome (Fig. 2B), indicating that the detected telomeres were mostly those annotated or formed close to annotated chromosome ends, presumably due to heterogeneities at chromosome termini [45, 46]. As the cells progressed in age, the relative positions of telomeres shifted toward the center of the chromosomes, a trend observed in two independent time courses (Fig. 2B). The absolute distance between telomeres and the nearest reference chromosome end also increased during ageing (Fig. 2C). Because the reference MAC genome of Paramecium is composed of many scaffolds with gaps, most of which are not flanked by telomeres on both ends, we next extracted and analyzed telomeres mapped on scaffolds defined as complete chromosomes (i.e. containing telomeres on both ends in [31]). Compared to the signals on all chromosomes (Fig. 2D), the signals on complete chromosomes showed stronger enrichment in distal regions (Fig. 2E). Nonetheless, all measurements indicated that the telomere signals shift toward the center of chromosomes during clonal ageing. This shift means that new telomeres (chromosome ends) form in the chromosome body, indicating that DSBs and chromosome fragmentation occur during ageing.

During new MAC development of some ciliates, the MIC loci which fragment (chromosome breakage sites; CBSs) are predefined in the genome architecture [48, 49]. Likewise, age-induced CBSs may also be present in the MAC genome of Paramecium, and the MAC may age by a predefined pattern of fragmentation. To test this, we looked for overlaps of fragmentation sites among samples by calculating Dice-Sørensen coefficients (Supplementary Fig. S2A). However, there were only modest overlaps in fragmentation positions among ages or between biological replicates, except for the four-division samples (Supplementary Fig. S2), suggesting that DSBs and neotelomere formation occur in a seemingly random manner during the course of ageing.

Identification of unannotated chromosome ends using Subtle-seq

Because the current MAC reference genome contains many scaffolds with no telomere annotations on one or both ends, we next used our Subtle-seq data to identify additional chromosome ends on “incomplete” scaffolds (those not containing telomeres on both ends in [31]). To do so, we extracted the first and last 2 kb of complete chromosomes (“true” chromosome ends), to which we mapped our Subtle-seq reads from clonal age 4. These signals were then counted, and the median was used as a threshold to identify unannotated chromosome ends. Next, the entire reference genome was binned into 2 kb sliding windows, and regions with signals higher than the threshold were extracted. Upon manual inspection of the identified telomere sites on the 188 largest scaffolds, we found that most sites were located at or near the ends of the scaffolds (Supplementary Table S2). As previously reported, we also identified heterogeneities at chromosome termini: while some chromosomes do have strong telomere signals at their immediate ends (Fig. 2F), this was not the case for most chromosomes. Instead, the signals were often heterogeneous and spread over a large window, which sometimes extended up to 10 kb from the end of the scaffold (Fig. 2G). This was also the case for scaffolds annotated as “complete”. We therefore considered signals within 10 kb of a scaffold end as the left- or right-side telomere. This resulted in the completion of 10 previously incomplete scaffolds and added one end to another 7 incomplete large scaffolds (Supplementary Table S2). We additionally identified internal telomere sites on large scaffolds, many of which were barely outside the window for being defined as a chromosome end, which may suggest that the heterogeneity of chromosome ends extends even further than 10 kb. Interestingly, we also noted assembly gaps at or near many of these newly identified sites, perhaps the most notable of which is at the center of scaffold51_82, previously annotated as complete (Fig. 2H). At this location, the Subtle-seq reads map in both reverse and forward orientations at either side of the gap, effectively splitting the scaffold into two. The newly identified telomere sites around assembly gaps are therefore likely not an artefact of Subtle-seq but may rather reflect the true architecture of the scaffolds. We also identified several new telomere sites on the smaller scaffolds, but none of these had both ends, and these scaffolds are in general short and highly fragmented with varying depth of coverage and often large gaps, suggesting that they are not standalone chromosomes.

Little selective pressure against neotelomere formation in genic sequences

Since random fragmentation of chromosomes is presumably harmful to the cell, we wondered whether Paramecium evolved a way to avoid neotelomere formation on specific genomic features. To ask this question, we superposed the detected telomere sites over various genomic features, including genes and repetitive sequences. In young cells, telomeric reads were predominantly detected on intergenic, but not genic, regions (Fig. 3A), indicating that the initial chromosome fragmentation during MIC to MAC differentiation is controlled to some degree. As they progress in age, the genic signals increased proportionally and eventually reached around 80%, the theoretical level expected from the MAC genome structure (MAC genome is 81.16% genic). This transition suggests a lack of regulated fragmentation during the ageing process. We next investigated if actively expressed genes exhibit a lower level of neotelomere signals, which would imply the existence of selective constraints to avoid their fragmentation. For this analysis, we first focused on vegetative genes, defined as satisfying both “Expression_profile” being “none” in the ParameciumDB, and a VEG score >100 [32]. The vegetative genes account for 51.934% of all annotated genes (21 570 genes out of 41 533). Our analysis revealed a similar trend of neotelomere formation on these genes as the dataset containing all genes (Fig. 3B), with no apparent suppression of neotelomere formation. Nonetheless, the telomeric signals were lower in younger clonal ages, suggesting that the initial telomere formation (i.e. new MAC development) avoids chromosome fragmentation on genes required for vegetative growth. This is consistent with previous findings reporting that new MAC chromosomes are generated from the zygotic genome by introducing DSBs and de novo telomeres at predefined loci during sexual development, even though the process is imprecise and adds telomeres at locally heterogeneous positions [50].

Next, we analyzed neotelomere formation on autogamy-specific genes, as they are not expressed nor needed during vegetative growth. Interestingly, the formation of neotelomeres on autogamy-specific genes already saturated in the youngest samples (clonal age 4) (Fig. 3C), suggesting that there is no control of telomere formation on lowly expressed/autogamous genes. Encouraged by this finding, we grouped all annotated genes into five quantiles by their vegetative expression level based on previously published RNA-seq data [32]. Each group contains the same number of genes, where genes in group 5 have the highest expression level (Fig. 3D). We counted the number of neotelomeres formed within genes of each group and found that the signals in group 1 and 2 were enriched in the two youngest time points (Fig. 3E), but that the difference between the groups became less pronounced with increasing divisions. Overall, our results suggest that DSBs and neotelomere formation are regulated on genes important for vegetative growth during new MAC development and/or in young vegetative cells, but that this regulation becomes fuzzier in aged Paramecium cells. Moreover, while the MAC differentiation process generally avoids fragmentation on highly expressed genes, age-induced neotelomere formation show little selectivity on genic and intergenic features, regardless of their gene expression level.

Neotelomeres are enriched at putative nucleosome positioning sequences

In the related ciliate Tetrahymena thermophila, chromosome fragmentation and telomere-addition are induced in a sequence-dependent manner on sites known as CBSs during sexual development [48]. Although CBSs are seemingly absent during Paramecium MAC formation, it has never been investigated whether or not similar sequence-dependent fragmentation occurs during age-induced chromosome fragmentation. We therefore applied motif detection tools, including Sequence Logo [36] and STREME [51] to the reference genome sequences upstream and downstream of the telomere-added loci. Although we found a putative AT-rich motif around some neotelomere sites, we could not identify clear motifs at the center or near neotelomeres, suggesting that neotelomere formation is not controlled by a sequence-level motif (Supplementary Fig. S3A). We also did not identify any evidence for base-pairing with the telomerase RNA template, consistent with reports that ciliate telomerases do not require base-pairing with the template DNA to initiate de novo telomere addition [12, 52, 53]. This was neither the case when analyzing all telomere sites, nor when distal and proximal chromosome sites (within 2 kb of a chromosome end in the reference genome) were analyzed separately (Supplementary Fig. S3B–D). We did, however, identify a putative CNNCNNCN motif on a third of proximal neotelomere sites (excluding the 2 kb distal regions), but the significance of this motif remains unclear (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

Next, we investigated if neotelomere sites feature inverted repeats or palindromes. Inverted repeats and palindromes contain a pair of complementary sequences placed adjacent to each other with or without linker sequences, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Inverted repeats and palindromes form stem-loop or cruciform structures and are a cause of chromosome instability [54, 55]. Due to a lack of established tools to assess inverted sequences and palindromes, we calculated the Levenshtein distance between the sequence of interest and its reverse complement. The distance should be smaller if a palindrome/inverted repeat is present (Supplementary Fig. S4A; see “Materials and methods” section). However, there was no decrease in Levenshtein distances in neotelomere sequences compared to random control sequences, suggesting that there are no such structural motifs at neotelomeres (Supplementary Fig. S4B).

Interestingly, during the motif search, we noticed that telomere healing sites feature a nucleotide imbalance (Fig. 4A). Curiously, the imbalance was not a simple GC% change but an over/underrepresentation of G compared to C, which is commonly known as a GC skew. Similarly, the frequency of A compared to T was also skewed. Upon further investigation, we found that the frequency imbalance was determined by whether the nucleotide is purine (R; A and G) or pyrimidine (Y; T and C). While the nucleotides within R and Y behave similarly, R and Y fluctuate exclusively, and this skew sustained for 5–10 nucleotides (Fig. 4A). In other organisms, distortions of RR and YY dinucleotides are often associated with nucleosome positioning sequences (NPSs) [56–59]. We therefore calculated the occurrences of dinucleotides known to constitute NPSs and found that the dinucleotides frequency pattern bore a good resemblance to known NPSs (Fig. 4B). The telomere sites of the 4- and 93-division cultures were analyzed separately, and similar NPS-like signals were detected in both (Fig. 4B). Hence, the putative NPSs constitutively enrich at telomere sites, suggesting an involvement of nucleosomes in the formation of neotelomeres in Paramecium.

Neotelomeres enrich at nucleosomal core positions

Following the identification of putative NPSs at telomere sites, we next investigated if those loci are indeed nucleosome dense. The nucleosome landscape of Paramecium during vegetative growth has been investigated mainly in the context of gene expression or splicing [39, 60]. We reanalyzed published MNase-seq data [39] and found a striking accumulation of nucleosome signals at the positions where telomere sites were recorded in our samples (Fig. 4C). The accumulation was evident when using any of the Subtle-seq datasets in the clonal lifespan from young (4 divisions) to old (94 divisions) samples (Fig. 4C). Moreover, the nucleosome enrichment was more obvious at the positions of neotelomeres (telomeres detected in the chromosome body) than at annotated chromosome ends (Fig. 4D). Metagene analyses supported the same conclusion, and heatmaps of the signals at each telomere-healing site further confirmed that the enrichment was detectable at nearly all telomere healing loci (Fig. 4E and F). Since the annotated chromosome ends are the “original” telomeres added during new MAC development, these results indicate that the nucleosome enrichment is specific to the loci of age-induced neotelomeres, suggesting that nucleosomes are hotspots of chromosome fragmentation. It is worth noting that the MNase-seq data were acquired in a different laboratory at a different time. Therefore, we additionally performed nucleosome profiling in-house, which confirmed that the accumulation of nucleosome signals on neotelomere sites was reproducible (Supplementary Fig. S5). We conclude that even though neotelomere formation is seemingly random in the context of genomic features, its dependency on nucleosomes is strongly conserved in Paramecium.

The most well-defined nucleosomes in vegetative Paramecium are the +1 nucleosomes around transcription start sites (TSSs) [39, 60]. In some organisms, such as yeast and nematodes, the DNA sequence is not a major determinant of the positioning of the +1 nucleosome, whereas DNA motifs were detected in human and mouse +1 nucleosomes [56, 59, 61]. In Paramecium, we found no apparent NPS-like signals at the position of the +1 nucleosome, even though we initially detected a GC skew at this position (Supplementary Fig. S6A and B). The neotelomere signals at +1 nucleosomes or at the following periodical nucleosome array were also moderate compared to those at rogue NPS-driven nucleosomes (Supplementary Fig. S6C–E). Nonetheless, neotelomere signals at TSSs or transcription end sites (TESs), two positions where nucleosomes are depleted, were lower than in gene bodies (Supplementary Fig. S6C–E). Interestingly, the signal difference between TSS/TES and the gene body was absent in young Paramecium and was only detectable in aged cultures (Supplementary Fig. S6D and E). Heatmaps of the signals at each telomere-healing site further confirmed that this was not caused by a few loci with strong signals, but instead a general trend. Thus, the +1 or the following nucleosome array may induce DSBs and telomere healing. Moreover, the differences between the two age groups suggest that chromosome fragmentation on gene bodies is inducible by ageing.

Endo-SRC clusters are nucleosome-dense and accumulate neotelomere signals

In Paramecium, 23-nucleotide small RNAs are naturally induced from specific clusters known as siRNA producing clusters (SRCs) [62, 63]. SRCs largely overlap with genic regions, and may positively or negatively regulate gene expression. Some SRCs, however, are located in intergenic regions, and the function of such SRCs is unclear. In other organisms, a class of small RNAs termed DSB-induced small RNA (diRNA) or DNA damage response small RNA (DDRNA) have been reported at or around DSBs in plants or mammals, respectively [64, 65]. Although the precise role of diRNA/DDRNAs remains elusive, they have been suggested to facilitate the DSB repair response [66]. Since diRNA/DDRNA are widely observed from plants to humans, we were intrigued by the possibility of sRNA production at DSBs in Paramecium, and therefore investigated the overlap between previously annotated SRCs and neotelomere locations obtained in our study. Interestingly, we observed a clear accumulation of telomeric reads in several annotated SRCs (Fig. 5A). Moreover, we found the neotelomere signals to be significantly enriched in SRCs throughout the course of ageing (Fig. 5B; P < 2.2e-16). Thus, some endogenous siRNAs are produced from loci annotated as neotelomere sites (Fig. 5C). To determine whether SRC sites are an independent factor in breakage and telomere addition, we investigated the nucleosome density specifically in SRCs. Similar to the case at genome-wide neotelomere sites, sharp nucleosome signals were also detected at neotelomere sites in SRCs (Fig. 5D). Strikingly, the SRC sites themselves were much more nucleosome-rich than other parts of the genome (Fig. 5E), suggesting that neotelomere formation in SRCs may also be nucleosome-dependent. Moreover, the subset of SRCs with neotelomere signals showed higher nucleosome densities than those without neotelomeres (Fig. 5F). Taken together, our data indicates that SRCs are hotspots of chromosome breakage and neotelomere formation, presumably due to their high nucleosome density.

Reduced gene expression in aged cells is linked to neotelomere formation

Paramecium is known to show an ageing phenotype with reduced physiology, such as a loss of endocytic capacity or an increase in sensitivity to external stress [11, 67, 68]. How is this related to age-cumulative neotelomeres? To address this question, we performed mRNA-seq of young (24 divisions) and old (93 divisions) cells. As expected, differential expression (DE) analysis showed more downregulated (244 genes) than upregulated (32 genes) genes (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table S3). These genes did not appear to be downregulated simply by proximity to chromosome ends, as they were not preferentially enriched at the ends or the centers of chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B), suggesting that the altered expression of these genes is not driven by a location bias. GO-term enrichment analysis further revealed that most of the enriched pathways were from the downregulated gene sets and linked to various functions important for cell physiology, highlighting the reduced physiology of aged cells (Fig. 6B). We did not find evidence of a general decrease of DNA repair (increase in mutations) in the MAC as a cause of senescence, as there was neither an enrichment for GO terms involved in DNA mutation and repair (Fig. 6B) nor an increase in age-dependent mutations (Supplementary Fig. S7C and D).

Formation of neotelomeres on a gene will destroy its integrity, or it may induce transgene silencing in which a truncated gene reduces the allelic mRNA level [69, 70], collectively reducing the expression of the gene. Disruption of the 5′ regulatory region may also suppress transcription. Therefore, we examined whether the 244 differentially downregulated genes received more neotelomeres (Fig. 6C). Strikingly, the downregulated genes showed significantly more frequent neotelomere formation on their gene bodies than any of the 100 control trials containing a random set of 244 genes (the same genes used in both replicates), a trend observed in both biological replicates (P < .01; more frequent than any of the 100 randomized-control trials) (Fig. 6D). The distance between the downregulated genes and the closest upstream neotelomeres showed a similar trend (Fig. 6C and E), where downregulated genes tend to have neotelomeres closer (P < .01 for replicate 1). Hence, gene expression is negatively linked to neotelomeres on or in close proximity to genes, which suggests that these genes may have been destroyed by chromosome fragmentation.

In Paramecium, gene expression has been reported to positively correlate with nucleosome occupancy in the CDS [39]. Intriguingly, genes downregulated during ageing were predominantly highly expressed (Fig. 6F) and therefore likely to require higher nucleosome densities. Indeed, the downregulated genes showed higher nucleosome occupancies (Fig. 6G). Our data suggest that although a higher nucleosome occupancy is likely needed to upregulate gene expression [39], this may also induce chromosome fragmentation. As a result, the DE genes were likely downregulated by more frequent chromosome fragmentation on gene bodies as “collateral damage” of the higher nucleosome densities needed for active gene expression (Fig. 7).

The collateral DNA damage model of Paramecium ageing. Highly expressed genes recruit more nucleosomes as a means of gene expression control in Paramecium [39]. Nucleosomes induce telomere healing upon DNA damage during ageing, as shown in Fig. 4. As a consequence, neotelomeres preferentially accumulate on highly expressed genes, likely resulting in a downregulation of gene expression (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Paramecium has long served as a model of telomere biology and cellular ageing. A concept of cellular lifespan was proposed in ciliates by Sonneborn in 1954 [9], which preceded the discovery of the Hayflick limit for cultured cells in 1961 [71]. It has been speculated that de novo telomere formation has a pivotal role in Paramecium ageing, yet the details of neotelomere formation had been virtually unknown. Moreover, the rare nature of telomere healing in higher organisms and the challenges in detecting them with conventional sequencing methods, has constrained our understanding of telomere healing mechanisms, even though their importance is highlighted by an association with various diseases. Unlike most organisms, Paramecium has relaxed criteria for telomere addition and will indiscriminately add telomeres to any sequence in the macronucleus, even foreign injected linear DNA [12]. Consequently, mapping neotelomere sites during ageing allows the detection of senescence-induced chromosome fragmentation, and the high frequency of neotelomere sites makes Paramecium an ideal model to develop a method to detect and investigate neotelomere formation. In this study, we developed Subtle-seq to identify positions of genome-wide chromosome fragmentation by selectively sequencing telomere–subtelomere junctions. Using this method, we mapped age-accumulated neotelomeres throughout the clonal lifespan of Paramecium, which allowed us to identify genomic signatures of their formation and uncover a potential link between neotelomeres and the reduced expression of genes in aged cells.

Intriguingly, by analyzing public and in-house nucleosome profiling data, we found that age-related chromosome fragmentation is highly enriched on nucleosome core DNA. At the sequence level, age-related chromosome fragmentation shows no preference for repeat sequences, gene features, or sequence motifs, suggesting that age-related neotelomere formation occurs in a close-to-random manner in the sense of genomic features. A lack of motifs also supports the previous notion that the Paramecium telomerase RNA does not require base-pairing with its template DNA to initiate telomere elongation, unlike the case in other organisms [12]. However, at the epigenetic level, we detected a strong enrichment of neotelomere signals on nucleosome core DNA, which we propose as a new hallmark of neotelomere formation in ageing cells. This finding contradicts the common understanding of nucleosomes acting as a shield against DSBs [72–76]. An accumulation of DSBs in nucleosomes has also been reported in some other cases, including germline cells or when partially damaged DNA interacts with histones; in the former case, the DNA mutation level was elevated [77–79]. A simulation study has reported that the release of DSB lesions from nucleosomes is slower and restricts the access of the repair machinery [75], which may favor telomerization rather than DSB repair in organisms such as Paramecium with particularly high telomerase activity. Paramecium ageing is known to correlate with replication numbers rather than chronological age [80, 81], suggesting an influence of DNA replication stress on ageing. DNA replication stress is also a known potent inducer of DSBs in mammals [82–85], and a genome-wide analysis of lagging strand synthesis has revealed that the ligation of Okazaki fragments preferentially occur near nucleosome midpoints [86]. Given that DSBs are inducible at DNA single-strand breaks by the collapse or runoff of replication forks [83], neotelomere formation may also be consequence of DSBs arising at the replication fork. The precise molecular mechanism underlying DSBs and consequent telomere healing, however, requires further investigation.

To investigate the connection between neotelomeres and ageing, we performed differential gene expression analysis of young and old clones. This identified many down-regulated genes in the old clones, which had a higher frequency of neotelomere formation either on the gene itself or upstream of the ORF. The nucleosome occupancy has been reported to correlate with gene expression levels in Paramecium [39], and the differentially downregulated genes were predominantly highly expressed and showed higher levels of nucleosome signals in young cells. Hence, the density of nucleosomes needed for high expression of genes likely results in chromosome fragmentation as “collateral damage”, which may lead to reduced transcription and contribute to subsequent ageing phenotypes. However, it is important to note that although these two phenomena correlate, further work is required to determine if a causative relationship between them exists.

Our work based on an ageing theory in which the ageing phenotype is explained as a consequence of systemic dysfunction of the cell, induced by random chromosome fragmentation [13]. However, other works have suggested that there may be a single or a small number of genes that can drive the ageing phenotype in Paramecium. Tanabe et al. identified a long non-coding RNA termed MS2 (PTET.51.1.G0240166; GenBank AB231862) and its homolog as differentially expressed between different ages, and proposed MS2 as putative master regulators of ageing [87–89]. As intriguing as the theory may be, the genes were cloned more than two decades ago, yet there is no clear evidence that they are truly responsible for ageing. In our mRNA-seq data, these genes failed to pass our DE threshold (p.adjust = .839074 and .262733), and therefore we did not investigate them further. Mutations are another well-understood mechanism of ageing in model organisms, in addition to structural DNA damage such as DSBs [1,2,17]. Despite frequent telomere-healing in the MAC, Paramecium is known to have a highly accurate DNA replication system in the MIC, with base-substitution mutation rates magnitudes lower than fruit flies and nematodes [90]. In our RNA-seq data, neither GO term nor KEGG pathway analyses supported a general suppression of DNA repair as a cause of ageing (Fig. 6B and data not shown). We also directly measured SNPs in our RNA-seq reads, but did not observe increased mutation burdens (Supplementary Fig. S7C and D). Taken together, we conclude that the accumulation of mutations is less likely a cause of ageing in Paramecium.

The neotelomere signals detected in our study partially overlapped with published vegetative SRCs, which may suggest a DNA damage response similar to diRNA/DDRNAs at DSBs [64, 65]. Alternatively, these subtelomeric siRNAs may be transcribed at already-established neotelomeres. In mammals, dysfunctional telomeres induce the production of telomeric DDRNA (tDDRNA) from telomeric damage-induced long noncoding RNAs (tdilncRNAs) with the aid of DICER and DROSHA [91]. The siRNAs in Paramecium are also produced from longer precursors by a dicer protein [63, 92–94]. Therefore, if subtelomeric siRNAs are induced at established neotelomeres, a large number of siRNAs containing telomeric repeats should also be present. In the related ciliate Tetrahymena thermophila, sRNAs containing telomeric repeats have been described, though they belong to a class of conjugation-specific sRNAs, so-called scanning RNAs, which are transcribed from the entire genome [95]. We reanalyzed the Paramecium vegetative sRNA data from Karunanithi et al. [62], and indeed detected telomeric sRNAs, albeit in low abundance (up to 901 reads, 0.02% of the input). However, precursors of telomeric and subtelomeric siRNAs [tdilncRNAs or telomeric repeat-containing RNA (TERRA)] have not been described in Paramecium to date. The low abundance of telomeric siRNAs, together with our failed attempt to detect tdilncRNA by northern blotting (data not shown), implies that subtelomeric siRNAs are not produced from established neotelomeres. As such, we are unable to draw a clear conclusion as to whether the siRNAs produced from SRCs are truly diRNA/DDRNAs. Follow up experiments including experimental induction of DSBs at specific DNA sequences, as well as attempts to block tdilncRNA synthesis by gene silencing, would be required to address this question.

Recording telomere healing sites is challenging due to their scarcity. Recently, several groups reported long read sequencing protocols of telomeres, enabling measurement of the entire telomere length [16, 19]. However, methods utilizing long-read technology generate shallower read depth, reducing the detection power of rare neotelomeres. Since our interest was the comprehensive identification of genome-wide telomere healing, not the telomere or its length per se, we developed a method to specifically detect telomere–subtelomere boundaries. Subtle-seq was designed to detect rare telomere healing sites in a highly polyploid genome, where neotelomere reads can easily be overwhelmed by the large number of sister chromosomes. In a heterogeneous population of animal tissues, rare telomere healing sites can similarly be masked, not by sister chromosomes, but by the cells themselves existing in majority. Chromosome fragmentation by direct neotelomere formation has been reported ubiquitously across life, including human, the malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum, and yeast [96–99]. Thus, Subtle-seq can potentially be applied to detect rare chromosome fragmentation events in a wide range of samples. However, it is important to note that the method depends on DpnII digestion, and the efficiency by which a telomere site can be captured depends on the density of DpnII sites in the genome.

In conclusion, we successfully recorded ageing-associated chromosome fragmentation in Paramecium. We identified a novel role of an epigenomic mark in the formation of neotelomeres. While further work is required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanism of how nucleosomes induce neotelomere formation, our work uncovered a potential link between the accumulation of neotelomeres and the downregulation of genes in its proximity, thus greatly expanding our understanding of ageing in Paramecium.
