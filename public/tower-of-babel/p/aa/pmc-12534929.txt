Indications of missed malnutrition cases and growth monitoring challenges in a middle-income country
Authors: Merve Cicek Kanatli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition in children is closely associated with low socioeconomic status (SES). Effective growth monitoring is essential for identifying cases of wasting, stunting, or overweight. This study aimed to identify previously undiagnosed cases of malnutrition and to examine challenges related to growth monitoring in a low-SES region of Türkiye.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study included children aged 0–18 years who presented to a pediatric outpatient clinic in a low-SES region of Türkiye and were identified with malnutrition (stunting, wasting, or overweight) based on WHO criteria. Standardized measurements were used to assess height-for-age, weight-for-height, and BMI-for-age. A structured questionnaire was used to gather information on prior diagnoses, timing and setting of the last growth assessment, diagnostic work-up, and treatment. Data were analyzed with descriptive statistics.ResultsMalnutrition was detected in 105 (6.2%) of the 1,687 children evaluated during the study period. Two-thirds of the 105 malnourished children were undernourished (wasting and stunting), and one-third were overweight. Three-quarters of the malnourished children were aged ≥ 5 years. The majority (78%) reported that it was the first time they had been diagnosed with malnutrition, although 91% of them had seen a physician within the past year. Less than half could recall any previous measurements in the child’s lifetime.ConclusionIn conclusion, inadequate anthropometric measurements and missed malnutrition diagnoses indicate problems in growth monitoring in this low SES region. Missed malnutrition diagnoses, particularly above 5 years of age, require further action.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06184-5.

Background

Pediatric malnutrition, a prominent marker of societal and global health inequalities, poses a significant threat to children’s health, brain development, and broader societal progress. Malnutrition remains an important problem in both low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and high-income countries (HICs) [1, 2].

WHO defines malnutrition as including undernutrition (stunting and wasting), overweight, and micronutrient deficiencies. The subtypes of malnutrition that can be identified through growth monitoring are undernutrition (stunting and wasting) and overweight [3]. Stunting, which results from chronic undernutrition in early childhood, is associated with poor cognitive and academic outcomes in school-aged children and with an increased risk of chronic disease and lower economic productivity in adulthood [4, 5]. At the other end of the malnutrition spectrum, overweight in childhood is also associated with chronic diseases that lead to premature mortality in adulthood [6].

Despite its serious consequences, malnutrition may be overlooked by both families and physicians when growth is not assessed using anthropometric measurements. In one study, pediatric providers’ definitions of weight status on the basis of photographs of children were found to be accurate in only 58% of the cases [7]. Similarly, many families may be unaware of their children’s malnourishment. A systematic review revealed that 62% of families with overweight children perceived them to be of normal weight [8]. The differences in the perceptions of typical growth patterns may result in inadvertent overlooking of growth deviations, particularly in regions where growth issues are prevalent [4]. Accordingly, the assessment of growth must be conducted by healthcare professionals with the use of standardized tools.

Growth monitoring, which necessitates periodic and comparative assessments over time, provides a more comprehensive understanding of a child’s growth trajectory than a single point-in-time measurement. International healthcare institutions recommend growth monitoring at specific intervals [9, 10]. Furthermore, growth monitoring has been demonstrated to be a cost-effective screening method for the detection of treatable diseases associated with short stature such as growth hormone deficiency [11]. Furthermore, monitoring data on malnutrition in any given population is crucial for the development of appropriate prevention and intervention programs. A further function of growth monitoring is that it can draw attention to health inequalities by reporting prevalence of malnutrition in diverse socioeconomic groups. However, monitoring the growth of populations with low socioeconomic status (SES) can be challenging, even though this group exhibits the highest prevalence of malnutrition and would benefit the most from regular monitoring. Children living in low SES areas are known to have low rates of participation in well-child care, where growth monitoring is conducted, due to factors such as financial hardship, housing instability, and limited childcare options for siblings [12]. It has also been shown that those living in rural areas attend fewer well-child care visits than those in urban areas do[13]. Consequently, socioeconomic inequalities affect not only children’s growth but also their access to regular growth monitoring.

This study was conducted in Türkiye, which is classified as a middle-income country. There are significant inequalities in the growth markers of children between urban and rural regions and between low- and high-SES regions [14]. Despite a decline in Türkiye’s national average stunting rate in line with WHO targets [3]this overall improvement masks deep socioeconomic disparities: stunting remains 5.5 times more prevalent among children in the lowest SES quintile (12.3%) than in the highest (2.2%)[14]. Additionally, notable discrepancies exist in the growth markers of children across geographical regions, particularly between the eastern and western regions of the country. The frequency of stunting in northeast Anatolia, where this study was conducted, is approximately 8 times higher than in the region with the most favorable conditions—18.7% in northeast Anatolia compared to 2.35% in Istanbul, among the 12 regions included in the study [14]. This study was conducted in a state hospital in a small town in northeastern Anatolia in response to clinically observed but often unrecognized and inadequately managed cases of stunting and wasting. This study aimed to investigate whether caregivers of children with malnutrition were aware of a previous diagnosis of malnutrition (wasting, stunting, or overweight). It also examined when and where the child’s most recent growth assessment had taken place, in order to identify potential gaps in growth monitoring practices in this region.

Methods

Study design and setting (socioeconomic status and healthcare structure)

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in Taşlıçay, a small town in the province of Ağrı, which is located close to the eastern border of Türkiye. Among the 973 towns in Türkiye, Taşlıçay ranks 942nd in terms of socioeconomic development, representing the lowest level of the six stages of socioeconomic development [15].

In Türkiye, all Turkish citizens under the age of 18 years are eligible for comprehensive health insurance coverage [16]. While the availability of services is relatively high, challenges remain in accessing them owing to factors such as residence in a rural area, adverse winter conditions, and a physician-to-population ratio in the province that is half the national average [17]. Primary-level healthcare services are generally provided by family physicians in Türkiye. However, in the absence of a mandatory staged referral system, patients frequently present directly at a second-level or tertiary-level state hospital. The hospital where the study was conducted is a small, state-run facility serving the local community. In the Pediatric Outpatient Clinic (POC) of this hospital, approximately 50 patients a day are seen by a single pediatrician.

Participants and definition of malnutrition

The study included all children aged 0–18 years who visited the pediatric outpatient clinic between March 1 and May 31, 2018, underwent routine growth assessments, and met criteria for anthropometrically identifiable malnutrition subtypes (stunting, wasting, and overweight). This study focused on the relationship between malnutrition and growth monitoring; therefore, micronutrient deficiencies were not included, and no specific tests were conducted for their assessment. Malnutrition subtypes were identified and classified according to WHO standards [3, 18, 19]. For children aged 0–59 months, wasting was defined as weight-for-height < − 2 SD and overweight as weight-for-height > + 2 SD. For those aged 5–19 years, wasting was defined as BMI-for-age < − 2 SD and overweight as BMI-for-age > + 1 SD. Stunting was defined as height-for-age < − 2 SD across all age groups, based on the WHO growth references [3, 18, 19].

Anthropometric measurements

Each child’s height and weight were measured via standardized techniques. A stadiometer with a weighing scale capable of measuring to an accuracy of 0.1 cm and 100 g was used to assess the height and weight of the children aged ≥ 2 years. The weights of the children under 2 years of age were measured via an electronic digital platform, and their lengths were measured in the supine position on a measuring board by two measurers. To ensure consistency, all anthropometric measurements were conducted by the same trained healthcare professional.

Questionnaire

A questionnaire was developed to obtain information about patients’ last medical visit, anthropometric measurements, previous malnutiriton diagnosis and treatment (Supplementary file). The questionnaire inquired about the most recent height and weight measurements, the presence of a growth-related diagnosis, whether any work-up (e.g., blood/urine tests or hand–wrist radiography) was performed, and whether treatment or dietary intervention was given in diagnosed cases. Prior to the main data collection, the questionnaire was pilot-tested on 15 participants with similar demographic characteristics to the target population to assess clarity, and feasibility. In this population with a low literacy level, the questions were asked in Turkish by the pediatrician researcher or in Kurdish by the bilingual secretary who worked in the clinic.

Statistical analysis

The data collected through the questionnaire were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 24.0. Descriptive statistics are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and as numbers (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables. The primary variables studied included age, sex, malnutrition subtype (wasting, stunting, overweight), prior diagnosis and clinical evaluation for malnutrition, reasons for admission, reported anthropometric measurements within the past year, and socioeconomic indicators such as parental education level and number of siblings.

The normality of distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Based on the distribution, group comparisons were conducted using either the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In addition, due to the distinct growth patterns and health implications of malnutrition in early childhood versus school-age and adolescence, the data were also analyzed separately for children under and over 5 years of age [20].

Results

A total of 1,687 children presenting during the three-month study period were evaluated, and growth assessments were performed. To avoid duplication, repeat visits by the same child were excluded. Malnutrition was identified in 105 patients (6.2%), and all of these patients were included in the study without exclusion. Of those, 48 (45.7%) were stunted, 34 (32.4%) were wasted (4 patients were classified as both wasted and stunted), and 28 (26.7%) were overweight. Reasons for malnourished children’s presentations at the POC

The reasons for presenting to the POC were mostly concerns related to an acute disease such as sore throat and cough in 91 (86.6%) children. Nine (8.6%) patients presented with growth-related concerns such as poor weight gain and short stature and, 3 (2.9%) presented with chronic symptoms such as enuresis, and only 2 (1.9%) presented for well-child care.

Patients who had previously undergone any type of work-up because of malnutrition

Twelve (11.4%) of the 105 patients with malnutrition had been previously tested because of malnutrition. A work-up related to malnutrition was performed in 8 (16.6%) of the 48 patients with stunting and in 5 (14.7%) of the 34 patients with wasting. None of the 28 patients who were overweight indicated that they had undergone any type of work-up (Table 2).

Patients who had previously received treatment/diet recommendations

Seventeen patients (16.1%) stated that they had received diet recommendations/treatments (iron, zinc, vitamins, antiparasitic medication) due to this malnutrition subtype (Table 2).

Previously undiagnosed malnourished children

A total of 75 (91.4%) of the 82 (78,1%) previously undiagnosed patients had been seen by a physician (family physician, pediatrician, or general practitioner in the emergency department) within the past year. Among these children, 61 (71.3%) visited the emergency department, 34 (41.4%) family medicine, and 25 (30.4%) pediatrics. An evaluation of the 82 malnourished patients who had not been previously diagnosed revealed that the majority (78.1%) of them were children over the age of five (p < 0.001).

Previous growth assessments

The recall of any previously conducted measurement at any medical visit was markedly inferior in children aged > 5 years (29/80, 36.2%) than in children aged < 5 years (20/25, 80.0%) (p = 0.001). Among the 105 patients with malnutrition, 29 (27.6%) indicated that no prior measurements had been taken, whereas 26 (24.8%) could not recall whether the measurements had been conducted previously. A previous measurement was recalled by 50 (47.6%) of the subjects.

To ascertain whether a diagnosis of malnutrition had been established following recent measurements, data were reviewed for 49 (46.6%) malnourished children who had undergone growth assessments within the past year. Among these, 27 (55.2%) indicated that they had consulted with a physician within the past year but had not received a diagnosis.

Discussion

This study highlights critical gaps in growth monitoring and the possibility of missed malnutrition cases in a low-SES population, despite the presence of comprehensive health insurance in a middle-income country.

Among the children identified with malnutrition and included in the study, prior diagnosis was reported in only 21% of cases. Notably, 91% of those without a reported prior diagnosis had been seen by a physician within the past year. Another finding that raises concern regarding growth monitoring is that fewer than half of the malnourished children had received a diagnosis despite having undergone anthropometric measurements during the same period.

Although the children may have met the diagnostic criteria for malnutrition during previous medical visits, it is possible that the condition was not recognized or formally diagnosed at the time, which warrants further investigation. Alternatively, malnutrition may have developed after the medical visit. Another plausible explanation is that a diagnosis might have been made but not effectively communicated to the family. In some cases, language barriers or low parental education levels may have hindered understanding or recall of the diagnosis.

When examining the underlying causes of inadequate growth monitoring, certain controversial dynamics emerge. Despite the presence of universal health insurance in the country and the absence of major financial barriers to accessing to healthcare services, regional disparities in the distribution of healthcare professionals persist [21]. Interestingly, these disparities do not appear to hinder the near-complete coverage of childhood immunizations. This contrast suggests a prioritization within healthcare services, indicating that growth monitoring may not be receiving adequate attention or priority in the current system.

When the identified malnutrition cases were examined by age, the overwhelming majority of malnourished children (76%) and previously undiagnosed cases (78%) were over five years of age. This discrepancy may be partly explained by the larger population of children in the 5–18 age group compared to those under five. Additionally, growth assessments are conducted less frequently in older children, which may have contributed to the underrecognition and persistence of malnutrition under-five. Regardless of the underlying causes, it is reasonable to conclude that this considerable number of children, particularly those with undernutrition, are not receiving the requisite attention. This is evident at both the local and international policy levels. A high level of priority is given to children under-five in the malnutrition reports of the WHO and UNICEF [3]. This is reasonable, as undernutrition under-five is associated with increased mortality, and the early childhood period can be said to have a significant effect on life overall [3]. Nevertheless, malnutrition during school-age childhood and adolescence has specific risks and implications, including the potential to perpetuate a cycle of intergenerational malnutrition by affecting future maternal nutritional status and birth outcomes [4].

The distribution of malnutrition subtypes by age revealed notable findings. All children with wasting were over five years of age. This may partly be explained by more frequent growth monitoring in children under five. In school-aged children, potential contributors to wasting may include insufficient school nutrition, excessive consumption of snacks, micronutrient deficiencies, acute conditions such as gastroenteritis, parasitic infections, and the tendency of families in large households to prioritize the nutritional needs of younger siblings [22]. Stunting was the most common form of malnutrition among children under five years of age. It is estimated that the majority of early stunting cases are prenatally programmed and closely linked to maternal nutritional status [4]. The province where the study was conducted had the highest rate of marriages under the age of 18 (11%) among Türkiye’s 81 provinces, along with a relatively high proportion of adolescent mothers [23]. Additionally, the predominance of wasting from school age onwards in this study highlights the importance of investigating the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition in further research.

Although the overall rate of reported prior diagnosis across the three malnutrition subgroups was low (21%), it was particularly low among children with overweight, of whom only 7% had reported a prior diagnosis. Given that undernutrition is observed more frequently in this low SES region, families and healthcare professionals may consider being overweight to be less of a problem. When the national data are examined, the rate of 7.2% of overweight children in this region is slightly lower than the average for Türkiye of 8.1%[14]. However, the epidemiological data from other LMICs throughout the world have shown that in stunted children, there is an increased risk of obesity in adolescence and adulthood, a phenomenon referred to as the “double burden.” This paradoxical trajectory reflects the complex nutritional transitions occurring in LMICs, and is believed to result from early-life growth restriction followed by rapid weight gain later in childhood, which disrupts normal metabolic programming. Such a pattern increases susceptibility to metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease in later life [24]. Although overweight has not yet reached critical levels in this population, the predominance of stunting among malnutrition types, along with findings indicating low awareness of overweight, highlights the need to raise awareness among both healthcare workers and the general population.

The most common reason for presentation was acute disease (86%), and only 1.9% of the children presented for well-child care. This is a region of low SES, and low SES is known to be associated with low attendance in well-child care, where growth monitoring is a core component. Low attendance at well-child visits can be attributed to the low level of health literacy (some families do not know of the need for well-child visits when not ill) and transport problems of families living in rural areas [25]. Since malnutrition is more common in low SES populations, the low attendance of well-child care visits should be addressed.

Owing to low attendance at well-child visits and frequent presentations with acute disease [26]“opportunistic growth measurement”[27] can be applied in low SES children presenting for any reason. In the period when the current study was conducted, every child who visited the POC for any reason, including acute disease, was assessed for growth. Over a three-month period at a single medical facility, more than 100 children were diagnosed with malnutrition, and according to caregiver reports, approximately four out of five had not previously been diagnosed—underscoring the importance of opportunistic growth assessments.

Strengths and limitations

Through careful observations of a pediatrician engaged in solitary and busy practice within a modest rural hospital, this study highlights significant health and healthcare problems in the region. This study has several limitations. Due to working conditions such as time constraints, the scope of data collection was limited. Demographic variables—particularly education level, socioeconomic status, and residential location (urban vs. rural)—were not collected. One of the key limitations is the reliance on parent-reported information regarding children’s prior health monitoring, in the absence of access to medical records. This introduces a potential recall bias. Future studies should consider integrating electronic health records to improve data accuracy and reduce reliance on caregiver recall.

Conclusions

This study reveals substantial weaknesses in the implementation of routine growth monitoring in this region, resulting in a significant number of malnutrition cases going undiagnosed. Despite healthcare contact and, in some cases, recent anthropometric measurements, malnutrition remained unrecognized in many children. Notably, the fact that the majority of undiagnosed malnutrition cases were observed among children over five years of age highlights the necessity of ensuring effective growth monitoring and appropriate management of malnutrition in this age group. These findings underscore the urgent need to strengthen growth monitoring practices as an integral part of pediatric healthcare delivery across all pediatric age groups.
