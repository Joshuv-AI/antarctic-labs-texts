Patient‐derived forebrain cortical organoids reveal biphasic tau–MAP6–microtubule axis dysfunction in tauopathy
Authors: Xiaohuan Sun, Skandha Ramakrishnan, Victor C Ogbolu, Nicholas M Kanaan, Celeste M Karch, Peter W Baas, Liang Qiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIn frontotemporal dementia (FTD), tau detaches from axonal microtubules and forms pathological aggregates. Rather than stabilizing microtubules, tau promotes labile microtubule domains, redefining its role in neurodegeneration and underscoring the need for human models that capture temporal disease progression.METHODSHuman induced pluripotent stem cells carrying MAPT WT/P301L, MAPT WT/P301S, or MAPT WT/R406W mutations and isogenic controls were differentiated into forebrain cortical organoids (1 to 8 months). Tau isoforms, microtubule dynamics, MAP6 regulation, neuronal activity, tau mRNA stability, and tau pathology were analyzed using biochemical, imaging, and electrophysiological approaches, some of which were benchmarked to postmortem behavorial variant FTD cortex.RESULTSEarly‐phase tau mutant organoids showed elevated tau, hyperdynamic microtubules, and neuronal hyperexcitability, partially reversible by tau reduction. Late‐phase organoids exhibited insoluble tau accumulation, microtubule hyperstability, and neurodegeneration and reactive astrocytes, accompanied by opposing, phase‐dependent MAP6 changes.DISCUSSIONThis work reveals a biphasic tau‐MAP6‐microtubule mechanism driving tauopathy and establishes these organoids as a platform for phase‐specific therapy.Keywords: cortical organoids, MAP6, microtubule, tau, tauopathy

BACKGROUND

Tau is a microtubule (MT)‐associated protein encoded by the MAPT gene on chromosome 17q21.31, where alternative splicing yields six major isoforms in the adult human brain. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 In neurons, MT‐bound tau localizes predominantly to axons, where it promotes the labile (dynamic) domains of MTs, enabling cytoskeleton remodeling and efficient axonal transport rather than directly stabilizing the lattice. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 In tauopathies such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), tau becomes aberrantly phosphorylated, shows reduced axonal MT binding, mislocalizes, and assembles into insoluble aggregates (oligomers and neurofibrillary tangles). 10 , 11 Tauopathies also commonly feature loss of MT mass and defects in organelle transport. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Despite decades of study, the MT‐related mechanisms underlying these axonal failures remain incompletely understood.

Historically, MT loss in tauopathy was attributed to lattice destabilization after tau dissociation, a view that motivated trials of MT‐stabilizing drugs. Despite encouraging preclinical effects, these agents failed clinically. 17 , 18 , 19 More recent work challenges this dogma, showing in cultured rodent neurons that tau depletion reduces axonal MT mass by shortening labile domains rather than destabilizing the stable lattice, and the remaining labile regions become more turnover‐resistant as MAP6 binds more avidly when tau is reduced. 5 , 6 , 9 However, because these findings are derived largely from tau knockdown in rodent systems, it remains unclear whether the same MT‐related mechanisms operate in human tauopathies.

Recent observations further support a model in which tau and MAP6 function as opposing regulators of microtubule behavior. 9 , 20 , 21 In several tauopathies, tau levels appear to rise early, expanding the pool of MT‐bound tau; however, this increase may eventually give way to progressive sequestration of tau into oligomers and filaments, reducing the fraction of functional tau available to regulate the cytoskeleton. 11 , 20 , 21 Thus, an early phase may feature excess MT lability, not because tau stabilizes the lattice, but because heightened MT‐bound tau outcompetes endogenous MT stabilizers including MAP6, shifting MT composition toward labile domains. A subsequent phase would produce the inverse state, as functional tau is depleted by sequestration, MTs become abnormally stable. Therefore, we hypothesize a two‐phase MT dysregulation in tauopathy, as an early, abnormally labile phase followed by a later, abnormally stable phase. Here, to test this biphasic model directly in a human context, we use patient induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived forebrain cortical organoids carrying MAPT WT/P301L, MAPT WT/P301S, or MAPT WT / R406W mutations alongside their isogenic CRISPR‐corrected controls, as well as postmortem frontal cortex from human behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD) cases, to define the phase‐dependent microtubule dysregulation that drives tauopathy.

METHODS

Human iPSC (hiPSC) cultures

To enhance rigor, we included three individual tau mutations as well as corresponding isogenic control hiPSCs for this study, obtained from Dr. Celeste Karch's laboratory at Washington University and established in our lab. The hiPSCs included cells derived from FTD patients carrying the heterozygous MAPT WT/P301L mutation (hereafter designated as “P301L”). Its isogenic control was corrected via CRISPR‐Cas9 (referred to as “Control”). Additionally, this control line was later modified using CRISPR‐Cas9 to introduce the heterozygous MAPT WT/P301S mutation, creating the “P301S” line. The last set included heterozygous MAPT WT/R406W mutant hiPSCs (referred to as “R406W”) and its corresponding isogenic control corrected by CRISPR‐Cas9 (referred to as “Control”). 22 All hiPSCs were cultured using a feeder‐free system with mTeSR plus medium (Stem Cell Technologies, Catalog No.:100–0276) on Matrigel‐coated dishes. Matrigel is prepared in cold DMEM‐F12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:11330032) for 1 h at 37°C before use. ReleSR (Stem Cell Technologies, Catalog No.:05872) was used for cell passaging. Briefly, the hiPSCs were treated with ReleSR for 1 min at room temperature before aspirating the solution. Subsequently, hiPSCs were incubated for 4 min at 37°C. The hiPSC colonies were detached by gently tapping on the sides of the plate, using mTeSR plus medium with 10 µM Rock inhibitor Y‐27632 (Tocris, Catalog No.:1254), and then the hiPSC colonies were transferred to the Matrigel‐coated wells. Maintenance of hiPSCs involved changing the medium to 2 mL every day or 3 mL every other day. When hiPSCs reached around 90% confluence, they were used to generate organoids.

Forebrain cortical organoid generation and long‐term culture

We generated forebrain cortical organoids using an adapted protocol based on the previous published strategy. 23 The process began with the formation of embryoid bodies (EBs) by collecting hiPSCs using either 0.35 mg/mL dispase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:17105041) for 20 to 30 min or ReleSR (Stem Cell Technologies, Catalog No.:05872) for 4 to 5 min. These EBs were cultured in six‐well ultra‐low attachment plates (Stem Cell Technologies, Catalog No.:38071) using mTesR plus supplemented with SMAD inhibitors: 5 µM dorsomorphin (Sigma‐Aldrich, Catalog No.:P5499) and 10 µM SB‐431542 (Tocris, Catalog No.:1614) for the first 6 days. Additionally, 10 µM of Rock inhibitor Y‐27632 (Tocris, Catalog No.:1254) was added during the first 2 days to enhance cell survival. From day 6 to day 23, we transitioned to a neural differentiation medium consisting of Neurobasal A medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:10888022), B27 supplement without vitamin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:12587010), and GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:35050061), further enriched with 20 ng/mL each of epidermal growth factor (Peprotech, Catalog No.:AF‐100‐15) and basic fibroblast growth factor (Peprotech, Catalog No.:100‐18B). Following this, we employed a neural maturation medium from day 23 onward, comprising Neurobasal A medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:10888022), B27 plus supplement with vitamin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:A3582801), GlutaMAX, and Normocin (InvivoGen, Catalog No.:ant‐nr‐05), supplemented with 20 ng/mL each of Brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (Peprotech, Catalog No.:450‐02) and Neurotrophin‐3 (NT‐3 (Peprotech, Catalog No.:450‐03). At approximately day 30, the developing organoids were transferred to an orbital shaker for long‐term culture at 65 rpm, which continued for up to 8 months. Throughout this extended culture period, we performed half medium changes twice weekly to maintain optimal growth conditions and nutrient levels.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

We extracted RNA samples from organoids at two distinct developmental stages (1 month and 8 months) and from frozen postmortem human tauopathy brain tissue. The extraction process was carried out using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Catalog No.:12183018A), adhering strictly to the manufacturer's protocol to ensure high‐quality RNA yield. Following extraction, we quantified the RNA concentration using a nanodrop spectrophotometer. For reverse transcription, we utilized up to 2 µg of the extracted RNA. The conversion of RNA to cDNA was performed using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Catalog No.:4368813). This kit was selected for its reliability in producing high‐quality cDNA suitable for downstream applications. Upon completion of the reverse transcription process, the resulting cDNA was immediately ready for use in quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and reverse transcription PCR (RT‐PCR) analyses.

Tau mRNA stability assay

To assess tau mRNA stability, we conducted a transcription inhibition assay on 1‐month organoids. Organoids were treated with either DMSO (as control) or 2 µg/mL of the transcription inhibitor actinomycin D (Sigma, Catalog No.:A9415‐2MG) for durations of 12 and 24 h. Following treatment, we extracted RNA from these samples and synthesized cDNA using the protocol described earlier. We then evaluated MAPT mRNA levels in the presence of actinomycin D, a transcription inhibitor, at 12 and 24 h, comparing it to the DMSO‐treated control. This quantification was performed using qPCR with tau‐specific primers. The assay employed 2X Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (Abclonal, Catalog No.:RK21203), with each reaction containing 25 ng of cDNA and 0.4 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers. As a housekeeping control, we used primers for ribosomal protein S18 (RPS 18). The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 32 s), and concluded with a default melt curve analysis. We used the resulting cycle threshold (Ct) values for subsequent data analysis to quantify tau mRNA levels and assess its stability under transcription inhibition.

Evaluation of tau isoforms by semi‐quantitative RT‐PCR

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of all six tau mRNA isoforms using semi‐quantitative PCR. Our approach employed tau‐specific primers (Table S1) designed to target either the N‐terminal or MT‐binding domains, with ß‐actin primers serving as the housekeeping control. The RT‐PCR assay utilized GoTaq Green Master Mix (Promega, Catalog No.:M7123), with each reaction containing 100 ng of cDNA and 1 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial pre‐denaturation phase (55°C for 30 min, followed by 94°C for 2 min), followed by 40 cycles of amplification. Each cycle involved denaturation at 94°C for 15 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 60 s. The process concluded with a final extension step at 72°C for 5 min. Following amplification, we separated the PCR products on a 2% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide for DNA visualization. The resulting bands were imaged using a Bio‐Rad imaging system, and band intensities were quantified using Fiji (ImageJ) software. To ensure reliability, each sample was run in duplicate, with the mean intensity of each band represented as a single data point.

Postmortem human brain tissue samples

For this study, we acquired two sets of patient tissue samples. Postmortem human brain tissue from the superior and anterior frontal gyri was obtained through the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), Memory and Aging Center, Neurodegenerative Disease Brain Bank (NDBB). The cohort consisted of two distinct groups, with three individuals per group – control subjects, behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) patients with the MAPT P301L mutation. For each subject, we procured 5 g of fresh‐frozen tissue. The second set of samples from bvFTD patients with the MAPT IVS 10 + 16 C>T mutation was supported by Dr. Celest Karch from Washington University. De‐identified details on the patient samples, which include, age, sex, genotype, and postmortem interval (PMI), can be found in Table S2.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Protein lysates from 1‐ and 8‐month organoids, as well as postmortem human brain tissue, were prepared on ice using Pierce RIPA Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:89901) supplemented with Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (100X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:78446). The organoids and brain tissue were suspended in this combined lysis buffer and thoroughly homogenized. The lysates were then sonicated three times for 5 s each on ice, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:23227). Protein samples (20 µg) were prepared with 4X Bolt LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen, B0008) and 10X Bolt Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen, Catalog No.:B0004). The samples were denatured at 100°C for 10 min in IncuBlock Mini Dry Baths (labForce, DB‐M05), briefly centrifuged, and loaded into Bolt 4% to 12% Mini Protein Gels (Invitrogen, Catalog No.:NW04120BOX). Gel electrophoresis was performed in a mini gel tank (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:A25977) at 100 V for 2.5 h. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio‐RAD, Catalog No.:162‐0177) using the Mini Blot module (Invitrogen, Catalog No.:B1000), either at 0.34 A for 2 h 45 min for instant transfer or at 0.1 A at 4°C overnight. After transfer, protein bands were visualized using Ponceau S dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:BP102‐10), and the membranes were cut based on the molecular weight of the targeted proteins. Membranes were blocked for 1 h in Intercept Blocking Buffer (Li‐COR, Catalog No.:927‐60001). Primary antibodies were incubated with the membranes at 4°C overnight with constant rotation. The following day, membranes were washed three times for 10 min each with 1X Tris‐buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), then incubated with appropriate Li‐COR IRDye secondary antibodies (680 or 800) for 2 h at room temperature. After three more 10‐min washes with 1X TBST, the membranes were dried in the dark and prepared for imaging. Images were captured using a Li‐COR Odyssey imaging system, which allows for precise quantification of fluorescent signals. Band intensities were analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ). This software enables accurate measurement of protein expression levels by quantifying the relative density of each band. To ensure reproducibility and statistical validity, all experiments were independently replicated at least three times.

To investigate the soluble and insoluble fractions of tau, we developed a modified protein extraction protocol for 1‐ and 8‐month organoids, as well as human brain samples. 24 The samples were homogenized in ice‐cold 0.25 M sucrose buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA) containing protease inhibitors, then centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min at 4°C. An equal volume of cold sucrose buffer containing 1% Triton X‐100 was then added to the supernatants, which were then centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to separate supernatants (a soluble fraction seen in 0.5% Triton‐X‐100) and the Triton‐X‐100 insoluble fractions, seen as pellets were resuspended in 1% SDS in PBS. Both fractions were then subjected to the standard WB procedure as previously described, including denaturation, gel electrophoresis, transfer to PVDF membranes, and immunoblotting with human tau‐specific antibody (Tau13, homemade antibody, Kanaan lab, diluted 1:2000).

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) analysis

Organoids were rinsed with D‐PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.:10010023) once and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Fixation times varied based on organoid age: 1‐month organoids were fixed for 8 h to avoid over‐fixation, while 8‐month organoids were fixed overnight, both at 4°C. Following fixation, organoids were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose until fully saturated. Cryosections 25 µm thick were mounted on slides using M1 Embedding Matrix (Epredia Catalog No.:1310), with tau mutant and control organoids placed on the same slide for direct comparison. Slides were air‐dried at room temperature overnight before staining.

For staining, slides were washed in PBS for 15 min, then incubated in a quenching buffer (70% PBS, 30% methanol, 0.5% hydrogen peroxide) for 1 h at room temperature. After three 5‐min PBS washes, slides were blocked with either 10% goat or donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies, diluted in PBS with 0.1% Triton X‐100, were applied overnight at 4°C (see Table S3 for more information on dilutions and catalog numbers). Secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, goat or donkey, conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488, 555, or 647) were used at 1:1000 dilution in PBS. Slides underwent a final washing sequence: PBS, DAPI (Invitrogen, Catalog No.: D1306, final concentration 0.25 µg/mL in PBS), and PBS again. Coverslips (Corning, Catalog No.:2975‐245) were mounted using Fluoro‐Gel with Tris Buffer (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Catalog No.:1798510), and slides were dried overnight at room temperature before imaging.

Images were captured using a Leica SP8 inverted confocal microscope or Leica Thunder microscope with 20×, 40×, and 63× objectives, utilizing LAS X software. Navigation, tiling, and thunder functions were employed to obtain high‐resolution images of entire organoid sections without pixel saturation. Imaging parameters were kept consistent across all replicates and time points, with DAPI, Alexa Fluor – 488, 555, and 647 acquired simultaneously in each session. This approach minimized technical variabilities and ensured that the comparisons across conditions reflected biological differences rather than imaging‐related artifacts. Quantification was performed using Fiji (ImageJ) ROI manager. Average fluorescence intensity was measured by tracing the organoid section or individual neuronal processes as regions of interest, with normalization to DAPI intensity in the same region.

MT dynamic analysis via nocodazole in organoids

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of MT dynamics in 1‐month control and P301S mutant organoids using a time‐course nocodazole treatment. Organoids were exposed to nocodazole (Sigma, Catalog No.:M1404‐2MG) at a final concentration of 2 µg/mL, diluted from a 2 mg/mL stock solution. Treatment durations were 15, 60, and 120 min, with DMSO‐treated organoids serving as controls. Following treatment, organoids underwent a carefully optimized fixation and cryoprotection protocol. They were fixed for 8 h at 4°C, then transferred to a 30% sucrose solution for overnight cryoprotection at 4°C. Subsequently, the organoids were cryosectioned to a thickness of 25 µm. ICC was performed on these sections to visualize and quantify specific MT marker βIII‐tubulin. The quantifications were performed as βIII‐tubulin fluorescence intensity normalized to DAPI and plotted as relative change to control organoids treated with DMSO.

Multielectrode array (MEA) recording and tau antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) treatment

One‐month control and P301S organoids were treated with control ASO and human‐specific tau ASO, provided by IONIS Inc. The ASOs were administered at 10 nM in three doses over a 14‐day period, after which the organoids were harvested to assess tau protein levels via WB. Following validation of tau ASO efficacy, individual 1‐month organoids were positioned centrally within single wells of a MEA system (Harvard Apparatus, Catalog No.:MEA2100‐Systems). P301S mutant organoids underwent a targeted treatment with tau ASO at 2 nM for 3 days. Extracellular field potential recordings were conducted for each organoid over a 5‐min duration using Multi Channel Experimenter software. Spikes were detected when the signal exceeded 6 standard deviations from the baseline noise. Bursts were detected when there were ≥ 4 spikes in 50 ms with 100 ms between bursts, with a maximum interval of 50 ms to start and end the burst. Network bursts were quantified as bursts detected simultaneously in three electrodes out of six different electrodes. Subsequently, the acquired data were processed and analyzed using MultiChannel Analyzer to evaluate individual spikes, bursts, and network bursts. The resulting data were then collated and organized in Excel for further analysis.

Statistical analyses

Data were organized in Microsoft Excel, and the statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad; the graphs were generated using GraphPad. The data are shown as the mean ± SEM. Student's t‐test was used for the statistics comparing each sample group against its appropriate control, but one‐way or two‐way ANOVA was conducted to compare multiple groups/criteria against each other. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

hiPSC validation and forebrain cortical organoid generation

Our previous studies revealed that organoids that generated MAPT WT/P301S mutation showed an increase in total tau and phosphorylated tau. 25 Here, to determine whether distinct mutations could elicit the same changes in tau, we chose to compare the common P301L with the rare P301S mutation in our models. Both mutations occurred at the proline 301 site in exon 10. 26 Although P301S is less common than P301L, they are presumed to have similar effects. For instance, both P301S and P301L tau show a similar extent of aggregation. 27 However, other studies showed that P301L transgenic mice did not exhibit cognitive impairment, 28 unlike P301S transgenic mice (PS19 mice), which displayed neuronal loss and tau pathology, leading to impaired tauopathy‐related functions, including motor and memory deficits. 29 , 30 , 31 In addition to the two mutation lines at the P301 site, we generated forebrain cortical organoids from hiPSCs derived from a FTDP‐17 patient carrying a distinct MAPT mutation at a different site (MAPT WT/R406W), along with matched isogenic controls. To examine whether distinct tau mutations produce conserved or divergent MT‐related phenotypes affecting MT dynamics and stability. All cell lines were rigorously validated to confirm the presence of heterozygous MAPT mutations P301S, P301L, and R406W using targeted PCR and DNA sequencing analysis (Figure 1A–C). Subsequently, we generated dorsalized forebrain cortical organoids from all hiPSC lines using our established differentiation protocol (Figure 1D). 25 The cultures were maintained and analyzed at 1 month and 8 months of development. These organoids were characterized and validated using various cell‐specific markers to assess cell diversity and morphology through immunostaining. In 1‐month organoids, we observed neural progenitor cells marked by SOX2, proliferating cells marked by Ki67, neuronal nuclei marked by NeuN, astrocytes marked by glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and tau (Figure 1E–G). The 8‐month organoids showed significant expansion compared to 1‐month organoids and exhibited improved maturity and neuronal morphology. This was evidenced by the presence of dendrite marker MAP2, glutamatergic neuron marker vGLUT, and mature astrocytic marker S100β (Figure 1H,M).

Increased MAPT mRNA levels and stability in P301S organoids

During human brain development, MAPT exists in six isoforms due to alternative splicing of N‐terminal exons (2 and 3), resulting in 0N, 1N, and 2N variants. Additional alternative splicing of the MT‐binding domain exon 10 produces 3R and 4R isoforms. While 3R tau is expressed throughout life, 4R tau expression begins postnatally, and in the adult human brain, the 3R and 4R tau isoforms are maintained at an approximately 1:1 ratio. 32 , 33 , 34 Having successfully generated organoids, we first examined the expression pattern of tau isoforms in these organoids, focusing on the impact of the P301S mutation. Using semi‐quantitative RT‐PCR with specific MAPT primers as previously described, 35 we examined tau isoform expression in 1‐ and 8‐month organoids to determine whether these cultures recapitulate human developmental progression, with 8‐month organoids representing a more mature tau isoform state compared to 1‐month organoids. Our findings revealed that the 0N3R tau isoform was consistently expressed in both 1‐ and 8‐month control organoids. In contrast, the mature tau isoforms (0N4R, 1N4R, 2N4R), typically associated with adult brains, were only observed in 8‐month organoids (Figure 2A). We also evaluated changes in MAPT transcription levels due to the P301S and R406W mutation. The transcription level of the 0N tau isoform increased significantly by 30.4% in 1‐month P301S organoids and by 24.39% in 1‐month R406W organoids, and 3R tau increased by 74.8% in 1‐month P301S organoids and by 92.18% in 1‐month R406W organoids compared to controls (Figure 2B,C and E–G). Similarly, in 8‐month P301S organoids, the transcription levels of 0N + 1N + 2N tau increased by 35.1%, and 3R + 4R tau increased by 50.9% compared to controls (Figure 2B,C).

Out of the two mutations showing the increase in MAPT expression, the effects of P301S mutation were truly surprising. Given that the mutation was located on exon 10, an exon that regulates 3R and 4R tau balance and is typically spliced out during embryonic development, we investigated how a mutation in this exon influences MAPT expression. The observed increase in MAPT expression may reflect either enhanced transcription or reduced mRNA degradation. To distinguish between these possibilities, we treated 1‐month control and P301S mutant organoids with the transcriptional inhibitor actinomycin D 36 for 12 and 24 h, with DMSO‐treated organoids as controls. RNA was extracted and reverse‐transcribed to cDNA to evaluate the MAPT mRNA and assess its decay over time using qPCR. The results, plotted with treatment duration on the x‐axis and MAPT mRNA (relative to DMSO‐treated organoids) on the y‐axis, showed that after 12 h of actinomycin D treatment, mRNA levels in P301S organoids were 43.51% higher than in controls. After 24 h, the difference was 15.5%. This slower decay rate in P301S organoids indicates that the mutation leads to abnormally stabilized tau mRNA (Figure 2D).

Inverse regulation of tau and MAP6 protein levels in tau mutant organoids and human bvFTD postmortem brain samples

To evaluate the protein levels associated with the transcriptional changes we observed, we performed WB analysis to assess tau levels. We found that different tau mutations increased the total tau protein, evaluated using the total tau antibody Tau13, by 41.89% (P301S), 40.56% (P301L), and 33.37% (R406W) in 1‐month organoids and by 16.07% (P301S) and 94.23% (P301L) in 8‐month organoids compared to control, in either immunostaining or WB (Figure 2H,I,K,L,O,P). Interestingly, the level of the genuine MT‐stabilizing protein MAP6 decreased by 31.09% (P301S), 26.76% (P301L), and 31.87% (R406W) in 1‐month organoids and increased by 53.22% (P301S) and 99.93% (P301L) in 8‐month organoids (Figure 2H−J,M,N,O−Q, Table S4). Overall, this indicates that in 1‐month organoids, P301S, P301L, or R406W mutation increases total tau level and decreases MAP6 level, indicating tau and MAP6 elicit reciprocal expression patterns. However, in 8‐month organoids, both P301S and P301L mutations increase both total tau level and MAP6 level. To further validate our tau and MAP6 data observed in 8‐month tau mutant organoids and to anchor these findings to human pathology, we assessed their levels in postmortem brain tissue from bvFTD patients carrying the P301L and IVS 10+16 mutation. We observed a 35.46% increase in total tau levels in bvFTD brains compared to healthy brain tissue (Figure 2R,S). This finding is consistent with the increased total tau observed in our 8‐month P301S and P301L mutated organoids. Interestingly, MAP6 levels were elevated by 196.88% in bvFTD brains carrying the MAPT WT/P301L mutation and by 113.25% in brains with the MAPT WT/IVS 10 + 16 mutation (Figure 2T,U). To determine whether the observed increase in MAP6 was influenced by astrocyte population, we co‐stained 8‐month organoids with GFAP and MAP6 and quantified MAP6 fluorescence intensity (Figure S1). No significant difference was detected between control and MAPT WT/P301S, suggesting that the observed effect was primarily driven by neuronal populations. This increase in MAP6 levels aligns with our observations in 8‐month P301S and P301L mutated organoids. The recapitulation between organoid and human bvFTD brain data reinforces the pathological relevance of the biphasic tau‐MAP6 axis and validates our model as reflective of pathophysiology.

Biphasic microtubule dynamics associated with inverse tau–MAP6 alterations in tau mutant organoids

To assess MT stability across genotypes in 1‐ and 8‐month organoids, we first employed semi‐quantitative double‐label immunofluorescence as a proxy measure of MT dynamicity. MAPT WT/P301S and MAPT WT/P301L organoids were used as representative mutation models in this study. This approach combines total tubulin (βIII‐tubulin) with markers of stable MTs (acetylated or detyrosinated tubulin) 5 , 37 and labile MTs (tyrosinated tubulin). 38 , 39 Organoids were fixed at defined time points and stained with antibodies against these tubulin variants, allowing comparison of MT dynamics under consistent experimental conditions. Using this approach, we observed distinct phase‐dependent changes in MT dynamics in tau mutant organoids. In 1‐month P301S organoids, MT mass (βIII‐tubulin) increased by 43.95%, accompanied by a 41.34% increase in tyrosinated tubulin and a 32.68% decrease in detyrosinated tubulin compared to controls. These findings indicate a shift toward more labile MTs at early phases. In contrast, 8‐month P301S organoids exhibited an opposite profile, with a 16.72% decrease in βIII‐tubulin, a 62.09% reduction in tyrosinated tubulin, and an 84.16% increase in detyrosinated tubulin (Figure 3A–D), consistent with a transition toward more stable MTs at later phases, even with a significant loss of total MT mass. A similar biphasic pattern was observed in P301L organoids. At 1 month, P301L organoids showed a 38.31% increase in βIII‐tubulin and an 11.87% decrease in detyrosinated tubulin, consistent with increased MT lability. However, at 8 months, P301L organoids exhibited the opposite trend, with βIII‐tubulin decreasing by 22.72% and detyrosinated tubulin increasing by 20.71% (Figure 3E–G). Together, these results suggest a biphasic shift in MT dynamics, characterized by early instability followed by later stabilization in tau mutant organoids.

To validate our findings in a clinical context, we analyzed MT dynamics in postmortem brain tissue from bvFTD patients carrying P301L mutation and healthy controls. This investigation aimed to characterize potential alterations in MT composition, dynamics, and stability in bvFTD. In bvFTD patient samples, we observed a substantial 43.09% decrease in βIII‐tubulin levels, indicating a reduction in overall MT mass. Concurrently, there was a marked increase in PTM tubulins associated with MT stability. Specifically, acetylated tubulin levels were elevated by 31.98%, while detyrosinated tubulin showed a 29.3% increase compared to control brain tissue (Figure 3H–L). These findings mirror the MT hyperstabilization phenotype observed in 8‐month organoids, supporting the relevance of our model to patient pathology.

To more directly assess microtubule dynamicity, we next employed nocodazole‐induced depolymerization, a well‐established approach for functionally probing microtubule stability and turnover, as previously established in our work. 21 Nocodazole binds to β‐tubulin and induces MT disassembly, and the rate of depolymerization can be used as an indicator of MT dynamics across distinct domains. 40 Our established studies showed that during the first 15 min of nocodazole treatment, the most labile MT domains underwent rapid depolymerization, while more stable MT domains depolymerize more slowly, resulting in a biphasic decline. 38 , 41 The steepness of the depolymerization slope reflects MT dynamics. 5 , 38 Using this method, we evaluated MT dynamics in 1‐month P301S organoids exposed to nocodazole for 15, 60, and 120 min, with DMSO‐treated organoids as controls. Axonal MTs were visualized by βIII‐tubulin staining as a marker of MT levels (Figure 4A–H, Table S5). Analysis revealed clear segregation between labile and stable MT domains following 60‐min nocodazole treatment. In control organoids, MT mass distribution was nearly balanced, with 46.92% labile and 53.08% stable domains. In contrast, 1‐month P301S organoids showed a marked shift toward lability, with 73.81% labile and 26.19% stable MT mass. Notably, the P301S mutant showed a rapid decline within the first 15 min, suggesting increased lability of the MT labile domain relative to control. These findings indicate enhanced sensitivity to nocodazole in P301S organoids, supporting increased MT lability in the tau mutant organoids.

Age‐dependent tau pathologies in P301S organoids

Tau is a soluble protein that remains unfolded under normal physical conditions. However, in tauopathy, there is a characteristic deposition and aggregation of toxic tau species, predominantly insoluble in the cytosol. This insolubility serves as a hallmark in both clinical diagnosis and postmortem examinations of tauopathy. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 To investigate the soluble and insoluble components of tau in our organoids, we isolated the respective protein fractions and analyzed them using Tau13. Our data suggest that the P301S mutation leads to an increase in insoluble tau by 32.86% and a decrease in soluble tau by 26.26% in 8‐month organoids (Figure 5A,B), indicating an escalation in tau pathology. Interestingly, comparison of bvFTD brain samples relative to control samples revealed a significant 43.76% decrease in soluble tau and an 81.85% increase in insoluble tau in bvFTD postmortem brains carrying the P301L mutation (Figure 5C,D), consistent with other studies. 46 Taken together with the concurrent increase in MAP6, our data reveal a unique interaction between MAP6 and “functional” tau 47 – seen as an increase in MAP6 with a decrease in soluble tau.

It is well established that toxic tau species arise from various abnormal PTM, including hyperphosphorylation, acetylation, and glycosylation, along with conformational changes such as truncation, misfolding, and aggregation of tau (e.g., oligomeric tau). These contribute to the hallmark features of tauopathies, such as tau aggregates, including paired helical filaments (PHFs) and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). To assess potential tau pathology in our organoids, we first examined tau hyperphosphorylation, a common marker of tau pathology, using a WB with the AT8 antibody, which specifically recognizes phosphorylated tau at serine 202 and threonine 205 and commonly observed in various tauopathies including AD and FTD. 48 Our data showed that control organoids maintained consistently low levels of AT8 at different ages. There were no significant changes in AT8 levels between control and P301S organoids at 1 month. By 4 months, however, the P301S organoids showed an increase in AT8 levels by 207.11% compared to controls. More importantly, AT8 accumulation increased by 473.85% in 8‐month P301S organoids, indicating progressive tau pathology over time (Figure 5E,F). We also employed immunostaining targeting AT8, tau oligomerization via TOC1, and conformational changes in tau using TNT2. TOC1 identifies oligomeric tau, which has been implicated as particularly toxic in disease progression. 49 TNT2 detects tau aggregation in a conformation‐dependent manner, especially in early pre‐tangle NFTs. 50 Our results reveal a significant increase of 40.58% in AT8 tau, 225.35% in TOC1 tau, and 60.50% in TNT2 tau in 8‐month P301S organoids compared to controls (Figure 5G–L). Note that since TOC1 and TNT2 are conformation‐dependent antibodies, they are not suitable for WB analysis. The observed pathology in the 8‐month organoids reflects various aspects of tau pathology, indicating their utility in modeling disease progression.

Defining neurodegenerative phenotype in P301S organoids

Given that our model has recapitulated toxic tau species, we next assessed neurodegenerative phenotypes to define disease progression and underlying pathology. We focused on SMI32, which detects dephosphorylated neurofilament and is used as a marker of neuronal degeneration. 51 , 52 , 53 Our results reveal no difference in SMI32 levels between 1‐month control and 1‐month P301S mutant organoids. However, at 4 months, SMI32 significantly increased by 208.9% in P301S mutant organoids compared to control organoids. This increase became even more pronounced in 8‐month P301S mutant organoids, with a 248.78% rise compared to 8‐month control organoids (Figure 6A,B). Immunostaining of SMI32 in 8‐month organoids further corroborated these findings, showing a striking 1030.83% increase in SMI32 levels in P301S organoids compared to controls (Figure 6C,D).

Having established axonal degeneration using SMI32, we next assessed dendritic integrity. MAP2 serves as a crucial somatodendritic MAP in neurons that interacts with MTs, promoting their polymerization and acting as a cross‐linker between MTs and actin filaments. Consistent with data from various neurological and neurodegenerative diseases showing MAP2 loss, we observed that MAP2 levels were profoundly reduced in 8‐month P301S mutant organoids, exhibiting a 96.11% decrease relative to controls (Figure 6C,E). This marked depletion indicates severe dendritic disruption and supports the idea that our organoid model recapitulates key aspects of tauopathy‐related neuronal dysfunction.

Neuronal activity dysregulation in 1‐month P301S organoids rescued with tau ASO

To assess whether the changes caused by the P301S mutation also modulated neuronal excitability, electrophysiological examinations were conducted on 1‐month organoids cultured on a multielectrode array platform (Figure 7A). We first confirmed the efficiency of tau ASO treatment in both control and P301S organoids. ASO treatment significantly reduced tau levels while increasing MAP6 and detyrosinated tubulin levels, consistent with our model (Figure 7B,C, Figure S2). Neuronal activity was recorded for 5 min using a multielectrode array system, and an example of a 5‐s recording was plotted with time on the x‐axis and voltage on the y‐axis (Figure 7D–F). Both individual spike and network burst parameters were characterized. The 1‐month P301S organoids exhibited increased neuronal activity compared to controls, demonstrating higher spike count, duration, frequency, and network burst activity, indicative of hyperactivity (Figure 7G–L). The enhanced neuronal activity observed correlates with elevated tau levels in 1‐month P301S organoids. Our findings demonstrate that during early tauopathy, elevated total tau levels promote MT lability while reducing stable MTs, ultimately contributing to neuronal hyperactivity. 6 , 54 To reverse these pathological changes, we employed ASOs targeting both wild‐type and mutant tau (pan‐tau ASO) in 1‐month tau mutant organoids. Notably, P301S organoids treated with anti‐tau ASO exhibited normalized network bursting activity, although individual spike frequencies remained comparable to untreated conditions (Figure 7F–L).

Reactive astrocytic response in 8‐month P301S organoids

Reactive astrocytes are implicated in contributing to neurodegeneration in tauopathies. 55 Our organoid system contained a robust astrocyte population, allowing us to assess glial responses under the mutant tau context. To investigate astrocyte reactivity in our organoid models, we targeted both GFAP and S100β, two well‐established astrocyte markers (Figure 8A–H). There was minimal presence of astrocytes in 1‐month organoids, and no changes were identified between the control and tau mutant organoids (data not shown). However, in 8‐month P301S mutant organoids, we observed a striking increase in GFAP by 445.73% compared to controls (Figure 8I). This substantial upregulation of GFAP strongly indicates the presence of reactive astrocytes in our tau mutant models. S100β, a marker of mature astrocytes, provides excellent visualization of astrocyte morphology, including detailed processes and soma. 53 Leveraging this, we conducted Sholl analysis to evaluate morphological changes in astrocytes expressing the P301S mutation. 56 Representative astrocytes used for this analysis are shown in Figure 8J. Our results revealed a significant increase in both the maximum number of intersections (by 4.57) and the ramification index (by 0.5) in 8‐month P301S organoids compared to controls (Figure 8K,L). These findings indicate that astrocytes in the P301S organoids became more hypertrophic and exhibited characteristic features of reactive astrocytes.

DISCUSSION

Our study supports a new conceptual framework for tauopathy in which MT dysfunction is not static, but biphasic and phase‐dependent. Rather than viewing tauopathy simply as a progressive MT deficit mediated by a loss of tau, our data indicate that mutant tau first drives an aberrantly heightened labile MT state and later transitions into a hyperstable state. This distinction helps reconcile long‐standing inconsistencies in the field, where tau has traditionally been considered a canonical MT stabilizer and tauopathy interpreted primarily as lattice destabilization. 5 , 6 , 9 Indeed, our prior work and that of others have suggested that tau and MAP6 act as opposing regulators of labile versus stable MT domains. 6 , 7 , 9 The present study extends that concept into a human disease context and across disease phases, identifying the inverse tau–MAP6 relationship as an attractive mechanistic axis for understanding these transitions. Early tauopathy appears to be characterized by relative tau dominance, in which excessive or aberrantly potentiated tau promotes expansion of the labile MT domain, likely at the expense of MAP6‐associated stabilization, resulting in increased MT dynamicity and heightened neuronal excitability even prior to overt neurodegeneration. In this study, MT dynamics were primarily assessed using tubulin PTM status as a proxy. Complementarily, nocodazole‐based assays provide a more direct evaluation of MT dynamics, and the results are consistent with those obtained from the PTM‐based analyses. This early state is not benign; hyperdynamic MTs may disrupt intracellular transport and lower the threshold for circuit‐level dysfunction, 57 , 58 , 59 as supported by the rescue of network bursting with tau ASO in 1‐month cortical organoids. As the disease progresses, however, this balance appears to invert, shifting toward a MAP6‐dominant state, likely as soluble tau becomes depleted into pathological assemblies. Interestingly, immunostaining with cell‐specific markers shows that the MAP6 increase in 8‐month organoids is neuronal, not glial. In this later phase, the accumulation of insoluble tau species, compensatory MAP6 upregulation, and reduced MT mass collectively drive the cytoskeleton into a hyperstabilized yet dysfunctional configuration, consistent with signatures observed in human bvFTD cortex (see schematic in Figure S3). This “yin–yang” relationship is conceptually useful because it shifts attention away from total tau alone and toward the balance between functionally available tau, MAP6, and the resulting architecture of the MT array. While this model is largely supported by protein levels, further studies incorporating direct measurements of occupancy by tau and MAP6 will be required to precisely define the nature of their competitive interaction. Thus, tauopathy does not follow a single linear trajectory of MT instability but instead crosses a biological threshold, transitioning from excessive MT dynamics to pathological hyperstability, the two mechanistically distinct yet equally detrimental states. This framework may also have broader relevance beyond FTD, particularly considering emerging human genetic evidence linking MAP6 variation to neurodegenerative risk. 60 , 61

We acknowledge that the 1‐ and 8‐month organoids differ substantially in cellular composition, maturation state, and tissue architecture, including increased astrocyte representation and overall tissue complexity at later stages. Future studies incorporating cell‐type‐specific analyses and longitudinal studies with more development time points will be important to further refine this model. However, our proposed mechanism has clear therapeutic implications. If MT dysfunction is phase‐dependent, then MT‐targeted interventions must be matched to disease phases. Strategies that reduce excessive MT dynamicity may be beneficial early, whereas such approaches are unlikely to be effective and may be counterproductive, once the system has shifted to a hyperstable state. This framework may help explain the inconsistent outcomes of broadly applied MT‐stabilizing therapies. 19 , 62 , 63 Our data instead suggests a phase‐specific approach: early intervention with tau‐lowering strategies, and later strategies aimed at restoring functional tau‐MT interactions, reducing MAP6, or correcting aberrant MT hyperstability rather than further stabilization.

Note that the late phase may also involve additional toxic actions derived from aggregated tau oligomers and fibrils, which extend beyond simple sequestration away from MT. One possibility is that the accumulation of insoluble or conformationally altered tau imposes a direct structural burden on the MT polymer itself. In particular, pathological tau species may aberrantly engage tau regions such as the proline‐rich and adjacent MT‐interacting elements in a manner that no longer supports normal labile‐domain organization but instead perturbs lattice architecture, motor access, or even MT polarity. This possibility is especially intriguing in light of emerging evidence that polarity defects themselves can be a treatable driver of neurodegeneration. Although our study was not designed to directly assess MT polarity, hyperphosphorylated tau aggregates might aberrantly activate specific kinases that influence molecular motors responsible for maintaining axonal MT polarity. 59 , 64 This raises the possibility that late‐phase tau pathology disrupts not only MT dynamicity but also higher‐order MT organization.

Interestingly, our astrocyte findings, though limited, extend these observations beyond neuronal MT biology. The emergence of reactive astrocytes in tau‐mutant organoids suggests that tauopathy is not purely a neuron‐autonomous cytoskeletal disorder but instead evolves into a multicellular pathological state in which neuronal tau pathology, MT dysfunction, and glial reactivity reinforce one another. This is particularly relevant given that astrocytes may actively sustain, rather than simply respond to, the degenerative environment. The robust astrocyte differentiation in this organoid platform therefore enhances its translational relevance, especially for evaluating therapies that target non‐cell‐autonomous mechanisms.

Our findings establish patient‐derived cortical organoids as a uniquely tractable human platform for modeling tauopathy and interrogating its temporal evolution. This system captures mutation‐specific and shared features across genetically defined backgrounds, supports maturation into disease‐relevant phases, and recapitulates key biochemical and MT signatures observed in patient tissue. Critically, it enables phase‐resolved analysis of disease progression, an aspect that is inherently limited in static human postmortem tissue and insufficiently captured in animal models that lack key human‐specific features of tau pathology and MT regulation. By preserving the human cellular context while allowing longitudinal interrogation, this platform reveals that tauopathy does not operate under a single biological regime but instead transitions between distinct MT states.
