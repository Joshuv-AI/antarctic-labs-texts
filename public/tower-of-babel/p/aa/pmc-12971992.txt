Adapting a culturally grounded diabetes prevention program for Latino families in the Phoenix, Arizona metropolitan area – A mixed methods approach
Authors: Monica Diaz, Elvia Madrid, Mariana Rodriguez, Allison Nagle Williams, Armando Peña, Meg Bruening, Gabriel Q. Shaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Latino families experience a disproportionate burden of type 2 diabetes, yet diabetes prevention programs (DPPs) are rarely adapted for family-based delivery, despite the role of familismo in supporting behavior change. This study describes the adaptation of a DPP for Latino families in Phoenix, Arizona.Methods: Guided by community-based participatory research (CBPR), an academic-community partnership adapted a youth DPP into the Family Diabetes Prevention Program, positioning the family as the unit of change. Adaptations were identified across refinement phases using qualitative and programmatic data sources (e.g., curriculum) collected between 2020 and 2023. Content analysis applied the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Enhanced, expanded to capture adaptation leadership, timing, and rationale.Results: Sixty-six adaptations were identified, primarily during grant preparation and post-pilot refinement. Most were co-led by academic and community partners (62.1%). Content adaptations predominated (76.4%), with additional modifications to evaluation, context, and training. Adaptations were driven by organizational constraints and stakeholder feedback and included integrating family processes, adding home visits, and revising session structure.Conclusions: This study systematically documents the adaptation of a DPP for Latino families using a CBPR approach. Findings offer a culturally grounded yet transferable model for diabetes prevention across diverse populations and settings.

Background

Latino children, adults, and communities experience a disproportionate burden of type 2 diabetes (T2D) (Aguayo-Mazzucato et al., 2019). Latino adults have higher prevalence and incidence of T2D than non-Hispanic White populations (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. National Diabetes Statistics Report. Accessed October 17, 2025). This burden extends to youth, with Latino youth exhibiting higher rates of obesity, insulin resistance, and progression to early-onset T2D (Vidmar et al., 2023). Diabetes risk clusters within families due to shared genetic, behavioral, and environmental factors, highlighting the need for prevention strategies that extend to the family system (Aguayo-Mazzucato et al., 2019).

Familismo, a core Latino cultural value, emphasizes family interdependence, shared responsibility, mutual support, and plays a central role in shaping health behaviors (Katiria Perez and Cruess, 2014). Prior research has linked familismo to diabetes self-care, family organization, and collective problem-solving, suggesting that family dynamics represent an important focus for intervention in chronic disease prevention (Fisher et al., 2000). However, most diabetes prevention interventions have focused on individual behavior change, with limited attention to family-level prevention (Andreae et al., 2024).

The Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) established the efficacy of lifestyle intervention for preventing or delaying T2D in adults with prediabetes (Knowler et al., 2002). The lifestyle intervention aimed at achieving and maintaining at least 7% weight loss through reduced caloric intake and increasing moderate-intensity physical activity (Knowler et al., 2002). Since the DPP, numerous adaptations have sought to improve reach, feasibility, and cultural relevance for diverse populations (Hingle et al., 2019; Lindberg et al., 2012; Rosas et al., 2018). In Latino communities, culturally tailored adaptations of the DPP have primarily emphasized individual-level cultural tailoring and have demonstrated feasibility and modest improvements in weight and glycemic outcomes (Lindberg et al., 2012; Rosas et al., 2018). Efforts have often relied on community-academic partnerships to support implementation and address structural barriers to participation (McCurley et al., 2017a). However, despite growing recognition of the family's role in shaping diabetes risk and prevention behaviors, no DPPs have reported a systematic approach for adapting interventions to the Latino family context using a standardized framework (Andreae et al., 2024).

Community-based participatory research (CBPR) emphasizes equitable partnerships between researchers and community stakeholders and enhances cultural relevance and feasibility by aligning intervention design with community priorities (Collins et al., 2018). While family-focused lifestyle interventions have demonstrated efficacy for addressing weight management in children with obesity (Skelton et al., 2012), family-based DPPs remain scarce, and no studies have comprehensively documented family-level DPP adaptations.

To address these gaps, we partnered with a community-based organization using CBPR to adapt Every Little Step Counts (ELSC) diabetes prevention intervention (Peña et al., 2022) into the Family Diabetes Prevention Program (FDPP). This process was guided by a theory-informed synthesis of family-based intervention literature to support model development and decision-making. Systematic documentation of intervention adaptation is critical for transparency, replication, and implementation, yet many culturally adapted DPPs lack standardized reporting of how, when, and why modifications occur (Andreae et al., 2024). The Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications–Enhanced (FRAME) (Wiltsey Stirman et al., 2019) provides a structured approach to characterize adaptations across phases and decision-makers but has rarely been applied in community-engaged, family-based prevention contexts. The purpose of this study was to systematically describe and classify adaptations made during the development of the FDPP using FRAME across multiple phases, and to extend the framework to better capture adaptation processes relevant to CBPR and family-centered interventions.

Methods

Guided by CPBR, the ELSC was adapted for Latino families through a multi-phase process conducted between 2020 and 2023, spanning grant preparation through randomized trial implementation. This work engaged academic and community partners to ensure cultural relevance and contextual fit and was grounded in a long-standing academic–community partnership with nearly two decades of collaborative intervention development, implementation, and evaluation. Although initiated during the COVID-19 pandemic, the extended and iterative nature of the adaptation process ensured that intervention components reflected current community needs, delivery contexts, and implementation realities, supporting continued relevance to contemporary diabetes prevention and public health practice. The adaptation team comprised of academic and community partners with involvement in the original intervention and strong ties to the local Latino community. Community partners were engaged across all phases of adaptation, contributing to priority setting, curriculum feedback, and shared decision-making related to cultural relevance, feasibility, and implementation.

Team members were selected based on lived or practice-based experience working with Latino families, and long-term engagement in the academic-community partnership. Selecting the intervention

Given the team's experience in designing and implementing lifestyle interventions for Latino youth in the Phoenix, AZ metropolitan area, the most recent version of ELSC was selected to support improvements in glucose tolerance and quality of life among Latino youth with obesity and prediabetes (Peña et al., 2022; Soltero et al., 2019).

Deciding what needs adaptation: I

Following intervention selection, the team identified components requiring adaptation by drawing on its experience working with Latino families, knowledge of the theoretical constructs underlying effective family-based interventions (Bamberger et al., 2014; Dietz et al., 2015; Kulis et al., 2016; Walsh, 2016), and the original curriculum's core tenets (Table 3). During the grant development phase, weekly meetings were held to review and document proposed modifications to content, context, evaluation, and implementation.

Literature review

To inform adaptation decisions and ensure theoretical alignment, a narrative, theory-informed literature review was conducted using PubMed to support development of a Family Diabetes Prevention (FDP) Model. The review included publications spanning 1987 to 2020 and encompassed both theoretical frameworks and empirical studies relevant to family-based interventions. Rather than restricting the review to specific health behaviors or outcomes, we examined family processes shown to influence health behaviors and health outcomes across intervention contexts.

From this review, four core family processes: engagement (Bamberger et al., 2014), empowerment (Dietz et al., 2015), cohesion (Kulis et al., 2016), and resilience (Walsh, 2016) were identified as relevant to diabetes prevention in Latino families (Table 2). These processes form the foundation of the FDP model (Braxton et al., 2023) by emphasizing skill development (e.g., communication, role modeling, goal setting, problem solving, self-management, self-monitoring) that support health behaviors (e.g., physical activity and eating) to improve T2D-related outcomes. Adapting the curriculum

Guided by the FDP Model, the team reviewed the ELSC curriculum manual, previously implemented from May 2016 to March 2020 (Peña et al., 2022; Soltero et al., 2019), and adapted sessions, objectives, and activities to improve alignment with the model.

Interviewing the community

Qualitative interviews were conducted with participants from the prior intervention to better understand how the family processes could be integrated into the adapted curriculum. Informed by the literature review, interviews explored how family-level processes could be operationalized within diabetes prevention. The interview guide (Supplementary Material 1) consisted of four sections corresponding to the family processes and was designed with community partners to identify how each process could be enacted within the family to support healthy behaviors.

Families who had participated in the intervention arm of the prior study focused on Latino youth with obesity and prediabetes (Peña et al., 2022) were eligible for recruitment. Using a non-probabilistic convenience sampling approach, 15 families agreed to participate. To capture collective family-level perspectives, entire households, including children, parents, grandparents, and other caregivers, were invited to participate.

Interviews were conducted virtually by trained bilingual and bicultural research coordinators in English or Spanish based on participant preference and were completed within 60 min. Families who participated received a stipend of $50.

Eligibility criteria included participation in the lifestyle arm of the previous intervention (Peña et al., 2022). Participants reflected the local Latino community served by referral sources. While families represented diverse lived experiences within the Latino community, the study was not designed to ensure representation across Latino subgroups.

This study was approved by the Arizona State University (ASU), Institutional Review Board (IRB ID: STUDY00003735) and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. All interviews were digitally recorded and uploaded to a professional transcription service (GMR) through an encrypted web portal. Transcripts were prepared verbatim and formatted for analyses via NVivo 12. Coding was conducted by two researchers. A hierarchical coding structure was developed that consisted of parent nodes representing each family process, child nodes reflecting theoretically informed subcategories and emergent themes, and grandchild nodes capturing specific response phrases identified through an inductive coding approach. In vivo coding was used to tag participant responses using family identification numbers.

Following coding, transcripts were reviewed and reconciled by both coders to produce a finalized coding set. Codes were summarized as percentages and displayed in tables. Family process themes endorsed by more than 50% of families were presented to the adaptation team to inform the next phase of adaptation.

Deciding what needs adaptation: II

Findings from the interviews informed the selection of activities used to strengthen engagement, empowerment, resilience, and cohesion within intervention. Guided by this data, the team reviewed the curriculum to identify components requiring modification and to incorporate new activities supporting the family system. Adaptations were tracked using Excel.

Adapting the curriculum: I

After identifying components requiring adaptation, new sessions, objectives, and activities were incorporated. Adaptations included the addition of a home visit to increase family engagement and address program-related questions. Dietary content was updated to reflect the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2020) and American Diabetes Association Standards of Care (American Diabetes Association, 2021). Activities were refined and facilitator scripts were developed to enhance family engagement while maintaining alignment with the original intervention.

FDPP pilot study

Following adaptation, the FDPP curriculum was piloted to assess feasibility and acceptability. Participants included Latino families who had participated in prior versions of the intervention (Peña et al., 2022; Soltero et al., 2019). The study protocol and study-related materials were approved by the ASU IRB (IRB ID: STUDY00014551). Written informed consent was obtained from participants and data collection and management followed confidentiality in accordance with IRB principles.

A non-probabilistic convenience sample of families from a prior intervention cohort was recruited. Six families participated in the feasibility study, a sample size that reflects the typical enrollment of a single intervention class. Eligibility criteria mirrored those of the prior intervention (Soltero et al., 2019) and included Latino youth aged 12 to 16 years with overweight or obesity without diabetes. While families represented diverse lived experiences within the Latino community, the study was not designed to ensure representation across specific Latino subgroups.

Eligible families consisted of children, parents or guardians, and additional household members. Parents or guardians were eligible if they were the legal guardian of an enrolled child. Additional household members were eligible if they were siblings or adults aged ≥18 years old residing in the household. Adults were excluded from physical activity components if they were unable to safely participate based on the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (Schwartz et al., 2019).

Feasibility and acceptability were assessed using brief paper-based surveys completed after each class by participants, facilitators, and independent observers. Participant surveys were available in English and Spanish and assessed acceptability, engagement, perceived relevance, clarity of content, and class pacing using a combination of rating scales and open-ended items. Facilitator and observer surveys assessed delivery feasibility, family participation, facilitator preparedness, cultural appropriateness, session flow, and included both quantitative items and qualitative prompts.

Protocol

Prior to group sessions, home visits were conducted with families. Home visits were used to establish rapport, provide an overview of the program, review class schedule, distribute paper-based materials, and address participant questions. Following the home visits, families attended group sessions held at a local YMCA, a community-based nonprofit organization that offers health promotion, physical activity, and wellness programs.

Group sessions were delivered weekly over four weeks to groups of four to six families by bilingual, bicultural dietitians. Each session consisted of 1-h nutrition and wellness education, followed by a 1-h physical activity session delivered by YMCA personal trainers. Participating families received $50 per session.

Data collection

Evaluation data were collected at the conclusion of each home visit and group session using evaluation forms completed by participants, facilitators, and observers. Participant evaluation forms assessed satisfaction, perceived relevance, clarity of content, cultural appropriateness, family engagement, and perceived usefulness of the nutrition, wellness, and physical activity components. Items were primarily close-ended, including Likert-scale and yes or no responses, with space for open-ended comments. All forms were available in English and Spanish and completed in paper format to ensure accessibility.

Facilitator and observer evaluation forms were used to assess implementation fidelity, participant engagement, clarity of instruction, and session flow. These forms included structured checklists and brief open-ended fields to document observations and implementation considerations. Together, participant, facilitator, and observer data provided complementary perspectives on feasibility, acceptability, and implementation.

Following completion of the pilot intervention, focus groups were conducted to explore participant experiences. Focus groups were facilitated using a semi-structured guide developed by academic and community partners and included five open-ended questions organized around program experiences, family involvement, cultural relevance, and barriers to participation.

Focus groups were conducted at the YMCA, lasted 45 to 60 min, and were facilitated by trained bilingual, bicultural researchers. Individuals who participated received an additional $50. Focus groups were offered in English for youth participants and Spanish for parents/guardians. All discussions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

All focus group recordings were uploaded to a professional transcription service (GMR) through an encrypted web portal. Spanish-language transcripts were translated into English to facilitate coding and analysis, consistent with procedures described in the Interviewing the Community phase.

Finalizing the curriculum

Finalization of the curriculum occurred during the phases Deciding What Needs Adaptation: III and Adapting the Curriculum: II. During these phases, survey and focus group data from the pilot study were used to inform refinements to the intervention. Proposed modifications were reviewed and discussed during weekly meetings.

Community partners assessed the feasibility of proposed refinements based on staff capacity and available resources, informing decisions about which modifications could be implemented. All family-facing materials were revised to achieve a reading level ≤ 6th grade using the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level formula.

Topics covered in the finalized nutrition and wellness education curriculum are described in detail in the published study protocol paper (Braxton et al., 2023).

Expanded FRAME (Wiltsey Stirman et al., 2019) for Adaptation Coding

Adaptations data analysis

Content analysis

To document adaptations, a trained researcher conducted content analysis using multiple data sources, including meeting minutes, grant application materials, community interview data, pilot study data, adaptation tracking sheets, and versions of the adapted curriculum.

Defining adaptations

Only adaptations that occurred during the completed phases of the study were included in the content analysis (Fig. 1). To capture adaptations made to the curriculum content, adaptations were defined as follows: (1) modifications to session's objectives, coded as a single adaptation; (2) modifications to session structure; (3) removal or addition of sessions; and (4) modifications to materials and activities. Only adaptations incorporated into the final version of the curriculum were included in the analysis.

In addition to curriculum content, adaptations related to context, training, evaluation, implementation and scale-up activities were coded. Given the level of analysis and the iterative nature of adaptation, a single adaptation could be coded across multiple phases, particularly when changes to session objectives or instructional materials occurred in more than one phase. Similarly, individual adaptations could receive multiple codes across FRAME categories (Wiltsey Stirman et al., 2019).

Data triangulation

Following coding, adaptations were triangulated across stakeholders through consensus meetings. Consensus meetings included a review of the adaptation methodology, preliminary coding results, and cross-checking of classification, with reclassification as needed for consensus.

Quantitative analysis

All data were entered in duplicate into a database and analyzed via SPSS version 28. Variables were determined from FRAME categories and levels (Wiltsey Stirman et al., 2019). Descriptive statistics were used to examine the frequency and proportion of adaptations across FRAME categories and levels.

Results

Sixty-six adaptations were identified during the development of the FDPP. Most adaptations occurred during grant preparation (n = 24) and post-pilot refinement (n = 31), reflecting the iterative and community-engaged nature of the adaptation process. Over half of all adaptations (62.1%) were co-led by academic and community partners, indicating shared leadership in decision-making. Modifications to intervention content were most common (76.4%), followed by adaptations related to evaluation, context, and training. Organizational constraints and stakeholder feedback emerged as the primary drivers of adaptation.

Table 4 summarizes the adaptations by type, timing, leadership, and rationale. Examples of content adaptations included guidance on navigating healthcare visits, increased emphasis on use of community-based physical activity resources, and enhanced coaching support through instructor training, behavioral health training, and fidelity monitoring. Additional adaptations involved modifying eligibility criteria, adding a home visit, restructuring session objectives and sequencing, and embedding the FDP processes into curriculum content and study design. A complete list of all 66 adaptations, coded using the FRAME and examples of feedback informing each modification, is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Discussion

Despite strong evidence supporting the efficacy of the DPP in adults (Knowler et al., 2002) and family-based interventions for pediatric obesity (Skelton et al., 2012; Peña et al., 2022; Soltero et al., 2019; Shaibi et al., 2012), few DPPs have been systematically adapted for delivery at the family level, particularly for Latino populations. This gap is notable given both the disproportionate burden of T2D among Latino families (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. National Diabetes Statistics Report. Accessed October 17, 2025; Vidmar et al., 2023) and the central role familismo (Corona et al., 2017) plays in positioning the family unit for health promotion. This study addresses this gap by rigorously documenting the adaptation of a youth-focused DPP into a family-based intervention for Latino families. Beyond advancing health disparities research, this work makes three primary contributions: (1) operationalizing the family system as the unit of change in diabetes prevention, (2) extending the FRAME framework to more fully capture adaptation leadership, timing, and rationale, and (3) demonstrating how CBPR-guided processes shape adaptation decisions across the intervention lifecycle (Escoffery et al., 2019; Chambers, 2023).

A recent systematic review identified only four family-based DPPs, underscoring the limited evidence base and wide variability in how families were engaged (McCurley et al., 2017b). None fully described their adaptation processes for family contexts. In contrast, the FDPP positions the family system as the unit of change, integrates culturally relevant family processes through the FDP Model, and systematically documents adaptations using FRAME. This approach extends prior family-informed DPP efforts and highlights the importance of culturally grounded, family-level adaptations in Latino communities.

Modifications to the FRAME (Wiltsey Stirman et al., 2019) were necessary to capture key dimensions of the adaptation process, including who led adaptations, when they occurred, and the rationale for each change. These elements are particularly important in CBPR-guided interventions. Most adaptations were driven by integration of interventionist and community partner feedback, reflecting shared decision-making and aligning with prior research supporting stakeholder-engaged adaptation approaches (Kwon et al., 2018).

Although relatively few adaptations were attributed solely to recipient feedback, this pattern likely reflects structural and feasibility constraints during early-stage intervention development and the continued influence of intervention developers in final decision-making, as documented in prior studies (Kwon et al., 2018). Documenting adaptation leadership provides critical visibility into power dynamics and offers a mechanism for strengthening community influence and equity in adaptation decisions.

Adaptations occurred most frequently during grant preparation and post-pilot refinement, consistent with adaptation science emphasizing iterative development and stakeholder engagement (Kwon et al., 2018). Tracking adaptation timing highlighted when community input was most influential and when opportunities for modification narrowed, underscoring the value of systematic documentation across intervention phases.

Although tracking adaptations is time- and resource-intensive (Holtrop et al., 2022) it provides essential infrastructure for understanding how modifications relate to implementation and outcomes. By documenting adaptations across multiple levels, from macro-level model integration to micro-level curricular refinements, this study contributes a transparent and replicable approach that may help standardize adaptation reporting while reducing burden on implementers.

Finally, adaptation efforts must balance responsiveness with restraint. Excessive pre-implementation adaptation may be unnecessary when contextual similarities outweigh differences (Evans et al., 2021), and may delay dissemination and scale-up. Supporting and documenting implementer-led adaptations during delivery may represent a more efficient and equitable strategy for advancing evidence-based interventions across diverse settings (Chambers, 2023).

Although the FDPP is culturally grounded in Latino values and contexts, the adaptation approach is broadly transferable to other populations and settings where sociocultural factors and family dynamics influence health behaviors. This approach can guide diabetes prevention efforts in other communities by supporting the identification of locally relevant family processes and promoting transparent, replicable, and scalable adaptation practices.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the first to document adaptations to a family-based DPP using a rigorous, CBPR-guided approach. Latino families in the Phoenix metropolitan area were prioritized due to their high risk for T2D and the presence of a longstanding community-academic partnerships. While this contextual focus may limit generalizability, the adaptation framework and processes can inform similar efforts in other populations and settings.

Adaptations were coded retrospectively using the FRAME framework; but validated through triangulation with community partners to strengthen rigor. While adaptation timing thresholds were not formally analyzed and interrater reliability was not formally quantified through kappa coefficients, decisions were guided by community expertise in curriculum development, consistent with CBPR principles. Additionally, comprehensive descriptive statistics were not available for the full sample, as completion of the demographic survey was optional. As a result, sample representativeness cannot be fully assessed.

Conclusion

This study is among the first to systematically adapt a DPP for Latino families, addressing a major gap in family-centered diabetes prevention. We identified 66 adaptations and extended the FRAME framework to capture who led, when they occurred, and why they were made. This process preserved the core elements of the ELSC while integrating family dynamics to enhance relevance and feasibility. Our findings advance adaptation science and health equity by demonstrating how systematic documentation can improve transparency, replication, and scalability of evidence-based interventions, supporting sustainable, community-driven solutions.
