Successful endovascular therapy with “Direct tip injection in occlusive lesions (DIOL)” fashion for persistent sciatic artery aneurysmal occlusion: A case report
Authors: Masanaga Tsujimoto, Takuya Haraguchi, Yoshifumi Kashima, Katsuhiko Sato, Tsutomu Fujita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent sciatic artery (PSA) is a rare congenital anomaly that may involve aneurysmal formations. During endovascular treatment for PSA aneurysm (PSAA) occlusion, guidewire crossing can be challenging due to complex anatomy. We report successful endovascular intervention for PSAA occlusion using the “direct tip injection in occlusive lesions (DIOL)” fashion, in which hydraulic pressure with contrast facilitates guidewire crossing by visualizing the vessel course and expanding the microchannel and vessel lumen. An 80-year-old woman presented with severe intermittent left calf claudication due to PSA occlusion with PSAA. Endovascular treatment was attempted for the occlusion; however, bidirectional wiring in the PSAA was unsuccessful. Therefore, bidirectional DIOL was applied; antegrade-DIOL visualized and enlarged the subintimal plane of the proximal PSAA, while retrograde-DIOL showed intramedial lumen enlargement in the middle and distal PSAA. Consequently, bidirectional guidewires were successfully advanced into the expanded lumen, achieving guidewire externalization. After balloon angioplasty, a self-expandable stent and three stent-grafts were implanted throughout the PSA occlusion, with stent-grafts overlapping around the hip joint to overcome chronic external compression. The final angiogram revealed sufficient blood flow, and the symptoms improved. A 2-year follow-up computed tomography angiography indicated no restenosis. Thus, the DIOL fashion facilitates guidewire crossing in complex occlusions.Learning objectiveThis case report highlights the successful guidewire crossing for the treatment of a persistent sciatic artery aneurysm (PSAA) occlusion, which can be challenging. The “direct tip injection in occlusive lesions” fashion, using hydraulic pressure with a contrast medium to facilitate guidewire crossing with visualization of the vessel course and expansion of the lesion lumen, is a valuable and safe method for guidewire crossing in PSAA occlusion.Keywords: Chronic total occlusion, Endovascular treatment, Persistent sciatic artery, Peripheral arterial disease, Stent-graft, Subintimal angioplasty

Introduction

Persistent sciatic artery (PSA) is a rare congenital anomaly of the blood circulation in the lower limb that results from the persistence of an artery that typically regresses within the first 3 months of embryonic development. It may manifest in claudication and is more prone to arteriosclerosis and vessel wall degeneration and combined vascular complications: aneurysmal change with or without thromboembolism development due to sacrospinous ligament and hip joint compression. PSA is classified according to the anomaly of the anatomical connections to the iliac, superficial femoral, and popliteal arteries, as well as the presence or absence of a PSA aneurysm (PSAA) [1].

For symptomatic PSA occlusion, endovascular treatment offers an alternative therapy that has a lower risk of perioperative complications despite surgical intervention being a well-established treatment [2]. However, the difficulties in achieving guidewire crossing for PSA occlusion stem from anatomical defects that impede comprehension of the vessel's trajectory. Furthermore, if PSA occlusion is accompanied by a PSAA, a hard intraluminal thrombus inside the aneurysm may prevent the guidewire from crossing. The “direct tip injection in occlusive lesions (DIOL)” fashion uses hydraulic pressure and contrast agent to facilitate safe and easy guidewire passage through occlusive lesions. This approach visualizes the vessel pathway, enlarges microchannels, and expands the vessel lumen [3]. This report presents a case of successful endovascular treatment of a PSAA occlusion using the DIOL fashion.

Case report

An 80-year-old woman presented with severe intermittent claudication in her left calf and an ankle-brachial index of 0.74. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) and control angiography revealed a left PSA occlusion extending from the left internal iliac artery to the left popliteal artery and a hypoplastic left superficial femoral artery (Fig. 1A, B) and a 21 × 19 mm aneurysm in the middle of the PSA behind the hip joint (Fig. 1C, D). According to the Ahn–Min classification, these lesions were diagnosed as PSA type IIIa [1]. Endovascular treatment was performed based on the patient's frailty status instead of surgical treatment.

A 6-Fr guiding sheath (Destination; Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and 6-Fr sheath (Terumo Corporation) were inserted through the right common femoral and left posterior tibial artery, respectively; 5000-IU unfractionated heparin was injected intra-arterially. The angiogram showed an unclear connection of the internal iliac artery (IIA) to the PSA (Fig. 1E). Therefore, a retrograde approach was initially performed instead of an antegrade approach. However, 0.035-inch guidewire (Radifocus; Terumo Corporation) using knuckle wire technique and 0.014-inch hardest guidewire (Jupiter MAX; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) could not be advanced from the middle of PSAA. To confirm the guidewire route, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) (Visions PV 0.018; Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) showed that the retrograde system was in the intramedial lumen (Fig. 2A). To enlarge the intramedial lumen of the occluded lesion further forward, retrograde-DIOL was performed with forceful injection of the 10 ml contrast agent through a 4-Fr catheter (Tempo; Cordis, Miami Lakes, FL, USA). Retrograde-DIOL could visualize the intramedial lumen enlargement from the middle of PSAA to the distal occlusion site, named river contrast staining, which spreads with varying contrast densities on angiography (Fig. 2B) [3]. Then, visualized lesion anatomy allowed the antegrade guidewire (Vassallo NS3; Cordis) to be advanced into the occlusion. Antegrade IVUS was then used to confirm the antegrade guidewire position. After IVUS revealed that the antegrade guidewire was in subintimal lumen (Fig. 2C), knuckle wire technique with 0.035-inch guidewire was attempted to be advanced into the PSAA. However, the hard thrombus in the PSAA prevented the antegrade wire crossing. Therefore, antegrade-DIOL was performed with gentle injection of the 5 ml contrast through a 4-Fr catheter, and this fashion could expand the subintimal lumen in the proximal PSAA with tubular contrast staining (Fig. 2D) [3]. Following this, both bidirectional 0.035-inch guidewires were advanced further through the enlarged lumen. The retrograde guidewire successfully crossed the lesion and was advanced into the antegrade guiding sheath for externalization. After dilating the entire lesion with a 6.0 mm balloon (SABERX, Cordis), we implanted two 6.0 × 100 mm stent-grafts (VIABAHN; W.L. Gore & Associates, Inc., Newark, DE, USA) with a 6 cm overlap from the proximal to middle PSA around the hip joint to maintain flexibility and reinforce radial force to prevent fractures. Then, a 6.0 × 250 mm stent-graft was placed in the infrainguinal segment of the PSA. To obviate the potential complications of left IIA occlusion, a 10.0 × 40 mm bare-nitinol stent (SMART, Cordis) was deployed from the distal IIA to the proximal PSA, rather than a stent-graft. Subsequently, post-dilatation was performed using the 6.0 mm balloon, and IVUS showed that the minimal lumen area at the stent-graft overlap site was 22.3 mm2 with symmetrical expansion (Fig. 2E). The final angiogram showed adequate blood flow (Fig. 2F).

(C) IVUS identification of the guidewire's position within the subintimal space at the proximal PSAA. (E) Final IVUS showing symmetric stent expansion with a minimal stent area of 22.3 mm2 at the stent-graft overlap site. (F) Final angiography demonstrating sufficient blood flow.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 2 with improved symptoms. A 2-year follow-up CTA confirmed no restenosis without stent fracture (Fig. 3A, B) and a reduction in the PSAA size from 21 × 19 mm to 17 × 16 mm without endoleaks (Fig. 3C). During the 4-year follow-up period, there was no recurrence of thromboembolism or complications, and the patient did not require any reintervention.

(C) The aneurysm shrank from 21 × 19 to 17 × 16 mm without endoleaks (yellow arrow).

Discussion

Endovascular treatment for PSA occlusion has been reported as an alternative to surgery, with benefits such as lower risk of complications, shorter duration of hospital stays, and reduction in overall hospitalization costs [2].

In endovascular treatment for cases with challenging guidewire passage, the hydrodynamic boost technique, which facilitates antegrade re-entry from the subintimal plane into the distal true lumen using hydraulic pressure with contrast at the distal part of the occlusions, is a viable option for revascularization [4]. However, if the antegrade system fails to advance to the distal occlusion site, this technique cannot be applied. On the other hand, the DIOL fashion, which focuses on supporting the device passage rather than re-entry, can be used from either an antegrade or retrograde approach and at any part of chronic total occlusion and to clarify the vascular course and device position within the vessel due to the enlargement of the lesion lumen using the contrast medium, allowing the operator to advance the guidewire with confidence due to visualization and facilitating the guidewire crossing in the complex lesion. Furthermore, in terms of the enlargement of the vessel lumen to pass the guidewire, the knuckle wire technique also expands the vessel lumen in the softer part of the lesion, but if the vessel lumen is smaller than the knuckle wire loop, the guidewire passage is challenging. However, the DIOL fashion using hydraulic pressure enlarges even the smallest microchannels and expands the softer areas within the lumen, regardless of the vessel size, creating the larger space and supporting guidewire passage in more complex situations. Consequently, in this case, the DIOL fashion led to successful guidewire crossing after the knuckle wire technique failed.

For PSA occlusions, the ultimate device choice and implantation techniques are crucial for preserving long-term patency. A previous systematic review showed that PSA treatment using stent-grafts resulted in no symptom recurrence during a mean follow-up of 14 months [2]. In contrast, another clinical report demonstrated that re-occlusion with a stent-graft fracture occurred 6 months after implantation because of chronic trauma from the surrounding structures during hip flexion [5]. Based on a report that overlapping self-expandable bare-nitinol stents increased their radial force [6], in this case, we used two stent-grafts with overlap around the hip joint to increase the resistance to chronic external compressions. When overlapping stent-grafts, although over 2 cm overlap it is generally recommended to avoid endoleaks and stent separation, to maintain flexibility and prevent stent fracture, we staggered the two short stent-grafts of the same size by 6 cm, more than half their length, as complete overlapping could compromise their flexibility. Consequently, continued patency was observed, and no stent fractures were observed within 2 years.

This case report had two limitations. First, the DIOL fashion cannot be used if no device advances to an occlusion. Second, the long-term durability and patency of stent-grafts for PSA occlusion remain uncertain because of the lack of data with a large sample size.

Conclusions

The DIOL fashion is valuable and safe to facilitate guidewire crossing in PSAA occlusion.
