Giant grouper nervous necrosis virus subgenomic RNA3 is transcribed by a premature termination mechanism via intragenomic RNA-RNA pairing
Authors: Chao-An Cheng, Chuan-Siang Lai, Fu-En Liao, Shu-Ling Yeh, Ming-Hsien Chiang, Chen-Hung Li, Kuei-Yuan Fang, Ying-Chen Liao, Hui-Chih Hsu, Vertika Bajpai, Chen-Kung Chi, Chien-Wen Chen, Hsia-Wei Liu, Chia-Ching Chang, Chi-Yao Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNervous necrosis virus (NNV) is a bipartite positive-sense RNA virus that causes viral nervous necrosis disease in larval and juvenile marine fish. In its genome, the RNA1 segment encodes RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) for viral RNA synthesis, while RNA2 encodes coat protein for viral particle assembly. In addition, an RNA1-derived subgenomic RNA3 region encodes the non-structural B2 protein, which antagonizes host RNA interference mechanisms. However, the mechanisms of RNA3 transcription remain unclear.MethodsA DNA-based reverse genetic platform vector (pCMV-DRz) for recombinant (r) giant grouper (GG) NNV recovery was constructed. End sequences of GGNNV RNAs were determined by 5’ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) and/or circular RNA sequencing approach. Replication and transcription of viral RNAs in GGNNV-infected or mutant clones transfected grouper brain (GB) cells were detected by Northern blotting using anti-sense RNA DIG-labeling probes. Recombinant viral proteins were detected by using Western blotting or immunocytochemistry staining after GB cells transfected with pCMV-DRz-RNA1 or infected with rGGNNV. In addition, the viral kinetics of rGGNNV was determined using TCID50 assay. Finally, the minimum free energy secondary RNA structures were predicted using RNA-Fold of Vienna RNA Websuite.ResultsIn the reverse genetic platform, high viral titer of rGGNNV (108 TCID50/mL) was readily obtained from culture medium of pCMV-DRz-RNA1 and pCMV-DRz-RNA2 co-transfected GB cells, and the resulting genomic sequences at the viral 5′- and 3′-ends were confirmed to be accurate. The pCMV-DRz-RNA1 autonomous replicon alone (encodes RdRp) was sufficient to replicate RNA1 and produce RNA3. Without altering the encoded RdRp amino acid sequence, we performed mutagenesis of the RNA1 sequence and found that RNA3 is produced by a premature termination mechanism that involves an 8-base paired helix formed by a long-distance intragenomic RNA-RNA interaction. Furthermore, transcriptional promoter elements near the RNA3 negative-strand 3′-end also contributed to RNA1 and RNA3 production, and the expression levels of RNA1 and RNA3 were modulated by RNA2 co-expression.ConclusionOur results suggest that NNV utilizes premature termination mechanism for subgenome RNA3 transcription and NNV RNAs expression is coordinately regulated by viral cis- and trans-RNA sequences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02797-5.

Introduction

RNA viruses cause many emerging and existing viral diseases, including coronavirus, influenza virus and flavivirus in humans, and nodavirus in insects and fish. The error-prone nature of RNA dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) confer high replication-associated mutation rates and also high replication yields. As a consequence, widespread infection during pandemic periods may cause RNA viruses to behave as quasi-species, enhancing viral genetic diversity and increasing the evolutionary in vivo fitness of the viruses. This highly mutagenic nature of RNA genomes renders the viruses difficult to manipulate for pathogenic analyses and prophylactic development. However, the advent of reverse genetic approaches has solved this problem for the most part, as viral RNAs can now be reverse transcribed into DNA for genetic engineering and mutagenesis. In 1978, the first infectious RNA phage was rescued from a plasmid containing a complete complementary DNA (cDNA) of the Qβ phage genome in Escherichia coli [1]. A few years later, an infectious RNA virus was reconstructed from a full-length poliovirus cDNA-containing plasmid in mammalian cells [2]. Since then, reverse genetics systems have been widely exploited to recover RNA viruses, mainly by two approaches. In the RNA-based approach, a full-length viral RNA genome is in vitro transcribed by a T7 RNA polymerase from a viral cDNA with post-transcriptional modifications. Transfection into host cells allows rescue of the RNA virus. In the DNA-based approach, viral cDNAs are constructed in a DNA-expressing vector and transfected into host cells to rescue RNA viruses [3]. Currently, both of these approaches typically utilize a bacterial phage T7 RNA polymerase and its promoter to recover the RNA genome. However, when using this method, the reverse-form DNA cannot be precisely transcribed back to RNA because T7 RNA polymerase preferentially transcribes RNA sequences starting with two guanine nucleotides [4]. For this reason, two extra guanine nucleotides are routinely added to start the viral RNA in order to increase transcription efficiency of the T7 RNA polymerase expression system. As a consequence, two cytosine nucleotides appear at the 3′-end of the complementary virus RNA strand, which influences the recognition and binding of viral RdRp. This effect on RdRp activity can delay RNA replication and impair multiplication of the recombinant RNA virus [5, 6].

Nervous necrosis virus (NNV) belongs to the Nodaviridae family and Betanodavirus genus, and infection with NNV is a common cause of viral nervous necrosis disease in larval and juvenile marine fish worldwide [7]. The NNV genome contains two positive-strand RNA segments. The 3.1-kb RNA1 segment encodes an RdRp for viral RNA synthesis, whereas the 1.4-kb RNA2 segment encodes a coat protein for viral particle assembly [8]. In addition, a 0.4-kb subgenomic RNA3 is derived from the 3′-end of RNA1 and encodes a B2 protein that serves to antagonize host RNA interference mechanisms [5, 9]. All NNV RNAs are capped at their 5′-ends and lack poly(A) tails at their 3′-ends [9, 10]. Based on nucleotide sequence similarity in the variable region of the RNA2 genome, betanodaviruses are classified into four genome types, including tiger puffer (TP)NNV, striped jack (SJ)NNV, barfin flounder (BF)NNV and red-spotted grouper (RG)NNV [11]. Using a reverse genetic approach with a T7 RNA polymerase in vitro RNA transcription system, the first SJNNV and sevenband grouper (SG)NNV (RGNNV genome type) reassortant viruses were generated, which allowed researchers to identify the host-specificity determinants, temperature sensitivities and antigenic diversity of betanodaviruses by transfecting host cells with capped NNV reassortant RNA1 and RNA2 in vitro transcripts [9, 12–15]. Using a similar method, mutated in vitro-transcribed autonomous NNV RNA1 replicon transcripts were generated and transfected into host cells to explore RNA3 and B2 protein expression [10]. Along the same lines, a stable T7 RNA polymerase-expressing mammalian cell line, BSR T7/5 cells, was used in combination with a DNA-based reverse genetic vector harboring T7 RNA polymerase promoter to generate recombinant NNV for studies on temperature adaptation, viral RNA replication, virulence and attenuated vaccine development [5, 16–21].

The eukaryotic 5′-end-dependent scanning translational initiation mechanism only allows the first open reading frame (ORF) to be translated efficiently. Therefore, positive-strand RNA viruses utilize 3′ co-terminal subgenomic mRNAs to achieve regulated production of viral proteins positioned downstream of the 5′-proximal ORF in their polycistronic genomes. There are three strategies that have been identified as regulators of subgenomic mRNA transcription in positive-strand RNA viruses: (i) internal initiation, transcribing subgenomic mRNA from a localized promoter within a negative-strand genomic template [22]; (ii) discontinuous transcription, producing a non-continuous negative-strand template to transcribe subgenomic mRNA [23]; and (iii) premature termination, generating a truncated negative-strand template for production of 3′ co-terminal subgenomic mRNA [24]. In the premature termination process, the 3′-truncated negative-strand subgenomic RNA is initially formed via premature termination of RdRp polymerization during negative-strand genome replication from a positive-sense genomic template. Subsequently, the negative-strand subgenomic RNA is used as template for replication. Attenuation signals for premature termination include several key structural components. For one, long-distance intragenomic RNA-RNA interactions may lead to base-pairing just upstream from the transcriptional initiation site. Additionally, RNA-RNA interactions in trans between genome segments or spacer sequences between a proximal RNA-RNA base-pairing site and transcriptional initiation site can induce premature termination. Moreover, downstream sequences containing transcriptional promoter elements also serve as attenuation signals [25, 26]. The premature termination has been studied previously in the Alphanodavirus Flock house virus (FHV) [27–29]. In plant virus, the Dianthovirus Red clover necrotic virus (RC-NMV) [30], the Tombusvirus tomato bushy stunt virus (TBSV) [31], the Aureusvirus Cucumber leaf spot virus (CLSV) [32, 33], the Carmovirus turnip crinkle virus (TCV) [34] and the Pelarspovirus, Pelargonium line pattern virus (PLPV) [35] were also found to have premature termination mechanism. Although it was previously suggested that the premature termination mechanism might govern betanodavirus subgenomic RNA3 transcription [10], the hypothesis has not yet been tested directly. In this study, we establish a DNA-based reverse genetic platform for the precise and rapid recovery of viral cDNA back to a complete RNA genome. The platform is based on a 5′ human cytomegalovirus (CMV) main immediate early gene promoter and a 3′ human hepatitis delta virus (HDV) antigenome ribozyme. Utilizing this platform, we demonstrate that the giant grouper (GG)NNV RNA3 subgenome is produced by a premature termination mechanism involving long-distance intragenomic RNA-RNA base-pairing.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

The GB cell line was established from brain tissue of an immature orange-spotted grouper (Epinephelus coioides) as described previously [36]. GB cells were cultured in Leibovitz’s L15 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), L-glutamine (Gibco), and penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 28 ℃. GGNNV of the RGNNV genome type was isolated from head tissue of diseased giant grouper, which was kindly provided by Shang-Hai Aquatic Animal Hospital in Pingtung, Taiwan in 2014 [37].

Viral titer determination

Viral susceptibility was measured by the 50% of tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) method [38]. Briefly, aliquots of 105 GB cells were seeded into each well of a 24-well plate and incubated at 28 ℃ until 80% confluence of the monolayer was achieved. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), cells in each well were inoculated with serial 10 × dilutions of GGNNV in L15-2% FBS medium. The cytopathic effect (CPE) was monitored daily for 7 days in infected cells using an inverted microscope, and the titer was calculated.

Antibodies

Mouse anti-HA tag monoclonal antibody and anti-beta-actin monoclonal antibody (66,009–1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech. Rabbit anti-Myc tag monoclonal antibody was purchased from Cell Signaling. Sheep anti-digoxigenin (DIG)-POD, Fab fragments and sheep anti-DIG-AP, Fab fragments were purchased from Roche. Goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure antibody, goat anti-mouse IgG + IgM (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 antibody and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 647 Fab fragments were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch.

Determination of GGNNV nucleotide sequences

To determine GGNNV genomic sequences, total RNA was extracted from GGNNV-infected GB cells at 20 hpi with TRIzol reagent (Geneaid) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Primer sets (Table 2) were designed to RT-PCR amplify full-length RNA1 and RNA2 DNA fragments. After amplification, the DNA fragments were cloned into pJET1.2 vector (Thermo Scientific) for nucleotide sequencing using an ABI 3730XL autosequencer (Applied Biosystems). To determine 5′- and 3′-end sequences of the GGNNV genomic RNAs, a circular RNA sequencing approach was used. Released viral particles were precipitated from culture medium of infected cells by 5% polyethylene glycol (PEG) in 2.2% NaCl at 10,000 × g for 1 h. Viral RNAs were extracted from the pellet by acid phenol/chloroform. After dissolving in nuclease-free water, viral RNA was treated with tobacco acid pyrophosphatase (TAP) at 37 ℃ for 1 h to remove the cap structure from full-length RNA, and the resultant RNA was circularized with T4 RNA ligase at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Primer sets (Table 2) were designed to RT-PCR amplify the 5′ and 3′ junction region of viral RNAs. After checking the PCR product on an agarose gel, the DNA fragment was cloned into pJET1.2 vector for sequencing. To detect the viral dimer junction sequences, total RNAs were prepared from 20 hpi GGNNV-infected GB cells, following RT-PCR amplification with different primer sets (Table 2). The resultant RT-PCR-amplified DNA fragments were cloned and sequenced. To detect the 5′-end of GGNNV subgenomic RNA3, 5′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) was performed using a FirstChoice RLM-RACE kit (Applied Biosystems, AB(Design), Ambion) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, total RNA was treated with calf intestine alkaline phosphatase (CIP) at 37 ℃ for 1 h to remove 5′-phosphates from RNAs. After acid phenol/chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation, the RNA pellet was rinsed with 70% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. The resultant RNA was treated with TAP at 37 ℃ for 1 h to remove the cap structure and then ligated with the 5′ RACE adapter. After reverse transcription, RNA3 RACE primers for nested PCR (Table 2) were used to amplify the 5′ RACE DNA fragment. The resultant DNA fragment was cloned and sequenced. Accession numbers for GGNNV RNA1 and RNA2 are OQ139632 and OQ139633, respectively.

Plasmid construction

To transcribe GGNNV RNA1 and RNA2, the full-length cDNA sequences of GGNNV were cloned into the designed reverse genetic vector, pCMV-DRz to generate pCMV-DRz-RNA1 and pCMV-DRz-RNA2 plasmids. The vector contains a human cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate early gene promoter to initiate the first viral RNA nucleotide (21 nucleotides downstream of CMV TATA box), and a human hepatitis delta virus (HDV) antigenome ribozyme (DRz) to generate the last nucleotide at the viral 3′ end by autocleavage. The pCMV-DRz vector was modified from pcDNA3 (Invitrogen) by replacing the neomycin resistance gene with a firefly luciferase gene. For the expression of RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) and site-directed mutagenesis mutant RNA1, specific primer sets (Table 2) were used to perform reverse transcriptase (RT)-splicing by overlap extension PCR. Myc and HA tags were amplified, and substitution mutations were made in DNA fragments using Phusion Plus DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resultant amplified blunt RNA1 DNA fragments were ligated into Sac II/Klenow and Sma I sites of pCMV-DRz vector to generate pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) and substitution mutation constructs 1 to 32. The pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) plasmid contained a Myc tag at the C-terminus of RdRp and a HA tag at the C-terminus of B2 protein that enabled detection of RdRp and B2 fusion protein by commercial anti-Myc and anti-HA antibodies. All constructs were confirmed by sequencing.

Northern blotting

The day before infection 1 × 106 GB cells were seeded into a 10-cm dish. GB cells were inoculated with GGNNV at multiplicity of infection (moi) = 100 or transfected with 2.5 μg of pCMV-DRz vector using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen). One dish of infected or transfected cells was collected at the indicated time for total RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using Azol RNA reagent (Arrowtec) or TRIzol reagent (Geneaid) according to manufacturer’s instructions. One microgram of each total RNA was separated with a 1% formaldehyde denaturing agarose gel. After ethidium bromide staining, RNA samples were transferred onto a nylon membrane with tissue paper. After UV cross-linking, the nylon membrane was hybridized with different DIG-labeled viral RNA probes at 65℃ overnight. After several washes the blot was incubated with sheep anti-DIG-AP antibody, and the specific viral RNA signal was visualized by BCIP/NBT color development. Alternatively, incubation with sheep anti-DIG-POD antibody, the viral RNA signal was visualized with an ECL, Lumiflash Infinity Chemiluminescent Substrate (Energenesis Biomedical), and images were obtained with a Biospectrum 600 imaging system (UVP). ImageJ was used to analyze the relative band intensity. The RNA probes corresponding to RNA3 and coat protein ORF regions were prepared using DIG RNA Labeling Mix (Roche) according to manufacturer’s instructions. To optimize the expression of the RNA1 autonomous replicon, GB cells were seeded into a 3-cm dish one day before transfection. Cells were transfected with pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) construct. Heat shock treatments (42 ℃ for 30 min) were initiated at 10 hpt. At 24 hpt, the transfected cells were collected for Northern blotting analysis.

Western blotting

B2 protein synthesis was monitored after the pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) plasmid was transfected into GB cells. After removing the medium from transfected cells at 24 hpt, the cultures were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Then, the cells were collected with a cell scraper (Falcon) and pelleted by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 5 min. After supernatants were discarded, cell pellets were lysed in an appropriate amount of loading buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.8, 0.4% SDS, 0.4 mM EDTA, 2 mM DTT, 2% Glycerol, 20% 2-mercaptoethanol and 0.002% Bromophenol Blue). Protein samples were loaded in equal amounts for SDS-PAGE, and the resolved proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Pall) for Western blotting using a semi-dry transfer unit (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) (Omics Bio) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at RT, the blocked membrane was incubated with 1:1,000 dilution mouse anti-HA tag monoclonal antibody in 5% non-fat milk TBST at 4 ℃ overnight. The membranes were washed four times with TBST and then incubated with 1:5,000 dilution goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) peroxidase-conjugated antibody at RT for 2 h. After another four washes with TBST, membranes were developed with an ECL, Lumiflash Infinity Chemiluminescent Substrate (Energenesis Biomedical). Images were obtained using a Biospectrum 600 imaging system (UVP). ImageJ was used to analyze the relative band intensity.

Immunocytochemistry

GB cells were seeded on a Deckglaser cover glass (Carolina Assistant) inside a 3-cm dish (Falcon) 1 day prior to transfection. For the detection of RdRp-Myc and B2-HA, GB cells were transfected with 2.5 μg of pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. At 10 hpt, the transfected cells were heat shocked at 42 ℃ for 30 min and harvested at 24 hpt. Transfected cells were labeled with Mito tracker Red CMX Ros for 30 min, washed with ice-cold PBS twice and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS overnight at 4 ℃. After another two washes with ice-cold PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, rinsed twice with PBS and blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 2 h at RT. The antibodies used are as follows: 1:1,000 dilution for rabbit anti-Myc antibody and 1:1,000 dilution for mouse anti-HA antibody diluted in 3% BSA in PBS. The cells were hybridized with primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. After five washes with PBS, cells were incubated with 1:1,000 dilution of goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated AffiniPure Fab and goat anti-mouse IgG + IgM (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated AffiniPure secondary antibodies diluted in 3% BSA in PBS for 1 h at RT in the dark. After another five washes with PBS in the dark, the cover glass with stained cells was mounted onto a glass slide with DAPI (49.6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) Fluormount-G (SouthernBiotech) and sealed with CoverGrip coverslip sealant (Biotium). Images were collected with a Zeiss Observer Z1 (63 × objective) inverted fluorescence microscope.

RNA secondary structure prediction

GGNNV RNA1 genomic sequence folding was analyzed with the program RNA-Fold of Vienna RNA Websuite (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi) [39].

Statistical analysis

Statistical data are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Replication of GGNNV genomic RNAs and transcription of its subgenomic RNA in grouper brain (GB) cells

Northern blotting was performed to monitor the expression of cell-associated viral RNAs during GGNNV infection. GB cells were infected with GGNNV at a multiplicity of infection (moi) = 100, and the infected-cells were collected every hour post-infection (hpi) for Northern blot analysis. DIG-labeled, strand-specific RNA probes were used to detect positive- and negative-strand viral RNAs (Fig. 1). Expression of both positive- and negative-strands of viral RNA1, RNA2, and RNA3 was first observed at 4 hpi. The expression levels of sense viral RNA1, RNA2 and RNA3 gradually reached plateaus at later infection stages and were maintained until at least 24 hpi. The similar times of emergence and expression profiles for all positive- and negative-strand viral RNA1, RNA2 and RNA3 suggested that the expression levels of both sense viral RNAs are nearly equal. Therefore, it seemed likely that the expressions of viral RNAs are subject to coordinated regulation. Interestingly, the expression levels of both positive- and negative-strand RNA3 were always higher than those of RNA1; the highest expression difference (tenfold in molar ratio) was found for positive-strand viral RNA at 4 hpi (Fig. 1A, B, C and D). It is noteworthy that a significant level of covalently linked dimers of viral RNAs were also observed in the negative-strand RNA blot (10–24 hpi) (Fig. 1B and D). RT-PCR and sequencing results showed that these viral dimers consisted of tail-to-head junctions of RNA3-RNA1, RNA3-RNA2 and RNA3-RNA3 dimers (Table 1). Establishment of a DNA-based reverse genetic platform for recombinant GGNNV recovery

To understand the transcriptional regulation mechanism for the RNA3 subgenome, we established a DNA-based reverse genetic vector to facilitate manipulation of the viral genome and allow for precise recovery of NNV from its cDNA form. The full-length viral cDNA was driven by a human CMV main immediate early gene enhancer and promoter, which contains a TATA box sequence that allows for transcriptional initiation from a precise downstream position [40]. This promoter is unlike the T7 RNA promoter, which preferentially initiates transcription when double guanosine nucleotides are present. Consequently, the CMV promoter not only precisely transcribes the viral genome, but it also allows the 5′-end of transcript to undergo 7-methylguanosine (m7G) capping by RNA polymerase II-recruited enzymes immediately after transcriptional initiation. The 5′-cap can protect transcripts from decay and serves as a binding site for factors that mediate nuclear export of viral RNAs and subsequent translational initiation through the eIF4E/5′-cap interaction. Because the NNV viral RNA genome is 5′-end capped but the 3′-end lacks a poly(A) tail, we further utilized a human HDV antigenome ribozyme (DRz) in the reverse genetic vector (pCMV-DRz) [41] (Fig. 2A). Optimization of GGNNV RNA1 autonomous replicon launching conditions with pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA)

NNV RNA1 is an autonomous replicon that encodes RdRp and enables viral genome replication and subgenome transcription after transfection into susceptible cells. In this way, NNV RNA1 alone can initiate RNA1, RNA3, RdRp and B2 protein synthesis within cells, but it cannot autonomously create infectious particles. To verify positive- and negative- strand of GGNNV RNA1 and RNA3, both anti-sense of DIG-labeled GGNNV probes within RNA3 region were prepared for Northern blot detection (Fig. 2B). The RNA1 replicon was constructed in a DNA-based pCMV-DRz vector and optimal conditions for viral gene expression were investigated. For the ease in identifying the RdRp and B2 protein, the synthesized RNA1 replicon, pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA), encoded C-terminal Myc-tagged RdRp and HA-tagged B2 protein from GGNNV RNA1. Because the local environmental conditions are different between the human CMV promoter/HDV ribozyme catalytic system (37 ℃) and piscine GB cells/betanodavirus pathogen (28 ℃), we optimized expression of viral genes by adjusting factors such as temperature and Magnesium concentration. Since GB cells cannot be cultured for long times at high temperature (37 ℃), the temperature treatments were performed as a heat shock. After pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) was transfected into GB cells, Northern blot analyses showed that heat shock treatments could dramatically increase the expression of positive-strand RNA1 and RNA3. The best heat shock treatment for expression of RNA1 and RNA3 (4.29- and 13.85-fold) was 42 ℃ for 30 min at 10 hpt (Fig. 2C). Western blotting further showed 6.26-fold increased expression of B2-HA protein after 42 ℃ 30 min heat shock treatment, as compared to the control (28 ℃) (Fig. 2D). Although divalent Magnesium is known to increase the efficiency of self-cleavage for synthetic HDV antigenome RNA sequences in vitro [41], there was no apparent increase in RNA3 and B2-HA protein after treatment with different concentrations of Magnesium (0.5–4 mM) (data not shown). This result suggested that upon transfection of pCMV-DRz-RNA1 (RdRp-Myc, B2-HA) into GB cells, the primary positive-strand RNA1 was first transcribed by CMV promoter under the control of cellular RNA polymerase II. This action results in the synthesis of RdRp-Myc from the primary positive-strand RNA1 template. Because translation of RdRp-Myc and replication of negative-strand RNA1 both require positive-strand RNA1 as a template, the positive-strand RNA1 template must switch from translation to replication as viral propagation proceeds. Thus, RdRp-Myc can replicate the negative-strand RNA1 and RNA3 using the same primary positive-strand RNA1 template. The negative-strand RNA3 can be used as a template by RdRp-Myc for production of the initial positive-strand RNA3. Finally, the B2-HA protein can be translated from this positive-strand RNA3. Of note, the expression level of RdRp-Myc remained below the detection limit of our Western blot assay, even after heat shock treatment (data not shown).

Rapid recovery of high-titer precise recombinant (r)GGNNV from viral cDNAs using DNA-based reverse genetic vectors

To evaluate the efficiency of RNA virus recovery from our reverse genetic vector, full-length cDNA of wild-type GGNNV RNA1 and RNA2 sequences were each inserted into the vector to generate the pCMV-DRz-RNA1 and pCMV-DRz-RNA2 plasmids (Fig. 2A). These two plasmids were then co-transfected into GB cells at a one-to-one ratio in order to produce wild-type recombinant GGNNV (rGGNNV-WT). The transfected GB cells displayed a typical NNV-infected cytopathic effect (CPE) beginning at 1-day post-transfection (dpt) with small vesicles observable inside the cytoplasm. The vesicle number gradually increased each day, and the transfected cells eventually rounded up before detaching from the culture-dish surface. Overall, the transfected cells resembled GGNNV-infected cells but with a longer time to death (Fig. 3A). Seven days after transfection, the culture medium was collected for determination of the released virus titer. To our surprise, high viral titers (108 TCID50/mL) of rGGNNV-WT were obtained from the supernatant of transfected GB cell medium. This rGGNNV-WT viral titer was similar to that of wild-type GGNNV (GGNNV-WT) (Fig. 3B). To examine the accuracy of rescued RNA genome sequences in rGGNNV at the 5′- and 3′-ends, sequencing was performed by using a circular RNA approach. The viral RNAs were extracted from purified GGNNV-WT and rGGNNV-WT, and then treated with a tobacco acid pyrophosphatase (TAP) to remove the cap structure from the 5′ full-length viral RNAs. The resultant virus RNAs were covalently ligated tail-to-head by a T4 RNA ligase in order to generate circular RNAs. Primer sets outside of the tail-to-head junction were then used for RT-PCR amplification of viral genomic fragments for sequencing. The sequencing results showed that the rGGNNV-WT RNA1 and RNA2 matched with GGNNV-WT at both the 5′- and 3′-ends (Table 1). Expression of RdRp-Myc was observed in the mitochondria of cytoplasm, whereas B2-HA was detected in the cytoplasm and nuclei of infected GB cells (Fig. 3C). In silicostudy of GGNNV RNA1 secondary structure required for subgenomic RNA3 production in a premature termination model

The transcriptional initiation site of betanodavirus subgenomic RNA3 is known to be U2730 of RNA1 from AHNNV (BFNNV genome type) [43] and SJNNV [10]. To clarify the transcriptional initiation nucleotide of RNA3 in the GGNNV of RGNNV genome type used in this study, we performed 5′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) experiments. The 5′-RACE results showed that the RNA3 subgenome transcriptional initiation site was also U2730 of RNA1 of GGNNV. The 5′-end of RNA3 recovered from rGGNNV-WT also showed the same sequence as that from GGNNV (Table 1). According to premature termination model of subgenome production, the proximal subgenomic control element (PSCE) sequences, which lie just upstream of the subgenome transcriptional initiation site, should play an important role in ensuring efficient subgenome production. From a sequence alignment of different betanodavirus genome types, a 13-nt conserved PSCE sequence, 5’-TCCAAGCCGGTCC-3’ (nucleotides 2717 to 2729), was found to be the immediate upstream neighbor of the GGNNV RNA3 transcriptional initiation site (nucleotide 2730). Using this potential PSCE to search for a distal subgenomic control element (DSCE), we found a complementary 8-nt conserved sequence, 5’-ACCGGCUU-3’ (nucleotides 2286 to 2293), 427 nt upstream of the PSCE (corresponding to nucleotides 2720–2727). Therefore, we hypothesize that an 8-base-paired RNA helix mediates the premature termination necessary for production of the GGNNV RNA3 subgenome. To confirm the likely existence of this base-paired helix structure, the full-length sequence of positive-strand GGNNV RNA1 genome was subjected to minimum free energy (MFE) secondary RNA structure prediction. The RNA-Fold program of Vienna RNA Websuite indicated that the minimum free energy RNA folding structure in the 3′ eighth region of the RNA1 genomic sequence also forms an 8-base-paired helix, just two nucleotides upstream the RNA3 transcriptional initiation site. Discussion

Initially, we sought to uncover the molecular mechanism of GGNNV RNA3 transcriptional regulation. In our initial attempts, we failed to detect RNA1 and RNA3 transcription using capped in vitro transcripts of wild-type RNA1 replicon in transfected GB cells. This failure might have been due to rapid RNA degradation caused by GB cellular RNA interference. Such RNA interference occurs at different levels among different types of host cells [45]. Therefore, we addressed the questions by using another DNA-based reverse genetic approach that prevents RNA degradation from host RNA interference. In this study, we present a DNA-based reverse genetic platform that quickly and precisely generates high titers of rGGNNV from complete cDNA sequences. This platform surpasses the current standard T7 RNA polymerase/promoter system, which requires two extra guanosines be placed in front of transcriptional initiation site for efficient transcription. Moreover, the CMV promoter in our system not only initiated the viral genome at the right nucleotide position, but it also allowed capping of viral RNAs at their 5′-end when primary viral RNAs were produced via the RNA polymerase II complex. The self-cleavage of HDV antigenome ribozyme worked very well in vitro in the presence of divalent cation (11 mM MgCl2) and high temperature (10 min at 37 ℃ or 60 ℃) [41]. Although addition of Magnesium could not increase the transcription and translation of GGNNV RNA1 replicons in the cell transfection experiments, heat shock treatment at 42 ℃ for 30 min enhanced replicon protein expression by sixfold in vivo. It is therefore reasonable to presume that temperature elevation may enhance the initial formation of primary positive-strand RNA1 through ribozyme self-cleavage, and then the complete positive-sense RNA1 could serve as a template for viral RdRp translation and later the viral RdRp utilized the same primary positive-strand RNA1 as template to produce negative-strand RNA3 and RNA1. The suppression of host transcription and translation by heat shock treatment may also facilitate viral RNA template switching from RdRp translation to RNA replication. Thus, this pCMV-DRz reverse genetic platform seems particularly useful for recapitulating betanodavirus replication using a cDNA replicon. Because both promoter and ribozyme sequences come from human virus, therefore this platform may also be suitable for mammalian RNA viruses study. Furthermore, the quick and precise generation of high titers reverse genetic RNA virus may also facilitate emerging pandemic prevention and control.

To address whether the 8-base paired helix structure formed by a long-distance RNA-RNA interaction is involved in the synthesis of subgenomic RNA3, we created a series of site-directed substitution mutants. The mutations were made within the helix structure without altering the amino acid sequence of the overlapping RdRp ORF. The minimum free energy (ΔG) of the wild-type positive-strand RNA1 with an 8-base paired helix was −1094.23 kcal/mol. These mutant RNA1 sequences (construct 1–17) were also subjected to the minimum free energy secondary RNA structure prediction. In positive-strand mutant RNAs, the 8-base paired helix structure no longer exists in those mutants with free energy higher than wild-type except construct 3 (change A:U to G:U pairing with slight decreased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta$$\end{document}ΔG from −1094.23 to −1094.27 kcal/mol) and construct 12 (change G:C to G:U pairing with increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta$$\end{document}ΔG from −1094.23 to −1092.12 kcal/mol). In negative-strand mutant RNA secondary structure prediction, all the mutants cause different secondary structure change (Supplement 1). However, even the 8-base paired stem structure still form in construct 3 and 12, the accumulation of RNA1 and RNA3 was nearly abolished. Additionally, in the compensatory rescue experiment, the minimum free energy secondary RNA structure prediction showed that all compensatory rescue mutants (construct 18–25) rebuilt the 8-base paired stem structure of positive-strand RNAs from their mutant (Supplement 2). However, only 5 constructs (construct 19, 20, 21, 23 and 24) exhibited partial rescue. These results suggest that the 8-base paired helix structure in the positive-strand RNA1 sequence is required but not sufficient for viral RdRp to transcribe both negative-strand of RNA1 and RNA3. It seems that specific nucleotide sequence within 8-base paired helix is also necessary for RdRp transcriptional recognition and progression regardless of whether ΔG is higher or lower. This sequence- and structure-specific promoter recognition has been demonstrated with brome mosaic virus (BMV) [46].

The seven-nucleotide sequence, 5′-UAGUCAA-3′ (nucleotides 2730 to 2736 in RNA1; nucleotides 1 to 7 in RNA3), is evolutionarily conserved at the 5′-end of RNA3 among four betanodaviruses genome types [10], suggesting that this sequence may play an important role for RNA3 synthesis and/or B2 protein translation. In our current study, the accumulation of positive-strand RNA3 from transcriptional initiation nucleotide substitution mutations (U2730 A, U2730 C and U2730G, construct 26, 27 and 28) were mildly decreased as compared to that of wild-type RNA1 replicon. However, the accumulation of both strand RNA1 were significantly increased in all transcriptional initiation nucleotide substitution mutations. These results suggest that the 8-base paired helix downstream transcriptional promoter elements at the RNA3 negative-strand 3′-end also contributed to RNA1 and RNA3 production (Fig. 7B, C). Additionally, RNA3 accumulation was greatly reduced when the second and third double-nucleotides were substituted (AG2731/2732 UC, construct 29) and even eliminated when the fourth nucleotide was mutated (U2733 C, construct 30). In contrast, RNA1 and RNA3 accumulation were greatly enhanced when the seventh nucleotide was mutated (A2736G, construct 31). These results support the existing models of transcriptional promoter elements in downstream sequences of negative-strand RNA3 subgenome that were proposed along with the premature termination model (Fig. 7B, C) [25, 26]. Furthermore, ΔB2 RNA1 mutant (U2754 C, AUG to ACG eliminates the B2 protein start codon) in vitro transcripts suppressed RNA1 accumulation [5], and B2 protein was shown to bind dsRNA to protect viral dsRNA replication intermediates from Dicer-mediated cleavage and RNA degradation [47]. In the present study, results from our U2754 C substitution mutation (construct 32) confirmed the RNA1 and RNA3 accumulation deficiency seen with the ΔB2 mutation in the DNA-based replicon transfection experiment (Fig. 7B, C).

Segmented virus genomes must somehow coordinate production of each genomic segment at the right ratio for optimal viral gene replication, transcription, translation and viron assembly. Because RNA1 is an autonomous replicon, the expression level of RNA1 may be much higher than that of RNA2 at times. Nevertheless, several studies on alphanodaviruses revealed that the replication of genomic RNA2 is transactivated by subgenomic RNA3 regardless of B2 protein expression, while the synthesis of subgenomic RNA3 is inhibited by genomic RNA2 but not by coat protein [27, 44, 48]. Moreover, a negative-strand FHV RNA2-RNA3 heterodimer was shown to act as a template to initiate positive-sense RNA2 monomer replication, revealing a direct role of subgenomic RNA3 dependence on the transactivation of RNA2 replication [49]. Therefore, it was proposed that subgenomic RNA3 dependence on genomic RNA2 replication through negative-strand RNA3 dimers serves as a mechanism to coordinate replication of two genomic segments [48]. In a study on alphanodavirus RNA3-RNA2 heterodimers, only RNA2 was replicated efficiently, implying that RdRp can recognize internal promoter and terminator elements within viral RNA templates during polymerization [50]. In the current study, different amounts of negative-strand RNA3 dimers were observed in GGNNV-infected GB cells. The proportion of RNA2-RNA3 heterodimer to RNA2 monomer was much higher than the ratio of RNA1-RNA3 heterodimer to RNA1 monomer in viral negative-sense RNAs (Fig. 1B and D), suggesting that natural occurring RNA3 homodimer and heterodimers may have similar roles as their alphanodavirus counterparts, serving to transactivate the replication of positive-sense RNA2 monomer and maintain both genomic RNAs at equimolar levels for optimum virus yield. These results reinforced the conclusion that replication of GGNNV genomic RNA1 and RNA2 is coordinated by the subgenomic RNA3. However, further detailed investigations will be required to fully understand this coordination mechanism.

Whether coordinated regulation of viral genomic segments occurs in betanodavirus prompted us to co-transfect RNA2 construct with mutants of RNA1 replicon constructs into GB cells. The results showed that synthesis of GGNNV RNA3 and RNA1 from autonomous RNA1 replicons could be greatly inhibited by RNA2 co-transfection (Fig. 8). It has been proposed that down-regulation of alphanodavirus RNA3 synthesis is controlled by competition with RNA2 for viral RdRp [27] or the base-pairing between RNA1 and RNA2 sequences may act as a recognition site for RdRp to initiate RNA3 synthesis to impair the recognition process and inhibit RNA3 production [51]. Computer-assisted base-pairing between GGNNV RNA1 and RNA2 genomic sequences showed that a total of 40 discontinuous nucleotides forms complementary base pairs between the GGNNV 3′-end 578-nucleotide region of RNA1 (nucleotides 2423–2430, 2654–2662, 2759–2766, 2789–2794 and 2992–3000) and the 5′-end 59-nucleotide region of RNA2 (nucleotides 181–188, 172–180, 164–171, 152–157 and 130–138). Whether these intergenomic RNA-RNA interactions are involved in the coordinated regulation of RNA1, RNA2 and RNA3 expression will require further study.

Upon infection, NNV RNA1 and RNA2 genomes (positive-sense) are released from viral particle to translate their encoded RdRp and coat protein, respectively. Subsequently, viral RdRp utilizes RNA1 and RNA2 genome as templates to transcribe their negative-strand RNA1 and RNA2. Nevertheless, the subgenome RNA3 seemingly is transcribed even early than RNA1 and RNA2. The immediately transcribed RNA3 is observed with tenfold accumulation level than that of RNA1 in molar ratio at 4 hpi (Fig. 1A, B). This result suggests that RNA3 and/or B2 protein plays an essential role in the very beginning of viral life cycle. In Western blot analysis, B2 is easier to detect than RdRp indicating the crucial role of B2 protein in the early infection stage (Fig. 2D). Although both RdRp and B2 proteins can be examined in the more sensitive immunochemistry staining (Fig. 3C). Since B2 protein acts as a dsRNA binding protein to antagonize cellular RNA interference [5, 9], it is reasonable to attribute to B2 the viral RNAs protection ability. Therefore, lacking B2 protein protection will readily result in viral RNAs degradation. At 4 hpi, a small amount of RNA1 and RNA2 in both strands was also detected and their transcriptions were gradually increased until late infection stage (Fig. 1A, B, C, D). In the process of negative-strand synthesis, the positive-sense template sequence surrounding RNA3 transcriptional initiation site forms an 8-base paired helical barrier. This occasionally permits viral RdRp to continue for complete synthesis negative-strand RNA1 followed by production of positive-strand RNA1 and RdRp. However, at times the helical barrier also causes RdRp to cease transcription, resulting in formation of premature negative-strand RNA3 subgenome, which will be later used for synthesizing positive-strand RNA3 and B2 protein. Because both RdRp (for viral RNA synthesis) and B2 (for viral RNA protection) are essential for viral propagation, therefore their transcription need to proceed almost at the same time and one side tilting will cause viral transcription to stop. The appearance of RNA3 homo and heterodimer in the negative-strand RNA blot 10 hpi implies that the expression of RNA1, RNA2 and RNA3 are coordinated regulation. Subsequently, the nascent negative-strand RNA1, RNA2 and RNA3 can be further replicated by RdRp to produce complementary RNAs and form a dsRNA replication intermediate, resulting in the cyclic replication of viral RNAs between negative- and positive-strands. Worthy of note, NNV assembles each positive-strand RNA1 and RNA2 for viral particle package. Conclusion

We have established a simple and efficient reverse genetic platform for obtaining high viral titer of rGGNNV up to 108 TCID50/mL by transfecting pCMV-DRz-RNA1 and pCMV-DRz-RNA2 into GB cells. The detailed investigations using RNA1 mutants proved that an 8-base paired helix structure, formed in sequence-dependent manner through long distance intragenomic RNA-RNA interactions, along with specific nucleotide sequence surrounding RNA3 transcriptional initiation region, is responsible for NNV RNA3 premature termination. In addition, we also demonstrated that RNA1 and RNA3 expression is modulated by RNA2 co-expression in trans.
