Cost-effectiveness of concurrently available oral and long-acting injectable pre-exposure prophylaxis for preventing HIV infections among men who have sex with men in eastern China: A modelling study
Authors: Zhixin Zhu, Haibo Jiang, Lixia Ye, Shiwen Tan, Kun Chu, Zehao Ye, Yiya Wang, Yi Chen, Fengsheng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina faces a growing HIV burden among men who have sex with men (MSM), yet uptake of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) remains low. Long-acting injectable cabotegravir (CAB-LA) may improve prevention effectiveness, but its economic value in China remains uncertain.MethodsWe developed a risk-stratified dynamic compartmental model incorporating HIV natural history, diagnosis, ART, and both transmitted and acquired drug resistance. Model parameters were calibrated to published surveillance data in Zhejiang and Jiangsu Province from 2009 to 2019. Assuming zero PrEP coverage in 2025, we simulated scale-up among high-risk MSM through 2030 under six scenarios: no PrEP (status quo); 20%, 50%, and 80% oral PrEP only; and total PrEP coverage of 80% with CAB-LA comprising 20% or 50% of PrEP use. Outcomes were projected to 2050, including epidemic trend and PrEP cost-effectiveness presented as incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER).ResultsAcross Zhejiang and Jiangsu, expanding PrEP in high-risk MSM substantially reduced cumulative HIV infections, drug-resistant infections, and HIV-related deaths during 2025–2050. Oral PrEP-only strategies were cost-effective in Zhejiang (ICER US$45,961–55,053 per QALY for 20%–80% coverage) and highly cost-effective in Jiangsu (US$11,699–12,762 per QALY). At 80% total PrEP coverage, incorporating CAB-LA markedly increased ICERs and remained cost-effective only in Jiangsu under 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) (US$56,980 per QALY). In Zhejiang, CAB-LA would require an approximately 91% price reduction to potentially achieve cost-effectiveness. Expanding oral PrEP among low-risk MSM is not cost-effective or is only marginally cost-effective.ConclusionsRisk-targeted scale-up of oral PrEP among high-risk MSM is an efficient near-term strategy in eastern China. Wider CAB-LA implementation is unlikely to be cost-effective at current prices and will likely require substantial price reductions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28479-7.Keywords: HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis, Cost-effectiveness, Modelling study, Chinese MSM

Introduction

In 2024, the Asia–Pacific region had an estimated 6.9 million people living with HIV, more than 300,000 new infections, and 150,000 AIDS-related deaths, imposing a substantial disease burden [1]. In China, over 1.3 million individuals are living with HIV, and newly diagnosed cases have consistently exceeded 100,000 per year [2]. The proportion of infections attributable to male-male sexual transmission increased from 9.1% in 2009 to 25.7% in 2025 [3, 4]. Men who have sex with men (MSM) face a disproportionately high risk of HIV acquisition. An estimated 20%-40% of MSM in China can be classified as high-risk, characterized primarily by reporting more than ten sexual partners within the past six months, condomless sex, and other high-risk behaviors [5].

Maintaining current levels of HIV control is unlikely to achieve the 2030 target of reducing HIV incidence and HIV-related mortality by 90% relative to 2010 levels [6], creating urgency for more effective prevention strategies. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is among the most effective biomedical interventions for high-risk populations [7]; when taken as prescribed, oral PrEP can reduce HIV acquisition among adult men by up to 99% [8]. Yet PrEP implementation in China remains at an early stage. A recent meta-analysis reported limited awareness of oral PrEP among MSM (32.4%), very low uptake (4.9%), and suboptimal adherence (pooled adequate adherence 40.7%) [9]. Barriers include slow policy scale-up, confidentiality concerns, discrimination and stigma, pill burden and reliance on daily or event-driven dosing, and medication-related adverse effects [9, 10]. Long-acting PrEP modalities, such as long-acting injectable cabotegravir (CAB-LA), offer potential advantages by reducing dosing frequency and possibly improving effectiveness and acceptability compared with oral PrEP [11, 12].

Multiple modeling studies have evaluated the impact of implementing oral PrEP among MSM in China [5, 13–15], whereas modeling evidence for long-acting PrEP remains limited [16], and comparative effectiveness and economic evaluations have largely focused on U.S. settings [17–19]. One study suggested CAB-LA could avert more infections than oral PrEP but may not be cost-effective at current prices in China [16]. Notably, this analysis did not incorporate key dynamic features, did not account for antiretroviral therapy (ART) drug resistance, and did not assess strategies involving mixed use of multiple PrEP modalities. In addition, substantial heterogeneity across Chinese regions—spanning economic development, epidemic patterns, and prevention responses—limits the policy relevance of “one-size-fits-all” recommendations [20].

Zhejiang and Jiangsu are two economically developed eastern coastal provinces, and each reported more than 38,000 people living with HIV and more than 3,500 newly reported infections in 2022, with male-male sexual contact accounting for approximately 43.7% and 56.0% of cases, respectively [21–25]. Despite this burden, planned PrEP implementation among MSM in these provinces remains limited, and related evidence is scarce. One study developed a mathematical model of HIV prevention and control among MSM in Zhejiang but did not conduct an economic evaluation [26]. Another study rigorously modeled the cost-effectiveness of oral PrEP and expanded antiretroviral therapy in Jiangsu under ART drug resistance, yet did not consider long-acting injectable PrEP and did not distinguish between high- and low-risk subgroups [15]. To address these gaps, we develop a dynamic compartmental model to evaluate HIV epidemiologic impact and cost-effectiveness under multiple scenarios of combined oral PrEP and long-acting PrEP among high-risk MSM. By explicitly examining mixed-modality strategies in a province-specific context, our study aims to provide actionable evidence to inform differentiated PrEP planning and implementation in eastern China.

Methods

Model structure

Building on previously published modelling frameworks [15, 27], we extended and adapted the model in R version 4.5.0 by incorporating HIV natural history and intervention pathways to construct a dynamic compartmental model stratified by risk among MSM (low-risk i = 1, high-risk i = 2). The model equations were presented in the Supplementary Material on pages 1 and 2. Risk groups were defined based on the annual number of sexual partners: high-risk MSM (30% of the population) reported an average of more than ten partners per year, whereas low-risk MSM (70% of the population) reported an average of fewer than three partners per year, with an overall average of approximately five partnerships annually [5, 28]. The model incorporated both oral PrEP and CAB-LA and simulated the full cascade of HIV progression and care, from undiagnosed infection to diagnosis, treatment, and the development of drug resistance (Fig. 1).

Parameter calibration and collection

Model parameters and initial compartment values were estimated using nonlinear least squares (NLS). Parameters were calibrated by minimizing the weighted sum of squared errors (WSSE) between model outputs and observed data. Unknown parameters were sampled within their prespecified ranges using Latin hypercube sampling, repeated 1,000 times. For each simulation, we calculated the WSSE between model outputs and empirical data, and retained the top 10% of parameter sets with the smallest squared errors to construct 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All calibration data were obtained from published studies. HIV surveillance data from Zhejiang Province during 2009–2019 included annually reported drug-sensitive HIV cases, drug-resistant HIV cases [26], and deaths among treated individuals (estimated by multiplying the total number of deaths by the proportion of deaths among MSM and the proportion receiving treatment) [21] (Figure S1). For Jiangsu Province (2009–2019), data included the annual numbers of newly diagnosed individuals and newly treated individuals stratified by four CD4 cell-count categories (CD4 ≥ 500 cells/µL, 350–499 cells/µL, 200–349 cells/µL, and < 200 cells/µL), deaths among treated individuals [15] and the prevalence of ADR [29] (Figure S2).

Simulated expanding PrEP scenarios

Because no planned PrEP program has yet been implemented in Zhejiang or Jiangsu, we assumed zero PrEP coverage in both provinces in 2025 and initiated a PrEP scale-up strategy targeted to high-risk MSM through 2030. For each scenario, we further projected the total number of new HIV infections, drug-resistant cases, drug-resistant cases among treated, new deaths and HIV-related deaths among diagnosed/treated through 2050, as well as HIV incidence and prevalence among MSM in 2050 (Supplementary Materials, pp. 18–19). Cost-effectiveness analysis

We estimated the costs of PrEP, first- and second-line antiretroviral therapy, HIV testing, genotypic resistance testing, treatment of HIV-related opportunistic infections, diagnosis, and counselling based on published literature (Supplementary Materials, pp. 19–24; Tables S3-S4). The difference in costs between the two intervention strategies was defined as the incremental cost, while the difference in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) was defined as the incremental QALYs. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated as the ratio of incremental costs to incremental QALYs. In economic evaluations, a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold is commonly used as a benchmark against which ICERs are compared. The World Health Organization recommends using a WTP threshold of one to three times the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita to reflect the maximum amount that decision-makers are willing to pay for an additional QALY gained [30]. An intervention is considered highly cost-effective when the ICER is less than or equal to the GDP per capita; cost-effective when the ICER is between one and three times the GDP per capita; and not cost-effective when the ICER exceeds three times the GDP per capita. In 2024, the GDP per capita was approximately US$19,000 in Zhejiang Province [31] and US$22,000 in Jiangsu Province [32]. All costs in the cost-effectiveness analysis were expressed in 2024 US $.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted one-way sensitivity analyses to examine the impact of parameter uncertainty on the cost-effectiveness outcomes. Given that the model was calibrated primarily to diagnosis-related surveillance data, we additionally assessed uncertainty in the diagnosis-to-infection mapping by simultaneously varying all CD4-stratum-specific diagnosis rates () by ± 25% from their calibrated values. Other parameters varied in the analyses included the annual cost of CAB-LA (US$2,105–33,813), the annual cost of oral PrEP (US$148–3,445), the annual discontinuation rate of CAB-LA (0–0.1), the annual discontinuation rate of oral PrEP (0.01–0.5), the annual discount rate (0–6%), the effectiveness of oral PrEP (45–95%), the effectiveness of first-line antiretroviral therapy (80–100%), the effectiveness of CAB-LA (67.3–99%), and the rate of transmitted drug resistance (0.001–0.02).

Results

Impacts of combined oral PrEP and CAB-LA on new infections and drug-resistant infections

In Zhejiang Province, scaling up PrEP among high-risk MSM substantially reduced HIV infections and new infections with drug-resistant strains, from 2025 to 2050. Under the status quo, cumulative new infections were 32,041, and drug-resistant infections were 8,995. Only, 20% PrEP (no CAB-LA) reduced new infections by 8,497 (26.5%) and prevented 465 drug-resistant infections (5.2%). 50% PrEP (no CAB-LA) reduced new infections by 15,856 (49.5%) and prevented 983 drug-resistant infections (10.9%). 80% PrEP (no CAB-LA) reduced new infections by 20,720 (64.7%) and prevented 1,426 drug-resistant infections (15.8%). At a fixed total PrEP coverage of 80%, shifting a larger proportion of PrEP users to CAB-LA yielded additional benefits. 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) averted 21,747 infections (67.9%) and prevented 1,491 drug-resistant infections (16.6%). 80% PrEP (50% CAB-LA) averted 22,989 infections (71.7%) and prevented 1,576 drug-resistant infections (17.5%). The proportion of drug-resistant cases among treated individuals increased by 1.2–4.1%. (Table S5; Fig. 2). Expanding oral PrEP use among low-risk MSM further reduced new infections and drug-resistant infections (Table S5; Figure S3).

In Jiangsu Province, under the status quo, cumulative new infections were 91,798 and drug-resistant infections were 14,405. Oral PrEP-only strategies averted 15,682 (17.1%), 33,761 (36.8%), and 48,733 (53.1%) infections under 20%, 50%, and 80% PrEP (no CAB-LA), respectively; corresponding drug-resistant infections prevented were 659 (4.6%), 1,484 (10.3%), and 2,280 (15.8%). At 80% total PrEP coverage, infections averted increased to 51,936 (56.6%), 55,975 (61.0%), and 61,077 (66.5%) for 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) and 80% PrEP (50% CAB-LA), with drug-resistant infections prevented of 2,413 (16.8%) and 2,597 (18.0%), respectively. The proportion of drug-resistant cases among treated individuals increased by 0.6–2.5%. (Table S6; Fig. 3). Expanding oral PrEP use among low-risk MSM further reduced new infections and drug-resistant infections (Table S6; Figure S4).

Impacts on prevalence, and HIV-related deaths

In Zhejiang, PrEP scale-up reduced prevalence, and HIV-related deaths. Under the status quo, prevalence was 16.4%, and total new HIV-related deaths were 7,961. 20% PrEP (no CAB-LA) reduced prevalence to 14.1%, averting 1,095 deaths (13.8%). 50% PrEP (no CAB-LA) reduced prevalence to 12.2%, averting 2,145 deaths (26.9%). 80% PrEP (no CAB-LA) reduced prevalence to 11.0%, averting 2,925 deaths (36.7%). At 80% total PrEP coverage, increasing CAB-LA use further improved outcomes. 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) reduced prevalence to 10.8%, averting 3,071 deaths (38.6%). 80% PrEP (50% CAB-LA) reduced prevalence to 10.5%, averting 3,258 deaths (40.9%) (Table S5; Fig. 2).

Under the status quo, prevalence was 23.1%, and HIV-related deaths were 43,983 in Jiangsu. Oral PrEP-only strategies averted 3,921 (8.9%), 8,512 (19.4%), and 12,703 (28.9%) deaths under 20%, 50%, and 80% PrEP (no CAB-LA), respectively, in Jiangsu. At 80% total PrEP coverage, deaths averted increased to 13,510 (30.7%), 14,594 (33.2%), and 16,045 (36.5%) for 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) and 80% PrEP (50% CAB-LA), with prevalence declining to 15.6% and 15.0%, respectively (Table S6; Fig. 3).

Cost-effectiveness analysis

In Zhejiang, oral PrEP-only strategies in high-risk MSM were cost-effective versus the status quo: 20%, 50%, and 80% PrEP (no CAB-LA) generated 6,244, 12,476, and 17,001 incremental QALYs, with ICERs of US$45,961, US$50,415, and US$55,053 per QALY gained, respectively. By contrast, at 80% total high-risk MSM PrEP coverage, incorporating CAB-LA was not cost-effective: 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) and 80% PrEP (50% CAB-LA) yielded 17,861 and 18,920, incremental QALYs, but ICERs increased to US$218,147 and US$420,925 per QALY gained, respectively (Table 1; Fig. 4A). Scaling up oral PrEP among low-risk MSM was not cost-effective (Table S7; Figure S5).

In Jiangsu, oral PrEP-only strategies in high-risk MSM were highly cost-effective versus the status quo: 20%, 50%, and 80% PrEP (no CAB-LA) generated 18,494, 42,707, and 66,421 incremental QALYs, with ICERs of US$11,699, US$12,043, and US$12,762 per QALY gained, respectively. At 80% total high-risk MSM PrEP coverage, 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) remained cost-effective (incremental QALYs 70,362; ICER US$56,980/QALY), whereas higher CAB-LA shares were not cost-effective: 80% PrEP (50% CAB-LA) yielded 75,807 incremental QALYs but had ICERs of US$111,065 per QALY gained (Table 1; Fig. 4B). Scaling up oral PrEP among low-risk MSM remained cost-effective, but ICERs rose sharply: ICERs for 80% HR + 20% LR and 80% HR + 50% LR were US$34,837 and US$57,632 per QALY, respectively (Table S7; Figure S5).

Sensitivity analysis

In the one-way sensitivity analysis, the annual cost of PrEP remained the most influential driver of cost-effectiveness outcomes, followed by the discontinuation rate of oral PrEP, the cost of first-line antiretroviral therapy, and the diagnosis rates (Fig. 5). In Zhejiang Province, under 80% PrEP coverage, oral PrEP would become highly cost-effective if its annual cost decreased by 67% (to $520) (Figures S6-S7). For CAB-LA, the annual cost would need to decrease by approximately 91% from the base-case value (to $2,017 per year) to remain marginally cost-effective (Figures S6-S7). In Jiangsu Province, under 80% PrEP coverage, oral PrEP would become cost-saving if its annual cost decreased by 86% (to $216) (Figures S6-S7). For CAB-LA, the annual cost would need to decline by approximately 41% from the base-case value (to $13,074 per year) for the 80% PrEP coverage scenario (50% CAB-LA) to remain cost-effective (Figures S6-S7). If oral PrEP discontinuation rises to 0.5 or diagnosis rates increase by 25%, the 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) strategy for high-risk MSM in Jiangsu would no longer be cost-effective.

Discussion

This province-specific dynamic modelling study evaluated the epidemiologic impact and cost-effectiveness of scaling up oral PrEP and CAB-LA, including mixed-modality strategies, among high-risk MSM in Zhejiang and Jiangsu. Across both provinces, increasing total PrEP coverage substantially reduced cumulative HIV infections, HIV-related deaths, and new infections with drug-resistant strains from 2025 to 2050. At a fixed total PrEP coverage of 80%, shifting a larger share of PrEP users from oral PrEP to CAB-LA produced additional epidemiologic gains. However, these added benefits came at markedly higher ICERs, with only a small share of CAB-LA use remaining cost-effective in Jiangsu, whereas CAB-LA-inclusive strategies were not cost-effective in Zhejiang.

When benchmarked against each province’s own baseline under identical strategies, Zhejiang exhibited consistently larger relative reductions than Jiangsu: cumulative incident infections declined by approximately 10–13%, and HIV-related deaths declined by 5–8%. This suggests that, at the same intensity of PrEP scale-up, Zhejiang could achieve greater relative epidemiologic improvement. However, ICERs were substantially higher in Zhejiang across all strategies, indicating a higher cost per unit of health gain. A key explanation is the difference in baseline epidemic intensity between the two provinces. Jiangsu has a higher background incidence (as estimated by the model), so an equivalent expansion in coverage averts more infections in absolute terms and yields larger QALY gains, thereby lowering the ICER [33]. This pattern is consistent with cross-setting modeling evidence. Stansfield et al. [34] reported that scaling up oral PrEP and CAB-LA tends to be more impactful and economically attractive in higher-incidence settings (e.g., Atlanta), but less so in lower-incidence settings (e.g., Montreal and the Netherlands), with correspondingly lower ICERs in the former [19].

Compared with untargeted, population-wide coverage strategies that do not stratify by risk, prioritizing high-risk MSM typically increases the number of infections averted and QALYs gained per unit investment, thereby improving cost-effectiveness. Prior work in Jiangsu suggested that scaling up oral PrEP alone among all MSM may be insufficient to remain cost-effective, and that concurrent expansion of ART may be required [15]. In our study, after explicitly stratifying MSM by risk, we found that restricting oral PrEP scale-up to high-risk MSM substantially improved economic performance: in Jiangsu, ICERs declined to US$11,699 − 12,762 per QALY (20%-80% coverage), below 1× GDP per capita (≈ US$22,000/QALY) and therefore highly cost-effective; in Zhejiang, ICERs were US$45,961 − 55,053/QALY, remaining below the 3× GDP per capita threshold (≈ US$57,000/QALY) and thus acceptable as cost-effective. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence supporting risk-targeted PrEP delivery to maximize efficiency [14, 35]. However, ICERs increased steeply as the share of CAB-LA rose: in Jiangsu, cost-effectiveness was maintained only when total PrEP coverage reached 80% and CAB-LA accounted for a relatively small proportion (20%) of PrEP use, whereas in Zhejiang, none of the CAB-LA-inclusive strategies met the cost-effectiveness threshold.

In one-way sensitivity analyses, PrEP price was the most influential driver of the ICER, consistent with findings from other settings [36–38]. Under base-case assumptions, oral PrEP alone at 80% coverage was already cost-effective. With further price reductions, oral PrEP could become even more economically attractive: in Zhejiang, a 67% reduction in annual oral PrEP cost (to $520) would render the strategy highly cost-effective, whereas in Jiangsu, an 86% reduction (to $216) would make it cost-saving and thus the preferred option. Given that patent protection for Gilead’s PrEP product (Truvada) reportedly expired in China in 2024 and that two generic TDF/FTC products (Glyke and Taihe) have been approved, oral PrEP prices may decrease further [39].

For CAB-LA, high acquisition costs have emerged as the predominant barrier to scale-up. Zhao et al. [16] reported that expanding CAB-LA was not cost-effective in Australia, Thailand, or China at prevailing prices, and that achieving cost-effectiveness would require a 50%-90% reduction in CAB-LA costs, with CAB-LA positioned as a complement to oral PrEP rather than a wholesale replacement. Our findings are consistent. In Jiangsu, cost-effectiveness could be maintained under 80% total PrEP coverage among high-risk MSM only when CAB-LA constituted a relatively small share (20%) of PrEP use; in Zhejiang, CAB-LA would need to be discounted by at least 91% (to approximately US$2,017 per year) to potentially achieve cost-effectiveness. High prices may also suppress demand. In China, evidence indicates that only 14.7% of potential users were willing to pay more than 1,200 CNY (~ US$180) every two months for CAB-LA—substantially below prevailing commercial prices [40, 41]. The National Medical Products Administration approved CAB-LA for HIV-1 PrEP among at-risk adults and adolescents in May 2024, and it was launched in China in January 2025 [42]. Given that CAB-LA has been approved as an effective HIV prevention modality, addressing affordability and access is imperative [43]. Financial support mechanisms—including price reductions and insurance coverage—will therefore remain central to scaling up long-acting injectable PrEP in China.

At present, although large-scale expansion of CAB-LA coverage appears unlikely to be cost-effective, the cost-effectiveness of highly targeted CAB-LA provision for individuals at substantial HIV risk who face barriers to oral PrEP access, adherence, or persistence warrants further investigation. For example, younger MSM with limited financial resources often demonstrate fewer days of oral PrEP use and higher discontinuation rates [8], which may make them particularly suitable candidates for CAB-LA-based prevention strategies [34].

Our sensitivity analysis also showed that diagnosis-rate assumptions can materially affect cost-effectiveness estimates, particularly for CAB-LA-inclusive strategies with ICERs close to the willingness-to-pay threshold. When all CD4-stratum-specific diagnosis rates were increased by 25%, the 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) strategy in Jiangsu no longer met the cost-effectiveness threshold. Because diagnosis-related surveillance data were used as calibration targets, lower assumed diagnosis rates imply a larger underlying pool of undiagnosed and incident infections for a given number of observed diagnoses, which can increase the estimated infections averted and incremental QALYs from PrEP scale-up. Conversely, higher diagnosis rates imply fewer underlying infections and smaller incremental health gains from PrEP, while intervention costs remain largely unchanged, thereby increasing the ICER. Therefore, although a limited share of CAB-LA use appeared cost-effective in Jiangsu in the base-case analysis, this result should be considered conditional on the calibrated diagnosis process and interpreted with caution [44].

Our analysis has several limitations. First, we did not explicitly model behavioral changes, risk compensation, or transitions between high- and low-risk groups over time. Therefore, the model could not dynamically capture complex post-PrEP mechanisms, including changes in condom use, numbers of sexual partners, or individual risk behaviors. Evidence from international studies suggests that PrEP implementation may be associated with increased STI occurrence [45]. If substantial risk compensation or changes in risk status occur in real-world settings in China, the practical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of PrEP could be attenuated or underestimated.

Second, cost inputs and the WTP threshold are uncertain and may evolve over time. Cost estimates were derived from prior studies and standardized to 2024 US dollars; however, real-world drug price negotiations, centralized procurement, service delivery costs, and health insurance reimbursement policies may change rapidly. In addition, the commonly used benchmark of 1–3× GDP per capita per QALY gained does not necessarily reflect the true opportunity costs faced by provincial health decision-makers in China, which could shift the boundary of what is deemed “cost-effective,” although it remains the most widely applied approach.

Third, our model was calibrated to newly diagnosed cases, not directly to new infections. The gap between infections and diagnoses depends on the estimated diagnosis rates. External validation against independent infection estimates, such as Spectrum or Workbook, was infeasible due to incompatible data requirements (e.g., CD4 strata granularity and population‑level demographic inputs). Future studies should validate infection estimates using multiple approaches.

Fourth, we did not explicitly model the impact of adherence on oral PrEP effectiveness. Although this uncertainty was partly addressed by testing a wide range of PrEP effectiveness values in sensitivity analyses, the model could not capture heterogeneous adherence patterns, such as missed doses among individuals who remained on PrEP.

Conclusion

Scaling up PrEP among high-risk MSM in Zhejiang and Jiangsu substantially reduces cumulative HIV infections, drug-resistant infections, and HIV-related deaths to 2050. Risk-targeted scale-up of oral PrEP alone is highly cost-effective in Jiangsu and cost-effective in Zhejiang, whereas incorporating CAB-LA at current prices may be cost-effective in Jiangsu under the 80% PrEP (20% CAB-LA) strategy. These findings support prioritizing high-risk MSM for oral PrEP expansion as an efficient near-term strategy, while underscoring that wider CAB-LA implementation will likely require substantial price reductions.
