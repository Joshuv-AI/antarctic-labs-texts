Pulmonary impairment after interscalene block for orthopedic shoulder surgery: a systematic review
Authors: Zev Karve, Cooper Williams, Jacob Calpey, Aghdas Movassaghi, Jocelyn Lubert, Vani J Sabesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterscalene block (ISB) is the gold standard for regional anesthesia in shoulder surgery due to its proven efficacy in reducing post-operative pain and opioid use. However, ISB commonly causes temporary phrenic nerve palsy, resulting in ipsilateral hemidiaphragmatic paralysis and measurable declines in pulmonary function. While often well-tolerated in healthy individuals, these effects may pose significant risk in patients with limited pulmonary reserve or underlying cardiopulmonary disease. This systematic review aimed to quantify the impact of ISB on pulmonary function and identify patient-specific risk factors that may inform anesthetic decision-making for shoulder surgeons.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis guidelines. A comprehensive literature search of electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science) was conducted on March 23, 2025. Eligible studies were level I randomized controlled trials published in English. Data were extracted on study characteristics, patient demographics, block technique, and pulmonary outcomes.ResultsA total of 33 studies met inclusion criteria and included data on 1,481 patients. Diaphragmatic paralysis was reported in 25 studies, with an average of 53% (95% confidence interval [CI; 42%-64%]). Fifteen studies evaluated diaphragmatic excursion, with an average reduction from baseline of 48.6% (95% CI [37.1%-60.2%]). Changes in pulmonary function tests were reported in 18 studies for forced expiratory volume and 17 studies for forced vital capacity, with average reductions of 27.1% (95% CI, 23.0%–31.1%) and 24.8% (95% CI, 21.2%–28.4%), respectively. An unpaired t-test comparing patients above and below age 50 showed a significantly higher incidence of diaphragmatic paralysis in older patients (P = .029).ConclusionInterscalene block remains a highly effective technique for perioperative pain control in shoulder surgery, with well-established benefits in minimizing opioid use and improving early recovery. However, it is consistently associated with reductions in objective pulmonary function. Advanced patient age was associated with increased risk of diaphragmatic impairment, while anesthetic parameters did not demonstrate consistent associations with pulmonary outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of patient selection and suggest that further investigation into comorbidity-specific risk stratification may help guide anesthetic decision-making, particularly as surgical indications expand to include more medically complex populations.Keywords: Interscalene block, Pulmonary function, Hemidiaphragmatic paralysis, Shoulder surgery, Pulmonary complications, Nerve block, Orthopedic

Interscalene blocks (ISBs) have become a widely accepted and effective method of regional anesthesia for orthopedic shoulder procedures, including rotator cuff repair and shoulder arthroplasty. Over the past 2 decades, ISBs have emerged as an excellent option for post-operative pain control compared to supraclavicular, costoclavicular, and suprascapular nerve blocks.1,21,48 As such, ISBs have become increasingly common, especially in ambulatory orthopedic surgery.15 However, ISBs are not without risks, particularly concerning pulmonary function. Due to the proximity of the phrenic nerve (originating from C3–C5), ISBs commonly result in transient hemidiaphragmatic paralysis.2,4,44 This paresis can reduce forced vital capacity and forced expiratory volume (FEV1) by up to 25% to 30%, which may be clinically significant in patients with limited or reduced pulmonary reserve at baseline.44 The extent to which ISBs independently contribute to post-operative pulmonary complications, such as pneumonia, atelectasis, and acute respiratory distress syndrome, remains unclear. While the hemidiaphragmatic paresis (HDP) associated with ISBs is typically transient, these physiological effects may become consequential in patients with underlying pulmonary disease or when combined with general anesthesia.

Phrenic nerve palsy is a known and well-studied complication of ISB,10 underscoring the need for further investigation into the pulmonary safety of ISBs. Furthermore, it is important for surgeons to understand the patient- and anesthetic-associated risk factors, along with the extent and duration of pulmonary complications, as these issues can compromise surgical outcomes, delay recovery, and increase medicolegal risk. Potential preventative measures include careful patient selection for ISB and thoughtful anesthetic design—lock approach, local anesthetic concentration, and dosage—to maximize analgesia while minimizing phrenic nerve involvement.

The pulmonary consequences of interscalene block, particularly phrenic nerve–mediated hemidiaphragmatic paralysis, are well recognized and have been evaluated in prior systematic reviews. However, much of the available literature has focused on describing the physiological effects of ISB rather than identifying which patients may be at greatest risk for clinically meaningful respiratory compromise. As the indications for shoulder surgery expand to include older and more medically complex populations, an updated synthesis of the randomized literature with attention to patient-related risk factors is needed.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to identify patient- and anesthetic-related factors that influence the risk of pulmonary complications. The primary objective is to synthesize current evidence on the pulmonary impact of ISBs in shoulder surgery, with a focus on hemidiaphragmatic paralysis, pulmonary function tests, and post-operative respiratory outcomes.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

A systematic review was performed in accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis guidelines. A comprehensive literature search of electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science) was conducted from database inception through March 23, 2025. The search included a combination of the following terms combined with Boolean operators: “pulmonary function test”; “PFT,” “hemidiaphragmatic paralysis,” “hemidiaphragmatic excursion,” “shoulder surgery,” “orthopaedics,” “arthroplasty,” “interscalene,” “interscalene block,” and “ISB.” Inclusion criteria consisted of Level I randomized controlled clinical trials that were written in English, or with English translation, reporting pulmonary function testing parameters, hemidiaphragmatic paralysis or paresis, and post-operative pulmonary complications following interscalene block performed for orthopedic shoulder surgeries. Non-clinical studies, animal studies, cadaveric studies, review articles, case reports, letters to editors, conference abstracts, studies with overlapping cohorts, and expert opinions were excluded. Two independent authors conducted the initial title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. References from all included studies were reconciled to ensure all relevant studies meeting inclusion criteria were identified.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from included studies by 2 authors and entered into Microsoft Excel (version 16.63; Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) for additional analysis. The extracted data included the first author's name, publication year, patient demographics (age and sex), incidence of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis, pulmonary function testing data, anesthetic utilized, approach utilized for interscalene block, dose, and concentration of anesthetic. Age was dichotomized at 50 years based on known age-related decline in pulmonary function.

Statistical analysis

Data were stratified by patient demographics, anesthetic utilized, concentration, dosage of anesthetic utilized, and approach utilized for interscalene block. Weighted means were calculated based on sample size of patients from the included studies. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated utilizing the standard deviation and weighted means of the included studies assuming a normally distributed population.14 One-way analysis of variance and unpaired t-tests were performed to analyze differences between anesthetic used, dosage, and concentration. Variables such as injection technique, block approach, and catheter use were not analyzed due to variability and inconsistent reporting across included studies. Outcomes were summarized using weighted descriptive statistics across studies; a formal meta-analysis with pooled effect estimates was not performed due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias (RoB) for included randomized controlled trials was assessed independently by 2 reviewers using the Cochrane RoB 1 tool. This tool evaluates potential sources of bias across 7 domains: sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other sources of bias.18 Each domain was rated as low RoB, high RoB, or unclear RoB. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved via consultation from a third reviewer.

Results

The initial search yielded 861 studies for title and abstract review. Of these, 33 studies2, 3, 4,8,9,11, 12, 13,16,17,19,20,22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29,31,33, 34, 35, 36,38,39,41,43,45,46,49,51 met inclusion criteria (Fig. 1), all of which were Level 1 randomized controlled trials (Table I). Sample sizes ranged from 11 to 99 participants, resulting in a total of 1,481 patients included in final analysis (608 females, 848 males, 25 unreported). The average age was 49.5, and average body mass index (BMI) was 26.8. Table II summarizes included patient demographics. Hemidiaphragmatic paralysis was reported in 25 studies, occurring in 53% of cases on average (95% CI [42%, 64%]). Fifteen studies quantified changes in diaphragmatic excursion, demonstrating an average reduction of 48.6% from baseline (95% CI [37.1%, 60.2%]). Eighteen studies reported FEV1 outcomes, with an average reduction of 27.1% from baseline (95% CI [23.0%, 31.1%]). Seventeen studies reported forced vital capacity outcomes, showing an average reduction of 24.8% from baseline (95% CI [21.2%, 28.4%]).

An unpaired t-test demonstrated that patients older than 50 years had a significantly higher incidence of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis compared with those 50 years or younger (P = .029). Anesthetic drug type (P = .061), dosage (P = .768), and concentration (P = .234) were not associated with incidence of paralysis. Similarly, age, dosage, and concentration did not significantly affect changes in diaphragmatic excursion, FEV1, or forced vital capacity (FVC). However, patients receiving levobupivacaine (n = 119) experienced significantly greater reductions in FEV1 (P = .020) and FVC (P = .031) than those receiving other anesthetic agents (n = 1,362). Discussion

This systematic review highlights the pulmonary consequences of ISB, an increasingly common component of multimodal analgesia for shoulder surgery. Across 33 studies, ISB was consistently associated with significant—though typically transient—reductions in pulmonary function, most notably HDP. Hemidiaphragmatic paralysis occurred in approximately 53% of patients, with average reductions of 27% in FEV1, and 25% in FVC, respectively. These impairments are primarily attributable to unintended blockade of the phrenic nerve given its anatomic proximity to the C5–C6 nerve roots. Notably, patient age greater than 50 years was associated with a significantly higher incidence of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis (P = .029). However, no study stratified outcomes by other important patient-specific variables such as BMI, American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, or baseline pulmonary disease, representing a critical gap in the literature.

Our findings are in keeping with similar investigations. Across the literature base, ISB has a widespread association with a decrease in pulmonary function.5, 6, 7,42,50 The benefits of ISB—high patient satisfaction, improved pain control, and reduced opioid consumption—have been well documented.46,51 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses from Abdallah et al,1 Yan et al,48 and Hussain et al21 demonstrated significantly reduced pain with ISB use. Yan et al48 also noted a shorter extubation time and a lower incidence of adverse events compared with general anesthesia alone.1,21,40,48 As a result, ISB firmly maintains a role in multimodal analgesia following shoulder surgery. On a case-by-case basis, analgesic benefits must be weighed against the pulmonary risks.

Patient selection can play a critical role in mitigating risk associated with ISB as the transient HDP is typically well-tolerated in otherwise healthy patients. However, older patients and patients with decreased cardiopulmonary reserve are at higher risk of pulmonary complications—including severe hypoxia and respiratory failure.9,13,33,47 Our findings further support this assertion as patients greater than age 50 years were at a higher risk of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis. Cohort data indicate that asthma, congestive heart failure, and BMI can all contribute to pulmonary complication following ISB.30,37,47 Yet, most randomized trials have not stratified outcomes by BMI, baseline pulmonary disease, or American Society of Anesthesiologists class, limiting insight into which patients are most vulnerable to clinically significant respiratory compromise. Future randomized controlled trials can help quantify risk increases for each comorbidity.

Given the increased risk of pulmonary complications observed in older patients, alternative diaphragm-sparing regional techniques may be preferable including extrafascial injection. Approaches such as suprascapular nerve block, with or without axillary nerve block, have been explored as potential alternatives that may better preserve pulmonary function.8,40 However, evidence remains variable, and our findings did not demonstrate an association between anesthetic parameters and pulmonary outcomes. As such, the role of these alternative strategies in reducing pulmonary complications remains uncertain.

In addition to patient selection, anesthetic parameters may be modified to minimize pulmonary risk. Oliver-Fornies et al32 investigated anesthetic-related factors and their effect on HDP and found a significant decrease in HDP rates when following a low-volume technique of ISB—marking the first identified anesthetic parameter with a direct relationship to HDP incidence. Likewise, Riazi et al36 found that decreasing anesthetic volume from 20 mL to 5 mL reduced the incidence of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis from 100% to 45% and mitigated declines in FEV1, FVC, and oxygen saturation. However, our findings stand in stark contrast to these findings. Given the pooled analysis of our analysis, it is currently unclear if pulmonary outcomes can be effectively improved by modifying anesthetic parameters. Collectively, these findings highlight the interplay of how patient demographics and anesthetic technique conjointly influence the pulmonary impact of ISB.

This systematic review features a robust review of Level I evidence studies, consisting of randomized controlled trials. However, it does have limitations. As a systematic review, this study is limited by variability across included studies, including differences in patient populations, anesthetic agents, dosing, data collection methods, and outcome assessment. This variability makes direct comparisons challenging and may influence the observed findings. Additionally, many studies had relatively small sample sizes, which may limit generalizability. As such, reported values should be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than pooled effect estimates. Furthermore, our ability to identify specific patient-related risk factors was limited as most studies did not stratify patients by cardiopulmonary risk factors, such as obesity, asthma, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Although a RoB assessment was performed, heterogeneity was not formally quantified, and publication bias was not assessed. Lastly, our study was limited to papers written in English or with English translation, which may limit generalizability.

In summary, although ISB predictably reduces pulmonary function through transient phrenic nerve involvement, the clinical impact is often minimal in healthy patients and outweighed by its analgesic benefits. Care can be personalized according to risks and benefits as determined by patient characteristics. However, the lack of standardized criteria for risk stratification and the absence of comorbidity-stratified data limit patient selection.

Conclusion

Interscalene block remains a highly effective technique for perioperative pain control in shoulder surgery, with well-established benefits in minimizing opioid use and improving early recovery. However, it is consistently associated with reductions in objective pulmonary function. Advanced patient age was associated with increased risk of diaphragmatic impairment, while anesthetic parameters did not demonstrate consistent associations with pulmonary outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of patient selection and suggest that further investigation into comorbidity-specific risk stratification may help guide anesthetic decision-making, particularly as surgical indications expand to include more medically complex populations.
