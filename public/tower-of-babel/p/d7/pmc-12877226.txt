Associations of three healthy dietary patterns with homeostatic dysregulation: results from the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort study
Authors: Hongmei Zhang, Quzong Deji, Ning Zhang, Yi Xiang, Yuan Zhang, Jiajie Cai, Tingting Yang, Jianzhong Yin, Yonglan Wei, Xianbin Ding, Xiong Xiao, Xing Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHomeostatic dysregulation (HD), the measure of aging-related physiological dysregulation, serves as an essential intervenable indicator of aging.ObjectiveTo explore the associations of three healthy dietary patterns with HD, investigate the most recommended dietary patterns, and identify the significant beneficial and harmful food groupsMethodsThis prospective cohort study included 8,288 participants aged 30–79 years from the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC), with a female majority (61.6%). Dietary information was obtained through the baseline food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Three dietary patterns were constructed: Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), alternative Mediterranean diets (aMED), and Healthy Diet Score (HDS). HD was constructed based on clinical biomarkers and anthropometric measurements. Follow-up analyses adjusted for baseline data were employed to assess the longitudinal associations of three dietary patterns at baseline with HD at follow-up. Additionally, quantile G-computation was utilized to evaluate the relative contribution of each food group to the association with HD.ResultsOver a follow-up period of 2.0 years, all healthy dietary patterns exhibited negative associations with HD, with βQ5/Q1 = −0.112, 95%CI (−0.172, −0.051) for HDS, with βQ5/Q1 = −0.073, 95%CI (−0.134, −0.012) for aMED, with βQ5/Q1 = −0.047, 95%CI (−0.107,0.014) for DASH. The results of the component analyses revealed that soybean products were the most significant beneficial food group (relative contribution of 24.0%), while alcohol was identified as the major harmful food group (relative contribution of 76.9%).ConclusionHealthy dietary patterns, especially HDS, are negatively associated with HD. Additionally, soybean products and alcohol are the most significant beneficial and detrimental food groups respectively. Developing appropriate nutritional strategies may help reduce the burden of disease and promote healthy aging.Keywords: Diet, Homeostatic dysregulation, Longitudinal analysis

Introduction

During the aging process, the decline in physiological system functions, coupled with the loss of feedforward and feedback mechanisms among interrelated systems, may lead to a progressive dysregulation of multi-system physiological processes [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Physiological dysregulation is recognized as one of the primary factors of aging [6,7]. Homeostatic dysregulation (HD) measures the multi-system physiological dysregulation associated with aging, accounting for biomarkers related to physiological dysregulation and their interactions [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that HD is associated with various aging phenotypes [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Comparatively, HD effectively captures the complex degenerative processes involving multiple organ systems during aging compared to single-indicator measurements [8]. Furthermore, HD score is constructed based on clinically accessible and available biochemical markers, making it suitable for routine screenings [8]. It provides an effective approach for the early identification and intervention of aging [8,13].

Research has suggested that healthy diet may negatively associated with HD [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. However, most studies have focused on the association between specific dietary pattern [14,16,18] and HD, with a notable lack of research systematically evaluating the association between diverse dietary patterns and HD [21]. Specifically, few studies have compared well-established prior patterns such as the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) and alternative Mediterranean diets (aMED) with emerging patterns, such as the Healthy Diet Score (HDS) [23]. Among various recommended dietary patterns, the optimal dietary pattern for coping with aging-related homeostatic dysregulation remains unclear. Furthermore, most previous studies have explored the association between specific food groups and HD [15,17], with little focus on the relative contribution of each food group to HD, which could inform priority recommendations for specific food groups. Moreover, prior research on diet and HD has predominantly been conducted in developed regions [9,14,16,24,25]. Given the substantial socioeconomic disparities, as well as differences in diet and living environments between developed and less-developed regions, particularly less-developed ethnic minority regions (LEMRs), findings from developed regions should be generalized with caution. Thus, in LEMRs, investigating the most recommended dietary patterns and identifying the most beneficial and detrimental food groups for addressing aging-related homeostatic dysregulation may provide a reference for nutritional strategies to promote healthy aging in these regions.

The China Multi-Ethnic Cohort study (CMEC) is a large prospective cohort from southwestern China, which is typically characterized as a less-developed ethnic minority region. We aim to address two research gaps based on CMEC: (1) Investigate whether higher adherence to three dietary patterns (DASH, aMED, and HDS) is negatively associated with HD, and identify the optimal dietary pattern for coping with ageing. (2) Explore the relative contributions of each food group, and identify the most beneficial and detrimental food groups for addressing aging-related homeostatic dysregulation.

Method

Study population

CMEC is an ongoing large-scale community-based prospective cohort study that encompasses five provinces in less-developed southwestern China (Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, Chongqing, and Tibet). The CMEC study population exhibits significant variations in dietary habits, living environments, socioeconomic status, disease patterns and etc. The baseline survey was conducted from May 2018 to September 2019, collecting data from 99,556 participants aged 15–86 years. Subsequently, from August 2020 to July 2021, the first follow-up survey was conducted among 10% of the baseline study participants. A multistage, stratified cluster sampling method was employed to select the study population. The inclusion criteria included (i) age 30–79 years on the day of the investigation, except for Tibetan populations, whose age criterion was 18–79 years due to their shorter life expectancy and dietary habits were significantly different; (ii) permanent residents capable of completing baseline surveys and participating in follow-up studies; and (iii) completing questionnaire interviews, physical examinations and blood tests. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) inability to provide a unique national identification card; (ii) severe physical or mental diseases (e.g., schizophrenia and bipolar disorder); and (iii) refusal to comply with the requirements of the study [26]. The study conducted face-to-face interviews, medical examinations, and laboratory tests to obtain questionnaire data, physical examination data, biological samples, as well as information on the occurrence and diagnosis of diseases from the study participants [26]. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Sichuan University (K2016038, K2020022) and local ethics committees at each participating site. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

In this prospective study with a follow-up period of 2.0 years, among the 9042 participants who took part in the baseline and follow-up surveys and had complete dietary data at baseline and HD information at follow-up, we further excluded individuals with abnormal total energy intake (n = 180), implausible BMI (n = 4), and missing covariates (n = 570). Ultimately, we included 8288 participants with complete diet related data and covariates in baseline survey, as well as indicators utilized for constructing HD in follow-up survey. Detailed enrollment process provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. The missing information of covariates was presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Assessment of dietary patterns

We utilized a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) to obtain participants' dietary habits in the year before the baseline survey [27]. FFQ-related dietary information was collected by well-trained interviewers. The reproducibility and validity of the FFQ were evaluated by conducting repeated FFQs and 24 -h dietary recalls. The FFQ measured the consumption of 18 food groups in the CMEC. Intake for each food group was measured using intake amount (standard portion/grams) or intake frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, yearly), and eventually converted to daily intake in grams.

Based on the consumption information of food groups, we constructed three dietary patterns (HDS, DASH, aMED). In general, we categorized the consumption of each food group into five quintiles, assigned values ranging from 1 to 5, and summed up the scores for each food group within the dietary pattern. Finally, we obtained scores for three dietary patterns for each participant. A higher dietary score indicates greater adherence to specific dietary pattern. We constructed the DASH score using seven food groups (fresh fruit, fresh vegetables, soybean products, dairy products, coarse grain, red and processed meat, and salt) [27]. For red and processed meat as well as salt, we assigned reverse scores (the lowest quintile as 5, the highest quintile as 1), while the other food groups were assigned positive scores (the lowest quintile as 1, the highest quintile as 5). The total score range for DASH was 5−35. We established the aMED score based on eight food groups (fresh vegetables, soybean products, fresh fruit, coarse grain, fish/seafood, MUFA: SFA (the ratio of monounsaturated fatty acids to saturated fatty acids), red and processed meats, and alcohol) [27]. We assigned reverse scores for red and processed meat, while the other food groups (except for alcohol) received positive scores. Alcohol intake was scored higher for moderate consumption, lower for low or excessive intake, with scores ranging from 1 to 5. The total score range for aMED was 5−40. Finally, we developed the HDS based on five healthy food groups (fresh vegetables, soybean products, fresh fruits, fish/seafood, and dairy products) [28]. All food groups were assigned positive scores. The total score range for HDS was 5−25. Details of the construction of the dietary patterns were provided in the Supplementary Method. Supplementary Table S2 showed the scoring criteria for the food groups.

Assessment of homeostatic dysregulation

HD assesses age-related physiological dysfunction by measuring deviations of multiple molecular or cellular biomarkers from their normal values [8]. The magnitude of HD score is closely related to the reference population, which should choose healthy adults. In this study, among the 99556 participants who took part in the baseline survey, participants aged 30–39 years and self-reported free of any underlying diseases were chosen as the reference population.

The process of constructing the HD score was as follows: we select clinical biomarkers and anthropometric measurements that were significantly correlated with chronological age (|r| > 0.1) to construct HD. Ultimately, 15 indicators were included: systolic blood pressure (SBP), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), peak expiratory flow, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol(HDL-c), glycated hemoglobin (HBA1C), triglyceride (TG), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatinine, urea, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and platelet count. Details of biomarkers selection were provided in Supplementary Methods.

Individuals with higher HD were considered to be experiencing greater biological aging.

Assessment of covariates

The covariate data was obtained from baseline questionnaire and physical examination data. We constructed directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) based on the protocol of "Evidence Synthesis for Constructing Directed Acyclic Graphs" (ESC-DAGs) [29] (see Supplementary Figure S2 for the DAG). Following the causal framework and the backdoor criterion, we included the following covariates: age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, education, annual household income, occupation, family history of cardiovascular metabolic diseases, urbanicity, smoking status, total energy intake, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), insomnia symptom, dietary supplement, depressive symptom, anxiety symptom, and beverage consumption. In addition, we adjusted the baseline outcome variables to reduce potential reverse causality. Detailed information on the covariates can be found in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

We described the baseline characteristics across various categories of dietary pattern scores in the study population. Continuous variables were presented as median (25th, 75th percentile), while categorical variables were represented by numbers (percentages). Additionally, we employed t-tests and chi-square tests to compare the differences in baseline characteristics between the current study population and the corresponding entire population with missing covariates.

Firstly, we performed follow-up adjusted for baseline analysis using multiple linear regression model to assess the longitudinal associations of three dietary patterns with HD [30] (See Supplementary Methods for details of follow-up adjusted for baseline analysis). In our study population, HD exhibited a skewed distribution. Therefore, we performed a log transformation on HD and subsequently standardized it. For the control of confounding variables, we adjusted for corresponding covariates based on the constructed DAG, and also adjusted for baseline HD to reduce reverse causation and minimize potential residual confounding.

Secondly, we employed the quantile G-computation (QGC) [31] method (See Supplementary Methods for more details on the QGC construction process) to assess the relative contribution of each food group in the association of each dietary pattern with HD, as well as the relative weights of all food groups in the association with HD. QGC evaluates the positive or negative relative contribution of each food group [31], and has been widely used in epidemiological studies [32,33].

Potential effect modification by sex, age and BMI group on the association between three dietary patterns and HD was examined. To assess the robustness of the results, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we reanalyzed using the corresponding entire-population dataset with imputed missing covariates. Second, we included dietary scores as binary, ternary, and quaternary variables in the model. Third, we explored the cross-sectional associations between each dietary pattern and HD based on baseline data (n = 99556). Fourth, we further excluded participants with self-reported chronic diseases (diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, and stroke) at baseline to avoid potential reverse causality. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We used R version 4.2.1 for all statistical analyses.

Result

General characteristics

We described the baseline characteristics of the study population based on the quintiles of the dietary pattern scores (See Table 1). The median age of the participants was 52 years (44, 59), with a majority being female (61.6%), of Han ethnicity (60.6%), and residing in rural areas (64.3%). Participants with higher adherence to the three dietary patterns (DASH, aMED, HDS) were more likely to be Han ethnicity, reside in urban areas, have a higher level of education and economic status, and engage in the tertiary industry. We compared the follow-up population included in and excluded from this study. The results indicate that our study population is representative (Supplementary Table S5). The comparison between the current complete cases sample and the corresponding entire-population dataset can be found in Supplementary Table S6. The result indicated that there were no significant differences between the two datasets.

Association between three dietary patterns and HD

Table 2 displays the associations between three dietary patterns and HD. Overall, all healthy dietary patterns demonstrated the negative association with HD. Among these, DASH and aMED showed weakly significant or even insignificant associations. In contrast, HDS demonstrated a relatively strong significant association. The estimated sizes of the association between DASH and aMED with HD were comparable, while the HDS demonstrated the strongest negative association with HD. When comparing the highest and lowest quintiles of dietary pattern scores, the association between aMED and HD [βQ5/Q1 = −0.073, 95%CI (−0.134, −0.012)] was slightly stronger than the association between DASH and HD [βQ5/Q1 = −0.047, 95%CI (−0.107, 0.014)]. The aMED exhibited a weakly significant association, while the DASH showed a non-significant association. Notably, when participants transitioned from non-adherence to adherence, HDS demonstrated the strongest significant negative association with HD [βQ5/Q1 = −0.112, 95%CI (−0.172, −0.051)]. Similar results were observed when dietary pattern scores were treated as continuous variables in the models.

Sensitivity analyses

The association between three dietary patterns and HD exhibited robustness, encompassing reanalysis using the entire-population dataset, the inclusion of dietary pattern scores as binary, ternary, and quaternary variables in the model, the exclusion of baseline self-reported chronic diseases, and the exploration of cross-sectional associations (Supplementary Tables S9-14).

Discussion

Summary of main result

Based on a subsample of 8288 participants in the CMEC who participated in both the baseline and follow-up surveys, our analysis revealed that all three dietary patterns (HDS, DASH, aMED) were negatively associated with HD, with HDS demonstrating the strongest association with HD. Furthermore, within the food groups of these dietary patterns, soybean products emerged as the most beneficial food group (relative contribution of 24.0%), while alcohol was the most detrimental food group (relative contribution of 76.9%).

The three dietary patterns are negatively associated with HD and HDS shows strongest beneficial association

Our study aligns with previous research showing that healthy dietary patterns are negatively associated with aging. Although most studies focused on telomeres [34], frailty [35], epigenetic clocks [36], composite biomarkers [37] and other measures of aging, there is no universally accepted gold standard for measuring aging. Different aging metrics capture distinct aspects of the aging process [38]. Physiological dysregulation plays a significant role in aging, and Homeostatic Dysregulation (HD) can effectively reflect this aspect. The HD score, constructed from composite biomarkers, offers good accessibility while maintaining a certain degree of accuracy, making it suitable for research involving large populations. Collectively, our study, along with prior research, suggests that healthy dietary patterns may negatively associated with biological aging.

Furthermore, our study demonstrated that among the three dietary patterns examined, the HDS exhibited the strongest negative association with HD. Diets in LEMRs are highly heterogeneous and shaped by specific cultural preferences. Certain healthy food, such as olive oil, are relatively expensive. The HDS constructed from more accessible food may better address the dietary needs of diverse populations in LEMRs compared to the restrictive diets prevalent in Western contexts. Additionally, previous studies have shown that HDS was a superior predictor of adverse health outcomes, including mortality and cardiovascular events, compared to other dietary patterns like DASH and aMED, with consistent findings observed across over 80 countries worldwide [23].

Additionally, our findings indicates that dietary patterns recommended in developed regions may not represent the optimal dietary choices for LEMRs. Although previous studies have reported various health effects of the DASH [39,40] and the aMED [41,42], these patterns were constructed based on the dietary habits of developed Western countries, emphasizing the avoidance of nutrient overload and excessive food intake [43]. The DASH and aMED focus particularly on limiting the intake of full-fat dairy products, saturated fats, and red and processed meats to reduce the risk of vascular disease and death [44,45]. However, the health effects of these restrictive food vary considerably between developed and less developed regions [43]. Inadequate intake of essential healthy foods and malnutrition represent more pressing challenges in less developed areas [23,46].

Soybean products is the primarily beneficial food group, while alcohol is the major detrimental food group

The component analyses suggested that soybean products was the primarily beneficial food group. Soybean products are major source of plant protein, particularly in low- and middle-income regions where plant proteins account for a higher proportion of the protein supply compared to animal proteins. Studies have shown the benefits of partial replacement of animal proteins with plant proteins for human health and environmental sustainability [47]. Additionally, Soy products are rich in polyphenols, carotenoids, phytosterols, phytic acids, alkaloids, and other phytochemicals [48]. Previous studies demonstrated that polyphenolic compounds in soybean products may exerted multiple health benefits through anti-inflammatory mechanisms and modulation of the gut microbiome [49,50]. These benefits included a reduction in the risk of chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes, as well as deceleration of biological aging [51,52]. Since physiological dysregulation related to aging is closely linked to oxidative stress and persistent low-grade inflammation [53], the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of phytochemicals in soybean products may explain their beneficial associations with HD.

In contrast, our findings indicated that alcohol was the major detrimental food group. Although previous studies reported inconsistent findings regarding the association between alcohol and homeostatic dysregulation [[54], [55], [56], [57]], several studies supported our findings [56], showing positive association between alcohol consumption and homeostatic regulation. However, the underlying mechanisms of these associations remains unclear. Homeostasis dysregulation is a prominent marker of aging [58]. Our recent study found that any level of alcohol consumption seems to be positively associated with acceleration of biological ageing [59]. Understanding the physiological mechanisms linking alcohol consumption to aging might elucidate its role in aging-related homeostatic dysregulation. Potential mechanisms may include processes affecting DNA repair [60], cellular and organ damage caused by alcohol metabolites [61,62], and etc. Further research is needed to explore the association between alcohol and aging-related homeostatic dysregulation, as well as the underlying mechanisms.

Salt is another food group of concern. The CMEC study population exhibits a higher salt intake, and our previous studies have demonstrated that this population has a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, especially hypertension [27]. Consistent with previous studies, our research found a negative association between salt intake and HD [63,64]. Potential mechanisms may involve the inhibition of the renin-angiotensin system due to high salt intake, which subsequently stimulates the pituitary gland to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone. This hormone promotes adrenal synthesis and the secretion of cortisol [64]. Furthermore, elevated cortisol levels have been linked to various aging-related phenotypes, including metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease [65].

Strength and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the association between three dietary patterns and corresponding food groups with HD in LEMRs. Unlike most epidemiological studies that explore the association between dietary patterns and HD using cross-sectional data, our study utilized longitudinal data, which could provide stronger evidence.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the FFQ utilized in our study included 13 major crude food groups rather than specific food items. While this approach did not significantly affect the assessment of dietary patterns, it may impair the accuracy of calculations for specific food fractions (such as MUFA/SFA) and total energy intake. Nonetheless, the results from the 24-h dietary recall showed no significant differences compared to FFQ. In addition, our previous study showed that measurement error generally mitigated the observed association between dietary patterns and disease [66]. Therefore, we believe that these limitations are less likely to significantly impact our main conclusions. Secondly, we lacked rigorous quantification for sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) and lacked information on consumption of nuts. Therefore, we excluded SSBs from the DASH score, and excluded nuts from the aMED and HDS scores. This may result in the constructed dietary indices not fully reflecting true dietary patterns. However, given that consumption of SSBs and nuts is very low in the LEMRs, we believe that the impact of this deficiency on our results is limited. Thirdly, the diets were not assessed directly but rather through scores reflecting resemblance to the three dietary patterns. As a result, there could be overlap between patterns, with the same participants potentially being categorized into multiple groups. This overlap might reveal a tendency toward better eating habits rather than adherence to a specific diet. Fourthly, our reference population was selected to be healthy individuals aged 30–39 years; however, this choice may not be ideal for all biomarkers. Nonetheless, this has been the optimal choice in the CMEC study and identifying a reference population that is the best choice for all biomarkers may be challenging. Fifthly, in this study, the follow-up period was only 2.0 years. However, there is a paucity of relevant evidence in LEMRs, and our longitudinal study offers relatively reliable findings. Sixthly, although we have carefully controlled for the confounders identified in the DAG, the impact of unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, our study population was limited to less-developed ethnic minority regions in southwestern China, and the conclusions should be cautiously generalized to other populations of LEMRs.

Conclusions

Based on longitudinal data from CMEC, our study revealed that healthy dietary patterns (DASH, aMED, HDS), particularly HDS, were negatively associated with HD. Among various food groups, soybean products and alcohol were the most significant beneficial and detrimental food groups in the association of dietary patterns with HD respectively. This research provides a comprehensive exploration of the associations between three dietary patterns and HD, offering dietary recommendations for developing appropriate dietary guidelines to counter increasing disease burden companying with aging and promote healthy aging in LEMRs.
