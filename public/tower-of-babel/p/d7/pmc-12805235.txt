Emergomyces canadensis: A rare cause of disseminated mycosis in North America
Authors: Randy F. Ko, Melanie S. Jun, Sheshanna Phan, Darren M. Kempton, Marcos Burgos, Marissa A. Tafoya, Leonor A. Echevarria, Charles M. Phillips
Published: 2025

Introduction

Emergomycosis is an emerging systemic infection caused by a thermally dimorphic fungus of the genus Emergomyces. Five fungi are implicated, including Emergomyces canadensis (E canadensis), Emergomyces pasteurianus, Emergomyces africanus, Emergomyces orientalis, and Emergomyces europaeus. While cases of Emergomycosis have also been reported in Europe, Africa, and Asia, all cases reported in North America have been caused by E canadensis, with 2 cases in the southwest region of the United States and 3 cases in Canada.

Emergomycosis is most commonly associated with respiratory and cutaneous manifestations that share similar features with those of histoplasmosis.1,2 Symptoms typically occur among immunocompromised individuals with a reported mortality rate of 48% to 51%.3,4 Diagnosis involves multiple modalities of testing including sampling the affected tissue (eg, skin or lung) for microscopic evaluation to detect small (2-5 μm) intracellular and extracellular organisms which are then classified as round or oval yeast by performing special stains for periodic acid Schiff and Grocott’s methenamine silver. Yeast speciation is made by evaluating the culture after maturation or molecular testing on the tissue-embedded paraffin block, as it was in this case, at a specialized laboratory. There are no specific serologic tests for Emergomyces.5,6

Emergomycosis most frequently occurs among immunocompromised patients, particularly those with lymphoma or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), or in elderly patients on immunosuppressive therapy. Early recognition can be difficult because Emergomycosis mimics other endemic mycoses both clinically and histologically, and selecting definitive therapy is further complicated by limited susceptibility data. While few cases have been reported in North America, the first documented case of Emergomycosis in New Mexico appeared in 2015 in an HIV-positive patient.5 We present this rare case in New Mexico to increase awareness of Emergomycosis.

Case report

A 90-year-old male with untreated small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL) and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction presented with a 1-month duration of pruritic, indurated, and ulcerated purple nodules scattered on the trunk, face, and bilateral extremities (Fig 1). Two months earlier, a 10 mm left upper lobe pulmonary nodule was incidentally found on a computed tomography scan while the patient was hospitalized for decompensated heart failure. Upon repeat imaging 12 days later, the pulmonary nodule had grown to 14 mm × 10 mm. While awaiting a biopsy of the pulmonary nodule, the patient developed the aforementioned cutaneous lesions. Laboratory tests revealed a normal complete blood count and were negative for Coccidioides and Blastomyces serologies, cryptococcal antigen, histoplasma urine antigen, and 1,3-β-D-glucan. Three weeks following the biopsy, fungal cultures from the skin and pulmonary nodule grew on potato flake agar. Colonies were slow-growing, initially white with faint red-brown pigment becoming tan-white with yellow hues upon maturation. Fungal polymerase chain reaction sequencing identified E canadensis, performed on the paraffin block containing the skin biopsy, confirming the diagnosis of disseminated Emergomycosis. Antifungal susceptibility testing demonstrated sensitivity to amphotericin B, itraconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole, and isavuconazole.

The patient was referred to the infectious disease service and started on outpatient itraconazole (200 mg 3 times a day for 3 days and then 200 mg 2 times a day) due to concern for disseminated fungal infection (eg, histoplasmosis, blastomycosis). Upon culture-confirmed Emergomycosis diagnosis, the patient was admitted for intravenous liposomal amphotericin B treatment. The pulmonary nodule decreased in size by approximately 32%. Due to worsening heart failure and acute kidney injury, the patient was transitioned to posaconazole 300 mg 2 times a day for 2 doses, followed by posaconazole 300 mg daily. The patient’s lesions showed clinical improvement, with cutaneous nodules regressing. Ulcerated lesions demonstrated healing, and some resolved almost completely, becoming clinically unapparent. There was also resolution of ecchymosis on the abdomen and left chest. The patient reported that the pruritus associated with his lesions grew tolerable and decreased in size. The patient continued this dosage until death 3 months later due to heart failure.

Discussion

We present a unique case of Emergomycosis with features that highlight a rare infection in a vulnerable population. To this day, there remain few reports of Emergomycosis (Table I). Currently, E canadensis is found exclusively in North America, with 2 other cases occurring in the southwest region of the United States.5,7 Further investigations are necessary to better understand this systemic infection, warranting a higher awareness of Emergomycosis in the differential diagnosis of any immunocompromised patient with concurrent HIV infection, solid organ transplant, hematological malignancy, and/or immunosuppressant use that resembles a clinical presentation for histoplasmosis. A systematic review of 77 cases of Emergomycosis found skin lesions in 84% of patients and fever in 60%.4 Skin lesions were more common among HIV-positive individuals than among HIV-negative individuals, even in the presence of other immunocompromising conditions. Radiologic findings occurred in 75% of cases, with lung opacities and infiltrates in 60%. The current patient, who had SLL but was not receiving chemotherapy, developed cutaneous lesions without preceding fever. Although he reported dyspnea, it remains unclear whether it was attributable to Emergomycosis or his decompensated heart failure. Recognition of identical intracellular and extracellular budding yeasts in both cutaneous and pulmonary specimens determined the diagnosis and permitted appropriate treatment with posaconazole.

This case highlights the importance of diagnosing and visualizing cutaneous lesions commonly found in Emergomycosis, due to the high prevalence of cutaneous lesions in affected patients.
