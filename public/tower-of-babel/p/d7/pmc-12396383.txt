Ventricular fibrillation due to coronary vasospasm triggered by hyperventilation in a patient with panic disorder: case report
Authors: Shota Takimoto, Shinichiro Masuda, Shuzou Tanimoto, Nobuhiko Ogata, Takaaki Isshiki, Stefano Bordignon, Leo Bergau, Pok-Tin Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPanic attacks can trigger hyperventilation, which has been associated with the induction of coronary vasospasm. The resulting vasospasm may cause myocardial ischaemia and trigger life-threatening arrhythmias. Patients with panic disorders experiencing hyperventilation sometimes present with symptoms similar to those of acute coronary syndrome, making it difficult to determine their origin.Case summaryA 37-year-old man with a history of panic disorder was transported to our institute after he experienced hyperventilation during a panic attack and developed chest pain and a subsequent loss of consciousness. During transportation, ventricular fibrillation was observed on electrocardiography, and sinus rhythm was successfully restored with defibrillation. Suspecting hyperventilation-induced coronary vasospasm, a hyperventilation test was performed, which induced 90% coronary spasm accompanied by chest pain. An acetylcholine (ACh) provocation test revealed total occlusion of the left main bifurcation, with significant ischaemic ST-T elevation. Medication with a calcium channel blocker (nifedipine 40 mg per day), nitrate (isosorbide dinitrate 40 mg per day), and statin (rosuvastatin 20 mg per day) was initiated. Three months later, a repeat ACh provocation test performed to assess response to the medications revealed 90% stenosis with ischaemic ST-T changes and chest discomfort. Subsequently, a subcutaneous cardioverter defibrillator was implanted to address refractory vasospastic angina.DiscussionHerein, ventricular fibrillation was likely caused by coronary spasm induced by hyperventilation during the panic attack. Regarding the management of patients with panic disorder who present with chest pain, clinicians should carefully assess the symptoms and consider hyperventilation-induced coronary spasm as a differential diagnosis.Keywords: Panic disorder, Coronary vasospasm, Hyperventilation, Ventricular fibrillation, Case report

Introduction

Hyperventilation is known to be able to induce coronary artery spasm, which can cause life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias and lead to sudden cardiac death in some patients.1,2 Hyperventilation is a common manifestation in patients with panic disorders.3 Since the presentation of panic attack-induced hyperventilation can mimic those of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), differentiating between the two can be clinically challenging. Herein, we describe the case of a patient with panic disorder who presented with ventricular fibrillation (VF) due to hyperventilation-induced coronary spasm.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old man with a history of panic disorder and no other significant medical history developed hyperventilation accompanied by chest pain and loss of consciousness. At the time of initial contact with the emergency services, the patient had a palpable carotid pulse and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of E4V2M1. However, during transportation, he developed pulseless electrical activity (PEA), prompting immediate initiation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation. The PEA subsequently deteriorated into VF as documented on electrocardiography (ECG) (see Supplementary Material S1: Figure 1). Defibrillation promptly restored sinus rhythm. On admission, his GCS score was E4V5M6, blood pressure was 128/74 mmHg, heart rate was 104 beats/min, and percutaneous oxygen saturation was 99% with supplemental oxygen at a rate of 10 L/min. His physical examination results were unremarkable. The ECG at admission showed sinus rhythm with poor R progression in leads V1 to V3 (see Supplementary Material S1: Figure 2). Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 35%, with hypokinesis from the anteroseptal to the anterior wall of the left ventricle. The serum troponin-I level was significantly elevated at 0.225 ng/mL. Laboratory findings at admission are presented in Supplementary Material S1: Table 1. Owing to suspected non-ST elevation myocardial infarction, urgent coronary angiography (CAG) was performed, which revealed no flow-limiting lesions or significant stenosis requiring urgent revascularisation (Figure 1). The patient had a history of frequent hyperventilation followed by chest pain during panic attacks. Based on this sequence of events, hyperventilation-induced coronary spasm leading to VF was suspected. Therefore, hyperventilation and acetylcholine (ACh) provocation tests were performed in the catheterization laboratory in the early morning, 7 days after admission. After a 5 Fr sheath was inserted into the right femoral artery, the hyperventilation test was conducted at a rate of 30 breaths per minute for 7 minutes under continuous monitoring of sinus rhythm using ECG and blood pressure.4 Following the hyperventilation test, arterial blood pH increased from 7.44 to 7.64, and the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) decreased from 33.1 to 17.5 mmHg. As per the patient, the chest pain experienced during the hyperventilation test was identical to that experienced immediately before the loss of consciousness. CAG performed immediately after the hyperventilation test revealed 99% stenosis at the left circumflex artery and a high lateral branch without ischaemic changes in the ECG (Figure 2) (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). Subsequently, the ACh provocation test was conducted.5,6 CAG performed after the administration of 20 μg ACh into the left coronary artery showed total occlusion at the left main bifurcation, with ST-segment elevation in aVL and V2-V5 leads (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). Chest pain similar to that experienced during panic attacks was noted. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with multi-vessel vasospastic angina. Medication with a calcium channel blocker (nifedipine 40 mg per day), nitrate (isosorbide dinitrate 40 mg per day), and statin (rosuvastatin 20 mg per day) was initiated accordingly. Delayed post-contrast-enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed sub-endocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in the anterior wall of the left ventricle (Figure 3), suggesting chronic myocardial infarction secondary to repeated coronary spasm. The patient's clinical course was uneventful following the initiation of the medications, and he was discharged on day 15 of hospitalization. He remained event-free during the 3-month follow-up with a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator (LifeVest®, Zoll Medical, Chelmsford, MA, USA). After 3 months, hyperventilation and ACh provocation tests were repeated to assess the response to the medications. His arterial pH increased from 7.46 to 7.59, and PaCO2 decreased from 33.5 to 22.7 mmHg after 7 min of hyperventilation. The hyperventilation test did not induce significant coronary spasm or ischaemic changes in ECG (Figure 4). After the administration of 100 μg ACh into the left coronary artery, diffuse multi-vessel coronary spasm with significant ST-segment elevation in the anterior leads was observed on ECG (Figure 4) (see Supplementary material 2). As a secondary prevention strategy for VF in the setting of refractory vasospastic angina, a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD) was implanted. Following discharge, the serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor (duloxetine 20 mg per day) was initiated in addition to the as-required use of diazepam. The patient had attended outpatient follow-up visits every 3 months. At the 12-month follow-up, LVEF remained unchanged, indicating chronic left ventricular dysfunction, likely caused by recurrent coronary vasospasm. His clinical course remained uneventful over an 18-month follow-up period.

Discussion

This case highlights the importance of careful examination and management of patients with panic disorder presenting with hyperventilation, which can induce coronary vasospasm. Intensive treatment for panic attacks and coronary artery spasm has been suggested to be important to prevent recurrence. Clinicians should consider coronary spasm as a differential diagnosis for patients with panic disorders presenting with chest pain.

Coronary vasospasm is associated with the physiological effects of hyperventilation on coronary arteries.7 Hyperventilation significantly reduces blood carbon dioxide levels and induces various physiological changes. The consequent respiratory alkalosis can lead to coronary vasospasm by increasing calcium ion levels and calcium ion sensitivity in the smooth vascular muscles.7 Hyperventilation frequently occurs during anxiety or panic attacks.3 Chest pain resulting from coronary vasospasm can exacerbate anxiety, leading to a vicious cycle in which hyperventilation worsens vasospasm, and the pain exacerbates anxiety and hyperventilation.

Conversely, hyperventilation can be induced by coronary spasm,8 further complicating the differentiation between the two conditions. In the present case, it was initially unclear whether the chest pain originated from the panic attack or coronary spasm. Because the patient consistently experienced hyperventilation preceding chest pain before hospitalization, we suspected hyperventilation-induced coronary spasm. The hyperventilation test reproduced the chest pain, and severe stenosis was observed in CAG performed after the test, providing visual confirmation of the involvement of hyperventilation and coronary spasm. Furthermore, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated sub-endocardial LGE in the anterior wall of the left ventricle, along with a reduced LVEF. These findings suggest that recurrent episodes of hyperventilation-induced coronary artery spasm may have led to myocardial infarction. Although the clinical course of the patient was uneventful after initiating medication, coronary spasm was induced even after adequate pharmacological treatment, prompting S-ICD implantation according to current guidelines.6 In addition, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (enalapril 2.5 mg per day) and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (spironolactone 25 mg per day) were initiated as part of secondary prevention therapy after myocardial infarction. Identifying hyperventilation as a mechanism of coronary spasm may inform future treatment strategies. In this case, neither electrophysiological study nor substrate modification was performed prior to S-ICD implantation. The patient experienced VF in the setting of chest pain, with coronary spasm strongly suspected as the underlying cause. No monomorphic ventricular tachycardia, including non-sustained episodes, was observed during hospitalization or follow-up. In the absence of secondary arrhythmic substrates, S-ICD implantation was considered the most appropriate therapeutic approach.

In addition to pharmacological therapy with coronary vasodilators, intensive management of the panic attacks that precipitated hyperventilation may have contributed to further stabilization of symptoms. While diazepam can be effective when used as required, its long-term or prophylactic administration is not recommended because of concerns regarding dependence and other adverse effects.9 After consultation with a specialist, a serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor, recommended as a first-line pharmacological treatment for panic disorder, was initiated.9 Pharmacological therapy for panic disorder has been reported to have a relatively high response rate, and appropriate pharmacotherapy can therefore suppress panic attacks,9 which, in patients such as the present case, may help to prevent hyperventilation. Furthermore, psychological relief obtained from identifying the underlying mechanism of coronary spasm may also have contributed to the patient's favourable clinical course.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we describe a case of hyperventilation-induced coronary spasm in a patient with panic disorder. Panic disorders can cause hyperventilation, leading to coronary spasm. Intensive treatments for panic attacks and coronary artery spasm are important to prevent recurrence. In patients with panic disorders presenting with chest pain, clinicians should carefully assess the symptoms and consider coronary spasm as a differential diagnosis.
