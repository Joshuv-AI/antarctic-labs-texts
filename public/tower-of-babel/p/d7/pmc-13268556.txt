Awareness and adoption intent of cutaneous interstitial fluid-based biomarkers in autoimmune dermatoses among healthcare students: A descriptive cross-sectional study
Authors: Geetha Kandasamy, Khalid Orayj, Rayah Asiri, Asma M. Alshahrani, Asma Alzaydani, Rajalakshimi Vasudevan, Habab A. Osman, Hassabelrasoul Elfadil, Yahya Sharif, Karema Abu-Elfotuh, Vasudevan Mani, Kousalya Prabahar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cutaneous interstitial fluid (ISF) is a minimally invasive biofluid that reflects clinically relevant biochemical and inflammatory biomarkers comparable to blood, highlighting its potential role in autoimmune and inflammatory dermatoses. However, effective clinical translation of ISF-based diagnostics depends not only on technological validation but also on the awareness and readiness of future healthcare professionals. To assess healthcare students’ knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and willingness to adopt cutaneous ISF-based biomarker technologies for autoimmune dermatoses, and to identify factors associated with adoption intent. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 282 healthcare students using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. Data were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means ± standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Pearson correlation and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to examine associations and identify predictors of willingness to adopt ISF biomarkers. Among participants, 36.2% had heard of cutaneous ISF diagnostics and 27.3% could identify a specific ISF biomarker, with mean knowledge scores indicating low to moderate awareness (range: 1.57 ± 0.72 to 2.06 ± 0.83). Despite limited awareness, attitudes were favorable, with 68.8% perceiving ISF as a promising approach (mean 3.40 ± 1.05) and 73.0% expressing trust in its scientific validity (mean 3.63 ± 1.05). Willingness to adopt ISF diagnostics was high, particularly in the absence of cost constraints (73.4%, mean 3.94 ± 1.03) and with access to training (72.7%, mean 3.94 ± 1.03). Support for curriculum integration was also strong (mean scores: 3.95 ± 1.03 and 3.99 ± 1.00; Cohen d = 0.95–0.99). Knowledge and attitudes were positively correlated with adoption intent (r = 0.42–0.51). Multivariable analysis identified attitudes (β = 0.62, P < .001) and knowledge (β = 0.18, P < .001) as significant predictors, explaining 47% of the variance (R2 = 0.47). A clear gap exists between limited knowledge and strong readiness to adopt ISF biomarker diagnostics among healthcare students. Despite low awareness, participants demonstrated positive attitudes, confidence in scientific validity, and willingness to pursue training and future use. These findings highlight the importance of curricular integration and targeted education to support informed adoption of ISF diagnostics in clinical practice.

1. Introduction

Autoimmune dermatological disorders, including Psoriasis, Vitiligo, and Cutaneous lupus erythematosus, are chronic immune-mediated conditions characterized by dysregulated inflammatory pathways, recurrent disease activity, and significant psychosocial burden. Psoriasis is estimated to affect approximately 0.09% to 11.4% of the global population, as reported by the World Health Organization,[1] with epidemiological modeling studies further demonstrating substantial regional variation and higher prevalence in certain populations, including estimates up to ~8.5% among adults in Northern Europe.[1,2] Vitiligo, similarly, is now recognized as a systemic autoimmune disorder with a complex and lifelong clinical course.[3] These epidemiological patterns highlight the need for diagnostic modalities capable of supporting early detection, localized disease monitoring, and longitudinal assessment of inflammatory activity.

Traditional diagnostic approaches including clinical examination, skin biopsy, and serum biomarkers have notable limitations. Skin biopsy, while considered the gold standard, is invasive and impractical for repeated evaluations.[4] Blood-based biomarkers often fail to capture localized immune activity within affected skin, as systemic cytokine levels may not accurately reflect cutaneous inflammation.[5] Increasing evidence indicates that autoimmune dermatoses are driven by localized immune dysregulation within the skin, characterized by cytokine and chemokine pathways involving IL-17, IL-23, IFN-γ, and CXCL9/CXCL10, which orchestrate keratinocyte–immune cell interactions and sustain inflammatory responses.[6] These features underscore the need for biofluids that more closely represent in situ skin pathology.

Biomarkers are increasingly recognized as essential tools in modern healthcare, improving disease management by enhancing understanding of underlying mechanisms, refining diagnostic accuracy, predicting clinical outcomes, and enabling ongoing monitoring of treatment response. They are particularly valuable when they offer clinically meaningful information that supplements routine clinical assessment.[7] Because autoimmune dermatoses involve dynamic inflammatory changes, biomarker-based tools are uniquely suited to support precise evaluation of localized immune activity.

Cutaneous interstitial fluid (ISF), a biofluid within the dermal extracellular matrix, is an emerging minimally invasive medium for dermatological diagnostics. In vitiligo, cytokine profiling of ISF and related skin-derived fluids shows elevated levels of IFN-γ–associated chemokines such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, along with granzyme B, in both lesional and non-lesional skin. These findings highlight the IFN-γ–CXCL10 axis as a key pathway in immune-mediated melanocyte destruction and indicate ongoing local and systemic immune activation in vitiligo. Importantly, ISF cytokine signatures correspond more closely with local cutaneous immune activity than systemic measurements, making ISF an attractive candidate for disease-specific monitoring. Chemokines such as CXCL9 and CXCL10 key indicators of disease activity and therapeutic response, are readily detectable in dermal ISF.[8]

Recent technological progress has further strengthened the clinical feasibility of ISF-based diagnostics. Microneedle devices now allow minimally invasive, low-pain extraction of dermal ISF while effectively recovering clinically relevant biomolecules.[9] Advanced designs, including 3-dimensional tilted microneedle arrays, have improved ISF yield without compromising patient comfort.[10] Wearable biosensors capable of continuous, real-time ISF cytokine and metabolite detection through microfluidic or electrochemical platforms represent another significant advancement.[11] Moreover, vacuum-assisted microneedle systems have demonstrated enhanced ISF recovery efficiency.[12] Consistent findings showing strong concordance between serum biomarkers and dermal ISF further support its potential as a minimally invasive alternative to venous blood sampling.[13] However, the successful translation of ISF-based diagnostic technologies into routine clinical practice depends not only on scientific advances but also on the readiness of future healthcare providers to understand, interpret, and apply these biomarkers effectively.

For diagnostic innovations to achieve real-world impact, clinicians must possess adequate knowledge of biomarker biology, clinical relevance, and limitations. As emphasized by Jain,[14,15] biomarkers influence clinical decision-making only when healthcare providers are able to apply them meaningfully. This requirement is particularly relevant for ISF-based diagnostics, which represent a departure from conventional blood-based or histopathological testing and require familiarity with skin-specific immune processes, sampling technologies, and interpretation frameworks.[16] Previous research examining awareness and attitudes toward diagnostic innovations among healthcare students has largely focused on established or systemic biomarker platforms, such as blood-based, salivary, or imaging-based diagnostics. These studies commonly employ knowledge attitude practice (KAP) frameworks and consistently demonstrate positive attitudes toward innovation alongside important knowledge gaps and training-related barriers. However, such studies do not address emerging, tissue-specific biomarker technologies that rely on localized pathophysiology and novel sampling methods, such as cutaneous ISF-based diagnostics.

Emerging research on biomarker-based diagnostics among healthcare students reveals important patterns that are likely relevant to ISF technologies. A recent study conducted in Saudi Arabia on salivary biomarkers reported that although nearly two-thirds of students had heard of saliva-based diagnostic tools, fewer than half could accurately identify specific biomarkers or their clinical applications.[17] These findings highlight that awareness alone does not ensure readiness to adopt diagnostic innovations, particularly when technologies differ substantially from conventional diagnostic paradigms.

To date, no study has evaluated healthcare students’ knowledge, perceptions, or adoption intent specifically toward cutaneous ISF-based biomarkers for autoimmune dermatoses. This represents a critical gap in the literature, as ISF-based platforms are uniquely positioned to transform dermatological diagnostics by enabling minimally invasive, skin-localized immune monitoring. Unlike systemic biomarkers, ISF-based diagnostics require an understanding of cutaneous immunopathology, dermatology-specific biomarkers, and emerging microneedle and biosensor technologies. Assessing readiness for such innovations therefore extends existing KAP research into a novel and clinically significant diagnostic domain.

Despite growing scientific and technological progress, awareness and adoption readiness regarding ISF-based diagnostics among healthcare students remain largely unexplored. Healthcare students in medicine, pharmacy, nursing, dentistry, and allied health disciplines represent the future multidisciplinary workforce responsible for implementing diagnostic innovations across diverse clinical settings, including dermatology clinics, primary care, and community pharmacies. Understanding discipline-specific variations in awareness, perceptions, and adoption intent is essential for guiding curriculum development, interdisciplinary training, and implementation strategies.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate healthcare students’ knowledge, attitudes, perceptions (KAP), and willingness to adopt cutaneous interstitial fluid (ISF) based biomarker technologies for autoimmune dermatoses. By focusing on an emerging, skin-specific diagnostic paradigm rather than established biomarker platforms, this study advances existing awareness research and provides early insights into the educational and implementation needs required for successful clinical translation of ISF-based diagnostics.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted from November 11 to January 10, 2026 to assess healthcare students’ knowledge, attitudes, perceived barriers, and adoption intent toward ISF biomarkers for autoimmune dermatological diseases.

2.2. Study setting and population

The study targeted undergraduate students enrolled in the Medicine, Pharmacy, Dentistry, Nursing, and Applied Health Sciences programs at King Khalid University, Abha, Saudi Arabia.

2.2.1. Inclusion criteria

Participants were eligible if they were undergraduate male or female students aged 18 years or older, enrolled in one of the designated healthcare programs, had sufficient English proficiency to understand and complete the survey, and were willing to provide informed consent.

2.2.2. Exclusion criteria

Students were excluded if they refused to participate, submitted incomplete questionnaires, or were enrolled in non-healthcare disciplines such as business, engineering, computer science, English, or related fields. Individuals with advanced expertise in diagnostic technologies, including laboratory personnel involved in biomarker research and licensed healthcare professionals currently engaged in clinical practice, were also excluded.

2.3. Sampling strategy and sample size

A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit participants. A minimum sample size was estimated prior to data collection using conventional power parameters, including a 2-sided α level of 0.05, 80% statistical power, and a moderate effect size (d = 0.5). Based on this estimation, at least 221 participants were required, and 282 participants were included in the final analysis.

2.4. Data collection tool

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was developed following standard KAP survey guidelines[18,19] and adapted to assess healthcare students’ knowledge, attitudes, perceived barriers, and willingness to adopt cutaneous ISF biomarker diagnostics for autoimmune dermatoses. The questionnaire consisted of 6 sections:

Section 1: Demographic information such as age, gender, academic program, year of study, and grade point average (GPA).

Section 2: Assessed where students had learned about biomarkers or ISF technologies (e.g., scientific journals, university courses, social media).

Section 3: Knowledge (awareness): Comprised 5 items evaluating students’ familiarity with ISF, its biomarker components, and its relevance to autoimmune dermatological diseases. Each knowledge item was scored as one point for a correct response and zero points for incorrect or “don’t know” answers. The total knowledge score was computed by summing the 5 item responses. In accordance with WHO KAP guidelines,[19] knowledge levels were categorized using percentage-based cutoffs: ≥75% as high awareness, 50% to 74% as moderate awareness, and <50% as low awareness. Based on the 5-item scale used in this study, these thresholds corresponded to scores of 4 to 5 (high), 3 (moderate), and 0 to 2 (low).

Section 4: Attitude and perception: Included 6 statements assessing students’ beliefs regarding the promise, validity, acceptability, and potential concerns associated with ISF-based diagnostics.

Section 5: Barriers and willingness to adopt ISF-based diagnostics: captured perceived challenges (curricular gaps, regulatory issues, cost) and students’ readiness to integrate ISF biomarker testing into future clinical practice, as well as interest in training or workshops.

Section 6: Willingness for curriculum integration and educational readiness: This section included 2 items evaluating students’ support for incorporating ISF biomarker concepts and practical training into undergraduate healthcare curricula. Items in Sections 4 to 6 (attitude and perception; barriers and willingness to adopt ISF-based diagnostics; willingness for curriculum integration and educational readiness) were measured using a 5-point Likert scale (strongly disagree – 1, disagree – 2, neutral – 3, agree – 4, strongly agree – 5). higher scores indicated greater awareness, more positive attitudes, fewer perceived barriers, and stronger willingness to adopt or learn ISF-based diagnostic technologies. For descriptive analysis across all 3 sections, positive responses were defined as the combined “agree” and “strongly agree” categories.

2.5. Pilot testing, validity, and reliability

A pilot study with 20 healthcare students assessed questionnaire clarity, cultural appropriateness, and usability, leading to minor revisions. Face and content validity were established through expert review aligned with current literature on cutaneous interstitial fluid biomarkers and autoimmune dermatoses, with input from clinical pharmacy faculty, dermatology-related clinicians, and medical education specialists to ensure scientific accuracy, clinical relevance, and conceptual clarity. Internal consistency reliability was evaluated for each domain. The knowledge subscale (dichotomous items) showed acceptable reliability using the Kuder–Richardson formula 20 (KR-20; 0.78). The attitude and perception (Cronbach α = 0.91) and barriers (Cronbach α = 0.89) scales demonstrated good internal consistency. As the curriculum integration domain comprised only 2 Likert-scale items, reliability was assessed using inter-item correlation, which indicated strong consistency (Spearman r = 0.78).

2.6. Data collection procedure

Data were gathered through a web-based questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. Access to the survey was provided via university email, official learning platforms, QR codes shared during classes, and commonly used student communication platforms (e.g., WhatsApp). Electronic informed consent was obtained prior to participation, and data were collected anonymously and stored securely with restricted access.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp.). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. Likert-scale responses assessing knowledge, attitudes, perceived barriers, curriculum readiness, and willingness to adopt cutaneous ISF biomarkers were summarized using mean ± standard deviation. Pearson correlation examined relationships among study constructs, and multivariable linear regression identified predictors of willingness to adopt ISF diagnostics, reported as standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and explained variance (R2). A 2-tailed P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

2.8. Ethics approval

The study received ethical clearance from the King Khalid University Research Ethics Committee (Approval number: KKU-135-2025-31; HAPO-06-B-001). Informed consent was obtained electronically or in writing before participation. The survey does not collect any personally identifiable information, and all responses were kept strictly confidential.

4. Discussion

This study aimed to assess healthcare students’ knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and willingness to adopt cutaneous interstitial fluid (ISF)-based biomarker technologies for autoimmune dermatoses, and to identify factors associated with adoption intent. The findings revealed that although knowledge of ISF biomarkers was generally low, with over half of the participants demonstrating limited awareness, attitudes toward ISF diagnostics were strongly positive, with the majority recognizing its potential, scientific validity, and noninvasive advantages. Additionally, willingness to adopt ISF-based diagnostics was high, with more than 70% of students expressing readiness for future use and training, despite identifying curriculum gaps and regulatory concerns as key barriers. Multivariable analysis further demonstrated that attitudes were the strongest predictor of adoption intent, followed by knowledge, while demographic factors were not significant. These findings highlight a critical gap between awareness and readiness, emphasizing the need for targeted educational strategies to facilitate the successful integration of ISF-based diagnostics into future clinical practice.

Although only 36.2% of students reported familiarity with ISF as a diagnostic modality, participants consistently expressed strong trust, favorable perceptions, and willingness to adopt ISF-based diagnostics. This pattern underscores the early translational stage of ISF technologies within healthcare education and suggests that limited educational exposure, rather than resistance to innovation, is the primary barrier to adoption. Despite being conducted at a single Saudi university, these findings reflect broader educational trends relevant to healthcare students globally.

Precision medicine and treat-to-target strategies have reshaped the management of autoimmune dermatoses by enabling biomarker-guided, individualized care.[20-23] Recent reviews further reinforce this paradigm in atopic dermatitis, highlighting the importance of endotype-based stratification and biomarker-driven decision-making.[24] Despite these advances, the present study identified limited awareness of biomarker-based diagnostics and cutaneous ISF among healthcare students. Fewer than 40% recognized autoimmune disease detection through biomarkers, and only one-third were aware of ISF as a diagnostic sample. Although some students acknowledged that ISF contains biomarkers, applied knowledge and awareness of its minimally invasive diagnostic advantage remained low.

Previous experimental studies have established that dermal and cutaneous interstitial fluid contains clinically relevant proteins, inflammatory mediators, and cytokines, supporting its physiological validity as a diagnostic medium in immune-mediated and autoimmune conditions.[25-27] In contrast to this growing experimental evidence, awareness among healthcare students in the present study remained modest. Although 51.1% correctly recognized that biomarkers can be present within ISF, only 27.3% were able to identify at least 1 specific ISF biomarker, reflecting limited applied knowledge. The large effect size observed for this knowledge gap (Cohen d = 2.05) highlights a substantial disconnect between scientific advancement and student-level understanding.

In the present study, awareness of cutaneous ISF as a diagnostic medium was limited, with only 36.2% of students reporting familiarity and 32.3% recognizing its minimally invasive advantage over blood sampling. This contrasts with recent evidence demonstrating that microneedle-based, vacuum-assisted skin patches can rapidly and reliably extract clinically meaningful volumes of ISF suitable for biomarker analysis.[28] Although 51.1% acknowledged that ISF contains biomarkers, applied knowledge was low, as only 27.3% could identify a specific biomarker. These findings highlight a gap between technological readiness and educational exposure, underscoring the need to integrate emerging ISF sampling technologies into healthcare curricula.

Despite these knowledge limitations, attitudes toward ISF diagnostics were notably positive. Approximately 69% to 75% of participants viewed ISF as a promising diagnostic alternative, trusted its scientific validity, and perceived its noninvasive nature as enhancing acceptance. Mean attitude scores consistently exceeded 3.4 on a 5-point scale, with median responses of 4 and small-to-moderate effect sizes, indicating stable and meaningful agreement rather than polarized responses. This pattern suggests that healthcare students are receptive to ISF-based diagnostics even in the absence of detailed prior knowledge, aligning with early adoption trends observed for other minimally invasive diagnostic innovations.

Advances in CMOS-based point-of-care diagnostic platforms illustrate the broader shift toward compact, portable systems capable of detecting diverse biomarkers outside centralized laboratory settings.[29] Similarly, recent microneedle-based sensor technologies enable painless access to dermal ISF while preserving molecular fidelity.[7] In the present study, students’ strong confidence in ISF accuracy (65.2%) and comfort with sampling methods (63.1%), despite limited familiarity, reflects intuitive alignment with these technological trends. This reinforces the idea that positive perceptions may precede formal knowledge acquisition in emerging diagnostic domains.

Dermatologic biomarker research increasingly emphasizes minimally invasive sampling strategies such as tape stripping, microneedle extraction, and ISF analysis as alternatives to conventional skin biopsies, particularly in inflammatory dermatoses including psoriasis and atopic dermatitis.[30-32] These approaches have demonstrated molecular relevance while improving patient acceptability and feasibility in clinical settings.[33-35] However, consistent with early-stage translational challenges, our findings indicate that only about one-third of students recognized ISF sampling as less invasive than venipuncture, suggesting an incomplete understanding of its comparative clinical advantages.

In this context, the present study reveals a clear translational and educational gap. Although ISF-based diagnostics are supported by expanding evidence from microneedle technologies, molecular profiling platforms, and dermatologic biomarker research,[31,36] awareness and applied knowledge among healthcare students remained limited. This mirrors early adoption gaps reported for other emerging dermatologic and molecular diagnostic tools.[37,38] Importantly, the strong correlations between attitudes and curriculum readiness (r = 0.58) and between barriers–willingness and curriculum readiness (r = 0.63) suggest that targeted educational interventions could substantially improve preparedness and adoption intent.

Consistent with broader evidence on digital health tools, demographic variables such as age and gender have been shown to exert less influence on adoption intentions than attitudes and perceived value, supporting the observation that students’ cognitive factors may play a more decisive role in driving ISF adoption than fixed characteristics.[39]

Strong support for curricular integration further emphasizes this need. More than two-thirds of students supported the inclusion of ISF diagnostics in healthcare curricula, with large effect sizes (Cohen d = 0.95–0.99) and high mean scores approaching 4.0. Students also demonstrated a high willingness to participate in ISF-related training programs (72.7%) and to adopt ISF diagnostics in future practice (70.2%), even when acknowledging perceived barriers such as limited curriculum coverage (50.0%) and regulatory concerns (43.3%).

This coexistence of perceived barriers and strong willingness to adopt ISF diagnostics is evident in the present findings, where curriculum gaps and regulatory concerns were commonly reported, yet the majority of students expressed support for ISF use, training participation, and future clinical integration. Such a pattern reflects behavioral adoption models observed in digital health research, where adoption intention remains high when perceived advantages and value outweigh recognized barriers, consistent with innovation diffusion perspectives on telemedicine app acceptance.[40]

The findings of this study have important implications for healthcare education and policy planning. The coexistence of limited awareness and strong adoption intent highlights an opportunity to introduce ISF-based diagnostics early within professional training, supported by case-based learning, workshops, and integration into existing dermatology, pathology, and diagnostic technology curricula. The relationship between knowledge, attitudes, and adoption intent is consistent with established behavioral models such as the Theory of Planned Behavior and the Technology Acceptance Model. In this context, educational exposure (knowledge) shapes students’ perceptions (attitudes), which in turn influences their intention to adopt ISF diagnostics in future clinical practice. This intention is a key predictor of actual behavior and adoption once students transition into professional roles. Therefore, integrating ISF education into healthcare curricula using interactive and experiential teaching strategies can strengthen knowledge and attitudes, thereby increasing adoption intent and facilitating future clinical integration.[41-43] At a policy level, aligning educational frameworks with emerging biomarker technologies may facilitate evidence-based adoption while addressing regulatory, ethical, and standardization concerns. Collaborative efforts between academic institutions, clinicians, and regulatory bodies could help ensure that ISF diagnostics are implemented responsibly as they transition from experimental innovation to clinical practice.[44] ISF biomarker education could be further strengthened through simulation-based workshops and problem-based learning modules that present realistic clinical scenarios, allowing students to practice ISF sample collection, result interpretation, and clinical decision-making. Evidence supports the effectiveness of simulation-based learning in improving clinical competence and skill acquisition in healthcare education.[45–50] Problem-based learning approaches further enhance critical thinking and application of knowledge in clinical contexts.[51] Learning outcomes can be evaluated using pre- and post-training assessments, objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs), and structured student feedback to ensure competence and readiness for clinical application.[52,53]

4.1. Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. It is among the first to explore healthcare students’ awareness and adoption intent regarding cutaneous ISF-based biomarkers in autoimmune dermatoses, addressing an emerging and under-researched area. The use of a structured, validated questionnaire with demonstrated reliability enhances the robustness of the findings. Inclusion of participants from diverse healthcare disciplines improves the breadth and interdisciplinary relevance. Additionally, the comprehensive assessment of knowledge, attitudes, perceived barriers, and adoption intent, supported by multivariable analysis, strengthens the overall validity and interpretability of the results.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits inference to associations between knowledge, attitudes, perceived barriers, and willingness to adopt ISF-based diagnostics, and does not allow assessment of causal relationships or changes over time. Second, the use of convenience sampling within a single academic institution may restrict the generalizability of the results to healthcare students in other educational settings or regions. However, the study identifies educational patterns such as limited exposure but strong adoption readiness that are relevant to healthcare students globally and can inform future clinician training, as ISF-based diagnostics are expected to be adopted by multidisciplinary healthcare providers across diverse clinical settings. Third, adoption intent was assessed using self-reported measures, which may not directly translate into actual clinical implementation once students enter professional practice. Additionally, the study may be affected by social desirability bias, as students might respond in a manner they believe is expected or favorable. Cultural and institutional factors, including curriculum emphasis and local attitudes toward innovation, may also have influenced responses and limit the generalizability of findings to other settings.

Finally, although the questionnaire demonstrated good validity and reliability, the knowledge domain was assessed using a limited number of items, which may not fully capture the depth of understanding of ISF biomarkers. The curriculum integration domain comprised 2 closely related items; however, this approach was justified by a strong inter-item correlation, indicating acceptable internal consistency and conceptual alignment. Additionally, while the regression model explained a substantial proportion of variance in adoption intent, unmeasured factors such as prior research exposure, clinical training experiences, or institutional emphasis on innovation may have influenced students’ responses.

4.2. Future directions

Future research should progress beyond awareness assessment toward experiential educational models that enable healthcare students to interact with emerging cutaneous ISF diagnostic technologies through simulations, virtual demonstrations, or case-based learning. Such approaches may help translate positive attitudes into practical readiness. Given the strong role of perceived value in adoption intent, patient-centered and narrative-based learning strategies highlighting the minimally invasive and personalized nature of ISF diagnostics may further enhance acceptance. Interdisciplinary, student-led innovation initiatives could also support co-creation and early engagement with ISF-based solutions. Finally, extending research to include patients, clinicians, and real-world implementation settings using longitudinal and mixed-methods designs will be essential to ensure that ISF diagnostics are clinically relevant, socially acceptable, and sustainably integrated into precision dermatology.

5. Conclusion

This study highlights a notable gap between limited knowledge and strong readiness to adopt cutaneous interstitial fluid (ISF) biomarker diagnostics among healthcare students. Although most participants demonstrated low to moderate understanding of ISF biomarkers and their clinical relevance in autoimmune dermatoses, they expressed consistently positive perceptions toward their potential use. High confidence in scientific validity, appreciation of the minimally invasive nature of ISF sampling, and a strong willingness to pursue training and future clinical implementation were evident. Attitudes emerged as the strongest predictor of adoption intent, followed by baseline knowledge, while demographic and academic factors showed no significant influence. Overall, these findings indicate substantial educational receptiveness despite limited foundational awareness. Integrating ISF biomarker concepts into healthcare curricula, along with structured training initiatives, may effectively bridge this knowledge gap and support the informed and responsible adoption of ISF diagnostics in future clinical practice.
