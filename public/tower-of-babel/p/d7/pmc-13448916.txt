Synergistic Effect of Ce in Metal-Promoted Cu/Zeolite Beta Catalysts for Selective Ammonia Oxidation
Authors: Min Eui Lee, Yeon-Jeong Jo, Junghyeon Kim, Suhyun Ok, Chaewoo Jeong, Hyo Won Kwak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As global carbon neutrality policies intensify, mitigating ammonia (NH3) slip in carbon-free energy systems is crucial. In this study, a series of Cu-M/Zeolite Beta catalysts (M = Co, Ce, Mn, Cr, V, W, Fe) were synthesized for selective NH3 oxidation. The optimal 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst achieved over 90% NH3 conversion and high N2 selectivity above 350 °C (100 ppm of NH3, 10% O2, 10% H2O, GHSV of 35,000 h–1). Notably, this noble-metal-free catalyst exhibited comparable apparent performance to noble-metal-promoted benchmarks. Physicochemical characterizations (including XRD, TEM, XPS, H2-TPR, and NH3-TPD) revealed that Ce addition improved Cu dispersion while maintaining the zeolite framework. Strong Cu–Ce synergistic interactions increased the concentration of surface Ce3+, oxygen vacancies, and adsorbed oxygen (Oads). Additionally, the Ce-promoted catalyst exhibited a significant increase in Lewis acidity, enhancing NH3 adsorption and activation. These integrated structural and electronic effects suggest that 10Cu–5Ce/ZB has potential as a cost-effective candidate for ammonia oxidation processes, warranting further pilot-scale investigations.

Introduction

As global carbon neutrality policies intensify, interest in clean fuels as alternatives to conventional fossil fuels is increasing. Among various candidates such as hydrogen and ammonia, ammonia (NH3) has emerged as a particularly attractive carbon-free energy carrier due to its established infrastructure, ease of storage and transportation compared to hydrogen, and high energy density. − However, NH3 possesses a relatively high autoignition temperature and a narrow flammability range, which often leads to the emission of unreacted ammonia, commonly referred to as “ammonia slip,” during the combustion process. ,

The emission of ammonia slip poses significant environmental and technical challenges. It acts as a precursor to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and is hazardous to human health. In power generation sectors, it facilitates the formation of ammonium sulfate, leading to catalyst poisoning, reduced performance, and decreased heat transfer efficiency. To mitigate these challenges, Ammonia Oxidation Catalyst (AOC) technology is employed to convert unreacted NH3 into harmless nitrogen (N2) and water (H2O) using oxygen, without the need for additional reducing agents. The primary objective of AOC processes is to achieve high ammonia conversion while maximizing N2 selectivity and minimizing side reactions that lead to the formation of harmful nitrogen oxides (NO x , N2O).

Traditionally, noble metal catalysts such as platinum (Pt) and palladium (Pd) have been widely applied for AOC due to their excellent oxidation capabilities. Nevertheless, these materials have some limitations, including high costs and low N2 selectivity resulting from excessive oxidation. , To overcome these limitations, composite oxide catalysts that control structural and electronic properties by combining two or more metals have gained significant attention. Among transition metals, the studies have demonstrated that introducing metals like Co, Mn, or Fe into Cu oxides can compensate for the low-temperature limitations of single oxides and effectively improve catalytic performance through synergistic interactions. −

Recent research on non-noble metal catalysts for NH3–SCO or AOC has therefore focused on designing transition-metal oxides and zeolite-supported catalysts that can simultaneously enhance NH3 activation, oxygen mobility, and N2 selectivity. Among them, Cu-based catalysts have been extensively investigated because Cu species can provide effective redox sites for NH3 activation and oxidation, while zeolite supports can offer abundant acid sites for NH3 adsorption and help stabilize dispersed Cu species. − For example, Cu-containing zeolite catalysts and Cu-based mixed oxides have been reported to exhibit promising NH3 oxidation activity by regulating the oxidation state, dispersion, and nuclearity of Cu species. ,− In addition, recent studies have shown that coupling Cu with other transition or rare-earth metals, such as Co, Fe, Mn, Sn, and Ce, can further improve catalytic performance by promoting interfacial redox cycles, generating active oxygen species, and suppressing undesired overoxidation pathways. ,− In particular, Ce has been widely recognized as an effective promoter, where the Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle and the generation of oxygen vacancies play crucial roles in facilitating oxygen activation.

In this study, copper (Cu) was selected as the primary active species and introduced into a zeolite beta (ZB) support to evaluate ammonia oxidation performance within the temperature range of 300–400 °C, which corresponds to the standard operating conditions of power plants. Various transition metal promoters (M = Co, Ce, Mn, Cr, V, W, Fe) were systematically compared under identical conditions to enhance oxygen mobility and valence variability. To highlight the distinctiveness of the optimal Ce-promoted Cu/ZB catalyst, the Ce promotion effect was systematically interpreted in conjunction with Cu dispersion, the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio, the Oads/Olattice ratio, and NH3 adsorption characteristics. The physicochemical properties of the synthesized catalysts were comprehensively characterized using ICP, TEM, BET, XRD, XPS, and NH3-TPD to elucidate the fundamental factors contributing to the enhancement of AOC activity.

Methods

Preparation of Catalyst

The metal oxide precursors used in this study included manganese­(II) acetate tetrahydrate ((CH3COO)2Mn·4H2O), cobalt­(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co­(NO3)2·6H2O), copper­(II) nitrate trihydrate (Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O), iron­(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe­(NO3)3·9H2O), tungsten­(VI) oxide (WO3), ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3), cerium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Ce­(NO3)3·6H2O), and chromium­(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Cr­(NO3)3·9H2O). Zeolite Beta (ZB) was utilized as the support material. The target loadings were 10 wt % for the primary catalyst (Cu) and 5 wt % for the promoters, based on the total mass of the support.

The catalysts were synthesized via a wet impregnation method. First, the precursors were dissolved in distilled water and stirred at 30 °C. The ZB support was then added to the solution and stirred at a speed above 250 rpm. The solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator, and the resulting materials were dried at 110 °C for more than 12 h. Finally, the catalysts were calcined at 450 °C for 5 h under an air atmosphere. The synthesized samples were designated as 10Cu/ZB, 5Ce/ZB, and 10Cu-5M/ZB (where M represents the promoter). Additionally, noble-metal-promoted catalysts were prepared with the same method using precursors of platinum (Pt), palladium (Pd), and rhodium (Rh) to investigate further performance enhancement.

Characterization

The actual metal loadings of the prepared catalysts were verified by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) optical emission spectrometry using an Avio 500 (PerkinElmer, USA). The specific surface area and textural properties were determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method with a BELSORP MAX X instrument (MicrotracBEL, Japan). The chemical compositions and structural parameters obtained from ICP and BET analyses are summarized in Table . To evaluate the morphology and particle size distribution, field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM) was performed using a Tecnai G2 F30 (FEI, USA) operating at an acceleration voltage of 50–300 kV. The crystalline phases of the samples were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a SmartLab 9 kW (Rigaku, Japan).

The oxidation states of the active metals were investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a NEXSA G2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All binding energies, including Cu 2p, Ce 3d, and O 1s, were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV as the reference. To analyze ammonia adsorption and desorption behavior, temperature-programmed desorption of NH3 (NH3-TPD) was conducted using a BELCAT system (MicrotracBEL, Japan). For each TPD run, 0.1 g of the catalyst was pretreated at 400 °C under a He atmosphere for 30 min. After cooling to 60 °C, NH3 was adsorbed onto the catalyst surface. Following the removal of physisorbed NH3, the temperature was increased to 800 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min–1 in a He flow to monitor the desorption peaks.

Catalytic Characterization

The catalytic activity tests were performed in a customized system consisting of gas injection, reaction, and analysis units, as shown in Figure . The reactant gases were precisely regulated by mass flow controllers (MFC; SEC-N100, HORIBA, Japan). Detailed experimental parameters are listed in Table . Water vapor was introduced by vaporizing liquid water via a metering pump, and all gas delivery lines were maintained above 130 °C to prevent condensation and ammonium salt formation. The oxidation reaction was carried out in a fixed-bed reactor using catalyst particles (50–70 mesh) secured with quartz wool. A total of 1.5 g of catalyst was loaded, corresponding to a catalyst bed volume of 6.0 mL, and the total flow rate was set to 3,500 mL min–1 to maintain a space velocity (SV) of 35,000 h–1. The reaction temperature increased from 300 to 400 °C in 50 °C increments, with each interval maintained for at least 1 h to ensure steady-state conditions. The concentrations of outlet gas species, including NH3, NO, NO2, and N2O, were continuously monitored using an FTX-ONE CS gas analyzer (HORIBA, Japan). Based on the monitored inlet and outlet gas concentrations, the NH3 conversion and N2, NO x , N2O selectivity were determined using the following equations. In this case, the N2 selectivity was determined based on the measurable nitrogen species (NO, NO2 and N2O). Results and Discussion

Catalytic Performance for NH3 Oxidation over Various Metal Promoters

The catalytic activities of 10Cu/ZB catalysts modified with various transition metal promoters (M = Co, Ce, Mn, Cr, V, W, Fe) were evaluated to identify the most effective composition for the ammonia oxidation (AOC). As shown in Figure , NH3 conversion and N2 selectivity were systematically measured over the temperature range of 300–400 °C. At a reaction temperature of 300 °C, 10Cu–5Cr/ZB and 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalysts exhibited the most prominent catalytic activity, with NH3 conversion reaching approximately 80%. These were followed by Mn- and Co-promoted catalysts in terms of activity, while the addition of V, W, and Fe resulted in performance comparable to, or only marginally higher than, that of the monometallic 10Cu/ZB catalyst. The low performance of V- and W-promoted catalysts may be attributed to their high oxygen binding energies, which restrict the mobility and desorption of lattice oxygen, thereby hindering the catalytic oxidation cycle. ,

Ammonia oxidation activity and N2 selectivity of 10Cu/ZB catalysts using various promoters (M = Co, Ce, Mn, Cr, V, W, Fe) at (a) 300 °C, (b) 350 °C, and (c) 400 °C.

As the temperature increased to 350 °C and above, a significant increase in NH3 conversion was observed across all tested catalysts. Notably, both Cr- and Ce-promoted catalysts achieved the highest conversion rates (exceeding 90%) in this midto-high temperature range. However, despite its high conversion efficiency, the 10Cu–5Cr/ZB catalyst exhibited a sharp decline in N2 selectivity at elevated temperatures. This reduction is attributed to the excessive oxidative strength of Cr species, which facilitates the overoxidation of NH3 into undesired NO x or N2O. , Furthermore, Cr-based catalysts pose significant environmental risks due to the potential emission of toxic hexavalent chromium (Cr6+) during synthesis or operation, presenting a major challenge for industrial application.

In contrast, the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst maintained a superior balance between high NH3 conversion and stable N2 selectivity throughout the entire temperature range. The synergistic interaction between Cu and Ce appears to facilitate the NH3 oxidation process while effectively suppressing side reactions. Indeed, the outlet concentrations of nitrogenous byproducts (NO, NO2, N2O) remained at negligible levels below 10 ppm for catalysts, showing only a slight increase with rising temperature (Table S1). Considering the overall catalytic efficiency, selectivity, and environmental safety, 10Cu–5Ce/ZB was selected as the optimal candidate for further physicochemical investigation and performance comparison with noble-metal-based catalysts.

To further evaluate the hydrothermal durability of the catalyst, the long-term NH3 oxidation performance was investigated under different H2O concentrations at 350 °C. As shown in Figure S1, the NH3 conversion remained highly stable over 250 h regardless of the water content. In the case of 5, 10, and 15% H2O concentration, the NH3 conversion was maintained at approximately 88–90% throughout the entire reaction period, with only negligible fluctuations. No noticeable deactivation was observed even under the highest H2O concentration of 15%, indicating that the catalyst possesses excellent resistance to water vapor.

The nearly unchanged NH3 conversion with increasing H2O concentration suggests that competitive adsorption between NH3 and H2O on the active sites was not significant, at least up to 15% H2O, under the present reaction conditions. In addition, the stable catalytic activity during prolonged operation demonstrates that the active surface species and/or acid sites responsible for NH3 adsorption and oxidation were well preserved in the presence of water vapor. These results confirm the superior hydrothermal stability of the catalyst and its potential applicability under practical flue gas conditions containing high levels of moisture.

Comparison with Noble-Metal-Promoted Catalysts

To evaluate the potential for further performance enhancement and to benchmark against conventional high-activity materials, the catalytic performance of 10Cu–5Ce/ZB was compared with versions promoted by 0.5 wt % of noble metals (Pt, Pd, and Rh). At a reaction temperature of 300 °C (Figure a), the addition of Pt resulted in a slight increase in NH3 conversion compared to the noble-metal-free 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst. Generally, noble metals (such as Pt and Pd) are known to promote the dissociation of N–H bonds and activate surface oxygen more effectively at lower temperatures (below 300 °C), leading to superior oxidation performance in the low-temperature regime. However, as the temperature increased to 350 °C (Figure b), the performance gap between the Pt-promoted catalyst and the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst narrowed significantly.

Ammonia oxidation activity and N2 selectivity over noble metal (Pt, Pd, and Rh) promoted 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalysts at (a) 300 °C, (b) 350 °C, and (c) 400 °C.

As the reaction reached the high-temperature region of 400 °C (Figure c), the trend was reversed, with the noble-metal-free 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst exhibiting the highest NH3 conversion, surpassing the noble-metal-promoted variants. While noble metals are highly effective for low-temperature oxidation, their performance enhancement appears to be limited or even slightly detrimental in the midto-high temperature range (300–400 °C), as observed in this study. Across the entire temperature range, all catalysts maintained high N2 selectivity, indicating that noble metal addition did not significantly alter the reaction pathway toward overoxidation under these conditions. These results demonstrate that for standard power plant operating conditions, the additional introduction of expensive noble metals does not provide a substantial benefit to ammonia oxidation performance. Consequently, 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst represents a promising noble-metal-free candidate for AOC applications, as the introduction of noble metals did not provide a clear improvement in apparent NH3 oxidation performance under the reaction conditions used in this study. , However, it should be noted that this comparative evaluation is primarily based on apparent activity rather than intrinsic reaction rates or turnover frequencies (TOF). Therefore, further rigorous kinetic studies are required to establish a more fundamental benchmark against noble-metal systems.

Physicochemical Characterization of Cu- and Ce-Based Catalysts

To elucidate the factors contributing to the enhanced catalytic performance of the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst, its physicochemical properties were compared with those of 10Cu/ZB, 5Ce/ZB, and pristine zeolite beta (ZB). The chemical compositions and textural properties obtained from ICP and BET analyses are summarized in Table . ICP analysis confirmed that the actual loadings of Cu and Ce in all synthesized catalysts were within the expected error range of the target values.

Regarding the textural properties, with increasing metal content, a general decrease in specific surface area, total pore volume, and average pore diameter was observed. Specifically, the specific surface area decreased from 388.1 m2 g–1 for the pristine ZB to 362.7 m2 g–1 and 379.8 m2 g–1 for 10Cu/ZB and 5Ce/ZB, respectively. This trend is attributed to the partial blockage of the zeolite support pores by the loaded active metal oxide species or a decrease in the relative mass of the support.

Interestingly, the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst exhibited a specific surface area (364.7 m2 g–1), pore volume (0.7 cm3 g–1), and pore diameter (3.6 nm), which are very similar to those of the 10Cu/ZB catalyst. This indicates that the addition of Ce as a promoter does not significantly alter the primary textural framework of the Cu/ZB catalyst. These results suggest that the superior ammonia oxidation activity of 10Cu–5Ce/ZB is not primarily derived from changes in physical surface area or pore structure, but rather from electronic or structural synergistic effects between the Cu and Ce species, which were further investigated through TEM and XRD analyses.

To further investigate the morphology and dispersion of the active metal species, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was conducted. The results for 10Cu/ZB, 5Ce/ZB, and 10Cu–5Ce/ZB are shown in Figure . All three samples displayed a similar overall morphology; however, significant differences were observed in metal dispersion. In the 10Cu/ZB catalyst, numerous dark and dense clusters were localized on the support surface (Figure a). These features are attributed to bulk CuO aggregates formed due to the uneven distribution of the high copper loading. Conversely, the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst exhibited a marked reduction in such bulk CuO clusters (Figure c). This observation demonstrates that the introduction of Ce effectively inhibits the agglomeration of copper species and enhances their dispersion across the zeolite beta framework. In addition, the dispersion states of Cu and Ce species were further investigated by STEM-EDS elemental mapping. As shown in Figure S2, the Cu species in the Cu/ZB sample were not uniformly distributed, and several Cu-rich regions were observed, indicating the partial aggregation of Cu species on the catalyst surface. In contrast, the Ce mapping image of the Ce/ZB sample showed a relatively homogeneous distribution of Ce species over the support, suggesting that Ce species were well dispersed on the ZB surface (Figure S2­(b)). For the Cu–Ce/ZB sample, the elemental mapping images of Cu and Ce are shown in Figures S2­(c,d), respectively. Compared with the Cu/ZB sample, the Cu species in Cu–Ce/ZB exhibited a more uniform distribution, with no obvious Cu-rich aggregates. This result suggests that the introduction of Ce effectively improved the dispersion of Cu species. Such improvement in metal dispersion increases the effective active surface area available for the AOC reaction, serving as a primary factor in the enhanced catalytic efficiency.

TEM images of (a) 10Cu/ZB, (b) 5Ce/ZB, and (c) 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalysts.

The crystalline phases of the support and the synthesized catalysts were identified using XRD analysis, as shown in Figure . All catalysts maintained the characteristic diffraction peaks of zeolite beta (PDF#48-0074) at 2θ = 22.4° and 25.3°. This confirms that the skeletal framework of the zeolite support remained stable and intact even after the loading of active metals. , For both 10Cu/ZB and 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalysts, diffraction peaks corresponding to the CuO phase (PDF#48-1548) were observed at 2θ = 35.5° and 38.7°. , Notably, the 10Cu/ZB catalyst exhibited an additional small peak near 2θ = 12.8°, which is assigned to basic copper nitrate (JCPDS#15-0014) (Figure b). This suggests that a portion of the copper precursor was not fully decomposed or underwent recrystallization during calcination, likely due to its uneven distribution and localized concentration within the zeolite pores. In contrast, this impurity peak disappeared in the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst, while the overall intensity of the zeolite peaks decreased (Figure c). These results are consistent with the TEM observations and indicate that the addition of Ce promotes the dispersion of Cu species, thereby inhibiting the formation of impurity phases and bulk CuO aggregates while maintaining the structural stability of the support.

Surface Chemical States and Electronic Interactions

To investigate the oxidation states of active metals and surface oxygen species, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed (Figure ). Figure a shows the Cu 2p spectra for 10Cu/ZB and 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalysts. The Cu 2p3/2 spectra were deconvoluted into two primary peaks corresponding to Cu+ (933.4 eV) and Cu2+ (934.3 eV) species. The presence of Cu2+ was further confirmed by the characteristic satellite peaks observed in the 940–945 eV. Interestingly, the Cu2+/(Cu+ + Cu2+) ratio remained relatively consistent between 10Cu/ZB and 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalysts. This suggests that the introduction of Ce maintains the stability of the copper redox cycle, which is essential for the continuous generation of reaction intermediates during ammonia oxidation.

XPS spectra of Cu and Ce based catalysts: (a) Cu 2p, (b) Ce 3d, and (c) O 1s.

The Ce 3d spectra (Figure b) were deconvoluted into peaks corresponding to Ce3+ and Ce4+ oxidation states. Compared to the 5Ce/ZB catalyst, the Ce 3d peaks for the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst shifted toward lower binding energies with a simultaneous decrease in peak intensity. This shift indicates a strong electronic interaction between Cu and Ce, which facilitates the partial reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ upon Cu doping or through the formation of complex oxide boundaries. The resulting increase in Ce3+ concentration is directly linked to the generation of oxygen vacancies (Ov). − The formation of a composite Cu–Ov-Ce structure likely promotes the activation of oxygen molecules, thereby enhancing the overall ammonia oxidation activity.

Further evidence of these surface changes is provided by the O 1s spectra (as shown in Figure c), where peaks were differentiated into surface adsorbed oxygen (Oads) at 532.7 eV and lattice oxygen (Olattice) at 531.9 eV. Among all the samples, the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst exhibited the highest proportion of Oads species. This is consistent with the Ce 3d analysis, as the higher concentration of oxygen vacancies induced by Ce3+ facilitates more effective adsorption and activation of gaseous oxygen. Such highly active Oads species can form superoxide radicals, which are critical for accelerating the rate-determining steps in ammonia oxidation. − Consequently, the superior performance of the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst arises from these synergistic electronic interactions, which optimize the availability of active oxygen species and enhance surface reactivity. Effect of Ce Loading on Surface Redox Properties and Catalytic Activity

To further investigate the influence of promoter content on surface redox properties and catalytic performance, the Ce loading was systematically varied from 3 to 7 wt %. As illustrated in Figure , at 300 °C, both the Ce3+/ (Ce3+ + Ce4+) ratio and the ammonia oxidation activity were significantly affected by the Ce concentration. The Ce3+/ (Ce3+ + Ce4+) ratio, determined from deconvoluted Ce 3d XPS spectra, increased from approximately 28 to 34 as the Ce loading rose from 3 to 5 wt %, but slightly decreased to 32 at a higher loading of 7 wt %. This trend in surface Ce3+ concentration directly correlates with the observed ammonia oxidation activity. The occurrence of Ce3+ is generally associated with the formation of oxygen vacancies in ceria-based catalysts, because the partial reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ is accompanied by charge compensation through the removal or deficiency of lattice oxygen. These oxygen vacancies can provide coordinatively unsaturated sites that facilitate the adsorption and activation of gaseous oxygen, thereby promoting the formation of reactive oxygen species on the catalyst surface. In this regard, the increased Ce3+/ (Ce3+ + Ce4+) ratio observed for 10Cu–5Ce/ZB suggests the formation of a higher concentration of surface oxygen vacancies, which can enhance oxygen mobility and contribute to improved ammonia oxidation activity. −

Ce 3d XPS spectra and ammonia oxidation activity of 10Cu-xCe/ZB catalysts with varying Ce loadings (x = 3, 5, and 7).

The 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst exhibited the highest NH3 conversion, which aligns with its maximum Ce3+/ (Ce3+ + Ce4+) ratio. The presence of Ce3+ is fundamentally linked to the generation of oxygen vacancies, which serve as active sites for the adsorption and activation of gaseous oxygen. Therefore, optimizing the Ce content to 5 wt % maximizes the concentration of these surface oxygen vacancies, leading to enhanced AOC activity. Conversely, the decline in performance of the 10Cu–7Ce/ZB catalyst indicates that excessive Ce loading leads to the agglomeration of Ce species on the zeolite support. Such agglomeration likely hinders effective synergistic interaction between Cu and Ce species and reduces the active surface area available for ammonia adsorption and activation, ultimately resulting in decreased catalytic efficiency. These results underscore the importance of precise promoter loading in maximizing the structural and electronic synergy required for efficient ammonia oxidation.

Ammonia Adsorption and Desorption Behavior

The surface adsorption behavior of ammonia is a critical factor in determining the overall efficiency of the ammonia oxidation activity. To evaluate the surface acidity and adsorption capacity of the catalysts, NH3-TPD analysis was conducted (Figure ). All samples exhibited three distinct desorption regions between 100 and 650 °C: a low-temperature region (<250 °C) generally attributed to physisorbed NH3, a medium-temperature region (250–450 °C) associated with NH3 adsorbed on Lewis acid sites (primarily Cu sites), and a high-temperature region (>450 °C) corresponding to NH3 adsorbed on Brønsted acid sites originating from Si–O­(H)–Al groups in the zeolite framework. ,− The total acidity and the distribution of weak, medium, and strong acid sites were determined by integrating the respective desorption peak areas (Table S3).

NH3-TPD profiles of ZB, Cu, Ce, and Cu–Ce based catalysts.

The total amount of desorbed ammonia followed the order: 10Cu–5Ce/ZB > 10Cu/ZB > ZB > 5Ce/ZB. The 5Ce/ZB catalyst exhibited lower acidity compared to the pristine ZB, which can be attributed to the “covering effect” where cerium oxide species mask the inherent acid sites of the zeolite support. In contrast, the 10Cu/ZB catalyst showed significantly increased ammonia adsorption in the medium-to-high temperature regions compared to ZB, confirming that the loaded Cu species function as strong Lewis acid sites.

Notably, the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst displayed the highest ammonia adsorption capacity across all temperature ranges, with the medium-temperature desorption amount reaching as high as 4.6 × 105 a.u (Table S3). The peak intensity in the Lewis acid site region was markedly enhanced compared to the monometallic 10Cu/ZB catalyst. This enhanced capacity is attributed to the synergistic structural and electronic interactions between Cu and Ce, improved metal dispersion, and the formation of oxygen vacancies, which collectively contribute to increased surface acidity. The enhanced ability to adsorb and activate ammonia on the catalyst surface is a decisive factor contributing to the superior catalytic performance of 10Cu–5Ce/ZB observed in activity tests. These results confirm that Ce integration as a promoter not only optimizes the electronic state of active species but also maximizes the availability of surface acid sites necessary for efficient ammonia oxidation.

H2-TPR analysis was performed to evaluate the redox characteristics of the catalysts, and the results are shown in Figure (a). The 10Cu/ZB catalyst exhibits a major reduction peak at approximately 285 °C, which is attributed to the reduction of CuO x species. , In contrast, the reduction peak for the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst shifted to a lower temperature range (265 °C). This shift indicates that the introduction of Ce effectively enhanced the reducibility of the Cu species, suggesting that the reduction process was promoted through strong electronic interactions between Cu and Ce.

Mechanistic investigations: (a) H2-TPR profiles evaluating the redox properties of the 5Ce/ZB, 10Cu/ZB, and 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalysts. (b) Proposed schematic mechanism for selective NH3 oxidation over the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst.

Meanwhile, the 5Ce/ZB catalyst displayed a broad reduction peak in the high-temperature region (centered around 567 °C), which corresponds to the reduction of lattice oxygen in CeO2. Notably, in the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst, this high-temperature Ce peak disappears, while the Cu reduction peak shifts to a lower temperature. This phenomenon suggests that Ce exists in a highly dispersed state rather than forming independent bulk oxides, and it closely interacts with Cu species to form Cu–[O x ]–Ce linkages. Consequently, the synergistic effect between Cu and Ce significantly enhances the overall reduction behavior and redox properties of the catalyst, which indirectly supports the superior NH3 oxidation performance observed over the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst. While the current characterizations provide a plausible mechanistic framework, direct operando spectroscopic evidence is still needed to fully confirm the proposed oxygen activation pathways. In this regard, the potential reaction pathways over the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst are systematically correlated with these multifaceted characterization data. A comprehensive mechanism schematic illustrating NH3 adsorption on Cu sites, Cu–Ce electronic transfer, and O2 activation at oxygen vacancies is provided in Figure (b).

Conclusion

In this study, a series of Cu-M/Zeolite Beta catalysts were synthesized via the wet impregnation method to effectively address the challenge of ammonia slip in carbon-free energy systems. Among the various promoters screened, the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst demonstrated the highest catalytic performance, achieving over 90% NH3 conversion and high N2 selectivity at temperatures above 350 °C. Notably, this non-noble metal catalyst exhibited performance comparable to, or even exceeding, noble-metal-based catalysts such as Pt, Pd, and Rh in the midto-high temperature range of 300–400 °C. These results suggest that the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst is a highly promising and cost-effective candidate for industrial ammonia oxidation applications.

The superior catalytic efficiency of the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst is primarily derived from several integrated structural and electronic effects. Physicochemical characterizations confirmed that the addition of Ce inhibits the agglomeration of Cu species and improves their dispersion on the support surface while maintaining the structural stability of the zeolite beta framework. This improvement in metal dispersion effectively increases the active surface area available for the ammonia oxidation activity. Furthermore, the strong synergistic interaction between Cu and Ce facilitates the redox cycle, leading to an increased ratio of surface Ce3+ species and adsorbed oxygen (Oads). This transition promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies, which significantly enhances the adsorption and activation of gaseous oxygen.

In addition to these structural and electronic improvements, the Ce-promoted catalyst exhibited a marked increase in surface acidity, particularly in the concentration of Lewis acid sites. NH3-TPD analysis revealed that this enhanced acidity maximizes the capacity for NH3 adsorption and activation on the catalyst surface, which are critical for achieving high reaction efficiency. The combination of optimized metal dispersion, increased oxygen vacancy concentration, and enhanced surface acidity provides a robust mechanistic basis for the high activity and selectivity of the 10Cu–5Ce/ZB catalyst. Overall, this work suggests that strategic integration of Ce as a promoter may reduce the need for expensive noble metals under the investigated AOC reaction conditions. Despite these promising findings, some limitations remain to be addressed for practical industrial implementation. The current mechanistic interpretations of the Cu–Ce synergy remain largely indirect, and comparisons with noble-metal catalysts have been based on apparent activity. Future research will focus on evaluating intrinsic reaction rates (e.g., TOF calculations) and providing a complete nitrogen balance. Furthermore, extended durability tests under more realistic and complex flue gas conditions, including the presence of SO x and various other impurities, will be essential to fully validate the industrial applicability of the proposed catalyst.
