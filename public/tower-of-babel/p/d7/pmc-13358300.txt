Phylogenomic analysis of vocal fold length evolution reveals links between vocal and auditory systems in terrestrial mammals
Authors: Xu Zhou, Fangyuan Liu, Miaomiao Liu, Ziying Hu, Linqing Zhang, Xiangnan Wang, Ziyi Zhou, Yilai Shu, Wenjing Sun, Shengbo Bi, Li Jin, Zeshan Lin, Menghan Zhang, Jeffrey Townsend
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVocal fold length is a primary anatomical determinant of pitch and fundamental frequency in terrestrial mammal vocalizations, playing a key role in social communication, mate attraction, and competition. However, its phenotypic diversification and genetic basis remain unclear. Here, using a phylogenetic genotype-to-phenotype mapping (PhyloG2P) framework to 75 species representing Primates, Carnivora, and Artiodactyla, we investigate the evolutionary dynamics and genetic architecture of relative vocal fold length (RVFL), accounting for allometric trends. Phylogenetic comparative analyses reveal similar patterns of RVFL variation among orders and strong adaptive responses supporting the acoustic size exaggeration hypothesis. Correlation analyses further reveal the potential driving role of social organization in RVFL evolution. Comparative genomics identifies 25 hearing-related genes showing rapid evolution, positive selection, lineage-specific mutations, and coevolution with RVFL, suggesting a reciprocal evolutionary interplay between vocal and auditory systems. This genetic link is supported by laryngeal MRI evidence from wild-type and Pjvk knock-out mice, where PJVK is identified as a candidate gene through our PhyloG2P analyses. Additionally, pathways related to peptide hormone secretion and neural regulation appear to mediate RVFL variation. Together, these findings advance our understanding of the genetic and evolutionary mechanisms driving vocal fold diversity in terrestrial mammals and highlight the links between vocal and auditory systems, offering new insights into the origins of vocal communication.Keywords: relative vocal fold length, terrestrial mammals, phenotypic diversification, coevolution, hearing-related genes, laryngeal MRI scans

Introduction

Vocalization is an innate behavior in vertebrates that likely originated over 400 million years ago (Hernandez-Miranda and Birchmeier 2018; Jorgewich-Cohen et al. 2022). This behavior fulfills multiple critical roles across species, including communication (e.g. emotional expression [Briefer 2012; Brudzynski 2013]), survival (e.g. territory defense [Litvin et al. 2010]), and social bonding (e.g. courtship and mating [Clutton-Brock 2016; Bryant 2021]). These functions highlight the evolutionary adaptability and diversity of vocal behaviors across terrestrial mammals and other taxa. The paired vocal folds in the larynx, a vital physical organ, play a central role in voice production by generating vibrations that serve as the primary sound source (Broad 1979; Jiang et al. 2000). According to the source-filter theory (Titze 1988; Taylor and Reby 2010) and the myoelectric-aerodynamic theory (Elemans et al. 2015), the pitch and quality of this source sound are governed by intrinsic properties of the vocal folds, including their length and tension (Titze et al. 1989; Titze and Martin 1998; Titze and Hunter 2004). Among these properties, vocal fold length (VFL) plays a pivotal role as an anatomical determinant of pitch and sound frequency, directly influencing the vibratory characteristics of the vocal folds (Titze 1976; Childers et al. 1986; Schuberth et al. 2002). Specifically, longer vocal folds typically produce lower-pitched sounds due to decreased vibratory frequency, whereas shorter vocal folds result in higher-pitched sounds (Hollien and Moore 1960; Nishizawa 1988). Therefore, this close relationship between VFL and sound production is integral to understanding the mechanisms of vocalization in terrestrial mammals (Hirano et al. 1969; Riede and Brown 2013).

Terrestrial mammals exhibit remarkable diversity in VFL, with available data ranging from 0.605 mm in white-footed mice (Riede et al. 2022) to 104 mm in African elephants (Herbst et al. 2012). Smaller-bodied species generally have shorter VFL, whereas larger-bodied species possess longer ones (Riede and Titze 2008; Riede and Brown 2013), a pattern that reflects the allometric relationship between VFL and body size (Garcia and Dunn 2019). In addition to the allometry, the previous cross-species comparative studies also concentrated on the relationship between the VFL and fundamental frequency (f0). Controlling for phylogenetic relationships, VFL is negatively associated with average f0 in mammals (Baotic et al. 2015; Titze et al. 2016). Consequently, VFL variation may serve as a key physiological factor shaping mammalian communicative behaviors. For example, compared to other primates, howler monkeys have longer VFL and can produce the loud calls, which function as aggressive signals to defend food, territory, and offspring (Schön 1971; Kitchen et al. 2015). Similarly, other terrestrial mammals (e.g. wolves, deer, and baboons) also employ vocalizations produced using vocal fold vibration in diverse social interactions, such as sexual communication, competitive signaling, territorial defense, and individual recognition (Owings and Morton 1998; Fischer et al. 2004; Palacios et al. 2007; Vannoni and McElligott 2008). Despite these insights, the diversification of VFL across species remains poorly understood from an evolutionary perspective. Moreover, genes related to vocal fold morphogenesis may contribute to genetic influences on length variation. For example, the SHH gene, GLI genes, and Wnt/β-catenin pathway are known to be regulators of vocal fold and laryngeal development (Lungova and Thibeault 2020). Recent single-nuclei RNA sequencing of human vocal folds has further confirmed the critical role of the Hippo pathway in their development (Mohad 2019; Laitman et al. 2024). Theoretically, genetic variations affecting these pathways could also drive developmental changes in VFL. However, to date, no studies have elucidated the genetic basis underlying VFL variations.

Aiming to elucidate these evolutionary mechanisms, our study investigated the phenotypic diversification process and identified the genetic potentials of VFL across terrestrial mammals. Our study encompassed 75 terrestrial mammals from the orders Primates, Carnivora, and Artiodactyla. In accordance with allometric principles, we employed phylogenetic comparative approaches to derive the relative vocal fold length (RVFL) by calculating residuals from the PGLS regression of VFL with body mass (BM). We then reconstructed the ancestral states of RVFL and charted its temporal phenotypic trajectory based on the phylogenetic relationships of our samples. We also examined the associations between RVFL and potential life history and ecological factors to identify the key drivers of RVFL evolution. Furthermore, we adopted the phylogenetic genotype-to-phenotype mapping framework (phyloG2P) to probe the genetic basis of RVFL evolution. PhyloG2P integrates genotypic and phenotypic trajectories along evolutionary lineages delineated by phylogenetic trees, thereby facilitating the identification of genetic factors driving trait evolution (Smith et al. 2020). Through this approach, we derived genetic potentials from the associations between gene evolutionary rates and RVFL across species. Complementarily, we conducted selective pressure analyses and examined specific amino acid substitutions to identify pivotal genes implicated in RVFL variation. Further experimental validation (i.e. MRI scan and VFL measurement analysis) in Pjvk knock-out (KO) mice, downstream functional enrichment analysis, and manual annotation of our candidate genes further deepened our understanding of the genetic evolution of RVFL. Collectively, our findings provide novel insights into the phenotypic and genetic evolution of vocalization in terrestrial mammals and shed light on the origins of human speech and language.

Results

The evolutionary features of RVFL across species

We calculated the value of Pagel's λ to measure the strength of phylogenetic signal for VFL. The result showed VFL is highly coherent with the phylogenetic tree (Pagel's λ = 1, P value = 2.35e-08). Given the phylogenetic conservatism in VFL, we subsequently employed the phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) regression to investigated the corresponding phenotypic allometry between VFL and BM across 75 terrestrial mammals. The results (Fig. 1a) indicated a significantly positive correlation between log10VFL and 1og10BM (R2 = 0.6435, P value < 2.2e-16). Residuals from the log-log PGLS regression of VFL on BM were calculated to represent RVFL with values ranging from −0.5089 (Procyon lotor) to 0.6156 (Alouatta sara) (Table S1). Additionally, the RVFL*10 (from −5.089 to 6.156), the log10VFL (from 0.5998 to 1.8319), and the 1og10BM (from 2.0932 to 5.9845) were mapped onto the tips of the phylogenetic tree, highlighting the broad variation in VFL and BM among the sampled species (Fig. 1b). Beyond the overall allometric pattern, we examined the scaling relationships among three terrestrial mammalian orders. Phylogenetic ANCOVA (pANCOVA) showed no significant difference in slopes (F slopes = 0.1143; P slopes = 0.8922) or intercepts (F intercepts = 0.0284; P intercepts = 0.9720) among these orders (Figure S1a). These results suggested the absence of evolutionary grade shifts and indicated similar patterns in their allometric regimes.

To visualize the evolutionary dynamics and diversification of phenotypic traits, we reconstructed the ancestral states of RVFL from the observed tip values, revealing through the ancestral phenogram that multiple lineages within the three orders experienced rapid variation in RVFL during recent evolutionary periods (Fig. 2a). Additionally, to assess broader phenotypic traits trends, we calculated the evolutionary trajectories of mean RVFL and BM across all sampled species (Fig. 2a). These trajectories indicated a sharp increase followed by intermittent declines of mean RVFL and mean BM shortly after the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) extinction event, suggesting strong selective pressures driving adaptability.

To evaluate the overall evolutionary trend of RVFL, we thus analyzed branchwise evolutionary rates (σ2) across all species. The results showed a general tendency for RVFL evolutionary rates to transition from slow to fast (Fig. 2b), consistent with the rapid RVFL variation observed in ancestral state reconstruction of RVFL (Fig. 2a). Notably, the howler monkeys Alouatta exhibited the highest evolutionary rate with strong confidence (posterior probability > 0.975; Fig. 2b). The high evolutionary rates were also observed in Ateles fusciceps, Phoca vitulina, and Procyon lotor, suggesting species-specific evolutionary pressures (Fig. 2b). Pairwise comparisons of average rates among the three orders showed no significant differences in RVFL evolutionary rates between the orders (Figure S1b), which reflects the similar evolutionary patterns of residual variation in allometry across these orders. After all, slower evolutionary rates were associated with lower residual variation and weaker allometric integration, reinforcing the link between evolutionary dynamics and trait variability of RVFL (Kverková et al. 2022).

To comprehensively investigate the life history or ecological drivers of RVFL evolution in terrestrial mammals, we included original VFL (OVFL) in the analysis (Table S2). We first used PGLS regression to test for associations between nine continuous traits and both OVFL and RVFL (Orme et al. 2023). OVFL was significantly correlated with most continuous traits, including brain size, gestation length, weaning age, female and male age at maturity, litters per year, and interlitter/interbirth interval, whereas RVFL showed no significant association with any continuous trait (Table S3). By contrast, analyses of discrete traits using the R package MCMCglmm (Hadfield 2010) showed that RVFL was significantly associated only with social organization (Table S4).

Genes associated to RVFL evolution

To identify RVFL-associated genes (RAGs), we collected the genetic data of 51 species and employed the PGLS approach to examine the correlations between the evolutionary rates of genes (root-to-tip ω values) and RVFL. The PGLS results identified 62 genes significantly associated with RVFL (p_all < 0.05; Table S5) in all 1990 genes. Following a two-step P values calibration approach by Ma et al. (Ma et al. 2015), 18 genes were confirmed as RAGs (p_max < 0.05; Fig. 3a, Table S5). Intriguingly, functional enrichment analysis revealed that these 18 RAGs were predominantly enriched in GO terms related to mechanical stimulus (GO: 0009612) and ear development (GO: 0043583 and GO: 0048839), with three genes (PJVK, TCAP, and TIFAB) assigned to both terms (Fig. 3b, Table S6).

Root-to-tip ω values of genes with significant correlation with RVFL. a) Ranked correlation coefficients from correlation analyses between log10 (root-to-tip ω) of 18 genes and RVFL. b) The top 10 GO terms in GO enrichment of identified RAGs. c) Regression analyses between root-to-tip ω and RVFL in four genes related to ear development. ω values, dN/dS values; RAGs, RVFL-associated genes; p_all, the P value calculated from correlation analyses without removing any species; p_max, the final P value obtained from a two-step correction in correlation analyses.

Among four RAGs linked to ear development, the evolutionary rate of TIFAB was significantly negatively correlated with RVFL. In contrast, the rates of the other three genes (PJVK, INSIG2, and TCAP) showed a significantly positive correlation (Fig. 3c). Specifically, the PJVK (Pejvakin) gene, expressed in the inner hair cells, outer hair cells, supporting cells, and spiral ganglion neurons, has mutations that cause autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss in humans (Delmaghani et al. 2006; Collin et al. 2007; Schwander et al. 2007). Similarly, the TCAP (Titin-cap) gene, whose transcript was observed in the otic vesicles of the mouse inner ear, plays a role in outer hair cell function and maintenance (Gregorio et al. 1998). Downregulation of TCAP gene expression is associated with hearing loss (Matsuoka et al. 2019).

For genes lacking specific functional enrichment terms, manual literature searches confirmed their links to auditory function (Table S16). For example, KO studies of KCNA1 gene, which encodes the low-threshold potassium channel subunit Kv1.1 (Adamson et al. 2002), can reduce sensitivity to sound localization in mice (Allen and Ison 2012). The ACYP1 (Acylphosphatase 1) gene, expressed in the human cochlea, has been identified as a candidate for nonsyndromic hereditary hearing loss (Sajan et al. 2007). These findings suggested that the adaptive evolution of genes associated with hearing loss or ear development could contribute to the variation of RVFL in these species.

Rapidly evolving genes (REGs) and positively selected genes (PSGs) for long RVFL species

To investigate selective pressures by comparing the long RVFL species group with the short RVFL species group (species with RVFL >0 and species with RVFL <0, respectively), we applied the branch and branch-site models in PAML to identify REGs and PSGs specific to the long RVFL group. Using the branch model, we identified 38 REGs with significant evolutionary rate acceleration after false discovery rate (FDR) correction (adjusted P < 0.05; Fig. 4a, Table S7). The ratio of evolutionary rates (dN/dS) of these genes in the long RVFL species group to the short RVFL species group was as high as 8.2 (Figure 4a). Functional enrichment analysis of the 38 REGs revealed significant associations with the KEGG pathway for arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (hsa05412) and GO terms related to histone binding (GO: 0042393) and cardiac chamber morphogenesis (GO: 0003206) (Fig. 4b, Table S8).

Furthermore, we identified 20 PSGs in the species with long RVFL using the branch-site model (Table S9). The enrichment analysis illustrated that these PSGs were significantly associated with the response to peptide hormone (GO biological processes, GO: 0043434) and neuronal cell body (GO cellular components, GO: 0043025) (Fig. 4b, Table S10). To further investigate the functional roles of these genes, we combined the 20 PSGs with 58 REGs and conducted enrichment analysis on the merged gene set. This analysis indicated significant enrichment in GO biological processes related to the cellular response to organonitrogen compounds (GO: 0071417) and regulation of neuron projection development (GO: 0010975) (Fig. 4b, Table S11). Across these enrichment analyses, terms linked to cardiac and neuronal functions were prominently represented (Fig. 4b). Additionally, the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the merged gene sets identified RYR2 protein as the central hub protein with the highest node degree of 4 (Fig. 4c).

Among the REGs and PSGs identified in species with long RVFL, there was no significant enrichment in auditory-related functions or pathways. However, our manual functional annotation revealed that nearly a quarter of these genes (14/58) were directly or indirectly associated with hearing (Table S16). For example, the RYR2 gene is expressed in inner hair cells and supporting cells of the organ of Corti (Lioudyno et al. 2004). This gene plays a critical role in releasing calcium ions during auditory neurotransmission and sound transduction (Morton-Jones et al. 2006). The inactivation of ATF6 gene, encoding activating transcription factor 6, has been implicated in progressive sensorineural hearing loss in humans. Additionally, it causes widespread damage or localized loss of cochlear hair cells in mice (Lee et al. 2024). Interestingly, the ATF6 gene is also linked to the destruction of ossicles (Dong et al. 2019). Among PSGs, a mutation in PKHD1L1 gene (the posterior probabilities of sites 2718 and 3982 are 0.84 and 0.88, respectively.) which is expressed at the tips of inner ear stereocilia, was observed to cause hearing loss in mice, zebrafish, and humans (Wu et al. 2019; Redfield et al. 2024). Similarly, mutations in COL1A1 (Collagen type I alpha 1 chain) gene (among the positively selected sites, the posterior probabilities for sites 1210 and 1212 are 0.93 and 0.94, respectively.), another PSG, have been linked to hearing loss (Pan et al. 2023).

Furthermore, considering the potential systematic biases introduced by different tree topologies, we reconstructed gene trees for each gene and repeated the analyses (Table S12, Data S1). The density distributions of both P values and FDR values showed extremely high similarity between two frameworks (Figure S2 and S3). Of the 34 REGs identified using gene trees, 33 overlapped with the 38 genes detected using the species tree (Table S13). Similarly, of the 17 PSGs identified via gene trees, 10 overlapped with the 20 genes from the original analysis (Table S14). These results further demonstrate that the main selection signals are broadly robust to differences in tree topology.

Genes with unique mutations (GUM) among species with extreme phenotypes

To examine whether there were specific amino acid substitutions in proteins influencing the evolution of RVFL, we screened for unique mutations across all 1990 proteins. The results showed no consistent substitutions when species with long RVFL (RVFL > 0) were treated as the foreground group. However, in species with extreme RVFL phenotypes (RVFL > 0.2 or <−0.4), we identified seven unique amino acid substitutions in seven proteins (Table S15). Notably, the PPP1R36 protein also exhibited a specific substitution (R290H) in black howler monkeys (Alouatta caraya), localized in a loop structure of the protein (Fig. 5a and 5b). Of the five identified substitutions in these key proteins, four were situated within key functional domains, with two involving amino acids of distinct physicochemical properties (Fig. 5a, Figure S4). Functional predictions using PolyPhen2 and SIFT indicated that three of these mutations might impact protein function (Fig. 5a). Among the seven proteins with unique mutations, four proteins (PPP1R36, PKHD1L1, TOGARAM2, and FRAS1) have been implicated in inner hair cell function or hearing loss (Table S16). Specifically, the PPP1R36 (Protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 36) gene, expressed in the inner hair cells of the inner ear, showed evidence of positive selection in mammal lineages (Pisciottano et al. 2019). Likewise, variants in TOGARAM2 gene, which is also expressed in cochlear hair cells, can cause autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (Ramzan et al. 2024). It highlighted the potential role of hearing-related genes in RVFL evolution.

The intersection of multiple gene sets

We analyzed the overlaps among multiple gene datasets to integrate the genetic changes identified in this study. The results showed that at least one gene in each of the four datasets, REGs, PSGs, RAGs, and GUM, has undergone relaxed selection (classified as genes with relaxed selection, GRS) (Fig. 6a, Table S17). Specifically, the intersection with GRS included 28 REGs, two GUMs (FRAS1 and TOGARAM2), two RAGs (PJVK and BMP2 K), and one PSG (SOGA1) (Fig. 6a). Notably, these 28 REGs were all under purifying selection (Table S7). Furthermore, the PKHD1L1 gene, identified as both a PSG and a GUM (Fig. 6a), underscores the potential role of adaptive changes in PKHD1L1 gene for the evolution of RVFL.

Moreover, we conducted a comprehensive functional analysis of genes falling under REGs, PSGs, GUM, and RAG. We identified 25 genes linked to hearing loss or ear development (Table S16, Fig. 6b). Further functional enrichment of these 25 genes revealed significant associations with processes such as embryonic morphogenesis (GO: 0048598) and chordate embryonic development (GO: 0043009), in addition to the inner ear development (GO: 0048839) and response to mechanical stimulus (GO: 0009612) (Figure S5, Table S18). These findings suggested that the genetic mechanisms underlying RVFL evolution could be closely tied to embryonic developmental pathways.

VFL alterations in Pjvk KO mice

Taking into account the precision of the screening method, the optimality of the parameters in PGLS correlation analysis, and the maximization of limited resource utilization, we finally chose the positively correlated gene PJVK, which is associated with hearing loss and inner ear development (Delmaghani et al. 2006), for further experimental validation. To know whether the VFL of mice would change significantly after knocking out the Pjvk gene, we measured the VFL in WT and KO mice after MRI scanning (Fig. 7a and 7b, Data S2). The result revealed that the VFL in Pjvk KO mice (n = 4) was significantly greater than that in WT mice (WT: 615.75 ± 32.18, KO: 760.55 ± 22.88, t = −7.34, P < 0.001) (Fig. 7c, Table S19).

Discussion

Terrestrial mammals have evolved vocal folds of varying lengths, enabling diverse vocalization functions such as conveying interspecific and intraspecific size, mate quality, and emotional states (August and Anderson 1987; Taylor et al. 2008; Briefer 2012; Charlton et al. 2012). Despite their ecological and behavioral significance, little is known about the phenotypic and genetic evolution of VFL. Aiming to deepen our understanding of this evolution, in this study, we investigated the dynamics of phenotypic diversification of RVFL and its driving factors using phylogenetic comparative methods across 75 species (Cornwallis and Griffin 2024). We followed the research guide of PhyloG2P (Smith et al. 2020) and further identified the genetic basis of RVFL variations through experimental validation.

The diversified evolution of RVFL in terrestrial mammals

Our study demonstrated a significant positive relationship between VFL and BM across diverse terrestrial mammals. This allometric relationship corroborates earlier findings derived from a limited subset of mammalian species (Garcia and Dunn 2019). Based on the observed allometry, RVFL values were calculated from the PGLS regression of VFL on BM across species and further utilized to reconstruct ancestral states and estimate evolutionary rates. All analyses revealed rapid diversification in RVFL across three mammalian orders during recent evolutionary periods. This similar pattern of RVFL variation marks a pivotal step in the evolutionary dynamics of terrestrial mammal vocalizations. Given the high intelligence, cognitive abilities (Dicke and Roth 2016), and close association between VFL and f0 (the crucial acoustic feature of vocal communication) in mammals (Titze et al. 2016), we suggest that the diversification of RVFL represents an adaptive response to the functional demands of complex vocal communication (Bowling et al. 2017; Blank 2021). In addition, our analysis traced changes in both RVFL and BM over evolutionary time. After the K–Pg extinction, results showed a rapid increase in mean RVFL, peaking shortly thereafter, indicative of intense selective pressure favoring enhanced vocalization. Similarly, BM expanded significantly after the K–Pg extinction event, consistent with previous findings (Smith et al. 2010). In fact, the phylogeny based on extant taxa may not directly capture variation in fully extinct lineages; our reconstruction reflected the inferable range of states for the common ancestor based on extant taxa.

In addition to examining overall trends, we assessed the differences in allometric integration and evolutionary rates across three mammalian orders. No significant differences were observed in either metric. The similar patterns of allometric integration suggest that the vocal folds in these orders likely exhibit comparable responses to fluctuations in evolutionary pressures (Bowling et al. 2020). As higher evolutionary rates are associated with greater residual variation, weaker scaling relationships, and reduced allometric integration (Kverková et al. 2022), these similarities implied consistent evolutionary dynamics across the groups. Notably, within our dataset, two species in the genus Alouatta (Alouatta caraya and Alouatta sara), recognized for their loud and quiet calls (de Cunha et al. 2015), exhibited unexpectedly high evolutionary rates of RVFL. Given the established negative correlation between VFL and fundamental frequency, howler monkeys produce exceptionally low vocal frequencies (Dunn et al. 2015). These lines of evidence suggested a potential adaptive role of extended VFL in intergroup competition or defense among howler monkeys (Kitchen et al. 2015). In contrast, the rapid adaptive evolution of VFL in harbor seals may reflect their more vocally reticent behaviors compared to other pinnipeds (Matthews et al. 2017). For raccoons, this phenomenon might result from their remarkable adaptability to changeable environments (Lotze and Anderson 1979; Daněk et al. 2023). Previous studies indicated that terrestrial mammals and birds could exaggerate perceived body size acoustically by lengthening their vocal organs (Fitch 1999; Charlton and Reby 2016; Pisanski and Reby 2021). This rapid and efficient acoustic adaptation or deceptive strategy may enhance mate choice and territorial dominance and even contribute to the origin and evolution of vocalic complexity (Pisanski et al. 2022). Our findings align with the acoustic size exaggeration hypothesis, illustrating how vocal anatomy and acoustic properties diversify in response to selective pressures (Charlton and Reby 2016; Dunn et al. 2026). These provide further insights into the interplay between anatomical adaptation and the evolution of vocal communication systems among terrestrial mammals.

Additionally, this study aimed to identify the key life history and ecological factors underlying RVFL evolution in terrestrial mammals. We found a significant correlation between OVFL and most continuous traits, whereas RVFL showed no significant association with any continuous trait. This pattern suggests that the raw cross-species associations between VFL and many continuous variables are largely mediated by body size, consistent with the pervasive role of allometric scaling in biological evolution, whereby body size constrains a wide range of traits across species (Peters 1986). Furthermore, our results point to the important role of social organization in RVFL evolution. Because longer vocal folds generally produce lower fundamental frequencies and may enable a broader range of pitch variation, they may provide a physical basis for conveying diverse information during complex social interactions, such as maintaining group cohesion or signaling individual identity (McComb and Semple 2005). These findings further suggest that deviations in VFL from allometric expectations may be associated with social selection, analogous to cases in which some species exaggerate their apparent body size through laryngeal enlargement (Hauser 1993). Notably, RVFL showed no significant association with the other 12 traits examined, further suggesting that RVFL evolution may be more closely linked to social factors than to broad life-history strategies or resource-allocation constraints.

Hearing-related genes underlay the most prevalent genetic changes in RVFL evolution

Lineage-specific evolutionary rates of genes can elucidate the evolutionary mechanisms underlying phenotypic diversification (Gillooly et al. 2005). In our study, we identified a total of 25 hearing-related genes using a series of molecular evolutionary analyses, functional enrichment, and manual literature searches (Table S16). Of the eight hearing-related genes that coevolved with RVFL, four (PJVK, INSIG2, TCAP, and TIFAB) were associated with ear development, and all eight (TCAP, MORC2, MAP3K14, PJVK, ACYP1, KCNA1, INSIG2, and TIFAB) were implicated in hearing loss or impairment. Intriguingly, our work also observed a large proportion (4/7) of hearing-related genes (FRAS1, PPP1R36, PKHD1L1, and TOGARAM2) when testing for mutations specific to species with extreme phenotypes (RVFL values >0.2 or <−0.4). Among these amino acid substitutions, the unique R290H mutation in PPP1R36 protein was also found in black howler monkeys (a species with extremely long RVFL), strictly classified as a convergent or parallel substitution. This substitution, identified in the PPPI_inhib domain that regulates the phosphatase activity of protein phosphatase 1 complexes, was located in the loop region of the 3D structure and was predicted to be damaging to protein function (Hendrickx et al. 2009). Similarly, the amino acid change (S1704L) in PKHD1L1 protein involved a shift from nonpolar amino acid (Leucine, L) to uncharged polar amino acid (Serine, S), which was predicted to be damaging to the protein function. This mutation was located in the IPT domain found in cell surface receptors and intracellular transcription factors involving DNA binding (Collesi et al. 1996). Moreover, the amino acid substitution (G150A) in TOGARAM2 protein was predicted to affect the protein function, and the TOGARAM2 gene was also detected as the gene with relaxed selection in species with long RVFL. These findings highlighted how mutations in hearing-related genes, mainly associated with ear development or auditory dysfunction, underlie the molecular evolution of RVFL among terrestrial mammals. In addition, we identified five PSGs (RRM2, CIB1, COL1A1, PKHD1L1, and MRPS15) and nine REGs (PLAG1, RYR2, TMEM11, ATF6, TMCC3, ITGB3, RSAD2, SAV1, and EXOC2) that were directly or indirectly associated with auditory function. Notably, the PKHD1L1 gene emerged as a prominent gene, functioning as both a PSG and one harboring unique mutation. The rapidly evolving RYR2 gene, which has undergone relaxed selection, encodes a central hub protein in two PPI networks (Fig. 4c, Figure S6). Collectively, the association of many REGs and PSGs with hearing loss or cochlear expression reinforced a key role of adaptive evolution in these genes during the RVFL evolution in terrestrial mammals.

Why do genes associated with hearing loss or ear development play a crucial role in the evolution of RVFL among terrestrial mammals? This question was inspired by our results of functional enrichment analysis, which revealed a coevolutionary relationship between the vocalization and auditory systems in terrestrial mammals. Notably, these hearing-related genes were significantly enriched for functions related not only to inner ear development and sensory perception, as expected, but also to embryonic morphogenesis and chordate embryonic development (Figure S5, Table S15). These findings suggested that the evolution of RVFL may be linked to broader developmental processes regulated by these genes, illustrating the intricate interdependence between vocalization and hearing systems during the evolution of terrestrial mammals.

Pharyngeal gill slits, one of the common characteristics of chordates (Rychel et al. 2006), evolve into gills in fish but exist only during embryonic development in tetrapods (Gillis and Tidswell 2017). In human embryos, pharyngeal arches—also referred to as visceral arches in tetrapods—begin forming during the fourth week of development and emerge as paired structures (Graham 2003). These arches develop into various muscles, bones, nerves, and arteries. Among them, the first and second pairs of pharyngeal arches develop into structures such as the malleus of the middle ear and the auricular muscles, respectively (Sadler 2018). In contrast, the sixth pair contributes to the formation of most of the intrinsic laryngeal muscles, including the vocalis muscle and the thyroarytenoid muscle (Netter 2014). Moreover, there is a clear interaction between vocalization and hearing. Specifically, previous studies showed that vocalization influences auditory processing within the auditory cortex (Metzner 2009), while auditory input affects the acoustic structure of vocalizations across a wide range of birds and mammals (Tyack 2016). In summary, the hearing-related genes played a critical role in the evolution of RVFL in terrestrial mammals, highlighting the reciprocal coevolution between the vocal and auditory systems.

To further confirm the coevolutionary relationship between the vocal and auditory systems, we analyzed the VFL between WT and Pjvk KO mice by MRI scan. The experimental result showed that the VFL in KO mice was greater than that in WT mice. Pjvk KO mice have been reported to primarily exhibit hearing impairment characterized by high-frequency hearing loss (Delmaghani et al. 2015; Kazmierczak et al. 2017). To explore further possibilities, we also measured the BM and ten acoustic features (including f0) in both groups of mice (Methods, Data S3). Comparison of BM revealed no significant difference between two groups (Figure S7, Table S19). Regarding the acoustic features, we observed some differences between two groups (Figure S8, Table S20), which may suggest that alterations in vocal anatomy could affect sound quality. However, statistical tests indicated that these differences were not significant (Table S21). We speculated that the acoustic distinctions might have been minimized by laryngeal movement control during mouse vocalization (Simonyan 2014) or that neuromuscular control strategies may have compensated for changes in vocal anatomy, thereby maintaining vocal stability (Ruat et al. 2022). However, there is indeed a negative correlation between VFL and f0 (Titze et al. 2016), which means that longer vocal folds represent lower f0. To sum up, this synergistic change between VFL and hearing frequency not only highlighted the crucial role of adaptive changes in PJVK gene in RVFL evolution but also further confirmed the coevolution between the vocalization and auditory systems in terrestrial mammals. In the future, provided resources and funding permit, there is the possibility of incorporating more screened key genes for functional validation.

Other potential genetic basis underlying the evolution of RVFL among terrestrial mammals

In addition to numerous hearing-related genes identified above, other functionally related genes may contribute to the evolution of RVFL in terrestrial mammals. For example, the GHRH (growth hormone-releasing hormone) gene whose evolutionary rate was associated with RVFL encodes hypothalamic hormone (Frohman and Kineman 2002). Deficiencies in its receptor can influence laryngeal size and vocal fold compliance, resulting in voice alterations such as shrillness, hoarseness, and spectral abnormalities (Moore et al. 2005). Mutations in the FRAS1 gene, which encodes an extracellular matrix protein, are associated with Fraser syndrome (McGregor et al. 2003). This syndrome results in abnormal vocal fold development, disrupting typical vibration patterns and altering phonation and voice quality (Tohidast and Mansuri 2020). The amino acid substitution (A1559T) in FRAS1 protein represented a shift from hydrophobic amino acid (Alanine, A) to uncharged hydrophilic amino acid (Threonine, T). This substitution occurred in the CSPG repeat domain, which can mediate the interaction between cells and the extracellular matrix (Staub et al. 2002). In addition, the GNPTAB gene has been implicated in vocalization. Its KO studies in mice demonstrate that the absence of this gene results in shorter calls, increased intervals between calls, and reduced pitch variability (Barnes et al. 2016). The mutation (V502I) in GNPTAB protein was located in the NL domain, which functions as an intercellular signal that specifies cell fate during animal development (Kimble et al. 1998). Furthermore, the TCAP gene, previously linked to hearing function, has also demonstrated significant roles in stretch perception and muscle development, which could influence vocal fold mechanics and adaptability (Knöll et al. 2002). Additionally, the SAV1 gene identified in this study was a core component of the Hippo pathway that influences vocal fold development (Yu and Guan 2013; Laitman et al. 2024). This suggested that the adaptive selection of SAV1 may also participate in RVFL evolution in terrestrial mammals by regulating the Hippo pathway to affect vocal fold development. Accordingly, adaptive changes in these genes, affecting both functional and structural properties of the vocal folds, likely represented important drivers of RVFL evolution in terrestrial mammals.

The functional enrichment analysis of REGs, PSGs, and their combination revealed that these genes were primarily associated with processes such as response to peptide hormones, cardiac chamber morphogenesis, and regulation of neuron projection development. Among these, we identified genes associated with cardiac chamber development, which may reflect a phenomenon of developmental gene pleiotropy (Paaby and Rockman 2013). In other words, during RVFL evolution, selective pressures may have indirectly driven changes in these pleiotropic genes, rather than acting on specialized genes. Notably, most REGs were under purifying selection but showed signals of relaxed selection, which is a frequently observed phenomenon (Wertheim et al. 2015). The pituitary gland releases thyroid-stimulating hormone and growth hormone, both of which are peptide hormones (Siddle and Hutton 1991). These hormones can influence the function and morphology of the larynx and vocal folds, ultimately affecting voice frequency production (Kadakia et al. 2013). This aligns with the aforementioned description of growth hormone deficiency caused by GHRH receptor deficiency (Moore et al. 2005). Furthermore, laryngeal motor neurons, located in the ventral medullary nucleus ambiguous, regulate vocal fold tension and control laryngeal muscles in mammals (Shiba et al. 2007).

Taken together, the adaptive evolution of these genes influencing vocal fold function and development—through peptide hormone release and neuronal control—may have played a crucial role in shaping VFL in terrestrial mammals. Therefore, the evolution of the vocal fold is essentially a complex process involving numerous genes acting via different evolutionary pathways. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the evolution of vocal folds and vocalization in terrestrial mammals, future studies could explore selection patterns using a more comprehensive and explicitly inferred gene set, ideally in combination with additional omics data and developmental biology approaches.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the evolutionary process and genetic changes driving the RVFL variation in terrestrial mammals under the computational framework of phylogenetic comparative methods and phyloG2P. There was a shared pattern of diversified evolution in three mammalian orders, underscoring adaptations for vocalization and supporting the acoustic size exaggeration hypothesis. We also found that social organization may potentially drive the RVFL evolution. We identified numerous hearing-related genes as pivotal to RVFL evolution combined with experimental verification in Pjvk KO mice, suggesting a coevolutionary link between vocalization and audition. Additionally, genes associated with peptide hormone release and neural control emerged as contributors to the molecular evolution of RVFL. These findings provide fresh insights into vocalization evolution in terrestrial mammals and offer better understanding of the origins of human speech and language.

Materials and methods

Trait and genetic data preparation of covered species

For trait data, our study contained 75 mammalian species across the orders Primates (n = 29), Carnivora (n = 32), and Artiodactyla (n = 14). These three orders were chosen because VFL data were available for multiple species within each that are sufficiently representative of VFL diversity across the order, in contrast to the limited data for other mammalian orders (e.g. VFL data are available for only one species of the nearly 1,400 species of Chiroptera). VFL measurements were primarily obtained from Bowling et al. (Bowling et al. 2020), supplemented with additional sources (Table S1). BM data were predominantly sourced from the PanTHERIA database (Jones et al. 2009), with supplementary measurements collected from other references (Table S1). All quantitative traits were averaged for each sampled species. Phylogenetic signal can describe the tendency for closely related species to exhibit similarities beyond what would be expected by chance (Blomberg et al. 2003). Using the phylosig function from phytools package, we calculated the Pagel's λ to detect the phylogenetic signal based on the log-transformed VFL (Revell 2024). The values of Pagel's λ range from 0 to 1, where 0 signifies phylogenetic independence (absence of phylogenetic signal) and 1 indicates perfect adherence to the Brownian motion model of trait evolution (Pagel 1999). Considering the allometric relationship between VFL and BM (see Results), we calculated RVFL (see Introduction) as the residuals from a log-log PGLS regression of absolute VFL on BM using the Caper R package (Orme et al. 2023) (Table S1).

For genetic data, we downloaded the multiple codon and protein alignments from an integrated comparative genomics platform (https://genome.senckenberg.de/download/TOGA/), which utilized human GENCODE 38 annotation as reference (Kirilenko et al. 2023). Of the 75 species in this study, 51 species (i.e. 16 primates, 23 carnivorans, and 12 artiodactyls) were selected for genetic analyses, based on data availability (Table S1). To obtain strictly aligned codon sequences and protein sequences, we downloaded the “codonAlignments.fa.gz,” “proteinAlignment.fa.gz,” and “orthologsClassification.tsv.gz” files for each species from this platform. Then, based on the ortholog classification in the “orthologsClassification.tsv” file, we selected all genes classified as “one2one.” Subsequently, using the gene IDs corresponding to “one2one,” we retrieved the corresponding codon sequences for humans and other species from the codon alignment files. Based on the sequences obtained, we merged them together, ensuring that the codon sequences for each species (including gaps introduced by alignment) were the same length as the human reference sequence. Finally, we obtained 1990 one-to-one orthologous genes shared among these species, each with a sequence length exceeding 150 bp. Stop codons were removed from codon alignments, and ambiguous sequences (e.g. NNN) were replaced with gaps to prepare data for downstream molecular evolution analyses. Phylogenetic trees for most analyses were retrieved from TimeTree5 (Kumar et al. 2022) using species names as input.

Ancestral state reconstruction and detection of evolutionary rates of RVFL

The ancestral phenogram was generated using the phenogram and fastAnc functions from phytools package in R (Revell 2013, 2024), which reconstruct ancestral states and map phylogenetic trees in a combined phenotype-time space (Evans et al. 2009). Ancestral states of mean RVFL were reconstructed using the Evomap package (https://github.com/JeroenSmaers/evomap). We rescaled the branches of the phylogenetic tree using the multiple-variance Brownian motion (mvBM) approach, which permits evolutionary rates to differ among various branches of the phylogenetic tree (Smaers et al. 2016). Utilizing this rescaled tree, we reconstructed ancestral states and derived ancestral values for each branch. Following the approach by Stiller et al. (Stiller et al. 2024), we calculated the mean and standard error of these ancestral values over all branches within 2-million-year (2 Ma) time windows, spanning from the root of all species to the present, to depict evolutionary changes over time.

Branchwise rates of RVFL were modeled using evorates R package (Martin et al. 2023). This tool employs a Bayesian approach to allow evolutionary rates to vary gradually and randomly across clades. The model estimates the rate evolution process as a product of two parameters: one controlling the rate at which random changes accumulate and another determining whether rates increase or decrease over time. Differences in average evolutionary rates among groups exhibiting allometric shifts were quantified using the compare.evol.rates function from geomorph package (Adams and Otárola-Castillo 2013). Statistical significance was assessed via permutation analysis of group-to-group rate ratios. The probability density plots of evolutionary rates among different groups in figures were generated using mvBM function of package evomap with 1,000,000 iterations (Smaers et al. 2016). Higher evolutionary rates correspond to increased residual variation per unit of time, indicating weaker allometric integration (i.e. less constrained allometric scaling). To analyze allometric shifts among groups, we applied the gls.ancova function from evomap package (Smaers and Rohlf 2016). Unlike standard ANCOVA, this method accounts for phylogenetic covariance among data points and allows for differences in regression intercepts among groups.

Correlation analyses between VFL and life history or ecological factors

To investigate the potential drivers of RVFL evolution in terrestrial mammals, we compiled data for 13 candidate life history and ecological traits and examined their associations with RVFL. The continuous traits included brain size, litter size, gestation length, weaning age, female maturity age, male maturity age, litters per year, interlitter/interbirth interval, and maximum longevity. The discrete traits included diet, mating system, social organization, and diel activity. Most trait data were obtained from the AnAge database (De Magalhaes and Costa 2009), the ADW database (https://animaldiversity.org/), and the AnimalTraits database (Herberstein et al. 2022). All data and additional sources are provided in Table S2.

We used different analytical approaches depending on the trait type. For ternary discrete traits (e.g. diet and diel activity), we followed the method described by Chen and Wiens (Chen and Wiens 2020) and re-coded them into binary variables. For example, omnivorous and carnivorous species were coded as 1 and herbivorous species as 0; this binary variable was termed “minimum herbivore.” Other trait categorizations were coded analogously. Associations between two continuous variables were tested using pgls function in R package Caper (Orme et al. 2023). Variables were log-transformed when necessary to improve normality. Associations between continuous and discrete traits were analyzed using the MCMCglmm function in R package MCMCglmm (Hadfield 2010), which fits generalized linear mixed models within a Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo framework. This approach accommodates different error distributions, including Gaussian, Poisson, and multinomial distributions. For each analysis, the number of iterations, burn-in period, and thinning interval were adjusted as needed to ensure adequate model convergence.

Scanning for RAGs

To identify genes coevolving with RVFL, we adopted PGLS regression method implemented in Caper package in R (Orme et al. 2023). Evolutionary rates for each gene across species were estimated by calculating the ω value (the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions, dN/dS) at all nodes of given phylogenetic tree using the free-ratio model of CODEML program in PAML 4.9j (Yang 2007). ω values greater than 1, less than 1, and equal to 1 indicate positive selection, purifying selection, and neutral evolution, respectively (Yang 2007). Based on these estimates, we derived root-to-tip ω values for each species by averaging ω values across all nodes from the last common ancestor to each tip (Montgomery et al. 2011). Log-transformed root-to-tip ω (log10 root-to-tip ω), along with RVFL and phylogenetic tree, were then used as inputs for the PGLS regression to preliminarily identify RAGs.

To ensure the reliability of the identified genes, we performed a two-step P value (defined as “p_all”) correction procedure which is an alternative to multiple testing correction, adapting the approach described by Ma et al. (Ma et al. 2015). In the first step, we removed the species with the largest residual from the initial PGLS regression and re-performed the analysis to obtain corrected P values, hereafter referred to as “p_robust.” In the second step, starting with the dataset from the first step, we sequentially excluded each remaining species, re-conducted PGLS regression analyses, and recorded the largest P value across these iterations, which we termed “p_max”. After two steps of rigorous P value correction, genes with p_all, p_robust, and p_max values all below the threshold of 0.05 were identified as true RAGs.

Testing for REGs and PSGs

REGs and PSGs were identified using the CODEML algorithm implemented in PAML v4.9j (Yang 2007). Branches corresponding to species with RVFL >0 were designated foreground, whereas all other branches were treated as background.

We identified REGs using the branch model in PAML v4.9j, comparing the two-ratio model (alternative hypothesis) with the one-ratio model (null hypothesis). The one-ratio model allows the foreground branches to have the same ω value as the background branches, while the two-ratio model assumes that the ω values of foreground branches are different from those of background branches. Model fit was assessed using a likelihood ratio test (LRT), and significance was determined via a chi-square test with FDR correction. Genes with ω values on foreground branches greater than those on background branches and corrected P values <0.05 were classified as REGs.

PSGs were identified using the branch-site model, comparing the Ma model (alternative hypothesis), which allows each codon in the foreground branch to have a different ω value, with the Ma0 model (null hypothesis), which assumes no positive selection in the foreground branch. LRTs and FDR corrections were applied, and codons with Bayes empirical Bayes posterior probabilities >0.8 were considered under positive selection (Yang et al. 2005). To investigate genes under relaxed selection in species with long RVFL, we implemented LRT analyses on the orthologous genes in RELAX of HYPHY (Wertheim et al. 2015). The null model with K = 1 and the alternative model allowing K to be estimated for each hypothesis test were compared. Values of K < 1 indicated relaxed selection, while K > 1 indicated intensified selection in the tested branches.

Following the recommendation of Álvarez-Carretero et al. (Álvarez-Carretero et al. 2023), our selection pressure analyses were based on thousands of 1:1 orthologous genes, making the species tree the preferred framework. Nevertheless, considering the potential systematic biases introduced by different topologies, we additionally inferred a maximum-likelihood gene tree for each gene using the best-fit substitution model selected by IQ-TREE and reran the analyses (Minh et al. 2020). Using these gene trees, we repeated the identification of REGs and PSGs. Furthermore, to systematically compare the results between the species tree and gene tree frameworks, we employed two complementary approaches to compare the distributions of statistical inferences (P values and FDR values) across different topologies: the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test and the 1-Wasserstein distance (W1). KS tests were performed in R 4.4.0 with the ks.test function, from which the D statistic was obtained (Massey 1951). The W1 (P = 1) was calculated using the wasserstein1d function from the transport package (https://github.com/dschuhmacher/transport). For both metrics, values closer to 0 indicate higher similarity between distributions.

Identification of unique mutations in species with extreme phenotype

Unique amino acid substitutions associated with extreme RVFL phenotype were identified using Fasparser2 software, leveraging multiple sequence alignments (Sun 2018). Species with extreme phenotypes were classified as Group 1, while all other species were assigned to Group 2. Genes with key mutations were picked out by comparing the two groups of sequence data using the Segregating Site Identification function. To define extreme phenotypes, species were categorized into four RVFL classes: >0.2, <−0.2, <−0.3, and <−0.4, based on the distribution of RVFL data.

Phenotypic convergence can be caused by specific amino acid substitutions that evolved independently among different species (Natarajan et al. 2015). To investigate molecular convergence in the strict sense in species with RVFL >0.2, we used the high-quality human reference sequence to identify corresponding sites in other species (e.g. Alouatta caraya) without any genomic annotation via NCBI BLASTn (Johnson et al. 2008). To ensure reliability, we excluded the sites whose upstream and downstream sequences were relatively not conserved.

We retrieved the functional domain information for the key sites from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) and labeled these sites to 3D protein structure predicted by AlphaFold (Jumper et al. 2021) using PyMOL (Yuan et al. 2017). To evaluate the potential effects of key mutations on protein function and physicochemical properties, we conducted predictions using PolyPhen-2 (Adzhubei et al. 2010) and SIFT (Sim et al. 2012). In PolyPhen-2, the scores range from 0 to 1, representing benign, possibly damaging (with low confidence), and probably damaging (with high confidence). In SIFT, a score less than or equal to 0.05 means damaging; otherwise, it means the substitution is tolerated.

Enrichment analysis and PPI analysis

Gene Ontology (GO; including biological process, cellular component, and molecular function) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using Metascape (https://metascape.org/) (Zhou et al. 2019) in conjunction with clusterProfiler R package (Wu et al. 2021), both with the human gene annotation library as the reference. Among them, the reference backgrounds used in Metascape and clusterProfiler were 30,286 and 20,607 functionally annotated human protein-coding genes, respectively. The PPI network was constructed using STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/) with default parameters (Szklarczyk et al. 2019). The minimum required interaction score was set to 0.4.

Mice and genotyping

Pjvk KO mice were generated on the 129S2/SvPasCrl background using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing. Single-guide RNAs were designed to target the third exon of PJVK, inducing a 34 bp deletion that resulted in a frameshift mutation and premature translational termination. Successful introduction of the mutation was verified by Sanger sequencing analysis.

Mouse genomic DNA was extracted from toe tissue samples using the One Step Mouse Genotyping Kit (Vazyme, PD101). For PJVK gene amplification, PCR reactions were prepared in 20 μL volumes containing 10 μL 2× Taq PCR MasterMix (Vazyme, P131), 1 μL each of gene-specific primers (forward, 5′-CCTACCTCAGATTCACACACGTCAT-3′; reverse, 5′-CGACATCAAGGCTTACTTTATGTGG-3′; 10 μM each), 1 μL genomic DNA template, and 7 μL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; followed by final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplified products were resolved by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels.

All mice were housed and maintained under standard conditions at the Department of Laboratory Animal Science, Fudan University. Animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (3% to 5% for induction, 1% to 2% for maintenance) delivered via an RWD small animal anesthesia system. Respiratory rate and pedal reflex were monitored to ensure proper anesthesia depth. Animals were positioned in a dedicated magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-compatible holder to minimize motion artifacts during scanning. High-resolution MRI was performed using an 11.7 T preclinical MRI system (Bruker BioSpec, Billerica, MA, 117/16) with a Tx/Rx quadrature coil. Higher-resolution T2-weighted image was acquired using a TurboRARE sequence with parameters as follows: repetition time/echo time = 4500/22 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, 40 slices, flip angle = 90, field of view = 35 × 35 mm2, matrix = 256 × 256, rare factor = 8, average = 4, and acquisition time = 9 min 36 s. The laryngeal region was localized using rapid gradient echo localizer scans.

Phenotypic measurements and statistical analysis

VFL measurements were performed in the Weasis v4.5.1 software (https://weasis.org/en/index.html). All statistical analyses here were conducted with R 4.4.0 in RStudio software. Prior to the statistical analysis, both normality and homogeneity of variances of the data were confirmed in this study. All measurements were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed by two-tailed t-test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
