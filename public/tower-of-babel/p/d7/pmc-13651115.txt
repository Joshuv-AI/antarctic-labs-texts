Physical activity in social contexts during the COVID-19 pandemic: a latent transition analysis
Authors: Nerida Koert van der Linden, Matthew Bourke, Patricia Tucker, Jennifer D. Irwin, Shauna M. Burke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile physical activity behaviours have been explored during the COVID-19 pandemic, little is known about the social contexts in which physical activity took place during this time of crisis. The purpose of this longitudinal study was to explore the physical activity-related social contexts in which young people (aged 15–24) engaged during the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic in Ontario, Canada. Specific objectives included identifying: (1) how patterns of engagement in social contexts changed over time; and (2) demographic/descriptive predictors of social context-related class membership.MethodsOnline surveys were completed from: (1) July–September 2020; (2) November 2020–January 2021; and (3) July 2021–January 2022. Data on gender, living situation, physical activity, and physical activity-related social contexts were collected. Latent transition analysis was conducted to determine how participant’s social contexts changed over time and chi-squared tests were used to assess predictors of latent profile membership.ResultsParticipants (n = 328; Mage = 22.95) were categorized into three latent classes: 1 = mainly active alone; 2 = limited physical activity in all contexts; and 3 = mainly active with others. At Time 1, nearly 70.0% of participants were in Class 1; at Times 2 and 3, most participants (62.1% and 53.6%, respectively) were in Class 2. From Times 1–2, most participants (67.1%) transitioned from Class 1 to Class 2. From Times 2–3, more than 75.0% of participants remained in their respective classes. Class membership was associated with various predictors at different time points.ConclusionResults underscore the need to promote participation in a variety of physical activity-related social contexts, particularly during times of crisis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28590-9.

Regular participation in physical activity is associated with a wide variety of health benefits [1–3]. For adolescents and young adults specifically, such benefits include improved cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness, as well as enhanced skeletal and cardiometabolic health [1–4]. Participation in regular physical activity has been found to play an important role in improving some psychological variables (e.g., self-esteem, anxiety and depression symptoms, cognitive function) [4, 5], satisfaction with life [1], and overall wellbeing in young people [1, 5]. Physical activity is also related with numerous social benefits, including an enhanced sense of belonging [1], reduced loneliness [6], and greater social cohesion [1, 6]. Despite such benefits, physical activity levels among young people remain low. Globally, more than 80% of adolescents (aged 11–17) [7] and nearly one third of adults (aged 18+) [8] have been found to be insufficiently active. In Canada, similar trends have been observed in that almost 55% of youth (aged 12–17) [9] and half of adults (aged 18+) are considered to be insufficiently active [10].

Given the challenges and consequences associated with physical inactivity, numerous factors and interventions have been explored in relation to the promotion of physical activity [11–14]. One such factor is the social context in which physical activity takes place. There has been a substantial amount of support—from both theoretical and empirical perspectives—for the conclusion that exercising in a group context is superior to exercising alone in terms of adherence [15–17]. From a theoretical perspective, Baumeister and Leary [18] have been widely cited for their seminal work outlining humans’ innate need to belong and connect meaningfully with others. In addition, self-determination theory (SDT [19, 20])—which has been used to explain and predict physical activity behaviour in many contexts [21, 22]—includes a focus on the fulfilment of basic psychological needs including relatedness (i.e., feeling connected to others) [19]. Similarly, Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) suggests that health behaviours are shaped through reciprocal interactions among personal, behavioural, and environmental factors [23, 24], emphasizing the important role of social and physical environments in shaping physical activity and supporting its maintenance over time.

From an empirical perspective, several studies have been conducted to explore the relationship between social context and physical activity behaviour [15, 16, 18]. For example, Burke and colleagues conducted a meta-analysis to compare the effectiveness of different exercise contexts on five categories of outcomes—adherence, social interaction, quality of life, physiological effectiveness, and functional effectiveness [15]. Physical activity interventions implemented in 44 studies (including > 4,500 participants) were categorized into one of four social contexts: home-based programs with no contact from researchers or health-care professionals; home-based programs with some contact; standard or ‘typical’ group exercise classes; and group exercise classes in which the leader/instructor implemented group dynamics strategies to enhance class cohesion (i.e., “true groups”). Results revealed a common trend across dependent variable categories, in that exercising with others in a true group setting was found to be superior to exercising in a standard exercise class or at home with contact from researchers or health professionals. Further, exercising at home with contact was found to be superior to exercising at home without contact.

Burke and colleagues have also explored exercise preferences for [25], and the propensity of young people to meet physical activity guidelines depending on [26], the social context(s) in which they exercise. With regard to preferences, it was found that in comparison to three other social contexts (i.e., exercising in a structured class, exercising alone in an exercise setting, and exercising completely alone), the most preferred context for aerobic and strength training for both female (n = 403) and male (n = 198) university students was exercising with others outside of a structured exercise class. Conversely, the least preferred context reported by female students was exercising completely alone, whereas for males, it was exercising in a structured class [25].

Burke and colleagues also conducted a cross-sectional study to examine whether exercising in the abovementioned social contexts was related to whether female (n = 398) and male (n = 196) university students met the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/American College of Sports Medicine physical activity guidelines (i.e., accumulating at least 30 min of moderate intensity physical activity on most—and ideally all—days of the week) [26, 27]. Results showed that university students who participated in three or more physical activity contexts were significantly more likely to meet the physical activity guidelines (61.2%) than those who reported engaging in physical activity in only one (9.9%) or two (28.9%) social contexts.

Throughout the pandemic, governments in Canada and elsewhere have implemented a range of public health measures aimed at reducing the spread of the COVID-19 virus [28, 29]. Given the aim and inherent nature of many of these public health measures (i.e., minimizing in-person interactions), the social behaviours of humans, including those pertaining to physical activity, were altered considerably. Within Canada, the Government of Ontario implemented some of the longest and most stringent COVID-19 restrictions of all provinces and territories [29–31]. In fact, it was documented that gyms and fitness facilities in Ontario were closed for more than 400 days throughout the first few years of the pandemic [32]; and, when permitted to re-open, additional restrictions (e.g., capacity limits, physical distancing, masking, vaccination requirements) were imposed on these establishments in an ongoing effort to protect citizens [33].

The pandemic-related closures and measures outlined above resulted in diminished opportunities for adolescents and young adults to be physically active with others [30]. In fact, it was widely noted, both in the literature and mainstream media, that many individuals transitioned their exercise routines from gym to home settings during the early stages of the pandemic [34–38]; and, even when gyms and recreation facilities re-opened with additional restrictions, many members were hesitant to re-join [37] or had established other (more solitary) exercise routines [34, 35]. These restrictions and shifting behavioural patterns likely limited the number of social contexts in which physical activity was performed throughout the pandemic, which is particularly problematic for young people given that participation in a variety of social contexts (i.e., 3+) has been associated with a greater likelihood of meeting activity guidelines [26].

Taken together, the literature presented above—alongside the numerous pandemic-related challenges experienced by young people and their overall low levels of physical activity in Canada [7–10]–raise important questions about the social contexts in which physical activity took place during this time of crisis. Moreover, an examination of changes in physical activity-related social context participation over time is warranted given their potential influence on physical activity and to better understand how young people changed their behaviours and routines during the early pandemic. Importantly, these insights could be used to inform future public health strategies and interventions aimed at supporting physical activity during times of disruption.

Although much research has focused on overall declines in physical activity during the pandemic [9, 28, 30], the physical activity patterns of young Ontarians throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, from a social context perspective, remain unexplored. From a theoretical standpoint, belongingness theory [18] and Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [19] suggest that when physical activity environments support and satisfy individuals’ need for connectedness, motivation and sustained engagement are more likely to follow. Consistent with these perspectives, the SCT [23, 24] reinforces the importance of social context by highlighting the influence of social and environmental factors on individual health behaviours. Despite this theoretical foundation, little is known about how such supportive social contexts may—or may not—have been present during the pandemic. Addressing these gaps, the overall purpose of this longitudinal study was to examine the social contexts in which adolescents and young adults (aged 15–24) engaged in physical activity throughout the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic in Ontario, Canada. Specific objectives included: (1) identifying how patterns of participant engagement in physical activity-related social contexts changed over time throughout the pandemic (from 2020 to 2022); and (2) exploring whether specific demographic and descriptive variables (i.e., gender, educational information, living arrangement, physical activity level) predicted social context-related class membership at each of the three time points.

Methods

Study design

This longitudinal, survey-based study was conducted as part of the second phase of a program of research entitled “iBelong” [39], which was designed to explore adolescents’ and young adults’ perceptions of public health guidelines, social connectedness and belonging, social media use, health behaviours, and psychological wellbeing during the COVID-19 pandemic in Ontario, Canada. Data were collected via online surveys at three timepoints during the first two years of the pandemic: (1) July 2020 – September 2020; (2) November 2020 – January 2021; and (3) July 2021 – January 2022. Ethical approval for this project was obtained from the Non-Medical Research Ethics Board and Research Ethics Board at the two host universities.

Study context

Throughout the data collection phases of this study, pandemic-related public health measures in Ontario were implemented, removed, and re-instated at various times [40]. Time 1 coincided with the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic and Stage 3 of Ontario’s gradual reopening framework, wherein almost all businesses (including gyms and recreational facilities) and public spaces were permitted to re-open with ongoing restrictions after being closed since March 2020 [41–43]. At Time 2, Ontarians were in the midst of the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic [44, 45]; amidst rapidly growing COVID-19 numbers, public health restrictions varied by health unit region, and a subsequent province-wide shutdown was imposed wherein all schools and businesses were closed for 2–4 weeks depending on location and local case counts [43]. As a result, gyms and exercise facilities were closed for most (if not all) of the second data collection period. Lastly, at Time 3—and following a 4-week provincial stay-at-home order in April 2021 [46] — Ontarians experienced the third, fourth, and fifth waves of the COVID-19 pandemic [45]. In June 2021 (just prior to Time 3 data collection), and alongside an increase in vaccination uptake across the province [47], the Government of Ontario implemented the Roadmap to Reopen framework [48] which eventually allowed for gyms, community centres, and recreation/fitness facilities to re-open with capacity limits and masking requirements [49].

Participants and recruitment

Participants were eligible for the study if they were between 15 and 24 years of age, were living in Ontario, and had access to the internet. Eligible participants were excluded if they did not provide consent. Parental/guardian consent for participants under the age of 16 was waived by the two research ethics boards (Western University Non-Medical Research Ethics Board and Lakehead University Research Ethics Board), as these participants were deemed to have the capacity to understand the study procedures and to make an informed decision about participation. Given that latent transition analysis (LTA) requires a relatively large sample size for meaningful interpretation [50], we aimed to include at least 300 participants in this study as per recommended sample size ranges for LTA and similar analyses [51, 52].

Recruitment took place from July-September 2020 via social media, blog posts, course announcements and mass e-mails at the host universities, and word-of-mouth. Post-survey draws for a chance to win one of four pairs of Apple AirPods were advertised as a study incentive.

Procedure

Potential participants were able to access the first of the three online surveys directly through social media posts (via a QR code or link found in the post description and/or account bio), or through the link provided in class announcements and e-mails. Participants were asked to create a unique participant ID, after which they were directed to the first online survey which took approximately 30–40 min to complete. Participants were then asked to provide their email address for receiving subsequent survey links and reminders, followed by a question inviting them to opt into the post-survey draws.

For surveys two and three, participants were sent an email with the respective survey link at the start of each data collection window (i.e., November 2020 and July 2021, respectively). After entering their unique ID, participants were directed to the online survey, each of which took approximately 30 min to complete. Participants were again invited to provide an e-mail address for the study incentive draws.

Measures

The online surveys, hosted on Qualtrics, consisted of validated instruments and researcher-developed items, which were pilot-tested prior to implementation. With the exception of participant demographics, all survey sections were administered in random order to facilitate completion rates and reduce order bias [53]. For the current study, only participant responses pertaining to demographics, self-reported physical activity level, and physical activity-related social contexts were included (see Supplemental File 1 for a list of survey questions included in the study).

Demographic information

The demographic information and participant characteristics used in the present study included: age (calculated from date of birth); gender; ethnicity; student status; and living arrangement. Apart from age, participants were asked to select one option for each category. Open-ended text boxes were provided for participants who selected ‘self-describe’ (gender) or ‘other’ (ethnicity, living arrangement). Demographic information was collected at Time 1 only.

Physical activity level

An adapted version of the Godin-Shephard Leisure-Time Physical Activity Questionnaire [54] was used to assess participants’ self-reported physical activity level at all three time points. This questionnaire measures self-reported leisure-time physical activity over a 7-day period [54], and has been validated with adolescents and adult samples [55–57]. Rather than asking about a “typical 7-day period” as per the original instrument, participants in the present study were asked: “During the COVID-19 pandemic, approximately how many times per week do you do the following kinds of exercise for more than 15 minutes during your free time?”. Three exercise intensities were provided, with examples for each (e.g., mild – e.g., yoga; moderate – e.g., easy bicycling; and strenuous – e.g., running). Participants were instructed to enter a number (i.e., times per week) for each exercise intensity in the space provided.

Per Godin [54], a total weekly leisure-time activity score was calculated by multiplying the weekly frequencies of strenuous exercise by nine, moderate exercise by five, and mild/light exercise by three. Each exercise category was then summed to create a total score for each participant. Participants with a total score greater than 24 are considered “active”, whereas those with total scores between 14 and 23 are considered “moderately active” and those with total scores less than 14 are considered “insufficiently active/sedentary” [54].

Physical activity-related social contexts

Participants were asked the same four researcher-developed questions about their participation in physical activity-related social contexts at all three study time points. These questions were designed based on the literature and considering the dynamic nature of public health measures in Ontario (e.g., questions about group exercise classes could not be asked given restrictions in place). All items began with: “When thinking about your physical activity during the COVID-19 pandemic, how often have you engaged in physical activity … ?”. The context-specific portion of the items included: alone; with family members (parents or siblings), in-person; with a friend or romantic partner, in-person; and, with friends or fitness groups virtually. A 5-point Likert scale was used, with response options ranging from never (0) to always (4).

Response categories were collapsed into three categories: engaging in physical activity alone; engaging in physical activity with others in-person (including family members and friends/romantic partners); and, engaging in physical activity with others virtually. The response options were also collapsed into the categories of never/rarely, sometimes, and often/always.

Data analysis

Descriptive data

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were computed for participant demographic information and self-reported physical activity using SPSS (Version 28). The average age of participants was calculated using the mean and standard deviation. In line with guidance on the reporting of race and ethnicity in scientific journals [58], ethnicity responses were re-categorized as: Indigenous, White, South Asian, East Asian, Black, Southeast Asian, Latin American, North African/Middle Eastern, and Multiethnic. College and university student response options were combined for student status, and the categories for living arrangement were collapsed into three categories: living alone, with family (parent/guardian, siblings, other relatives), or with friends/roommates/significant other.

Objective 1: patterns of physical activity-related contexts over time

Latent transition analysis (LTA) was conducted in MPlus (Version 8.4) [59] to determine how participant membership in different classes [60] (i.e., transition probabilities/patterns) of social contexts changed across the three study time points. As noted above—and in line with recommendations by Collins and Lanza [60]—social context-related response categories were collapsed prior to conducting the LTA; this approach is considered acceptable when data sparsity arises (e.g., when response categories have small cell counts) and to improve the parsimony, stability, and interpretability of the LTA model [60].

A number of fit indices were considered to determine optimal class enumeration in the LTA model [50, 51]. Specifically, the log-likelihood [60], Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [61, 62] and sample-size adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SSA-BIC) [62] were used, where a repeated maximum log-likelihood value (i.e., global maxima) [62] and low values for the BIC and SSA-BIC indicate superior model fit [61, 62]. Relative entropy [51, 63] was also used to determine the number of classes to be used in the model, where higher values indicate a better model fit on a scale from 0 (randomly assigning participants to classes) to 1 (clear separation between classes and greater precision with assigning participants to classes) [63]. Although not a direct measure of model fit, entropy is commonly used to assess the quality of class classification in latent class and transition models [51]. Additionally, instead of estimating cross-sectional latent class analysis at each time point, class enumeration was guided by the longitudinal LTA model to provide a more accurate representation of the data [60]. In addition to empirical metrics, theoretical and research-based considerations were used to guide the selection of the appropriate number of classes, as well as the labelling of classes [50, 51]. This process was carried out by three researchers (NK, MB, SB) who discussed and agreed upon subjective labels for each distinct latent class.

To test if the classes had the same meaning across time, longitudinal measurement invariance was assessed [51]. Both weak (metric; constraining item-response probabilities but allowing thresholds to vary) and strong (scalar; constraining item-response probabilities and thresholds) invariance models were tested [51]. A model with varying estimated thresholds (i.e., weak invariance model) could not be supported. Thus, consistent with recommendations by Collins and Lanza [60] and Nylund-Gibson and colleagues [51], we proceeded with a more parsimonious and stable model assuming strong invariance by constraining item thresholds across time. Further, rather than modelling random effects, the LTA model estimated the correlation between latent categorical variables over time.

Once the optimal number of classes was determined, the LTA model was specified, which included estimating the latent status prevalence (i.e., percentage of individuals across classes) and transition probabilities (i.e., probability of moving from one latent class to another over time) [64]. This was carried out by sequentially regressing the classes (via multinomial logistic regression) onto each other in a single model (i.e., class membership at Time 2 on class membership at Time 1, and class membership at Time 3 on class membership at Time 2) to produce transition patterns of class membership over time. The syntax for the LTA model can be found in Supplemental File 2.

Objective 2: predictors of class membership at each time point

After estimating latent status prevalence and transition probabilities, four covariates (i.e., gender, living situation, student status, physical activity behaviour) were added to the specified LTA model. A series of omnibus chi-squared tests were conducted using MPlus [59] to explore whether these variables predicted class membership at each of the three time points. When omnibus tests were significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted to examine differences between classes (e.g., Class 1 vs. Class 2, Class 1 vs. Class 3, Class 2 vs. Class 3). Given the explanatory nature of the analysis, a conservative significance threshold of p < .01 was selected in place of a formal adjustment [51].

Missing data

Participants with data for at least two of the three study time points were included in the analyses, excluding 193 (37.1%) participants who provided data at only one time point. For the remaining participants (n = 328, 62.9%), 198 (60.7%) had complete data at all three time points, whereas 129 (39.3%) had data at two time points. Data missingness was explored using Little’s Missing Completely at Random Test [65], which did not provide evidence to reject the MCAR assumption (p = .290). Consequently, missing data were handled using full-information maximum likelihood given its common use with model-based analyses such as LTA [60, 66].

Results

Participants

A total of 328 participants (Mage = 22.9, SD = 2.2) were retained for analyses. Most participants identified as women (82.3%, n = 270), white (56.7%, n = 186), and being a university/college student (73.7%, n = 242). More than half (57.1%, n = 182) of participants reported living with family (parents, siblings, or other relatives). With regard to physical activity, most participants reported being physically active (56.1%, n = 184) at Time 1, while 37.2% (n = 122) were categorized as insufficiently active at this time point. Objective 1: patterns of physical activity-related contexts over time

LTA was used to identify how patterns of participant engagement in physical activity-related social contexts changed throughout the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic. The initial LTA model was fit for 2, 3, and 4 classes (see Supplemental File 4 for an overview of the indicators for each model). Ultimately, the 3-class model was selected for analyses given it had the lowest BIC and SSA-BIC, as well as relatively high entropy [63, 64]. In addition, global maxima was achieved, as the maximum log-likelihood value was replicated when the model was rerun with multiple random values [63]. The three classes in the LTA model were labelled and defined as follows: Class 1 – Mainly active alone: Characterized by a high proportion of participants who reported “often” or “always” engaging in physical activity alone, as well as high proportions of participants who reported “never” or “rarely” engaging in physical activity with others, in-person or virtually; Class 2 – Limited physical activity in all contexts: Characterized by high proportions of participants who reported “never” or “rarely” engaging in any of the physical activity-related social contexts (alone and/or with others in-person or virtually); and Class 3 – Mainly active with others in-person: Characterized by a high proportion of participants who reported “often” or “always” engaging in physical activity with others in-person, along with high proportions of those who indicated “never” or “rarely” engaging in physical activity alone and/or with others virtually. Class membership at each time point

The percentage of participants in each class at each time point is represented in Fig. 1. At Time 1, most participants (68.6%, n = 225) were in Class 1 (mainly active alone), whereas at Times 2 and 3, the highest proportions of participants were in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts; 62.1%, n = 203 at Time 2 and 53.6%, n = 176 at Time 3). Across all time points, the lowest proportions of participants were in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person) despite a small increase over time (14.9%, n = 49 at Time 1; 18.1%, n = 62 at Time 2; and 22.9%, n = 75 at Time 3).

Class transitions across time

Transition patterns from time 1 to time 2

Analyses showed that from Time 1 to Time 2, 67.1% of participants in Class 1 (mainly active alone) transitioned into Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts), and 20.5% of those in Class 1 transitioned into Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person).

The majority (70.0%) of participants in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts) at Time 1 remained in this class at Time 2, while the remaining 30.0% of participants in Class 2 transitioned to Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person). Interestingly, 100.0% of participants in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person) at Time 1 transitioned to another context at Time 2; 69.4% moved to Class 1 (mainly active alone), and 30.6% moved to Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts). Transition patterns from time 2 to time 3

Unlike the transition patterns observed from Time 1 to Time 2, analyses revealed minimal movement of participants between latent classes from Times 2 to 3. The vast majority (87.5%) of participants in Class 1 (mainly active alone) at Time 2 remained in this latent class at Time 3; the remaining 12.5% transitioned to Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts). Similarly, most (76.9%) participants in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts) at Time 2 remained in this class at Time 3, whereas 9.3% and 13.7% of participants transitioned to Class 1 (mainly active alone) and Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person), respectively. Lastly, most participants (75.9%) in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person) at Time 2 remained in this latent class at Time 3, while 18.2% transitioned to Class 2 (limited activity in all contexts) and 5.8% transitioned to Class 1 (mainly active alone). Objective 2: predictors of class membership at each time point

At Time 1, class membership was found to be significantly associated with gender (p < .001) and self-reported physical activity level (p < .001). Post-hoc analyses revealed that Class 1 (mainly active alone) had a significantly higher proportion of women (p < .001) than Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts) and that participants in Class 1 (mainly active alone) were significantly more likely to be physically active compared to those in the other latent classes (p < .001 versus Class 2; p = .003 versus Class 3). Additionally, participants in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts) were significantly more likely to be insufficiently active (p = .001) than those in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person).

At Time 2, class membership was found to be significantly associated with living arrangement (p < .001). Post-hoc analyses revealed that participants in Class 1 (mainly active alone) were significantly more likely to live with family compared to participants in Classes 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts; p < .001) and 3 (mainly active with others in-person; p < .001). Additionally, participants in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person) were significantly more likely to live with friends/roommates compared to those in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts; p = .006).

At Time 3, class membership was significantly associated with living arrangement (p < .001), student status (p = .008) and physical activity level (p = .002). Similar to Time 2, post-hoc analyses revealed that participants in Class 1 (mainly physically active alone) were significantly more likely to live with family compared to those in Classes 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts; p = .007) and 3 (mainly physically active with others in-person; p < .001). Again, participants in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person) were significantly more likely to live with friends/roommates than those in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts; p = .008). Participants in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person) were also significantly more likely (p = .008) to report being a non-student than those in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts). With regard to activity level, participants in Class 3 (mainly active with others in-person) were significantly more likely to be categorized as active (p = .002) than those in Class 2 (limited physical activity in all contexts). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the physical activity patterns of young people, longitudinally and in relation to social contexts, throughout the COVID-19 pandemic. Several key findings related to both the overall and specific objectives warrant further discussion and are highlighted below.

Regarding the patterns of engagement in physical activity-related social contexts over time (Objective 1), between Times 1 and 2, the largest observed shift in participant engagement was among those in the mainly active alone class who subsequently transitioned to the limited activity in all contexts class. This transition is unsurprising given numerous contextual (i.e., COVID-19 pandemic-related) and theoretical factors. Contextually speaking, the Ontario government had closed all businesses, including gyms and recreational facilities, prior to Time 1 [33, 43, 44] and the social/physical distancing measures in place necessitated that individuals reduce in-person interactions [35, 67]. As such, for many months early in the pandemic, exercising alone (or with those in one’s ‘bubble’ or ‘social circle’ [68]) was likely the only option available for many Ontarians [34–37]. Thus, since the assumed primary context in which individuals could be physically active during this time was alone (where nearly 70% of participants at Time 1 were in the mainly active alone class)—and given that exercising alone has been identified as the least preferred context among a sample of young people in Ontario [26]—participants in our study may have been less inclined or motivated to exercise at all and transitioned to limited activity in all contexts (the class with the largest proportion of participants at Times 2 and 3). Indeed, this assumption is supported by the self-reported physical activity data collected in our study, which showed significant decreases over time (i.e., from Time 1 to Time 2 and Time 1 to Time 3) in the proportion of participants who reported being sufficiently active, as well as significant increases over time (i.e., from Time 1 to Time 2 and Time 1 to Time 3) in the proportion of participants who reported being insufficiently active/sedentary (again, see Supplemental File 3 for self-reported physical activity data).

From Times 2 to 3, more than three quarters of participants in our study remained in the limited activity in all contexts latent class (Class 2); a finding that is also unsurprising given the limited physical activity options available for most Ontarians throughout these data collection time periods. Again, there were very few opportunities for young people in Ontario to exercise with others in a group setting; a social context that has been shown to be superior to other physical activity contexts for outcomes including exercise adherence, social interaction, and quality of life [17, 18, 26].

Additionally, research has shown that young people who participate in physical activity in a variety of social contexts are more likely to meet physical activity guidelines [26]. Therefore, given the lack of variety in social contexts available for physical activity during this time, it is unsurprising that the proportion of young people in our study who reported being insufficiently active/sedentary increased throughout the study period. These findings are also in line with data showing a general downward trend in physical activity levels among young people in Canada, and in Ontario in particular, from before the COVID-19 pandemic through its first two years [30].

From a theoretical perspective, SDT posits that individuals are more likely to engage in and sustain behaviours when their basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied [19, 20]. As noted above, the shifts in social contexts observed in our study are likely due, in large part, to the restrictive public health measures in place during the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic in Ontario. Generally speaking, however, our findings also appear to provide support for the notion that when young people are in an exercise context that does not fulfil their basic need for connectedness (as per both SDT [20, 21] and belongingness theory [19]), physical activity engagement is negatively impacted. Furthermore, the basic needs of autonomy and competence may not have been fulfilled during this time, as young people had limited options in terms of how, where, and when they could be physically active (i.e., autonomy), and may have lacked access to supportive physical activity environments that would have enabled feelings of competence. These unmet psychological needs may also help explain the overall decline in self-reported physical activity levels observed across time. Although some young people in our study, and in other recent studies [69, 70], seem to have demonstrated resilience and adapted their routines in the face of limited physical activity opportunities, the current findings underscore the importance of considering the satisfaction of basic psychological needs in physical activity promotion—particularly during times of widespread public health restrictions.

Insofar as predictors of engagement in social contexts are concerned (Objective 2), analyses revealed that at Time 1, class membership was significantly associated with gender and physical activity level. Specifically, a significantly higher proportion of women were found to be in the mainly active alone class (Class 1) versus the limited physical activity in all contexts class (Class 2). In their study examining exercise context preferences among university students, Burke and colleagues [23] reported that 43.5% of the female participants (Mage = 19.36) in their sample identified exercising alone as their least preferred physical activity context. While Burke and colleagues’ study focused on preferences, the present study measured self-reported engagement in various social contexts during a time of crisis which resulted in very few exercise options. As such, to engage in any physical activity at all during this time, women in the present study may have resorted to a lesser preferred context.

Our results also showed that participants in the mainly active alone class were significantly more likely to be physically active at Time 1 compared to those in the other two classes, and that participants in the limited physical activity in all contexts class (Class 2) were more likely to be insufficiently active compared to those in the mainly active with others in-person class (Class 3). At first glance—and given the evidence showing that exercising in group-based contexts is superior to other contexts in terms of adherence [17, 18, 26]—it may seem surprising that exercising mainly alone would be associated with greater self-reported physical activity levels. This may be reasonable, however, given the more limited contexts that were available for physical activity at the time of data collection as noted above for women. Immediately prior to the first wave of data collection, all businesses, including gyms and recreational facilities had been closed [33, 43]. The finding that those in the limited physical activity in all contexts class were more likely to be insufficiently active (versus those in the mainly active with others in-person class) is also important as it provides support for the argument that there may indeed be a positive association between engagement in various exercise contexts and physical activity levels.

At Times 2 and 3, class membership was found to be associated with living arrangement, wherein participants who were classified in the mainly active alone class were significantly more likely to live with family compared to those in the other latent classes. Further, participants who were in the mainly active with others in-person class were more likely to live with friends/roommates compared to those in the limited physical activity in all contexts class. There is evidence to suggest that living arrangements may influence young people’s physical activity levels [71–74]. For example, Villarreal et al. [74] investigated physical activity levels and living situations among university students (n = 109; Mage = 19.7) in the United States during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic (data collected in November, 2020) and found that university students who lived with their parents/guardians engaged in significantly less physical activity than students who did not. One explanation for this finding, as noted by the authors, could be that young people may have been more cautious or concerned about contracting and spreading the COVID-19 virus while living with family members versus living alone or with others outside of the home. Similarly, Small et al. [73] found that living off-campus (e.g., with family or roommates outside of the university setting) was associated with a significant decline in physical activity amongst undergraduate students in the United States. In the current study, more than half (56.7%) of participants reported living with family; as such—and even when permitted to be open during the pandemic—the use of public spaces such as gyms and fitness/recreation facilities might also have been less convenient for some students, thus disrupting physical activity levels and the social contexts in which they could be performed.

At Time 3, participants who were in the mainly active with others in-person latent class were found to be significantly more physically active than those in the limited physical activity in any context class. Indeed, as gyms and recreational facilities were gradually re-opening in Ontario around June 2021, and as other public health measures were also easing, the social contexts in which individuals could be physically active expanded. This is also reflected in the present study in that the proportion of participants who were in the mainly active with others in-person class increased steadily across all three time points. Thus, when young people were finally able to satisfy their need for connectedness in an exercise context [19–21], and given the research documenting the benefits of exercising in a group setting [17, 18, 26], it is no surprise that exercising with others in-person was associated with higher levels of physical activity.

Strengths and limitations

The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the importance of social connections for the health and wellbeing of all individuals; thus, from a health promotion perspective, understanding the social contexts in which people were physically active (or not) during the initial stages of a global crisis is critical. The longitudinal design of this study represents an important strength given that we were able to assess the patterns of engagement in physical activity-related social contexts among young people over time.

In terms of limitations, most participants in the current study identified as women, white, and being a university/college student; as such, generalizability is limited. Further, despite providing participants with the option to self-describe their gender, we were unable to include all gender identities in the analyses due to the limited number of self-described responses (n = 5). Another study limitation is the use of self-reported measures for physical activity behaviours and physical activity-related social contexts. Social desirability bias [75] could have led participants to overestimate their physical activity participation [56].

Implications and future directions

The findings of this study support previous literature suggesting that it is important, from an adherence perspective, to provide access to a variety of physical activity options and social contexts [26]. Our results revealed that a large proportion of young people shifted towards limiting their physical activity in all social contexts during the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic. As such, leveraging creative options to ensure that a variety of contexts remain available for physical activity during times of crisis and/or disruption (e.g., future pandemics, natural disasters, prolonged injuries, major life transitions) is critical. This might include the promotion of virtual group-based fitness programs and/or the safe use of outdoor exercise spaces for group-based activity. Indeed, recent literature has identified that leveraging digital platforms (e.g., Peloton, Apple Fitness) to create structured, socially engaging, and flexible physical activity options may help individuals maintain their activity levels in the absence of traditional or preferred physical activity contexts [76]. Promoting activities that could be completed on one’s own when necessary, but that are socially engaging and fulfill humans’ needs for social connectedness, could help maintain physical activity engagement during times of crisis and beyond. To that end, evidence-based group dynamics strategies should be promoted and used in virtual group-based settings to foster a sense of belonging to a group and ultimately, to increase exercise adherence [16].

Our findings also revealed important demographic differences in that women and participants living with family were more likely to be active alone. Clearly, efforts to promote physical activity among young people must include a meaningful focus on equitable access to physical activity opportunities across different demographic groups and living situations. Developing innovative health-promotion strategies that address demographic and contextual nuances in general and during times of crisis—particularly when physical activity contexts are limited—is crucial.

Moving forward, there is a need for research exploring the impact and effectiveness of hybrid fitness models (combining in-person and virtual activities) [42]. Understanding the benefits and challenges associated with these models among different cohorts (e.g., differing age groups and abilities), and in different geographical locations, is important for shaping and enhancing health promotion programs and strategies. Moreover, exploring and highlighting the needs and experiences of equity-deserving groups in research related to physical activity contexts and preferences is essential to creating targeted initiatives that benefit all individuals.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the physical activity-related social contexts engaged in by adolescents and young adults during the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic in Ontario, Canada. The findings reveal distinct shifts in physical activity patterns over time that were likely influenced by COVID-19-related public health measures and the availability of different social contexts and physical activity environments. The findings of this study also lend support to theories [19–21] suggesting that when opportunities for social interaction through physical activity are restricted and the fulfillment of the basic need for connectedness is not met, physical activity behaviours—from a social context perspective—could be impacted.
