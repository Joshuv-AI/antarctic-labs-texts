Pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and local tolerance at injection site of penicillin and gentamicin administered by intravenous regional limb perfusion in standing horses: comparison between weightbearing and flexed limbs
Authors: Marguerite Guillot, Céline Mespoulhes-Rivière, Alain Bousquet-Mélou, Marlène Z. Lacroix, Béatrice B. Roques, Elodie A. Lallemand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntravenous regional limb perfusion (IVRLP) of antibiotics is a therapeutic method used to treat distal limb infections in horses. The objectives of this study were to determine the pharmacokinetic parameters and tolerance of sodium benzylpenicillin (NaBP) administered via IVRLP; to compare the feasibility, tolerance, and efficacy of performing IVRLP on a flexed limb versus a weightbearing limb; and to predict the efficacy of IVRLP using gentamicin or NaBP. A prospective crossover study was conducted with six horses, each undergoing four phases of antibiotic infusion (gentamicin at 2.2 mg/kg vs. NaBP at 7,333 IU/kg of BP) and limb positioning (weightbearing vs. flexed). Each antibiotic administration was followed by serial synovial and blood sampling to assess antibiotic concentrations. Inflammation and sensitivity to palpation were evaluated at both the injection site and the sampled joint. A pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analysis was carried out to predict the efficacy of IVRLP by estimating area under the concentration–time curve to minimum inhibitory concentration (AUC/MIC) index values for various minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs).ResultsHigh synovial concentrations of NaBP and gentamicin were achieved, with notable variability between horses. NaBP IVRLP was well tolerated, although cephalic vein inflammation scores were significantly higher following NaBP infusion than gentamicin (p = 3.4 × 10− 11). For all horses and both antibiotics, synovial antibiotic exposures were significantly greater in weightbearing limbs than in flexed limbs (p = 0.04 and 0.02 for NaBP and gentamicin, respectively). Cephalic vein inflammation scores were also significantly lower in weightbearing limbs than in flexed limbs (p = 2.5 × 10− 5). In terms of predicted efficacy, a daily dose of 2.2 mg/kg gentamicin should be adequate to treat synovial infections involving Escherichia coli or Staphylococcus aureus, including resistant strains. For NaBP, a daily or every-other-day dose of 7,333 IU/kg of BP should be adequate to treat infections caused by Streptococcus equi and sensitive Staphylococcus aureus.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that daily IVRLP with NaBP (7,333 IU/kg of BP) could be an effective treatment for distal limb infections caused by susceptible bacteria with a MIC of ≤ 2 µg/mL, with only mild local inflammation at the injection site. IVRLP with 2.2 mg/kg gentamicin appears effective for treating infections involving susceptible bacteria with an MIC of ≤ 16 µg/mL. Performing IVRLP on a weightbearing limb resulted in higher antibiotic exposure in the metacarpophalangeal joint and better tolerance than the flexed limb method.

Background

Intravenous regional limb perfusion (IVRLP) of antibiotics is widely used among equine surgeons to treat distal limb infections [1], enabling the treatment of severe infections while using a reduced total dose compared with systemic treatments. This diminishes potential systemic side effects for patients and reduces antibiotic release into the environment. Another potential advantage is the achievement of effective antibiotic concentrations even in tissues with poor blood perfusion [2, 3]. The antibiotics used can be either concentration- or time-dependent [4]. The pharmacokinetics (PK) of gentamicin with this method of administration has already been described [5, 6], but to the authors’ knowledge, sodium benzylpenicillin (NaBP) has not been tested alone—only in combination with amikacin [7, 8]. The first objective of our study was to determine the PK parameters and tolerance of NaBP administered alone via IVRLP. The goal of this administration technique is to obtain maximum concentrations of antibiotics in the perfused tissues for a prolonged period while reducing systemic toxicity [1, 8]. Several studies have compared technical modalities: whether the horse is standing or under general anaesthesia, use of a wide rubber tourniquet versus a pneumatic tourniquet, volume of the infusate, and use of local analgesia or not [9]. Kilcoyne and Nieto reported that placing rolled gauze under the tourniquet in the divot where the cephalic vein courses improves pressure over the vein and tourniquet efficacy [10]. Redding et al. reported that using a wide rubber tourniquet and performing perfusions with the addition of local analgesia achieved significantly greater antibiotic concentrations [9]. As far as we know, in published studies, all standing administrations were performed with the perfused limb weightbearing. Limb movement during the perfusion period can lead to variations in tourniquet pressure and vascular leakage of antibiotic beyond the tourniquet into the systemic circulation [4, 11, 12]. We hypothesise that placing the tourniquet on a flexed limb and maintaining the limb in flexion until tourniquet removal would reduce leakage because the efficacy of the tourniquet will be increased on relaxed tendons and muscles. The second objective of our study was to compare, for both gentamicin and penicillin, synovial concentrations obtained with the limb weightbearing versus flexed.

PK/pharmacodynamic (PD) modelling is the initial step in optimising dosage regimens for systemically acting antibiotic drugs [13]. Numerical values of PK/PD indexes are used to predict dose and dosing intervals on a rational basis. Three PK/PD indexes are routinely used in veterinary medicine: the ratio of the area under the curve (AUC) of the drug plasma concentration to the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) (AUC/MIC), the ratio of the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) to MIC (Cmax/MIC), and the time that the plasma concentration exceeds the MIC over the dosing interval (T > MIC) [13]. The third objective of this study was to predict the efficacy of IVRLP with gentamicin or NaBP using the PK parameters obtained in this study, relevant recommended values of PK/PD indexes, and existing MIC distributions for selected bacteria.

Methods

Animals

This study involved six adult horses (two mares and four geldings; three French Trotters and three Thoroughbreds; mean age ± SD: 3.5 ± 1.4 years; mean weight: 453 ± 84 kg). All horses were confirmed healthy based on a full physical examination as well as haematological and blood biochemical analyses, and they were free from lameness or abnormalities in the distal aspect of the limb according to history and clinical examination findings. None of the horses had received medical treatment within 15 days prior to the study. The horses were housed individually, had free access to water, and were fed a maintenance diet. The study was approved by the local and national institutional animal care and use committees (APAFIS #34714-202201142131762 v5).

Study design

Gentamicin (2.2 mg/kg [G4®; Virbac]) or NaBP (7333 IU/kg of benzylpenicillin, equivalent to 4.12 mg/kg of benzylpenicillin [Penicilline G Panpharma 5 000 000 UI®; Panpharma]) was used. A vial of NaBP was diluted in 10 mL of sterilised water for injections. For each horse, one forelimb was randomly selected (coin toss) to be perfused with gentamicin, while the other forelimb was used to perfuse NaBP, with a 2-week washout period between treatments. For each drug, one treatment was performed with the limb weightbearing (i.e., horse standing square), and another with the limb flexed—resulting in six limbs per treatment and four treatments in total. To obtain and maintain flexion, the distal limb of the horse was rested on a bale of wood shavings (Fig. 1).

The forelimbs were clipped prior to perfusion. The horses were sedated with detomidine hydrochloride (10–15 µg/kg IV [Sedomidine®; Le Vet]) and butorphanol (10–15 µg/kg IV [Torphadine®; Dechra]). A tourniquet (10 cm wide [Esmarch band, BAN 169, 10 mm × 5 m; Centravet]) was applied to the injected forelimb 10 cm above the accessory carpal bone, with the limb either weightbearing or flexed depending on the position being studied. Gauze rolls were placed beneath the tourniquet over the medial and lateral aspects of the limb to enhance compression of the vascular structures. Following aseptic preparation, a 22-gauge, 20-mm butterfly needle (Venofix®; BBraun) was inserted into the cephalic vein. Perfusion began with 20 mL of lidocaine (Laocaine®; Intervet), followed by either gentamicin or diluted NaBP, and finally a volume of saline sufficient to reach a total of 60 mL. The solution was administered manually via slow intravenous bolus injection over 2 min. The tourniquet was removed immediately after synovial fluid collection at 20 min. To minimise variability in tourniquet application, the same clinician applied all tourniquets and performed all perfusions throughout the study. After perfusion, the butterfly needle was removed, and pressure was applied to the venipuncture site using rolled swabs and tape until the tourniquet was removed. During the 20-minute tourniquet application, all movements of the injected limb were recorded on camera.

Whole-blood samples were obtained by venipuncture of a jugular vein, and synovial fluid was collected from the metacarpophalangeal joint via aseptic arthrocentesis of the perfused limb following surgical preparation. For horses with the weightbearing limb, the dorsal recess was preferentially punctured if accessible; otherwise, the palmarolateral recess was used, with the limb in a weightbearing position during tourniquet application and flexed after its removal. For horses with the flexed limb, the palmarolateral recess was consistently used with the limb maintained in flexion. Time 0 was defined as the end of antibiotic perfusion. Blood samples (4 mL) were collected in heparinised tubes before antibiotic perfusion and at 1, 10, 19, and 21 min (i.e., 1 min before and after tourniquet release), as well as at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-perfusion. Synovial fluid samples (0.5 mL) were collected in heparinised tubes at 19 min, 35 min, and 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after perfusion. Additional synovial fluid samples (0.5 mL) were collected in EDTA tubes at 19 min and 48 h post-perfusion for cytological evaluation. Meloxicam (0.6 mg/kg, PO, q 24 h [Inflacam 20 mg/mL injectable solution®; Virbac]) was administered for 3 days, with the first dose given 1 h prior to IVRLP. The horses were stall-rested for 5 days and closely monitored for signs of lameness, swelling of the treated limb, or infection for 1 week. Clinical examinations were conducted twice daily for 14 days following IVRLP, and the distal limbs were kept bandaged between synovial fluid collections, with bandages removed 2 days after final sampling.

Inflammation evaluation

To assess the effects of repeated arthrocentesis, the tapped metacarpophalangeal joints were evaluated by the same examiner throughout the study. Joint effusion was graded as follows: 1 = no effusion observed or palpated, 2 = mild to moderate effusion palpable, and 3 = severe or prominent effusion. Sensitivity of the joint to digital pressure was also graded: 1 = no sensitivity (the horse does not move the limb in response to mild palpation or firm pressure), 2 = mild (the horse moves the limb when firm pressure is applied), and 3 = moderate to severe (the horse lifts the limb when lightly touched). Evaluations were conducted on days 1, 2, 3, and 5 post-perfusion.

Local tolerance and inflammation at perfusion sites were assessed by the same examiner using palpation sensitivity and visible inflammation scoring on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 post-perfusion. A subjective score for visible inflammation was assigned using a previously published 4-point scale: 1 = no apparent tissue reaction, 2 = mild tissue reaction with the cephalic vein easily identified, 3 = moderate tissue reaction with the cephalic vein difficult to identify, and 4 = severe tissue reaction such that no further IVRLP would be possible [14]. Sensitivity to digital pressure was graded similarly to that used for the joint assessments. If the inflammation score was 3 or 4 out of 4, an ultrasonographic examination was performed using a linear transducer (10 MHz). The IVRLP injection site, along with a 5-cm region both proximal and distal to it, was scanned in the transverse and longitudinal planes, and colour flow Doppler techniques were employed to evaluate blood flow within each cephalic vein.

Movement analysis

Video recordings of the perfusions were analysed to count each movement—classified as either minor or major—occurring from the time of butterfly needle catheterisation to tourniquet removal. For weightbearing perfusions, movement classification followed the criteria described by Plunkett et al. [15]: minor movement was defined as a decrease in weightbearing with the hoof remaining in contact with the ground, while major movement involved the entire hoof being lifted off the ground. For flexed limb perfusions, a minor movement was defined as a clearly visible movement of the limb without removal from the wood shavings bale, and a major movement was defined as removal of the limb from the bale, with the hoof touching the ground.

Antibiotic analysis

Gentamicin and NaBP concentrations were assayed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Plasma or synovial samples (100 µL) were extracted via protein precipitation by adding 10 µL of ampicillin (50 µg/mL) and 150 µL of acetonitrile; the resulting supernatant (100 µL) was then diluted with 200 µL of ultrapure water. Ten microlitres of the extracted solution were eluted at 40 °C on a C18 column at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, using an acidified (0.2% formic acid, 0.02% heptafluorobutyric acid) H2O/acetonitrile gradient. The method was validated with a calibration curve ranging from 0.01 to 100 µg/mL for NaBP and from 0.5 to 100 µg/mL for gentamicin, both following a quadratic model weighted by 1/X2 (X = concentration), with accuracy ranging from 90 to 124%. Extracted samples remained stable in the autosampler, maintained at 15 °C, for up to 8 h.

Synovial fluid was evaluated at 19 min and 48 h post-perfusion through both macroscopic inspection and cytological examination. Colour and viscosity were subjectively assessed and recorded. The total red blood cell and white blood cell counts were measured using an automated haematology analyser (ProCyte Dx; Idexx), and the total protein concentration was determined with a refractometer (Refractomètre 50 301 251; Rogo-Sampaic).

PK analysis

A non-compartmental analysis of the antibiotics’ concentration–time profiles was performed using commercial software (PKanalix 2024R1; Lixoft SAS, a Simulations Plus company). The best-fitting slopes of the terminal elimination phase were calculated by linear regression using the ‘points’ rule for λ, requiring at least three data points. The area under the curve (AUC) was computed using the linear-up log-down trapezoidal method.

PK/PD indexes

To evaluate the predicted efficacy of gentamicin or NaBP at the administered dose, efficacy indexes were calculated using the PK parameters obtained from non-compartmental analysis of synovial fluid concentration–time profiles and EUCAST MIC distributions of common equine pathogens.

Simulations were performed to evaluate the probability of target attainment. For NaBP, a time-dependent antibiotic, the PK/PD indexes commonly used are the time that the synovial concentration of BP exceeds the MIC of the pathogen (T > MIC) and the AUC/MIC index, the latter being considered more stable [13]. For gentamicin, a concentration-dependent antibiotic, Cmax/MIC and AUC/MIC indexes are applicable [16, 17]. In our study, the AUC24h/MIC index was selected for daily administration of both antibiotics, with target values of 24, 48, and 72 h used [18]. These target values correspond to average concentrations over 24 h equal to one MIC (AUC24h/MIC = 24 h), twice the MIC (AUC24h/MIC = 48 h), or three times the MIC (AUC24h/MIC = 72 h). To explore every-other-day administration, an AUC48h/MIC index was used with target values of 48, 96, and 144 h, following Toutain et al. [19], similarly translating to average concentrations over 48 h of one MIC (AUC48h/MIC = 48 h), two MICs (AUC48h/MIC = 96 h), or three MICs (AUC48h/MIC = 144 h). Achieving concentrations equal to three times the MIC, rather than just once, is expected to be associated with a higher probability of clinical cure. Bacterial MIC distributions were sourced from EUCAST, considering Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus for gentamicin, and Streptococcus equi subsp. equi, Streptococcus equi subsp. zooepidemicus, and Staphylococcus aureus for BP. For E. coli and S. aureus, both distributions of sensitive strains only and full distributions including resistant strains were evaluated. Simulations were conducted using Oracle Crystal Ball software, version 11.1.3.0.000 (Oracle, Austin, TX, USA), assuming that the entire perfused dose reached the synovial fluid, with 100% of the antibiotic unbound. Bioavailability was set at 100% because of the intravenous route of administration. Interindividual variability in PK parameters was accounted for by simulating a log-normal distribution of clearance. These indexes were calculated only for the weightbearing limb, given its demonstrated superiority over the flexed limb method in our study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (R 4.1.0; R Development Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for PK data, or with BiostaTGV (biostagv.sentiweb.fr) for tolerance parameters. Data are expressed as mean ± SD for normally distributed data and as median and range for non-normally distributed data. A significant difference was defined as p ≤ 0.05.

Results

The mean time for infusion was 2.0 ± 0.5 min, and the mean time from the end of perfusion to tourniquet removal was 20.6 ± 1.9 min. In some horses, difficulty in collecting synovial fluid during the first sampling (1 min before tourniquet removal) may have caused a slight delay in tourniquet removal. Daily clinical examinations over the 10 days following IVRLP were unremarkable. One horse (Horse 2), during phase 2 (NaBP/flexed limb), began to struggle 8 min after perfusion and eventually fell onto the injected side at 18 min post-perfusion. The tourniquet was removed at 18.3 min, and synovial sampling was performed immediately afterwards. This horse exhibited mild lameness in the treated forelimb for 3 days.

All perfusions performed on the flexed limb were more difficult than those on the weightbearing limb because of poor visualisation of, and difficulty in catheterising, the cephalic vein. Additionally, more movements were observed, and increased doses of sedation were required during flexed limb perfusions.

Collection of synovial fluid was successful at all time points, except in one horse where the volume collected was insufficient for cytological analysis. From the second puncture onward, synovial fluid samples were blood-tainted, although viscosity remained normal. The synovial fluid variables are summarised in Table 1. For all treatments, red blood cell counts, white blood cell counts, neutrophil percentage, and total solid proteins were higher at 48 h post-perfusion than at 19 min post-perfusion. White blood cell counts remained below 6 × 109 cells/L, except in Horse 3 with gentamicin/flexed limb. The neutrophil percentage remained below 60% and total solid proteins below 40 g/L, except in Horse 1 with gentamicin/weightbearing limb and Horse 3 with NaBP/flexed limb. No significant differences were observed at 48 h post-perfusion between antibiotics or limb positions.

Inflammation scoring

All gradings of metacarpophalangeal joint sensitivity to digital pressure were 1/3 (no sensitivity). Joint effusion grades remained stable throughout the study, with median values of 1.5/3 (day 1: 1.5 [1.0; 2.0]; day 2: 1.5 [1.4; 2.0]; day 3: 1.5 [1.0; 1.5]; day 5: 1.5 [1.0; 1.5]).

No discharge or sensitivity was observed at the site of perfusion in any of the horses, and all gradings of vein sensitivity to digital pressure were 1/3 (no sensitivity). Regarding the subjective score for visible inflammation, there was a decrease in scores from day 1 to day 5 (Fig. 2). When comparing limb positions, visual inflammation scores were significantly higher for the flexed limb than weightbearing limb (Fig. 2). When comparing antibiotics, NaBP was associated with significantly higher visual inflammation scores than gentamicin (Fig. 2).

One horse (Horse 1) developed thrombophlebitis of the injected cephalic vein following the second treatment (NaBP/flexed limb, first treatment of this limb), without any associated sensitivity or lameness. Thrombophlebitis was confirmed via ultrasonographic examination and resolved within 3 weeks after the perfusion, with daily cold water application and topical treatment using Ekyflogyl (1.8 mg/mL + 8.7 mg/mL gel pour chevaux®; Audevard). The contralateral limb was used for treatments 3 and 4 in this horse.

Movement analysis

Observations and gradings from the video recordings were easier to perform for weightbearing limbs than for flexed limbs; as a result, some movements may have been missed in the flexed limb recordings. Additionally, there was considerable interindividual variability. Despite this, more movements were observed in flexed limbs than in weightbearing limbs (25 [15; 44] vs. 18 [9; 47]), although there was no statistically significant difference between limb positions (p = 0.60) or between antibiotics (p = 0.69).

Plasma AUC values prior to tourniquet removal were lower, but not significantly different, for weightbearing limbs than for flexed limbs (537 ± 666 vs. 1346 ± 1231 µg·min/mL for gentamicin, and 246 ± 423 vs. 479 ± 669 µg·min/mL for BP; p > 0.05 regardless of the antibiotic).

The mean plasma BP exposure, measured as AUC0–INF, was 915 ± 270 µg·min/mL for weightbearing limbs and 907 ± 378 µg·min/mL for flexed limbs. The mean Cmax was 11.6 ± 3.6 µg/mL and 12.0 ± 9.6 µg/mL, respectively. Plasma clearance was 4.9 ± 1.5 mL/kg·min in weightbearing limbs and 5.3 ± 2.4 mL/kg·min in flexed limbs. The mean terminal half-life (T½) in plasma was 116.1 ± 18.5 min for weightbearing limbs and 171.8 ± 47.4 min for flexed limbs.

In the synovial fluid, high BP concentrations were achieved, with substantial variability observed between horses. Synovial AUC0–INF values were significantly higher in weightbearing limbs than in flexed limbs (30,929 ± 17,370 µg·min/mL vs. 7,105 ± 3,643 µg·min/mL, p = 0.04). A high Cmax of 220 ± 127 µg/mL was reached in the synovial fluid of weightbearing limbs, compared with 31 ± 22 µg/mL in flexed limbs. The mean terminal T½ in synovial fluid was 165 ± 84 min for weightbearing limbs and 178 ± 143 min for flexed limbs.

Regarding gentamicin, high synovial concentrations were achieved, with considerable variability between horses. Synovial AUC0–INF values were significantly higher in weightbearing limbs than in flexed limbs (94,383 ± 47,342 µg·min/mL vs. 33,693 ± 24,389 µg·min/mL, p = 0.02). A high Cmax of 288 ± 189 µg/mL was reached in the synovial fluid of weightbearing limbs, compared with 76 ± 69 µg/mL in flexed limbs. The mean terminal T½ in synovial fluid was 1,372 ± 445 min for weightbearing limbs and 1,234 ± 598 min for flexed limbs.

PK/PD indexes

The AUC/MIC parameters calculated for synovial fluid are presented in Table 4 for daily administrations and Table 5 for every-other-day administrations. For gentamicin administered daily via IVRLP, the 2.2-mg/kg dose used in this study should be sufficient to effectively treat synovial infections involving E. coli or S. aureus, even when accounting for resistant strains (Table 4). Given the low exposure needed to treat S. aureus infections, administering 2.2 mg/kg of gentamicin every 2 days via IVRLP would likely still result in clinical success, whereas treating resistant E. coli strains with this dosing interval is more uncertain (Table 5). For NaBP, the dose of 7,333 IU/kg of BP used in this study—administered either daily or every other day—would be adequate to treat synovial infections caused by S. equi subsp. equi, S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus, and sensitive S. aureus strains. However, the PK/PD parameters observed for resistant S. aureus suggest that NaBP administered via IVRLP would be ineffective, even at a higher dose of 12.36 mg/kg of BP, which corresponds to the standard systemic dose of 22,000 IU/kg (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

One of the objectives of this study was to describe the use of NaBP via IVRLP. We found that NaBP can be delivered to the metacarpophalangeal joint in high concentrations for an extended period through perfusion of the cephalic vein, although mild intolerance at the injection site was observed.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the PK of NaBP administered alone via IVRLP. Previous studies evaluated the combined administration of amikacin and NaBP [7, 8]. In those studies, a dose of 10 × 106 IU of BP was used, whereas in the present study, a dose of 7,333 IU/kg of BP was administered, corresponding to 2.5 to 4.4 × 106 IU per horse. Considering this dose difference, the synovial Cmax observed in our study with weightbearing limbs (mean of 220 µg/mL) are comparable to that reported by Dahan et al., who observed a mean of 507 µg/mL [8]. This contrasts with the study by Nieto et al., which reported a much lower synovial Cmax of 8.5 µg/mL [7], attributed to vascular leakage under the tourniquet.

The horses tolerated NaBP injection via IVRLP well, with no local sensitivity to vein pressure or lameness observed following infusion. Mild to moderate local inflammation of the cephalic vein was noted after infusion, with the highest scores recorded on Day 1, followed by a gradual decrease over subsequent days. However, it is important to note that our study involved only a single administration, and the risk of local inflammation may increase with repeated treatments. Such an inflammatory response could impair identification of the cephalic vein for future IVRLP procedures. Dahan et al. [8] demonstrated that increasing the dilution of NaBP (to a total volume of 60 mL) helps reduce this side effect.

One horse developed thrombophlebitis of the cephalic vein following NaBP IVRLP on a flexed limb. This horse exhibited significant movement during the NaBP injection, resulting in withdrawal of the butterfly needle from the vein. The injection had to be repeated after compression of the vein, which led to immediate subcutaneous swelling, indicative of perivenous leakage of the perfusate. This leakage likely contributed to the subsequent inflammation and thrombophlebitis. Because this complication was not observed in any other horses, the use of NaBP should not be considered an additional risk factor for the development of thrombophlebitis following IVRLP, provided the procedure is performed correctly and with appropriate dilution of NaBP.

Cytological analysis of the synovial fluid remained within inflammatory limits, and previous studies have shown that repeated joint sampling can itself induce inflammation [11]. Regarding the potential chondrotoxicity of high intra-articular antibiotic concentrations, an article evaluating potential chondrotoxicity of various intra-articular antibiotics reported that penicillin exhibited chondrotoxicity with an estimated IC50 of 11.6 mg/mL [20]. This concentration is more than 50 times higher than the mean maximal synovial concentration of BP (220 µg/mL) observed in our study. Furthermore, cytological parameters were comparable—and in some cases lower—for NaBP than for gentamicin. Based on these findings, we suggest that NaBP can be safely administered via IVRLP at a dose of 7,333 IU/kg.

The hypothesis of our study regarding limb positioning during IVRLP was that placing the tourniquet on a flexed limb and maintaining this flexion for 20 min would provide better compression of the cephalic vein and reduce leakage of the perfusate into the systemic circulation compared with the weightbearing position. However, our findings showed quite the opposite: performing IVRLP on a weightbearing limb resulted in reduced leakage under the tourniquet, greater exposure of the metacarpophalangeal joint to the antibiotics, and better overall tolerance than the flexed limb method.

Synovial benzylpenicillin and gentamicin exposures, measured by AUC, were both significantly higher with the weightbearing limb method than with the flexed limb method, and Cmax values were also markedly increased. Additionally, greater leakage of antibiotics into the systemic circulation prior to tourniquet release was observed with the flexed limb method than with the weightbearing limb method.

Regarding tolerance, fewer movements were observed during perfusion in weightbearing limbs than in flexed limbs, although there was considerable interindividual variability and the difference was not statistically significant. For flexed limbs, video recordings did not allow for accurate counting of all small movements, likely resulting in an underestimation. Several techniques were considered to maintain the limb in a flexed position, aiming to improve horse tolerance and reduce movement. In a pilot study, use of a Kimzey splint was attempted, but sedated horses exhibited more small, continuous movements and alternating flexed/weightbearing cycles than when using the wood shaving bale ultimately chosen for the study. The final protocol involved flexing the carpus to approximately 90° and resting the metacarpus on the edge of a bundle of wood shavings. This method provided better stability but required one person to hold the limb throughout the tourniquet application period and proved challenging because of horse tolerance and difficulty catheterising the cephalic vein. A potential solution to limit movement could be to perform the procedure with the limb secured in a splint allowing carpal flexion, as described by Kadic et al. [21] for standing carpal arthroscopy. Regarding injection sites, no evidence of discharge or sensitivity to digital pressure was observed in any of the horses; however, cephalic vein inflammation scores were significantly higher in flexed limbs than in weightbearing limbs.

Therapeutic concentrations of gentamicin or BP for equine-susceptible pathogens were achieved in the metacarpophalangeal synovial fluid using a third of the usual systemic dose—the doses used in our study.

NaBP IVRLP administered daily or every other day is active against sensitive staphylococci and streptococci (including Streptococcus equi subsp. equi and S. zooepidemicus) and could be used by equine practitioners to treat distal infections involving sensitive bacteria. Although the optimal dose and frequency of NaBP for IVRLP require further investigation, the dose used in this study (7,333 IU/kg of BP) should provide adequate antibiotic coverage for sensitive bacteria with an MIC of ≤ 2 µg/mL with daily IVRLP and an MIC of ≤ 1 µg/mL with IVRLP every 2 days. In other studies, a high dose (10 million IU) of NaBP was used (7,8) because of the time-dependent characteristics of penicillin, under the assumption that a higher dose might prolong the antibiotic’s half-life, although this was not supported (7). Regarding gentamicin at 2.2 mg/kg, daily IVRLP would effectively treat synovial infections involving E. coli or S. aureus, even when resistant strains are considered, and would be suitable for treating sensitive pathogens with an MIC of up to 16 µg/mL. An IVRLP every other day would remain effective for sensitive pathogens with an MIC of up to 8 µg/mL. In addition, aminoglycosides exhibit post-antibiotic effects, meaning gentamicin continues to suppress bacterial growth even after limited exposure—further supporting its use [24].

Our study has some limitations. Only six horses were included, which is common in equine PK studies; however, repeated sampling allowed for adequate computational analysis of PK parameters. All horses received oral meloxicam and distal limb bandaging as part of the standard experimental protocol, which may have biased the visual assessment of injection site reactions (meloxicam) and metacarpophalangeal inflammation (meloxicam and bandage). The potential effect of meloxicam on synovial fluid antibiotic concentrations is unknown. However, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and bandages are routinely used in the treatment of equine septic arthritis, and most IVRLP antibiotic studies employ similar protocols using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (typically phenylbutazone or meloxicam), making our results broadly comparable to existing literature. Another limitation is the use of healthy horses. In a study investigating the effects of experimentally induced synovitis on amikacin concentrations following IVRLP, the authors noted an earlier Tmax and higher Cmax of intra-articular amikacin in inflamed joints than in normal joints, and also demonstrated a more rapid decline in intra-articular antibiotic concentrations in infected joints [22]. Additional investigation of gentamicin and NaBP IVRLP in infected joints is therefore warranted. Furthermore, our protocol focused solely on antibiotic concentrations in the metacarpophalangeal joint, and the results may not be applicable to other joints. Finally, we observed considerable interindividual variability in plasma and synovial antibiotic concentrations over time, which is consistent with findings from most IVRLP studies [4, 9, 23]. Efforts were made to reduce this variability: dosing regimens were based on body weight, tourniquet application and antibiotic infusion were performed by the same operator, lidocaine was administered prior to antibiotic infusion, and sedation was adjusted as needed based on individual horse behaviour.

Conclusions

Our data suggest that daily administration of NaBP at a dose of 7,333 IU/kg of BP via IVRLP should be considered an effective treatment for horses with distal limb infections caused by susceptible bacteria with an MIC of ≤ 2 µg/mL, while gentamicin at a dose of 2.2 mg/kg would be effective against sensitive pathogens with an MIC of up to 16 µg/mL. The findings of the present study also emphasise that performing IVRLP with a weightbearing limb is superior to perfusion with a flexed limb in terms of practical implementation, tolerance, and synovial antibiotic exposure.
