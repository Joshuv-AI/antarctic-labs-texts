Evaluation of Venous Structures that Are Involved in Transsylvian Approach Using 3D Rotational Venography
Authors: Yoshiro ITO, Hisayuki HOSOO, Masayuki SATO, Aiki MARUSHIMA, Mikito HAYAKAWA, Yuji MATSUMARU, Eiichi ISHIKAWA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In the transsylvian (TS) approach, as characterized by clipping surgery, the presurgical visualization of the superficial middle cerebral vein (SMCV) can help change the surgical approach to ensure safe microsurgery. Nevertheless, identifying preoperatively the venous structures that are involved in this approach is difficult. In this study, we investigated the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach using three-dimensional (3D) rotational venography (3D-RV) and evaluated the effectiveness of this method for presurgical simulation. Patients who underwent 3D-RV between August 2018 and June 2020 were involved in this retrospective study. The 3D-RV and partial maximum intensity projection images with a thickness of 5 mm were computationally reconstructed. The venous structures were subdivided into the following three portions according to the anatomic location: superficial, intermediate, and basal portions. In the superficial portion, predominant frontosylvian veins were observed on 31 (41%) sides, predominant temporosylvian veins on seven (9%) sides, and equivalent fronto- and temporosylvian veins on 28 (37%) sides. The veins in the intermediate (deep middle cerebral and uncal veins) and basal portions (frontobasal bridging veins) emptied into the SMCV on 57 (75%) and 34 (45%) sides, respectively. The 3D-RV images were highly representative of the venous structures observed during microsurgery. In this study, 3D-RV was utilized to capture the details of the venous structures from the superficial to the deep portions. Presurgical simulation of the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach using 3D-RV may increase the safety of microsurgical approaches.

Introduction

Presurgical simulation for cerebral aneurysm clipping surgery is vital and can be accomplished using various modalities. The majority of presurgical simulations have concentrated on the structures of the aneurysms and adjacent arteries.1-5) Despite this, meticulous dissection of the arachnoid space surrounding the veins provides safe aneurysm exposure.6,7)

One of the most common surgical approaches for clipping surgery is the transsylvian (TS) approach. Cerebral arteries, veins, and trabeculae are found in the Sylvian fissure. Cerebral arteries can be imaged via computed tomography angiography (CTA) or digital subtraction angiography (DSA); therefore, the arteries involved in the TS approach can be determined preoperatively. Meanwhile, multiple veins that are involved in the TS approach present variations and anomalies in the population and can interfere with the surgical field. The dissection method of the Sylvian fissure varies depending on the pathway of the superficial middle cerebral vein (SMCV) and bridging vein into the SMCV.6-11) Hence, presurgical simulation of the venous structures can help identify the most suitable surgical approach and thereby result in safer microsurgery and improved outcomes.

Previous studies have reported the cerebral vein and sinus characteristics using CTA; the examined structures were usually large veins and sinuses due to the imaging resolution.12) CTA has insufficient spatial resolution to visualize the detailed venous structures that are involved in the TS approach. Previously, the superficial cerebral veins and sinuses considered were considered to be more challenging to visualize using three-dimensional (3D) rotational venography (3D-RV) than cerebral arteries.13) The development of angiography equipment and the validation of appropriate imaging methods have enabled accurate visualization of venous structures.13,14) The preoperative evaluation of the superficial cerebral veins using cone-beam CTA has been reported recently.14) Conversely, the preservation of the deep veins in the TS approach is still a significant challenge in the prevention of postoperative venous congestion.15) The presurgical simulation of venous structures requires a detailed 3D visualization of the venous structures that are involved in the surgical approach using a single modality. We utilized 3D-RV to create images of the superficial to deep venous structures that are involved in the TS approach. This study examined the venous structures using 3D-RV and verified their usefulness in presurgical simulation for actual clipping surgery. The present findings can aid in achieving an effective presurgical simulation for the TS approach, as characterized by clipping surgery.

Materials and Methods

This study is in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Study design

All patients provided written informed consent, and our hospital's ethics committee (R03-207) approved this study. The clinical data were anonymized and retrospectively collected.

Patient population

The registry included all patients who underwent 3D-RV at our institution between August 2018 and June 2020. Those with a history of craniotomy and intracranial tumors in association with the TS approach were excluded.

DSA and venous structures

Cerebral angiography was conducted using Azurion 7 (Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). Three-dimensional RV was performed after conventional angiography. On the basis of conventional angiography findings, the imaging delay time was determined in the phase that best depicted the cortical veins. The dose and rate of contrast media injection through the internal carotid artery and common carotid artery were 15 mL at 2.5 mL/s and 25 mL at 4.0 mL/s, respectively. The 3D-RV and partial maximum intensity projection (MIP) images that are 5 mm thick were computationally reconstructed using a 3D workstation (XtraVision; Philips Healthcare).

We reconstructed the veins pertinent to the TS approach and subdivided the venous structures into three portions and a drainage route for the analysis (Fig. 1). The three portions were the superficial portion, which is the area from the cortical surface to the insula; intermediate portion, which is the area from the insula to the temporal pole; and basal portion, which comprises the frontal base. We classified the superficial portion of SMCV into the following five types (Supplementary Fig. 1), based on a previous report:9) Type A, predominantly frontosylvian veins; Type B, predominantly temporosylvian veins; Type C, equal frontosylvian and temporosylvian veins running parallel along the Sylvian fissure; Type D, equal frontosylvian and temporosylvian veins merging at the end of the Sylvian fissure; and Type E, undeveloped SMCVs on the Sylvian fissure. The intermediate portion comprised the deep middle cerebral veins (DMCVs) and uncal veins (UVs). We investigated whether the DMCV or UV emptied into the SMCV. The basal portion consisted of frontobasal bridging vein (FBBV), which is a general term for the anterior cerebral vein or fronto-orbital vein that empties into the SMCV. The main drainage routes were classified as the cavernous sinus, sphenoparietal sinus, pterygoid plexus, and superior petrosal sinus/sphenobasal sinus. The structure was considered a vein if the cross-sectional image was circular and a venous sinus if it was flat or triangular.

Assessments

Using 3D-RV, we explored the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach. Furthermore, using 3D-RV, the presurgical simulation and intraoperative findings of the TS approach for clipping surgery of cerebral aneurysms were evaluated. Two experienced neurosurgeons (Y.I. and H.H.), who were blinded to the patients' clinical information, independently reviewed all images and intraoperative findings. Any discrepancies in their evaluations were resolved by consensus. To evaluate the efficacy of 3D-RV, the intraoperative findings were assessed postoperatively using surgical videos.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and discrete data are presented as counts and percentages.

Results

The subjects included 64 (65.5% female) patients and 76 sides. The mean age was 53.9 ± 16.8 years. There were 48 patients with cerebral aneurysms and 13 with epilepsy (Table 1).

Patients characteristics and venous structures

Superficial portion

Type A was observed on 31 (40.8%) sides, and the mean number of SMCVs was 1.58 ± 0.67 on the frontal side and 0.45 ± 0.57 on the temporal side. Type B was observed in 7 (9.2%) sides, and the mean number of SMCVs was 0.29 ± 0.49 on the frontal side and 1.14 ± 0.38 on the temporal side. Types C, D, and E were observed in 26 (34.2%), two (2.6%), and 10 (13.2%) sides, respectively (Table 1).

Intermediate portion and basal portion

Either the DMCV or UV emptied into the SMCV in 57 (75.0%) sides (Table 1). The FBBV emptied into the SMCV in 34 (44.7%) sides (Table 1). Furthermore, we compared the frequencies of the intermediate and basal portions of each superficial portion type. Both the intermediate and basal portions were highly developed in Type B (7/7; 100% and 6/7; 85.7%, respectively), and the intermediate portion was highly developed in Type E (10/10; 100%). The basal portion was less developed in Type C than in the other types (7/26; 26.9%) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Drainage route

The main drainage routes to the cavernous sinus, sphenoparietal sinus, pterygoid plexus, and superior parietal sinus/sphenobasal sinus were observed in 28 (36.8%), 22 (28.9%), 18 (23.7%), and eight (10.5%) sides, respectively (Table 1).

Presurgical simulation and illustrative cases

Eighteen (37.5%) of the 48 patients with cerebral aneurysms underwent clipping surgery via the TS approach. Three-dimensional RV enabled successful presurgical simulation in all cases. Based on the 3D-RV images, the following were preoperatively simulated in the clipping surgery: 1) The dissected SMCV site was assumed based on the route of the superficial portion of the SMCV. 2) The intermediate and basal portions were evaluated for vessels that might interfere with the clip deployment within the surgical field. 3) The extent of SMCV dissection was referenced with the drainage route. The predominance of SMCV observed on the brain surface was highly concordant with that observed using 3D-RV images, providing a reference for the dissected SMCV site. Nevertheless, in cases with multiple SMCVs (e.g., case 2), the confluence of SMCVs was occasionally difficult to observe because of the resolution of 3D-RV. The 3D-RV images of the intermediate and basal portions resembled the intraoperative findings of the DMCV, UV, and FBBV emptying into the SMCV. Preoperative recognition of the DMCV or UV provided clues for dissection around the middle cerebral artery (MCA) and clipping of the MCA aneurysm. The presence of the FBBV aided the determination of the dissected side of the SMCV. The identification of the drainage route was helpful in the dissection of the deep Sylvian fissure and retraction of the brain, especially in cases of the sphenoparietal sinus (Figs. 2 and 3).

Discussion

In this study, we revealed the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach and the effectiveness of presurgical simulation via 3D-RV.

The 3D-RV images were highly representative of the venous structures observed during microsurgery. As shown in the illustrative cases, the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach could be ascertained preoperatively from the superficial to the deep portion of the brain. Furthermore, additional information could be obtained by combining 3D-RV with 3D rotational angiography (3D-RA). Presurgical simulation of the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach using 3D-RV may produce safe microsurgical approaches. Particularly, this method would be useful for less experienced surgeons.

The pathway from the SMCV to the venous sinus may help determine the dissection line for the TS approach.9,12,14,16-19) We investigated the pathway in three portions, including the drainage route. The present findings on the superficial portion of the SMCV are difficult to compare with those of previous studies because of the differences in classification methods. Multiple SMCVs may account for approximately 45% of the cases.6,8) In the case of multiple SMCVs, either the frontal or temporal side can be dominant.9) The dissection line of the Sylvian fissure depends on the route and confluence of SMCVs on the brain surface; thus, we employed a classification system based on a previous report that considered SMCV dominance.9) The frequencies of Types A, B, C, and E in the present study were comparable to those reported previously, but Type D (3%), in which the equal frontosylvian and temporosylvian veins merging at the end of the Sylvian fissure, was found to be less common (Table 2).6,8,9) This difference can be attributed to multiple factors, which include the number of cases and race. Furthermore, some reports did not describe Type D, which could be due to differences in the classification used.6,8) There have also been reports on the intermediate portion of the SMCV.6,16) In previous reports using DSA or intraoperative findings, the DMCV emptied into the SMCV in 53% of the cases, and the UV emptied into the SMCV in 36% of the cases.6,16) In the present study, the DMCV or UV emptied into the SMCV in 75% of the cases (Table 2). These discrepant findings could be attributed to the DMCV and UV being combined in the present study. Only one study has previously presented surgical findings for the basal portion; nonetheless, the frequency of FBBV in the present study was found to be comparable to that in a previous study (Table 2).6) Several previous studies have reported the drainage route of the SMCV using CTA or magnetic resonance imaging.12,17,18) In the present study, the drainage routes were similar, although the pterygoid plexus was slightly more common (Table 2). Overall, the present findings indicate that 3D-RV can capture the details of superficial and deep venous structures and drainage pathways.

Recently, there were some reports on venography performed with CTA for preoperative evaluation.17,20) Although CTA is noninvasive, venography with CTA does not always provide useful 3D images for presurgical simulation because the optimal scan timing for the venous phase is affected by the cardiac function or respiratory fluctuations.21) Furthermore, the detection rate of deep cerebral veins, including the DMCV, UV, and FBBV, is rather low.20) We encountered a case in which the FBBV was not sufficiently detected by venography with CTA (Supplementary Fig. 3). Although 3D-RA/RV is relatively invasive compared to CTA, it has excellent spatial and temporal resolution,13) and the cerebral deep veins are well detected as shown in the illustrative cases. Therefore, 3D-RV can detect deep cerebral veins more clearly than CTA-based venography. Furthermore, the treatment options for unruptured cerebral aneurysms are increasing, which include clipping, coil embolization, flow diverter stent, and intrasaccular flow disruption, and DSA is indispensable for preoperative evaluation, especially when considering flow diverter stents or intrasaccular flow disruption.

In a previous study, the time to peak for cortical arteries was 2.47 ± 0.35 s, that for cortical veins was 6.44 ± 1.14 s, and that for venous sinuses was 8.18 ± 1.12 s.13) In the present study, the imaging protocol for 3D-RV was decided on the basis of conventional angiography settings. In most cases, images were acquired 8 s after contrast injection. The 3D-RV images were produced by trimming the required area in the workstation. It took 5-10 min to obtain the images from the angiography equipment. The surgeon could understand the stereoscopic vessel structure better because the images were freely mobilized in the workstation.

Minimally invasive techniques that do not require wide exposure to the Sylvian fissure have an advantage over the less invasive approach.22,23) Nevertheless, a wide exposure of the Sylvian fissure may require less retraction than that necessary for minimally invasive techniques.7,24) Hence, not all patients are suitable to undergo these techniques.23) Anatomical orientation may also be lost with minimally invasive techniques because of the narrow surgical field.22) Additionally, the venous structures on the surface of the brain in cases of subarachnoid hemorrhage are difficult to distinguish.25) We believe that presurgical simulation of venous structures can be applied in these situations where recognition of the surgical field is insufficient.

The 3D-RV technique has several disadvantages. First, distinguishing between cortical veins and venous sinuses is challenging; nevertheless, considering that partial MIP images can be utilized to create cross-sectional images of the veins, disparities in shape between cortical veins help inform the differentiation. Second, the angiography equipment employed in the present study could not capture parenchymal brain images and could not produce composite images with the brain tissue. Third, cerebral angiography is a relatively invasive imaging modality.

This study has several limitations. First, 12 (18.8%) of the 64 patients had bilateral lesions: Therefore, each side could affect the results of another side. Second, if the image quality deteriorated because of motion artifacts or the timing of contrast injection, the confluence of the veins may have been difficult to determine. Third, not all patients underwent the surgery; thus, the venous structures were not confirmed intraoperatively. The 3D-RV images would possibly be different from the actual anatomy. Finally, although we performed the operation with reference to 3D-RV images, we did not objectively evaluate its actual impact on treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

In this study, the 3D-RV images were obtained swiftly and without difficulty. They aided in capturing the details of the superficial and deep portions of the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach. Presurgical simulation of the venous structures that are involved in the TS approach using 3D-RV can be helpful in developing safe microsurgical approaches.
