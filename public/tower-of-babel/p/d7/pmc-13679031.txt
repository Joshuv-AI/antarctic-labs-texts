The efficacy and safety of endoscopic ultrasound selective variceal devascularization for the treatment of esophagogastric varices: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chengqi Guan, Fan Huang, Hongyuan Shen, Wei Huang, Yachen Wang, Zhenbiao Mao, Cuihua Lu, Jian Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastroesophageal variceal bleeding is a life-threatening complication of portal hypertension. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of endoscopic ultrasound-guided selective variceal devascularization (EUS-SVD) compared with conventional endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study adhered to the STROBE guidelines. Between August 2022 and June 2024, 107 patients were enrolled and allocated to conventional endoscopy (n = 52) or EUS-SVD (n = 55). Propensity score matching was performed to reduce confounding. Primary outcomes included rebleeding, ulcer formation, and resource utilization.ResultsCompared with the conventional group, the EUS group required significantly lower volumes of polidocanol and cyanoacrylate (both P < 0.05). The 6‑month rebleeding rate and 3‑month ulcer formation rate were significantly lower in the EUS group (P < 0.05). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a reduced cumulative rebleeding incidence in the EUS group (HR 0.38, 95%CI 0.17–0.86, P = 0.021).ConclusionsWithin the limitations of a single-center retrospective design, these findings suggest that EUS-SVD may represent a promising therapeutic option for gastroesophageal varices, although it requires validation in prospective randomized trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05151-3.

Background

Liver cirrhosis is a common liver disease in clinical gastroenterology. When cirrhosis progresses to the decompensated stage, collateral circulation forms, leading to portal hypertension (PH), which results in gastroesophageal varices [1] (GOVs). Gastrointestinal bleeding, presenting as melena, hematemesis, and hemorrhagic shock, is a clinical manifestation of variceal rupture [2] and one of the main causes of death in patients with cirrhosis [3, 4]. Therefore, taking timely hemostatic measures and which prevents rebleeding are key to reducing mortality in these patients [5]. Currently, clinical treatments for GOV include surgical procedures, interventional therapy, medication, and gastrointestinal endoscopy techniques. Both surgical approaches, such as splenectomy and devascularization, and interventional embolization focus on addressing the source of the portal-systemic shunts. In contrast, endoscopic treatments intervene at the peripheral side of the collateral circulation. While theoretically and practically, surgery and radiological interventions are more effective in preventing rebleeding than endoscopic treatments are, they are usually performed electively, and the high preoperative bleeding rate further burdens the liver. In recent years, with the increasing maturity of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) in clinical practice, it has increasingly demonstrated its unique advantages in the prevention and treatment of esophageal and gastric varices [6, 7]. EUS enables precise localization and direct action on the key blood vessels causing varices. This targeted treatment strategy not only improves the cure rate but also, through refined management and control, effectively avoids the side effects associated with excessive use of cyanoacrylate glue, such as ectopic embolism, which can occur with traditional endoscopic treatments [8, 9]. This comprehensive approach enhances both treatment safety and patient quality of life. In the present study, we propose the term “endoscopic ultrasound‑selective variceal devascularization (EUS‑SVD)” to describe a refined EUS‑guided therapeutic strategy. Unlike conventional EUS‑guided cyanoacrylate injection, which primarily targets the varix lumen itself, EUS‑SVD emphasizes the identification and obliteration of the feeding and draining collaterals responsible for varix perfusion. Under Doppler guidance, the puncture needle is selectively introduced into these key vessels rather than blindly into the varix cavity, aiming to achieve true devascularization—i.e., cessation of flow within the entire collateral network—rather than partial luminal filling. This conceptual shift prioritizes hemodynamic control over simple mechanical occlusion. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to analyze the efficacy and safety of EUS treatment compared with conventional endoscopic treatment in patients with GOV to assess the clinical value of EUS in managing gastroesophageal varices.

Methods

Study design and participants

A total of 107 patients with gastroesophageal varices (GOVs) who underwent elective endoscopic cyanoacrylate glue injection at the Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Center of Nantong University Affiliated Hospital between August 2022 and June 2024 were selected for the study. The patients were divided into two groups: a conventional endoscopy group (52 patients) and an EUS group (55 patients). During the perioperative period and follow-up, all patients underwent routine pulse oximetry monitoring. Patients with suspected embolism underwent chest and abdominal CT scans for confirmation. No symptomatic or asymptomatic ectopic embolism occurred in this study. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce confounding biases.

The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) met the diagnostic criteria for moderate to severe GOV with a history of rupture and bleeding; (3) were eligible for endoscopic examination and cyanoacrylate glue injection treatment; and (4) provided signed informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) isolated gastric varices (IGV type); (2) absolute contraindications for endoscopic examination; and (3) concurrent malignant tumors such as liver cancer or portal vein thrombosis, severe cardiovascular diseases, or failure of other organs; (4) Patients who have had esophageal or gastric surgery (Fig. 1).

Allocation Criteria: Since this was a retrospective study without randomization, the allocation to conventional endoscopy or EUS-guided treatment was determined by clinical practice conditions. Patients were selected for EUS treatment if the operator deemed the vascular anatomy complex or if resources permitted. Conversely, patients were treated conventionally if EUS equipment or expertise was unavailable at the time of admission. To minimize confounding bias, Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was subsequently employed.

Interventions

EUS Group: In this group, polidocanol and cyanoacrylate glue injections were performed with the assistance of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS). The procedure is as follows: (1) The conventional endoscope is inserted into the stomach to observe the mucosa of the lower esophagus and the gastric fundus near the cardia for varices (Fig. 2); (2) if varices are observed, it is switched to an ultrasound endoscope (EUS model: OLYMPUS GF-UCT260) for sector scanning (varices appear as echo-free areas on EUS, and Doppler flow signals can be detected), and the diameter and source vessels of the varices can be measured (Fig. 3); (3) under the guidance of Doppler ultrasound (ultrasound machine model: OLYMPUS EUME2 PREMIER PLUS), the puncture needle (Boston Scientific 22G endoscopic ultrasound biopsy needle) was selectively advanced into the main feeding or draining collateral vessels rather than solely into the varix lumen.; (4) guided by the EUS, injects polidocanol and cyanoacrylate glue [Surgical glue, N-butyl cyanoacrylate GLUBRAN2] via the “sandwich” technique (sugar-glue-sugar injection). Special attention was paid to ensuring that the polidocanol and cyanoacrylate glue reached the proximal and distal segments of the targeted collateral; (5) Successful devascularization was defined by complete loss of Doppler flow signals within the treated varix and its supplying vessels, EUS guidance allows observation of the echo-free area completely filled with high or mixed echoes; (6) after the injection, use color Doppler again to observe the blood flow in the varices and evaluate the embolization effect (Fig. 4).

Conventional Endoscopy Group: In this group, polidocanol and cyanoacrylate glue injections were performed with the assistance of a conventional endoscope. The procedure was as follows: (1) The conventional endoscope was inserted into the stomach; (2) the extent of the lesion was determined, and polidocanol and cyanoacrylate glue were injected via the “sandwich” technique (sugar-glue-sugar); and (3) After the injection, the embolization of the varices was performed under the guidance of the conventional endoscope.

Scheduled follow-up endoscopies were performed at 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months post-treatment to assess variceal eradication, rebleeding, and ulcer healing. Patients presenting with symptoms suggestive of rebleeding between scheduled visits underwent urgent endoscopy.

Observation indicators

(1) Comparison of treatment doses between the conventional endoscopy group and the EUS group: the doses of polidocanol and cyanoacrylate glue used, as well as the total number of treatments in each treatment technique. (2) Comparison of rebleeding rates between the conventional endoscopy group and the EUS group: The number of bleeding cases at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively, was compared, and the rebleeding rate was calculated. (3) Comparison of ulcer formation rates between the conventional endoscopy group and the EUS group: The number of glue expulsion ulcers formed at 3 months postoperatively, was calculated, and the ulcer formation rate was calculated (Fig. 5).

Endpoint definitions

Rebleeding was defined as any episode of hematemesis, melena, or hematochezia accompanied by a decrease in hemoglobin level of ≥ 2 g/dL within 24 h, or requiring hospital admission for gastrointestinal bleeding. All suspected rebleeding episodes were confirmed by urgent endoscopy, which documented active bleeding from gastroesophageal varices (Forrest Ia/Ib) or the presence of fresh thrombus overlying varices. Bleeding originating from other sources, such as portal hypertensive gastropathy, gastric ulcers, or erosive gastritis, was excluded from the rebleeding endpoint.

Ulcer formation referred specifically to mucosal defects occurring at the site of previous cyanoacrylate injection (glue expulsion ulcers). Ulcers were diagnosed endoscopically according to standard morphological criteria (crater with exudate or erythema) during scheduled follow-up examinations, primarily at 3 months post-procedure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS 27.0 software. For continuous data, normality tests were performed. If the data followed a normal distribution, statistical descriptions are given as the means ± standard deviations (x̅ ± s), and comparisons between two groups were conducted via t test. For categorical data (e.g., bleeding rate, and ulcer formation rate), the χ² test was used for statistical description, expressed as counts (%), with P < 0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. Throughout the manuscript, only P values < 0.05 are described as statistically significant; P values ≥ 0.05 are referred to as trends or numerical differences, without implying statistical significance.

To adjust for baseline imbalances due to the non-randomized nature of the study, Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was conducted to minimize selection bias. Variables included in the matching model were age, gender, Child-Pugh classification, Sarin classification, and variceal grading. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm was used with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to assess balance, with SMD < 0.1 indicating good balance.

Results

Using either conventional endoscopy or EUS endoscopy, both groups of patients achieved effective occlusion of the target varices after treatment, and the surgical success rate was 100% in both groups (Table 1). No severe complications, such as organ embolism or death, were observed in either group. No thromboembolic events were observed during hospitalization or follow-up. Embolism was assessed clinically and, when indicated, by imaging; no asymptomatic embolisms were detected during follow-up. However, postoperative adverse events, such as fever, abdominal pain, melena, or hematochezia, occurred more frequently in the conventional endoscopy group than in the EUS group. In the conventional endoscopy group, there were 9 cases of melena or hematochezia (17.31%) after surgery, whereas there was only 1 case (1.81%) in the EUS group, and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.016). The endoscopic treatment costs for the two groups were 15197.72 (12073.29,21446.58) RMB and 14048.86 (10924.43,17948.86) RMB, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Comparison of the rebleeding rate and ulcer formation rate between the conventional endoscopy group and the EUS group

Both groups achieved effective hemostasis after treatment and were followed up regularly. Although numerically higher in the EUS group at 3 months, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.281). Rebleeding rates at 3 months and 1 year postoperatively were lower in the EUS group than in the conventional endoscopy group. Furthermore, the 6‑month rebleeding rate and the 3‑month ulcer formation rate were significantly lower in the EUS group (both P < 0.05; Table 5). The most pronounced benefit of EUS‑SVD emerged at 6 months, suggesting a time‑dependent treatment effect.

Kaplan‒Meier analysis was conducted to compare the cumulative incidence of rebleeding between the two groups (Fig. 6). The cumulative incidence of rebleeds was significantly lower in the EUS group (HR 0.38, 95%CI 0.17–0.86, P = 0.021) than in the conventional endoscopy group. Rebleeding rate and ulcer formation rate in GOV-1 and GOV-2 after different treatment methods

Patients were divided into two groups according to the Sarin classification: the GOV-1 group and the GOV-2 group. This classification was proposed by Indian scholar Sarin and describes gastric varices (GV) and esophageal varices (EV) on basis of their relationship within the stomach and the location of the GV in the stomach. It can be divided into gastroesophageal varices (GOVs) and isolated gastric varices (IGVs). The GOV types are further divided into GOV1 and GOV2 types. GOV1 refers to a GV located in the lesser curvature of the stomach, combined with the presence of an EV. GOV2 refers to a GV located in the greater curvature of the stomach and is also combined with an EV. In this study, patients were divided into two groups: GOV-1 group and the GOV-2 group. Both groups achieved effective hemostasis after treatment and were followed up regularly.

Patients in the GOV-1 group were categorized to receive two different treatments and were divided into the conventional endoscopy group and the EUS group. No statistically significant differences were observed in rebleeding rates at 3, 6, or 12 months or in ulcer formation rate in the GOV-1 subgroup (all P > 0.05), although numerically lower rates were noted in the EUS group (Table 6).

Discussions

Gastroesophageal varices (GOVs) are a common clinical manifestation in patients with portal hypertension and are a frequent complication of cirrhosis. The risk of variceal rupture is closely related to the severity of cirrhosis [10]. This condition is also a common cause of upper gastrointestinal bleeding, second only to duodenal and gastric ulcers. Prolonged portal hypertension weakens the vessel walls of varices, making them more prone to rupture because of factors such as gastric acid erosion, mechanical friction from food, or a sudden increase in intra-abdominal pressure [11]. Once rupture occurs, the bleeding is often rapid and recurrent, even if successful hemostasis is achieved during emergency care. The rebleeding rate can still reach 70%-80%, and the mortality rate from rebleeding is as high as 25%-50% [12, 13]. Therefore, gastrointestinal endoscopy has become the first-line treatment for emergency hemostasis and rebleeding prevention in cases of GOV. Clinically, endoscopic sclerotherapy combined with tissue adhesive injection is commonly used. The tissue adhesive reacts with blood to solidify quickly, forming a clot that blocks the ruptured vessel, thereby achieving hemostasis and preventing rebleeding. However, since gastric varices are located in the submucosa, distinguishing between varices and gastric folds via conventional endoscopy is difficult. If sclerotherapy and adhesive injections are performed under conventional endoscopy, there is a risk of injection adjacent to the varices, increasing the risk of complications. Therefore, while conventional endoscopic sclerotherapy and adhesive injection are currently among the first-choice treatments for GOV, precise injection into the varices is necessary to ensure complete occlusion, thereby achieving high hemostasis rates while reducing rebleeding and complications.

Within the limitations of this single-center retrospective study, the advantages of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided treatment are more pronounced than those of conventional endoscopy, including precise localization of the varices, ultrasound visualization throughout the injection process, and the ability to confirm variceal occlusion through Doppler imaging. It should be noted that EUS-guided intervention requires advanced endoscopic skills and familiarity with Doppler ultrasound interpretation, which may limit its immediate applicability in centers without specialized expertise. According to Boustiere et al., EUS increases the detection rate of varices sixfold [14]. Iwase et al. also reported that residual blood flow after endoscopic treatment was closely related to the risk of rebleeding [15]. EUS can also measure variceal diameter to control the amount of tissue adhesive injected.

Meanwhile, recent high-level evidence has consistently highlighted the evolving role of EUS-guided interventions. Meta-analyses by Baig et al. and Mohan et al. [16, 17]have shown that EUS-guided coil plus cyanoacrylate improves variceal obliteration and reported trends toward lower rebleeding compared with conventional endoscopic glue injection, albeit with substantial heterogeneity. Supporting this trajectory, the network meta-analysis by Giri et al. suggested that BRTO and EUS-guided therapies may rank superior to direct glue injection for specific outcomes, although the overall certainty of evidence remains limited [18]. Moreover, while the Cochrane review by Jackson et al. confirmed the superiority of cyanoacrylate over band ligation for rebleeding reduction, it did not capture the emerging EUS-based techniques, underscoring the rapid evolution of this field [19]. Contemporary technical reviews and observational studies from 2020 to 2024 have further refined these concepts, describing various EUS-guided approaches, coil-glue combinations, and their favorable safety profiles.

Therefore, EUS-guided sclerotherapy and adhesive injection may be safer and more effective treatment options for patients with GOV. In our study, we used EUS to scan and measure the diameter and connections of varices to determine whether to use coil embolization. We then performed the “sugar‒glue‒sugar” sandwich method under EUS guidance. The advantages of this method include the following: (1) Precise localization of the varices, including their diameter and connections. (2) Real-time EUS scanning to ensure that the needle enters the varices. (3)”Ultrasound visualization” of the entire injection process, monitoring changes in the vessel lumen and the disappearance of blood flow. (4) Reduced tissue adhesive dosage, which lowers the risk of ectopic embolization and reduces treatment costs.

To ensure the accuracy of the data, we did not administer postoperative β-blockers therapy for primary prevention. The results of this study show that both the conventional endoscopy group and the EUS group achieved effective hemostasis after treatment and regular follow-up. Compared with the conventional endoscopy group, the EUS group received less sclerosing agent, tissue adhesive, and required fewer treatment sessions. Additionally, EUS was associated with a reduction in rebleeding at 3 months and 1 year, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. The most robust benefit was observed at 6 months, consistent with more complete varix obliteration. These findings suggest that compared with conventional endoscopy, that EUS-guided sclerotherapy and adhesive injection require fewer materials during surgery and result in lower rebleeding and ulcer formation rates. EUS provides precise ultrasound scanning of the gastric lumen, revealing the detailed structure of the gastric wall, and color Doppler flow imaging allows for clear and vivid visualization of the tortuous veins. Under EUS guidance, variceal vessels can be accurately localized, reducing the likelihood of off-target injections seen in conventional endoscopy. Furthermore, EUS allows for precise determination of whether the varices have been completely occluded, reducing the risk of rebleeding. EUS also offers the ability to visualize concealed or hard-to-detect bleeding points, providing a solution to the limitations of traditional diagnostic and therapeutic methods. This strengthens the technological support for emergency clinical interventions. In this study, we compared treatment outcomes between the conventional endoscopy group and the EUS group for different types of GOVs and varying degrees of varices. The results revealed that for patients with GOV1, GOV2, or moderate to severe varices, the rebleeding rates at 6 and 12 months and the ulcer formation rates at 3 months were lower in the EUS group than in the conventional endoscopy group. This demonstrates that EUS results in a higher variceal occlusion rate than conventional endoscopy does, resulting in a lower rate of late rebleeding [20, 21].

Owing to the limited number of cases in this study, there may be a lack of statistical power, meaning that potential differences in adverse events between treatment groups may not have been fully revealed. Whether EUS should be widely applied in precise devascularization surgery requires further collection of large amounts of data for comparative studies. Another limitation is the small number of rebleeding events, which restricted our ability to perform robust multivariate logistic regression analysis. The odds ratios for some variables, particularly etiology and treatment modality, were notably small, potentially reflecting overfitting and instability rather than strong biological effects. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate these associations.

Limitations of the study

First, as a single-center retrospective cohort, selection bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded despite PSM.

Second, all subgroup analyses were exploratory, and multiple comparisons were not adjusted; therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Third, the limited number of rebleeding events restricted our ability to perform robust multivariable logistic regression analysis. To minimize overfitting, we adopted a parsimonious model including only core clinical covariates; nevertheless, these regression results remain exploratory.

Fourth, external validity may be limited to centers with similar EUS expertise.

Conclusions

Previous studies have shown that endoscopic ultrasound selective variceal devascularization for gastroesophageal varices has a good therapeutic effect on variceal rupture and bleeding. The aim of this study was to understand the application value of precise endoscopic ultrasound-guided disconnection in the treatment of esophageal and gastric varices caused by portal hypertension and to provide a new approach for clinical diagnosis and treatment. We identified that the clinical outcomes are significant and that the postoperative complications are mild, with most being effectively alleviated through symptomatic treatment.

In conclusion, within the limitations of this single-center retrospective study, EUS-SVD appears to be associated with reduced rebleeding and ulcer formation rates compared with conventional endoscopy. However, given the non-randomized design and potential for operator-dependent variability, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Further large-scale, prospective, randomized controlled trials are required to validate the efficacy and safety of EUS-SVD before it can be broadly recommended for routine clinical practice.
