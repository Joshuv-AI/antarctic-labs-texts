CYBB regulates synovial fibroblast inflammatory activation and sEV-mediated synovial–cartilage communication in osteoarthritis
Authors: Kai Chen, Xiaoyu Dai, Peizhen Wang, Chuyi Wang, Chang Lv, Huaqiang Tao, Dechun Geng, Wenge Ding, Shujun Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAlthough synovial inflammation is recognized as a factor in the progression of osteoarthritis (OA), the specific role of fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) in cartilage degradation has yet to be fully elucidated. This study sought to identify key regulators that connect inflammatory FLS to chondrocyte dysfunction.MethodsAn integrated re-analysis of bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing data from OA synovial tissues was conducted to identify consistently upregulated genes. Functional assays, including migration and invasion tests, were performed on inflammatory FLS. FLS with silenced CYBB expression were co-cultured with chondrocytes to evaluate paracrine effects. small extracellular vesicle (sEV) isolation and tracking were utilized to assess intercellular communication. An OA rat model was employed for in vivo validation through local CYBB knockdown.ResultsCYBB expression was markedly upregulated in OA synovium, particularly within inflammatory FLS. Inhibition of CYBB reduced FLS inflammation, migration, and invasion. In co-culture experiments, inflammatory FLS induced chondrocyte inflammation and disrupted matrix gene expression in a manner dependent on CYBB. These effects were mediated by sEVs derived from fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), with their inflammatory cargo being modified through CYBB silencing. In vivo experiments demonstrated that localized suppression of CYBB alleviated synovitis and cartilage degeneration.ConclusionsCYBB serves as a pivotal mediator linking synovial inflammation to cartilage damage in osteoarthritis (OA), by mechanistically regulating the sEV-mediated communication from FLS to chondrocytes. Targeting CYBB could potentially represent a novel therapeutic strategy for OA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13075-026-03866-y.

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a widespread chronic joint disorder causing significant pain and disability worldwide [1], affecting about 7.6% of the global population, or nearly 595 million people, as of 2020 [2]. This condition severely impacts quality of life and poses a major socioeconomic burden. Despite its prevalence, early diagnosis and intervention remain challenging, and effective strategies to slow its progression are limited, underscoring the need for deeper insights into its biological mechanisms.

Traditionally, OA was viewed mainly as a condition involving mechanical stress and cartilage degeneration. However, evidence now shows that OA affects various joint tissues, including the synovium and subchondral bone, and is recognized as a whole-joint disease involving complex interactions among cellular and structural components [3–5]. Recent research, such as studies by Chou C et al., has emphasized the importance of synovial and cartilage cell interactions in OA progression [6]. Advances in single-cell sequencing have further revealed how cell communication can influence pro-inflammatory macrophages and osteogenic stem cells, impacting the disease’s progression [7].

Recent research highlights the significant role of synovial inflammation in OA, alongside cartilage degeneration [8]. Evidence links synovial inflammation to structural damage and pain in OA, with the immune status of the synovium correlating with inflammation progression and pain perception [9, 10]. This makes it a potential target for therapeutic interventions. During OA progression, synovial fibroblasts, macrophages, and chondrocytes interact, releasing cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, and matrix metalloproteinases, which contribute to joint degeneration [8]. Activated FLS further worsen synovial inflammation by releasing chemokines, pro-inflammatory mediators, and matrix-degrading enzymes, accelerating cartilage matrix degradation [11, 12]. Despite these observations, the regulatory mechanisms controlling synovial fibroblast activation and their interactions with other joint cells during OA progression are not fully understood.

By re-analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing and bulk transcriptomic data from public database, this study explored cell interactions in osteoarthritic joints and identified CYBB as a key dysregulated gene in synovial inflammation. Targeting CYBB reduced inflammatory markers in FLS and slowed OA progression. The research also revealed that sEVs from FLS play a crucial role in FLS-chondrocyte communication in OA. These findings suggest that CYBB-regulated, sEV-mediated signaling is a novel pathway in OA pathogenesis, offering potential targets for early intervention and therapy.

Methods

Data search and analyses

Five datasets were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including GSE143514, GSE89408, GSE55235, GSE211584 and GSE152805, covering RNA-sequencing and expression profiling of synovial tissues. The raw data of osteoarthritis and control groups from GSE143514 and GSE89408 were analyzed using DESeq2 in R to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and p < 0.05. Heat maps were created with DESeq2 [13], and the protein-protein interaction network was analyzed using the STRING database and visualized with Cytoscape [14, 15]. CytoHubba (MCC analysis, top 15 hub genes) and MCODE in Cytoscape identified hub genes and subnetworks. MiRWalk predicted microRNAs interacting with these genes. Venn diagrams, GO, and KEGG enrichment of DEGs were analyzed using Oebiotech platform (https://www.oebiotech.com/). GSE211584 and GSE152805 were analyzed with Seurat in R [16]. Cell identification used the single R package and CellMarker 2.0 [17, 18]. CellChat analyzed cell-cell communication [19].

Inference scores of hub genes on orthopaedic diseases

Inference scores for hub genes linked to osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and osteoporosis were sourced from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTDbase, http://ctdbase.org/) [20]. Histograms of these scores were then created using GraphPad Prism software (v9.3.1).

Gene expression level and ROC curve

The hub gene expression levels in GSE55235 were analyzed using GraphPad Prism, employing an unpaired t-test with p < 0.05 as the significance threshold. Significant genes were marked with an asterisk (*) on box plots. ROC curves for these genes were created via the Oebiotech platform.

Drugs and reagents

IL-1β was sourced from SinoBiological (Beijing, China). DMEM/F-12 medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from EallBio (Beijing, China). Trizol reagent, RIPA Lysis Buffer, Dil dye, and Crystal violet solution were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). siCYBB came from GenePharma (Suzhou, China), and Lipofectamine™ 3000 from ThermoFisher Scientific (MA, USA). Antibodies used were listed in Table S1.

Cell culture

Rat FLS cells and articular chondrocytes from Procell System (Wuhan, China) were cultured in DMEM/F-12 with 10% FBS. IL-1β (10 ng/ml) was applied to FLS cells for 6, 12, and 24 h. Before IL-1β exposure, siCYBB was transfected using Lipofectamine 3000. Cells were transfected at 70% confluence. For each well of a 6‑well plate, 50 pmol of siRNA and 3 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 were added; for a 10‑cm dish, 250 pmol of siRNA and 10 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 were used. Fresh medium was replaced 6 h after transfection. Co-culturing was done in Corning Transwell plates, with FLS cells in the upper chamber and chondrocytes in the lower chamber. After IL-1β treatment, FLS and chondrocytes were co-cultured. FLS membranes were labeled with Dil dye. Cells were washed with PBS, cultured, and the supernatant was used for 24-hour co-culture with chondrocytes. Fluorescence was analyzed using a Zeiss LSM900 confocal microscope.

OA model

For the animal model experiment, 8-week-old female Sprague Dawley rats from Soochow University were used, with ethical approval from the university’s Animal Ethics Committee (SUDA20221009A03). Initially, rats were divided into sham surgery and osteoarthritis (OA) groups. During treatment, they were assigned to sham, OA with normal saline (OA + NS), and OA with siCYBB (OA+siCYBB) groups, n = 7 per group. The OA model was established by destabilizing the medial meniscus (DMM) in the left knee [21]. The OA+siCYBB group received 50 µl of 300 µM siCYBB injections in the knee on days 7, 14 and 21 post-surgery, as per previous protocols [22]. The study lasted four weeks, after which rats were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation.

RT-PCR

The expression levels of Ctss, Cybb, Il-6 and Tnf-α were measured with RT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Subsequently, the RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) utilizing the 5X PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Otsu, Shiga, Japan). The RT-PCR was conducted with a reaction mixture comprising 2 µl of cDNA, 0.5 µl of primers, 2 µl of double-distilled water (ddH2O) and 5 µl of TB Green PREMIX Ex Taq II (Takara, Otsu, Shiga, Japan). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) served as the housekeeping gene. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the sequences for each gene-specific primer are provided in Table S2.

Western blot

After intervention, cells were washed by PBS and subsequently lysed using RIPA reagent (Beyotime, China) for approximately 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 14,300 rpm, and the supernatant was collected and quantified by a BCA kit (Beyotime, China). Protein samples were prepared by adding a reducing buffer and heating in a metal bath, followed by electrophoresis on a gel (Epizyme Biotech, China) and transfer onto a PVDF membrane for blocking and antibody incubation. Images were captured using a Tanon luminescence imaging system (Tanon, China) and analyzed with Image J software. All the original bands of the western blots are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Scratch assay

Scratch assay was undertaken in FLS cells on 6-well plate by using a 200-ul gun tip [23, 24]. After washed by PBS, cells were cultured with 1% FBS and DMEM/F-12 as Su et al. reported [24]. The FLS cells were treated with IL-1β (10 ng/ml) and siCYBB + IL-1β before observation. The healing situations were photographed at 0 h and 24 h by an inverted microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Image J and graphpad prism were used to analyzed the wound closure%.

Migration and invasion assay

After intervention, 2*104 FLS cells or chondrocytes were placed in the upper chamber of a transwell with 100 µl serum-free DMEM/F-12. The lower chamber contained 600 µl DMEM/F-12 with 20% FBS. For invasion assays, a matrix glue layer was added to the upper chamber before cell placement. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed with PBS, and the upper cells were removed with a cotton swab. The lower cells were stained with crystal violet for one hour, photographed under an inverted microscope (Zeiss, Germany), and counted using Image J software.

Immunofluorescence

After treating with different interventions, cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. Then cells were blocked using a QuickBlock buffer (Beyotime, China) for 1 h and incubated primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. In the next day, cells were incubated with F-actin, DAPI and secondary antibody. Samples were observed and photographed by a Zeiss laser scanning microscope LSM900 (Zeiss, Germany) and analyzed by Image J software.

RNA sequencing

After the intervention was completed, total RNA of the cells were extracted by a TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, China). Samples were sent to Genewiz (Suzhou, China) for further sequencing on dry ice. The results were then analyzed by Oebiotech platform (https://www.oebiotech.com/). Significant threshold was set as p < 0.05 and fold change > 2 or < 0.5.

sEV extraction

After intervention, cells were washed with PBS and cultured for 3 days in DMEM/F-12 with 10% sEV-depleted FBS (VivaCell Biosciences, China). The supernatant was collected, centrifuged at 2000 g for 15 min, then at 10,000 g for 30 min in a high-speed freezing centrifuge (Thermofisher, USA), and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore, USA). The filtered samples underwent ultracentrifugation at 120,000 g for 90 min in an ultracentrifugation (Himac, Japan), resuspended in PBS, and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The final sEV precipitate was resuspended in PBS. Chondrocytes were treated with sEVs at a concentration of 2 µg/ml, based on total sEV protein [25]. Samples were analyzed by western blot for CD9, CD81, TSG101, Calnexin, and β-actin. sEV extraction and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) in this study were performed by Servicebio (Wuhan, China).

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

TEM assays were carried out by Servicebio (Wuhan, China). For cell samples, after intervention, cells were fixed using a TEM fix regent (Servicebio, China) and scraped off by a cell scraper. Then samples were centrifugated and resuspended by the TEM fix regent and sent to Servicebio on wet ice for TEM test. sEV samples were sent to Servicebio on dry ice and undertaken negative staining and TEM scanning.

Label-free quantitative proteomics

FLS cells were divided into 3 groups, including Control group, IL-1β group and siCYBB + IL-1β group. Each group including threefold repetitions. After intervention, sEVs were extracted and sent to Servicebio (Wuhan, China) to carry out label-free quantitative proteomics by a Q Exactive™ system (Thermofisher, USA). The results were analyzed by Oebiotech (Shanghai, China).

Histological observation

Rat knee samples were gathered 4 weeks after operation. Then samples were fixed, decalcified, embedded and sliced as our previous study [22]. Then hematoxylin eosin (H&E) staining, safranine O-fast green staining, Masson staining and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining were performed in accordance with established protocols. The outcomes were visualized by an upright microscope (Zeiss, Germany) and analyzed by Image J software. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining involved the following steps: dewaxing, gradient hydration, antigen retrieval, incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, and photographed using a Zeiss confocal microscope (LSM900, Zeiss, Germany). The quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was carried out using the Image J software to calculate the mean fluorescence intensity (Mean = IntDen / Area).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism v9.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Student’s t-test compared two groups, while one-way ANOVA assessed differences among three or more groups. For non‑continuous data, the Kruskal‑Wallis H test was used for multiple‑group comparisons. Significance was set at p < 0.05, with p < 0.01 indicating high significance, “ns” denotes no significance.

Results

CYBB expression is elevated in OA synovium

By analyzing transcriptome sequencing data from OA patients and healthy individuals, we identified 1,473 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in GSE89408 and 515 in GSE143514, both are bulk RNA-sequencing datasets (Fig. 1A-B). These DEGs are linked to pathways like rheumatoid arthritis and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig. 1C-D). The intersection of DEGs from both datasets revealed 81 potential genes (Fig. 1E, Table S3), which are mainly associated with the extracellular space and pathways such as inflammation response and antigen processing (Fig. 1F-G). This suggests these DEGs in synovium may play a role in OA inflammation. The PPI network shows complex interactions among these 81 DEGs (Fig. 1H). The top 15 hub genes identified include CYBB, SELL, VEGFA, TLR8, CLEC12A, CLEC4A, MS4A6A, CTSS, IFI30, LY86, IGSF6, VSIG4, HLA-DRA, HLA-DPB1, and HLA-DPA1, with CYBB scoring the highest (Fig. 1I). These genes form three subclusters (Supplementary Fig. 1A) and are part of a complex microRNA regulatory network (Fig. 1J). They are linked to orthopedic diseases like osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and rheumatoid arthritis (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Notably, CTSS, CYBB, LY86, HLA-DRA, IFI30, CLEC4A, HLA-DPB1, HLA-DPA1, SELL, and VEGFA show differential expression in GSE55235 with a strong ROC curve (Supplementary Fig. 1C-E), suggesting their potential as markers for distinguishing OA synovium from healthy synovium.

We targeted FLS cells with IL1-β and observed a gradual increase in CYBB and CTSS protein and gene expression over time (Fig. 1K-L, Supplementary Fig. 1F). Immunofluorescence indicated higher CYBB fluorescence in IL1-β-treated FLS cells and OA rat synovial membranes compared to controls (Fig. 1M-N).

To investigate the role of fibroblasts in extracellular space pathways, we analyzed a mouse OA synovial tissue single-cell sequencing dataset across different modeling times. The synovial cells were categorized into six types: B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, ILCs, macrophages, and stromal cells (Supplementary Fig. 2A). We observed significant dynamic changes in the proportions of these cell types over time (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Notably, macrophages were most abundant on day 7 but decreased by day 28, indicating varying cellular compositions during different inflammation stages. We also examined the expression and distribution of Cybb, Ctss, Tlr8, Ifi30, Igsf6 and Ly86, which were predominantly expressed in macrophages, followed by fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 2C-D). Analysis revealed interactions between fibroblasts and other cells, particularly macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 2E-H), with 24 significant ligand-receptor pairs identified (Supplementary Fig. 2I-J). The highest scores were for C3-C3ar1 and C3-(Itgam+Itgb2). This suggests that fibroblasts in the synovium play a role in OA inflammation and regulation and interact with other synovial cells.

Intervention of the FLS CYBB gene not only affects the inflammatory indicators of FLS, but also can indirectly influence chondrocytes

To investigate the impact of CYBB interference on synovial cells, we designed four siRNA molecules targeting CYBB. After confirming successful siRNA transfection, siRNA-3 and siRNA-4 effectively reduced CYBB mRNA expression (Fig. 2A-B, Table S4). We selected siRNA-3 for further study. Western blot and qRT-PCR assays showed that IL1-β increased CYBB, IL-6, and TNF-α expression in FLS cells, and si-CYBB transfection significantly reduced these levels (Fig. 2C-D, G, Supplementary Fig. 1G). Additionally, IL1-β enhanced wound healing, invasion and migration in FLS cells, which were significantly reversed by si-CYBB (Fig. 2E-F). RNA sequencing showed that siCYBB impacts genes related to inflammation, extracellular space, IL-17, and TNF signaling pathways (Fig. 2H-I, Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). This suggests that inhibiting CYBB significantly reduces FLS cell inflammation.

We observed the extracellular space pathway in our enrichment results and hypothesize that inflammatory changes in the synovium, along with CYBB intervention, may lead to the secretion of factors affecting surrounding cells. To investigate if these synovial changes impact chondrocytes, we co-cultured chondrocytes with intervention-treated FLS cells using 6-well transwell chambers and conducted transcriptome sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 3C-G). In co-cultured chondrocytes, the IL1-β group showed significant enrichment in pathways like cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and chemokine signaling (Fig. 2J). Comparing the si-CYBB and IL1-β co-culture groups, pathways such as IL-17 signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction were notably enriched (Fig. 2K). Immunofluorescence results revealed that in the IL1-β co-culture group, IL-6 and TNF-α fluorescence intensities rose, while type II collagen intensity fell. This trend was reduced in the si-CYBB co-culture group (Fig. 2L), suggesting that FLS can affect chondrocytes via mechanisms like the cytokine-cytokine receptor pathway.

Cytokines-cytokine receptor pathway is one of the interaction pathways between fibroblasts and chondrocytes in OA patients

To confirm the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction between fibroblasts and chondrocytes, we analyzed a single-cell sequencing dataset from synovial membranes and cartilage, including both minimally damaged outer lateral tibial (oLT) plateau cartilage and damaged medial tibial (MT) plateau cartilage. Using cell markers from the single R and CellMarker 2.0 databases, we categorized the cells into seven types: chondrocytes, fibroblasts, monocyte-macrophages, muscle cells, endothelial cells, T cells, and mast cells (Fig. 3A-B). In severely damaged cartilage, the proportion of cartilage decreased while fibrotic cells increased, and monocyte-macrophages were notably present in synovial tissue (Fig. 3C). We validated fibroblast and chondrocyte classification using COL1A1 and COL2A1 and examined gene expression patterns like CTSS, CYBB, LY86, HLA-DRA, HLA-DPA1 and IFI30 (Fig. 3D-E). CTSS, CYBB, HLA-DRA, and HLA-DPA1 are primarily expressed in mononuclear macrophages but also appear in fibroblasts, suggesting fibroblasts may play a role in antigen presentation. Cell interaction analysis reveals significant interactions among fibroblasts, macrophages, and chondrocytes in the OA microenvironment (Fig. 3F-I). Fifteen ligand-receptor pairs, particularly involving the ANGPTL signaling pathway, were identified between fibroblasts and chondrocytes (Fig. 3J). Heatmap analysis indicates ANGPTL’s role in interactions with chondrocytes, muscle, and endothelial cells, involving pairs like ANGPTL4-SDC4, ANGPTL4-SDC2, and ANGPTL4-SDC1. The heatmap analysis reveals that the ANGPTL signaling pathway primarily facilitates interactions between fibroblasts and chondrocytes, muscle cells, and endothelial cells (Fig. 3K).

sEV pathway is another interaction pathway between fibroblasts and chondrocytes

To investigate alternative communication between fibroblasts and chondrocytes, we stained FLS cell membranes with Dil dye and co-cultured the supernatants with chondrocytes (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence revealed that chondrocytes absorbed fluorescent microvesicles, suggesting fibroblasts secrete vesicular structures for signaling (Fig. 4B). We hypothesized these might be sEVs and performed ultra-high-speed centrifugation on various groups’ supernatants. TEM confirmed the microvesicles were round with a double-layer membrane (Fig. 4D), and NTA showed their diameters ranged from 100 to 200 nm (Fig. 4E-F). The western blot showed positive sEV markers CD9, CD81 and TSG101, confirming that FLS-secreted small vesicles are sEVs (Fig. 4G). Additionally, TEM scans revealed that IL1-β treatment led to many vacuolar structures in FLS cells, which si-CYBB reduced (Fig. 4C), indicating that local inflammatory factors significantly affect fibroblasts.

To investigate the impact of FLS sEVs on chondrocytes, we co-cultured sEVs from FLS cells with chondrocytes and performed invasion and migration assays [26]. The results indicated that sEVs in the IL1-β treatment group reduced chondrocyte invasion and migration, but this effect was mitigated in the IL1-β + si-CYBB group (Fig. 4H-K). RNA sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 3H-J) revealed that sEVs in the IL1-β group significantly influenced cell adhesion, calcium signaling, cytokine pathways, pH reduction, and peroxide-mediated cell death in chondrocytes (Fig. 4L-M). In the si-CYBB intervention group, the previously enriched pathways were absent, but AMPK signaling, cartilage homeostasis, and certain metabolism pathways were present (Fig. 4N-O). This suggests that inflammatory FLS cells impact chondrocytes via sEV secretion. Inhibiting CYBB expression in IL1-β-treated FLS cells allows their sEVs to restore chondrocyte homeostasis.

Analysis of the internal proteins contained in sEVs secreted by FLS cells after IL1-β intervention and si-CYBB treatment

To determine which sEV components from different FLS groups impact chondrocytes, we performed a label-free quantitative proteomics assay (Fig. 5A-B). In the IL1-β group compared to the control, 98 proteins were highly expressed and 139 were minimally expressed (Fig. 5C). In the siCYBB+ IL1-β group versus IL1-β group, 184 proteins were upregulated and 77 downregulated (Fig. 5D). The differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the IL1-β group were linked to extracellular space, cell adhesion, calcium ion binding, and several pro-inflammatory pathways, including IL-17, complement and coagulation cascades, and TNF signaling (Fig. 5E-G, Supplementary Fig. 4A-B). After IL-1β treatment, FLS sEVs showed increased expression of proteins in these pro-inflammatory pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4A). This indicates that FLS cells can indeed transfer inflammatory signals to the extracellular environment and communicate with surrounding cells through sEVs.

In the siCYBB+IL1-β group compared to the IL1-β group, exosomal DEPs were enriched in pathways such as autolysosome, extracellular space, ferric iron binding, cell adhesion, mineral absorption, ferroptosis, and necroptosis (Fig. 5H-J, Supplementary Fig. 4C-D). Following siCYBB intervention, proteins related to mineral absorption, necroptosis, and ferroptosis were downregulated in FLS-secreted sEVs (Supplementary Fig. 4D). This suggests that siCYBB may reduce the transmission of ferroptosis and necroptosis signals from FLS cells to the extracellular environment, potentially protecting nearby chondrocytes.

These DEPs are involved in key pathways like inflammation and ferroptosis and show intricate protein-protein interactions (Fig. 5K-L), forming a complex regulatory network between synovial cells and chondrocytes in OA progression. We also identified proteins whose expression trends reversed after siCYBB intervention compared to IL1-β alone. The Venn diagram reveals that siCYBB reversed 7 proteins from an elevated trend and 54 from a decreased trend under IL1-β (Fig. 5M). We speculate that these proteins may serve as target proteins for the synthesis of engineered sEVs, which can be used for the treatment of OA.

Local inhibition of CYBB can alleviate the progression of OA in vivo

To investigate CYBB’s impact on OA progression in vivo, we created an OA rat model and administered local si-CYBB injections. Immunofluorescence showed that si-CYBB effectively reduced CYBB fluorescence in OA knee tissue (Fig. 6A). H&E, safranin O-fast green, and Masson staining revealed that OA led to significant inflammation and cartilage damage in the knee, increasing OARSI and synovitis scores [27, 28]. However, inhibiting CYBB alleviated these tissue changes and improved scores (Fig. 6B-D, H-I). IHC indicated that OA increased IL-6 and TNF-α positive cells and decreased collagen II-positive chondrocytes, trends reversed by si-CYBB (Fig. 6E-G, J-L). The above results indicate that CYBB can serve as an effective therapeutic target for alleviating the progression of OA.

Discussion

This study utilized integrative transcriptomic analyses and targeted functional assays to investigate the role of CYBB in FLS and its impact on cartilage in osteoarthritis. Through analysis of bulk RNA-sequencing datasets and single-cell transcriptomic profiles of OA synovium, we found CYBB to be consistently upregulated in association with inflammatory and extracellular pathways. In IL-1β-stimulated FLS, CYBB expression increased at both the transcript and protein levels. Silencing CYBB with siRNA reduced inflammatory gene expression and diminished FLS migration and invasion, highlighting its role in their inflammatory activation. Co-culture systems and sEV-based assays revealed that inflammatory FLS influenced chondrocyte inflammatory markers and matrix gene expression in a CYBB-dependent manner. Extracellular vesicles from FLS were isolated and confirmed using various methods, and they were found to be absorbed by chondrocytes. Analysis showed that inhibiting CYBB changed the inflammatory and stress-related contents of these sEVs. This suggests that CYBB influences synovial inflammation and affects sEV communication between FLS and chondrocytes, playing a role in OA cartilage damage.

Recent studies show that extracellular vesicles are active mediators of osteoarthritis progression, not mere byproducts of joint pathology. Proteomic and functional analyses reveal that these vesicles disrupt chondrocyte balance, promote catabolic gene expression, shift macrophage polarization to pro-inflammatory states, and hinder mesenchymal stem cell chondrogenesis, all contributing to joint degeneration [29]. Furthermore, osteocyte-derived extracellular vesicles facilitate bone-to-cartilage communication, accelerating osteoarthritis progression [30]. Evidence from inflammatory arthritis indicates that fibroblast-derived vesicles can directly signal chondrocytes, leading to apoptosis and cartilage damage in vivo [31]. In parallel, Inflammatory conditions alter sEV content, influencing the joint’s pro-inflammatory environment and aiding cartilage protection, highlighting that vesicle function depends on the donor cells’ inflammatory state [32]. Immune cell-derived vesicles, particularly macrophage-derived sEV microRNAs, regulate macrophage polarization and chondrocyte hypertrophy by targeting inflammation and cartilage maturation pathways [33]. These findings suggest that inflammatory states in joint-resident cells, like synovial fibroblasts, are encoded in extracellular vesicles and transmitted to cartilage and other joint cells. Our data indicate that inflammatory activation of fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) changes the impact of their sEVs on chondrocytes, and modifying fibroblast inflammation can reduce these sEV-mediated effects. This highlights the potential of targeting synovial fibroblast inflammation to limit pathogenic vesicle-mediated signaling in osteoarthritis progression.

CYBB, also known as NOX2, is a key component of the NADPH oxidase complex studied primarily for its role in regulating inflammation in immune cells, rather than causing cellular damage [34]. It influences inflammatory gene expression and cytokine production in myeloid and antigen-presenting cells through regulated intracellular pathways [35, 36]. However, its role in synovial fibroblast biology is largely unexamined. In osteoarthritis, synovial fibroblasts are significant contributors to inflammation and the local inflammatory environment. Our data show that CYBB expression is elevated in inflammatory FLS and influences fibroblast inflammatory programs, impacting chondrocytes. CYBB affects intercellular communication through secreted factors like sEVs, influencing chondrocyte inflammation and gene expression. These findings identify CYBB as a key regulator of synovial fibroblast inflammation and synovial-cartilage interaction in osteoarthritis progression.

Beyond establishing CYBB as a regulator of synovial fibroblast inflammatory activation, our study provides mechanistic insight into how FLS influence cartilage degeneration through sEV-mediated communication. Although cytokine interactions are known to facilitate synovial-cartilage communication, our findings show that extracellular vesicles also play a significant role in transmitting inflammatory signals. We demonstrated that FLS-derived sEVs are released during inflammation and are effectively taken up by chondrocytes. These sEVs are not merely passive carriers; they significantly influence chondrocyte behavior, affecting inflammatory gene expression, matrix pathways, and cell migration.

Proteomic profiling showed that sEVs from inflammatory fibroblasts were rich in proteins linked to pro-inflammatory pathways like IL-17 and TNF. Inhibiting CYBB altered the sEV protein profile, reducing associations with cell death and mineral handling pathways. While individual sEV components weren’t analyzed, our findings indicate that inflammatory processes in synovial fibroblasts influence vesicle content and function. This distinguishes our study from others focusing on vesicle effects on recipient cells and identifies CYBB as a key link between fibroblast inflammation and vesicle-mediated communication with cartilage.

The pathological relevance of this axis is further supported by our in vivo observations. In a rat osteoarthritis model, inhibiting CYBB locally reduced synovial inflammation and improved cartilage histology. Although sEV-mediated signaling isn’t isolated as the sole mechanism, combined in vitro, transcriptomic, proteomic, and animal data suggest that CYBB suppression decreases synovial inflammation and subsequent cartilage damage. Due to the complexity of joint biology, this effect likely involves multiple pathways, with altered intercellular communication via extracellular vesicles being a significant and previously overlooked factor.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although this study supports vesicle-mediated signaling in synovial-cartilage communication, more targeted in vivo methods to modulate sEV production would enhance causal inference. Additionally, since CYBB is expressed in various joint-resident cell types, future research using cell type-specific manipulation will clarify the roles of fibroblast-derived and non-fibroblast CYBB signaling. In addition, local injection of siRNA may have certain limitations. Due to limited conditions, we were unable to use adeno-associated virus or CYBB conditional knockout animals, which is another limitation of this study.

In summary, we identify CYBB as a pivotal node in OA pathogenesis, regulating both synovial fibroblast inflammation and their sEV-mediated cross-talk with chondrocytes (Fig. 7, drawn by Figdraw 2.0, https://www.figdraw.com/#/paint_index_v2 ). This CYBB-sEV axis represents a novel mechanism in synovial-chondral communication, providing a framework for developing targeted strategies to disrupt this pathogenic signaling loop in OA.
