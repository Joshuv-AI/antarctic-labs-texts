Bionomics and malaria infection rates in anopheline species in Yanomami villages of the Brazilian Amazon Forest
Authors: Jordi Sánchez-Ribas, Teresa Fernandes Silva-do-Nascimento, Cleomar Pereira-Ribeiro, Maycon Sebastião Alberto Santos-Neves, John W. Barnwell, Ricardo Lourenço-de-Oliveira, Joseli Oliveira-Ferreira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a major public health challenge in Brazil, particularly in the Amazon region, where over 90% of cases occur. Indigenous populations, such as the Yanomami, are disproportionately affected due to geographic isolation and environmental vulnerabilities. Despite logistical challenges in remote areas entomological surveillance is essential for developing effective, culturally sensitive and ecologically appropriate vector control strategies.MethodsFrom March 2013 to March 2015, a longitudinal entomological survey was conducted in five Yanomami villages in the Marari community to study vector abundance, biting behaviour and transmission dynamics. Three mosquito collection methods were used: human-protected baited traps (MosqTent), manual Nasci aspirators and CDC light traps. Detection of malaria parasites in Anopheles was performed using molecular methods.ResultsA total of 1962 adult Anopheles from seven species were collected. Anopheles darlingi was the predominant species in Alapusi (87.9%), Gasolina (81.9%) and Taibrapa 1 (52.5%), while Anopheles nuneztovari s.l. was most abundant in Taibrapa 2 (80.0%). Although An. darlingi was mainly captured in peridomestic settings, it was the predominant species biting and resting indoors. Molecular analysis confirmed that An. darlingi was infected with both Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium falciparum with infection rates up to 2.1% in Gasolina and Taibrapa 1. Nightly human biting rate (nHBR) indicated An. darlingi as the predominant vector in both peridomicile and extradomicile environments across most villages. Notably, in Taibrapa 2, An. nuneztovari s.l. exhibited the highest nHBR in the extradomestic area, with estimates of up to 359 bites per night compared to only two bites per night in the peridomestic space. Entomological inoculation rate (EIR) estimates further underscore the central role of An. darlingi in malaria transmission, as the outdoors EIR recorded in Gasolina, Taibrapa 1, and Alapusi ranged from 49.3 to 79.3.ConclusionThese findings highlight the need for village-specific vector control strategies that consider the nocturnal activity and infection dynamics of local Anopheles populations. Tailored interventions such as optimized use of insecticide-treated hammock nets, targeted indoor residual spraying and community led environmental management should be implemented in alignment with local transmission patterns to enhance malaria control effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05598-x.

Background

In Brazil, major successful control efforts decreased the number of malaria cases from 615,246 in 2000 to 143,145 in 2014. Based on this achievement, Brazil has established the goal of eliminating Plasmodium falciparum malaria by 2030 and all autochthonous malaria transmission by 2035, as outlined in the National Malaria Elimination Plan [1]. In 2023, the number of reported autochthonous cases in the country was 140,265, of which more than 90% were acquired in the Amazon basin [2].

In this region, malaria is endemic in some indigenous populations, which are often considered a challenge to malaria elimination due to their geographic isolation. According to the 2022 IBGE Census, 1,693,535 people are indigenous and most of this population is distributed in thousands of villages and settlements located throughout the indigenous lands, from the north to the south of Brazilian territory, but nearly 51% are in the Legal Amazon area [3]. While 36.2% of the population live in urban areas, the majority (63.8%) still inhabit rural or remote areas in the Amazon rainforest [3]. Among these groups, the Yanomami ethnic group is considered to be one of the largest Amerindian communities, with estimated 27,152 members constituting a society of hunters, gatherers and agriculturalists who inhabit the tropical forests in the North of the Brazilian Amazon and neighbouring areas of Venezuela [3, 4]. Until the 1960 s, the Brazilian Yanomami indigenous population remained largely isolated, with limited interaction with other indigenous and non-indigenous peoples. Contact occurred mainly through occasional expeditions into the remote region or the expansion of the extractive activities, particularly by rubber tappers [5]. Over time, however, contact with broader society increased, leading some Yanomami villages to adopt a more sedentary lifestyle. Up to the 1980 s, malaria cases in the Yanomami territory were more restricted to its periphery , where contacts with non-indigenous populations took place [6]. Between 1987 and 1990, the Yanomami territory, particularly in the State of Roraima (RR), was subjected to a large-scale invasion by more than 40,000 gold prospectors called “garimpeiros”, and again between 2020 and 2022 [7–9]. These incursions led to the emergence of autochthonous malaria and sparked severe epidemic outbreaks characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates [8, 9]. Despite ongoing interventions, such as indoor residual spraying (IRS) and the distribution of insecticide-treated hammock nets (ITHNs), malaria control in indigenous territories continues to face serious logistical, environmental, and sociocultural barriers [10, 11]. In 2024, 34,231 malaria cases were reported in the Brazilian Yanomami territory [12].

In the Americas, one of the most important vectors of human malaria is Anopheles (Nyssorhynchus) darlingi, whose geographic distribution ranges from southern Mexico to northern Argentina, extending from the Eastern side of the Andes to the Atlantic coast. In Brazil, this species is by far the main primary malaria vector in the Amazon with considerable anthropophilic and endophagic behaviour, along with partially endophilic habits [13, 14]. It is highly efficient in transmitting malaria, even when found in low population densities [15, 16]. Although An. darlingi predominates in Brazilian malaria endemic areas, other species of anophelines (Anopheles albitarsis s.l., Anopheles nuneztovari s.l., Anopheles triannulatus s.l., Anopheles oswaldoi s.l. and Anopheles medialis) have also been found naturally infected in distinct Amazonian settings and are considered secondary vectors [16–18].

Malaria remains a significant public health threat in the Yanomami region, where access to healthcare is limited and uneven. Entomological studies in remote indigenous territories of the Amazon rainforest are notably scarce, primarily due to the geographic dispersion of villages, restricted access to these areas and the considerable logistical challenges of conducting fieldwork in such environments. This study seeks to characterize the diversity, distribution, and bionomics of Anopheles species in Yanomami villages, with a particular focus on identifying principal malaria vectors. Understanding malaria vectors is crucial for identifying transmitting species, assessing their behaviour, and developing effective, culturally appropriate control strategies. Moreover, monitoring transmission in response to environmental and social factors provides critical insights to guide evidence-based public health interventions and to protect these vulnerable communities.

Methods

Study sites

The study was conducted in five Yanomami villages belonging to “Polo Base Marari”, herein referred to as the Marari community, Amazonas State, Brazil (Fig. 1). Polo Base Marari is one of the 37 Healthcare Base Hubs that comprise the Yanomami Special Indigenous Health District (DSEI-Y) created to deliver healthcare services to indigenous populations. Marari was selected as the study site because it presented the highest number of malaria cases among all 37 Base Hubs at the time of the study design, indicating an intense transmission scenario. Furthermore, all villages under the jurisdiction of Marari were included in the study to ensure comprehensive coverage of this high-burden area. The Marari community is located in a remote, roadless region of the Amazon, characterized by lowland rainforest surrounded by mountains exceeding 1700 m. The area is traversed by four low-order rivers that shape local geography and accessibility. The Xirinita and Catanal Rivers flow near the villages of Castanha and Ahima, while the Catanal and Taibrapa Rivers run along the border of Taibrapa 1. The larger Marari River runs adjacent to the villages of Alapusi, Gasolina, and Taibrapa 2. The Catanal River, a tributary of the Marari River, merges with it near the Gasolina village. The Marari Healthcare Base Hub provides medical assistance to 915 indigenous Yanomami residing in five villages: Castanha/Ahima (with 237 and 56 Yanomami residents, respectively), Gasolina (233), Taibrapa 1 and Taibrapa 2 (234) and Alapusi (155).Fig. 1Map of northern Brazil highlighting the Brazilian Yanomami territory (green area) within the states of Amazonas and Roraima (A), indicating the location of the villages of the Marari community (B)

Malaria transmission across the vast Brazilian Yanomami territory varies significantly, ranging from villages with no reported cases to others experiencing intense and perennial transmission. Historically, the Marari community had the highest malaria burden among all Yanomami communities [2]. For instance, in 2013, although Marari accounted for only 4% of the total population living within the Brazilian Yanomami territory, it was responsible for 35% of all reported malaria cases. The number of malaria cases reported in the five villages of the Marari community during the years of our study, 2013 and 2014, were as follows, respectively: Alapusi, 188 and 260; Castanha/Ahima, 484 and 392; Gasolina, 297 and 367 and Taibrapa, 313 and 400. In all villages, the number of malaria cases per year exceeded the total population, indicating that some individuals experienced more than one episode of malaria annually. Moreover, the Marari community is highly vulnerable to the risk of introduced malaria cases, particularly from the nearby Nova Jerusalem area, a critical malaria hotspot in the Padauiri region. Located just outside the demarcated Yanomami territory, this area hosts Yanomami who frequently travel there to work in piassaba extraction.

In each village, the Yanomami live collectively in a single traditional communal dwelling known as shabono. This traditional circular structure features a thatch-roof and lacks internal walls, with families arranging their hammocks and belongings within the shared space. The shabono serves as the central gathering place for family’s daily activities, including cooking, sleeping and social interaction. The considerable number of inhabitants per shabono is a factor that enhances the attraction of anopheline vectors, as human aggregation can increase host availability and influence mosquito behaviour [19].

For the purposes of this study, Castanha and Ahima were considered a single village due to their proximity (approximately 300 m apart). Similarly, Taibrapa was analyzed as two distinct yet interrelated settlements: Taibrapa 1 (primary site) and Taibrapa 2 (a secondary seasonal location to which the same indigenous group migrates in response to ecological cycles). The villages of Castanha, Ahima, Gasolina, Taibrapa 1 and Alapusi are located within walking distance of the main Marari healthcare post by foot, with travel time ranging from a few minutes to two hours. In contrast, Taibrapa 2 is more geographically isolated and can only be reached by a four to six-hour boat journey along the Marari River from the village Gasolina. Access to the region by healthcare teams is achieved by small aircraft, and each village has a basic healthcare facility that provides accommodation and logistical support during health missions.

Adult anopheline collections

In 2013, collections took place in March (end of the dry season) and August (end of the rainy season), using uninterrupted 12-h samplings (18:00 to 06:00 h) with human attraction using MosqTent traps (human-protected baited traps) [20] in the peridomicile (within 10 m of the outer wall of the shabono) and extradomicile (at the main river area used by the Yanomami for bathing and fetching water). Subsequently, from September 2014 (beginning of the dry season) to March 2015, uninterrupted 12-h collections were performed every 2 months, covering the entire 2014–2015 dry season. During the 2014–2015 period, in addition to the 12-h captures, supplementary 4-h collections with MosqTent were simultaneously carried out at three specific sites: intradomicile (inside the shabono), peridomicile and extradomicile. The 4-h peri- and extradomiciliary collections were conducted from 18:00 to 22:00 h, while indoor collections were carried out from 20:00 to 00:00 h. Samplings were conducted over four consecutive days in Castanha/Ahima, Taibrapa 1 and Gasolina, and over three days in Alapusi. Following the 4-h MosqTent sampling in the intradomicile, a manual search for mosquitoes resting inside the shabonos was carried out using a Nasci Aspirator, a vacuum cleaner powered by a 12 V 12 A battery, specifically designed for the safe and efficient aspiration of mosquitos resting on surfaces within the shabono [21]. Aspirations lasted 30 min per session and targeted the main walls of the shabono up to 2.5 m high as well as objects hanging on the walls, such as baskets, clothes, bows and arrows. To complement aspiration and MosqTent collections, six CDC light traps were operated from 18:00 to 06:00 h as follow: one inside the shabono, one in the peridomicile and four in the extradomicile, with each trap placed every 50 m towards the nearest previously mapped anopheline larval habitat [22, 23]. A Supplementary Table has been provided, summarizing the collection methods and sampling effort across study villages and timeframes. Anophelines occasionally attempting to bite the team in proximity to the principal larval habitats during daytime were also captured. In all field activities involving the MosqTent, trained members of the entomology team served as attractants remaining fully protected inside the trap throughout the collection period, in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol [20].

Specimen identification was performed based on external morphology, using dichotomous keys [18, 24]. In addition, the cytochrome oxidase (COI) gene of a sample of collected anophelines was analysed as previously described [25].

Molecular detection of malaria parasites in Anophelines

All 1962 anophelines collected were individually tested for infectivity using PCR. DNA was extracted from the head and thorax of all female anopheline using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA). To eliminate potential protein inhibitors, DNA was further purified using the StrataPrep PCR purification kit (Agilent Technologies). After purification, DNA samples were concentrated from 50 to 10 μL using a vacuum concentrator (Eppendorf, Vacufuge model). The DNA of each anophelines was tested for Plasmodium DNA using a PET-PCR protocol (photo-induced electron transfer PCR), which enhances sensitivity and specificity for detecting parasite genome fragments [26]. The DNA extractions and PCRs were carried out by two team members during three-months, without the use of a robotic arm. PCR trays were prepared with 92 samples each, besides one negative and three positive controls. Reactions were performed in a final volume of 20 μL, containing 2XTaqMan Environmental Master Mix 2.0 (Applied BioSystems), 0.5 μL (10 μM) of each forward and reverse primers, and 2 μL of DNA template. Amplifications were carried out on an Agilent MX3005P QPCR System (Agilent technologies, Santa Clara, USA) under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial hot-start at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 40 s. Any sample with a CT value ≤40.5 was considered positive. Samples with CT values between 40.5 and 45, as well as positive samples, were subjected to a confirmatory Nested-PCR to identify the Plasmodium genus and species [27]. Positive controls for Plasmodium falciparum (strain 3D7), Plasmodium vivax (strain Thailand 3) and Plasmodium malariae (strain China 1) were included.

Entomological indices and data analysis

Mosquito collection data were entered and cross-validated using a database created in Epi Info version 3.5.4 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA, USA). The nightly Human Biting Rate (nHBR) was estimated using only data from the MosqTent collections and stratified by species and village. The nHBR was calculated as the geometric mean of the total number of Anopheles mosquitoes captured, divided by the total number of collection nights (each consisting of 12-h sampling periods) in the peri- and extradomiciliary environments [28]. The overall infection rate (IR) was defined as the percentage of mosquitoes testing positive for Plasmodium among all Anopheles analysed and was also stratified by species and village. The annual entomological inoculation rate (EIR), defined as the estimated number of infectious mosquitoes bites per person per year, was calculated using the following formula [29]: (nHBR × IR × 365)/100. Transmission metrics were derived from 12-h captures conducted with MosqTent in both the peridomicile and extradomicile. These indices provide a quantitative measure of the malaria transmission potential in each ecological setting evaluated in the study.

The seasonal dynamics of the two most abundant Anopheles species in each of the Marari villages was assessed using only data from the 12-h peridomiciliary collections, as this was the only collection method consistently employed across all surveys and villages.

Results

Species distribution of adult anopheline

A total of 1962 adult anophelines belonging to seven species were collected across the surveyed localities (Table 1). Anopheles darlingi was the predominant species in Alapusi (87.9%), Gasolina (81.9%) and Taibrapa 1 (52.5%), with Alapusi having the lowest diversity of Anopheles (H′ = 0.589). In contrast, Taibrapa 2 exhibited the highest diversity (H′ = 1.138), with An. nuneztovari s.l. being overwhelmingly dominant in accounting for 80.0% of the specimens collected there. In Castanha/Ahima An. oswaldoi s.l. (50.0%) was the most abundant species, followed by An. darlingi (35.7%). Anopheles darlingi and An. oswaldoi s.l. were the only species detected in all five surveyed localities, while An. medialis was recorded at all sites except in in Taibrapa 2. Conversely, An. triannulatus s.l. and Anopheles mattogrossensis were exclusively found in Taibrapa 2, while Anopheles costai-like was found only in Gasolina (Table 1). Species-specific behaviour of Anophelines in the Yanomami Territory

Anopheline distribution varied across the study villages in the Yanomami Territory, with notable differences in spatial and species-specific behaviours (Table 2). In Alapusi, nearly all human-seeking anophelines were captured in the peridomicile. Similarly, the peridomicile accounted for 50 and 58% of anophelines captures in Castanha/Ahima and Gasolina, respectively. A striking pattern of exophagic behaviour was observed at Taibrapa 2, where only two anophelines were collected using Human-Protected Baited Traps inside the shabono, compared to 1218 predominantly An. nuneztovari s.l. captured at the extradomicile site located approximately 400 m away from the shabono (Fig. 2). This location is regularly used by the community for bathing, including during twilight hours. In this village, the contrast between extra- and peridomiciliary collections was notable with only 17 anophelines captured immediately outside the shabono. Conversely, An. darlingi generally exhibited a preference for the peridomicile across villages, actively seeking human host near the shabonos. However, in the Taibrapa 1, An. darlingi was more abundant in the extradomicile. Among the An. darlingi females collected resting indoors, 57.1% were blood-fed while 42.9% were unfed females, indicating that this species may rest inside Yanomami dwellings both before and after blood feeding. Additionally, four An. oswaldoi s.l. (two in Alapusi and two in Taibrapa 1) and one An. nuneztovari s.l. (in Gasolina) were found biting during the day, respectively between 14:00 and 17:00 h and nearly midday. Nocturnal biting activity and Plasmodium infection in Anopheles species from Marari villages

The evaluation of hourly biting activity from sunset to sunrise of the most abundant Anopheles species in different Marari villages revealed distinct nocturnal hematophagy patterns (Fig. 3). This parameter was analyzed separately for peri- and extradomiciliary environments. Dynamics and epidemiological indices of malaria transmission in remote indigenous settings

The assessment of Anopheles biting and infection rates in the Marari community revealed significant spatial and species-specific variations in malaria transmission potential. Anopheles darlingi exhibited consistently high infection rate (IR) across the sampled villages and with the highest IR (~2.1%) recorded in Gasolina and Taibrapa 1, followed by Alapusi (1.63%). In contrast, An. nuneztovari s.l., although the dominant species in Taibrapa 2, presented a low IR of less than 0.1%.

Results from the peri- and extradomiciliary environments were combined to calculate the nHBR and the EIR for each village (Table 3). Anopheles darlingi was the dominant and primary vector in most villages in both peri- and extradomicile, with the notable exception of Taibrapa 2, where An. nuneztovari s.l. exhibited the highest nHBR in the extradomicile. At this site, it was estimated that a person could receive up to 359 bites per night from An. nuneztovari s.l. in the extradomicile (mostly concentrated in the two first hours of the night), in stark contrast to just two bites per person per night in the peridomicile. This striking difference may reflect ecological heterogeneity, as the collection points were approximately 400 m apart, highlighting the influence of microenvironmental factors on vector abundance. Estimates of the entomological inoculation rate (EIR) highlight the pivotal role of An. darlingi in sustaining malaria transmission in the region. High transmission risk was observed in the villages of Gasolina (EIR = 79.3), Alapusi (EIR = 60.1), and Taibrapa 1 (EIR = 49.3). For instance, in Gasolina, an individual could receive one infective bite every 4.6 days in outdoor settings. In contrast, the annual outdoor EIR for An. nuneztovari s.l. in Taibrapa 2 could not be calculated due to limited data—only two surveys were conducted, with a single all-night collection in the extradomiciliary area in September 2014. Furthermore, the Plasmodium species infecting one An. nuneztovari s.l. specimen could not be identified.

Discussion

Malaria transmission within the Yanomami indigenous communities of the Amazon is influenced by a complex interplay of ecological, entomological, and sociocultural factors. This study highlights significant spatial variation in anopheline species composition across the Marari region, emphasizing the necessity for localized vector surveillance and tailored control strategies. Understanding any malaria scenario requires assessing temporal and spatial overlap between human activity and vector behaviour [30, 31].

In the Marari community, all villages were found to be highly receptive to malaria transmission due to the widespread presence of An. darlingi. Although most specimens were collected in the peri- and extradomicile environments, An. darlingi was also observed biting and resting indoors in all localities. This highlights its behavioural plasticity and potential for indoor malaria transmission. [29, 32, 33]. Recognized as the primary malaria vector in the Amazon basin [14, 32–34], An. darlingi was the most abundant anopheline species in three of the five main villages—Alapusi, Gasolina, and Taibrapa 1. Its broad ecological distribution highlights not only its adaptability but also its pivotal role in sustaining malaria transmission in the region. Anopheles darlingi exhibits high behavioural plasticity, with its feeding, resting and biting time behaviours influenced by ecological factors, human population density, land-use practices, and vector control interventions [35–39]. In Peru, the introduction of impregnated long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) was initially associated with a shift toward exophagic behaviour. However, this trend reversed over time, with a resurgence of endophagic behaviour as the insecticidal efficacy of the nets waned due to aging [37]. Similar behavioural adaptation in An. darlingi have also been reported following the implementation of IRS [40]. In some riverine communities increased human activity outdoors during the early evening hours may favour more exophagic and earlier biting behaviour in An. darlingi. For example, in the criollo village of Jabillal along the lower Caura River in Venezuela, peak biting activity was observed at sunset, with high levels sustained until 22:00 h. This temporal overlap between human and vector activity was associated with elevated malaria transmission risk between 18:00 and 22:00 h, particularly in outdoor settings where both human and vector activity overlapped [38]. A similar situation was reported in the Beni Province of the Bolivian Amazon, where 83% of all outdoor biting by An. darlingi occurred by 22:00 h, coinciding with the time when most residents retire for the night [39]. Unlike many other riverine Amazonian populations, most of the Yanomami population typically remains indoors soon after dark and stays inside until after 06:00 h.

Striking differences in malaria receptivity were observed between villages. In the more isolated and seasonally occupied village of Taibrapa 2, An. nuneztovari s.l. was the predominant species. Mosquito captures occurred predominantly in extradomicile settings, particularly at a heavily frequented bathing site located approximately 400 m from the shabono. Although An. darlingi was also present in Taibrapa 2, it was detected in low densities, and none of the specimens tested positive for Plasmodium sporozoites. Nonetheless, the exophagic behaviour of An. nuneztovari s.l. and its occurrence in areas of daily use by residents suggest it may contribute to outdoor malaria transmission, potentially complementing the primary role of An. darlingi in sustaining disease transmission in this village. While An. nuneztovari s.l., including An. nuneztovari A and Anopheles goeldii, has been found naturally infected with Plasmodium, and is recognized as a significant malaria vector in Colombia and Venezuela [25, 41–44], its role in Brazil has perhaps been underestimated due to its predominantly zoophagic behaviour [24, 45].

In the Amazon Region, the biting behaviour of An. darlingi can be crepuscular, nocturnal or variants of the two with the pattern varying depending on the geographical location of the collection [31, 34, 46]. In the Marari villages, An. darlingi exhibits a predominantly nocturnal biting pattern with peak activity around midnight and most infected mosquitoes captured after 21:00 h. This nocturnal biting profile aligns with observations from the Yanomami village of Ocamo in Venezuela, where indoor biting rates and EIRs were similarly high [47]. In these communities, where residents remain indoors overnight indicating a high potential for indoor transmission and supporting the effectiveness of interventions like ITHNs [48]. In contrast, in others indigenous territories in Brazil, such as the Xavante and Kayapó-Mentuktíre communities [49, 50], An. darlingi shows a crepuscular biting peak shortly after sunset, followed by a brief pre-dawn resurgence with most mosquitoes collected in outdoor areas near riverbanks where communal bathing is common. The reduced indoor transmission in these communities is likely due to well-constructed "mosquito-proof" dwellings. Most Anopheles mosquitoes found in the Marari villages, including An. darlingi, were captured outside indigenous dwellings. Of the eight An. darlingi specimens infected with Plasmodium in Marari, seven were collected in peridomiciliary or extradomiciliary areas, while only one was found inside indigenous shabono. Although An. darlingi densities were generally low early in the evening, one P. vivax-infected mosquito was captured outdoors between 18:00 and 19:00 h, a time when many community members remain active outside—bathing or returning from hunting or fishing. This isolated significant finding demonstrate that transmission can occur in the early evening and in outdoor settings, highlighting a vulnerability not easily addressed by indoor-focused interventions. By contrast, during the peak biting activity of An. darlingi around midnight in peridomiciliary areas, most Yanomami residents are asleep indoors increasing their vulnerability to indoor transmission. This risk is compounded by the species’ pronounced anthropophilic and endophagic behaviour, as well as its late-night activity.

Supporting this observation, one An. darlingi specimen infected with P. falciparum sporozoites was collected indoors in the village of Gasolina, between 22:00 and 23:00 h. A comparable scenario has been reported in Maroon villages of the Suriname Amazon rainforest, where An. darlingi exhibited a monomodal peak in biting activity near midnight. As residents in these communities typically remain indoors by 21:00, it was inferred that most of mosquito bites likely occurred indoors [51].

Routine microscopy-based surveillance conducted in the Marari region between 2009 and 2014 revealed that most malaria cases were concentrated in children under 10 years of age, accounting for 70.4, 69.4, 75.6, and 74.2% in the villages of Alapusi, Castanha, Gasolina, and Taibrapa 1, respectively. This disproportionate burden among young children strongly suggests that malaria transmission occurs predominantly indoors [52]. On the other hand, in settings where malaria incidence is higher among older age groups, outdoor transmission may be more relevant. For example, in the villages of Candeias do Jamary in Rondonia, girls aged 11 to 15 were identified as the highest-risk female group due to their participation in outdoor activities—such as washing clothes in rivers—that increase exposure to infected anophelines, while older women who typically remained indoors experienced lower infection rates [53].

The detection of P. falciparum in An. darlingi exclusively in Gasolina, and only during the late-night period (22:00–23:00 h), may point to localized transmission dynamics possibly influenced by human behaviour, environmental conditions, or vector population structure. Again, these findings reinforce the adaptability of An. darlingi to various ecological settings and highlight its behavioural plasticity in terms of biting and resting patterns [36, 54]. These differences highlight the behavioural plasticity of An. darlingi and the importance of socio-environmental factors—such as settlement patterns, daily routines, and traditional housing structure—on malaria transmission risks. Among the Yanomami, the use of semi-open communal dwellings like the shabono, often located near mosquitoes larval habitats, facilitate human-vector contact both indoors and outdoors. Once An. darlingi female enters the expansive indoor space of a shabono, the high density of unprotected individuals provides ample opportunities for blood-feeding. Consequently, mosquito collectors may capture fewer mosquitoes in this indoor environment, since the biting pressure is spread across multiple nearby hosts, potentially reducing the effectiveness of traps like the MosqTent compared to the abundance of available human targets lacking ITHNs. One factor that may partially counteract the 'biting pressure dilution effect' indoors is the documented ability of An. darlingi to take multiple blood meals [55]. This behaviour is particularly relevant in the large communal indoor environments of the circular shabonos in Marari where the high concentration of individuals significantly increases the risk of malaria transmission [56]. In contrast this biting pressure dilution effect does not apply to other vector species, such as An. oswaldoi s.l., An. nuneztovari s.l. and An. medialis, which exhibit a strongly exophagic and early biting behaviour. Notably, except for a single An. nuneztovari s.l. specimen collected indoors in Taibrapa 2, An. darlingi was the only species captured indoors across the Marari villages using all three collection methods. Meanwhile, in communities like the Xavante, where dwellings are more enclosed and better protected, vector access to human indoors is reduced, thereby shifting the risk of transmission to outdoor settings [49, 50].

In Taibrapa 2, the risk of malaria transmission is further exacerbated by a pronounced early-evening peak in An. nuneztovari s.l. activity at dusk observed along the riverbanks, which coincide with high levels of human exposure during communal bathing—a common practice among the Yanomami. This spatial and temporal overlap may increases the likelihood of outdoor transmission, and mirrors findings from other forested regions of the Amazon, where An. nuneztovari s.l. is known to exhibit strong exophagic tendencies and a preference for biting at dusk and early night [57]. Four oxbow lakes located within 1.3 km of the shabono were identified as the principal larval habitats contributing to the adult anopheline populations collected in Taibrapa 2 [22, 23]. In September 2014, oxbow lake 2 (Fig. 2) exhibited particularly high densities of An. nuneztovari s.l. larvae. This lake was notably well-exposed to sunlight and contained turbid water during the survey [22, 23].

This result is likely context-dependent and exceptional, driven by the unique ecological conditions of Taibrapa 2 specifically, its location near four large oxbow lakes at the beginning of the dry season, which appear to have supported explosive breeding of An. nuneztovari s.l.. Additionally, the Plasmodium-positive An. nuneztovari s.l. specimen in question presented as a non-identifiable Plasmodium sequence with a CT value near the detection limit, suggesting low parasite numbers not allowing a species-specific diagnosis or degraded DNA, or simply a false positive result. Thus, while the potential vectorial role of An. nuneztovari s.l. in this context cannot be dismissed, it should be interpreted with caution and warrants further investigation, including seasonal monitoring and molecular characterization. As evidenced by the malaria transmission dynamics in the Yanomami villages, the infection rate (IR), human biting rate (HBR), and entomological inoculation rate (EIR) underscore the ongoing challenge of malaria control in these regions. Across most surveyed villages, An. darlingi emerged as the dominant vector, with particularly high infection (IR) and entomological inoculation rates (EIR) recorded in Gasolina, Taibrapa 1, and Alapusi. These elevated indices confirm An. darlingi’s central role in sustaining malaria transmission in these regions, consistent with its known status as the primary vector in South America. The IR of approximately 2.1% for this species in Gasolina and Taibrapa 1, and respective outdoor EIRs as high as 79.3 and 49.3 suggest intense transmission pressure, warranting strengthened vector control strategies in these critical hotspots.

Alapusi, with IR for An. darlingi of 1.6 and outdoors EIR of 60.1, also highlights the importance of targeting both indoor and outdoor transmission. Interestingly, in Taibrapa 2, An. nuneztovari s.l. emerged as the most abundant species in the extradomicile, with an exceptionally high human biting rate (359 bites/person/nigh), necessitating tailored outdoor control measures such as environmental management.

Despite its high abundance, An. nuneztovari s.l. showed a low Plasmodium infection rate (0.09%), suggesting limited vector capacity, though the potential detection of a Plasmodium-positive specimen supports further research into this Anopheles species potential role in malaria transmission. These findings along with the heterogeneous transmission patterns observed across surveyed villages, suggest the need for adaptive and species-specific vector control strategies.

The seasonal dynamics of An. darlingi in the Marari villages followed a pattern typical of low-order river systems (1th to 5th order), characterized by frequent short-duration and irregular flood pulses, primarily driven by local rainfall in the surrounding mountainous regions, occurring more frequently during the wet season [58].

These hydrological events modulate An. darlingi larval habitats availability, particularly in floodplain-associated environments, such as oxbow lakes. Initially, flood pulses often exert a flushing effect on An. darlingi larvae but as waters recede and stabilize, these habitats become more favourable for larval development until the occurrence of a subsequent flood pulse or a pronounced dry phase. During the rainy season, these productive periods are shortened due to increased flood frequency. In contrast, An. darlingi larval habitats located in areas not directly influenced by riverine dynamics—such as sunlit, rain-fed depressions in agricultural fields—display more predictable seasonal availability, contributing to mosquito production during and shortly after the rainy season. Thus, in low-order systems, the erratic dry-season flood pulses may lead to subsequent and more predictable increases in An. darlingi densities, potentially contributing to malaria transmission peak.

Throughout the study period, An. darlingi densities in the Marari villages showed irregular seasonal peaks (Fig. 4). Conversely, in the Yanomami village of Ocamo, situated near the confluence of the Ocamo and Orinoco river, a high-order river (6th to 12th order), a monomodal and predictable flood pulse was observed. In this setting, An. darlingi displayed a distinct monomodal annual seasonal pattern, with peak densities occurring in September, approximately one month after the river’s highest water level. This timing coincided with the formation of extensive floodplain lakes—generated by the receding monomodal flood pulse of the Orinoco River [47].

This study has some limitations, notably the under sampling of intradomiciliary environments as no all-night mosquito captures were performed inside the shabonos. This limited the ability to fully characterize the indoor biting behaviour of An. darlingi, a critical gap given the importance of indoor transmission in the Yanomami context. Nevertheless, the focus on outdoors collection allowed the detection of a broader diversity of exophagic anopheline species, including potential secondary vectors, which might have been missed with indoor-only sampling. The MosqTent is more practical to install outdoors, and this also influenced the choice of the collection points. Additionally, while the total number of outdoor all-night captures was lower than in some comparable studies, the multi-year sampling (2013–2015) and inclusion of different seasonal periods provided a robust dataset for estimating local EIRs.

Brazil’s strategic malaria elimination plans aims to eliminate P. falciparum by 2030 and the remaining malaria burden by 2035 [1]. To achieve these goals will require focused efforts in indigenous territories, which remain key transmission hotspots. The Yanomami territory, in particular, has the highest malaria burden among all indigenous lands in Brazil, highlighting the critical need for targeted interventions in these areas. The study provides important guidance for malaria vector control in Yanomami territories. Intradomiciliary collections using the manual Nasci aspirator confirmed the partially endophilic behaviour of An. darlingi. This observation supports the use of IRS at four-month intervals, targeting mosquitoes that rests indoors during blood-feeding [52, 59]. Such targeted interventions could significantly reduce transmission by addressing the behaviour of the dominant vector in high-burden areas. The study found that An. darlingi bites indoors in Marari, predominantly during hours when most Yanomami residents were asleep, underpinning the use of ITHNs as a highly effective intervention. Supporting evidence comes from Magris et al. [48] whose study in Venezuelan Yanomami villages showed that lambdacyhalothrin-treated hammock nets reduced malaria incidence by 56% compared to placebo-treated nets. Moreover, their protective efficacy would likely be even greater compared to no net use.

Finally, a third recommended malaria vector control for Marari: environmental management to reduce An. darlingi population[60]. This involves targeting larval habitats such as local oxbow lakes and sun-exposed segments of forest streams caused by micro-deforestation to limit vector breeding sites. This strategy complements indoor measures like IRS and ITHNs by also reducing transmission in peri- and extradomiciliary areas.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex and heterogeneous ecology of Anopheles vectors in Yanomami territories, with species-specific behaviours and varying risks of malaria transmission across villages. Anopheles darlingi remains the primary vector in most villages, demonstrating high infection rates and the ability of transmitting malaria both indoors and outside the shabonos. Its monomodal biting activity around midnight and frequency indoors combined with the fact that the great majority of the Yanomami remains inside the shabonos for most of the night suggest that intradomiciliary transmission may be very important in Marari villages. In contrast, An. nuneztovari s.l. exhibited pronounced exophagy in Taibrapa 2, which may contribute to a localized but intense transmission risk, if found to be capable of malaria transmission. These findings emphasize the importance of tailored vector surveillance and control strategies that account for spatial and behavioural differences among vector species. Integrating community practices and environmental characteristics into control programmes is crucial for effective and sustainable malaria prevention in indigenous territories.
