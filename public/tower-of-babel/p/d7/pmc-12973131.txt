Evolution of cortical cerebral microinfarcts on 3T MRI: risk factors and clinical relevance
Authors: Jiannan Huang, Christopher Chen, Wei Ying Tan, Jiangbo Cui, Eddie Jun Yi Chong, Narayanaswamy Venketasubramanian, Saima Hilal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCortical cerebral microinfarcts (CMIs) are associated with cognitive dysfunction and dementia, while their evolution on sequential magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains unclear.METHODSThe study enrolled 490 patients (72.5 ± 7.9 years) from a memory‐clinic cohort, with 5‐year follow‐up. Cortical CMIs were graded at baseline and year 2 to identify incident lesions and other evolutionary patterns. Cognitive function was assessed annually. Clinical events, including dementia, stroke, and mortality, were recorded with time to event.RESULTSForty‐one (8.4%) patients showed incident cortical CMIs at year 2. BACKGROUND

Cortical cerebral microinfarcts (CMIs) are small ischemic lesions (<4 mm in size) restricted to the cerebral cortex. 1 They are considered a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) marker of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) 2 and are associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including cognitive dysfunction, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 stroke, 7 and mortality. 7

Cortical CMIs can be detected at different stages, depending on the MRI sequence used. 1 Acute cortical CMIs are typically identified on diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) as small DWI‐positive lesions (<5 mm) in the cortex, with signals typically fading in 2 weeks. 1 , 2 , 8 These acute DWI‐positive lesions may disappear or evolve into chronic cortical CMIs on subsequent structural MRI (e.g., T1‐weighted images), 9 , 10 , 11 suggesting that they may follow diverse evolutionary patterns. A previous study reported that 5.9% of DWI‐positive lesions evolved into chronic cortical CMIs during a mean follow‐up of 4.7 years. 11 However, these DWI‐based studies generally relied on a single follow‐up structural MRI and therefore provided limited insight into the longer‐term evolution of cortical CMIs.

In contrast, structural MRI allows detection and longitudinal tracking of cortical CMIs beyond the acute phase 8 and is more suitable for studying their evolution over extended periods. To date, only one study has examined the evolution of cortical CMIs using repeated structural MRI, reporting an annual incidence of 42.9% in patients with cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and 12.5% in patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD). 12 However, the small sample size limited exploration of risk factors and MRI correlates of incident cortical CMIs. Further studies are warranted to illustrate the dynamic nature of cortical CMIs on sequential structural MRI. Moreover, no studies investigated the association of incident cortical CMIs with cognitive decline or incident clinical events. Understanding their clinical significance beyond prevalent lesions may extend our knowledge of the potential value of modifying cortical CMI evolution in the prevention and treatment of cognitive impairment and dementia.

This study aimed to (1) characterize the epidemiological features of cortical CMI evolution on structural MRI in terms of incidence, risk factors, and MRI correlates and (2) investigate the associations of incident cortical CMIs with cognitive decline and the risk of dementia, stroke, and mortality. We hypothesize that incident cortical CMIs can be detected on sequential MRI and are related to pre‐existing vascular risk factors, such as hypertension, and MRI markers of cerebrovascular disease (CeVD). Furthermore, incident cortical CMIs may be associated with cognitive decline, CeVD progression, and other clinical events, independent of prevalent lesions.

METHODS

Study population

This study was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain‐Specific Review Board of Singapore and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

This study included patients from the Harmonization study, an ongoing prospective study conducted at the memory clinics of National University Hospital and St. Luke's Hospital in Singapore. The patient recruitment procedure of the Harmonization study has been documented elsewhere. 5 , 6 Briefly, from August 2010 to February 2020, 700 patients were enrolled in the Harmonization study (Figure S1). Patients were followed for 5 years, with annual clinical and neuropsychological assessments and biennial brain MRI scans. Of the 700 patients, 686 completed a baseline MRI (14 refused or were not suitable for MRI), and 496 had a follow‐up MRI at year 2 (122 were lost to follow‐up at year 2; 68 refused or were not suitable for MRI). After excluding an additional six patients who completed neuropsychological assessments at only one time point, 490 patients with MRI data at both baseline and year 2 follow‐up and with neuropsychological assessments at two or more time points were included in this study.

Neuroimaging

To avoid inter‐scanner variability, all brain MRI scans (baseline and follow‐up) were performed using the same whole‐body 3T Siemens Magnetom Trio Tim Scanner system with a 32‐channel head coil at the Clinical Imaging Research Centre, National University of Singapore. The protocol comprised T1‐ and T2‐weighted images, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI), and time‐of‐flight magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). Detailed sequence parameters are provided in Table S1.

Cortical CMIs

Cortical CMIs were identified as perpendicular lesions in the cortex, <4 mm in diameter, hypointense on T1‐weighted images, hyperintense on T2, and hyperintense or isointense (with the surrounding tissue) on FLAIR. 1 A subset of lesions showed typical appearance on T1‐weighted images but were undetectable on both T2‐weighted and FLAIR sequences, likely due to the relatively large slice thickness (3 mm) of T2 and FLAIR. These lesions were retained as possible cortical CMIs in the primary analysis, consistent with our previous studies using the same study sample. 5 , 6 , 13 , 14 Two raters (J.H., S.H.) independently graded cortical CMIs, and all identified lesions were discussed in weekly consensus meetings. A random subset of 100 MRI scans was graded by both raters, showing good to excellent inter‐rater reliability (Cohen's kappa = 0.88).

All CeVD markers were treated as binary variables (absent vs present). Count‐based, volumetric, or location‐specific measures were not considered in the current analysis.

Evolution of CeVD markers

Cortical CMI evolution was determined using strict lesion‐by‐lesion matching between baseline and year 2 MRI scans. Specifically, each pair of lesions was visually matched based on anatomical location and verified across three planes (sagittal, axial, and coronal) and four MRI sequences (T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, FLAIR, and SWI). If a cortical CMI was identified at year 2 and no corresponding signal was observed at baseline across all planes and sequences, the lesion was classified as an incident cortical CMI. Conversely, if a cortical CMI was identified at baseline with no corresponding signal at year 2, the lesion was classified as a cortical CMI becoming invisible. Cortical CMIs classified as incident or invisible at year 2 were further verified on year 4/5 MRI images when available. Other patterns of cortical CMI evolution, such as lesions incorporated into new large infarction, were also assessed.

Progression of other CeVD markers, including intracranial stenosis, cortical infarcts, lacunes, and CMBs, was defined by the appearance of new lesions on year 2 images compared to baseline. WMH progression was assessed using the modified Rotterdam Progression Scale, which visually rates the absence or presence of progression (0 or 1, respectively) across three periventricular regions (frontal caps, occipital caps, bands), four subcortical white matter regions (frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal), basal ganglia, and infratentorial region. 18 A total score ≥2 (range: 0 to 9) indicated WMH progression.

Neuropsychological assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery, 6 , 19 including six cognitive domains: (1) Attention – Digit Span Forward and Backward; (2) Executive function – Verbal Fluency and Color Trail Test A and B; (3) Memory – Rey Complex Figure Test and Hopkins Verbal Learning Test (immediate recall, delayed recall, and recognition); (4) Language – 15‐item Modified Boston Naming Test; (5) Visuomotor speed – Symbol Digit Modalities Test; and (6) Visuospatial function – Rey Complex Figure Test (copy).

Global and domain‐specific cognitive z‐scores were calculated using a previously established procedure. 6 , 19 Briefly, raw scores from individual tests were normalized to the means and standard deviations (SDs) of the reference group, that is, baseline scores of patients without cognitive impairment. Normalized test scores were then averaged within each cognitive domain and standardized to the composite means and SDs of the reference group to calculate six domain‐specific cognitive z‐scores. These domain‐specific z‐scores were averaged and standardized again to generate global cognitive z‐scores. MMSE and MoCA raw scores were also normalized to the means and SDs of the reference group to derive corresponding z‐scores.

Clinical diagnosis of cognitive impairment

Based on clinical and neuropsychological assessments, patients were diagnosed with (1) no cognitive impairment (NCI), if they had no impaired domains in neuropsychological assessments and no functional loss in daily life; (2) cognitive impairment no dementia (CIND), if they had at least one impaired domain but no functional loss; (3) dementia, if they met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM‐IV) criteria 20 for dementia.

Assessment of clinical events

Incident dementia was defined as conversion from NCI or CIND to dementia, according to the aforementioned diagnostic criteria. Patients with dementia at baseline or year 1 follow‐up were excluded (n = 172), leaving 318 patients in the analysis of incident dementia. Stroke was defined based on World Health Organization criteria 21 and confirmed by neuroimaging when required and available. Incident stroke included both first‐ever and recurrent events. Vital status was determined through medical records and hospital databases and was defined as death from any cause. A composite outcome, defined as the first occurrence of any of these clinical events, was also assessed. For each patient, the date of event occurrence, lost to follow‐up, or study completion was recorded, whichever occurred first.

Covariates

A comprehensive questionnaire was administered during the first interview to document patient demographics, including age, sex, height, weight, and years of education. Additionally, patients reported their histories of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, stroke, and cardiac diseases (ischemic heart disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation), which was verified through medical records and medication use when available. Smoking status was also self‐reported and categorized as non‐smoker or current/ever smoker. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight/height2 (kg/m2).

Of the 490 patients in the final sample, six had missing height and/or weight (two males) required for calculating BMI. As height and weight were approximately normally distributed, we imputed missing values using sex‐specific means and then calculated BMI. In addition, two patients had missing information on hypertension history. These values were determined based on reported use of antihypertensive medication.

Statistical analysis

Patients’ baseline characteristics were summarized as mean (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, median (IQR) for non‐normally distributed continuous variables, and count (percentage) for categorical variables. Two‐sample t‐test, Mann–Whitney U test, and Fisher's exact test were used to compare baseline characteristics between groups where appropriate.

Univariable logistic regression was employed to assess the associations between potential risk factors and the odds of incident cortical CMIs. Multivariable logistic regression was then performed, with stepwise adjustment for potential confounders.

Linear mixed‐effects models (nlme package in R) were used to examine the longitudinal associations between incident cortical CMIs and cognitive function across six time points (baseline to year 5), accounting for repeated measurements. A random intercept or both a random intercept and slope were fitted in the models, based on likelihood ratio tests comparing nested models. Interaction terms between incident cortical CMIs and time were included to test for time‐dependent effects. Effect estimates were derived at each time point. Estimated marginal means of cognitive scores were derived to visualize cognitive trajectories (emmeans package in R). To account for potential confounders, Model 1 was adjusted for demographics (age, sex, and education); Model 2 additionally adjusted for vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, and stroke history); Model 3 further adjusted for baseline cortical CMIs and significant CeVD. Significant CeVD was defined as the presence of at least one intracranial stenosis, at least one cortical infarct, at least one CMB, or extensive WMHs at baseline, following a previously reported approach. 19 , 22 These markers were included as they showed independent associations with incident cortical CMIs in this study.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between incident cortical CMIs and progression of other CeVD markers. Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for the associations of incident cortical CMIs with incident dementia, stroke, mortality, and the composite outcome. Confounder adjustment followed the same strategy as previously described. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to visualize survival probabilities for clinical events in patients with and without incident cortical CMIs. Group differences were assessed using log‐rank tests.

To assess the effects of incident cortical CMIs beyond prevalent lesions, their associations with cognitive decline and clinical events were re‐examined in the subgroup of patients with baseline cortical CMIs (n = 108) using the statistical methods described earlier.

To examine whether baseline dementia status modified the association between incident cortical CMIs and cognitive decline, subgroup analyses were performed. Patients were stratified into non‐demented (n = 318) and demented (n = 172) groups at baseline.

To further address potential residual confounding due to baseline imbalance, sensitivity analyses were performed using inverse‐probability‐weighted regression following well‐established procedures. 23 , 24 Briefly, propensity scores for incident cortical CMIs were estimated using logistic regression including hypertension, diabetes, stroke history, baseline cortical CMIs, significant CeVD, and baseline cognitive diagnosis. Stabilized weights were calculated and truncated at the first and 99th percentiles. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences and propensity score overlap plots (Cobalt package in R). The results showed substantial improvement in baseline balance after weighting (Figures S2 and S3). Stabilized weights were then applied to linear mixed‐effects models and Cox proportional hazards models to re‐examine the associations of incident cortical CMIs with cognitive decline and clinical events, adjusting for age, sex, and education.

Furthermore, additional sensitivity analyses were performed to minimize potential lesion misclassification due to possible cortical CMIs (a subset of lesions undetectable on T2 and FLAIR). As cortical CMIs were treated as binary variables (absent vs present), we excluded patients with only possible cortical CMIs identified (n = 14) and re‐examined the associations of incident cortical CMIs with cognitive function and clinical events.

Model assumptions for linear mixed‐effects models (linearity, normality, and homoscedasticity) and Cox proportional hazards models (proportional hazards assumption) were tested, with no violations observed. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). All VIF values across regression models were below 2, indicating no meaningful collinearity among covariates (Table S2).

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.2.1 (https://www.r‐project.org/), and a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To account for multiple comparisons across six cognitive domains, Bonferroni correction was applied, with significance threshold set at p value < 0.008 (0.05/6).

RESULTS

General findings

Among 490 patients with baseline and year 2 MRI images, incident cortical CMIs were identified in 41 (8.4%) patients, with a total of 68 new lesions detected (Figure 1). Of these patients, 31 had a single incident cortical CMI, while 10 had multiple incident cortical CMIs (Figure S4).

Evolution of cortical cerebral microinfarcts. FLAIR, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery; SWI, susceptibility‐weighted imaging.

A total of 108 patients had cortical CMIs (358 in total) at baseline. Of these, 12 (11.1%) had cortical CMIs (21 in total) that became invisible at year 2 (Figure 1), six (5.6%) had cortical CMIs (11 in total) that were incorporated into new large infarctions, and two showed both invisible and incorporated cortical CMIs. Among the 10 patients with only invisible cortical CMIs, one had no detectable cortical CMIs at year 2 (Figure S4), whereas nine continued to have cortical CMIs (median: 2, range: 1 to 6).

Baseline characteristics

Patients with incident cortical CMIs had a higher prevalence of ever/current smokers, hypertension, and stroke history and were more likely to have intracranial stenosis, large cortical infarcts, lacunes, extensive WMHs, and CMBs at baseline, compared to those without (Table 1). Additionally, patients with incident cortical CMIs were more often diagnosed with dementia at baseline and were more likely to have baseline cortical CMIs, particularly multiple lesions.

Risk factors for incident cortical CMIs

In univariable regression analysis, ever/current smoking, hypertension, diabetes, stroke history, baseline cortical CMIs, other CeVD markers, and cognitive impairment were associated with increased odds of incident cortical CMIs (Table 2). After adjusting for confounders, hypertension, diabetes, stroke history, intracranial stenosis, large cortical infarcts, extensive WMHs, CMBs, and baseline cortical CMIs remained associated with incident cortical CMIs. Large cortical infarcts (OR = 5.61 [2.40, 13.44], p < 0.001) and baseline cortical CMIs (OR = 4.31 [2.09, 9.03], p < 0.001) showed the strongest associations.

Incident cortical CMIs and cognitive function

Compared to patients without incident cortical CMIs, those with incident cortical CMIs exhibited significantly lower scores in MMSE, MoCA, and global cognition, particularly at later time points (e.g., years 2 to 5), after adjusting for demographics, vascular risk factors, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers (Figure 2A, Table S3). P interactions with time were also significant, indicating accelerated cognitive decline over time.

Additionally, incident cortical CMIs were associated with lower scores in specific cognitive domains, particularly executive function, language, and visuospatial function, after adjusting for confounders and applying Bonferroni correction (Figure 2B, Table S4). Significant p interactions with time suggested accelerated decline across all cognitive domains except attention.

Cognitive trajectories indicated that patients with incident cortical CMIs showed more accelerated cognitive decline over time than those without (Figure 3). The differences were statistically significant in global cognition (p = 0.021), memory (p = 0.002), and language (p = 0.009).

Among patients with baseline cortical CMIs (n = 108), incident cortical CMIs were significantly associated with lower scores in MMSE, MoCA, and global cognition, after adjusting for confounders. Incident cortical CMIs were also associated with lower scores in specific cognitive domains, particularly executive function and language (Figure S5). Additionally, incident cortical CMIs were associated with more accelerated cognitive decline, especially in MoCA, global cognition, and language (Figure S6).

Incident cortical CMIs and CeVD progression

Incident cortical CMIs were associated with the progression of all five CeVD markers after adjusting for demographics (Table S5). The association with WMH progression remained borderline significant after further adjustment for vascular risk factors, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers (OR = 2.10 [1.03, 4.50], p = 0.047).

Incident cortical CMIs and clinical events

Over the 5‐year follow‐up, 48 (15.1%) patients developed incident dementia, 22 (4.5%) had incident stroke, and 45 (9.2%) died. Patients with clinical events had a higher prevalence of incident cortical CMIs than those without (14.6% vs 4.4% for dementia, 27.3% vs 7.5% for stroke, 13.3% vs 7.9% for mortality). Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed significant differences in survival probabilities for dementia (p = 0.002), stroke (p = 0.027), and the composite outcome (p = 0.001) between patients with and without incident cortical CMIs (Figure 4).

Survival probabilities for incident dementia (A), incident stroke (B), mortality (C), and the composite outcome (D) were visualized using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and were compared between patients with and without incident cortical cerebral microinfarcts using the log‐rank test. Incident cortical CMIs were significantly associated with increased risk of dementia, stroke, and the composite outcome after adjusting for demographics (Table 3). The associations with incident dementia (HR = 3.41 [1.49, 7.80], p = 0.004) and the composite outcome (HR = 1.99 [1.14, 3.47], p = 0.016) attenuated but remained significant after further adjustment for vascular risk factors, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers. No significant association was observed between incident cortical CMIs and mortality.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Among patients without baseline dementia, incident cortical CMIs were associated with significantly lower scores in MMSE, MoCA, and global cognition, particularly at later time points (e.g., years 2 to 5), after adjusting for demographics, vascular risk factors, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers (Figure S7A). Incident cortical CMIs were also associated with lower scores in specific cognitive domains, particularly executive function, visuomotor speed, and visuospatial function, after adjusting for confounders and applying Bonferroni correction (Figure S7B).

In contrast, among patients with baseline dementia, incident cortical CMIs were associated with lower global cognitive scores only at year 5, after adjusting for confounders in the final model (Figure S8A). No significant associations were observed with MMSE or MoCA scores. Additionally, incident cortical CMIs were not associated with any specific cognitive domains after Bonferroni correction (Figure S8B).

We further examined trajectories of global cognition, memory, and language scores, which showed significant differences between patients with and without incident cortical CMIs in the full sample. Among patients without baseline dementia, incident cortical CMIs were associated with lower global cognition and memory scores at all time points (Figure S9). However, the slopes of cognitive decline were not statistically significant. In contrast, cognitive trajectories largely overlapped in the demented subgroup.

After inverse‐probability weighting, incident cortical CMIs remained associated with lower scores in MMSE, MoCA, and global cognition (Figure S10A), as well as lower scores in specific cognitive domains, particularly executive function, language, and visuospatial function (Figure S10B). Weighted cognitive trajectories also showed more accelerated cognitive decline over time among patients with incident cortical CMIs, particularly for global cognitive function and memory and language domains (Figure S11).

Furthermore, incident cortical CMIs remained associated with incident dementia, stroke, and the composite outcome, after adjusting for demographics and applying inverse‐probability weighting (Table S7).

Exclusion of patients with only possible cortical CMIs (n = 14) did not change the associations between incident cortical CMIs and global cognitive function (Figure S12A) or specific cognitive domains (Figure S12B). Incident cortical CMIs also remained associated with incident dementia and the composite outcome, after adjusting for demographics, vascular risk factors, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers (Table S8).

DISCUSSION

In a memory‐clinic population, we found that the annual incidence of cortical CMIs on 3T structural MRI was 4.2%. Cortical CMIs could also become invisible or incorporated into new large infarction. Hypertension, diabetes, stroke history, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers were independent risk factors for incident cortical CMIs. Furthermore, incident cortical CMIs were associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including cognitive decline, WMH progression, and incident dementia.

A previous study investigating cortical CMI evolution on structural MRI reported an annual incidence of 42.9% in patients with CAA and 12.5% in those with AD, with one cortical CMI becoming invisible at follow‐up. 12 However, our study showed a lower annual incidence of 4.2% in a larger sample. CAA, characterized by amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition in vessel walls, compromises both the functional and structural integrity of cerebral small vessels, resulting in hemorrhagic and ischemic injury, including cortical CMIs. 25 In contrast, our cohort reflected a broader memory‐clinic population with a lower CSVD burden than CAA patients. The lower incidence observed in our cohort suggests that more severe CSVD pathology is associated with higher cortical CMI occurrence. This is supported by a study reporting a 13% incidence of cortical CMIs over 10 monthly DWI scans in patients with progressive CSVD. 26

Apart from new lesions, cortical CMIs became invisible in 11.1% of patients. Previous studies also reported regression or disappearance of other CSVD markers, including WMHs, 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 lacunes, 32 , 33 , 34 and CMBs. 32 , 33 , 35 , 36 While such regression is partly attributed to methodological limitations, including partial volume effects and inter‐scan variability, biological processes such as edema resolution and tissue resorption or salvage may also contribute. 33 , 37 Similarly, cortical CMIs becoming invisible may reflect salvageable tissue in the lesion locale, with tissue repair possibly contributing to lesion shrinkage or disappearance. Notably, as the relatively thick slices (4 mm) of our DWI sequences may limit the detectability of smaller lesions, we were unable to reliably determine the clinical stage of the identified cortical CMIs. Therefore, cortical CMIs becoming invisible at follow‐up may represent acute or subacute lesions that shrink and become undetectable on structural MRI but may remain present on neuropathological examination. 26 , 38 Previous studies also reported the accumulation of hemorrhagic components within microinfarcts, which may alter MRI appearance over time. 39 , 40 Given their small size, such signal changes may further limit detectability on conventional MRI. Future integrated MRI‐histopathology studies are needed to explore the mechanisms and clinical significance of cortical CMIs becoming invisible. We also found 5.6% of cortical CMIs incorporated into new large infarction at follow‐up. This aligns with previous findings that incident lacunes may occur near or within preexisting WMHs. 41 Such spatial clustering of ischemic lesions may reflect ongoing arteriolosclerosis or thromboembolic vulnerability within shared vascular territories.

We further investigated risk factors for incident cortical CMIs and found their associations with hypertension, diabetes, stroke history, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers. Our findings align with previous evidence linking vascular risk factors and CeVD markers to WMH progression 28 , 37 , 42 and incident lacunes 37 , 42 and CMBs. 35 , 36 , 37 Different types of CSVD may have shared etiologies, such as arteriolosclerosis and microembolism, and are often interrelated. 1 Our study extends current knowledge on CSVD progression, reinforcing the idea that pathophysiological changes in cerebral small vessels are dynamic and may exhibit diverse neuroimaging features. Incident cortical CMIs may be part of this dynamic process, representing broader cerebrovascular changes. This perspective helps explain the association between vascular risk factors and incident cortical CMIs, supporting the potential for modifying cortical CMI evolution through vascular risk factor management. Notably, hyperlipidemia was not associated with incident cortical CMIs, inconsistent with findings for prevalent lesions. 5 , 19 This may be due to the use of lipid‐lowering medication, which could exert long‐term protection against the development of new cortical CMIs. Furthermore, although lacunes have been linked to cortical CMIs in cross‐sectional studies, 3 , 4 , 5 we did not observe a significant association with incident cortical CMIs after adjusting for confounders. As most patients with stroke history in our cohort had lacunes, controlling for stroke history alone had already eliminated the association between lacunes and incident cortical CMIs (data not shown).

Previous studies have established associations of cortical CMIs with cognitive dysfunction and decline. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Our study extends the knowledge of their impact on cognition by demonstrating an association between incident cortical CMIs and cognitive decline over 5 years. Notably, this association remained significant after adjusting for baseline cortical CMIs and was also demonstrated in patients with baseline lesions. These findings suggest that incident cortical CMIs may be an independent risk factor for cognitive decline beyond prevalent lesions, underscoring the importance of monitoring cortical CMI evolution over time. Moreover, the association between incident cortical CMIs and cognitive decline was more prominent in non‐demented individuals, consistent with findings for other CSVD markers. 43 , 44 In patients with dementia, extensive neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular pathology may have reached a ceiling effect, thereby attenuating the incremental impact of newly developed lesions.

The mechanisms linking cortical CMIs to cognitive outcomes are multifactorial. First, most cortical CMIs at brain autopsy are below the detection limit of in vivo MRI. 45 , 46 Higher field‐strength MRI detects a higher lesion burden, 4 , 47 indicating that cortical CMIs are typically underestimated. Although small, their widespread distribution may lead to considerable neuron loss, directly affecting cognitive function. Second, cortical CMIs may disrupt adjacent white matter fibers or impair white matter connectivity beyond the lesion site, compromising cognitive network integration. 48 , 49 Third, cortical CMIs have been associated with global brain atrophy, 50 , 51 which partially mediates their relationship with cognitive dysfunction. 14 Fourth, cortical CMIs may induce persistent neuroinflammation, potentially leading to secondary neuronal injury and accelerated Aβ deposition. 52 , 53 Finally, cortical CMIs may reflect dynamic and broader vascular pathology, which collectively contribute to cognitive impairment. This is supported by their associations with other CeVD progression in our study. Future studies integrating structural MRI with diffusion and functional imaging are warranted to clarify the network‐level consequences of cortical CMIs.

Furthermore, we found that incident cortical CMIs were significantly associated with incident dementia, even after adjusting for vascular risk factors, baseline cortical CMIs, and other CeVD markers. This association also remained significant in patients with baseline cortical CMIs. In addition to the established cross‐sectional association between cortical CMIs and dementia, 3 our findings suggest that incident cortical CMIs may serve as an independent risk factor for dementia development, likely through similar mechanisms described previously. However, we did not observe a significant association between incident cortical CMIs and incident stroke or mortality in the final model, inconsistent with previous reports 7 on prevalent cortical CMIs. This discrepancy may be partly due to our smaller sample size. Our focus on incident lesions further reduced the number of events available for analysis.

Previous DWI‐based studies linked acute CMIs to cognitive decline and other outcomes. 38 , 54 Consistently, our study demonstrates the clinical relevance of incident cortical CMIs identified on structural MRI. Patients who develop such incident lesions may reflect a subgroup with more active and progressive cerebrovascular injury and, thus, at higher risk for adverse clinical outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, cortical CMIs were assessed on 3T MRI, which has lower spatial resolution than ultra‐high‐field MRI. No formal motion correction or imaging registration across time points was performed, and thus inter‐scan variability may have affected lesion detectability in some cases. Moreover, the relatively thick slices (3 mm) of T2 and FLAIR may have limited detection of smaller lesions. These methodological limitations may have led to underestimation of incident cortical CMIs and misclassification of lesions becoming invisible. To mitigate this, cortical CMIs were reviewed across multiple planes and sequences, and longitudinal matching was performed carefully on a lesion‐by‐lesion basis. Second, cortical CMI evolution was assessed only between baseline and the year 2 follow‐up, which may underestimate their full evolutionary patterns over the 5‐year study period. Due to ongoing data collection, we were unable to incorporate year 4/5 images into a comprehensive multi‐time‐point analysis. This will be addressed in our future research. Third, learning effects from repeated neuropsychological assessments may have underestimated the extent of cognitive decline. Fourth, although the annual attrition rate in our cohort was relatively low (11.7%), patients with better compliance may be healthier and differ from those lost to follow‐up in cortical CMI evolution, cognitive decline, and clinical outcomes. Fifth, as this study was conducted in memory‐clinic patients with a high prevalence of vascular risk factors and CeVD markers, caution is needed when generalizing our findings to other populations. Sixth, CeVD markers were treated as binary variables. While this approach facilitates interpretation, it may overlook additional information provided by lesion number, volume, or anatomical location. Finally, although we adjusted for a wide range of confounders, the influence of residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Given the observational nature of this study, our findings indicate associations between incident cortical CMIs and cognitive outcomes but should not be interpreted as evidence of causality. Strengths of this study include the large number of patients with repeated MRI scans, the longitudinal design with relatively long follow‐up, and the comprehensive clinical outcome assessments, allowing us to investigate the risk factors and clinical relevance of cortical CMI evolution beyond cross‐sectional associations.

CONCLUSION

Our study provides evidence that cortical CMIs may evolve dynamically, particularly in individuals with pre‐existing vascular risk factors and CeVD markers. Incident cortical CMIs may have additional clinical significance beyond prevalent lesions, reflecting ongoing brain vascular pathology and showing independent associations with future cognitive decline and dementia. Cortical CMI evolution may represent a potential MRI marker or treatment target for strategies aimed at improving cognitive outcomes. However, detection of cortical CMIs on structural MRI currently relies on manual visual assessment, which is time‐ and labor‐intensive. The development of reliable automated detection methods is required before cortical CMIs can be feasibly applied in large‐scale studies or clinical trials.
