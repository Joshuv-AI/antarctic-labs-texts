Flavin-deficient erythrocytes offer protection against malaria parasites
Authors: Ayman Hemasa, Welmoed van Loon, Jonathan Fu, Christina Spry, Julia Jäger, Donelly A. van Schalkwyk, Roberto Reverberi, Jules Ndoli, Frank P. Mockenhaupt, Carlo Contini, Kevin J. Saliba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHumans have evolved several traits that protect against malaria. An asymptomatic trait described in Italy some 40 y ago, whereby individuals have erythrocytes deficient in flavins—the active metabolites of riboflavin (vitamin B2), has been postulated to protect against malaria, but this hypothesis has never been tested. We show that this trait exists not only in Italy but also in currently malaria-endemic areas and that erythrocytes from individuals with this trait are unable to fully support malaria parasite proliferation. Furthermore, we recreate this trait in vitro by riboflavin depletion and show that these erythrocytes are inhospitable to the parasite, and the mechanism is likely related to a reduced capacity by these red blood cells to cope with oxidative stress.

Malaria, caused by Plasmodium parasites infecting human red blood cells, continues to be a leading cause of death in many regions worldwide. In 2023, there were 263 million cases of malaria, resulting in approximately 600,000 deaths, mostly children under 5 y of age in sub-Saharan Africa and almost exclusively by Plasmodium falciparum (1). Numerous, particularly erythrocytic, malaria-protective genetic traits are common in endemic areas and are suggested to have undergone evolutionary selection because of the protection they provide against malaria (2, 3). In addition to the classic example of sickle cell trait, protective traits reaching polymorphic frequencies in (former) malaria-endemic regions include β-thalassemia and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. The latter two are common in the Ferrara province of northern Italy, where malaria was endemic until the late 1960s (1, 4).

Flavin deficiency has long been suspected of being protective against malaria. The condition is characterized by a deficiency in one or two essential enzyme cofactors known as flavins: flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). The precursor of FMN and FAD is dietary riboflavin (vitamin B2). Numerous in vivo studies in humans and animals with nutritional riboflavin deficiency showed reduced malaria parasite proliferation (5–13). Additionally, the intraerythrocytic stage of P. falciparum appears to have a requirement for riboflavin when cultured in vitro (14).

The first step of riboflavin metabolism involves phosphorylation of riboflavin by flavokinase, producing FMN. FMN is then adenylylated by FAD synthase, yielding FAD. FMN and FAD bind to flavoenzymes, which are involved in a variety of reactions in cellular metabolism, notably cellular redox homeostasis (15, 16). Without FMN and FAD bound, these flavoenzymes are nonfunctional (15).

Studies carried out in Italy in the late 1980s and early 1990s identified a condition characterized by FAD and/or FMN-deficient erythrocytes. These erythrocytes were detected by measuring the activity of the FAD-dependent erythrocyte glutathione reductase (EGR) and/or the FMN-dependent pyridoxine phosphate (PNP) oxidase (4, 17–19). Deficiency in FAD and/or FMN impairs the activity of any flavoenzyme, many of which are vital for protecting cells from oxidative damage (20). Former malaria-endemic regions of Italy exhibited a higher prevalence of individuals with flavin-deficient erythrocytes (FDE, ~31%), compared to nonendemic areas (~18%) (4). Likewise, in Sardinia, individuals with FDE were commonly found in former malaria-endemic coastal settlements, but they were rare, or absent, in mountain villages (18). As the phenotype appeared to run in families, and the participants involved in these studies had adequate dietary riboflavin (eliminating a dietary deficiency as the cause), a hereditary component was likely (4, 19). The authors referred to affected individuals as having familial flavin-deficient erythrocytes (FFDE) and hypothesized that the condition may have arisen as a protective mechanism against malaria and therefore has been selected by it. Whether FFDE is present in other previous, or current, malaria-endemic countries has not been investigated.

In this study, we set out to confirm the presence of FFDE in the previously malaria-endemic area of Ferrara, Italy, and to determine whether it is present in southern Rwanda, a malaria-endemic area in Africa that has not been previously investigated in this context. Moreover, we aimed to determine whether erythrocytes from FFDE individuals suppress malaria parasite proliferation in vitro. In addition, we emulated erythrocyte flavin deficiency in vitro via riboflavin depletion and investigated whether these erythrocytes can support parasite proliferation and what the underlying protective mechanism might be.

Methods

Screening Individuals for FFDE.

Volunteers were recruited in 2011 in the Italian province of Ferrara, where multiple prior investigations on FFDE had been carried out (4, 17–19) and where the prevalence of the condition has been reported to be as high as 30% (4). Healthy volunteers were recruited during a blood donation at the Immunohematology Transfusion Centre in Ferrara. Individuals with blood counts (MCV/MCH) consistent with thalassemia were excluded from the study. A total of 150 participants were admitted into the study (aged 21 to 67 y). Erythrocytes from a subset of deficient and control subjects were resampled for use in subsequent in vitro P. falciparum proliferation experiments.

In Huye district, Rwanda, 169 malaria patients (aged 1 to 80 y) were recruited in March to June 2018 as part of a study on antimalarial drug resistance markers (21). The district is characterized by densely populated farmland hills (population, approx. 390,000), an altitude of 1,600 to 1,800 m, yearly rainfall of 1,200 mm, and a mean temperature of 19 °C. Malaria transmission is perennial with peaks after the two rainy seasons (October-November; March-May). All patients reported fever in the preceding 48 h or were febrile upon presentation. Patients were clinically examined, malaria was microscopically confirmed, and venous blood collected. Plasmodium species was confirmed by PCR (22).

Blood samples from volunteers (Ferrara) and malaria patients (Huye) were collected in tubes containing an anticoagulant (heparin, citrate, or EDTA). The whole blood was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant plasma layer was collected and stored at −80 °C until shipped to Australia on dry ice for high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis. The erythrocytes were washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 8 min at 4 °C at 1,500 × g) and then lysed with four volumes of ice-cold HPLC-grade water, followed by centrifugation for 15 min at 4 °C at 10,000 × g to collect the supernatant for use in the flavin deficiency assay. These erythrocyte lysate samples were stored on ice if used on the same day or were frozen at −80 °C until required (samples are stable for at least a month at −80 °C). To identify flavin-deficient erythrocytes, we used the EGR activity assay, a standard approach for detecting flavin deficiency in individuals (23). GR is an FAD-dependent enzyme that catalyzes the reduction of oxidized glutathione (GSSG, glutathione disulfide) and the oxidation of the reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) (24). The oxidation of NADPH causes a decrease in absorbance at 340 nm (ΔA340), which is equivalent to the GR activity of the sample. We used the GR Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical Company) to measure EGR activity in a 96-well microtiter plate (Nunc), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

The erythrocyte lysates were diluted 10 to 20× with water, and two aliquots (20 µL) from each sample were placed in a 96-well plate. In a total volume of 140 µL, GSSG (9.5 mM), 1 mM EDTA, and 50 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.5 (GR assay buffer) were then added to each well. Positive and negative controls were included to validate the GR activity assay. Positive controls consisted of the addition of GR from Saccharomyces cerevisiae and were expected to show GR activity consistent with the manufacturer’s protocol. Negative controls, which lacked hemolysate, were included to confirm the absence of GR activity. Each well then received 50 µL of the provided NADPH solution to initiate the reaction. The absorbance of each well was measured at 340 nm every 60 s for 5 min. The GR activity was calculated using ΔA340/min divided by the NADPH extinction coefficient at 340 nm for a path length of 0.6 cm (determined by the volume of the well), 0.00373/µM, adjusted for the hemolysate volume in the reaction, total reaction volume and dilution of erythrocytes in water, and converted to µmol NADPH oxidized per 1012 erythrocytes per min. The first criterion for identifying erythrocyte flavin deficiency is indicated by a GR activity of less than 160 µmol/1012 erythrocytes/min (4). An additional two hemolysate aliquots (20 µL) from each sample were included to which 10.2 µM FAD was added to the GR assay buffer. The introduction of FAD can enhance GR activity in FAD-deficient erythrocytes, but it does not increase GR activity in erythrocytes with normal FAD levels (4). The EGR activity coefficient (EGRAC) was calculated by dividing the GR activity in the presence of FAD by the activity in the absence of FAD. The activity coefficient of fold-stimulation of GR activity in response to FAD reveals the extent of FAD insufficiency and, thus, the flavin status of the erythrocytes. An EGRAC of ≥1.4 is considered as an indication of a flavin deficiency (4). Thus, individuals showing both a GR activity of <160 μmol/1012 erythrocytes/min and an EGRAC of ≥1.4 were deemed to have flavin-deficient erythrocytes (4).

HPLC Detection of Riboflavin in Plasma Samples.

The amount of riboflavin in plasma samples from Italy and Rwanda was measured using HPLC. Plasma samples from Ferrara were injected into an Oasis HLB online extraction column (2.1 × 20 mm, Waters Corp., Milford, MA) using an Alliance 2695 (HPLC, Waters Corp., Milford, MA) system to clean up the samples by eluting unwanted materials, such as remnants of the cell matrix, while retaining the desired sample components on the column. A Two-Position Six-Port Switching Valve system (Waters Corp., Milford, MA) was used to transfer samples to a Synergi Max-RP C12 analytical column (4.6 × 250 mm., Phenomenex Inc., CA). Riboflavin is naturally fluorescent when excited at 440 to 500 nm, with maximum emission at 530 nm. A 2475 fluorescence detector (Waters Corp., Milford, MA) was used to measure the eluent’s fluorescence (excitation 470 nm, emission 530 nm). Plasma samples were injected (200 μL/injection) into the online extraction column using a linear gradient elution of acetonitrile-diluted AccQ-Tag Eluent A buffer (0:100 to 1:99) from 0 to 3 min at a flow rate of 3 mL/min. The mobile phase flow rate was reduced to 1 mL/min between 3.0 and 3.5 min. From 4 min, a switching valve was used to transfer the sample to the analytical column, and the linear gradient elution of acetonitrile-diluted AccQ-Tag Eluent A buffer (1:99 to 15:85) was applied between 4 and 20 min. The temperature of both the online and analytical columns was maintained at 35 °C. Prior to injection, plasma samples were thawed on ice while protected from light, and filtered through Whatman nylon membrane syringe filters (0.45 μm pore size, 13 mm diameter; Sigma).

Plasma samples from Rwanda were processed by removing the phospholipids and proteins using the optimized Phree phospholipids removal tube procedure (25). Briefly, 50 µL of plasma was added to 450 µL of methanol and 1% (v/v) formic acid (dilution factor 10) and vortexed for 2 min. The sample was then centrifuged for 5 min at 500 × g and the filtrate collected. A 350 µL aliquot of the filtrate was lyophilized using a freeze dryer (SP Industries, Inc.) for 2 h and reconstituted into 120 µL of the 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer solution, pH 3.5. The samples were then filtered using a nylon syringe filter (0.45 µm pore size, 13 mm diameter), into microsampling, 300 µL amber glass HPLC vials, and applied directly to an HPLC column [Kinetex® 2.6 µm (particle size) Polar C18 100 Å (pore size), 30 mm (length) × 2.1 mm (internal diameter)]. The following solvent system was used at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min: 20 mM potassium phosphate pH 3.5 (mobile phase A), 100% methanol (mobile phase B). The injection volume was 90 µL using a gradient elution type. A gradient to 60% of methanol at the period of 10 to 12 min was used to help remove the nonpolar impurities. The system was then returned to 100% of phase A until the end of the run (16 min total run time). Detection of riboflavin was performed using a Dionex fluorescence detector (excitation at 480 nm and emission at 520 nm).

Standard curves (R2 ≥ 0.9978) of riboflavin concentrations in plasma were generated for each HPLC method and utilized to calculate the corresponding riboflavin concentrations in the samples. The median human plasma riboflavin concentration is 8.6 nM (5 to 95% percentiles = 2.7 to 42.5 nM) in subjects with typical riboflavin levels in their diets (26). Individuals with a plasma riboflavin concentration lower than 2.7 nM were deemed to be flavin deficient due to insufficient dietary riboflavin intake (26).

In Vitro Culture of P. falciparum.

The 3D7 strain of P. falciparum was kept in synchronous continuous culture, as previously described (27, 28). Briefly, P. falciparum parasites were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 11 mM glucose, 24 µg/mL gentamycin, 200 µM hypoxanthine, and 0.6% w/v Albumax II (termed complete RPMI-1640) (27, 28). Cultures were maintained in 75 cm2 Nunc culture flasks at a 4% hematocrit (HCT) in O+ erythrocytes. The cultures were kept in constant agitation at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 96% nitrogen, 3% carbon dioxide, and 1% oxygen. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium daily, and the infected erythrocytes were diluted with uninfected erythrocytes, such that the parasitemia (percent infected erythrocytes) did not exceed 5%.

Generation of Flavin-Depleted Erythrocytes In Vitro.

We generated in vitro flavin-depleted erythrocytes and tested their GR activity to validate their flavin deficiency. Uninfected erythrocytes, from malaria-naïve donors with normal flavin levels, were typically used within 1 wk of phlebotomy and stored as buffy coat-poor erythrocytes (i.e., in plasma) at 4 °C prior to use. Erythrocytes were washed multiple times and then maintained for 7 to 21 d at 4 °C in complete RPMI-1640 without riboflavin (in-house preparation from the individual ingredients of RPMI-1640), and as controls, in the same riboflavin-free medium to which 0.532 µM riboflavin (Sigma, Australia) was added (in-house assembled complete RPMI-1640 medium) as well as in commercial complete RPMI-1640. The uninfected erythrocytes were washed every 48 h to ensure complete flavin depletion. After incubation, the uninfected erythrocytes were used for parasite proliferation assays.

Parasite Proliferation Assays.

Parasite proliferation in control, FFDE, or in vitro riboflavin-depleted uninfected erythrocytes was measured by inoculating with trophozoite-infected erythrocytes that had been enriched to ≥98% parasitemia by magnet purification (29), such that the starting parasitemia was ~0.5%. Parasite growth was monitored for 5 d via Giemsa-stained smears.

Oxidative Stress Tolerance of Flavin-Depleted Erythrocytes.

The formation of methemoglobin in flavin-depleted and control (nondepleted) erythrocytes, in the presence or absence of H2O2, was carried out as described by Jezewski et al. (30). Briefly, flavin-depleted and flavin-replete erythrocytes from the same donor were incubated at 37 °C in the presence of 0 or 40 mM H2O2 for 30 min before being centrifuged (16,000 g × 1 min) and the supernatant removed. The pellets were then lysed by the addition of 1 mL water and centrifuged again (16,000 g × 1 min). Four 200 µL supernatant aliquots from each sample were then transferred to a 96-well plate and the absorbance measured at 630 nm (to measure methemoglobin) and 575 nm (to measure oxyhemoglobin). The amount of methemoglobin present was expressed as a percentage of the total hemoglobin present in the samples. Oxidative stress-induced hemolysis of flavin-depleted and control erythrocytes was also determined by measuring the absorbance at 405 nm as an indicator of hemoglobin released from lysed cells. Erythrocytes were washed three times via centrifugation at 2,000 g × 8 min and resuspended in PBS. Aliquots (500 μL) were transferred to 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, and erythrocytes were lysed by adding H2O2 (0 to 160 mM, final concentration diluted in PBS) to the erythrocyte suspensions to a final hematocrit of 1%. The different concentrations of H2O2 were allowed to react with the erythrocytes for 10 min at room temperature (RT), before being replaced twice more with the same concentration of H2O2 via centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 min and resuspended in fresh H2O2 (at the same concentration, 1% hematocrit), with a 10 min incubation at RT in between. An aliquot of nondepleted uninfected erythrocytes was resuspended in water as a total hemolysis control.

Osmotic Fragility of Flavin-Depleted Erythrocytes.

The osmotic fragility of control and flavin-depleted erythrocytes was determined by suspending erythrocytes in solutions of different osmolarity, prepared by diluting saline (PBS containing 10 mM glucose) in water. Erythrocyte aliquots (500 μL) at 1% hematocrit were centrifuged (16,000 × g for 1 min) and resuspended in 1 mL of the saline solutions adjusted to different osmolarities. The suspensions were then immediately centrifuged again (16,000 × g for 1 min) and 4 × 200 µL aliquots of the supernatant from each sample transferred to a 96-well plate and the absorbance, measured at 405 nm, used as an indicator of hemolysis. Cells suspended in water and saline were used as total hemolysis and no hemolysis controls, respectively.

Sequencing of Flavokinase and FAD Synthase.

We sequenced the coding regions of the flavokinase and FAD synthase genes in samples from Huye to detect potential differences in participants with FFDE and without. Samples from all participants that were found with FFDE were assessed, and a random selection of samples without FFDE was taken to match the number of FFDE samples. Venous blood DNA was extracted using a commercial kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The human flavokinase gene consists of four exons, which were amplified by three separate PCRs (FK1, FK2, and FK3), and FAD synthase gene consists of four exons, amplified with two PCRs (FAD4 and FAD5). The primers for these reactions are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. In short, PCR assays contained, in a 20 µL reaction volume, 0.2 mM dNTPs (each, New England Biolabs, MA), 1× B1 solution (Solis Biodyne, Tartu, Estonia), 1.5 U hot-start taq polymerase (5 U/µL HOT FIREpol, Solis Biodyne), 1.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.3 µM forward and reverse primers (all from Eurofins Genomics, Germany). Thermocycling conditions for each PCR assay were 95 °C for 15 min, 40 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, assay-specific annealing temperature for 1 min, 72 °C for 1 min, and final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min. Assay-specific annealing temperature was 58 °C for FK1, 61 °C for FK2, 62 °C for FK3, 57 °C for FAD4, and 60 °C for FAD5. Amplicons were sent for commercial purification and sequencing (Eurofins Genomics, Germany), and reads were aligned to reference genes (gene data bank, Uniprot accession numbers for riboflavin kinase (RIFK_Human) and FAD synthase (FAD1_HUMAN) are Q969G6 and Q8NFF5, respectively) using Geneious Prime Version 2022.

Statistical Analysis.

Shapiro–Wilk and Anderson–Darling normality tests as well as Student’s t tests were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.3 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). If datasets failed normality tests, they were compared with the nonparametric Brunner-Munzel test (31) (data in Figs. 3 and 4) using Jamovi computer software (version 2.6; www.jamovi.org), otherwise Student’s t test for paired samples was used (data in Figs. 5 and 7). For the data in Fig. 4, five comparisons were made (one at each time point). Since the same hypothesis was tested with each comparison, we did not apply a post hoc correction (32).

Ethics.

Ethics approval was obtained from the Australian National University Human Ethics Committee—protocols 2011/266 and 2018/220 and the Rwanda National Ethics Committee—protocol 416/RNEC/2017. Informed consent, of guardians in case of minors, including that related to the genetic experiments, was obtained at the outset of the study.

Results

The Prevalence of Erythrocyte Flavin Deficiency in Italy and Rwanda Was 23% and 13%, Respectively.

Erythrocyte lysates prepared from individuals from Italy (n = 150) and Rwanda (n = 169) were tested for GR activity to determine their flavin status (Fig. 1). Positive and negative controls confirmed the assay’s validity (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The effect of adding 10.2 µM FAD to normal and flavin-deficient samples is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2.

Screening subjects in Ferrara (A) and Huye (B) for flavin-deficient erythrocytes. In Ferrara, Italy, 150 subjects (130 males, 20 females) were screened by testing the GR activity of their erythrocytes. All samples were analyzed for riboflavin plasma levels using HPLC. Erythrocytes from 9 volunteers with normal flavin levels and 18 with flavin deficiency were further tested for their ability to support P. falciparum proliferation. In Huye, Rwanda, 169 subjects (79 males, 90 females) underwent a similar screening process. The samples from Italy were obtained from healthy volunteers and were recruited during a routine blood donation visit. The samples from Rwanda were obtained from patients with uncomplicated malaria and had been recruited into an independent study.

We found that 35 (23.3%) participants from Ferrara had flavin-deficient erythrocytes (Fig. 2A). This is in line with the FFDE prevalence (up to 30%) reported previously in Ferrara by Anderson et al. (4). In comparison, 22 (13%) of the Huye participants had flavin-deficient erythrocytes (Fig. 2B).

Erythrocyte Flavin Deficiency Is Not Due to Insufficient Dietary Riboflavin.

To exclude the possibility that erythrocyte flavin deficiency resulted from insufficient dietary riboflavin intake, we measured riboflavin concentration in the plasma of all the samples from Ferrara and a subset of the samples from Huye (Fig. 1). None of these samples indicated insufficient dietary flavin intake (plasma riboflavin concentration < 2.7 nM). In samples from Ferrara, plasma riboflavin concentrations were similar among individuals without and with flavin-deficient erythrocytes (N = 115; mean ± SD: 8.1 ± 3.6 nM; range: 2.7 to 29.1 nM versus N = 35; mean ± SD: 7.6 ± 3.1 nM; range: 3.7 to 17.0 nM; P = 0.446, Fig. 3A). In contrast, plasma riboflavin concentrations in samples from Huye were more than twice as high in individuals with normal erythrocytes compared to those with flavin-deficient erythrocytes (N = 22; mean ± SD: 26 ± 14 nM; range: 3.5 to 50.0 nM versus N = 22; mean ± SD: 57 ± 43 nM; range: 7.2 to 169.0 nM; P = 0.007, Fig. 3B).

The box extends from the 25th to the 75th percentile, and the line in the middle shows the median. Whiskers show the data range. Note the different X-axis scale between panels A and B. P. falciparum Proliferation in Flavin-Deficient Erythrocytes Is Impaired In Vitro.

Erythrocytes from 27 Italian volunteers (18 with flavin-deficient and 9 with normal erythrocyte flavin levels) were resampled for the parasite proliferation assays (Fig. 1). Parasite proliferation in commercial RPMI-1640 medium (with riboflavin) was measured by blind-counting of parasitemias from Giemsa-stained slides (Fig. 4). On each of days 1 and 2 of the assay, there was no significant difference in P. falciparum proliferation between control and flavin-deficient erythrocytes (P ≥ 0.249). On day 3, however, control erythrocytes had an average parasitemia of 9.3 ± 1.4% (mean ± SEM, N = 9), which was significantly higher than the 5.7 ± 0.8% observed in flavin-deficient erythrocytes (N = 18; P = 0.023) (Fig. 4). This is consistent with a reduced parasite proliferation in flavin-deficient erythrocytes from day 2 onward. On day 4 of the experiment, the average parasitemia in control erythrocytes was 16.1 ± 2.1%, significantly greater than the 8.9 ± 1.5% observed in flavin-deficient erythrocytes [P = 0.042 (Fig. 4)]. On day 5 of the assay, the parasitemia appeared to plateau, and again it was higher in control erythrocytes than in flavin-deficient erythrocytes (P = 0.032) (Fig. 4). The parasite proliferation rate was then measured by linear interpolation of the period between days 2 and 4 (dotted red lines in Fig. 4). The parasite proliferation rate was roughly double in normal erythrocytes as compared to flavin-deficient erythrocytes (means ± SEM, 7.6 ± 1.0% versus 3.9 ± 0.8% per day; P = 0.003). These findings support the hypothesis that erythrocyte flavin deficiency inhibits the in vitro intraerythrocytic proliferation of P. falciparum parasites.

Riboflavin-Depleted Erythrocytes Have a Reduced GR Activity.

The extent of flavin depletion in the erythrocytes incubated for 12 to 24 d in the absence of extracellular riboflavin was determined by measuring GR activity. Control erythrocytes maintained in the presence of riboflavin had a basal GR activity of 259 ± 22 µmol NADPH/1012 erythrocytes/min (Fig. 5A), which did not significantly increase (273 ± 23 µmol NADPH/1012 erythrocytes/min, P = 0.092) when FAD was added, resulting in an EGRAC of 1.05 (Fig. 5B). On the other hand, erythrocytes incubated in the absence of extracellular riboflavin had a GR activity of 157 ± 13 µmol NADPH/1012 erythrocytes/min, which was statistically lower than the activity of control erythrocytes (Fig. 5A, P = 0.011). FAD supplementation significantly increased their GR activity to 232 ± 17 µmol NADPH/1012 erythrocytes/min (P = 0.008), resulting in an EGRAC of 1.49, higher than the cutoff ≥ 1.4 required for classification as flavin-deficient (Fig. 5B). These data are consistent with incubation of erythrocytes in riboflavin-free medium (for >7 d) resulting in erythrocytes with flavin deficiency equivalent to those observed in individuals with FFDE.

Riboflavin Depletion of Erythrocytes Suppresses P. falciparum Parasite Proliferation.

Having now demonstrated that we can generate flavin-deficient erythrocytes in vitro that appear to be biochemically indistinguishable from FFDEs observed in vivo (in Ferrara and Huye), we looked at the ability of these in vitro-generated flavin-deficient erythrocytes to support parasite proliferation. Parasite proliferation was initially monitored for 5 d via counting of parasitemias in riboflavin-depleted erythrocytes incubated in riboflavin-free medium and in nondepleted erythrocytes maintained in riboflavin-containing RPMI-1640 (Fig. 6). Parasite proliferation was hindered in riboflavin-depleted erythrocytes maintained in riboflavin-free medium during the proliferation assay when compared to erythrocytes maintained in normal (riboflavin-containing) medium during both the preincubation and parasite proliferation periods. By day 5 of the assay, proliferation in the absence of riboflavin was reduced by ~13-fold. Although in the absence of riboflavin the parasitemia did not increase substantially by day 5 of the assay (Fig. 6), parasites were still visible in the culture and looked viable. These findings are consistent with riboflavin being required for normal in vitro P. falciparum proliferation. We have previously shown that parasite proliferation is unaffected for at least 96 h in the absence of extracellular riboflavin (33, 34). The inhibition observed in this study is therefore likely due to the riboflavin depletion period rendering the erythrocytes unable to fully support parasite growth. To test this directly, the parasite culture that had been maintained in the absence of riboflavin and in riboflavin-depleted erythrocytes was divided evenly into three, and maintained for three further days. One parasite subculture was maintained in riboflavin-free medium and riboflavin-depleted erythrocytes, a second culture was supplemented with extracellular riboflavin, but maintained in erythrocytes that had been riboflavin-depleted, and the third culture was supplemented with extracellular riboflavin and erythrocytes that had not been depleted of riboflavin. The addition of riboflavin-containing medium to riboflavin-depleted cultures did not alter parasite proliferation by day 8 of the assay (P = 0.17, paired t test, Fig. 6). The addition of a new batch of erythrocytes that had been maintained in riboflavin-containing medium, and cultured in the presence of riboflavin-containing medium, restored parasite proliferation to the control rate (Fig. 6). The observed effect can therefore be attributed to the addition of erythrocytes that had been maintained in riboflavin, as riboflavin alone could not restore proliferation.

Flavin-Depleted Erythrocytes Are More Susceptible to Oxidative Stress and osmotically fragile.

To determine whether flavin-depleted erythrocytes are more susceptible to oxidative stress, we first measured the formation of methemoglobin, an oxidized form of hemoglobin. We found that baseline methemoglobin levels are unchanged following a 3-week riboflavin depletion period at 4 °C (~1.9% of total hemoglobin in both conditions, p = 0.8, Fig. 7A), but when challenged with 40 mM H2O2 for 30 min, methemoglobin levels were higher (by approximately 4%) in flavin-depleted erythrocytes compared to the flavin-replete counterparts from the same donor (p = 0.02, Fig. 7A). We then tested the ability of flavin-depleted erythrocytes to resist lysis when exposed to increasing concentration of H2O2 (35). Control erythrocytes that had been incubated in the presence of riboflavin (and were therefore not flavin deficient) started lysing when subjected to H2O2 concentrations above 10 mM, with an EC50 (concentration that results in the lysis of 50% of the erythrocytes) of 46 ± 11 mM (Fig. 7B). Flavin-depleted erythrocytes (i.e., erythrocytes that have been preincubated in the absence of riboflavin for 12 to 24 d) were more susceptible than control erythrocytes to peroxide-induced lysis, with lysis starting to occur at concentrations above 5 mM H2O2 and at a significantly lower EC50 of 15 ± 1 mM (P = 0.03, Fig. 7B). This is consistent with flavin-depleted erythrocytes (generated via riboflavin depletion) having an increased susceptibility to oxidative stress. We also show that flavin-depleted erythrocytes are osmotically fragile, lysing more readily than their flavin-replete counterparts when the osmolarity of the suspending solution is reduced (p = 0.04, Fig. 7 C).

Mutations Detected in FAD Synthase in Some FFDE Samples from Huye.

To investigate a possible genetic basis for flavin-deficient erythrocytes, we sequenced the coding regions of the human FAD synthase (FLAD1) and flavokinase (RFK) from the 22 individuals with flavin-deficient erythrocytes and 22 control samples from Huye. We identified three nonsynonymous mutations in FAD synthase, each in an individual with flavin-deficient erythrocytes. No mutations were detected in FAD synthase of control samples, nor were any mutations detected in the human flavokinase gene of both flavin-deficient and control samples. The three mutations, R437C, R555W, and G472S, were caused by the following single-nucleotide changes: CGC to TGC, CGG to TGG, and GGT to AGT, respectively. According to the single-nucleotide polymorphism database (dbSNP), the mutations have a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.0008, 0.000007, and 0.00002, respectively, with these frequencies calculated based on data from at least 140,000 subjects of global populations using the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) (36). The frequency of all three mutations in the general population is therefore rare, but they may, of course, be more common in populations with specific genetic traits.

The human FAD synthase gene encodes several different isoforms (37). Two of the mutated residues (R437 and G472) are within the C-terminal 3-phosphoadenosine 5’-phosphosulfate (PAPS) reductase domain that is common to the best characterized isoforms [mitochondrial isoform 1 and cytosolic isoform 2; (38–41)]. The third residue that is mutated (R555), is in the C-terminal region following the PAPS reductase domain, which is also present in isoforms 1 and 2. X-ray crystal structures of full-length human FAD synthase 2 and the FAD-bound PAPS reductase domain were recently determined (42). As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S3, R437 is located adjacent to the binding site of the adenine moiety of FAD. G472, on the other hand, is located adjacent to where the flavin moiety of FAD binds. However, neither interacts directly with FAD. R555 forms part of the C-terminal “cap” (composed of residues I538 to T587) that extends over the active site and is required for activity (42). Computational tools were then used to quantify the potential impact of the variants on enzyme function. SIFT analysis (43) resulted in scores of 0.11, 0.15, and 0.44 for the R555W, R437C, and G472S mutations, respectively. SIFT scores range from 0 to 1, and while only scores <0.05 are considered deleterious, the low scores obtained for R555W and R437C are consistent with them potentially influencing enzyme activity. This is supported by PolyPhen analysis (44), which indicates that the two mutations with the low SIFT scores are “probably damaging.”

Discussion

In this study, we set out to identify individuals with flavin-deficient erythrocytes in both Ferrara, Italy, and Huye, Rwanda, and investigated the potential causes and consequences of this deficiency. We found a significant prevalence of FFDE in both Ferrara (23%) and Huye (13%; Fig. 2). The FFDE prevalence observed in Ferrara aligns well with previous studies reporting figures as high as 30% (4). The lower prevalence in Huye may be indicative of the general population, but it may also reflect that FFDE, shown to be malaria-protective in the present study, should be less frequent among the sampled individuals who all had malaria. A separate study to test this hypothesis directly would need to be carried out. We did, however, directly test the hypothesis that FFDE suppress the proliferation of P. falciparum in vitro (Fig. 4) and may therefore offer protection against malaria. Our finding that P. falciparum proliferation is impaired in flavin-deficient erythrocytes from participants in Ferrara supports the hypothesis that FFDE may have a protective mechanism against malaria. Whether the reduction in parasite proliferation we observed in these erythrocytes would be sufficient to reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with malaria remains to demonstrated.

The fact that hematologically detected abnormalities were an exclusion criterion in Ferrara reduces the possibility that other hemoglobinopathies confounded our study, and it is very unlikely that our participants had hematologic manifestations of thalassemia (both alpha and beta). Hematologically silent donors with beta-thalassemia trait are rare in Ferrara, up to only 3/1,000 (45). We are not aware of population studies in Ferrara on alpha-thalassemia (milder compared to beta-thalassemia), but in another province of northern Italy, the frequency was 3 to 5%, depending on the screening method (46). The frequency of G6PD deficiency in Italy is just 1.1% (47). Hemoglobinopathies are rare at the Rwandan study site compared to other areas of Africa, likely due to the altitude (1,500 to 1,800 m), with historically limited malaria. In Huye, most hemoglobinopathy traits as well as G6PD deficiency are mild and/or heterozygous (48).

To support our observation that flavin-deficient donor erythrocytes are incapable of supporting parasite proliferation at control erythrocyte levels, we generated flavin-deficient erythrocytes in vitro by incubating erythrocytes in riboflavin-free medium for several days. We found that this maneuver effectively emulates the flavin-deficient profile seen in individuals with FFDE (Fig. 5) and dramatically impairs parasite proliferation when compared to flavin-replete erythrocytes (Fig. 6). Notably, the addition of riboflavin during the parasite culture period did not restore parasite proliferation, consistent with flavin-deficient erythrocytes becoming an inhospitable environment for the parasite, rather than an effect on parasite proliferation due to the parasite’s direct requirement for riboflavin.

Several conditions other than hemoglobinopathies have been put forward as potentially protective against malaria, including, for example, pyruvate kinase deficiency, which increases phagocytosis of ring-stage infected erythrocytes (49). Our observation that flavin-deficient erythrocytes are more susceptible to methemoglobin formation upon oxidative stress by H2O2 and threefold more susceptible to H2O2-induced lysis (Fig. 7) provides a possible explanation for the reduced parasite proliferation in these erythrocytes. Malaria parasites are known to generate H2O2 and OH radicals (twice as much as uninfected erythrocytes) during hemoglobin digestion; these reactive oxygen species make their way from the parasite into the infected erythrocyte, damaging the erythrocyte membrane (50). This reduced tolerance to oxidative stress by flavin-deficient erythrocytes would be expected to inhibit parasite proliferation, possibly via premature lysis or splenic clearance of the erythrocyte and/or oxidative damage to the parasite from radicals normally neutralized by the erythrocyte (51). We also show that flavin-deficient erythrocytes are more sensitive to osmotic lysis (Fig. 7). This opens the possibility that following the formation of new permeability pathways due to infection by Plasmodium (52) [~15 h postinvasion (53)], which greatly increase the permeability of the infected erythrocyte to a wide range of ions and solutes (54), the influx of osmolytes triggers osmotic rupture of infected, flavin-deficient erythrocytes.

In this study, we found that plasma riboflavin levels appeared to be higher, and covered a wider range, in Huye compared to those from Ferrara. The reason for this is unclear but may be related to different diets between the two countries and/or the fact that 41% of the participants in Huye were children, previously shown to have lower limits of plasma riboflavin levels that are higher than those of adults (55). Importantly, however, we also show that individuals with flavin-deficient erythrocytes (from both Ferrara and Huye) are not deficient in dietary riboflavin (Fig. 3). Therefore, there needs to be an alternative explanation for the flavin deficiency within these erythrocytes. The previous observation that the condition was familial strongly points to a genetic element (17, 18). We therefore sequenced the genes coding for the enzymes responsible for generating FMN and FAD from riboflavin, namely flavokinase and FAD synthase. The identification of three nonsynonymous mutations in the FAD synthase gene, which were absent in control samples, is consistent with these mutations potentially being responsible for the development of FFDE, at least in some individuals. Although these mutations are extremely rare, the fact that they were all identified in a small cohort of 44 individuals is highly significant, 247-fold higher incidence than their average MAF. Whether these mutations have an impact on the activity of the enzyme and thereby play a role in the development of FFDE requires further investigation. Irrespective of the role these mutations play, if any, in FFDE, mutations in other genes would need to be identified to explain FFDE in individuals with no mutations detected in both flavokinase and FAD synthase. An obvious candidate for further investigation is the erythrocyte riboflavin transporter. Reduced riboflavin transport into the erythrocyte could result in the same phenotype we observed in flavin-deficient erythrocytes generated in vitro by riboflavin starvation. In humans, multiple riboflavin transporters have been identified, including RFVT1, RFVT2, and RFVT3 (56), yet the specific transporter responsible for riboflavin uptake in erythrocytes has not been definitively established (56–58). Erythrocyte proteomics have also not identified any of the known human riboflavin transporters, adding to the uncertainty (59–62). Due to this lack of clarity regarding the presence and identity of the riboflavin transporter in erythrocytes, we opted not to sequence the riboflavin transporter genes at this point.

Although none of the three FAD synthase residues found to be mutated in FFDE samples from Huye have been shown to form direct interactions with the enzyme’s substrates, in all cases the amino acid substitution results in a change in the physicochemical properties at the site; i.e., a positively charged arginine is changed to an uncharged and/or hydrophobic residue (cysteine or tryptophan, respectively) and a nonpolar glycine is changed to a polar serine residue. In all cases, this could impact local structure and, given the proximity of the affected residue to the active site, ligand binding. Furthermore, with R437C, a cysteine residue is introduced near to the active site of the PAPS reductase domain. The PAPS reductase domain ordinarily contains seven cysteine residues, with some forming stable disulfide bonds and others being redox active, and likely to play a role in enzyme activity (41). Addition of a cysteine could potentially impact the preexisting cysteine network and, in turn, enzyme activity and/or interaction with redox effectors that may regulate the enzyme.

In conclusion, we have provided evidence for the prevalence of FFDE in both malaria-endemic and previously endemic regions. The observation that flavin-deficient erythrocytes reduce intraerythrocytic P. falciparum proliferation, as well as the susceptibility of these erythrocytes to oxidative stress, supports the hypothesis that FFDE may play a crucial role in mitigating the severity and progression of malaria. The identification of mutations in the FAD synthase gene raises intriguing questions regarding a potential genetic basis of FFDE and warrants further exploration. Although the understanding of a malaria-protective genetic trait has thus far not resulted in a novel malaria control strategy, the recent reports (33, 34) of riboflavin analogues with potent antiplasmodial activity raise the possibility that the mechanism/s underlying FFDE could ultimately result in one such strategy.
