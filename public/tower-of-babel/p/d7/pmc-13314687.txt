Ruptured cerebral arteriovenous malformation associated with enlarged parietal foramina: a rare surgical case with pathogenetic implications
Authors: Toru Umehara, Hiroki Kiyokawa, Shuhei Yamada, Yoshihiro Yano, Toshiaki Fujita, Haruhiko Kishima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnlarged parietal foramina (EPF) are rare, bilateral, symmetric calvarial defects, most commonly inherited in an autosomal-dominant manner. We report an exceptionally rare case of a ruptured parietal arteriovenous malformation (AVM) situated immediately beneath an EPF and resected via the calvarial defect. A 46-year-old man with no prior medical history presented with acute left upper-limb incoordination. Head computed tomography revealed a 3-cm right parietal subcortical hemorrhage and bilateral symmetric parietal defects; similar defects in his mother, uncle, and grandfather supported a diagnosis of familial EPF. The hemorrhage was directly subjacent to the defect, without antecedent trauma. Angiography identified a Spetzler–Martin grade I AVM within the hematoma. Using the EPF as a corridor, microsurgical resection was performed. The osseous defect was filled with fibrous connective tissue, and the dura was focally thickened with firm dural–arachnoid adhesions. The AVM nidus was removed en bloc. Postoperatively, left upper-limb incoordination resolved promptly and the patient was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0. Histopathology confirmed an AVM nidus and demonstrated infiltration of inflammatory cells, including polymorphonuclear leukocytes, around abnormal vascular walls and within the dura. Whole-genome sequencing revealed a pathogenic ALX4 nonsense variant (c.793C>T, p.Arg265Ter) and no pathogenic variants in established brain-AVM predisposition genes. The spatial concordance of EPF and AVM, together with the inflammatory histopathology, raises the possibility that EPF-associated inflammation may influence subjacent AVM biology or rupture susceptibility, although causality remains unproven.Keywords: Enlarged parietal foramina, ALX4, Inflammation, Cerebral arteriovenous malformation

Introduction

Enlarged parietal foramina (EPF) are rare congenital defects of the posterior parietal calvarium; they are typically bilateral and symmetric along the sagittal suture and range from a few millimeters to several centimeters. The prevalence is estimated at 1 in 15,000–50,000 [1, 2]. Most cases are inherited in an autosomal-dominant manner, and pathogenic variants in ALX4 or MSX2 have been implicated [3, 4]. EPF are most often asymptomatic and follow a favorable natural history, with neurodevelopment generally normal. Management is primarily conservative, emphasizing minimization of head-injury risk [2, 5]. Prophylactic cranioplasty is generally not indicated but may be considered for large defects, high trauma risk, or substantial cosmetic concerns [1, 6]. EPF frequently co-occur with developmental venous anomalies—most notably a persistent falcine sinus with hypoplasia or absence of the straight sinus—whereas associations with other cerebrovascular lesions have been only rarely reported and remain unsubstantiated [1, 2, 7]. Here, we report a rare case of a ruptured cerebral arteriovenous malformation (AVM) located immediately beneath an EPF and resected through the osseous defect. We discuss embryologic considerations and explore a potential pathogenetic link suggested by this case.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old man with no prior medical history presented to our emergency department with left upper-limb incoordination. A head computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated a 3-cm right parietal subcortical hemorrhage and bilateral, symmetric parietal bone defects (Fig. 1A, B). A family history revealed identical defects in the patient’s mother, uncle, and grandfather, consistent with familial EPF. The right parietal hemorrhage lay beneath the right EPF defect without any traumatic episode. CT angiography and subsequent cerebral angiography demonstrated an arteriovenous shunt within the hematoma cavity. A cerebral micro-AVM was suspected and considered the source of hemorrhage. Hypoplasia of the straight sinus and a persistent falcine sinus were also noted, without direct involvement in the arteriovenous shunt (Fig. 1C–F). Given the ruptured Spetzler–Martin grade I micro-AVM with a single feeder, we proceeded with microsurgical resection through the right EPF corridor without preoperative endovascular embolization and planned single-stage reconstruction of the bilateral calvarial defects to address cosmetic concerns.

Through the right EPF corridor, the intracerebral hematoma was evacuated and the AVM was resected. The bilateral calvarial defects were reconstructed with bone cement. Notable intraoperative findings were as follows: (1) fibrous connective tissue filling the calvarial defect and (2) focal thickening with adhesions of the dural–arachnoid immediately subjacent to the EPF defect (Fig. 2). Postoperative imaging confirmed complete hematoma evacuation and resection of the AVM, with successful reconstruction of the calvarial defects (Fig. 3). Left upper-limb incoordination improved rapidly postoperatively; he was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0. The parietal depression resolved, and the patient reported high satisfaction with the cosmetic outcome. At the last follow-up (56 months postoperatively), magnetic resonance angiography demonstrated no evidence of recurrent AVM and no new arteriovenous shunt on either side.

Histopathology confirmed an AVM nidus. Inflammatory infiltrates—including polymorphonuclear leukocytes—were present within and around the abnormal vessel walls, as well as in the dura and fibrous tissue occupying the bony defect (Fig. 4). Whole-genome sequencing on peripheral blood DNA identified a pathogenic ALX4 nonsense variant, c.793C>T (p.Arg265Ter), while no pathogenic variants were found in established brain-AVM predisposition genes, arguing against a hereditary AVM syndrome. The case was diagnosed as familial EPF with a coexisting sporadic AVM.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of an intracranial AVM coexisting with familial EPF due to a germline ALX4 loss-of-function variant. The ruptured AVM lay strikingly beneath the EPF; however, because EPF-associated vascular abnormalities have not been shown to consistently occur beneath the calvarial defect, this anatomical relationship remains hypothesis-generating rather than causal. The absence of antecedent head trauma also made a trauma-related mechanism unlikely.

The EPF-associated venous phenotype may have embryologic relevance. A persistent falcine sinus—an embryonic venous remnant—together with straight sinus hypoplasia suggests altered fetal venous remodeling [2, 7]. In this case, these venous anomalies were not directly involved in the arteriovenous shunt. Although abnormal venous development or outflow may contribute to intracranial vascular malformation biology [8], these observations remain indirect and do not establish causality.

Histologic inflammatory infiltrates in the AVM and adjacent dura, together with the gross dural–arachnoid adhesions beneath the EPF, suggest a chronically inflamed local microenvironment. EPF-associated inflammation may have influenced subjacent AVM biology or rupture susceptibility, consistent with evidence linking inflammation to AVM remodeling and progression [9, 10]. However, because the tissue was examined after AVM rupture and no molecular assessment of inflammatory pathways was performed, it remains unclear whether the inflammation preceded the hemorrhage or reflected a secondary reaction to rupture. These findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of de novo AVM formation. Further studies are required.

ALX4, located at 11p11.2, encodes a homeobox transcription factor involved in cranial suture biology. Pathogenic ALX4 loss-of-function variants have been associated with EPF and other developmental anomalies [1]. We identified a germline nonsense variant, c.793C>T (p.Arg265Ter). While an exact ClinVar match is unavailable, prior reports of nonsense changes in the same region causing EPF through loss-of-function render a pathogenic interpretation for this variant plausible [3, 11, 12].

Clinically, given the striking co-localization of the ruptured AVM with the right-sided EPF, the contralateral EPF shares a similar anatomic and embryologic milieu and could theoretically harbor an occult shunt or confer susceptibility to future lesion development. Longitudinal follow-up of the contralateral side with noninvasive cerebrovascular imaging may therefore be prudent. Although the present case occurred in an adult, EPF is congenital and often familial; early identification in childhood provides an opportunity for counseling on head protection and consideration of cerebrovascular screening in affected children and at-risk relatives.

In conclusion, this case raises the hypothesis that EPF-associated local inflammatory or venous microenvironmental changes may influence subjacent cerebrovascular lesion biology or rupture susceptibility, although causality remains unproven. Additional histopathologic and molecular studies are required. Baseline noninvasive cerebrovascular screening may be considered in selected patients with EPF.
