DT-TRAP enables accurate target recognition via simplified dosing and low-temperature incubation
Authors: Hanqing Zhang, Wenjie Yuan, Yi Li, Chunyu Li, Yizhou Xie, Ning Wan, Haiping Hao, Hui Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTarget-responsive accessibility profiling (TRAP) is a powerful chemoproteomic strategy for drug target discovery that monitors ligand-induced changes in lysine accessibility. However, TRAP and related approaches remain constrained by high sample consumption and extended analysis time, while the systematic impact of incubation temperature on target identification remains unexplored. Here, we introduce dose- and temperature-optimized TRAP (DT-TRAP), a streamlined workflow that integrates rational dosing strategies with controlled incubation temperatures to enhance the specificity and reliability of ligand-protein interaction profiling. Using the pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) activator TEPP-46 and the heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) inhibitor geldanamycin as model compounds, we demonstrate that a simplified three-dose design is sufficient for the robust identification of bona fide targets. Notably, we show that incubation at 4 °C significantly suppresses nonspecific interactions and enhances binding specificity compared to 25 °C or 37 °C. Applying DT-TRAP to staurosporine and aspirin, we successfully identified multiple targets within complex proteomes, with peptide-level analyses pinpointing binding sites predominantly within ligand-binding pockets. Collectively, these findings establish DT-TRAP as a practical framework for optimizing experimental parameters in chemical proteomics, thereby improving target identification accuracy and facilitating drug discovery.Keywords: Target-responsive accessibility profiling, Chemical proteomics, Drug target discovery, Incubation temperature, Rational dosing, Nonspecific binding events

Introduction

Target-responsive accessibility profiling (TRAP) has emerged as a powerful chemoproteomic strategy for drug target discovery. By capturing ligand-induced perturbations in lysine accessibility, it provides a sensitive, albeit indirect, readout of protein engagement and structural changes, and has been successfully applied across diverse biological systems [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. However, false positives remain a persistent challenge for TRAP and related chemoproteomic approaches [7,8], significantly compromising the specificity and reproducibility of target identification. To mitigate this, dose-responsive experimental designs are commonly employed. Recent studies highlight that dose-dependent validation is critical for distinguishing bona fide targets from nonspecific noise [[9], [10], [11]]. Furthermore, profiling across concentration gradients not only authenticates binding events but also enables the estimation of binding affinities [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Nevertheless, such experiments typically require substantially more biological material, consume approximately tenfold more reagents than standard workflows, and necessitate extended instrument runtime (often >24 h). These factors drastically increase experimental complexity and cost, thereby hindering the feasibility of these methods for high-throughput applications.

Beyond dosing, other experimental parameters—most notably incubation temperature—can critically influence assay outcomes. Temperature is known to fundamentally alter drug-protein interaction kinetics [17,18]. Elevated temperatures increase molecular motion and collision frequencies, accelerating both association and dissociation rates. Conversely, lower temperatures reduce thermal energy, dampening conformational fluctuations and biasing proteins toward more energetically stable, constrained conformations [[19], [20], [21]]. Despite these known physical principles, the systematic evaluation of incubation temperature as a tunable variable to optimize chemoproteomic performance remains limited.

In the absence of streamlined optimization frameworks, complex multidimensional strategies have been developed to enhance target identification [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. For instance, two-dimensional thermal proteome profiling (2D-TPP) determines temperature-dependent half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values but typically necessitates six drug concentrations across ten temperature points, resulting in thirty liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) injections for a single compound [24]. Similarly, the matrix thermal shift assay (mTSA) employs multiple dosing schemes to improve discrimination between true and nonspecific targets [25]. While these approaches have substantially improved specificity and sensitivity, their elaborate experimental designs and prohibitive resource demands often restrict their scalability and broader adoption.

In this study, we employed the TRAP platform to systematically evaluate incubation temperature as a controllable experimental variable and to optimize the two critical parameters of dose and temperature, establishing the dose- and temperature-optimized TRAP (DT-TRAP) workflow. Our results demonstrate that a streamlined three-dose design is sufficient to reliably capture bona fide targets, while incubation at 4 °C markedly suppresses nonspecific background and reduces experimental complexity. Applying DT-TRAP to staurosporine and aspirin, we successfully identified multiple targets with binding sites resolved at the peptide level. Overall, DT-TRAP provides a robust and resource-efficient framework for optimizing chemoproteomic parameters, enhancing both the accuracy and throughput of drug target discovery.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and general materials

Human HeLa and K562 cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HeLa cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) and K562 cells in roswell park memorial institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (both from Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Borane pyridine complex (BPC; CAS No. 110-51-0, Cat. 121499) was purchased from J&K Scientific (Beijing, China). The small-molecule compounds TEPP-46, geldanamycin, staurosporine, and aspirin were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). LC-MS-grade water, acetonitrile (ACN), and formaldehyde-d2 solution (CD2O; CAS No. 1664-98-8) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Unless otherwise specified, all other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

DT-TRAP analysis

Cell lysis and proteome preparation

Adherent HeLa cells were washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), harvested by scraping, and pelleted by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min at 4 °C. Suspension K562 cells were harvested and pelleted under identical conditions. The resulting cell pellets were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in Pierce™ M-PER™ Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Cat. No. 78503, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat. No. K1007, ApexBio, Houston, TX, USA) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cat. No. K1013, ApexBio, Houston, TX, USA). Lysis was allowed to proceed on ice for 30 min. The lysates were subsequently clarified by centrifugation at 18,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat. P0011, Beyotime, Beijing, China). Finally, cell lysates were normalized to a concentration of 1 μg/μL with M-PER buffer prior to use. All DT-TRAP experiments described herein were performed using these prepared lysates.

Dose-responsive TRAP

For dose-dependent profiling of TEPP-46 and geldanamycin, cell lysates were incubated with serial dilutions of each compound across eight concentrations (1 nM, 10 nM, 50 nM, 100 nM, 500 nM, 1 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM) or vehicle control. Specifically, 100 μL of lysate (1 μg/μL) was incubated with the compound at 25 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, lysine labeling was initiated by adding 2 μL of 1% CD2O and 30 μL of 9 mM BPC. The reaction proceeded at 25 °C for 30 min and was quenched by the addition of hydroxylamine to a final concentration of 5%. Proteins were precipitated using sample/methanol/chloroform (4:4:1, v/v/v) and pelleted by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein pellets were washed three times with methanol and resolubilized in 8 M urea (in 25 mM NH4HCO3). Samples were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAM), with excess IAM quenched by additional DTT. Prior to digestion, the urea concentration was diluted to <2 M with 25 mM NH4HCO3. Proteins were then digested overnight with trypsin (enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50, w/w) at 37 °C. Digestion was terminated by acidification with formic acid (final pH ≈ 3). The resulting peptides were desalted using Sep-Pak C18 cartridges (Cat. No. WAT054955, Waters, Milford, MA, USA), dried in a vacuum concentrator (JM Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and stored at −80 °C until LC-MS analysis.

Temperature-responsive TRAP

HeLa lysates (100 μL, 1 μg/μL) were treated with geldanamycin (5 μM) or vehicle control (0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 60 min at three distinct temperatures: 4, 25, or 37 °C. To account for the temperature dependence of reductive dimethylation kinetics, the volumes of CD2O and BPC were adjusted to maintain comparable lysine labeling efficiency (≈50%) across all conditions. The labeling reagents were added as follows:

•4 °C: 6 μL 1% CD2O + 90 μL 9 mM BPC.

4 °C: 6 μL 1% CD2O + 90 μL 9 mM BPC.

•25 °C: 2 μL 1% CD2O + 30 μL 9 mM BPC.

25 °C: 2 μL 1% CD2O + 30 μL 9 mM BPC.

•37 °C: 1.34 μL 1% CD2O + 20 μL 9 mM BPC.

37 °C: 1.34 μL 1% CD2O + 20 μL 9 mM BPC.

Reactions were allowed to proceed for 30 min at the respective temperatures. Subsequent sample processing, including quenching, precipitation, and digestion, followed the identical procedure described in Section 2.2.2. The same workflow was applied for TEPP-46 (5 μM).

DT-TRAP analysis of staurosporine and aspirin

Staurosporine and aspirin were analyzed using the DT-TRAP workflow. For each compound, three concentrations (10, 20, and 50 μM) were tested across three incubation temperatures (4, 25, and 37 °C). K562 cell lysates (100 μL, 1 μg/μL) were incubated with staurosporine for 1 h at the indicated temperature, followed by TRAP labeling for 30 min at the same temperature. Aspirin was processed using the same workflow, except that HeLa cell lysates were used instead of K562 cell lysates.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Orbitrap eclipse tribrid

Lyophilized peptides from TEPP-46 and staurosporine experiments were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid and loaded onto an Acclaim PepMap RSLC column (75 μm × 250 mm, 2 μm, 100 Å; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Separation was performed at a flow rate of 300 nL/min using a 120 min gradient, comprising a linear increase from 3% to 38% B over 98 min, followed by a high-organic wash and equilibration.

Proteomic analysis was conducted on an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer coupled to an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). The instrument was operated in data independent acquisition (DIA) mode with the following settings: MS1 scans were acquired across m/z 400−1200 at a resolution of 60,000, with an AGC target of 4 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. MS2 scans involved higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation (normalized collision energy (NCE), 34%) at a resolution of 30,000, with an AGC target of 5 × 104 and a maximum injection time of 54 ms. Specific isolation window settings are detailed in Table S1.

ZenoTOF 7600+

Lyophilized peptides from geldanamycin experiments and temperature-optimization datasets were analyzed using a ZenoTOF 7600+ mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Samples were loaded onto a PepMap Neo trap column (300 μm × 5 mm, 5 μm C18; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 5 μL/min and equilibrated for 4 min. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) M-class system (ACQUITY, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a capillary analytical column (75 μm × 200 mm, 1.9 μm C18; Dr. Maisch GmbH, Ammerbuch, Germany). Mobile phases A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) were delivered at 300 nL/min. The elution gradient was programmed as follows: 0–3 min, 4%–7% B; 3–48 min, 7%–28% B; 48–63 min, 28%–35% B; 63–70 min, 35%–55% B; 70–75 min, 55%–90% B; 75–80 min, 90% B (hold); 80–81 min, 90%–4% B; and 81–90 min, 4% B (equilibration). Zeno SWATH DIA were performed across an m/z range of 400–1200, with isolation window schemes provided in Table S1.

TRAP data processing

Proteomic database searching

Raw data files (Thermo RAW and SCIEX WIFF) were processed using Spectronaut v18 via a direct search against the UniProt human reference proteome (release 2023.10; https://www.uniprot.org/). Searches specified trypsin digestion with a maximum of two missed cleavages allowed. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine (+57.0215 Da) was set as a fixed modification. Variable modifications included methionine oxidation (+15.9949 Da), as well as lysine monomethylation (+16.0282 Da) and dimethylation (+32.0564 Da) derived from CD2O labeling. For label-free quantification (LFQ), precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively, with a retention time alignment tolerance of 1 min. Peptide identifications were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1%.

Peptide classification

Identified lysine-containing peptides were categorized into three distinct classes [1]: Type A, peptides containing internal lysines with non-terminal methyl labeling; Type B, peptides containing lysines without methyl labeling; and Type C, peptides containing lysines immediately preceded by another lysine residue. In cases of overlapping features, assignment to Type A was prioritized.

Filtering criteria and statistical analysis

Peptides were considered significantly regulated if they satisfied two criteria: (i) a statistical significance of P < 0.001, determined by Student's t-test (for two-group comparisons) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test (for multiple comparisons); and (ii) an absolute fold change ≥2 (treated/control). Statistical analyses were performed using R (https://www.r-project.org) and GraphPad Prism 8. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with sample sizes (n = 3 unless otherwise stated) indicated in the figure legends. Significance levels are denoted as: not significant, P > 0.05; ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

TRAP score calculation

To integrate effect size and statistical confidence into a single metric, a “TRAP score” was calculated for each peptide quantifying the ligand-induced accessibility change. The score is defined as:

TRAP score incorporates two components: (i) the log2 fold change (magnitude of accessibility change); (ii) the P-value (statistical significance). For protein-level analysis, all mapped lysine-containing peptides were evaluated, and the peptide with the highest TRAP score was selected as the representative feature for that protein.

Classification of target types in TRAP analysis

Proteins identified by TRAP were categorized into three classes—true targets, off-targets, and nonspecific binders—based on their dose-response behavior, reproducibility, and consistency. True targets were defined as proteins exhibiting reproducible, monotonic dose-dependent accessibility changes that aligned with the specific binding kinetics or the primary pharmacological pathway. Off-targets were defined as proteins showing reproducible accessibility changes but lacking a clear mechanistic link to the ligand. Proteins displaying non-monotonic or irreproducible changes were classified as non-specific binders, attributed to experimental noise or transient accessibility fluctuations [10,11].

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed using the deep learning-based model DiffDock [26] (https://build.nvidia.com/mit/diffdock). For each protein-ligand complex, 20 candidate binding poses were generated. Ligand conformations were refined using 18 diffusion inference steps, applied along a diffusion trajectory discretized into 20 diffusion time divisions, allowing iterative optimization of ligand positioning within the predicted binding pocket and sufficient sampling of conformational space. The top-ranked pose (highest confidence score) was selected for visualization and downstream structural analysis.

Results and discussion

Dose-responsive TRAP identifies the true target of the model compound TEPP-46

To assess the performance of the dose-responsive TRAP strategy in complex proteomes, we utilized TEPP-46 as a model compound (Fig. S1A). TEPP-46 is a highly selective small molecule known to non-covalently bind PKM2, promoting its transition from a dimeric to a tetrameric state [[27], [28], [29]]. HeLa cell lysates were treated with increasing concentrations of TEPP-46 (1 nM–10 μM), followed by TRAP labeling and DIA-MS analysis [30,31]. This approach enabled the identification of peptides exhibiting altered lysine accessibility relative to solvent controls. Consistent with established target engagement, PKM2 displayed pronounced ligand-induced accessibility changes beginning at a concentration of 1 μM (Fig. 1A). Concurrently, we observed additional protein signals lacking known mechanistic relevance, with their frequency increasing in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1A, and Table S2).

Dose-response analyses revealed that lysine residues K305 and K311 on the target protein PKM2 exhibited the most significant accessibility changes upon TEPP-46 treatment (R2 = 0.9951), demonstrating highly reproducible behavior across three independent biological replicates (Figs. 1B and S1B). We next performed a quantitative comparison with other lysine-containing peptides within PKM2. Among the evaluated sites, the peptide containing K305/K311 showed the most prominent dose-dependent accessibility shift, whereas other regions displayed weaker or negligible changes (Fig. 1C).

To further contextualize these observations, we incorporated structural information from available crystallographic data. In the co-crystal structure of PKM2 (PDB: 3U2Z), TEPP-46 binds in close proximity to the peptide region containing K305/K311, with the K311 residue located approximately 6.6 Å from the ligand. This structural arrangement aligns well with our experimental data, as the peptide exhibiting the altered accessibility overlaps closely with the reported binding pocket [1,29,32] (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these findings confirm the peptide-level resolution of dose-responsive TRAP and its utility in distinguishing specific targets from background signals.

Two- and three-dose analyses confirm the true target of TEPP-46

Previous studies have shown that compounds typically require reaching a threshold concentration before effective binding to their targets becomes apparent [11,27]. In agreement with this, our TEPP-46 dose-responsive experiments revealed significant target engagement primarily at higher concentrations. Based on this observation, we investigated whether reduced-dose designs could be sufficient for reliable target confirmation.

We first examined TRAP experiments performed at two concentrations (1 and 5 μM) in HeLa cell lysates. Under this two-dose condition, although most proteins did not exhibit consistent accessibility changes, a small subset of proteins could not be unambiguously excluded as potential targets despite lacking dose-responsive behavior at other concentrations (Fig. 2A). This indicates that a two-dose design carries an inherent risk of false positives or ambiguity. We next applied a three-dose design (1, 5, and 10 μM) to assess target consistency. A three-dimensional scatter plot revealed that PKM2 was the sole protein exhibiting consistent accessibility changes across all three doses (Fig. 2B). This consistency provides robust evidence that a three-dose design can effectively identify true-positive drug targets.

Subsequent quantitative analysis of all peptides in PKM2 across the three-dose series revealed that the peptide containing K305/K311 displayed the greatest ligand-induced accessibility change (Fig. S1C). Structural analysis further confirmed that TEPP-46 binds near K305/K311 within PKM2 (Fig. 2C, PDB: 3U2Z). Collectively, these results support the conclusion that the three-dose TRAP strategy provides a robust and practical framework for high-resolution drug target discovery.

Dose-responsive TRAP identifies specific targets of geldanamycin

To assess the general applicability of the dose-responsive TRAP strategy, we next evaluated geldanamycin (Fig. S2A), a well-characterized non-covalent HSP90 inhibitor with extensively documented structural interactions [33,34]. Multi-dose TRAP experiments performed in HeLa cell lysates revealed that increasing concentrations of geldanamycin induced dose-dependent accessibility changes across multiple lysine residues. Notably, the most significant accessibility alterations were observed in peptides derived from HSP90 family proteins (Fig. 3A, and Table S3).

Two- and three-dose analyses confirm specific targets of geldanamycin

To further assess the feasibility of reduced-dose strategies for drug target identification, we performed two- and three-dose TRAP analyses of geldanamycin. In the two-dose experiment (5 and 10 μM), only a limited number of proteins exhibited consistent accessibility changes; however, HSP90 family proteins were prominently represented among them (Fig. 4A). When the analysis was extended to three doses, HSP90 family proteins were the only targets consistently identified across all concentrations (Fig. 4B). These observations indicate that employing three doses is sufficient to effectively capture authentic binding targets while minimizing background noise. Quantitative analysis of HSP90AA1 showed that the peptide containing K58 exhibited the largest dose-dependent accessibility change (Fig. 4C and S3A). Similarly, the peptides containing K53 in HSP90AB1 and K126 in TRAP1 also exhibited significant dose-responsive accessibility changes (Figs. 4D, 4E, S3B and S3C).

Benchmarking temperature-responsive TRAP

To optimize the TRAP workflow with respect to incubation temperature, we systematically benchmarked the temperature-responsive TRAP strategy under different thermal conditions. As outlined in the experimental design (Fig. 5A), three incubation temperatures (4, 25, and 37 °C) were evaluated, with both drug treatment and TRAP labeling performed at the corresponding temperature. We first examined whether TRAP labeling efficiency remained consistent across these conditions. The results showed comparable labeling efficiencies across all three temperatures, reaching approximately 50% [1] (Fig. S4A, and Tables S4–S6). Correlation analysis revealed strong sample-to-sample correlation coefficients (R > 0.87) and set-level analysis indicated reproducibility exceeding 80% across conditions (Figs. S4B and C). Further quantitative analysis demonstrated that labeled peptide abundances were highly consistent regardless of temperature, as reflected by overlapping medians, standard deviations, and distribution profiles (Fig. S4D). Pairwise comparisons between temperature conditions (25 °C vs. 4 °C; 25 °C vs. 37 °C; 37 °C vs. 4 °C) showed that more than 98% of peptide ratios fell within the range of 0.83–1.2, with no evident directional bias (Fig. S4E). Collectively, these results confirm that lysine labeling efficiency is robust and comparable across all tested temperature conditions.

We next applied temperature-responsive TRAP to two model compounds, geldanamycin and TEPP-46, across the three incubation temperatures to evaluate drug-induced protein accessibility changes. Under all temperature conditions, the corresponding known targets HSP90 family proteins for geldanamycin and PKM2 for TEPP-46 were consistently identified (Figs. 5B and S5A, and Tables S7 and S8). However, comparison across temperatures revealed that incubation at 37 °C was associated with a pronounced increase in nonspecific interactions. For geldanamycin, the number of potential targets beyond those reported in the literature rose from 9 to 24, while for TEPP-46 the number increased from 8 to 51 (Figs. 5C, 5D, S5B and S5C). In contrast, lower incubation temperatures resulted in significantly fewer nonspecific signals. Collectively, these results suggest that elevated incubation temperatures favor nonspecific interactions, whereas performing temperature-responsive TRAP at lower temperatures (e.g., 4 °C) enhances the specificity of authentic drug-target detection.

DT-TRAP strategy identifies the target of the broad-spectrum kinase inhibitor staurosporine

Here, we introduce DT-TRAP, a streamlined framework that integrates a reduced three-dose design with controlled incubation temperatures to improve target-identification specificity. To demonstrate its applicability, we applied DT-TRAP to profile the binding targets of the non-covalent, broad-spectrum kinase inhibitor staurosporine (Fig. S6A). [10,13]. Using a three-dose design (10, 20, and 50 μM) in K562 cell lysates, volcano plot analysis revealed that staurosporine induced widespread accessibility changes across multiple lysine residues, consistent with its promiscuous kinase inhibition profile (Fig. 6A, and Table S9). Notably, DT-TRAP performed at 4 °C yielded a higher proportion of kinase proteins (62.22%) than at 25 °C (43.38%) or 37 °C (33.66%), where increased nonspecific binding was observed (Figs. 6B, and S6B–E). Among the 45 dose-responsive candidate proteins detected at 4 °C, 28 were validated kinases (KinMap: http://kinhub.org/kinmap/, Fig. S6F). In addition, DT-TRAP shared eight kinase targets with the classical drug-target discovery method TPP [10] and nine with the study by Ruan et al. [33], while uniquely identifying 15 additional kinase targets (Fig. S6G).

We further analyzed staurosporine DT-TRAP data obtained at 4 °C, as this condition yielded a higher proportion of kinase proteins and markedly reduced nonspecific binding. Among the identified kinase targets, cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2), a serine/threonine kinase involved in cell cycle progression and transcriptional regulation, was prominently detected [35]. DT-TRAP revealed a consistent accessibility shift at residue K33. Structural analysis confirms that K33 is situated in close proximity to the binding pocket shared by staurosporine and ATP, supporting a competitive binding mechanism (Fig. 6C). [36]. Further analysis of the identified kinases revealed that staurosporine predominantly perturbed lysine accessibility at residues located within or immediately adjacent to known kinase active sites. For example, TRAP peptides showing significant accessibility changes in kinases such as aurora kinase A (AURKA), cAMP-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PRKACA), and dual specificity mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 2 (MAP2K2) were mapped to regions proximal to their ATP/ADP-binding pockets, whereas peptides exhibiting minimal changes were positioned distally from these sites (Figs. 6D–F) [[37], [38], [39]]. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis of the 4 °C staurosporine-interacting kinases (Figs. S7 and S8) showed that most DT-TRAP-identified accessibility-altered lysine residues were located within or proximal to the staurosporine binding pocket. Collectively, these residue-level observations support the conclusion that DT-TRAP can pinpoint ligand-affected structural regions with site-specific resolution, providing mechanistic insights complementary to the global stability changes measured by TPP-based approaches.

DT-TRAP strategy identifies targets of the multi-target drug aspirin

We selected the clinically used drug aspirin, which exhibits both covalent and non-covalent interactions with proteins, as an additional model to evaluate the applicability of DT-TRAP to challenging multi-target compounds (Fig. S9A). DT-TRAP experiments performed at 4 °C captured clear dose-dependent accessibility changes induced by aspirin while maintaining the lowest level of nonspecific binding (Figs. 7A, S9B, and S9C, and Table S10). Based on the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/) and published literature, we identified 13 high-confidence targets from the DT-TRAP candidates, including the classical target prostaglandin G/H synthase 2 (PTGS2), as well as key AMPK-pathway proteins such as cAMP-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PRKACA) and Mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1) (Fig. 7B) [[40], [41], [42], [43]].

KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these targets were mainly enriched in energy metabolism, hormone signaling, and cancer-related pathways, displaying characteristic multi-pathway interactions (Fig. S9D). GO enrichment analysis further indicated their involvement in metabolic regulation, cellular stress responses, transmembrane transport, and cell adhesion (Fig. S9E). We next performed molecular docking analysis of aspirin-interacting proteins identified at 4 °C. For the known target PTGS2, a pronounced accessibility change was observed at residue K432 (Fig. 7C). Similarly, a significant accessibility change was observed at residue K49 of the kinase PRKACA, with docking analysis indicating this site is located in close proximity to the aspirin binding pocket (Fig. 7D). Accessibility-altered peptides from other candidate interacting proteins were likewise positioned close to the predicted aspirin binding sites (Fig. S10). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DT-TRAP is applicable not only to model compounds with well-defined targets but also to complex, multi-target drugs, enabling specific target identification with residue-level mechanistic insight.

Conclusions

In this study, we established an optimized DT-TRAP workflow by systematically evaluating dose and temperature parameters using model compounds with either well-defined targets or broad multi-target profiles, including TEPP-46, geldanamycin, staurosporine, and aspirin. Our results demonstrate that a simplified three-dose design coupled with low-temperature incubation (4 °C) reliably captures bona fide targets while effectively minimizing nonspecific background signals. Although this workflow prioritizes robust and reproducible interactions, it remains adaptable, allowing adjustment of incubation time or dose gradients to accommodate low-affinity or slow-binding ligands. Thus, low-temperature implementation represents a strategic balance between suppressing experimental noise and maintaining high-confidence target identification.

In summary, the optimized DT-TRAP strategy provides a reliable and streamlined framework for drug target discovery. By effectively distinguishing specific engagement from background noise and offering residue-level resolution, this approach broadens the applicability of chemoproteomics in elucidating the mechanism of action for complex pharmacological agents.
