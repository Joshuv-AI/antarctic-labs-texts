Successful management of postoperative tracheal metastasis in small cell lung cancer with radiotherapy and durvalumab: A case report
Authors: Yasoo Sugiura, Hiroyuki Fujimoto, Toshinori Hashizume
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe patient in this report was a 73‐year‐old male who was diagnosed with small cell lung cancer (T3N0M0 stage IIB) after presenting with a 5.5 cm tumor detected in the right lower lobe of the lung by radiography. After right lower lobectomy and lymphadenectomy, postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was administered with cisplatin and etoposide. At 27 months after surgery, the patient complained of wheezing. Tracheal metastasis was identified through computed tomography and bronchoscopy. Biopsy confirmed metastasis of small cell lung cancer. Radiotherapy (60 Gy) was administered to the mediastinal lymph nodes, including the supraclavicular region. The patient then underwent four cycles of carboplatin‐etoposide‐durvalumab, followed by durvalumab maintenance therapy. At 49 months after surgery, the patient complained of discomfort while speaking, leading to the discovery of a nodule in the subglottic space, which confirmed small cell lung cancer metastasis. Radiotherapy (30 Gy) was administered to the larynx and mediastinum, and the patient continued with durvalumab monotherapy. As of 61 months after surgery, he remains recurrence‐free after the second course of radiotherapy. Our favorable outcome could be explained by the synergy between immunotherapy and radiotherapy. Here, we report a rare case of postoperative tracheal metastasis in small cell lung cancer successfully managed with radiotherapy and durvalumab. This shows promise in achieving local disease control and extending survival in postoperative metastatic lesions, highlighting a potential therapeutic approach.Keywords: immune checkpoint inhibitor, radiotherapy, small cell lung cancer, tracheal metastasis

INTRODUCTION

Small cell lung cancer often metastasizes to mediastinal lymph nodes, liver, bone, adrenal, and brain, but tracheal metastasis is rare. 1 Here, we report a case of postoperative small cell lung cancer treated with radiation and durvalumab for tracheal metastasis.

CASE REPORT

A 73‐year‐old male presented with an abnormal chest shadow on chest radiography (Figure 1a). His medical history included diabetes (hemoglobin A1c was 8.9%), hyperlipidemia, and diabetic retinopathy. His pack‐years index was 43 but he quit smoking 10 years ago. A chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a 5.5 cm tumor in the right lower lobe (Figure 1b). Bronchoscopy confirmed a diagnosis of small cell lung cancer. The clinical stage was T3N0M0, stage IIB. He underwent a right lower lobectomy, lymph node dissection, and the pathological stage was T3N0M0, stage IIB. Adjuvant chemotherapy with cisplatin and etoposide was administered. Prophylactic encephalic radiotherapy just after surgery was not performed because the patient did not give consent due to the risk of dementia. Twenty‐seven months after surgery, the patient developed wheezing, and 1 mm slice CT for follow‐up and bronchoscopy revealed a nodule on the trachea at the level of the 10th tracheal cartilage which occluded about 70% of the tracheal lumen. A bronchoscopic biopsy diagnosed the nodule as a tracheal metastasis of small cell lung cancer. There was no continuity with the surrounding mediastinal tissue (Figure 2a, b). The patient was irradiated with 60 Gy from the mediastinal lymph nodes to the supraclavicular lymph node area including the metastatic lesion. For distant metastasis, he received four courses of carboplatin, etoposide, and durvalumab, followed by maintenance durvalumab. Bronchoscopy 4 months after irradiation showed that the nodule had vanished, and normal mucosa could be observed (Figure 2c). Forty‐nine months after surgery, the patient felt discomfort when speaking, and CT and bronchoscopy revealed a nodule in the subglottic space, which was biopsied and diagnosed as small cell lung cancer metastasis (Figure 3). He underwent radiation to the larynx and mediastinum. As of 61 months after surgery, the patient is recurrence‐free since the second course of radiotherapy.

DISCUSSION

Airway metastasis in lung cancer is estimated to be 0.44%, while it is rare in small cell carcinoma (0.18%). 2 Kiryu et al. classified tracheal metastases into four types: type I, direct metastasis to the bronchus; type II, bronchial invasion by a parenchymal lesion; type III, bronchial invasion by mediastinal or hilar lymph node metastasis; and type IV, peripheral lesions extended along the proximal bronchus. 3 In this case, there was no continuity with the surrounding tissue and the metastasis was considered to be direct metastasis to the bronchus (type I). A PubMed search for reports of postoperative tracheal metastasis of small cell lung cancer using the keywords “small cell lung cancer” and “tracheal metastasis” revealed five case reports. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 The search results are summarized in Table 1. Including the present case, there were five male cases and one female case. The symptoms were wheezing in three cases, hemoptysis in two cases, cough in one case, dyspnea in one case and one patient was asymptomatic. The median time to postoperative tracheal metastasis was 2 years and 3.5 months. Treatment consisted of bronchoscopic intervention followed by chemoradiation in one patient, chemoradiation in three patients, and radiotherapy alone in two patients. Four patients achieved complete response. Only the present case was treated with an immune checkpoint inhibitor and chemotherapy.

In the CASPIAN trial, treatment of patients with advanced small cell lung cancer with etoposide plus platinum plus durvalumab resulted in a progression‐free interval of 5.1 months and overall survival of 13.0 months. This shows an overall survival improvement of 2–3 months with the addition of immunotherapy to conventional first line therapy. 9 , 10 Platinum‐etoposide chemotherapy and concurrent radiotherapy with the addition of durvalumab has been reported to improve overall survival and progression‐free interval. 10 , 11 In the present case, localized irradiation of tracheal metastases followed by an immune checkpoint inhibitor led to good results. The rationale is the synergistic effect of immunotherapy and radiation. Radiation causes neoantigen release from cancer cells. 10 , 12 In 1953, Mole observed that after local tumor irradiation, distant nonirradiated sites in the same organism regressed. 12 It is possible that this abscopal effect contributed to the favorable progression‐free period in this case.

In conclusion, we experienced a rare case of postoperative airway metastasis of small cell lung cancer. The patient achieved 23 months of recurrence‐free interval with etoposide, platinum and durvalumab after radiotherapy for tracheal metastasis, and 12 months of disease control with radiotherapy and maintenance durvalumab for a second tracheal metastasis. The combination of radiation therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors may contribute to local control of metastatic disease and prolong prognosis after surgery for small cell lung cancer.
