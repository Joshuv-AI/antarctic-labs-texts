Effects of temperature, brine, and inhibitor chemistry on emulsion stability in gas condensate systems under pipeline mixing conditions
Authors: Mohammad Amin Behnam Motlagh, Amir Hossein Saeedi Dehaghani, Hadi Tanhaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn oil and gas production, the coexistence of water and condensate in subsea pipelines often leads to emulsion formation, complicating separation processes and increasing operational challenges in flow assurance. Emulsions can be stabilized or destabilized depending on temperature, salinity, inhibitor chemistry, and water cut, yet their combined effects remain poorly understood. This study investigates the emulsification tendency and phase behavior of South Pars condensates (SPD10 and SPD15) under pipeline-representative mixing. Experiments were conducted at condensate-to-water ratios of 4:1 and 2:2, across four temperatures (12, 30, 45, and 70 °C). Samples were agitated with controlled high-shear pulses, and separated water and emulsion volumes were monitored at different settling times. Results show that at low temperature (12 °C), corrosion inhibitor (CI) with brine produced large but short-lived emulsions (~ 165 mL at 5 min decreasing to ~ 71 mL at 1 h), while kinetic hydrate inhibitor alone did not generate stable emulsions. At higher water cuts (2:2), CI with brine generated much larger emulsions, up to ~ 195 mL initially and ~ 20 mL remaining after 24 h, compared with rapid collapse when deionized water was used. At 70 °C, emulsions diminished and sludge-like interfacial layers dominated. Thermal path also influenced stability, as heating produced larger emulsions than cooling at the same set point. MEG/MDEA blends acted as non-emulsifiers but consistently produced sludge. Overall, the findings highlight CI–brine interactions as the main driver of emulsion and sludge formation. Introduction

The formation and stability of emulsions in oil and gas production systems remain critical challenges in flow assurance. When hydrocarbon condensates and aqueous phases coexist in subsea pipelines, intense mixing and the presence of surface-active compounds often lead to the formation of oil–water emulsions. These emulsions are problematic because they increase pressure drop, complicate downstream separation, and may interfere with hydrate and corrosion management strategies1. The stability of such emulsions depends on multiple parameters, including temperature, water cut, brine salinity, and the chemistry of additives such as corrosion inhibitors (CIs) and kinetic hydrate inhibitors (KHIs). Corrosion inhibitors, while essential for protecting pipeline integrity, are often amphiphilic and can promote emulsion stability by reducing interfacial tension and forming rigid interfacial films2. Conversely, kinetic hydrate inhibitors are primarily designed to delay hydrate nucleation and growth but may also interact with the oil–water interface. Despite extensive studies on individual parameters, the combined effects of temperature, brine composition, and inhibitors on emulsion stability in gas–condensate systems remain poorly understood.

Recent research has highlighted that high brine salinity enhances emulsion persistence due to ion–surfactant interactions, while elevated temperatures tend to destabilize emulsions, leading to phase separation or formation of sludge-like interfacial deposits3,4. Moreover, thermal history, whether the system undergoes heating or cooling, can significantly affect droplet coalescence and film drainage. However, systematic experimental investigations under conditions representative of offshore pipeline environments are still scarce. Taheri et al. (2020) investigated the effect of hydrate and corrosion inhibitors on water–hydrocarbon emulsification in South Pars gas pipelines. Experiments were performed at two representative temperatures (12°C and 70°C) using condensate and distilled water with commercial inhibitors. Their findings demonstrated that KHI alone did not generate emulsions, and rapid phase separation occurred. In contrast, the presence of CI promoted stable emulsions, with volumes persisting even after 24 h. Interestingly, when KHI and CI were applied simultaneously, the emulsions formed were less stable than those with CI alone, indicating an antagonistic effect. Temperature strongly influenced phase behavior: at 70 °C, emulsions destabilized faster and separation was more efficient compared with 12 °C, where CI-driven emulsions were highly stable5. Abdulredha6 investigated the general mechanisms underpinning emulsion formation in petroleum systems. The study emphasized that asphaltenes and resins, even in low concentrations, act as potent natural emulsifiers due to their amphiphilic nature, increasing emulsion viscosity and making separation processes more challenging. This underscores the complexity of crude systems, where inherent surface-active components contribute significantly to emulsion persistence6. Almashwali et al.7 reviewed hydrate formation and mitigation behaviors in oil-dominated multiphase systems. They highlighted that water cut, oil volume fraction, and hydrate formers critically influence hydrate tendencies, and consequently emulsion behavior and flow assurance strategy design7. Fakhreeva et al.8 provided a thorough review on the development of green KHIs based on biopolymers such as polysaccharides. These novel KHIs offer biodegradable alternatives with both thermodynamic and kinetic inhibition properties. The inclusion of such green inhibitors brings new potential to minimize environmental impact while managing hydrate formation, insights that could inspire future directions for inhibitor selection in emulsion‑prone systems8. Xu et al.9 studied the stability and microstructural characteristics of high internal phase water-in-oil (HIP W/O) emulsions prepared as compound collectors for fine coal flotation. Their findings showed that when the aqueous phase volume fraction was increased to 90%, the emulsions exhibited optimal stability, with water separation rates as low as ~ 2% after 30 days. The addition of sodium chloride (0.3 mol/L) and gelatin (7 g/L) created a three-dimensional gel-like network within the droplets, which enhanced viscosity, suppressed coalescence, and effectively delayed Ostwald ripening. These results underline that designing emulsions with optimized stabilizer systems and controlled internal phase fractions can significantly improve both performance and sustainability in industrial applications9.

Kumar et al.1 showed that heating and chemical demulsifiers play critical roles in accelerating water separation in crude oil emulsions, demonstrating that temperature directly affects interfacial film drainage and droplet coalescence. These findings emphasize that thermal and chemical controls are inseparable in predicting emulsion behavior. The ionic composition of the aqueous phase plays a decisive role in determining interfacial behavior1.

Mahmoudvand et al.10 reported that the presence of divalent cations such as Ca2+ and Mg2+ enhances emulsion persistence by altering the interfacial tension and increasing the rigidity of the interfacial film. The ions compress the electrical double layer and promote cross-linking between surfactant head groups, thereby stabilizing the dispersed droplets10. Similarly, Liu et al.11 highlighted the role of ionic interactions and polymer chain flexibility in determining the adsorption behavior of inhibitors at oil–water interfaces. Their results confirm that small variations in molecular architecture, such as chain length, head-group polarity, and degree of ionization can drastically alter interfacial tension, droplet coalescence, and emulsion rheology11.

The coupling between emulsion stability and hydrate formation has also been recognized as a critical issue in flow assurance. Tang et al.12 demonstrated that hydrate inhibitors and corrosion inhibitors interact synergistically or competitively depending on their molecular composition and dosage. At low temperatures, stable emulsions reduce the gas–water contact area, thus kinetically delaying hydrate nucleation. However, excessive emulsification may trap water and inhibitors within viscous sludge layers, complicating restart operations and separation efficiency. This dual behavior highlights the need for balanced inhibitor formulation and dosage optimization12.

In W/O emulsion systems, wax–surfactant coupling can strongly modify hydrate behavior by changing the emulsion microstructure. When wax and Span 80 coexist, a protective wax shell may fully encapsulate water droplets and restrict gas diffusion into the aqueous phase, which delays hydrate nucleation. This coupled “wrapping” can be stronger than the individual effect of wax or Span 80 alone. After nucleation, fully encapsulated hydrate particles show reduced collisional aggregation, limiting large agglomerate formation13.

Recent analysis also indicates that mass-transfer hindrance can inhibit hydrate nucleation but does not necessarily prevent hydrate formation even when a wax shell is fully formed. Moreover, the anti-aggregation performance of emulsifier-like additives depends on their dosage. Aggregation may still occur when emulsifier content is insufficient or when water content is high, even in the presence of both wax and emulsifier. In practice, many emulsifier-type chemicals are also applied as anti-agglomerants (AAs), i.e., they primarily mitigate plugging risk by reducing hydrate particle adhesion and agglomeration rather than universally preventing hydrate formation. Accordingly, hydrate-related effects are discussed here in terms of microstructure and mass-transfer considerations, while direct hydrate-kinetics quantification is beyond the scope of this work14.

Temperature further dictates the phase behavior of emulsions and sludge formation. As reported by Kokal3,4 and Kilpatrick3,4, increasing temperature generally destabilizes emulsions by reducing viscosity and weakening interfacial films. However, in the presence of strong surfactants or multivalent ions, high temperatures can lead to the aggregation of surface-active species and formation of dense interfacial sludge3,4. Such sludge deposits can adhere to pipeline walls and significantly impact fluid flow, as also noted by Tanhaei and Saeedi Dehaghani15 in related studies on asphaltene aggregation under thermal and chemical stimuli. Therefore, the transition from emulsion to sludge with increasing temperature represents a key mechanism that determines the long-term flow behavior in gas–condensate systems15.

Classical emulsion formation has traditionally been associated with heavy or asphaltenic crudes because of their abundance of natural surfactants such as asphaltenes, resins, and polar aromatics. However, recent laboratory and field studies have demonstrated that gas-condensate systems, although largely devoid of asphaltenes, can also generate stable and voluminous emulsions when specific physicochemical conditions are satisfied, particularly in the presence of corrosion inhibitors (CIs) and saline brines under turbulent flow regimes.

Our experiments with South Pars condensates (SPD10 and SPD15) confirm these findings. Under subsea-relevant temperatures (12–30 °C) and high-shear mixing, emulsions with volumes up to ≈ 195 mL were observed, whereas at elevated temperatures these emulsions transitioned into sludge-like interfacial layers. The mechanism is attributed to CI–brine interactions, in which amphiphilic inhibitor molecules adsorb at the oil–water interface to form viscoelastic films reinforced by ionic bridging with multivalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+) present in brine5,10,11.

Turbulent flow in gas-condensate lines generally exhibits Reynolds numbers exceeding 105, corresponding to shear rates of 103–105 s⁻1, depending on flow velocity (1–5 m s-1), internal diameter (0.1–0.3 m), and phase viscosities. The associated energy-dissipation rates of 0.01–1 W/kg are comparable to laboratory mixing intensities of 5000–25,000 rpm in small-volume setups. These values confirm that the experimental shear conditions are sufficient to produce fine, transient, and occasionally stable emulsions consistent with field observations16.

Under such subsea pipeline conditions, multiphase flow turbulence induces interfacial renewal and droplet fragmentation rates high enough to sustain emulsions whose persistence depends strongly on inhibitor chemistry and brine salinity.

Regarding emulsion type, both water-in-oil (W/O) and oil-in-water (O/W) structures may transiently coexist; however, water-in-condensate (W/O) emulsions are consistently dominant under subsea flow conditions. This predominance arises from the low viscosity and large volume fraction of the hydrocarbon phase. Emulsion stabilization is governed primarily by amphiphilic corrosion inhibitors and trace aromatic components (toluene, xylene, naphthalene), in agreement with previous studies3.

One of the main objectives of this study is to address this knowledge gap by examining the emulsification tendency and stability of South Pars condensates (SPD10 and SPD15) under pipeline-relevant mixing. The effects of temperature, brine salinity, inhibitor type, and water cut on emulsion formation and interfacial sludge are investigated through controlled laboratory experiments. The findings are expected to provide insights for inhibitor screening and operational strategies in offshore gas production, thereby improving flow assurance design and risk mitigation.

This study provides an integrated and comprehensive assessment of the key parameters controlling emulsion formation and stability in gas-condensate systems under pipeline-representative shear conditions. Unlike previous investigations that examined the effects of temperature, brine salinity, or inhibitor chemistry separately, the present work systematically evaluates their coupled and interacting influences on emulsion behavior. The findings reveal that temperature governs the transition between emulsions and sludge, while ion–inhibitor interactions, particularly involving multivalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+), enhance interfacial film rigidity and prolong emulsion persistence. At higher temperatures, these same interactions promote interfacial aggregation and sludge formation. This mechanistic understanding not only advances the fundamental knowledge of emulsion stability in asphaltene-free condensates but also provides practical insights for flow assurance by offering a predictive framework to assess emulsion and sludge risks. Overall, the results highlight the novelty, scientific contribution, and operational significance of this study for inhibitor design and subsea gas-condensate management.

Experimental section

All experimental procedures were designed to simulate pipeline-relevant conditions for emulsion formation in gas–condensate systems. The study employed condensate samples (SPD10 and SPD15) from the South Pars gas field, mixed with deionized water or brine at controlled ratios. Tests were conducted under systematically varied temperatures and mixing intensities to evaluate the effects of inhibitors, salinity, and thermal history on emulsion formation and stability.

Materials

Both SPD10 and SPD15 condensates were selected from the same field to ensure chemical comparability and reduce compositional uncertainty during mechanistic evaluation. Their close similarity allowed us to isolate the roles of temperature, water cut, inhibitor type, and salinity as the dominant operational factors controlling emulsion behavior.

Two representative condensate–brine systems were sampled from the South Pars gas field, namely SPD10 and SPD15. For each system, both the gas condensate and the associated brine were collected to capture field-representative hydrocarbon and aqueous phase compositions. Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted using a laboratory setup designed to reproduce mixing and settling conditions representative of subsea pipelines. The system consisted of a thermostatic water bath (± 0.1 °C) to maintain the required temperature range (12–70 °C), a high-shear mixer (IKA Ultra-Turrax T25, Germany) capable of producing up to 25,000 rpm, and graduated glass separation cylinders (250 mL, ISO standard) for visual observation and quantitative measurement of emulsion and separated water phases. A digital tachometer and timer were used to control the agitation speed and duration, while thermocouple probes connected to a data logger continuously monitored temperature throughout mixing and settling. The apparatus was arranged to ensure that mixing was performed under controlled shear conditions and that samples could be immediately transferred to calibrated cylinders without disturbance for phase separation analysis19.

All separation cylinders were carefully sealed using Teflon-lined glass stoppers immediately after mixing to prevent vapor loss. The total sample volume was kept constant at 200 mL with a minimal headspace (< 2 mL). Temperature was maintained in a closed thermostatic bath (± 0.1 °C), minimizing any condensation or vaporization at elevated temperatures (up to 70 °C). Post-experiment volume checks after 24 h and 48 h showed deviations within ± 1 mL (< 0.5%), confirming negligible evaporation. Experimental procedure

For each experiment, a total volume of 200 mL was prepared by mixing condensate (SPD10 or SPD15) with the corresponding aqueous phase (deionized water or field brine). Two condensate-to-water ratios were investigated, namely 4:1 (low water cut) and 2:2 (high water cut). Depending on the test condition, the aqueous phase contained either CI, KHI (PVCAP), MEG, or MDEA. After loading into the mixing vessel, the samples were subjected to a 30-s high-shear pulse at 10,000 rpm and immediately transferred into calibrated separation cylinders, which were placed in the thermostatic bath at the desired test temperature (12, 30, 45, or 70 °C). Phase behavior was monitored at defined time intervals (5 min, 1 h, 6 h, and 24 h), and the volumes of emulsion and separated water were recorded along with visual observations of interfacial sludge layers. An example of the observed emulsion structure in the SPD10 + corrosion inhibitor + deionized water system after 24 h at 12 °C (4:1 ratio) is shown in Fig. 2. For additional representative physical images of the various test states, please refer to the Supplementary Information (Figs. S1–S15).

Emulsion layer observed in SPD10 + corrosion inhibitor + deionized water system after 24 h at 12 °C (4:1 ratio).

To isolate thermal‐history effects, three test modes were applied uniformly. In the fresh-at-target mode, the filled vessel was equilibrated at 12, 30, 45, or 70 °C (± 0.5 °C) for 10 min and then subjected to a single 30 s high-shear pulse at 10,000 rpm, followed by immediate transfer to calibrated separation cylinders held at the same temperature. In the cooling path, the vessel was pre-equilibrated at 70 °C for 30 min without shear (gentle 2–3 s inversions every 5 min), sheared once for 30 s at 70 °C, and rapidly transferred to the lower-temperature bath (e.g., 12 °C); the first reading 5 min after transfer is reported as the 5-min point. In the heating path, the vessel was pre-equilibrated at 12 °C for 30 min without shear, sheared once for 30 s at 12 °C, and immediately moved to 45 or 70 °C for monitoring. In all cases, only one high-shear event was used per test; temperatures were controlled to ± 0.5 °C with a thermostatic bath, and a thermocouple in a dummy vessel verified liquid temperature during preconditioning and shear. Volumes of separated water and emulsion, and the presence of interfacial sludge, were recorded at 5 min, 1 h, 6 h, and 24 h.

The chosen mixing speed (10,000 rpm) and duration (30 s) were selected to replicate the average shear intensity observed in turbulent pipeline flow. Based on established correlations this speed generates shear rates of 104–105 s-1, equivalent to energy dissipation rates between 0.01 and 1 W/kg. Preliminary tests showed that longer mixing (> 60 s) produced no significant change in emulsion volume but slightly increased temperature (~ 1–2 °C). Thus, 30 s was chosen as optimal to ensure homogeneity without thermal distortion16.

Each experiment was performed in duplicate (n = 3) to ensure reproducibility, and results are reported as mean ± SD. A high-shear mixer was employed as a controlled simplification to reproduce the average shear intensity characteristic of turbulent flow while maintaining experimental repeatability. This design isolates the primary effects of temperature, salinity, and inhibitor chemistry, but does not capture turbulence intermittency, slug-flow dynamics, or oscillatory pressure typical of real pipelines. Future studies should therefore utilize loop-flow or dynamic multiphase facilities capable of reproducing turbulence spectra and slug structures to more accurately assess emulsion evolution under field-representative conditions. Statistical analysis

Each experimental condition was measured with n = 3 independent replicates due to material availability and instrument-time constraints. where is the sample mean, is the sample standard deviation, and is the critical t-value at a 95% confidence level with one degree of freedom. To visually represent variability under small sample sizes, individual replicate values are overlaid on all graphical summaries (bar charts, line plots, and scatter plots). For key pairwise comparisons, Hedges’ g (small-sample corrected effect size) and its confidence intervals are reported, providing an estimation-based interpretation of magnitude and direction of differences. Formal null-hypothesis significance testing (e.g., t-tests or ANOVA) was not performed, as n = 3 per condition does not meet the statistical assumptions for these procedures. Instead, results are interpreted through estimation statistics, emphasizing the uncertainty and effect sizes rather than binary significance20.

Results and discussion

This section presents and analyzes the experimental observations on emulsion formation and stability under varying operating conditions. The results are organized to highlight the role of temperature, water cut, brine salinity, and inhibitor type on phase behavior. Quantitative measurements of emulsion volume, separated water, and interfacial sludge formation are reported at different settling times, followed by interpretation in the context of flow assurance. Comparisons between SPD10 and SPD15 condensates are also made to assess the influence of condensate composition on emulsion tendency.

To provide a mechanistic framework beyond descriptive trends, the observed responses are interpreted using basic interfacial and kinetic principles. Emulsification in these W/O systems is governed by (i) interfacial adsorption and film formation by surface-active species (primarily CI, and mixed CI + KHI layers), which can lower interfacial tension and generate mechanically resilient interfacial films; (ii) drainage and coalescence kinetics, which are accelerated by increasing temperature through reduced viscosity and faster film thinning; and (iii) electrolyte effects in brine, where ionic screening and possible ion-bridging can strengthen interfacial packing and suppress coalescence, thereby increasing emulsion persistence. When emulsions are not sustained, surface-active or polymeric residues can accumulate and compact at the interface, producing sludge-like deposits as an alternative interfacial outcome. In the following subsections, each operating parameter is discussed in terms of how it shifts the balance between interfacial-film strength, droplet–droplet collision/coalescence, and interfacial accumulation, using the measured emulsion-layer and separated-water volumes (at 5 min, 1 h, and 24 h) as practical indicators for these physicochemical processes.

Effect of temperature on emulsion formation

To systematically evaluate the influence of temperature on emulsion behavior, a series of tests were conducted with SPD10 condensate at a 4:1 condensate-to-water ratio using either deionized water or brine as the aqueous phase. Six experimental conditions were examined: (i) fresh samples at 12 °C, (ii) fresh samples at 70 °C, (iii) fresh samples at 30 °C, (iv) preconditioned samples cooled to 12 °C, (v) samples heated to 45 °C, and (vi) preconditioned samples heated to 70 °C. For each condition, tests were performed in the presence and absence of inhibitors (CI, KHI, and CI + KHI). The evolution of emulsion and sludge-like interfacial layers was monitored at 5 min, 1 h, and 24 h. All data represent the mean ± SD (n = 3), with 95% confidence intervals (CI) estimated from the distribution . Individual replicate values are shown in the figures to illustrate within-condition variability.

At 12 °C (fresh samples, 4:1 ratio), systems containing only condensate and water (deionized or brine) or KHI alone showed negligible emulsion formation. However, in the presence of CI, a substantial emulsion layer formed immediately after mixing (165 ± 12 mL, 95% CI: 148–182 mL; n = 3) but declined to nearly half after 1 h (83 ± 10 mL, 95% CI: 69–97 mL). The combination of CI + KHI produced more persistent emulsions, especially in brine, where residual layers were still visible after 48 h. In contrast, emulsions in deionized water collapsed completely within two weeks. These observations confirm the stabilizing influence of salinity under low-temperature conditions (For additional quantitative comparison, see Supplementary Fig. S16).

At 12 °C with SPD10 (4:1 ratio), the comparative bar charts (Fig. 3a,b) clearly differentiate the fresh and preconditioned behaviors. In fresh samples, water separation is generally higher while the emulsion layer is thinner, particularly for systems without CI (e.g., blank/KHI-only), indicating limited interfacial stabilization. By contrast, preconditioned samples show systematically lower separated water volumes (Fig. 3a) accompanied by thicker emulsion layers (Fig. 3b), most notably in CI-containing formulations (with or without KHI) and in brine. This trend suggests that repeated cooling enhances the persistence of the interfacial film and slows droplet coalescence, thereby retaining more water within the emulsion phase. Mechanistically, cooling preconditioning can promote stronger adsorption/organization of surface-active CI components at the oil–water interface and increase the contribution of low-temperature structuring (e.g., viscosity increase and/or waxy/solid-like interfacial networks in condensate), which collectively restrict drainage and coalescence. As a result, the system favors emulsion retention at the expense of rapid water release under low-temperature conditions (Fig. 3).

Comparative bar charts of water separation and emulsion volume for SPD10 condensate at 4:1 condensate-to-water ratio under different conditions. (a) Water volume in fresh samples at 12 °C. (b) Emulsion volume in preconditioned samples at 12 °C.

At 70 °C (fresh samples), emulsions were transient and short-lived. Only the CI + KHI + brine system produced detectable emulsions at 5 min (22 ± 4 mL, 95% CI: 16–28 mL) that vanished within hours. Instead, sludge-like interfacial films appeared, particularly in brine systems, remaining visible for at least 1 h (Fig. 4). This indicates that at elevated temperatures, interfacial films collapse into dense sludge rather than extended emulsions (For additional quantitative comparison, see Supplementary Fig. S17).

Condensate and brine samples from SPD10 with corrosion inhibitor after 1 h at 70 °C.

At 70 °C with SPD10 (4:1 ratio), the bar charts indicate that separated water volumes are broadly comparable between fresh and preconditioned samples across all formulations (Fig. 5a), whereas the emulsion volume is essentially negligible in nearly all cases (Fig. 5b). This quantitative pattern corroborates the visual observation that elevated temperature strongly suppresses emulsion persistence and shifts the system toward rapid phase separation. Mechanistically, higher temperature reduces continuous-phase viscosity and accelerates droplet coalescence and drainage, while weakening the interfacial viscoelastic film formed by surface-active CI components. As a result, dispersed water droplets cannot remain kinetically trapped as a stable emulsion, and the interfacial material tends to contract into thin, localized sludge-like films (most apparent in CI- and brine-containing systems) rather than forming an extended emulsion layer. Overall, the 70 °C data confirm a temperature-driven transition from emulsion-dominated retention at low temperature to separation/sludge-dominated behavior at elevated temperature (Fig. 5).

(a) Water volume in fresh and preconditioned samples at 70 °C. (b) Emulsion volume in fresh and preconditioned samples at 70 °C.

At 30 °C, behavior resembled that at 12 °C: CI promoted thick emulsions (148 ± 8 mL, 95% CI 137–159 mL) that diminished after 1 h (90 ± 12 mL, 95% CI 73–107 mL). Deionized-water systems showed partial water separation after 1 h, whereas brine-containing systems retained emulsions more effectively. Over 72 h, CI-only + DI systems mostly collapsed, while CI + KHI + brine systems maintained both emulsions and an interfacial sludge layer (For additional quantitative comparison, see Supplementary Fig. S18).

At intermediate temperatures (30 °C and 45 °C, 4:1 ratio), the bar charts for preconditioned samples (Fig. 6a,b) show a clear temperature-controlled trade-off between water separation and emulsion retention. As temperature increases from 12 °C to 30 °C and 45 °C, the separated water volume increases systematically (Fig. 6a; T45 > T30 > T12), while the emulsion volume decreases in the opposite order (Fig. 6b; T12 > T30 > T45). This trend is most pronounced in CI-containing formulations (with or without KHI), and it is generally amplified in brine relative to deionized water, consistent with salinity-enhanced interfacial stabilization. Mechanistically, moderate heating reduces the viscosity of the continuous phase and weakens the interfacial film, accelerating drainage and coalescence, whereas salinity can strengthen the interfacial layer and suppress coalescence by modifying interfacial adsorption and electrostatic interactions. Therefore, emulsion persistence at intermediate temperatures reflects a competition between thermal destabilization and salinity-assisted stabilization, producing progressively less retained emulsions as temperature rises (Fig. 6).

The cooling path (preconditioned to 12 °C) revealed that samples cooled from higher temperatures formed initially larger emulsions (180 ± 15 mL, 95% CI: 159–201 mL) but with faster decay over 24 h compared to freshly prepared ones. Conversely, the heating path (45 °C) generated smaller initial emulsions (120 ± 9 mL, 95% CI: 107–133 mL) that mostly disappeared within 1 h, except for the CI + brine system, where a thin but persistent emulsion (28 ± 5 mL) and visible sludge deposits remained at the interface (For additional quantitative comparison, see Supplementary Fig. S18)15.

At 70 °C (heating path), no stable emulsions persisted, confirming the destabilizing role of temperature. Nevertheless, sludge layers were evident in CI + brine and CI + KHI + brine systems after 5 min and persisted up to 24 h. Sludge in deionized-water systems was weaker and disappeared within 24 h, reaffirming the synergistic role of brine salinity with CI at high temperatures (See Supplementary Tables S1–S3, and Fig. S19).

Overall, these results clearly demonstrate that temperature critically governs emulsion fate in gas–condensate systems. At lower temperatures (12–30 °C), CI acts as an emulsifier producing voluminous, though transient, emulsions, especially in brine. As temperature increases (45–70 °C), emulsions collapse but sludge-like residues form, highlighting the dual role of CI as both an emulsion promoter and sludge inducer, and the key stabilizing influence of brine salinity (See Supplementary Fig. S18). Quantitative uncertainty analysis (mean ± SD, 95% CI) underscores that these trends are consistent across replicate measurements despite the limited replication (n = 3)21.

The contrasting behaviors observed between heating and cooling paths can be explained by the interplay of interfacial thermodynamics and inhibitor adsorption kinetics. During cooling, the rapid decrease in temperature increases the viscosity of both the condensate and the aqueous phase, reducing molecular mobility and slowing the desorption of surface-active components (mainly CI molecules) from the interface. This transient kinetic trapping leads to the formation of large, metastable emulsions with high initial volume but poor long-term stability, as the rigid films eventually relax and coalesce upon standing.

Conversely, during heating, the gradual temperature increase enhances the diffusion of inhibitors and accelerates rearrangement within interfacial films. This promotes selective adsorption and reorganization of amphiphilic inhibitor molecules, resulting in fewer but more stable droplets with stronger interfacial elasticity. The higher temperature also facilitates film dehydration and polymer aggregation at the interface, promoting the formation of sludge-like residues instead of dispersed emulsions.

These mechanisms collectively explain the observed hysteresis between heating and cooling cycles. Cooling favors rapid emulsification through kinetic trapping, whereas heating promotes film restructuring and sludge deposition. Such temperature-path dependence is consistent with emulsion-transition behaviors reported for surfactant- and asphaltene-rich crude-oil systems, reinforcing the generality of the observed trends22.

In contrast, CI-containing formulations shift the balance toward emulsion retention (Fig. 8a–c), with the strongest effect observed at low temperature (12 °C) and at early times (5 min–1 h), where large emulsion layers form while the corresponding separated-water volumes remain comparatively lower (Figs. 7 vs 8). Mechanistically, these temperature trends reflect competing effects on interfacial-film strength and bulk-phase transport. At low temperature, CI components can adsorb at the oil–water interface and form a more resilient interfacial film, while higher viscosity of the continuous phase slows drainage and coalescence, enabling water droplets to remain kinetically trapped as a stable emulsion (Fig. 8). Brine generally enhances this stabilization relative to deionized water by strengthening interfacial adsorption/packing and reducing coalescence efficiency, which sustains higher emulsion volumes and delays water release over time (Figs. 7 and 8). With increasing temperature, viscosity decreases and coalescence/drainage accelerate, while interfacial viscoelastic films weaken and can no longer withstand droplet collisions, thereby promoting separation and suppressing persistent emulsification (Figs. 7 and 8).

Mechanistically, the paired trends in Figs. 7 and 8 can be rationalized by the competition between interfacial-film strength and temperature-controlled drainage/coalescence kinetics. At low temperature, higher continuous-phase viscosity slows film thinning and droplet coalescence, enabling kinetically trapped water droplets to persist as a bulk emulsion when a sufficiently resilient interfacial layer is present. This is most evident for CI-containing formulations, where amphiphilic CI components adsorb at the oil–water interface and form a viscoelastic film that resists rupture during droplet–droplet collisions, yielding high emulsion retention at 12 °C (Fig. 8) and correspondingly reduced rapid water release (Fig. 7). As temperature increases, viscosity decreases and molecular mobility increases, which accelerates film drainage and coalescence and weakens the kinetic stabilization window; consequently, emulsion volumes collapse and separated-water volumes rise systematically from 30–45 °C toward 70 °C (Figs. 7and 8). Brine further modulates this balance: ionic screening and possible ion-bridging can increase interfacial packing/cohesion of CI-rich films and suppress coalescence relative to DI water, sustaining higher emulsion volumes at low-to-intermediate temperatures. At elevated temperature, however, the same interfacial material can compact into localized sludge-like deposits rather than sustaining a dispersed emulsion, consistent with the observed shift from bulk emulsion retention to separation/sludge-dominated behavior. Overall, temperature acts as the primary kinetic driver, while inhibitor chemistry (CI vs. KHI) and salinity tune the interfacial-film resilience that determines whether the system remains in an emulsion-retained state or transitions toward interfacial deposition (Figs. 7and8).

Effect of water cut (4:1 vs. 2:2 condensate-to-water ratios)

To evaluate the effect of water cut on emulsion formation and stability, experiments were conducted with SPD10 condensate at a 2:2 condensate-to-water ratio (100 mL condensate and 100 mL aqueous phase). Tests were performed at 12, 30, 45, and 70 °C under both cooling and heating paths and compared with the 4:1 result discussed previously. The aqueous phase consisted of either deionized water or brine, with and without inhibitors (CI, KHI, and CI + KHI). The objective was to determine how increasing the relative proportion of water alters emulsion volume, stability, and sludge formation. All quantitative data represent mean ± SD (n = 3), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using the distribution for small samples . Individual replicate values are plotted in the figures to visualize data dispersion.

At 70 °C (fresh samples), increasing the water cut to a 2:2 condensate-to-water ratio markedly intensified emulsion formation in CI-containing systems compared with the 4:1 ratio (Fig. 10b). In particular, CI + brine generated a large emulsion layer within 5 min (195 ± 10 mL; n = 3), whereas emulsification in the 4:1 case remained limited under the same temperature condition (Fig. 10b). Although the emulsion volume decreased substantially with time, a measurable residual layer persisted at long times (≈20 ± 3 mL after 24 h; n = 3), indicating that higher dispersed-phase availability can promote longer-lived interfacial structures even at elevated temperature. Consistent with this behavior, water separation trends (Fig. 10a) reflect the expected trade-off: formulations exhibiting stronger emulsification show comparatively reduced rapid phase disengagement. Mechanistically, raising the water cut increases the interfacial area generated during mixing and the frequency of droplet–droplet collisions; in the presence of CI—especially with brine—this enhances interfacial adsorption and transient film formation, delaying coalescence. In contrast, CI + deionized water formed only a thin emulsion (6 ± 2 mL; n = 3) that collapsed completely within 24 h, supporting the role of salinity in strengthening interfacial stabilization under high-temperature/high-water-cut conditions. Finally, the combination CI + KHI + brine predominantly produced sludge-like interfacial films that persisted for at least 24 h (See Supplementary Fig. S20).

At 45 °C (cooling path), higher water cut again led to substantial emulsion formation in CI + brine and CI + KHI + brine systems, reaching 188 ± 9 mL (95% CI: 174–202 mL) after 5 min. Even after 24 h, 122 ± 8 mL of emulsion remained, confirming the stabilizing influence of brine under elevated water content. Conversely, deionized-water systems showed much smaller emulsions (42 ± 4 mL) that disappeared within 24 h.

Specifically, the CI + brine and CI + KHI + brine systems produced extensive emulsions shortly after mixing (175 ± 11 mL; n = 3) that remained relatively stable over time (≈110 ± 10 mL after 24 h; n = 3), whereas the corresponding DI-water systems destabilized rapidly (≈35 ± 5 mL to ~ 0 mL). Consistent with these trends, water separation increased as emulsions collapsed in DI formulations, while brine formulations retained more water within the emulsion phase (Fig. 11a). Mechanistically, at intermediate temperature the continuous-phase viscosity is still sufficient to slow drainage, and the high water cut generates a large interfacial area; in the presence of CI—especially with brine—rapid interfacial adsorption and strengthened interfacial films suppress droplet coalescence, promoting sustained emulsification. At this temperature, sludge formation remains negligible, indicating that the system favors bulk emulsion retention rather than localized interfacial deposition under high-water-cut, intermediate-temperature conditions (See Supplementary Fig. S21).

Mechanistically, thermal history can alter interfacial adsorption/packing of surface-active CI components and the degree of condensate structuring (e.g., waxy/viscoelastic contributions), which changes interfacial-film strength and drainage rates. Heating can also reduce viscosity and facilitate interfacial rearrangement toward a more cohesive film upon subsequent equilibration, while cooling can promote faster phase separation unless a sufficiently strong interfacial network forms.

Overall, increasing the water cut from 4:1 to 2:2 markedly enhances both the volume and persistence of emulsions, particularly in CI + brine systems. Brine consistently outperformed deionized water as a stabilizing medium, while CI acted as the primary emulsifier. Thermal path history influenced emulsion behavior: cooling favored faster collapse in DI systems, whereas heating prolonged emulsion lifetime. At high temperature (70 °C), sludge dominated in low-water systems but emulsions persisted under higher water content. Mechanistically, the strong water-cut dependence observed in Figs. 10, 11, 12, 13 arises from how dispersed-phase fraction controls interfacial area generation and collision-driven coalescence dynamics under mixing. Increasing the water cut from 4:1 to 2:2 produces a larger number of water droplets and a substantially greater total oil–water interfacial area immediately after mixing. This amplifies the opportunity for interfacially active CI components to adsorb and form a protective viscoelastic film around droplets, increasing the probability of kinetic trapping as a bulk emulsion rather than rapid phase disengagement. Accordingly, CI-containing brine systems exhibit larger and more persistent emulsion volumes at higher water cut (Figs. 8b, 9b and 10b) together with reduced immediate water separation (Figs. 8a, 9a and 10a). In deionized-water systems, however, interfacial films are generally weaker and more prone to drainage and rupture; therefore, even when water cut is increased, emulsions decay faster and water disengagement accelerates relative to brine (Figs. 1011, 12, 12). The thermal-path dependence summarized in Fig. 13 further indicates that water-rich systems are more sensitive to thermal history because interfacial restructuring and drainage kinetics during heating/cooling can shift the balance between droplet stabilization and coalescence. Overall, higher water cut increases the “interfacial loading” of the system and strengthens CI-driven emulsification—especially in brine—whereas in DI water the stabilization window is narrower and emulsions remain less persistent (Figs. 1011, 12, 13).

To assess whether condensate composition influences emulsion formation and sludge behavior, comparative experiments were performed using SPD15 condensate under the same operating conditions previously tested for SPD10. Two representative temperatures, 12 °C and 70 °C, were selected at a 4:1 condensate-to-water ratio, employing both deionized water (DI) and brine as aqueous phases. Tests were carried out with and without inhibitors (CI, KHI, and CI + KHI) to evaluate how compositional differences between the condensates affect phase behavior. Individual replicate values are plotted in the figures to indicate within-condition variability.

At 12 °C, both condensates (SPD10 and SPD15) displayed similar qualitative behavior. Systems containing only condensate with DI or brine, or with KHI alone, produced negligible emulsions after settling. The addition of CI generated large, initially voluminous emulsions, particularly in brine (SPD10: 165 ± 12 mL; SPD15: 170 ± 10 mL; n = 3), and these persisted much longer than those in DI systems. This pattern for SPD15 is consistent with Fig. 15b, which shows appreciable emulsion volumes at 12 °C primarily in CI-containing formulations. When CI and KHI were combined, emulsions in brine were the thickest and most stable, whereas those in DI water collapsed more rapidly.

Mechanistically, CI acts as the primary interfacially active agent, rapidly adsorbing at the oil–water interface and forming a viscoelastic film that suppresses droplet coalescence, while brine can further enhance interfacial stabilization (e.g., through altered adsorption/packing and reduced coalescence efficiency), leading to lower effective water release and thicker retained emulsions (Fig. 15a,b).

At 70 °C, emulsions were short-lived for both condensates, and no stable emulsion persisted beyond 1 h under any formulation. This behavior is supported for SPD15 by Fig. 15b, where emulsion volumes at 70 °C are essentially negligible. Instead, sludge-like interfacial deposits developed, particularly in CI and CI + KHI + brine systems, with SPD15 exhibiting slightly denser and more cohesive sludge layers. The sludge remained visible for at least 1 h, especially in brine systems, indicating a cooperative effect between CI and salinity under elevated temperatures. Mechanistically, higher temperature reduces continuous-phase viscosity and accelerates drainage/coalescence, while weakening interfacial viscoelastic films; consequently, interfacial material tends to contract into thin, localized sludge-like deposits rather than sustaining a bulk emulsion layer.

Direct comparison between SPD10 and SPD15 (Supplementary Tables S8–S11) shows that emulsion and sludge behaviors are largely independent of condensate type. Differences in hydrocarbon composition between SPD10 and SPD15 appear not to significantly affect emulsion volume, persistence, or the transition to sludge. Instead, temperature, inhibitor chemistry, and brine salinity dominate the observed trends (Fig. 15a,b; See Supplementary Fig. S23)23.

In summary, both condensates exhibit the same temperature-dependent emulsion–sludge transition: voluminous emulsions at low temperature (12 °C) and predominantly sludge-like interfacial deposits at high temperature (70 °C). This overall similarity is further supported by the condensate-type summary plots (Fig. 16), where the separated-water volumes remain comparable between SPD10 and SPD15 under the selected inhibitor/salinity conditions (Fig. 16a), while the corresponding emulsion-layer volumes show only modest differences in magnitude and persistence (Fig. 16b). Mechanistically, these results indicate that the governing controls are not small compositional variations between SPD10 and SPD15, but rather the combined effects of temperature on viscosity and coalescence/drainage kinetics, and the formulation-dependent strength of interfacial films formed by CI/KHI components. Brine salinity can further enhance interfacial stabilization by promoting stronger adsorption/packing of surface-active species and suppressing droplet coalescence, thereby shifting the balance toward emulsion retention at low temperature, whereas at high temperature accelerated drainage and weakened interfacial viscoelasticity favor separation and sludge formation. Overall, the data imply that external operational parameters—temperature, water fraction, inhibitor presence, and brine salinity—dominate phase stability, with condensate type exerting only a secondary influence (Fig. 16).

Influence of inhibitor thermal treatment

To examine whether prior thermal exposure of the kinetic hydrate inhibitor (KHI) affects its emulsification or sludge-forming behavior, additional experiments were conducted using pre-heated KHI at 70 °C, 30 °C, and 12 °C with both SPD10 and SPD15 condensates at a 4:1 condensate-to-water ratio. Each system was tested with KHI alone and KHI combined with CI, in both deionized water (DI) and brine as aqueous phases. Results were compared with those obtained using non-heated KHI under identical conditions. All measurements represent mean ± SD (n = 3), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated using the distribution for small samples . Individual replicate points are shown in the figures to illustrate variability.

At 70 °C (SPD10 and SPD15, 4:1 ratio), heated KHI alone produced no stable emulsions, identical to the non-heated case. However, when heated KHI was combined with CI in brine, persistent sludge-like interfacial layers formed (22 ± 4 mL, 95% CI: 16–28 mL), remaining visible after 1 h and even after 48 h of settling. The sludge volumes were comparable to those in non-heated systems, suggesting that thermal conditioning of KHI has negligible influence on its behavior at elevated temperatures.

At 30 °C, results again indicated minimal difference between heated and non-heated KHIs. Heated KHI alone failed to stabilize emulsions in either aqueous phase. The CI + KHI mixtures produced transient emulsions (40 ± 6 mL, 95% CI: 31–49 mL) that diminished within 1 h, leaving small residual volumes (10 ± 3 mL). Interestingly, sludge formation was less pronounced than in the corresponding non-heated systems, implying that heating may slightly reduce the propensity of CI + KHI combinations to generate interfacial residues while not affecting overall emulsion persistence.

At 12 °C, the behavior of heated KHI was nearly identical to that of non-heated KHI. No emulsions were detected in systems containing KHI alone, whereas CI-containing systems exhibited voluminous emulsions (165 ± 12 mL, 95% CI: 148–182 mL for SPD10; 170 ± 10 mL, 95% CI: 156–184 mL for SPD15) with brine-enhanced stability. When heated KHI was combined with CI, brine systems formed slightly thicker emulsions (~ 180 ± 9 mL) than DI systems (~ 150 ± 8 mL), consistent with earlier observations. Importantly, no significant sludge appeared in any low-temperature trials, confirming that KHI pre-heating exerts minimal effect on phase stability at 12 °C.

In summary, across all tested conditions, thermal pre-treatment of KHI does not substantially modify its role in gas–condensate/brine emulsions. Emulsion behavior continues to be dictated by CI presence and brine salinity, while sludge generation remains primarily a high-temperature phenomenon in CI + KHI mixtures. Consequently, pre-heating KHI has limited operational importance; inhibitor selection should instead focus on chemical formulation and aqueous salinity rather than prior thermal conditioning.

To directly address the impact of inhibitor thermal treatment using experimental data, Figs. 18 and 19 compare non-heated versus thermally treated conditions for both condensates (SPD10 and SPD15) across aqueous chemistry (DW vs. brine), formulation (Blank/CI/KHI/CI + KHI), and settling time (5 min, 1 h, and 24 h). For both condensates, the separated-water volumes show only minor and non-systematic differences between treated and non-treated cases at both 12 °C and 70 °C (Figs. 18a,b and 19a,b), indicating that thermal conditioning does not fundamentally alter the bulk phase-disengagement capacity of the systems. The emulsion-layer responses follow the same overall conclusion: large emulsions are primarily associated with CI-containing formulations at low temperature (12 °C), whereas emulsions are largely suppressed at 70 °C regardless of thermal treatment (Figs. 18c,d and 19c,d). Any treatment-dependent changes in emulsion volume appear secondary relative to the dominant effects of temperature, CI presence, and salinity, and generally remain within the qualitative variability expected for these complex multiphase systems.

Mechanistically, this weak dependence on thermal treatment is consistent with the roles of the additives. KHI is predominantly water-soluble and is not expected to act as a strong interfacial emulsifier; therefore, pre-heating should not substantially increase interfacial adsorption or film elasticity. Instead, emulsion persistence is governed mainly by the formation and stability of interfacial films created by surface-active CI components and their interaction with brine ions. At low temperature, higher viscosity and slower drainage favor kinetic trapping of dispersed water droplets, allowing CI-rich interfacial layers (and CI + KHI mixtures) to stabilize emulsions, particularly in brine where ionic screening/bridging can enhance interfacial packing and suppress coalescence (Figs. 18c and 19c). At high temperature (70 °C), accelerated coalescence and film drainage dominate and rapidly erode interfacial stabilization, so emulsions do not persist even when inhibitors are thermally conditioned (Figs. 18d and 19d). Overall, the paired datasets in Figs. 18 and 19 demonstrate that inhibitor thermal history has a limited effect compared with the stronger operational controls of temperature and salinity and the presence of CI-driven interfacial activity.

Mechanistically, the limited influence of thermal pre-treatment is consistent with the physicochemical role of KHI. KHI formulations are predominantly water-soluble and polymeric, and their primary function is hydrate management rather than acting as strong interfacial emulsifiers that substantially lower interfacial tension or generate highly elastic interfacial films. Therefore, pre-heating is not expected to produce a pronounced increase in interfacial activity or a systematic enhancement of emulsion stability. This interpretation aligns with the data-based comparisons in Figs. 18 and 19, where separated-water and emulsion volumes for thermally treated versus non-heated conditions show only minor and non-directional differences across temperatures and aqueous chemistries. If small deviations are observed, they are more plausibly attributed to subtle changes in polymer conformation/association (e.g., coil–globule balance) or indirect interactions within mixed interfacial layers when CI is present, rather than KHI itself becoming an emulsifying agent. Overall, the results indicate that the dominant controls remain temperature, salinity, and CI-driven interfacial film formation, with inhibitor thermal history exerting a secondary effect (Figs. 18 and 19).

Corrosion inhibitors (CI) with cationic amphiphilic headgroups act as the primary emulsifying agents by rapidly adsorbing at the oil–water interface, reducing γ, and generating comparatively rigid/viscoelastic interfacial films that resist rupture during droplet–droplet collisions (Table 5). In brine, multivalent ions (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+) can screen charges and promote headgroup association/ion-bridging, increasing interfacial packing and suppressing coalescence. This mechanism explains why CI-containing brine systems display larger and more persistent emulsions at 12–30 °C, whereas at higher temperatures (45–70 °C) the system preferentially transitions toward sludge-like deposits as faster drainage/coalescence and increased molecular mobility promote interfacial compaction and consolidation (Table 5).

In contrast, KHI (polyether-type, nonionic) is predominantly water-soluble and generally exhibits weaker interfacial activity; therefore, KHI alone is not expected to substantially lower γ or generate highly elastic interfacial films, consistent with its inability to stabilize persistent emulsions under most conditions (Table 5). When CI and KHI are combined, mixed interfacial layers may form in which CI drives adsorption while polymeric components increase cohesion and film thickness. Such mixed films can enhance emulsion retention at low temperature but may also promote sludge formation under heating by favoring denser interfacial networks that compact rather than remaining as extended emulsions (Table 5).

The MEG/MDEA blend is highly aqueous-affine and behaves as a non-emulsifier across temperatures, which favors clean phase separation rather than emulsion retention (Table 5). However, MEG/MDEA can indirectly facilitate interfacial residue buildup by altering aqueous-phase polarity/solvation and concentrating surface-active species at the interface, consistent with sludge-like deposits observed even when measurable emulsions are suppressed. A detailed qualitative mapping of these relationships is provided in Supplementary Table S18, while quantitative outcomes (mean ± SD, 95% CI; n = 3) are reported in Tables S1–S15.

The effect of inhibitor physicochemical properties on interfacial behavior is also illustrated schematically in Fig. 21, emphasizing the role of amphiphilic head groups and mixed-film cohesion in CI + KHI formulations. As temperature increases, enhanced molecular mobility and faster drainage shift the balance from bulk emulsion retention toward interfacial-film compaction and sludge formation, particularly in brine systems where ionic effects strengthen interfacial packing (Fig. 21; Table 5).

Effect of MEG/MDEA blends

To examine the influence of thermodynamic inhibitors and gas-sweetening agents on emulsion and sludge behavior, experiments were performed using an aqueous blend of monoethylene glycol (MEG) and methyldiethanolamine (MDEA). The mixture composition was maintained at 70% MEG, 4% MDEA, and 26% water or brine, representing field-representative formulations. Tests were conducted with SPD10 condensate at a 4:1 condensate-to-aqueous ratio and at 12, 30, and 70 °C, both in the presence and absence of KHI. The objective was to determine whether MEG/MDEA blends promote or suppress emulsion formation and/or facilitate sludge accumulation at the oil–water interface. All results are reported as mean ± SD (n = 3), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated via the -distribution for small samples . Individual replicate data points are displayed in the figures to illustrate variability.

At 12 °C, no measurable emulsions were detected in any configuration containing MEG/MDEA, either with deionized water or brine. Consistent with this observation, the separated-water volumes remained close to their initial values and changed only marginally with time, indicating rapid phase disengagement and negligible kinetic trapping (Fig. 22). After 24 h, the measured water volume was nearly unchanged (50 ± 2 mL, 95% CI: 47–53 mL), confirming that the MEG/MDEA blend strongly suppresses emulsion-layer formation at low temperature. Mechanistically, MEG/MDEA is highly hydrophilic and preferentially partitions into the aqueous phase, which reduces the propensity for forming viscoelastic interfacial films and promotes droplet coalescence and drainage rather than interfacial stabilization. Visual inspection, however, revealed thin but persistent sludge-like interfacial residues, particularly when CI or KHI was present. This suggests that even when bulk emulsification is inhibited, surface-active components (e.g., CI-derived species and/or polymeric fragments) can still accumulate at the interface and compact into a cohesive residue layer. Therefore, MEG/MDEA appears to shift the dominant outcome from dispersed emulsion stabilization to interfacial deposition, yielding clean phase separation in the bulk while potentially promoting localized sludge buildup over extended settling periods (Fig. 22).

Summary bar chart of separated-water volumes for MEG/MDEA-containing systems at 12 °C (4:1 condensate-to-water ratio), comparing DW and brine with and without KHI at three settling times (5 min, 1 h, and 24 h).

At 30 °C, the MEG/MDEA-containing systems remained largely consistent with the 12 °C behavior, showing no measurable emulsion formation and rapid phase disengagement. The separated-water volumes stayed close to their initial values with only minor time-dependent changes, confirming minimal kinetic trapping in the bulk liquid (Fig. 23). Nevertheless, sludge formation was clearly observed in CI and CI + KHI systems, and the interfacial deposits appeared denser and darker than those at 12 °C, with volumes around 8 ± 2 mL (95% CI: 5–11 mL). Mechanistically, this suggests that at intermediate temperature the reduced viscosity and enhanced molecular mobility promote interfacial transport and packing of surface-active species (e.g., CI-derived components and polymeric residues), facilitating their consolidation into a compact sludge layer rather than a dispersed emulsion. In other words, MEG/MDEA continues to suppress emulsification by favoring coalescence/drainage and limiting viscoelastic interfacial-film stabilization, but it does not eliminate interfacial accumulation; instead, the dominant outcome shifts toward interfacial residue aggregation. Overall, the combined observations (Fig. 23) indicate that MEG/MDEA strongly inhibits emulsion formation across temperatures, while sludge stability is governed by the availability and accumulation of surface-active species and can intensify at intermediate conditions.

At 70 °C, no measurable emulsions formed under any condition, reaffirming the strong anti-emulsifying behavior of the MEG/MDEA blend at elevated temperature. Consistent with this, the separated-water volumes remained high and showed only modest variation across time and aqueous chemistry, indicating fast coalescence and efficient drainage rather than kinetic trapping in an emulsion layer (Fig. 24). However, compact and persistent sludge deposits were observed in CI-containing brine systems (10 ± 3 mL, 95% CI: 6–14 mL) and remained visible up to 24 h. Compared with lower temperatures, these deposits appeared more compact, suggesting a thermally assisted consolidation/packing process rather than dispersed droplet stabilization.

Overall, across the tested range (12–70 °C), MEG/MDEA does not promote emulsion formation; instead, it shifts the interfacial outcome toward clean bulk separation with a tendency for interfacial residue accumulation when surface-active components are present. Mechanistically, MEG/MDEA primarily partitions into the aqueous phase and suppresses the formation of elastic interfacial films that would otherwise stabilize emulsions. Yet, when CI is present—especially in brine—surface-active species can still adsorb and concentrate at the interface; higher temperature accelerates mass transport and film drainage, enabling these materials to compact into a cohesive sludge layer rather than remaining dispersed as droplets. This reveals an operational trade-off: MEG/MDEA can reduce emulsion-related separation problems, but it may simultaneously favor sludge accumulation under CI–brine conditions, which can introduce additional flow-assurance and separation challenges24.

Mechanistically, the MEG/MDEA blend suppresses bulk emulsification while still permitting sludge formation because it shifts the interfacial balance away from elastic-film stabilization and toward interfacial accumulation/compaction. MEG and MDEA are strongly water-affine and increase the polarity and cohesion of the aqueous phase, which reduces the tendency of amphiphilic CI components to organize into a persistent, viscoelastic interfacial film capable of kinetically trapping droplets as a stable emulsion. In effect, the system loses the “film elasticity window” required for long-lived W/O emulsions, consistent with the near-constant separated-water volumes and the absence of measurable emulsion layers across temperatures (Figs. 22, 23, 24).

At the same time, the same aqueous strengthening and partitioning behavior can promote interfacial enrichment of poorly soluble or surface-active residues (e.g., CI-derived components and condensate heavy ends) because these species are excluded from the bulk aqueous phase and preferentially accumulate at the oil–water boundary. Rather than remaining as a dispersed emulsion, the interfacial material can densify into a compact, sludge-like layer through progressive association and consolidation, particularly in brine where ionic effects can further tighten interfacial packing and reduce redispersion. This explains the coexistence of “no emulsion” yet “persistent sludge” outcomes under MEG/MDEA conditions, and why sludge becomes more compact at higher temperature even though emulsions remain suppressed (Figs. 22, 23, 24).

In summary, the bar chart analysis provides a clear validation of the experimental observations and offers deeper insights into the governing mechanisms of emulsion and sludge behavior in condensate–brine systems. The results confirm that temperature is the dominant control parameter, as it dictates whether emulsions or sludge prevail. At low temperatures (12–30 °C), reduced molecular motion and increased viscosity suppress droplet coalescence and film drainage, thereby favoring the persistence of emulsions.

In contrast, at elevated temperatures (45–70 °C), enhanced molecular mobility accelerates water–oil separation, but the interaction of surface-active species from corrosion inhibitors with brine salts promotes the accumulation of dense sludge layers at the oil–water interface. Water cut plays an equally significant role by amplifying both the initial emulsion volume and its persistence. A higher aqueous fraction increases the interfacial area between water and condensate, providing more sites for inhibitor adsorption and droplet stabilization. This explains the large, long-lived emulsions observed at 2:2 ratio compared with 4:1 ratio. The effect of brine salinity is particularly critical. The presence of dissolved ions such as Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ modifies interfacial tension and enhances the strength of inhibitor-stabilized films around water droplets. Multivalent cations, in particular, can bridge negatively charged groups in corrosion inhibitors, leading to more rigid interfacial films and greater emulsion stability. This also explains the stronger and more persistent sludge formation in brine compared with deionized water. The role of inhibitors is also decisive. CI act as the primary emulsifying agents because of their amphiphilic structure, which lowers interfacial tension and stabilizes dispersed droplets.

In contrast, KHI alone is ineffective as an emulsifier since its primary role is to interfere with hydrate nucleation rather than stabilize oil–water interfaces. However, when combined with CI, KHI can contribute indirectly to sludge formation by altering interfacial film structure. The thermal history of the system introduces an additional layer of complexity. Cooling pathways typically favor the formation of initially large emulsions due to rapid viscosity increase and kinetic trapping of droplets, but these emulsions often collapse more quickly. Heating pathways, on the other hand, promote slower but more persistent emulsion stability by altering the adsorption–desorption dynamics of inhibitors at the interface. This duality underscores the importance of simulating realistic thermal cycles in laboratory studies to capture field-representative behavior.

Finally, the comparison between SPD10 and SPD15 condensates revealed only marginal differences in emulsion and sludge behavior. Although SPD15 contains slightly heavier hydrocarbon fractions, its influence on interfacial stability was negligible relative to the dominant roles of temperature, aqueous salinity, and inhibitor chemistry. This suggests that in practical flow assurance applications, operational variables and inhibitor formulations outweigh condensate composition in determining emulsion risks.

Several studies have demonstrated that the presence and type of ions, particularly divalent cations such as calcium and magnesium, significantly affect interfacial properties. Lima et al. (2013) showed that specific ions alter the water–oil interfacial tension by interacting with surfactant and polar functional groups17. Mahmoudvand et al. (2019) reported that brines containing Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions influence dynamic interfacial properties and strengthen interfacial films compared with Na⁺-based systems10. Similarly, Liu et al. (2024) confirmed that ion valency and concentration affect interfacial tension and wettability in crude oil–brine systems. The differences in emulsion and sludge stability observed in our experiments between brine and deionized water systems are therefore consistent with these established mechanisms11.

To deepen the scientific interpretation of the experimental findings, the mechanistic and theoretical discussion has been substantially expanded in the revised manuscript. The new analysis provides a cohesive physicochemical framework that links emulsion formation and stability to fundamental interfacial processes. Specifically, the role of corrosion inhibitors (CIs) is explained in terms of their amphiphilic behavior, which lowers the oil–water interfacial tension and leads to the formation of viscoelastic interfacial films that stabilize dispersed water droplets. In the presence of saline brines, cations such as Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ compress the electrical double layer and bridge the polar headgroups of inhibitor molecules, thereby strengthening interfacial film rigidity and enhancing emulsion stability25.

Temperature effects are also crucial: as temperature increases, both interfacial tension and viscosity decrease (∂γ/∂T < 0), which accelerates interfacial film drainage and droplet coalescence, explaining the observed transition from stable emulsions to sludge-like phases. Additionally, the influence of dispersed-phase fraction is interpreted using classical hydrodynamic models, which relate droplet collision frequency and coalescence kinetics to water-cut and shear rate.

Finally, sludge formation at elevated temperatures is attributed to the aggregation and structural reinforcement of interfacial films caused by strong CI–brine coupling, leading to semi-solid interfacial networks. Collectively, these mechanistic insights establish a unified understanding of how temperature, salinity, and inhibitor chemistry interact to control emulsion behavior under field-relevant conditions10,17.

Overall, the bar chart analysis demonstrates that the interplay of thermal conditions, water cut, salinity, and inhibitor chemistry governs whether emulsions or sludge dominate in subsea condensate systems. These findings provide mechanistic insights that are crucial for the rational design of inhibitor strategies and separation protocols in flow assurance.

Although the present experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, the findings can be meaningfully scaled to real pipeline operations by considering dimensionless similarity parameters. The flow behavior and interfacial dynamics observed here correspond to Reynolds numbers (Re) on the order of 103–104, Weber numbers (We) of 101–102, and Capillary numbers (Ca) of 10–3–10–2, which are comparable to those encountered in multiphase flow within subsea pipelines. These similarities indicate that the relative balance between inertial, viscous, and interfacial forces governing emulsion formation is preserved between laboratory and field scales26.

Furthermore, the trends identified, namely the temperature-dependent transition between emulsions and sludge, the stabilizing influence of brine salinity, and the amplifying role of corrosion inhibitors, can be incorporated into dimensionless correlation frameworks for use in flow-assurance modeling tools. While the absolute volumes measured in this study are specific to the small-scale setup, the observed dimensionless relationships between droplet coalescence rate, shear intensity, and inhibitor concentration remain representative of field behavior. This approach enhances the scalability and operational applicability of the results for subsea production systems.

Testing two compositionally similar condensates (SPD10, SPD15) was a deliberate control to assess whether external operating variables, temperature, water cut, and brine–inhibitor chemistry govern emulsion/sludge outcomes within a representative condensate window. The consistent responses across SPD10 and SPD15 support this hypothesis. While broader multi-basin screening (including fluids with markedly different PVT/aromaticity) and CI formulations with explicit salinity sensitivity would further expand applicability, the mechanistic picture developed here (CI–brine–temperature coupling and thermal-history effects) is expected to translate to comparable condensate systems. Future work will extend the matrix to diverse brines and CI chemistries tailored to site-specific ion speciation.

The collective results from this study enable the formulation of a mechanistic model describing how temperature, salinity, and inhibitor chemistry govern emulsion and sludge behavior in gas–condensate/brine systems. As illustrated schematically in Fig. 25, the interfacial dynamics are controlled primarily by the adsorption of amphiphilic corrosion inhibitor (CI) molecules at the oil–water boundary. At lower temperatures (12–30 °C), these CI molecules form viscoelastic interfacial films stabilized through ionic bridging with divalent cations (Ca2+ and Mg2+) in brine, resulting in the generation of stable and voluminous emulsions. As temperature increases (45–70 °C), enhanced molecular motion promotes desorption and dehydration of the interfacial layer, which leads to film collapse and the aggregation of interfacial residues into sludge-like structures. The kinetic hydrate inhibitor (KHI), being a nonionic polymer, shows limited interfacial activity and thus minimal impact on emulsion stability, whereas the MEG/MDEA blend acts as an anti-emulsifier, reducing film strength and preventing droplet stabilization. This conceptual framework integrates the experimental findings across different temperatures and chemical conditions and clarifies the competing pathways that determine whether emulsions or sludge dominate under field-relevant conditions.

Mechanistic synthesis and implications for prediction

Across the investigated formulations, the observed phase behavior can be viewed as the outcome of two competing interfacial “end-states”: (i) dispersed emulsion retention versus (ii) interfacial consolidation into sludge-like deposits. Emulsion retention dominates when a sufficiently resilient interfacial film forms rapidly after mixing and remains resistant to drainage and rupture during droplet collisions (e.g., CI-driven adsorption with brine-enhanced packing at low-to-intermediate temperatures). In contrast, sludge-like deposits become dominant when the interfacial material is present but the kinetic window for maintaining dispersed droplets closes—either because higher temperature accelerates drainage/coalescence, or because the interfacial layer compacts and consolidates rather than sustaining a distributed droplet population. This framework explains why similar chemical ingredients may yield either large emulsions or compact sludge depending on temperature, salinity, and thermal history, and why “no emulsion” can coexist with persistent interfacial residues under MEG/MDEA conditions.

From a predictive standpoint, the experiments indicate that emulsion/sludge outcomes are governed by a small set of physically meaningful inputs that control (a) interfacial-film formation/strength and (b) drainage/coalescence kinetics. The most influential variables to include in a predictive model are: temperature (T) as the primary kinetic driver; water cut as a control on total interfacial area generation and collision frequency; salinity and ion speciation (notably the presence of divalent ions) as modifiers of interfacial packing and coalescence suppression; additive identity and combination (CI, KHI, CI + KHI, and MEG/MDEA) as determinants of adsorption, IFT reduction, and film elasticity; and thermal path/conditioning (cooling vs. heating, preconditioning/thermal treatment) as it can shift interfacial structuring and drainage dynamics even at the same nominal temperature. Consistent with this, the data-based summary plots added in each subsection show clear parameter–response relationships for separated-water and emulsion-layer volumes, which can be interpreted as practical measures of separation tendency and emulsion retention, respectively.

Finally, the dataset generated here provides a structured basis for data-driven correlation development without overextending the scope of the present work. Because each experiment is labeled by operating conditions (T, water cut, brine vs. DI, additive formulation, and thermal path) and yields quantitative outputs (emulsion volume, separated-water volume, and sludge presence at defined times), the results can support regression-style mappings from inputs to phase outcomes. Such mappings could be further extended to simple machine-learning classifiers or regressors to predict whether a given condition is more likely to produce emulsion-dominated behavior versus sludge-dominated behavior, and to estimate the expected magnitude/persistence of each. In this study, we present these quantified trends and mechanistic interpretations as a foundation for future predictive model development under South Pars–relevant operating windows.

Limitations and mechanistic interpretation

It should be noted that the mechanistic interpretations proposed in this study, including ion bridging effects, interfacial film elasticity, and drainage or coalescence kinetics, are inferred from observed bulk-phase behavior rather than directly measured interfacial properties. While the experimental trends are internally consistent and supported by estimation-based statistical analysis, direct interfacial measurements such as interfacial tension, droplet size distribution, or interfacial rheology were beyond the scope of the present work.

Future studies incorporating higher replication levels (n ≥ 4) and direct interfacial characterization techniques would be valuable for further validating and quantifying these mechanisms.

Several experimental simplifications should also be considered when interpreting the results in relation to field conditions. First, the laboratory procedure involved a single controlled mixing step followed by static settling. While this approach allows systematic comparison of emulsion formation and phase separation behavior, it does not fully reproduce the continuous turbulence and shear conditions typically encountered in pipelines and processing facilities. Therefore, the observed separation dynamics should be interpreted as representative of controlled laboratory conditions rather than fully dynamic flow environments.

Second, vapor losses during the experiments were estimated volumetrically and remained below 0.5% of the total liquid volume. Nevertheless, at elevated temperatures (particularly 70 °C), minor compositional shifts associated with the evaporation of light hydrocarbons (e.g., C3–C5 components) cannot be completely excluded and may slightly influence interfacial behavior and phase stability.

Finally, sludge formation in this work was evaluated primarily through macroscopic visual observation and volume estimation. Although this approach provides practical insight into sludge formation tendencies under different conditions, detailed compositional or microstructural characterization of the sludge phase was beyond the scope of the present study. Future work incorporating analytical techniques such as microscopy or compositional analysis would provide deeper insight into the mechanisms governing sludge formation.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated emulsion formation and stability in South Pars gas-condensate/brine systems under laboratory conditions representative of pipeline flow. The experimental program explored the effects of temperature, water cut, inhibitor chemistry, brine salinity, and thermal history, using two representative condensates (SPD10 and SPD15) and several additive classes (CI, KHI, and MEG/MDEA blends). All quantitative results were obtained from duplicate experiments (n = 3) and are reported as mean ± SD with 95% confidence intervals, emphasizing estimation rather than hypothesis testing. Despite limited replication, the trends were clear and internally consistent across conditions.

Temperature emerged as the dominant control on phase behavior. At lower temperatures (12–30 °C), corrosion inhibitors (CI) acted as strong emulsifiers, generating voluminous and relatively persistent emulsions, particularly in brine, where dissolved salts enhanced interfacial-film rigidity and delayed droplet coalescence. In contrast, at higher temperatures (45–70 °C), emulsions rapidly collapsed but sludge-like interfacial residues formed and persisted, most notably in CI + brine systems. This transition from emulsions to sludge reflects the interplay of thermal energy and inhibitor–ion interactions.

Increasing the water cut from 4:1 to 2:2 nearly doubled the initial emulsion volume and extended its lifetime, confirming that a larger interfacial area enables stronger adsorption of inhibitor molecules. This effect was most pronounced in CI + brine systems, which produced emulsions approaching 200 mL (mean ± SD) and measurable volumes after 24 h at 70 °C. Brine salinity consistently stabilized both emulsions and sludge relative to deionized water; multivalent ions such as Ca2+ and Mg2+ likely promoted inhibitor cross-linking at the interface.

Regarding inhibitor chemistry, CI was the principal emulsifier, whereas KHI alone had negligible effect. The combination CI + KHI intensified sludge deposition at elevated temperatures, indicating synergistic interfacial film formation. MEG/MDEA blends, by contrast, acted as anti-emulsifiers across all temperatures but promoted persistent sludge accumulation, highlighting a trade-off between reduced emulsion risk and increased interfacial fouling.

Cooling paths produced large, short-lived emulsions, while heating paths generated smaller yet longer-lived emulsions, emphasizing the need to consider realistic thermal cycling in flow-assurance testing. Comparison between SPD10 and SPD15 showed only minor differences. Despite compositional variations (SPD15 being marginally heavier), both condensates exhibited nearly identical emulsion–sludge patterns, demonstrating that operational conditions and aqueous chemistry exert far greater influence than condensate type.

In summary, the coupled effects of temperature, water cut, salinity, and inhibitor chemistry govern the delicate balance among emulsion formation, emulsion collapse, and sludge deposition in condensate–brine systems. Integrating controlled laboratory studies with statistical estimation, inhibitor-qualification protocols, and mechanistic modeling will be critical for developing predictive tools for emulsion and sludge-risk management in subsea production operations. The novelty of the present work lies in experimentally mapping the interacting CI–brine–temperature controls, and demonstrating how water cut and thermal history modulate these interactions under pipeline-representative shear conditions, thereby distinguishing emulsion-dominated retention from sludge-dominated interfacial consolidation. Moreover, the proposed ERI/WSI indices provide a practical, transferable basis for rapid formulation screening and for building parameter–response correlations that support prediction of emulsion/sludge propensity within the studied operating window.
