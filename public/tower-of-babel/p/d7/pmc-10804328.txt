Trial‐based economic evaluation of mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy compared to treatment as usual for bipolar disorder
Authors: Ben Wijnen, Maud Jansen, Annelieke van Velthoven, Imke Hanssen, Marloes Huijbers, Silvia Evers, Anne Speckens
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAim of this study was to assess the cost‐effectiveness and cost‐utility of mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy (MBCT) and treatment as usual (TAU) compared to TAU alone in adults with Bipolar disorder (BD).MethodsAn economic evaluation with a time horizon of 15 months was conducted from a societal perspective. Outcomes were expressed in costs per quality adjusted life years (QALYs) and costs per responder using the inventory of depressive symptomatology clinician rating score.ResultsPeople with BD (N = 144) were included in this study. From a societal perspective, the difference of total costs between MBCT + TAU and TAU was €615, with lower costs in the MBCT + TAU group. Only healthcare costs differed significantly between the two groups. A small difference in QALYs in favor of MBCT + TAU was found combined with lower costs (−€836; baseline adjusted) for MBCT + TAU compared to TAU, resulting in a dominant incremental cost‐utility ratio. The probability that the MBCT + TAU was cost‐effective was 65%. All sensitivity analyses attested to the robustness of the base case analyses.ConclusionConcludingly, MBCT + TAU seems to be cost‐effective compared to TAU alone, indicated by a small or neglectable difference in effect, in favor of MBCT + TAU, while resulting in lower costs.Keywords: bipolar disorder, cost‐benefit analysis, mindfulness, quality‐adjusted life years

INTRODUCTION

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a chronic mental disorder, characterized by recurrent episodes of depressive, (hypo)manic and/or mixed mood states (Kessler et al., 2004). Bipolar disorder has a significant impact on the quality of life of people with BD. It is ranked as the 16th leading cause of years lived with disability (multiplying prevalence by disability weights quantifying the severity of the health loss). Among mental disorders, BD is the fifth leading cause of disability‐adjusted life years (Ferrari et al., 2016). Moreover, mood disorders have the greatest impact on the health‐related quality of life among mental disorders (Fernandez et al., 2010). The prevalence of BD in the Netherlands is 1, 2% in adult men and 1.5% in adult women (deGraaf et al., 2012), and in Europe, the estimated 12‐month prevalence of BD is approximately 1% (Pini et al., 2005). Not only does BD impact persons' health, it also impacts society as a whole. The annual per capita costs in the Netherlands for mood disorders is calculated at €5009 (Smit et al., 2006). Regarding BD, the annual societal costs per person with BD is estimated to vary between US$1904 to US$33,090 (Jin & McCrone, 2015). These costs are for example, due to higher rates of unemployment (Chang et al., 2016; Dittmann et al., 2002). A German study shows that only 30% of all people with BD worked full‐time according to their qualifications (Dittmann et al., 2002), and 85% of the total cost of mental disorders can be explained by production losses (Smit et al., 2006). The high rate of unemployment could be due to functional deterioration caused by the illness (Chang et al., 2016). Concludingly, BD both has a substantial impact on people's health and poses a substantial economic burden on society.

Several therapies have been shown effective in reducing current symptoms and/or maintain long‐term clinical remission. Pharmacological therapy, including lithium and antiepileptics, is the most common treatment for BD (Joshi et al., 2019; Swartz & Swanson, 2014). However, even with pharmacological treatment, up to 48,5% of persons experienced relapse or recurrence of depressive or (hypo)manic episodes during 2 years of follow‐up (Perlis et al., 2006). Psychological interventions, including cognitive behavioral therapy and family therapy, could be added as an adjunctive therapy for people with BD. Adding psychological to pharmacological treatments has benefits in preventing or delaying relapses in people with BD (Lam et al., 2009; Swartz & Swanson, 2014).

For that reason, there is growing interest in treatment outcome of mindfulness based interventions in people with BD. One mindfulness based intervention that has recently been included in the Dutch national guidelines for BD is Mindfulness‐Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) (Kupka et al., 2015). Mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy incorporates cognitive therapy and mindfulness‐based meditative practices with the intention to be aware of distressing thoughts and feelings and disengage from them without judgment (Segal et al., 2013). Even though MBCT was included in the Dutch guidelines, evidence about the (cost)effectiveness of BD is scarce. Mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy has promising results in uncontrolled and controlled trials (Lovas & Schuman‐Olivier, 2018; Xuan et al., 2020), but only two randomized control trials (RCTs) have been performed of MBCT in people with BD (deDios et al., 2021; Perich et al., 2013).

To our current knowledge, there has been no economic evaluation of MBCT in people with BD, with the exception of one economic evaluation that is still ongoing (Fletcher et al., 2018). An economic evaluation considers both the effects and costs of at least two different therapies in order to determine incremental costs and effects of offering the new therapy over the current alternative (i.e. usually treatment as usual (TAU) or best available care). Given that economic evaluations aim to provide guidance on real‐world reimbursement decisions, the comparator is frequently TAU or best available care. In the context of healthcare budget constraints, it is important to assess the cost‐effectiveness of MBCT for BD in the Netherlands (Kaljouw & Wijma, 2020). Evidently, this economic evaluation could help policy makers with evidence‐based decisions regarding healthcare budget allocation.

Aim of the study

The aim of this study was to determine whether MBCT added to TAU compared to TAU alone was cost‐effective for people with BD from a societal and healthcare perspective.

METHODOLOGY

Study design

This economic evaluation was based on data derived from a prospective, evaluator blinded, multicenter RCT of MBCT + TAU versus TAU alone in people with BD. This economic evaluation consists of a cost‐effectiveness analysis and cost‐utility analysis, by comparing the societal cost and effects of the intervention group with the control group, with a time horizon of 15 months. This study was conducted according to the Dutch guidelines for economic evaluations (Nederland, 2015). Data was reported according to the consolidated health economic evaluation reporting standards checklist (Husereau et al., 2013). The study protocol has been approved by the ethical review board CMO Arnhem—Nijmegen and is registered under number NL63319.091.17. Detailed information regarding the methodology of the RCT has been published elsewhere (Hanssen et al., 2019).

Study population

The study population consists of adults (18 years or older) with BD. The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of bipolar I or bipolar II disorder, according to the DSM 5 and confirmed by using the Structured Clinical interview for DSMI‐IV Axis I Disorders (SCID‐I); (2) having suffered at least two confirmed lifetime depressive episodes, either current or in (partial) remission at baseline; (3) having suffered at least one mood episode (either depressive and/or (hypo)manic) within the year prior to baseline; and (4) a Young Mania Rating Scale score of 12 or lower. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) insufficient comprehension of the Dutch language; (2) previous participation in an 8‐week mindfulness‐based intervention; (3) having suffered a severe manic episode within 3 months before baseline; (4) a lifetime diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, current substance abuse disorder, organic brain syndrome or antisocial or borderline personality disorder; (5) increased risk of suicide or aggression; (6)for practical reasons, were about to receive another psychological intervention between baseline and T1; and (7) the presence of a concurrent significant medical condition impeding the ability to participate.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited at seven outpatient clinics for people with BD between May 2018 and February 2020. The recruitment procedure was as follows: people with bipolar I and II disorder received an information letter from their attending clinician. When interested they were asked to contact the research team, after which a research interview took place in which in‐ and exclusion criteria were checked thoroughly. Participants were randomized based on setting, gender, depression status (current vs. remitted) and type of BD, using an Electronic Data Capture program (CASTOR). Participants started with MBCT within 1.5 months after randomization. Data was collected via clinical interviews and self‐reported questionnaires obtained at baseline (T0) and at 3 (T1), 6 (T2), 9 (T3), 12 (T4) and 15 (T5) months follow‐up.

Treatment as usual

In this study, MBCT was compared to TAU, typically consisting of pharmacotherapy, psycho‐education and/or self‐management interventions (Kupka et al., 2015). Given the pragmatic nature of the study, TAU was not protocolized and was expected to be in line with the Dutch treatment guidelines for BD. In line with these guidelines, most participants had already received psycho‐education (79%) or were using mood stabilizing pharmacotherapy (90%) before start of the study. These treatments are usually provided and monitored by specialized nurses or psychiatrists. Hence, during the study, participants were allowed to switch, taper or augment their medication, to enlarge clinical representativeness.

Intervention

A detailed overview of the MBCT program (e.g., themes, mindfulness exercises, didactic teaching styles, and homework assignments) can be found in the protocol paper (Hanssen et al., 2019). In short, the MBCT program was based on the original MBCT protocol for major depressive disorder (Segal et al., 2013), with changes to tailor the program specifically to BD (e.g., adding information and exercises focusing on mania). The MBCT program consisted of eight weekly 2.5 h group sessions, a 6 h day of guided silent practice, and home assignments consisting of formal (e.g. sitting meditation and bodyscan) and informal (e.g. doing daily activities with awareness) meditative exercises for about 45 min per day. Each group consisted of 8–10 participants. The MBCT was provided at the Radboud University Medical Center in Nijmegen, and at seven specialized outpatient clinics for BD in the Netherlands. The intervention was taught by two teachers, of whom at least one qualified the advanced criteria of the Association on Mindfulness Based Teachers in the Netherlands (Crane et al., 2014). Teachers receive this qualification after they completed an accredited post‐academic teacher training program with a minimum of 150 h. The co‐teacher often was a psychiatric nurse or psychologist with expertise in treating BD, who were interested following the post‐academic mindfulness teacher program after the study.

Measure of effectiveness

The IDS‐c (clinician‐rated version) was used for the cost‐effectiveness analysis (Inventory of depressive symptomatology: IDS‐c, 2005). The IDS‐c is a 30‐item scale to assess depressive symptom severity, administered by a clinician, which is scored on a 0–3 point scale with the total score ranging from 0 to 84 (i.e. 28 of 30 items are scored) (Rush et al., 1996). The IDS‐C has acceptable psychometric properties based on people with major depression and BD (Trivedi et al., 2004). For the cost‐effectiveness analysis, the reduction in IDS‐c scores was dichotomized in either less or more than 50% in order to calculate the incremental costs per responder.

The quality adjusted life year (QALY) was used as outcome measure for the cost‐utility analysis and was based on the standard Dutch version of the EuroQol 5‐dimensions 5‐level (EQ‐5D‐5L) questionnaire (Versteegh et al., 2016). The EQ‐5D‐5L is recommended by the Dutch guidelines for cost‐effectiveness studies and it is sufficient in obtaining utility for depressive episodes (Nederland, 2015). The utility values have been calculated from the different health states by using the Dutch algorithm (Versteegh et al., 2016). The area under the curve method was used to compute QALY scores by using the utilities at several time points (i.e. linear interpolation).

Following the Dutch guidelines, an annual discount rate of 1.5% was applied for QALYs (Nederland, 2015).

Measure of resources and costs

The Trimbos/iMTA questionnaire for costs associated with psychiatric illness (TiC‐P) was used to determine the societal costs of a treatment, focusing on people with a psychiatric disease (Hakkaart‐van Roijen et al., 2002). The TiC‐P questionnaire was slightly adapted to the BD population. The questionnaire focused on relevant medical resources, volumes of consumption and productivity losses in a feasible and reliable manner (Bouwmans et al., 2013).

Costs were divided in healthcare costs, patient and family costs and other cost (see online supplementary Table S1). Subsequently, the total costs were calculated using a bottom‐up approach: each element of resource used was multiplied by an appropriate unit cost and summed to provide an overall total cost (Drummond et al., 2015). Costs for healthcare resources were obtained from the Dutch guidelines for costing research in health care research (Nederland, 2015). If cost prices were not available in the cost guideline, average commercial prices were used. Costs for pharmaceuticals was based on daily defined dosage ((Kupka et al., 2015); “Medicijnkosten.nl,”). Traveling costs were calculated based on the mean distance to healthcare providers defined in the Dutch guidelines for costing research in health care research (Hakkaart‐van Roijen et al., 2015). Productivity losses were calculated according to the friction cost method, based on the mean income of the Dutch population (Nederland, 2015). The costs of providing the MBCT program were based on the training costs, total number of sessions, patients' time, and time of the teachers for each session. The prices per unit can be found in online supplementary Table S2. Future costs were discounted to their present value by a rate of 4%. All cost prices were converted to the 2020 price level.

Data analysis

Before analyzing the data, the data was cleaned. For example, the hours of (un)paid work and help from family and friends was set to a maximum of 60 h per week and the maximum GP visits was set to 5 per week.

Our data analysis was performed according to the intention‐to‐treat principle (Ramsey et al., 2015). To simultaneously evaluate both costs and outcomes, seemingly unrelated regression equations (SURE) models were used. The SURE models were baseline‐adjusted with baseline IDS‐c scores, baseline EQ‐5D utility, or baseline costs as covariates. Because costs are usually non‐normally distributed the SURE models were bootstrapped (2500 times). Missing data was imputed using single imputation based on predictive mean matching nested in nonparametric bootstraps of SURE models of costs and effects, as recommended by Brandt and colleagues (Brand et al., 2019). The imputed values were based on baseline variables that were predictive of clinical outcomes and cost; and predictive of dropout. This was done to enhance the precision of the imputed observations and to adjust for possibly selective dropout.

The incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated based on the incremental costs and effects based on the IDS‐C of MBCT + TAU and TAU. IDS‐C scores at baseline were compared to the mean IDS‐C score during follow‐up. If the mean score decreased with 50% or more compared to baseline score, participants were characterized as responders. The incremental cost‐utility ratio (ICUR) was calculated based on the incremental costs and QALYs of TAU and MBCT + TAU. Bootstrapped ICERs/ICURs were plotted into a cost‐effectiveness plane. Moreover, a cost effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) was created, to determine the probabilities that the new intervention is cost‐effective given a range of WTP values for a QALY. The WTP depends on the burden of disease, which could lead to a maximum of 80,000 euro per QALY. For the current study, a threshold for people with moderate or severe depression of 50,000 euro per QALY was assumed (Vijgen et al., 2018).

Sensitivity analyses

The following sensitivity analyses have been performed: (1) the data was analyzed from a health care perspective instead of a societal perspective, excluding patient and family costs and productivity losses; and (2) a per protocol analysis was performed in which all participants who attended at least four sessions were included in this analysis (i.e. attending at least four MBCT sessions was considered to be the minimal “dose” to exert at least some of the expected effect, in line with previous studies of MBCT).

The cost‐utility acceptability curve indicated that the probability that the MBCT + TAU was cost‐effective with a WTP threshold of €50,000 was 64.8%.

Looking at the effectiveness of MBCT + TAU based on the IDS‐c score, the MBCT + TAU group showed more responders (difference in response rate of 0.06) and less costs (−€836) (Table 4). This resulted in a dominant ICER. The cost‐effectiveness (CE) plane is presented in Figure 2b. However, since most ICERs were located in the southeast quadrant, MBCT + TAU seems to be cost‐effective based on the response rate (as determined with the IDS‐c).

Sensivity analysis

The ICUR and ICER analyzed from a healthcare perspective showed lower costs for MBCT + TAU compared to the TAU, with a bootstrapped incremental cost difference of €1380. This resulted in a dominant ICUR/ICER. The CE plane and CEAC can be found in Figure 3. The CE plane shows that 49% of the bootstrapped ICUR are located in the southeast quadrant (indicating more effect and lower costs) and 48% in the southwest quadrant (indicating less effect and lower costs). The probability that the intervention is cost‐effective based on the ICUR with a WTP of €50,000 is 97.6%. In the CE plane, 82% of all bootstrapped ICERs were located in the southeast quadrant (indicating higher effect and lower costs). The per protocol analyses demonstrated similar results, with higher incremental QALY gains in the MBCT + TAU group combined with lower total costs (see Table 4).

DISCUSSION

The current study aimed to determine whether MBCT added to TAU compared to TAU alone was cost‐effective for people with BD, from a societal perspective. The results showed that MBCT + TAU resulted in lower costs with similar or higher effect. The reduced costs were largely explained by significantly lower healthcare costs for MBCT + TAU. Other costs (i.e. productivity costs) were higher in MBCT + TAU, especially costs for absenteeism and presenteeism. This could be due to the fact that the percentage of employed participants is slightly higher in MBCT + TAU (42%) compared to TAU (25%). Based on the societal perspective, most of the bootstrapped ICURs indicated lower QALYs and lower costs in Euros. Overall, there was a 64.8% chance that the intervention is cost‐effective based on a WTP threshold of €50,000. Most of the bootstrapped ICERs indicated lower costs and higher effects based when looking at the response rate. This resulted in an ICER of −€8041 (inferior). Moreover, the healthcare perspective showed clearer differences between MBCT + TAU and TAU. The healthcare costs of MBCT + TAU were lower compared to TAU. The responder rate based on the IDS‐c showed an increase of response rate. Accordingly, most of the bootstrapped ICERs were located in the southeast quadrant: less costs and more effect. Concludingly, MBCT + TAU seems to be cost‐effective compared to TAU alone, especially from a healthcare perspective. However, looking at the CE plane of the base case cost‐utility analysis (i.e. the costs per QALY), most of the ICERs scatter around the Y‐axis, indicating a small or neglectable difference in effect while resulting in lower costs (i.e. this could be considered as a cost‐minimization analysis).

To our current knowledge, this is the first full economic evaluation of MBCT for people with BD. Currently, one economic evaluation from Fletcher et al. is ongoing (Fletcher et al., 2018). This study will establish the cost‐effectiveness of a guided self‐help mindfulness based intervention for people with BD. A few studies have evaluated the cost‐effectiveness of MBCT in people with depression (Kuyken et al., 2015; Pahlevan et al., 2020; Saha et al., 2020). Mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy seems to be potentially cost‐effective compared to maintenance antidepressant medication (standard of care) in people suffering from major depressive disorder (Pahlevan et al., 2020), from both healthcare and societal perspective. This study adopted a 24‐month time horizon. A Swedish study showed that mindfulness group therapy was as effective as TAU in people with depression, anxiety or stress and adjustment disorders. In terms of costs, the mindfulness group had significantly lower healthcare and societal costs (Saha et al., 2020). Lastly, Kuyken et al. (2015), did not demonstrate MBCTs superiority to maintenance antidepressant treatment but this study focused on relapse prevention instead of symptom reduction. Hence, because these studies were performed with different patient groups, it is difficult to compare these results with our results.

The current study has several strengths. At first, no previous cost‐effectiveness research has been conducted on MBCT in BD. This allows the current study to provide new evidence. Secondly, the cost‐effectiveness and cost‐utility analyses were conducted from a societal perspective. Societal costs are an important category for people with BD, therefore it is important to take these costs into account (Chang et al., 2016; Dittmann et al., 2002; Smit et al., 2006). Thirdly, an incremental bottom‐up micro costing approach has been used. This approach is seen as the best method to calculate costs in economic evaluations (Drummond et al., 2015). Finally, the follow‐up period of this study was 15 months. Most other studies have a follow‐up between immediately after MBCT and 12‐months (Lovas & Schuman‐Olivier, 2018; Perich et al., 2013; Xuan et al., 2020). So, the follow‐up period of this study was longer compared to most other studies. However, because of ethical reasons, people with BD who had completed the TAU period were offered to take part in the MBCT if they were interested in doing so. This study also had several limitations. Eighteen out of 72 participants (25%) did not complete the MBCT according to the protocol (i.e. four or more sessions). Reasons for dropout from MBCT were, among others, a depressive or manic episode, being unable to combine the training with work, study or household demands or traveling time. Nonetheless, as per intention‐to‐treat protocol, these patients were included in the analyses. This might have impacted the estimated effects of MBCT + TAU on clinical outcomes in our main analysis (i.e., an over‐ or underestimation of the true treatment effect) and may stress the need to careful explanation of the MBCT program before start of the treatment. During follow‐up, 38 participants (26%) were lost and 74 participants (51%) had complete data on the Tic‐P questionnaire. Common reasons for dropout during follow‐up were work or study interference, being unwilling to complete assessments without being offered MBCT, personal circumstances, mood episodes or interference with other treatments. Participants dropping out resulted in missing data, and missing data or imputed data could have led to incorrect or biased means and 95%‐CI.

CONCLUSION

Based on the results of this study, it is likely that MBCT provides a valuable addition to current care for people with BD in the Netherlands with more effect and lower costs per person. Future studies could potentially look at identifying those participants for whom MBCT appears to be particularly effective. For example, a study of Hanssen et al. (2023), using the same data as the current study, showed that people with higher depressive symptom severity and higher functional impairment at baseline benefitted more from MBCT (Hanssen et al., 2023). Identifying participants with a higher chance on treatment success may increase cost‐effectiveness estimates and helps moving toward patient‐centered and personalized care in the future.
