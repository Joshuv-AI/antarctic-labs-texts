Efficacy and safety of compound glycyrrhizin in combination with conventional therapy in treatment of vitiligo: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Yang Jian, Xiaoyu Wang, Yunming Li, Dali Fu, Yalin Gong, Huiqing Shi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Vitiligo is an acquired chronic depigmentary disorder affecting approximately 0.5% to 1% of individuals worldwide. The compound glycyrrhizin (CG), a complementary medicine, has been reported for treatment of vitiligo, but the evidence has not been systematically evaluated. We systematically assessed the efficacy and safety of CG in combination with conventional therapy for the treatment of vitiligo.Methods:We searched Embase, Web of Science, PubMed, The Cochrane Library, Chinese BioMedical Literature Database (CBM), Wanfang Data, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and VIP information from inception to July 2022. Randomized controlled trials comparing CG combined with conventional therapy with conventional therapy alone for vitiligo were included in our analysis. The primary outcome was treatment response, which defined as >50% repigmentation rate of vitiligo after treatment. The secondary outcome was incidence of adverse events. Meta-analysis was performed using the Review Manager 5.4 software. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated with chi-square and I2 statistics, dichotomous data were expressed as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals using the Mantel–Haenszal method.Results:Thirty-nine studies enrolling with 3994 participants were subjected to this review. The results of our meta-analysis indicated that addition of CG had superior effectiveness on repigmentation rate than phototherapy (RR = 1.28; P < .001), immunosuppressant (RR = 1.76; P < .001), traditional Chinese medicine (RR = 1.38; P < .001), combination of phototherapy and immunosuppressant (RR = 1.42; P < .001), and combination of phototherapy and traditional Chinese medicine (RR = 1.37; P < .001). In addition, CG did not increase the incidence of adverse events for vitiligo (RR = 0.79; P = .05).Conclusions:CG as a complementary medicine has a potential benefit in treatment of vitiligo. However, since the methodological flaws in the studies we included, more high-quality randomized controlled trials are warranted.

1. Introduction

Vitiligo is an acquired chronic depigmentary disease that can lead to the destruction of melanocyte. Vitiligo affects approximately 0.5% to 1% of individuals worldwide.[1] and significantly exerts a detrimental influence on the quality of life, as well as imparts a mental and emotional burden. People with vitiligo will experience a series of psychological disorders or symptoms, including depression, anxiety, substance misuse, low self-esteem, and even suicidal tendencies.[2] The etiology of vitiligo is unknown; several hypotheses have been put forward, including autoimmune response, genetic factors, inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, and melanocyte detachment. Although each of these mechanisms may contribute, the autoimmune theory is now considered to be the prominent pathway.[3] According to the consensus definition of the Vitiligo European Task Force, vitiligo has been classified into 2 major forms: segmental vitiligo and non-segmental vitiligo.[4] Several guidelines have been put forward for the vitiligo management. These recommended treatments include phototherapy, systemic and topical immunosuppressants, and surgical therapy.[5–7] In addition, the Consensus of China also recommends traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) for vitiligo.[8] Licorice is a famous herbal plant that has been used in the treatment of many diseases since ancient time.[9,10] Numerous researches have highlighted the pharmacological properties of licorice extract including immunoregulatory,[11,12] anti-inflammatory,[13,14] antiviral,[15,16] antitumor,[17,18] and hepatoprotective[19,20] effects. Glycyrrhizin is the most important active ingredient of licorice.[21] Compound glycyrrhizin (CG) is a manufactured drug containing glycyrrhizin, glycine, and methionine. CG has been used for immune dermatologic disorders in China due to its immunoregulatory and anti-inflammatory effects.[22,23]

In recent years, CG has been used as a complementary medicine for vitiligo in clinical,[24] but such practice has not been systematically evaluated. Our study conducts a review of all randomized controlled trials (RCTs), aiming to assess the additional benefit and safety of CG in combination with conventional therapy for vitiligo. We present the following article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist.

2. Methods

2.1. Protocol and registration

The systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the PRISMA guideline, and registered on PROSPERO (CRD42022293811).

2.2. Search strategies

Two independent researchers searched the following databases from inception to July 2022: Embase, Web of Science, PubMed, The Cochrane Library, Chinese BioMedical Literature Database (CBM), Wanfang Data, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), VIP information. The following keywords were used in these databases: “vitiligo,” “compound glycyrrhizin,” “glycyrrhizic acid,” “giammonium glycyrrhizinate,” monoammonium glycyrrhizinate,” “NEO-Minophagen C®,” “Randomized controlled trials (RCTs).”

2.3. Eligibility criteria

RCTs comparing the combination of CG and conventional therapy with conventional therapy alone for vitiligo were considered in this review. We did not apply any language restrictions and included all clinically diagnosed criteria of vitiligo regardless of classification, progression, lesion sites, race, gender, and age. Primary outcomes: A significant clinical response to treatment is defined as an outcome of >50% repigmentation rate of vitiligo after treatment. The response rates were defined as the percentage of participants achieving >50% repigmentation in each study. Secondary outcomes: adverse events (AEs), including skin itching, rash, edema, erythematous rash, etc. We exclude non-randomized trials, posters, secondary research, meeting abstracts, and papers without full text.

2.4. Paper screening and quality assessment

In the first round, 2 researchers independently screened the titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Duplicate publications were identified by several criteria (authors, title, intervention, and number of patients). In the second round, 2 independent authors check the full texts of all considered publications. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, if necessary, by a third researcher. The risk of bias assessment was recommended by the Cochrane risk of bias tool,[25] including the domains of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, selective reporting, incomplete outcome data, and other sources of bias. A funnel plot was used to evaluate potential publication bias for analysis.

2.5. Data extraction

For all included trials, 2 authors independently extracted the information regarding the author, year of publication, study design, intervention, number and characteristics of patients enrolled, treatment duration, and outcomes (efficacy and AEs). Any disagreement was resolved by discussion. Where the trials evaluated different treatment durations, the data from the longest treatment duration were included in the analysis.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted with RevMan5.4 software. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using chi-square and I2 statistics: if significant with I2 < 50% or P > .05, the trials were thought to be homogeneous, the fixed-effects model was selected to analyze the data; otherwise, the trials were supposed to be heterogeneous, the random-effects model was used. Dichotomous data were expressed as risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using the Mantel–Haenszal method. P < .05 was judged as statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Search results and study characteristics

Using the above search strategy, our search yielded 604 relevant citations. After remove of duplicates, title and abstract screening, and full-text assessment, 39 eligible RCTs enrolling 3994 participants with vitiligo were evaluated in our meta-analysis.[26–64] The selection process is shown in Figure 1. The included trials were carried out in China and published in the Chinese language from 2005 to 2021, except one published in English.[65] The main features of the eligible studies are presented in Table 1. All trials evaluated the additional efficacy of CG to conventional therapy. Conventional therapy included phototherapy (NB-UVB, 308 nm excimer laser), immunosuppressant (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus, halometasone), other TCM (vernonia anthelmintica pills, baidianfeng pills, baixiaotang), a combination of phototherapy and immunosuppressant (Fractional CO2 laser + triamcinolone, UB-UVB + halometasone, fractional CO2 laser + triamcinolone), and a combination of phototherapy and TCM (NB-UNB + compound kaliziran tincture, NB-UVB + traditional Chinese medical Decoction, UVB + psoralea). All included trials administered CG orally within the recommended dosage, but one administered by injection.[63] The duration of treatment ranged from 10 weeks to 6 months, except for 2 trials that were not stated.[27,33]

3.2. Risk of bias

All eligible trials were assessed with Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. All studies were randomized controlled, but only eleven reported generation of random sequence and were evaluated as low risk.[29–31,36,41,42,44,45,57,60,61] All studies were classified as low-risk about selective reporting, incomplete outcome, other bias. All were judged as high risk in terms of performance bias since the lack of blinding of participants and personnel. There was insufficient information on blinding of outcome assessment and allocation concealment, these items were judged as unclear risk. The results of quality assessment of the included RCTs are shown in Figure 2.

3.3. Meta-analysis results

We performed subgroup analysis under different types of conventional inventions.

3.3.1. Add-on effect of CG to phototherapy.

Fifteen studies[26–40] with 1993 vitiligo patients evaluated the efficacy of CG in combination with phototherapy; the meta-analysis showed a significant improvement in those who received CG in combination with phototherapy compared to phototherapy alone (RR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.21–1.36, P < .001). The fixed-effect model was selected for analysis without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), as shown in Figure 3.

Forest plot of the efficacy of CG in combination with phototherapy versus phototherapy alone for vitiligo. CG = compound glycyrrhizin, CI = confidence interval.

3.3.2. Add-on effect of CG to immunosuppressant.

Pooled data from 6 trials[41–46] showed that a combination of CG and immunosuppressant had a better therapeutic effect than immunosuppressant alone (RR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.49–2.08, P < .001), with no significant heterogeneity was noticed (I2 = 41%), as shown in Figure 4.

Forest plot of the efficacy of CG in combination with immunosuppressants versus immunosuppressants alone for vitiligo. CG = compound glycyrrhizin, CI = confidence interval.

3.3.3. Add-on effect of CG to other TCM.

A meta-analysis including 7 trials[47–53] revealed that combination therapy of CG and TCM was better than TCM alone in terms of repigmentation > 50% (RR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.21–1.57, P < .001), there was no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), as shown in Figure 5.

Forest plot of the efficacy of CG in combination with TCM versus TCM alone for vitiligo. CG = compound glycyrrhizin, CI = confidence interval, TCM = traditional Chinese medicine.

3.3.4. Add-on effect of CG to the combination of phototherapy and immunosuppressant.

Compared to the combination of phototherapy and immunosuppressant, pooled data from 7 studies[54–60] suggested addition of CG could raise the number of patients achieving repigmentation > 50% (RR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.25–1.62, P < .001), without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 47%), as shown in Figure 6.

Forest plot of the efficacy of CG in combination with phototherapy and immunosuppressant versus combination of phototherapy and immunosuppressant for vitiligo. CG = compound glycyrrhizin, CI = confidence interval.

3.3.5. Add-on effect of CG to the combination of phototherapy and other TCM.

Four studies[61–64] evaluated that the add-on effect of CG to the combination of phototherapy and other TCM; the meta-analysis indicated CG plus the combination of phototherapy and TCM was superior to the combination of phototherapy and TCM (RR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.20–1.56, P < .001). Statistical heterogeneity was low, and the fixed-effect model was used (I2 = 0%), as shown in Figure 7.

Forest plot of the efficacy of CG in combination with phototherapy and TCM versus combination of phototherapy and TCM for vitiligo. CG = compound glycyrrhizin, CI = confidence interval, TCM = traditional Chinese medicine.

3.3.6. Adverse events.

Thirty-three included studies assessed the adverse effects; of which 7 reported no AEs in the control or treatment groups, and 26 reported mild AEs including pruritus, rash, edema, erythematous rash, dizziness, vomiting, nausea, and so on. The detailed AEs are listed in Table 2. Pooled the data from the 26 studies, the analysis results indicated that the overall incidence of AEs in the combination of CG and conventional therapy groups was similar to that in the conventional therapy groups (RR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.62–1.00, P = .05), without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 47%), as shown in Figure 8. No serious AEs were observed in the included studies.

4. Discussion

Although vitiligo does not reduce the expectancy of life, it does affect the quality of life and psychological state. Multiple hypotheses have been put forward for the loss of melanocyte function in vitiligo, including autoimmune response, genetic factors, oxidative stress, and inflammatory mediators. The role of each mechanism plays is still in dispute, but there is currently a consensus on the autoimmune property of vitiligo.[65] Melanocytes in patients with vitiligo are more susceptible to oxidative stress, leading to the release of exosomes and inflammatory cytokines that activate innate immune response activation, and then yield an adaptive immune response by activating autoreactive cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells targeting melanocytes are specifically responsible for the destruction of melanocytes.[3]

There is currently no known cure for vitiligo, the recommended treatment aims to halt disease progression, including repigmentation, and achieve an acceptable cosmetic result.[66] CG has been shown to be beneficial for immunological skin disorders for its immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory function. CG had therapeutic efficacy and safety in the treatment of mild to moderate alopecia areata with inhibiting CD3+CD8+.[67] Compared with acitretin alone, CG combined with acitretin had better therapeutic efficacy for psoriasis by downregulating Th17 cell differentiation.[68] In patients with simplex Henoch-Schonlein purpura, adjuvant treatment of CG significantly reduced the number of CD8+ cells and improved the proportion of CD4+/CD8+ cells and CD4+ cells.[69] Compared with desloratadine alone, the addition of glycyrrhizin had a better clinical effect for the treatment of chronic urticarial with increasing CD4+ T cells and decreasing IgE and CD8+ T cells.[23] Besides, glycyrrhetinic acid can restore glucocorticoid resistance, playing a glucocorticoid-like effect.[70] Moreover, oxidative stress plays an important role in melanocyte destruction, glycyrrhizin can protect human melanocytes from H2O2-induced oxidative damage via the Nrf2-dependent induction of HO-1.[71] A combination of CG and conventional therapy may yield a synergetic immunoregulatory, anti-inflammatory effect in treatment of vitiligo.

The incidence of the AEs revealed that there was no statistically significant difference between combination of conventional therapy with CG and conventional alone. Besides, both showed a low rate of AEs. In addition to common AEs of conventional therapy such as pruritus, erythema or papule, pseudoaldosteronism should be a specific adverse event of CG, 2 studies found CG might increase blood pressure,[44,57] 9 studies cause edema including lower feet edema, eyelids edema or facial edema,[29,31,34,36,38,40,44,48,57] and 2 studies found hypokalemia.[47,52] Patients with hypertension or cardiovascular disease should be cautious in receiving CG.

There were some limitations to our systematic review. Firstly, the overall risk of bias assessment showed that the quality of enrolled trials was not high. None of the included studies followed blinding of participants and personnel, and all of the included studies did not provide adequate information on allocation concealment and outcome assessment blinding. Second, vitiligo has a serious impact on quality of life and psychological condition; the degree of repigmentation itself may not be an indicator of treatment success for vitiligo. Still, our included studies did not assess the psychological condition and the quality of life. Moreover, the participants in all the included studies were in China, which may introduce genetic and geographical bias. Finally, we had eligible RCTs with different progression and classification of vitiligo, and various lesion sites, regardless of age, gender, or race.

5. Conclusions

Based on current studies, our review demonstrated that compared with conventional therapy alone, CG combined with conventional therapy had a superior efficacy and safety in terms of repigmentation for treating vitiligo. However, since the methodological flaws of the studies we included, well-designed RCTs that include the quality of life as an outcome are warranted.
