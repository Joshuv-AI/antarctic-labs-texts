Adherence to EAT-Lancet diet and odds of irritable bowel syndrome and functional dyspepsia: the Isfahan functional disorders (ISFUN) study
Authors: Asma Salari-Moghaddam, Awat Feizi, Parisa hajihashemi, Alireza Ani, Hamidreza Roohafza, Peyman Adibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNo study has been conducted to investigate the association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and odds of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and functional dyspepsia (FD). This cross-sectional study was aimed to assess the association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and odds of IBS and FD.MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis was done among 1892 Iranian apparently healthy adults, aged 18 to 65 years, in Isfahan, Iran. Usual dietary intakes of participants were assessed using a validated Dish-based, 106-item food frequency questionnaire. To measure participants’ adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet, the EAT-Lancet diet index (ELD-I) was calculated. IBS and FD were assessed using ROME IV criteria.RESULTSIn total, 5.29% and 3.17% of participants had IBS and FD, respectively. The mean age of study participants was 39.64 ± 10.24 years, and the mean BMI was 27.08 ± 4.91 kg/m². After adjustment for potential confounding factors, a significant positive association between lower adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and odds of IBS was revealed (OR: 1.74; 95% CI: 1.00-3.04), but not for FD (OR: 1.37; 95% CI: 0.65–2.89).ConclusionA significant positive association was observed between lower adherence to EAT-Lancet diet and odds of IBS. More prospective studies are needed to affirm these associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01195-5.Keywords: EAT-Lancet diet, ELD-I, Functional gastrointestinal disorders, Disorders of gut-brain interaction

Introduction

Functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) encompass a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by recurring symptoms such as fullness, early satiety, diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain and bloating, in the absence of any organic abnormalities [1, 2]. Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and functional dyspepsia (FD) are the most common FGIDs and are associated with significant impacts on functional status, health-related quality of life, and global health expenditures [3]. The global prevalence of IBS and FD has been reported as 5 to 18% and 10 to 30%, respectively, depending on the socio-demographic characteristics of different countries [3]. The exact etiology of IBS and FD has not yet been elucidated; however, the relevance of genetic susceptibility, immune dysfunction, altered gut-brain axis, dysbiosis, food intolerance, environmental factor, inflammation, and visceral hypersensitivity have been suggested [4].

Most patients with FGIDs report that food intake generates and aggravates their symptoms, while dietary restrictions improve gastrointestinal symptoms [4]. Therefore, dietary approaches are recognized as an important part of the medical management of FGIDs [4]. Recently dietary patterns have gained significant attention in this regard. Analyzing dietary patterns instead of individual nutrients or foods represents a broader picture of the complicated interactions and relationships between different foods and nutrients, and provides a more informative approach to predicting the diet-disease relationship [5]. Although the association of different healthy dietary patterns such as Mediterranean diet [6–10], and the Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH) [11, 12], with IBS and FD has been examined, knowledge about the health benefits of adherence to healthy diet is sparse and inconsistent.

Recent research proposed that a healthy dietary pattern should provide health benefits while reducing environmental impact [13]. Food production, especially meat and dairy, is one of the main drivers of environmental degradation, which is responsible for nearly 30% of global greenhouse gas emissions (GHGE) and 70% of freshwater use [13]. A growing body of evidence suggests that adopting a plant based dietary pattern leads to benefits not only for people but also for planetary health [14]. In this context, in 2019, the EAT-Lancet commission developed a sustainable and plant-based dietary pattern, considers the multiple links between nutrition, health, and the environment and aligns nutrition with planet preservation [15]. The EAT-Lancet reference diet is a 2,500 kcal/d diet that emphasizes intake of healthy foods including vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, and nuts, and de-emphasizes consumption of animal-based foods, saturated fat, and added sugar [16].

Similar to Mediterranean and DASH diets, the EAT-Lancet is rich in fiber and antioxidants, which may reduce inflammation and dysbiosis [17, 18]. Considering the inflammatory nature of IBS and FD, the hypothesis has emerged that healthy dietary patterns may prevent or alleviate FGIDs symptoms by affecting inflammation and gut microbiota. To our knowledge, there is no evidence examining the association of the EAT-Lancet diet with FGIDs in adults. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and IBS and FD, the most common lower and upper FGIDs among Iranian adults.

Methods and materials

Study population

This present cross-sectional analysis was performed within the framework of the prospective cohort Isfahan functional disorders (ISFUN) study. ISFUN was established in 2017, aimed to determine the epidemiology, risk factors, course and prognosis of functional somatic syndrome (FSSs) in a sample of Iranian adults in Isfahan, Iran over a period of four years through yearly visits. Definite data about the design and methodology of the ISFUN was published previously [19]. Apparently healthy adults (aged between 18 and 65) lived in the Kerdabad neighborhood of Isfahan were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: pregnant or lactating women, subjects with diseases such as cancer undergoing treatment, subjects with chronic diseases in the active phase of the disease and under treatment, subjects with physical or mental disability to the degree that could not participate in providing reliable data on themselves (severe depression, psychosis, etc.), and house-bound people who were unable to participate and attend the health center. A total of 1930 adults were recruited using census-based sampling technique. Self-administered questionnaires were used to collect information regarding functional symptoms, psychological assessment, quality of life, socio-demographic and lifestyle factors, including dietary habits, at baseline and following visits. Physical examination and anthropometric measurements were performed at baseline and during subsequent follow-ups.

All participants were asked to complete a written informed consent form before recruitment. The study protocol was approved by the National Research Ethics Committee of the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education and the Research Ethics committee of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (protocol number# IR.MUI.REC.1395.1.149).

Dietary intake assessment

To gather the dietary intakes of the study participants, a validated, self-administered, Willett-format, Dish-based, 106-item Semi-quantitative Food frequency Questionnaire (DS-FFQ) was employed. DS-FFQ was designed and validated specifically for Iranian adults. Further information regarding the design, foods, and the validity of this questionnaire has been reported elsewhere [20]. Participants reported the amount and frequency of their consumption of these 106 food items during the past year based on nine possible responses, ranging from"never or seldom to more than 12 times/day”. Finally, dietary intakes were converted into grams per day by computing the number of portion sizes using household measurements, and nutrients intake were estimated through using adapted version of Nutritionist IV software for Iranians food (Version 7.0; N-Squared Computing, Salem, OR, USA).

The EAT-Lancet diet index construction

Participants’ adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet was measured using the EAT-Lancet diet index (ELD-I), which has been described previously [21]. Briefly, this dietary index was developed based on the components, and cut-offs of the following 14 food groups: whole grains, tubers and starchy vegetables, fruits, dairy foods, beef/lamb/pork, chicken and other poultry, eggs, fish, nuts, saturated oils, unsaturated oils and sweeteners. Assessment of IBS and FD

In the present study diagnostic of IBS and FD was done based on ROME IV criteria for adults [22, 23]. According to the ROME IV criteria, IBS was characterized by recurring abdominal pain that occurs at least once a week in the last three months, accompanied by at least two of the following: changes in bowel habits, stool frequency, or stool appearance. FD was defined as experiencing one or more bothersome symptoms such as postprandial fullness, early satiety, epigastric pain, or epigastric burning frequently or consistently during the last three months.

Assessment of other variables

Anthropometric measurements including weight, height, and waist circumference were measured using standard protocols and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and its level was estimated as the metabolic equivalent (MET) hour/week [24]. The validated Iranian version of Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to assess Depression and anxiety symptoms [25]. HADS questionnaire consists of two sub-scales assessing depression and anxiety symptoms. Each scale includes 7 questions in a Likert scale (0–3) format. Therefore, the overall score ranged from 0 (the lowest degree of depression and anxiety) to 21 (the highest degree of depression and anxiety). Higher scores represent a higher severity of symptoms. Scores of ≤ 7 in either sub-class were regarded as normal, while scores of ≥ 8 were regarded as the presence of depression or anxiety. Dietary habits (chewing, diner-to-sleep-interval, and meal frequency) were assessed using meal pattern checklist [26]. Data on demographic characteristics, including sex, age, education, socio-economic status, and marital status were collected using demographic and socio-economic checklist [27]. Information regarding drugs usage was collected through self-administered questionnaires. The validated Persian version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire was used to evaluate sleep quality [28]. The validity and reliability of the complete Pittsburgh questionnaire for the Iranian population has been confirmed [29].

Statistical analysis

In this study, we categorized participants based on tertiles of the ELD-I rather than median, quartiles, or quintiles to achieve several advantages. Tertiles provide a balanced and simplified method for interpreting data, making it easier to visualize and compare distinct segments. This approach reduces complexity while maintaining interpretability, capturing significant variations within the data without overwhelming granularity. Unlike quartiles or quintiles, tertiles avoid excessive division that might complicate analysis or lead to overfitting. They allow for clearer identification of trends and patterns, especially in scenarios where fewer categories suffice to highlight differences. This method is particularly effective in studies requiring a broad yet discernible division of data into three main groups, thereby facilitating better decision-making and interpretation of findings. General characteristics of study participants were reported as means (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Normality of continuous variables was assessed by Kolmogorov- Smirnov test and Q-Q plot. To compare the mean of basic continuous and categorical characteristics of study participants based on the presence of IBS and FD, independent samples T-test and chi-square test were employed, respectively. Also, the differences across tertiles of the ELD-I were examined using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables. Dietary intakes of participants were compared between subjects with and without IBS and FD as well as across tertiles of the ELD-I using independent samples t-test (or Mann-Whitney U test), and ANOVA (or Kruskal-Wallis), respectively. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to investigate the associations between tertiles of the ELD-I and IBS and FD in crude and multivariable-adjusted models. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for OR are reported. The third tertile of the ELD-I was regarded as the reference group. Age, sex and marital status was adjusted in the first model. Second model was additionally controlled for mental disorders, sleep quality, and BMI. Finally, dietary habits (diner-to-sleep-interval, meal frequency), and daily energy intake (Kcal/day) were adjusted in the multivariable model. To assess the trend of ORs across increasing tertiles of the ELD-I, the tertile categories were considered as quantitative variable in the fitted models. The selection of confounding variables was based on a review of the literature and the observed baseline differences between the dependent and independent variables [30]. All statistical analyses were done using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, Inc., Chicago IL, USA; version 20). P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

The present study included a total of 1892 participants, of whom 100 (5.29%) had IBS, and 60 (3.17%) had FD. The mean age of the participants was 39.64 ± 10.24 years, and the mean BMI was 27.08 ± 4.91 kg/m².

General characteristics of study participants according to the presence or absence of IBS and FD are presented in Table 1. Participants with IBS were more likely to have depression, anxiety, lower sleep quality, and shorter diner-to-sleep-interval compared with those without IBS (P < 0.05). Subjects with FD were more likely to be younger, have depression and anxiety, and were less likely to be married (P < 0.05). Furthermore, patients with FD had lower BMI, sleep quality, less meal frequency, and shorter diner-to-sleep-interval than those without FD (P < 0.05).

Crude and multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for IBS and FD across tertiles of the ELD-I are shown in Table 5. In the crude model, we observed a significant positive association between lower adherence to the EAT-Lancet and IBS (OR: 1.94; 95% CI: 1.15–3.25); such that those with lower adherence to the EAT-Lancet had 94% higher odds of IBS compared with those with higher adherence. This association remained significant even after adjustment for potential confounders (OR: 1.74; 95% CI: 1.00-3.04). In addition, we observed a significant positive association between lower adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and odds of FD in the crude model (OR: 2.22; 95% CI: 1.11–4.44); however, this association became non-significant after adjustment for potential confounders (OR: 1.37; 95% CI: 0.65–2.89).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we observed a significant positive association between lower adherence to EAT-Lancet diet and odds of IBS. However, there was no significant association between adherence to EAT-Lancet diet and odds of FD. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine EAT-Lancet diet in relation to IBS and FD.

Dietary intakes appear to play a crucial role in the development of IBS and FD [1]. Therefore, preventive strategies, including lifestyle modifications, are of high priority in managing gastrointestinal disorders. The EAT-Lancet diet, similar to other healthy dietary patterns such as DASH and Mediterranean diets, promotes health benefits while also reducing environmental impact. To our knowledge, no previous study has examined the association of EAT-Lancet diet and odds for IBS and FD; however, the relationship between dietary patterns and odds of FGIDs has been examined in previous studies [6, 7, 11, 31]. A prospective study involving 44,350 participants found that following a Western dietary pattern was linked to a moderately increased risk of IBS [32]. Another cross-sectional study among Iranian adults reported that a lacto-vegetarian dietary pattern was associated with a reduced odds of IBS; whereas a fast-food dietary pattern was associated with a higher odds of IBS [33]. It is well known that IBS is an inflammatory condition [34]. The EAT-Lancet diet is rich in antioxidants through which may reduce inflammation [17]. Therefore, it is expected that an anti-inflammatory diet could be effective in preventing or managing IBS symptoms. Diet management has become crucial in treating IBS, with initial guidance focusing on adopting a healthy eating pattern and lifestyle [34]. Given the role of diet in preventing and managing IBS, current evidence suggests that dietary management should be integrated into standard IBS treatment protocols [35]. However, we failed to find any significant association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and odds of FD. Lack of finding a significant relationship between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and FD might be attributed to the low number of patients with FD in the present study. In addition, it must be noted that FD is a multifactorial condition and dietary patterns alone may not play a dominant role in its development [6].

The inverse association of EAT-Lancet diet with IBS might be explained by the following mechanisms. The EAT-Lancet diet emphasizes on vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and unsaturated oils, moderate consumption of seafood and poultry, and lower intakes of red and processed meats, added sugar, refined grains and starchy vegetables [15]. Previous studies have highlighted the role of diet and inflammation in the pathogenesis of IBS [32, 34, 36]. Furthermore, considering the established role of mental well-being in managing FGIDs [37]; and the link between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and mental well-being [38]; following the EAT-Lancet diet could be an effective strategy for preventing and managing IBS symptoms. In addition, growing evidence suggests that alterations in gut microbiota are linked to the development and progression of IBS [39]. Recent evidence has emphasized the importance of diet in managing IBS symptoms, particularly through its role in modulating gut microbiota, which has become a key factor in the pathology and treatment of IBS [40]. Given the lack of previous study on the association between EAT-Lancet diet and IBS and FD, further studies are required to shed light on this issue.

This study has some strengths, as well as limitations. The strengths of our study include having a large sample size, using a validated FFQ, and considering a wide range of potential confounders in statistical analyses. However, several limitations should be considered while interpreting our findings. Due to cross-sectional nature of the study, causal relationship could not be established. Therefore, further studies especially with prospective design are required in this area to provide evidence for the causal relationship. In the present study, we used a validated FFQ for dietary assessment and development of the EAT-Lancet diet score; however, measurement errors and misclassification of study participants in terms of exposure could not be avoided. In addition, for assessment of IBS, we used questionnaire-based data in this study. Although the validity of the Rome IV questionnaire has been shown in Iranian adults, the possibility of misclassification of study participants in terms of outcome could not be avoided. Estimating EAT-Lancet diet score and nutrient intakes using tools not specifically tailored for Iranian foods might be another limitation of this study. However, since our findings align with those of earlier studies, which serve as a method of validation, our study results can be considered reliable. Additionally, data regarding the participants’ adherence to specific diets and supplement intake was not gathered, which prevented us from managing their potential confounding effects. Finally, although we controlled for several potential confounders, residual confounders could not be excluded.

In conclusion, we found a significant positive association between lower adherence to EAT-Lancet diet and odds of IBS. However, there was no significant association between EAT-Lancet diet and odds of FD. Further studies, in particular with prospective design, are needed to confirm our results.
