Transvenous lead extraction (TLE) is a well-established solution in the management of cardiac implantable electronic device-related infections. Conversely, indications for TLE due to non-infectious causes—encompassing a variety of clinical scenarios—still raise several concerns, with limited evidence available, particularly regarding the management of nonfunctional leads.1

The aim of this study was to assess the proportion of TLE for non-infectious causes in a real-world clinical setting, with a particular focus on the management of nonfunctional leads and the decision-making process for TLE in these patients. Consecutive patients who underwent TLE from January 2020 to December 2024 across 12 high-volume centers (≥30 procedures per year) were retrospectively included. Each center provided data on TLE indications. In cases involving nonfunctional leads, additional clinical details were collected to investigate the cause of lead failure. The centers were asked to complete a questionnaire designed to evaluate their approach to extracting nonfunctional leads and the main factors influencing such decisions. The study was approved by the local institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

In this 5-year multicenter analysis, among 2349 TLE procedures, infectious indications accounted for the majority (71%), whereas the remaining 29% were due to non-infectious causes. Among TLE procedures performed for nonfunctional leads, the clinical success rate was 97%. Non-powered mechanical sheaths were used in 76% of cases, whereas powered extraction tools were employed in 38%, including laser sheaths in 5% of procedures.

Lead electrical dysfunction was the most common cause of TLE for both high-voltage and pacing lead malfunction (70% and 85%, respectively). Although lead abandonment was technically feasible in the majority of patients due to preserved venous patency (77% of patients with implantable cardioverter defibrillator and 83% of those with pacemaker) (Figure 1B), TLE was nonetheless performed. Procedure-related major complications occurred in 6 patients (1.1%) undergoing TLE for nonfunctional leads, with no deaths reported.

According to the survey, extraction of nonfunctional leads was preferred rather than abandonment, regardless of whether a conservative alternative was technically feasible. The primary rationale for this approach in 50% of centers was to avoid future complex TLE procedures associated with abandoned leads and prolonged dwell times. This behavior was supported by the centers’ experience, showing that up to 30% of TLE procedures for infection involved abandoned leads, which is an independent predictor for clinical failure and complications.2,3 TLE for nonfunctional leads was preferred in younger patients and those in good health, in whom long-term risk associated with an abandoned lead was considered more significant.

Similarly to the ELECTRa registry,4 our analysis shows how nonfunctional leads account for nearly one-quarter of all TLE procedures, with electrical failure and compromised lead integrity representing the main driving reasons. The data we presented reinforce the concept outlined in the recent consensus document, with a greater propensity toward extraction of nonfunctional leads particularly in younger patients, in whom lead abandonment may pose a long-term risk.1 Although mortality rates do not clearly support the strategy of extracting in this clinical scenario, this should be interpreted in the context of competing risks, as TLE carries immediate procedural risk, whereas abandonment shifts risk toward late complications such as lead interference, venous obstruction, and potentially more complex TLE procedures due to abandoned leads with prolonged dwell time.5

Despite the option of a conservative strategy, extraction of nonfunctional leads is primarily aimed at mitigating the risk of potentially more complex and hazardous TLE procedures and preventing negative effects on quality of life, mainly attributable to the decision to abandon a lead. In addition, patients’ younger age and/or good clinical status may favor an extraction strategy due to the lower procedural risk in these subsets.

The primary limitation of this study is its retrospective observational design. Although the aim was to evaluate the prevalence of TLE for nonfunctional leads among highly experienced centers, the lack of data on patients’ clinical characteristics and procedural features limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding this approach. Secondly, this strategy is mainly adopted by centers experienced in TLE procedures, which have a low rate of major complications, typically below 2%, and therefore may not reflect the practice of low-volume institutions. Finally, patients who were not selected for lead extraction were not included in the analysis. Further prospective studies incorporating long-term outcome analyses are needed to confirm our findings.

In this real-world analysis, nonfunctional leads account for a substantial proportion of TLE procedures, primarily driven by lead dysfunction. The decision to perform TLE for nonfunctional leads warrants careful consideration given the widespread use of cardiac implantable electronic devices and increasing population longevity. This strategy, aimed at minimizing the presence of implanted hardware, may help simplify extractions when they become mandatory, thereby preventing therapeutic delays that could negatively impact clinical outcomes.
