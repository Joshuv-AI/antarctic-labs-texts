Achondroplasia treatments in children aged 5 and older
Authors: Emmanuelle Zakheim, Sonal Sachdeva, Daniel Moon, Miguel Nicole Ortiz, Nishant Mistry, Floyd Culler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Achondroplasia, the most prevalent skeletal dysplasia, is a genetic disorder caused by activating mutations in the FGFR3 gene impairing endochondral ossification of long bones. Clinical manifestations include disproportionate short stature and multisystem complications. Management has been limited to supportive care, surgical limb lengthening, and recombinant human growth hormone. The latter two carry significant risks and provide modest benefits, respectively. Recently, targeted molecular therapies have emerged as promising alternatives. This review highlights three investigational agents: Vosoritide, Infigratinib, and Navepegritide. Vosoritide, an injectable C-type natriuretic peptide analog, reduces MAPK pathway overactivation; Infigratinib, an oral FGFR1-3 inhibitor, directly suppresses downstream signaling; and Navepegritide, a long-acting CNP prodrug, sustains MAPK inhibition. Clinical trials in children aged 5 years and older demonstrate improved annualized growth velocity compared to baseline: 1.7 cm/year with Vosoritide, 6.0 cm/year with Infigratinib, and 5.4 cm/year with Navepegritide. Safety profiles are favorable with few to no significant treatment-related adverse events reported in the trials reviewed; radius fracture, adenoidal hypertrophy, and sleep apnea were reported for Vosoritide, injection site reactions were reported for Navepegritide, and nasopharyngitis was reported for Infigratinib. Direct comparisons across trials are limited, but available data suggest all three therapies bring growth trajectories of children with achondroplasia closer to unaffected peers. Long-term outcomes, particularly regarding comorbidities such as foramen magnum stenosis, spinal stenosis, and sleep apnea, remain under investigation. These emerging treatments represent a paradigm shift in achondroplasia management, underscoring the need for head-to-head studies and evaluation of combination strategies to optimize efficacy and durability.

Introduction

Achondroplasia (ACH) is the primary cause of skeletal dysplasia-related short stature, affecting 1 in 20,000–30,000 people and accounting for 90% of disproportionate short stature cases [1, 2]. Inheritance is autosomal dominant, but 80% of patients have a de novo mutation [3]. The underlying genetic etiology of ACH is a defect in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) gene. This defect constitutively activates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) extracellular pathway in chondrocytes leading to inhibition of normal endochondral ossification and limiting bone growth [3, 4]. Clinically, this mutation produces severe short stature (adult males 127 to 135 cm and adult females 120 to 125.5 cm) [5]. Additionally, ACH patients may experience a spectrum of complications over their lifespan, ranging from relatively benign (nasopharyngitis, otitis media, genu varum, delayed motor milestones, flat nasal bridge, and broad forehead), to moderate (obstructive sleep apnea, kyphosis/lordosis, numbness and muscle weakness), to more severe and life-threatening (foramen magnum stenosis, cervicomedullary compression, hydrocephalus, spinal stenosis and sudden death) [6, 7].

In 2021, daily subcutaneous Voxzogo (Vosoritide) was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as the first pathophysiologically-targeted medication for treatment of ACH [8]. Recently, other drug treatments have been developed with two: Truseltiq (Infigratinib) and TransCon CNP (Navepegritide), showing early promise. Orally administered Infigratinib was granted breakthrough therapy designation by the FDA in 2024, and this drug is now showing promising results in phase 2 trials of children with ACH [9]. Once weekly injected Navepegritide has been accepted by the FDA for priority review of its New Drug Application and is expected to have review completion by November 2025 [10]. All three drugs have distinct mechanisms of action.

Vosoritide, infigratinib, and navepegritide methods of action

The genetic mutation most commonly seen in ACH causes constitutive activation of Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 3 (FGFR3), resulting in downstream inhibition of chondrocyte hypertrophy, differentiation, and proliferation (Fig. 1). This ultimately leads to a lack of endochondral ossification and short stature [11].

Vosoritide is a C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP) analog that binds and activates natriuretic peptide receptor-B (NPR-B) on chondrocytes. The CNP-NPR-B complex initiates a signaling cascade that inhibits the downstream over-activation of the MAPK pathway induced by FGFR3 (Fig. 2). By reducing MAPK signaling, Vosoritide disinhibits blocked chondrocyte hypertrophy and differentiation, allowing for improved cartilage growth and ossification [11].

In contrast, Infigratinib is a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor that directly targets the ATP-binding pocket of FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR3, preventing autophosphorylation and subsequent downstream signaling (Fig. 3). Thus, Infigratinib inhibits the FGFR3 pathway by suppressing both the MAPK pathway — disinhibiting chondrocyte hypertrophy and differentiation — and the STAT1 pathway, which disinhibits the block on chondrocyte proliferation seen in ACH. This dual pathway inhibition aims to normalize FGFR3 signaling toward levels observed in non-mutated, healthy chondrocytes.

Infigratinib method of action on chondrocytes and FGFR3 pathway

In contrast to Vosoritide – a synthetic CNP analog, – Navepegritide consists of human CNP-53’s C-terminal 38 amino acids conjugated with polyethylene glycol (PEG). Active CNP-38 is released from the PEG carrier via hydrolytic cleavage of the linker under physiologic conditions, specifically at body temperature and pH [12, 13]. CNP-38 subsequently initiates a signaling cascade that inhibits downstream over-activation of the MAPK pathway induced by FGFR3 (Fig. 4). Although Navepegritide disinhibits blocked chondrocyte hypertrophy and differentiation in a similar manner to that of Vosoritide, its conjugation to PEG allows for a sustained release of active CNP.

Data review

This review will focus specifically on currently available Vosoritide, Infigratinib, and Navepegritide clinical trial growth outcomes in children with ACH 5 years-of-age and older. For each drug a phase 2/3 clinical trial was reviewed for growth outcomes evidenced by annualized growth/height velocity and adverse effects. Vos was a phase 3 study (NCT03197766) which compared Vosoritide treatment against a placebo in children with a mean age of 8.71 years (SD: 2.47) [14]. Infig was a phase 2 study (NCT04265651) of varying oral dosages of Infigratinib in ACH children with a mean age of 7.05 years (SD: 1.99) [9]. Lastly, Nav was a phase 2 (NCT04085523) dose escalation trial of weekly Navepegritide administered subcutaneously in children with a mean age of 5.79 years (SD: 2.613) [15]. These studies were selected specifically for data comparison consistency as they all reported a similar growth metric at the same time point of 12 months. For clinical trials with more than one treatment cohort, the cohort showing the greatest annualized growth velocity was described [9, 15]. Based on the available clinical data, this review can offer an initial analysis of Vosoritide, Infigratinib, and Navepegritide in pediatric patients with ACH.

Annualized growth/height velocity

One of the most common metrics employed to evidence growth efficacy of ACH treatments is increases in annualized growth velocity (AGV), which is synonymous with annualized height velocity (AHV). This is because children with ACH have markedly lower reported annualized growth velocities than their peers. By about school age (5–7 years), ACH children have an annualized growth velocity averaging approximately 4 cm/year, which decreases further by 10 years of age to an average of 3.6 cm/year [16, 17]. In contrast, the average growth velocity for healthy children aged 5–7 years is approximately 6 cm/year and only continues to increase through their pubertal growth spurt [18].

The Vos study reported a least squares mean increase of 1.71 cm/year (CI: 1.40–2.01) after one year of treatment (15 µg/kg/d) from a baseline annualized growth velocity of 4.26 cm/year (SD: 1.53). Infig and Nav both reported the average annualized growth velocity at the end of the one year treatment period. Infig reported a parametric mean annualized growth velocity of 6.02 cm/year (CI: 5.087–6.949) after one year of treatment (0.25 mg/kg/d). Nav reported a least squares mean annualized growth velocity of 5.42 cm/year (CI: 4.74 to 6.11) after one year of treatment (100 mcg CNP/kg). While direct comparisons cannot be made due to the differing study methods, there is evidence showing all three drugs have potential to improve annualized growth velocities in children with ACH closer to those of their healthy peers.

Adverse events

All three drugs have shown positive safety profiles with few to no serious adverse events reported in the three trials reviewed. The Vos study had three reports of significant adverse events: radius fracture, adenoidal hypertrophy, and sleep apnea. In the Infig and Nav study cohort reviewed, no serious adverse events were reported.

The most common reported non-serious adverse events in Infig were nasopharyngitis (25%) and headache (25%). However, it is difficult to clearly determine if the upper respiratory disease-related events observed in the treatment trials are drug-influenced. There is an absence of placebo-controlled groups in Infig trials but Vos and Nav do include placebo cohorts. The Vos placebo group had a higher incidence of nasopharyngitis and upper respiratory tract infections compared to that of the Vosoritide-treated ACH patients (nasopharyngitis was reported in 29.51% of the placebo group compared to 26.67% in the treatment group). Upper respiratory tract infections were reported in 16.39% of placebo patients versus 13.33% of those with ACh receiving the Vosoritide treatment. Likewise, in the Nav cohort the placebo group reported 13.33% upper respiratory tract infections, whereas the treatment group reported none. These findings suggest that the observed respiratory events are likely related to the general risk of upper respiratory infections in school-aged children rather than the treatments themselves.

Overall, Vosoritide, Infigratinib, and Navepegritide appear largely safe, with no significant increase in serious treatment-related side effects. When evaluating therapeutic options for pediatric patients with ACH, the route of administration plays a critical role in adherence, long-term clinical tolerability, and success. Vosoritide relies on daily subcutaneous injections, with 73.33% injection site reactions in the Vos study. Navepegritide relies on weekly subcutaneous injections, and the Nav study reported injection site reactions in 9.09% of patients. These findings suggest that weekly dosing regimen may be better tolerated. However, injectable treatment options can still impose many challenges including fear, discomfort, and needle fatigue. Furthermore, repeated injections in the same area can cause injection site injury, pain, swelling, erythema, and pruritus [19]. In contrast, Infigratinib offers a significant advantage due to its oral administration which eliminates injection site reactions and minimizes treatment-related anxiety for patients and caregivers. There is a greater likelihood of acceptability and adherence with an oral medication like Infigratinib, especially given the extended treatment required for growth enhancement in children with ACH.

Additional therapies

Therapeutic options for achondroplasia aside from Vosoritide, Infigratinib, and Navepegritide including limb-lengthening surgery, recombinant human growth hormone, and several investigational agents targeting FGFR3 signaling or its downstream pathways.

Limb lengthening surgical intervention is used to increase height, though complication and revision rates can be high. A 20 year systematic review of lower limb lengthening in patients with achondroplasia found high complication rates (pin tract infections, nerve injuries, joint contractures, hardware failure, angular deformities) with many repeated surgeries [20]. In a retrospective study (n = 22) of patients who had undergone bilateral lower limb lengthening surgery, 123 total complications were reported including both minor and major adverse effects such as joint stiffness, nerve palsy, and hardware issues [21]. Of note, the American Academy of Pediatrics explicitly states that limb lengthening surgeries are associated with significant short- and long-term complications such that the surgical team must be readily available to manage these [5].

Recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) is only approved for the treatment of achondroplasia in Japan [22]. Although increases in height were observed during the first 2 years of therapy, the effects on body proportions and long-term complications remain unclear [23, 24]. Reported complications include intracranial hypertension, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, and glucose intolerance, all of which are rare but significant risks [25, 26].

Other approaches include anti-FGFR3 monoclonal antibodies, soluble FGFR3 decoy receptors, aptamers against FGF2, and CDK8 inhibitors, all of which are currently in preclinical or early clinical stages [27, 28].

Discussion

The landscape of ACH treatment was significantly altered by the advent of Vosoritide therapy. Prior to this medical breakthrough, ACH patients had little hope of treatment which could safely and effectively enhance their growth. Their only options were invasive limb lengthening surgeries and growth hormone injections, which are no longer considered standards of care for ACH. After Vosoritide, Infigratinib and Navepegritide have now come to compete in this same clinical market, widening growth therapy options for ACH patients.

Overall, a direct comparison of drug efficacy cannot be made due to the differing methods and study designs, but available clinical trial data indicate positive growth outcomes in ACH children. Further studies evaluating a head-to-head comparison of these three drugs must be done to elucidate differences in treatment efficacy. Additionally, the three drugs’ mechanisms of action suggest the possible value of combined or sequential therapy to better inhibit the constitutively active FGFR3 pathway. Perhaps multiple drug therapies could mitigate some of the gradual decline in growth enhancement which tends to occur with increased duration of therapy.

Furthermore, ACH is not characterized solely by short-stature, with life-altering comorbidities (i.e. foramen magnum stenosis, cervical medullary compression, hydrocephalus, and spinal stenosis) playing a large clinical role. Clinical trials regarding Vosoritide have begun to investigate its long-term effects on these ACH aspects, but further research is needed for all three drugs. Improvement in these additional factors will play a large role in determining which drugs are the most beneficial for children with ACH.

Conclusion

The drugs Vosoritide, Infigratinib, and Navepegritide were developed on the basis of FGFR3 signaling molecules and now offer a brighter future to children born with ACH. Time will tell if the major ACH complications will show benefits from a longer duration of therapy. Additional research is needed to evaluate the possible additive effects of multidrug therapy and to determine the effects of earlier life initiation. Further analyses of such studies can employ metrics similar to those discussed here. There is optimism that these emerging novel therapeutics may result in life-changing benefits for affected patients.
