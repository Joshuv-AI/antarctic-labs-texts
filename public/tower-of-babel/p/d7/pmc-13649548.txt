A single centre retrospective study of the long‑term weight and pregnancy-related outcomes in women of reproductive age before and after liraglutide exposure
Authors: Tariq Chukir, Hubaib Haider, Shaunak Sarkar, Siddhant Nair, Rami Kokash, Odette Chagoury, Shahrad Taheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of GLP‑1 receptor agonist exposure prior to pregnancy on pregnancy-related outcomes remains unclear, with studies reporting mixed findings.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study evaluating bodyweight and pregnancy-related outcomes among women of reproductive age who were initiated on liraglutide for obesity management and had 2–5 years of follow-up. We compared pregnancies occurring before and after liraglutide exposure in the same women.ResultsA total of 556 women met the inclusion criteria of which 97 (17.4%) conceived and 459 (82.6%) did not conceive after liraglutide exposure. Baseline characteristics were comparable. After 2–5 years, the mean final weight change was significantly different between women who conceived compared to those who did not (+1.1 ± 11.3% vs. −6.7 ± 9.9%, p < 0.001). The nadir weight reduction on liraglutide in women who conceived was –6.2 ± 7.8%. Gestational weight change was similar in pre- and post-exposure pregnancies (8.0 ± 10.9% vs. 9.2 ± 11.8%, p = 0.554). Additionally, there were no differences in gestational diabetes (32.1% vs. 37.7%, p = 0.629), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (9.2% vs. 0%, p = 0.063), need for c-section (33.3% vs. 38.9%, p = 0.549), pre-term delivery (3.7% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.500), large for gestational age (5.5% vs. 5.5%, p = 1) or small for gestational age infants (11.1% vs. 9.3%, p = 1) in pre- vs. post-exposure pregnancies. Subgroup analysis demonstrated a lower gestational weight gain with metformin use during pregnancy but was not statistically significant (5.8 ± 9.1 kg vs. 8.2 ± 9.5 kg, p = 0.369). The proportion of women with inconsistent obesity medication use during the study period was higher in those who conceived compared to those who did not (100% vs. 36.5%).ConclusionPreconception liraglutide exposure, followed by discontinuation before pregnancy, was associated with weight regain but not with excess gestational weight gain in our within-individual design. Despite a small sample size, we did not observe increased adverse gestational outcomes in the post-exposure pregnancies.Subject terms: Obesity, Obesity

Introduction

Obesity represents one of the most pressing global public health challenges, and its burden among women of reproductive age is particularly significant. Global estimates in this age group vary, but both the United States and Qatar are significantly affected. The prevalence of pre-pregnancy obesity in the United States and Qatar reached 29% and 41%, respectively [1, 2]. Obesity during the reproductive years is associated with a wide spectrum of adverse health outcomes, including ovulatory dysfunction, subfertility, early pregnancy loss, and an increased risk of maternal complications such as gestational diabetes mellitus and preeclampsia. The likelihood of these complications rises progressively with increasing body mass index (BMI) [3–5].

Weight reduction has been shown to restore ovulation and reduce the risk of both foetal and maternal complications [6, 7]. Therefore, it is routinely recommended for women with obesity who are planning to conceive. Many women struggle to achieve clinically significant weight reduction through lifestyle changes alone [8]. Bariatric surgery remains the most effective intervention for achieving sustainable weight loss and has been associated with a significant reduction in pregnancy-related morbidities [9, 10]. However, it may increase the risk of delivering a small-for-gestational-age infant or intrauterine growth restriction [11].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are increasingly being used in clinical practice to manage obesity in women of reproductive age. Their use is contraindicated during pregnancy and lactation, requiring their discontinuation at least 1–2 months prior to conception [12, 13]. Available evidence suggests that stopping these medications often leads to significant weight regain, raising concerns about their long-term effectiveness for women of reproductive age who are planning to conceive [14].

The impact of GLP‑1 receptor agonist exposure prior to pregnancy on pregnancy-related outcomes remains unclear, with studies reporting mixed findings [15]. In a retrospective analysis of the U.S. TriNetX Collaborative Network, exposure to GLP‑1 therapy within 24 months preceding pregnancy was associated with a lower risk of several obstetric complications, including gestational diabetes mellitus, preterm delivery, and caesarean birth [16]. Conversely, a more recent retrospective study reported that prior GLP‑1 RA exposure was associated with greater gestational weight gain and higher risks of preterm delivery, gestational diabetes, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [17]. A key limitation of these two studies is that exposed and unexposed pregnancies are frequently matched on post‑treatment BMI. Such matching may misrepresent the initial risk profile of women who required pharmacologic obesity management.

We conducted a retrospective study evaluating the long-term bodyweight and pregnancy-related outcomes among women of reproductive age who initiated liraglutide at a comprehensive weight management centre in Qatar. Our study employed a within‑individual design, comparing pregnancies occurring before and after liraglutide exposure in the same women. This design more effectively isolates changes in pregnancy outcomes by anchoring each woman’s results to her own pre‑treatment risk profile.

Subjects and methods

This retrospective study involved existing data and was determined to be minimal risk without direct participant contact. Therefore, it has been exempted by the Institutional Review Boards at Weill Cornell Medicine– Qatar (IRB 23–00039) and Hamad Medical Corporation (MRC-01-24-384).

We investigated long-term outcomes in women of reproductive age, defined as those aged 18–45 years, with overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 27) who were prescribed liraglutide for obesity management at the Qatar Metabolic Institute (QMI) between 2017 and 2022. Electronic health records were queried to identify all outpatient visits at our comprehensive obesity centre in which liraglutide therapy was newly initiated for obesity management between 2017 and 2022. Identification was performed using medication order records. A total of 2595 individuals were initiated on liraglutide treatment for obesity management. Adherence to treatment was verified by reviewing clinical notes. Women were excluded if they were: younger than 18 years, older than 45 years, were not taking liraglutide for at least 6 months, switched to other obesity medications e.g. semaglutide or tirzepatide. Patients who were switched to a newer GLP‑1 receptor agonist at the last visit were noted. A total of 556 women of reproductive age received liraglutide and met the inclusion criteria. Follow-up data were available for all participants at ≥2 years, and for 424 (76%) and 379 (68%) participants at ≥3 and ≥4 years, respectively.

Clinical data, including demographics, associated conditions, body weight, pregnancy-related outcomes and medications were extracted from the electronic medical records. All women attending QMI received counselling on lifestyle and behaviour modifications provided by physicians and/or dietitians.

Patients were divided into two groups: women who conceived during the study period and women who did not conceive. This investigation was designed as a real-world study reflecting routine clinical practice. Pregnancy intention varies substantially across individuals and is not consistently recorded in the medical record. The weight outcomes of the study were the percentage change in weight from baseline to the last documented weight up until November 2024 (final weight) and the nadir percentage change in weight over the follow up period. These timepoints represent clinical milestones rather than fixed intervals.

We also investigated pregnancy-related outcomes, comparing outcomes between pregnancy occurring prior to liraglutide exposure and pregnancy occurring after liraglutide exposure within the same individual. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software. Continuous variables were summarised as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between pregnant and non-pregnant groups were conducted using independent two-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, depending on data distribution, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Paired analyses comparing pregnancy outcomes within the same individual were performed using paired t-tests for continuous variables and McNemar’s tests for categorical variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Formal tests of homogeneity of variance were not performed due to small sample size.

Results

A total of 556 women of reproductive age received liraglutide (mean dose 3 mg) and met the inclusion criteria, of whom 97 (17.4%) conceived and 459 (82.6%) did not after liraglutide exposure. Women who conceived were younger (34.4 ± 5.7 vs. 36.8 ± 7.0, p = 0.002), had a lower prevalence of hepatic steatosis (11.3% vs. 21.4%, p = 0.024) and bariatric surgery (19.6% vs. 29.4%, p = 0.049). Other baseline characteristics were comparable between the 2 groups (Table 1).

Gestational weight change was similar in pre-exposure to liraglutide pregnancies and post-exposure pregnancies (8.0 ± 10.9% vs. 9.2 ± 11.8%, p = 0.554). Additionally, there were no differences in gestational diabetes (32.1% vs. 37.7%, p = 0.629), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (9.2% vs. 0, p = 0.063), need for c-section (33.3% vs. 38.9%, p = 0.549), pre-term delivery (3.7% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.500), large for gestational age (5.5% vs. 5.5%, p = 1) or small for gestational age infants (11.1% vs. 9.3%, p = 1) in pre- vs. post-exposure pregnancies (Fig. 2). A subgroup analysis of the post-exposure pregnancies demonstrated a lower gestational weight gain with metformin use during pregnancy (5.8 ± 9.1 kg) compared with those not using metformin (8.2 ± 9.5 kg); however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.369).

Pregnancy-related outcomes.

Pregnancy-related outcomes in pregnancies occurring before and after preconception liraglutide exposure among women with obesity (n = 53 pregnancies with available data). Gestational weight change and pregnancy outcomes were not statistically different between pregnancies before and after liraglutide exposure. Mean gestational weight change was 8.0 ± 10.9% in pre-exposure pregnancies and 9.2 ± 11.8% in post-exposure pregnancies (p = 0.554). No statistically significant differences were observed across maternal and neonatal outcomes; however, the study may be underpowered to detect modest differences.

The proportion of women with inconsistent obesity medication use during the study period, defined as either discontinuation of therapy or intermittent use, was higher in those who conceived compared to those who did not (100% vs. 36.5%). All women who conceived were not taking liraglutide at the time of pregnancy. A transition to newer-generation obesity medications was observed at the final visit in both groups. For example, participants who conceived during the study period and those who did not were switched to semaglutide (18.8% vs. 27.8%) and tirzepatide (1.6% vs. 4.2%) during the last visit.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that liraglutide use in women of reproductive age was associated with clinically meaningful weight reduction over 2–5 years in a real‑world clinical setting. In the SCALE Obesity and Prediabetes trial, participants receiving liraglutide 3.0 mg achieved a mean weight loss of approximately 8.0% at 56 weeks, compared with 2.6% in the placebo group. The slightly lower total weight reduction observed in our cohort likely reflects the inconsistent medication use that is common in real‑world practice [18–20]. Women who conceived during the study period experienced significant weight regain, consistent with patterns reported in clinical trials following withdrawal of GLP‑1 receptor agonists [21]. These findings underscore the chronic, relapsing nature of obesity and reinforce the need for ongoing treatment to maintain long‑term weight reduction with liraglutide.

Greater gestational weight gain, particularly in the first trimester, has been associated with increased risks of obstetrical complications, including gestational diabetes and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [22–24]. Concerns have been raised that prior exposure to GLP‑1 receptor agonists, followed by discontinuation before conception, may contribute to excess gestational weight gain. For example, one study reported that women with pre‑pregnancy GLP‑1 exposure experienced greater gestational weight gain compared with those without such exposure [17]. In contrast, our within‑individual comparison showed that gestational weight gain in pregnancies following liraglutide exposure was comparable to that observed in each woman’s prior pregnancy without GLP‑1 exposure. While gestational weight gain is influenced by multiple biological and behavioural factors, findings from Maya et al. suggested a trend toward greater gestational weight gain with semaglutide compared with liraglutide, although the differences did not reach statistical significance [17].

Furthermore, our results did not demonstrate an increased risk of obstetrical complications, including gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, caesarean delivery, preterm delivery, or abnormal birth weight for gestational age, in pregnancies following preconception liraglutide exposure despite the higher maternal age. Existing literature on this topic has been mixed, and heterogeneity in findings may reflect differences in study design, patient populations, comparator groups, and residual confounding, including uncertainty around true medication adherence in observational studies [16, 17]. Our study design addressed several of these limitations by confirming liraglutide exposure prior to pregnancy, assessing baseline characteristics during each pregnancy and using a within‑individual comparison. The lack of pregnancy-related benefits in our cohort may be attributable to the substantial weight regain prior to conception, which resulted in comparable pre-pregnancy characteristics, particularly weight and HbA1c levels. These important variables were not adequately accounted for in prior studies of this population. Our exploratory subgroup analysis suggested lower gestational weight gain with metformin use during pregnancy. Although not statistically significant, this observation is hypothesis-generating and merits prospective evaluation.

This study has several strengths, including the evaluation of long‑term outcomes of liraglutide in a special population beyond 2 years. The use of integrated electronic health records in a healthcare system that provides >90% of the care in the country enabled longitudinal tracking of weight, medication use, and pregnancy outcomes. This approach would not have been feasible in settings where care is provided in non-integrated systems.

In conclusion, this retrospective study demonstrated the long‑term efficacy of liraglutide in women of reproductive age. Preconception liraglutide exposure, followed by discontinuation before pregnancy, was associated with weight regain but not with excess gestational weight gain or increased obstetric complications in our cohort. While these findings provide reassuring real‑world data on liraglutide, they should be interpreted with caution given the study’s design and sample size. Although the within‑individual design addresses some of the limitations of prior studies, the average 4‑year increase in maternal age between pre‑ and post‑liraglutide pregnancies is an important confounder as higher maternal age might increase the risk of some pregnancy-related complications. Due to the limited sample size, we were not able to perform a meaningful sensitivity analysis adjusting for maternal age. Notably, we did not observe increased adverse gestational outcomes in the post‑exposure pregnancies within this small sample. Additional research is needed to confirm findings from available observational data and to evaluate the reproductive and obstetric effects of newer GLP‑1 therapies. Such work will be essential to inform preconception counselling, particularly for women with obesity who are candidates for GLP‑1–based treatments or bariatric surgery. Newer-generation obesity medications, particularly tirzepatide, were not widely available on formulary for most of the study duration. In current practice, liraglutide is being phased out at our institution, with increasing reliance on newer-generation medications.

Although liraglutide represents an older generation of GLP‑1 receptor agonists, outcomes in women of reproductive age remain highly relevant, particularly in resource‑limited settings where access to newer agents such as semaglutide and tirzepatide remains limited. The increasing availability of generic liraglutide formulations further underscores its continued clinical relevance [25]. Our results provide valuable insight into the long‑term outcomes of an older‑generation GLP‑1 receptor agonist in women of reproductive age; information that is important for guiding clinical discussions in this special population [26–28].
