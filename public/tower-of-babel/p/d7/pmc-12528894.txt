Biochemical characterization of a flavodiiron protein from bird parasite Histomonas meleagridis: superoxide as a reaction intermediate
Authors: Subrata Munan, Belem Yoval-Sánchez, Canglin Yao, Javier Seravalli, Evana N Abdulaziz, Mina L Heacock, Achyuta Ramesh, Alexander Galkin, Valentin Cracan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHistomonas meleagridis is a parasitic protozoan which causes histomoniasis (blackhead disease) in a wide range of birds, including domesticated chickens and turkeys, representing a significant health problem in avian veterinary medicine. Despite being classified as an anaerobic parasite, H. meleagridis can survive transient exposure to oxygen (O2) while little is known about the mechanisms that allow this organism to cope with exposure to varying O2 levels. Inspecting the H. meleagridis genome revealed that this organism possesses two flavodiiron proteins (FDPs) belonging to classes A and F, which are known in other organisms to reduce O2 to H2O. The class F FDP found in H. meleagridis (HmFDPF) has domain organization previously described for similar proteins and contains a middle rubredoxin-like and C-terminal NADH:rubredoxin oxidoreductase domains in addition to an N-terminal FDP core. Here, we present extensive biochemical characterization of this protein to improve understanding of the coordinated activities of multiple redox centers that enable NADH-dependent reduction of O2 to H2O. We found that although superoxide is an HmFDPF reaction intermediate, only a very small amount of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is released as a byproduct. Moreover, HmFDPF does not possess NADH:H2O2 oxidoreductase activity and the enzyme is prone to quick inactivation by H2O2. In summary, our work expands our understanding of how microaerophilic protozoan parasites maintain their optimal redox state and minimize O2 presence in their local environment, revealing mechanisms that can be exploited as drug targets against microaerophilic parasites.Keywords: flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), flavin mononucleotide (FMN), flavoprotein, superoxide dismutase (SOD), superoxide ion, catalase, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), oxidase, oxidation-reduction (redox), hydrogen peroxide

Histomonas meleagridis is a protozoan avian parasite that causes histomoniasis (blackhead disease) in turkeys and other domestic birds, characterized by pathological lesions in the caeca and liver (1, 2, 3). The first line of defense and prophylaxis in avian veterinary medicine for H. meleagridis was supplementation with nitroimidazoles and nitrofurans, two classes of antimicrobial agents that are effective against anaerobic bacteria and protozoa (1, 4, 5, 6, 7). These molecules are exclusively toxic in anaerobic/microaerophilic organisms because strong intracellular reductive environment in these organisms favors reduction of both nitroimidazoles and nitrofurans into their respective anion free radical forms (7, 8). However, due to changes in regulatory practices, both compounds were recently banned in both North America and the European Union, reestablishing a focus on the development of new approaches to treat histomoniasis in the context of the life cycle of this microaerophilic parasite (1, 8, 9). H. meleagridis, similar to human protozoan parasite Trichomonas vaginalis, lacks typical mitochondria and instead possesses hydrogenosomes (confirmed both using microscopy and immunohistochemistry), organelles that are characteristic of protozoan parasites from oxygen (O2)-deficient environments (10, 11). In these organelles, a fermentative oxidation of pyruvate yields acetate, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen with concomitant production of ATP exclusively by substrate-level phosphorylation (4, 12, 13). Although hydrogenosomes contain O2-sensitive enzymes, H. meleagridis and similar protozoan parasites are known to withstand transient exposure to O2 because of the presence of evolutionary adaptations that allow them to tolerate low O2 concentrations (4, 8, 14).

One of the most well-studied enzymatic systems capable of detoxifying O2 in microaerophilic bacteria, archaea, and protozoa are flavodiiron proteins (FDPs) (15, 16, 17, 18). Because FDPs in the most simple molecular architecture cannot receive electrons directly from NAD(P)H to catalyze the H2O-forming oxidase reaction and/or reduction of nitric oxide (NO) to nitrous oxide and water, they require various redox partners either as separate proteins or as gene fusions with the “FDP core” (Fig. 1 [reactions 1 and 2] and Fig. 2A) (16, 19, 20). The “FDP core” is composed of a head-to-tail dimeric assembly of an N-terminal β-lactamase–like subdomain, which contains a catalytic Fe-Fe (diiron) center and a C-terminal flavodoxin-like subdomain, the latter of which binds the flavin mononucleotide (FMN) cofactor (15, 16, 17, 18) (Fig. 2A). This head-to-tail dimeric assembly is absolutely required for catalysis, as in a monomer the distance between the diiron center and FMN is ∼40 Å, while in a dimer, this distance is reduced to ∼9 Å to allow for an efficient electron transfer (ET) (21, 22, 23) (Fig. 2A). This domain organization is termed class A FDP (15, 16, 17, 18) and is found in various bacteria, archaea, and multiple protozoan microaerophilic organisms, including Giardia intestinalis (22), Entamoeba histolytica (24, 25), T. vaginalis (21, 26), and H. meleagridis (this study, Table S1).

It is assumed that in bacteria and archaea, class A FDPs receive electrons from reduced rubredoxin, which in turn, is reduced by NAD(P)H:rubredoxin oxidoreductase (termed as NROR) or similar enzymes; however in all previous experiments with recombinant class A FDPs, only artificial substrates such as Escherichia coli NADH:flavorubredoxin oxidoreductase (FlRd-Red) and a truncated rubredoxin domain of flavorubredoxin from E. coli (FlRd) were used (18, 22, 27). Only recently a novel example of a complete electron-transfer chain to a class A FDP from Fusobacterium nucleatum was identified (28). Bystrom and Wolthers demonstrated that the gene encoding class A FDP from F. nucleatum is dicistronic with a gene encoding a multicomponent butyryl-CoA reductase (28). This butyryl-CoA reductase protein is composed of an N-terminal butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase containing an flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor, a middle electron-transfer flavoprotein (Etfα) with a second FAD cofactor, and a C-terminal Rb-like domain which harbors a FeS center. Both proteins form an α4β4 heterotetrameric complex, which couples butyryl-CoA to crotonyl-CoA conversion through a H2O-forming oxidase reaction within the FDP core (28). Based on analysis of multiple bacterial genomes, the authors also concluded that in numerous obligate anaerobic bacteria, a soluble terminal oxidase (such as class A FDP) serves as a vent for electrons and couples oxidation of multiple substrates to O2 (and potentially NO) reduction (28). Therefore, it is not surprising that in some bacteria, archaea, and protozoa, the FDP core is fused with additional domains that enable efficient ET (in a recent classification designated as classes B–H) (16). For example, in the class F FDP (FDPF), two domains are present (Rb and NROR) in addition to the N-terminal FDP core (19, 21) (Fig. 2A). This domain architecture represents a soluble ET chain which couples NAD(P)H oxidation to O2 or a “self-sufficient” H2O-forming NAD(P)H oxidase (19, 21).

Sequence analysis revealed that H. meleagridis contains both class A FDP and FDPF. We concentrated on the latter as it does not require any additional redox partners and we assumed that FDPF catalyzes the H2O-forming reaction using NAD(P)H in a similar manner to previously characterized enzymes from T. vaginalis (21). Through biochemical characterization, we confirmed that H. meleagridis class F FDP (HmFDPF) is a “self-sufficient” H2O-forming oxidase that uses primarily NADH as a source of reducing equivalents. In addition, we demonstrated that this enzyme is strictly O2-specific and does not react with NO or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which contrasts to previous studies with FDPF from Clostridium difficile and class H FDP from Syntrophomonas wolfei (19, 29). Unexpectedly, we observed that superoxide (O2•−) is the reaction intermediate for FDPFs. When the reaction mixture is supplemented with superoxide dismutase (SOD), superoxide species are converted to H2O2, which leads to a rapid enzyme inactivation. Overall, our study provides an important insight into how multiple redox components within a multidomain terminal oxidase FDP support ET without substantial side reactions. We anticipate that our study will facilitate the development of pharmacological approaches distinct from nitroimidazoles and related drugs for combating microaerophilic protozoan parasites, including H. meleagridis in domesticated birds and T. vaginalis in humans.

Results

Analysis of protein sequences

The XP_067779672.1 gene in H. meleagridis encodes a FDPF which is 876 aa in length (Table S1). The HmFDPF represents a domain organization recently described for FDPFs from T. vaginalis (60% aa identity) (21) and C. difficile (41% aa identity) (19) (Fig. 2A). The FDPF is a natural fusion between an N-terminal base unit containing both a diiron center and an FMN cofactor (when found separately, this base unit is called a class A FDP; in H. meleagridis XP_067769703.1 gene encodes a separate stand-alone class A FDP, which is 419 aa in length), a middle rubredoxin-like domain and a C-terminal NAD(P)H:rubredoxin oxidoreductase (NROR) domain (Fig. 2A and Table S1). We note that although the middle domain in FDPFs is designated as “rubredoxin-like,” in this domain, the two cysteine motifs that coordinate iron are separated by 12 residues as observed in rubrerythrins (in a classical stand-alone rubredoxin, the two cysteine motifs are separated by 29 residues) (21, 28). Interestingly, in H. meleagridis, three genes (XP_067770255.1, XP_067779717.1, and XP_067778086.1) encode three rubrerythrins, which are homologous to the middle rubredoxin-like domain of HmFDPF, all of which are 193 aa in length. Furthermore, in these proteins, the two cysteine motifs are also separated by 12 residues (Table S1). The closest homolog of the C-terminal NROR domain of HmFDPF in H. meleagridis is a dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase which is 472 aa in length (Table S1).

Purification of HmFDPF and its spectroscopic properties

HmFDPF was heterologously expressed in E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells grown in terrific broth (TB) medium supplemented with the Mohr's salt (ammonium iron(II) sulfate) under aerobic conditions, as described under “Experimental procedures”. Purified recombinant HmFDPF migrated on the SDS-PAGE consistent with its molecular weight of 96 kDa, predicted based on the amino acid sequence (Fig. 3A). Size-exclusion chromatography revealed that HmFDPF eluted as 283 ± 1 kDa protein, suggesting that it forms a dimer with a shape that deviates from a typical globular protein (Fig. S1). Because FDPFs contain multiple cofactors, it was important to evaluate the cofactor loading of the purified protein sample. We observed that purified HmFDPF had occupancy per monomer with FAD (0.90 ± 0.05), FMN (1.06 ± 0.05), and Fe (2.81 ± 0.10) which corresponds to a holo-enzyme form relative to all redox centers based on the molecular architecture of this protein (Tables 1, 2 and Fig. 2A). The prevalence of zinc (0.19 ± 0.01 per monomer) and other trace metals was very low despite the heterologous expression being performed in E. coli grown in rich TB medium (zinc incorporation instead of iron in the diiron center can lead to inactive FDP) (Table S2) (30). The purified HmFDPF protein was brownish in color, and the UV-visible absorbance spectrum had composite features of a flavoenzyme (the FMN-containing N-terminal domain and the FAD-containing C-terminal NROR domain) and a rubredoxin (due to the presence of Fe(SCys)4 center in the middle rubredoxin-like domain) (Figs. 2A and 3B). We note that in parallel with obtaining a holo-form of the enzyme we also collected fractions of HmFDPF with low FMN/Fe2+ occupancy of the N-terminal FDP domain (see sections on the HmFDPF with low FMN content [LowFMN-HmFDPF] below).

NADH and O2 are preferred substrates for HmFDPF

We and others recently demonstrated that FDPFs from T. vaginalis and C. difficile directly receive electrons from NAD(P)H to catalyze a H2O-forming oxidase reaction (19, 21) (Fig. 1 [reaction 1]). Similarly, in our Michaelis–Menten analysis with different NAD(P)H concentrations in air-equilibrated buffer (250 μM of O2) at 37 °C, we established that the HmFDPF enzyme had a clear preference toward NADH compared to NADPH with Vmax(NADH) and KM(NADH) values of 76 ± 9 μmol min−1 mg−1 and 75 ± 3 μM, respectively, while Vmax(NADPH) and KM(NADPH) values (based on theoretical Michaelis–Menten equation fits) were 0.18 ± 0.04 μmol min-1 mg-1 and 1039 ± 261 μM (Table 3 and Fig. 3C).

We next used a high-resolution respirometer (Oroboros Oxygraph-2k) to monitor O2 consumption during the HmFDPF-catalyzed reaction. In air-equilibrated buffer (250 μM of O2) at 25 °C, we observed that the NADH:O2 stoichiometry of the reaction was 2:1 as for each 50 μM NADH addition, ∼25 μM of O2 was consumed (Fig. 4A). Addition of catalase at the end of the experiment did not lead to a substantial increase in O2 concentration, suggesting that H2O2 was not accumulated at significant levels which were consistent with a water-forming oxidase reaction. However, we observed that the enzyme underwent slow inactivation over time, which was clearly visible in the reaction progress curves after addition of a third NADH bolus for both O2 consumption and NADH oxidation (Fig. 4, A and C). Multiple redox centers of HmFDPF can be a source of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anion (O2•−) and H2O2, which in turn can lead to enzyme inactivation. We therefore repeated our experiments when both catalase and SOD were included in the reaction mixture (in this scenario, any superoxide formed is converted to H2O2, which is subsequently transformed into O2 and water) (Fig. 4, A and C; see also “Experimental procedures” [reactions S3 and S4]). Under these experimental conditions, no lag in the reaction progress curves was observed, and the enzyme effectively consumed all three NADH boluses at similar reaction rates (compare red and gray traces in Fig. 4, A and C).

A typical reaction progress curve contains information about reaction rates at different substrate concentrations, which allows us to fit a Michaelis–Menten-like hyperbolic model and estimate KM (31). We applied this analysis to the reaction catalyzed by HmFDPF when the starting O2 concentration was 40 μM (as described under “Experimental procedures”), and the reaction mixture contained both SOD and catalase to minimize enzyme inactivation (Fig. 5, A–C). The apparent KM value for O2 of HmFDPF was 0.37 ± 0.09 μM, suggesting a very high affinity, comparable with that of cytochrome c oxidase (32, 33) (Table 3). Moreover, in our analysis of the O2 consumption, the specific activity for HmFDPF was 13.35 ± 1.46 μmol/min/mg, which was consistent with the values obtained during the NADH oxidation experiments (taking into account a 2-to-1 NADH:O2 stoichiometry and that the O2 consumption experiment was performed at 25 °C) (Table 3).

Furthermore, we tested other low molecular weight electron acceptors that were previously shown to react with multidomain FDPF and class H FDP NO and H2O2 (19, 29) (Fig. 1 [reactions 2 and 3]). To evaluate possible reactivity with NO, we monitored the NADH oxidation activity of HmFDPF under anaerobic conditions with different concentrations of NO donors (diethylamine NONOate, PROLI NONOate, and NOC-5) (Fig. S2, A–F). While we observed robust NO generation using 2,3-diaminonaphthalene (DAN) in a dose-dependent manner, no NADH consumption was observed when HmFDPF was incubated with NADH and different concentrations of all 3 NO donors under anaerobic conditions (Fig. S2, A–F). Because FDPF from C. difficile and class H FDP from S. wolfei were previously reported to react with H2O2, we also measured HmFDPF activity under anaerobic conditions in the presence of NADH and H2O2 (Fig. S3A). We did not detect any measurable NADH:H2O2 oxidoreductase activity in our experiments with HmFDPF (Fig. S3A).

Rapid mixing experiments with NADH and O2

We also used HmFDPF to study the optical changes that occur upon rapid mixing with NADH (Fig. S4, A–D). The experiments were performed aerobically, thus we assumed that the O2 concentration in both reaction syringes and the final mix was ∼250 μM. The absorbance change at 340 nm (Fig. S4, A and B) shows an amplitude equivalent to ∼88 μM oxidation of NADH, about eight turnovers of enzyme catalysis. The amplitude was higher than what was expected from an initial 74 μM NADH concentration after mixing. We posit that the reduction of FAD to FADH2 was also contributing to changes at 340 nm. Since the conditions of this experiment were not truly pre-steady state (NADH to enzyme ratio of ∼7), the observed exponential rate of 5 s-1 does not have to be either equal or higher than kcat for HmFDPF. We estimated that the steady-state rate under O2 saturation and at a 74 μM final NADH concentration should be ∼50% of kcat (114 s−1) or 57 s−1. The steady-state rate observed in the stopped-flow experiment thus corresponds to 74 μM × 5 s−1/10.8 μM = 34 s−1. This rate is much slower than the steady-state rate, suggesting that at low reductant to HmFDPF ratios, the turnover is somehow inhibited. The same reaction conducted with 295 μM NADH (27-fold NADH to enzyme ratio) yielded an observed steady-state rate of ∼75 s−1, which is a lot closer to the expected value from initial rates of ∼ 91 s−1. Nevertheless, at 453 nm (Fig. S4C), a very fast FAD reduction with a rate constant (104 s−1) similar to kcat was observed, albeit with an amplitude of 2.1 μM (based on an extinction coefficient for FAD of 11,300 M−1 cm−1). This is consistent with a mechanism in which only about 20% of the FAD undergoes reoxidation via internal transfer of electrons following the reduction of the flavin by NADH. Finally, at 561 nm (Fig. S4D), there are no significant absorbance changes observed, suggesting that changes in the absorbance of the FeS center are too fast to be observed, and to study them anaerobic stopped-flow experiments are required to evaluate both reductive and oxidative half-reactions.

Biochemical properties of LowFMN-HmFDPF

In order to better understand ET between different redox centers within the FDPF during protein purification, we also collected HmFDPF fractions with relatively low FMN and Fe content (0.19 ± 0.01 and 1.05 ± 0.11 per monomer, respectively) and the C-terminal NROR domain which was almost fully populated with FAD (0.85 ± 0.02 per monomer) (Tables 1, 2, Figs. 2B and 3, A and B). LowFMN-HmFDPF had a characteristic UV-visible absorbance spectrum feature compared to a holo-form as 451 nm and 475 nm peaks formed a distinctive “twin peaks” feature (Fig. 3B). During size-exclusion chromatography, LowFMN-HmFDPF eluted as 335 ± 3 kDa protein, suggesting that it is less compact dimer compared to a holo-form, something which we previously reported for FDPF from T. vaginalis (21) (Fig. S1). LowFMN-HmFDPF had a lower Vmax(NADH) of 13 ± 1 μmol min−1 mg−1 versus 76 ± 9 μmol min−1 mg−1 and a lower KM(NADH) of 33 ± 4 μM versus 75 ± 3 μM compared to a holo-form (Table 3 and Fig. 3, C and D). Although steady-state kinetic parameters based on initial rates for LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme resulted only in ∼2.7-fold decrease in kcat/KM, a robust enzyme inactivation was observed over the course of the reaction (Table 3 and Fig. 4, B and D). Exogenously added to the reaction mixture FMN or Fe2+ (100-fold excess) did not protect LowFMN-HmFDPF from inactivation (Fig. S5, A–D). We note that in experiments when FMN or both FMN and Fe2+ were added enzyme inactivation was even more pronounced (Fig. S5, C and D). Notably, when we included both SOD and catalase in the reaction mixture, we were able to completely abolish enzyme inactivation, suggesting that increased H2O2 production in the reaction may be responsible for the enzyme inactivation (Fig. 4, B and D). We also established that the apparent KM for O2 of LowFMN-HmFDPF was 0.55 ± 0.21 μM, which is not significantly different compared to the apparent KM of the holo-enzyme (0.37 ± 0.09 μM) (Table 3 and Fig. 5, A–C).

To better understand the mode of enzyme inactivation, we measured H2O2 levels during the course of the reaction catalyzed by both holo-enzyme and LowFMN-HmFDPF (we note that in these continuous assays, SOD was present to convert any superoxide formed to H2O2, which was then consumed by the Amplex Red/horseradish peroxidase [HRP] reagent) (Fig. 6A). We found that around 40% less of the total H2O2 was generated by HmFDPF (3.8 μM H2O2) compared to LowFMN-HmFDPF (5.9 μM H2O2) after 150 μM NADH was consumed (Fig. 6A). Despite this, only a slight difference was observed in the rate of H2O2 production, with rates of 0.8 and 1.12 μmol H2O2∗min−1 mg−1 for holo-enzyme and LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme, respectively, during the initial addition of NADH. On the other hand, the HmFDPF exhibited a ratio H2O2:O2 of 0.1 compared to 0.5 for LowFMN-HmFDPF. This indicated that at least half of the electrons from NADH are directed toward one-electron reaction with O2, leading to superoxide generation, while the other half goes to H2O for the LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme.

We also used Amplex Red/HRP reagent to measure H2O2 levels after the reaction with different NADH concentrations was fully completed (in these assays, SOD was absent) (Figs. 6B and S6, A–C). Under these conditions, we observed around 20 μM of H2O2 for the highest NADH concentration with LowFMN-HmFDPF, while the holo-enzyme only produced ∼5 μM H2O2 (Fig. 6B). We observed a very robust ∼110 μM H2O2 production in assays with FDPF1 from T. vaginalis (TvFDPF1), which had a very small FMN content (0.010 ± 0.004 equivalents of FMN per subunit) (Table 1, Fig. 1 [reaction 4]; Figs. 6B and S6C) (21). In addition, to confirm that H2O2 directly inactivates the enzyme, we monitored reaction progress curves when the reaction mixture was supplemented with different amounts of exogenously added H2O2 (Figs. 6C and S7, A–D). Exogenously added H2O2 robustly inactivated both HmFDPF and LowFMN-HmFDPF enzymes ∼1 min after the start of the reaction. Notably, for the holo-form enzyme, inactivation was precipitous compared to LowFMN-HmFDPF, which has already lower activity. This suggests that high occupancy of the N-terminal FMN/Fe-Fe center is very important for supporting the high H2O-forming oxidase activity of this enzyme (Fig. 6C). In addition, we also observed that LowFMN-HmFDPF does not have any measurable NADH:H2O2 oxidoreductase activity (Fig. S3B).

Superoxide is the reaction intermediate of the HmFDPF-catalyzed reaction

We used both holo- and LowFMN-HmFDPF preparations and analyzed progress curves at different concentrations of catalase, SOD, or when both catalase and SOD were present in the reaction mixture from the start of the reaction (Fig. 7, A–F). We established that even a holo-enzyme can be inactivated over time (the reaction progress curve deviates from the linearity), yet the addition of catalase completely reverses this effect, as determined by the shape of the reaction progress curve in the absence or in the presence of catalase (>0.5 U) (Fig. 7A). At the same time, in order for LowFMN-HmFDPF to have completely reversed enzyme inactivation, >5 U of catalase was required (Fig. 7D). Interestingly, we observed that SOD presence in the reaction mixture led to enzyme inactivation of both holo- and LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme preparations, suggesting that superoxide is a reaction intermediate and its conversion to H2O2 by SOD leads to rapid enzyme inactivation (Fig. 7, B and E). Furthermore, when catalase was present in the reaction mixture at different concentrations, it allowed reversal of SOD-mediated enzyme inactivation (Fig. 7, C and F).

Because we observed a robust SOD-mediated enzyme inactivation and because it has been previously established that NROR domain can donate electrons directly to cytochrome c (34, 35), we decided to use a cytochrome c–based assay to monitor possible direct superoxide production by holo- and LowFMN-HmFPDF as well as TvFDPF1 (an enzyme which is essentially missing the FDP domain due to low FMN/Fe occupancy and catalyzes primarily the H2O2-forming oxidase reaction) (Figs. 8A and S8, A–C). Interestingly, we found cytochrome c reduction even for a holo-enzyme, which was linearly dependent on NADH concentration (Fig. 8A). For both HmFDPF and LowHmFDPF, the superoxide levels measured as cytochrome c reduction were much higher than previously measured H2O2 levels, suggesting that superoxide is a reaction intermediate (Figs. 6, A and B and 8A). At the same time, even higher levels of cytochrome c reduction were observed across NADH concentrations tested for TvFDPF1, which is essentially devoid of the last redox center (within the N-terminal flavodiiron domain) (Fig. 8A). We note that superoxide levels were transient, as if the reaction with NADH was allowed to proceed until completion (i.e., all NADH was consumed), no superoxide could be detected (Figs. 8B and S9, A–D). Since cytochrome c can receive electrons directly from NROR domain or via superoxide (31, 32), we also performed our cytochrome c reduction experiments in the presence of SOD (Fig. 8C). For all three proteins we tested, we observed an SOD-sensitive portion of cytochrome c reduction, further emphasizing that the superoxide is an intermediate product of the reaction (Fig. 8C).

Discussion

Often, to gain an insight into multidomain enzymes, separate domains or truncated proteins of corresponding enzymes are purified and biochemically characterized (18, 19, 29). In our studies of multidomain HmFDPF, we took a different approach by using a full-length protein with varying occupancy of the active sites. This approach can be attractive as isolated domains or truncated proteins can have different oligomerization states, impaired protein–protein interactions or other differences in biophysical properties, such as modified redox potentials of relevant redox centers—all of which could ultimately impact interpretations of experimental results. The assumed electron flow within the dimer of a typical FDPF is (NADH - > FAD of the NROR domain - > Fe(SCys)4 center of the Rb-like domain - > FMN of the FDP domain (subunit 1) - > FeFe center of the FDP domain (subunit 2)) (21) (Fig. 2A). We have determined that substoichiometric occupancy (0.19 ± 0.01 of FMN per monomer) in LowFMN-HmFDPF leads to a ∼2.7-fold decrease in kcat/KM (when NADH was used as a substrate) compared to the holo-form (Tables 1 and 3). We note that despite the substoichiometric presence of the FMN and iron (1.05 ± 0.11 per monomer), the LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme variant primarily catalyzes the H2O-forming NADH oxidase activity (Fig. 1 [reaction 1] and Fig. 4, A–D). Only very low FMN occupancy (0.010 ± 0.004 per monomer) in another variant of FDPF1 from T. vaginalis (LowFMN-TvFDPF1) (21) led to a robust H2O2-forming oxidase activity within NROR/Rb domains as the terminal FDP oxidase domain was essentially lacking in that configuration (Tables 1, 2, and Fig. 1 [reaction 4], Figs. 2B and 6B). For this reason, apparent KMs for O2 for both holo- and LowFMN-HmFDPF are very close, as they report on the reaction catalyzed within the terminal oxidase FDP domain (the H2O-forming NADH oxidase reaction) (Table 3 and Fig. 5, A–C).

Despite both holo- and LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme preparations catalyzing the H2O-forming oxidase reaction, it became evident that both enzyme variants are susceptible to inactivation when inspecting the reaction progress curves for both O2 and NADH, with the effect being very pronounced for LowFMN-HmFDPF (Figs. 4, A–D and 7, A and D). We reasoned that observed enzyme inactivation is due to H2O2 formation, and this was confirmed in experiments with adding different amounts of catalase to the reaction mixture (≥5 U of catalase completely prevented LowFMN-HmFDPF inactivation) or when enzyme activity was evaluated in the presence of exogenously added H2O2 (Figs. 6, A–C and 7, B and E).

Interestingly, when only SOD was present in the reaction mixture, we observed a drastic enzyme inactivation for both holo- and LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme preparations. This enzyme inactivation suggests that superoxide can be a reaction intermediate, as SOD catalyzes conversion of superoxide to O2 and H2O2, and the latter can participate in enzyme inactivation (see “Experimental procedures” [reactions S2-S4]). Although it was previously demonstrated that the NROR domain can directly donate electrons to cytochrome c (34, 35), we believe that superoxide is a true reaction intermediate for the following reasons: 1) SOD leads to enzyme inactivation (as it converts superoxide to H2O2 and O2) (Fig. 7, B and E); 2) cytochrome c reduction is SOD-sensitive (SOD presence will eliminate the portion of the cytochrome c reduction that receives electrons directly from the superoxide) (Fig. 8C). In addition, we show that in FDPFs, FMN content inversely correlated with H2O2 and reduced cytochrome c production (a proxy for superoxide formation) (Figs. 6B and 8C). We hypothesize that in an FDPF-catalyzed reaction, superoxide is formed as a flavin-caged radical within the NROR domain, but further studies are required to detect the exact location of superoxide intermediate formation and other aspects of the reaction mechanism (36, 37, 38). We note that within the HmFDPF-mediated ET, all redox reactions are tightly controlled, as evidenced by the predominantly H2O-forming oxidase reaction even when the terminal oxidase domain was present in substoichiometric quantities in LowFMN-HmFDPF (Fig. 4, A–D). Mechanistically, low FMN occupancy (substoichiometric or complete absence) leads to buildup of reducing equivalents as evidenced by an increased H2O2 production and superoxide levels (most probably within the NROR domain) (Figs. 2B, 6B and 8A).

In this study, we also show that ET mediated by multiple redox centers within the FDPF protein is sensitive to H2O2. Our results also challenge previous reports in the literature of a robust NADH:H2O2 reductase activity by FDPF from C. difficile and by class H FDP from S. wolfei (19, 29) (Fig. 1 [reaction 3] and Fig. S2, A and B). Strict sensitivity to H2O2 and a lack of NADH:H2O2 reductase activity also suggests against superoxide reductase (SOR) activity of HmFDPF (previously described SORs imply formation of H2O2 as a final reaction product but without O2 formation: O2•− + 2H+ + e− - > H2O2) (39, 40). In the genome of H. meleagridis, we could not find catalase or 1Fe/2Fe-SOR homologs and only identified one protein (XP_067772491.1) which is a putative Mn or Fe-dependent SOD (Supplementary Table S1) (40). Previously it was suggested that some protozoan parasites lack catalases as by detoxifying H2O2 catalases are generating molecular O2 which is not a desirable reaction product for microaerophilic organisms (8, 41). At the same time, we identified that H. meleagridis contains four peroxiredoxins (XP_067775003.1, XP_067771557.1, XP_067768811.1, XP_067771462.1) which are known to reduce H2O2 to water via a cysteinyl-dependent mechanism (Table S1) (8, 42). Therefore, our analysis suggests that in H. meleagridis multiple peroxiredoxins can be important for protection of FDPs and other enzymes which are sensitive to H2O2.

Overall, our study provides important mechanistic insights into the H2O-forming oxidase activity that is achieved by concerted activity of four different redox centers within the multidomain FDP (Fig. 2, A and B). The characterization of enzyme reaction intermediates remains a significant challenge due to their transient lifetimes and high reactivity (43, 44). A detailed understanding of these intermediates, in combination with the redox energy landscape of associated cofactors, is crucial for elucidating the precise mechanisms of ET and identifying the points at which redox bifurcation may occur. We also note that the FDP system (including a stand-alone class A FDP) can be a target for antiparasitic interventions (8). For example, a potential small molecule not only can completely inhibit the FDP system but can also disturb ET within this protein and favor an “ROS bomb” (i.e., superoxide or H2O2 production). This targeted oxidative stress could selectively destroy the parasite without harming the host organism. In the future, understanding the dynamics and stability of the reaction intermediates of the enzymes in this study can help with the rational design of therapeutics that exploit redox vulnerabilities in H. meleagridis, T. vaginalis, Tritrichomonas foetus as well as other microaerophilic pathogens (8). Interestingly, it was demonstrated that nitrofurans but not metronidazole can be reduced to the respective nitro anion radical forms by the cytosolic “NAD(P)H oxidase” from T. foetus (7). Although that study was performed using crude cell homogenates (7) it appears that “NAD(P)H oxidase” activity described can be attributed to a “self-sufficient” FDPF in that organism (XP_068360298.1). Therefore, we hypothesize that in addition to classically described ferredoxin-linked reactions, FDPs can be directly involved in the metabolism of nitrofurans and nitroimidazoles in various protozoan parasites.

Protein expression and purification

HmFDPF was overexpressed in One Shot BL21 Star (DE3) E. coli cells grown in TB medium supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin and purified using the NGC Quest 10 Plus (Bio-Rad) chromatography system as previously described for TvFDPF3 (21). Briefly, after nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid affinity chromatography, the protein sample was exchanged into 50 mM sodium phosphate dibasic, pH 7.5 buffer, and applied onto Source 15Q anion exchanger column equilibrated with 50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.5, and 15 mM NaCl. Fractions that contained the most active HmFDPF with nearly full FMN/Fe/FAD occupancy (holo-form) belonged to the first major peak, which eluted in the 15 to 300 mM NaCl gradient. LowFMN-HmFDPF eluted in the second major peak in the 15 to 300 mM NaCl gradient, and corresponding fractions were also collected. Both holo- and LowFMN-HmFDPF proteins were subjected to size-exclusion chromatography on HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 column (GE Healthcare/Cytiva) equilibrated with 50 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, and 150 mM NaCl (buffer A). LowFMN-TvFDPF1 was purified as previously described (21).

Metal analysis

The inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis was performed in the Environmental and Complex Analysis Laboratory (The University of California, San Diego) as previously described (21).

UV-Visible spectroscopy

Unless otherwise specified, a Cary 3500 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies) was used to record protein spectra and perform activity assays under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. To determine concentrations of free FAD and FMN in solution, extinction coefficients for FAD at 450 nm (11.3 mM−1 cm−1) and for FMN at 446 nm (12.2 mM−1 cm−1) were used, respectively (48). For the Michaelis–Menten analysis enzymatic activity was monitored by following the decrease of the absorbance of NAD(P)H at 340 nm (ε340 = 6.22 mM–1 cm–1) in 0.2 ml of buffer A at 37 °C, and initial velocity in the first 0 to 60 s following the start of the reaction was used to estimate activity. A typical reaction mixture in 0.2 ml of buffer A contained NAD(P)H (10–500 μM) and the enzyme (0.48–79.0 μg). The kcat values were always calculated per monomer of the protein. In addition, the enzymatic activity was evaluated by monitoring NADH consumption at 340 nm (ε340 = 6.22 mM–1 cm–1) using a Cary 4000 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies). The reaction was initiated by sequential additions of 50 μM NADH to buffer A containing 3 μg/ml HmFDPF or 9 μg/ml LowFMN-HmFDPF and experiments were performed in the absence or presence of 5 U/ml SOD (Sigma, S9697) and 30 U/ml catalase (Sigma, C1345). To evaluate NADH oxidation activity upon exogenous addition of FMN/Fe2+ enzymatic assays were performed with LowFMN-HmFDPF. In our experiments, 5 μM Fe2+ (Mohr’s salt; Sigma, 215406), 5 μM FMN (Sigma, F2253), or their combination was added to 0.2 ml of buffer A and after samples were blanked NADH (250 μM) was added. Subsequently the reaction was initiated by addition of 1.0 μg of LowFMN-HmFDPF (final protein concentration was 0.05 μM of monomers).

O2 consumption

O2 consumption experiments were performed in buffer A using a high-resolution respirometer (Oroboros Oxygraph-2k) at 25 °C. Three bolus additions of 50 μM NADH were made in the presence of 3 μg/ml HmFDPF or 9 μg/ml LowFMN-HmFDPF. The same experiment was performed when reaction mixture was supplemented with SOD (5 U/ml) and catalase (30 U/ml). The KM and Vmax values for O2 for both HmFDPF and LowFMN-HmFDPF were assessed during oxidation of 250 μM NADH in buffer A containing 5 U/ml SOD, and 30 U/ml catalase, using protein concentrations of 0.5 and 2 μg/ml, respectively. The initial O2 concentration in these experiments was ∼40 μM, and the reaction was initiated by NADH addition. The O2 consumption was monitored until it reached ∼0 μM. Subsequently, the KM and Vmax values were obtained by calculating the O2 consumption rate, using OriginPro software (version 9.3; https://www.originlab.com/). These two reactions contribute to overall O2 consumption at different stoichiometries (we assumed that ∼ 90% of electrons went to water and ∼10% went to superoxide for both HmFDPF and LowFMN-HmFDPF, as the enzyme was still mostly catalyzing the water-forming oxidase reaction at 0.19 ± 0.01 of FMN/monomer context) (Table 1 and Fig. 2). The enzymatic water formation should follow Michaelis–Menten kinetics. In contrast, superoxide formation proceeds via a pseudo-first order process, where the reaction rate increases linearly with O2 concentration (31). This relationship can be plotted (as shown in Fig. 5, A–C) and it is possible to determine both KM for O2 consumption involved in water formation, and pseudo-first order rate constant (k‘) for superoxide formation using the linear relationship V = k” × [O2], where k’ is the slope from the linear correlation between the rate of O2 consumption and [O2] and thereby represents the rate of superoxide production (Fig. 5B); while k” represents the no linear portion associated to H2O production k” = v-[O2]∗k’, and is the result from subtracting the contribution of superoxide production.

Continuous measurements of H2O2 production

The production of H2O2 during the course of a steady-state assay was performed using a high-resolution respirometer (Oroboros Oxygraph-2k) equipped with two-channel fluorescence optical setup (49). The experiments were conducted in buffer A containing 10 μM Amplex Ultrared, 4 U/ml HRP, and 5 U/ml SOD at 25 °C. The reaction was initiated by the addition of the enzyme: HmFDPF (3 μg/ml) or LowFMN-HmFDPF (9 μg/ml). The resorufin fluorescence was calibrated by additions of aliquots of standard H2O2 solutions. In these conditions, H2O2 was measured over the course of the reaction, and because H2O2 is consumed (as measured by the Amplex Ultrared/HRP system), the enzymes were less susceptible to inactivation.

“End-point” assay for H2O2 production

We also wished to determine how much H2O2 is accumulated over the course of the reaction when all NADH substrate is consumed (unlike in “continuous” measurements, when H2O2 is measured as it is produced over the course of the reaction). We have investigated the formation of H2O2 using an “end-point” assay for FDPFs with three different FMN/monomer content: (1) HmFDPF (1.06 ± 0.05); (2) LowFMN-HmFDPF (0.19 ± 0.01), and (3) LowFMN-TvFDPF1 (0.01 ± 0.004; a paralog of FDPF from T. vaginalis from our previous study (21)). A catalytic amount of each protein (2.75 μg of HmFDPF; 8.2 μg of LowFMN-HmFDPF or 17.4 μg of LowFMN-TvFDPF1) was added to the assay mixture containing varying concentrations of NADH (0–500 μM) in 0.2 ml of buffer A at 37 °C. Once the NADH substrate was completely consumed, the reaction mixture was supplemented with 2 μl of HRP (Abcam, ab102500) and 2 μl of Amplex Red (Abcam, ab102500). The concentration of H2O2 generated in each reaction was quantified based on resorufin formation (ε570 = 54 mM-1 cm-1) using a Cary 3500 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies).

H2O2 reductase activity

To evaluate the ability of HmFDPF and LowFMN-HmFDPF to react with H2O2 (H2O2 reductase activity was previously reported for FDPF from C. difficile (19) and for Class H FDP from S. wolfei (29)), the catalytic activity assays were performed under strict anaerobic conditions by following the absorbance of NADH at 340 nm (ε340 = 6.22 mM–1 cm–1) using a Cary 3500 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies) at 37 °C. First, all anaerobic assay components, including buffer A, H2O2, and NADH stock solutions were prepared by purging argon gas for 45 min in sealed vials on wet ice. The enzyme stock solution was prepared in an anaerobic buffer A in a sealed vial and further deoxygenated by purging argon gas over the solution surface at 4 °C for an additional 30 min. Finally, a potential NADH:H2O2 oxidoreductase reaction was monitored by incubating the reaction mixture (0.2 ml of buffer A) containing 500 μM NADH and 250 μM H2O2 with 6.40 μg of HmFDPF or 8.2 μg of LowFMN-HmFDPF enzyme in a septum-tight cuvette while continuously purging with argon gas. In parallel, control experiments which contained NADH and HmFDPF were also performed under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions in the absence of H2O2. In our experiments, we could not detect any measurable NADH:H2O2 oxidoreductase activity for all proteins we tested.

NO reductase activity

To evaluate the NO reductase activity of HmFDPF, we used three different commonly used NO-releasing compounds: diethylamine NONOate (Sigma, D184), PROLI NONOate (Cayman Chemical, 82145), and NOC-5 (Sigma, A5456). Stock solutions (10 mM) of each NO donor were freshly prepared in 10 mM NaOH. Additionally, a 10 mM stock solution of DAN, a fluorescent probe for NO detection (Sigma, 88461) was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide. DAN itself is nonfluorescent but reacts rapidly and specifically with NO to form the highly fluorescent compound 2,3-naphthotriazole (50). Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded from 410 to 650 nm spectral range using multimode microplate fluorimeter features of Cytation C10 Imaging System (Agilent Technologies) with the following settings: excitation at 375 ± 15 nm, emission at 410 ± 15 nm, and gain set to 80. Test assays were performed in a final volume of 100 μl, containing 10 μM DAN and varying concentrations of NO donors (0, 10, 50, 100, and 200 μM) at 37 °C. A concentration-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity was observed, confirming robust NO release from all 3 NO donors compounds tested. The NO reductase activity was probed under strict anaerobic conditions. Initially, assay components including buffer A, NO releasing reagents, and NADH stock solutions were rendered anaerobic by purging with argon gas for 45 min in sealed vials. The enzyme stock solution was prepared in anaerobic buffer A and further deoxygenated by gently blowing argon gas over the solution surface at 4 °C for an additional 30 min. NO reductase activity was assessed by monitoring the decrease in NADH absorbance at 340 nm (ε340 = 6.22 mM–1 cm–1) at 37 °C using a Cary 3500 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies). The reaction mixture (200 μl), containing 250 μM NADH and 250 μM NO was assembled in a sealed, septum-capped anaerobic cuvette while being purged with argon gas for 5 min. The reaction was initiated by the addition of 1.28 μg of HmFDPF, and absorbance changes at 340 nm were recorded over time. No measurable NO reductase activity was observed with any of the tested NO donors, as evidenced by nearly flat reaction progress curves. For further validating the result, the septum was removed from the cuvette to allow reoxygenation under aerobic conditions, which resulted in a measurable decrease in NADH absorbance, indicating enzyme activity in the presence of O2. Control experiments were also performed under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions in the absence of NO, containing only NADH and the enzyme.

Enzymatic activity in the presence of exogenous H2O2

Enzymatic activity was assessed by monitoring the decrease in NADH absorbance at 340 nm (ε340 = 6. 22 mM-1 cm-1) using a Cary 3500 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies) under aerobic conditions. Different concentrations of H2O2 (0–500 μM) were added to a reaction mixture in 0.2 ml of buffer A, which was incubated for 3 min at 37 °C before adding a fixed amount of NADH (250 μM). Subsequently, 0.48 μg of the enzyme was added to the reaction mixture, and the specific activity was calculated based on the slope of the reaction progress curves at two different time points: the first slope (initial velocity) and a second slope (after 60 s). Drastic enzyme inactivation was observed for HmFDPF after 1 min into the reaction, indicating a rapid loss of activity over time in the presence of exogenous H2O2.

Enzymatic activity in presence of catalase, SOD, or when used in combination

To evaluate the effect of catalase, SOD, or their combination on the enzymatic activity of both HmFDPF and LowFMN-HmFDPF, a continuous assay was used. Reaction progress curves based on the absorbance at 340 nm were monitored using a Cary 3500 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies). Different amounts of catalase from bovine liver (0–20 U; Sigma, C1345) were added to the reaction mixture (200 μl of buffer A containing 250 μM NADH), and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 3 min. Reactions were initiated by the addition of the enzyme (0.48 μg for HmFDPF or 1.50 μg for LowFMN-HmFDPF). Upon inspection of progress curves, it was evident that both the HmFDPF and LowFMN-HmFDPF reactions were deviating from linearity, which cannot be explained simply by substrate depletion, suggesting enzyme inactivation (especially for LowFMN-HmFDPF). At the same time, catalase presence (0.5 U for HmFDPF) and (≥10 U for LowFMN-HmFDPF) restored progress curves and allowed complete NADH consumption under 9 min. For the enzymatic activity in the presence of SOD, different amounts of bovine SOD (0–20 U; Sigma, S9697) were added to 200 μl of buffer A containing NADH (250 μM). In these assays for both HmFDPF and LowFMN-HmFDPF enzymes, the reaction progress curves exhibited a pronounced and drastic deviation from linearity and a fast “flattening” suggesting that superoxide is the reaction intermediate. We reasoned that in these experiments, SOD converts superoxide reaction intermediate to H2O2, which results in a rapid enzyme inactivation. We also performed similar experiments when catalase was used in combination with SOD. In this set of experiments, different amounts of catalase (0–20 U) were added to a reaction mixture (0.2 ml of buffer A) which contained NADH (250 μM) and a fixed amount of SOD (20 U) together with 0.48 μg of HmFDPF or 1.50 μg of LowFMN-HmFDPF. Under these experimental conditions, catalase completely restored enzyme inactivation in a dose-dependent manner due to SOD presence.

Superoxide quantification using cytochrome c

Cytochrome c reduction assay was used to quantify the formation of superoxide (51, 52, 53). In our experiments, we used both continuous measurements and “end-point” assays to measure cytochrome c reduction immediately after the reaction was initiated or when all NADH substrate was completely consumed (similarly to experiments with H2O2 production, see section above). In our experiments, we used three different FDPFs with varying FMN/monomer content: (1) HmFDPF (1.06 ± 0.05); (2) LowFMN-HmFDPF (0.19 ± 0.01); and (3) LowFMN-TvFDPF1 (0.01 ± 0.004; a paralog of FDPF from Trichomonas vaginalis from our previous study (21)). First, a 1.95 mM stock solution of cytochrome c (cytochrome c from equine heart; Sigma, C7752) was prepared in buffer A. In the “continuous” assay, different concentrations of NADH (5–200 μM) were used with cytochrome c (200 μM), and the enzymatic reaction was initiated by the addition of 0.48 μg of the enzyme. Near simultaneous measurements of absorbance at 340 nm (NADH consumption) and 550 nm (cytochrome c reduction) were performed using a Cary 3500 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies). In the “end-point” assay, NADH (5–200 μM) was first incubated with 0.48 μg of HmFDPF, 4.80 μg of LowFMN-HmFDPF, or 17.40 μg of LowFMN-TvFDPF1. After complete consumption of NADH (confirmed by the disappearance of absorbance at 340 nm), cytochrome c (200 μM) was added, and the absorbance at 550 nm was recorded to determine cytochrome c reduction. The concentration of superoxide generated by each protein was quantified based on cytochrome c reduction (ε550 = 21.8 mM−1 cm−1).

Continuous measurements of cytochrome c reduction in the presence of SOD

The rationale behind this experiment is that if cytochrome c receives electrons through superoxide, the cytochrome c reduction reaction should be inhibited in the presence of SOD. In our experiments, we used three different FDPFs with varying FMN/monomer content: 1) HmFDPF (1.06 ± 0.05); 2) LowFMN-HmFDPF (0.19 ± 0.01); and 3) LowFMN-TvFDPF1 (0.01 ± 0.004). The “continuous” assay was carried out using 80 μM of NADH and 200 μM of cytochrome c in the presence or absence of 200 units of SOD. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by addition of 0.48 μg of the enzyme. The final volume of the assay mixture was 300 μl and in the reactions without SOD, 100 μl of buffer A was added instead of SOD bolus to account for dilution effects. The “continuous” measurements of absorbance at 340 nm (NADH consumption) and 550 nm (cytochrome c reduction) were performed using a Cary 3500 UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies). In this “continuous” assay, 30.8 μl of cytochrome c (from a 1.95 mM stock solution) was added to 200 μl of buffer A to prepare the blank, maintaining a final cytochrome c concentration of 200 μM in a 300 μl reaction mixture.

Rapid kinetics

Stopped-flow experiments were executed for the purpose of determining the optical changes that occur under pre-steady-state conditions. The rapid mixing runs were performed with a HiTek Stopped-flow instrument equipped with a diode-array that monitors the UV-visible spectra in the range of 330 to 700 nm. The experiments were conducted at room temperature (22 °C) with an observation window of 1 cm. Each reaction consisted of a rapid mixing of 21.6 μM HmFDPF (monomers) and variable NADH concentrations (590, 295, and 147 μM) in buffer A. The dead-time of the stopped-flow was estimated to be ∼25 ms. Spectra were collected with a detector integration time of 7 ms for 400 scans (∼3 s).
