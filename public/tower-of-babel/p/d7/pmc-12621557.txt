Clinical and financial outcomes associated with gastrointestinal complications among patients with type B aortic dissection☆
Authors: Troy N Coaston, Amulya Vadlakonda, Esteban Aguayo, Zihan Gao, Syed Shaheer Ali, Oh Jin Kwon, Peyman Benharash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastrointestinal complications (GIC) are an uncommon but serious sequelae of type B aortic dissection (TBAD), potentially contributing to increased mortality and healthcare resource utilization. Limited studies have characterized these complications among TBAD patients. The present work sought to evaluate the clinical and financial implications of GIC and identifies factors associated with their development.MethodsAll hospitalizations entailing a primary diagnosis of TBAD were identified in the 2016–2020 Nationwide Readmissions Database. Patients were stratified into those who developed GIC and those who did not. Outcomes included in-hospital mortality, length of stay (LOS), hospitalization costs, and the need for abdominal surgery. Multivariable logistic and linear regressions were employed to assess associations between patient, facility, and treatment factors with clinical and financial outcomes.ResultsOf 24,927 TBAD hospitalizations, 2.5 % developed GIC, and 24.0 % of these cases required an abdominal procedure. Patients with GIC were younger (median 61 vs. 67 years; p < 0.001), more commonly male (67.4 vs. 56.6 %; p < 0.001), and more likely to have Medicaid insurance (20.9 vs. 12.5 %; p < 0.001). GIC were independently associated with increased odds of in-hospital mortality (adjusted odds ratio 4.54; 95 % CI 3.41–6.04), greater LOS (β 5.80 days; 95 % CI 3.88–7.72), and increased costs (β $41,000; 95 % CI $31000–51,000).ConclusionsGIC in TBAD patients was associated with substantial clinical and financial burdens. Further study of early identification and targeted interventions is warranted to mitigate these complications and optimize resource utilization in this high-risk population.Keywords: TBAD, Resource use, Vascular surgery, Aortic surgery, TEVAR, Dissection

Introduction

Aortic dissection (AD) is the most common acute aortic syndrome in the United States and has been associated with in-hospital mortality rates as high as 27 % [1]. Despite advances in surgical and medical management, post- AD complications continue to pose significant clinical challenges [[2], [3], [4]]. Specifically, the development of gastrointestinal complications (GIC) following AD ranges from 5 to 19 % and carries a mortality of 7–26 % [5,6].

Previous studies have reported the development of GIC after AD to be associated with inferior clinical outcomes [7]. Indeed, Zindovic et al. reported that patients with type A aortic dissection (TAAD) who developed gastrointestinal malperfusion had a markedly higher mortality compared to others [8]. However, while outcomes associated with the development of GIC following TAAD have been explored, studies among patients with type B aortic dissection (TBAD), remain sparse. Importantly, GIC following TBAD may represent a distinct clinical entity from those arising after TAAD given the more distal site of blood entry into the false lumen. This difference in dissection morphology may influence predictors of complications, such as dissection flap progression, true lumen size, and perfusion to branch vessels, warranting further investigation [9].

In the present study, we investigated the clinical and financial outcomes associated with GIC in a nationally representative cohort of patients with TBAD undergoing surgical or medical management. Additionally, we sought to identify factors, including treatment modalities, independently associated with the development of GIC in this patient population. We hypothesized the development of GIC to be associated with increased risk of mortality and resource utilization.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study of the 2016–2020 Nationwide Readmissions Database (NRD). As part of the Healthcare Cost Utilization Project, the NRD is the largest readmissions database in the United States, providing accurate estimates for ~60 % of discharges using survey weighting methodologies (NRD Information). Readmissions within the same state and calendar year are tracked through unique linkage numbers [10].

All non-elective adult (≥18 years) admissions entailing a primary diagnosis of TBAD were identified in the 2016–2020 NRD using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplemental Table 1) [11]. Records were excluded if their hospitalization entailed cardiac surgery (use of cardioplegia, cardiac valve repair, operation on vessels of the heart) to avoid misclassification of TAAD [11]. Those undergoing open surgical repair of TBAD were similarly removed from analysis. Additionally, records missing key data such as age, sex, and survival status, were further excluded. Finally, subjects discharged after September of each calendar year were excluded to assure 90-days of follow up (Fig. 1).

Subjects were stratified into two groups: those who developed GIC and those who did not (non-GIC). Patient characteristics including age, sex, income, and insurance status, were defined in accordance with the NRD data dictionary. Facility characteristics such as teaching status and bed size were similarly defined. The van Walraven modification of the Elixhauser Comorbidity index was used to assess the burden of over thirty comorbid conditions. Furthermore, specific comorbidities including Marfan's disease, liver disease, and coagulopathy were defined using ICD-10 codes as reported elsewhere [11]. Similarly, procedures including thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR), spinal drain placement, subclavian revascularization, bowel resection, exploratory laparotomy, and colonoscopy were identified with previously validated ICD-10 codes [11,12]. Several GIC were examined including bleeding, bowel ischemia, and bowel obstruction [13]. Additional complications were categorized as stroke, respiratory, infectious, and renal as previously defined [14]. Hospitalization costs were derived from hospital-specific cost-to-charge ratios and adjusted for inflation using the 2020 Personal Health Index (PHI). Primary indication for readmission was derived from diagnosis related group coding [15].

The primary study endpoint was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included need for abdominal procedure (bowel resection, exploratory laparotomy, colonoscopy), development of complications, and resource utilization (hospitalization costs, 90-day non-elective readmissions, length of stay [LOS]).

The Pearson's chi-square and Wilcoxon Rank Sum tests were used to assess between group differences for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Categorical variables are reported as proportions (%) while continuous factors are displayed as medians with interquartile range (IQR). Cuzick's nonparametric rank-based test was used to assess temporal trends (nptrend). Multivariable logistic and linear regression was used to assess the association of cohort variables with outcomes of interest. Variable selection was aided by elastic net regularization. Briefly, elastic net regularization uses penalized least-squares methodologies to identify covariates while minimizing both collinearity and bias [16]. Goodness-of-fit was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (c-statistic) or coefficient of determination (R2) as appropriate. And Model outputs are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and β coefficients for logistic and linear regressions respectively, both alongside 95 % confidence intervals (CI). The threshold for significance was set at α < 0.05. All statistical analysis was performed using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp). This study was exempted from full review by the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, Los Angeles.

Results

Of an estimated 24,927 hospitalizations with a primary diagnosis of TBAD, 2.5 % developed GIC. The incidence of GIC did not significantly change over the study period (nptrend = 0.69). Compared to non-GIC, GIC was younger (61 [51–72] vs 67 years [56–79], p < 0.001), and more commonly male (67.4 vs 56.6 %, p < 0.001), and insured by Medicaid (20.9 vs 12.5 %, p < 0.001). Conversely, GIC was less commonly medically managed (74.9 vs 87.0 %, p < 0.001; Table 1) at initial hospitalization compared to others. Among GIC the most common complications were bowel ischemia (66.6 %), followed by GI bleed (19.3 %), and bowel obstruction (16.0 %). Within this group, 24.0 % required abdominal procedures which were typically performed on the first day of hospitalization (0 days [0–2]). The most frequent procedure was small bowel resection (14.4 %), followed by large bowel resection (11.4 %), colonoscopy (4.6 %), and exploratory laparotomy (3.3 %; Fig. 2). Ultimately, 35.7 % of GIC expired during their inpatient stay.

Factors associated with development of GIC

Following risk-adjustment, Medicaid insurance (AOR 1.63, 95 % CI 1.15–2.32, ref.: Private) and TEVAR (AOR 1.81, 95 % CI 1.40–2.35, ref.: Medical Management) were associated with increased odds of GIC. Other factors associated with the development of GIC included history of liver disease (AOR 4.89, 95 % CI 3.50–6.83) and transfer from outside facility (AOR 1.64, 95 % CI 1.31–1.12-2.40; Fig. 3).

Clinical outcomes associated with GIC

The development of GIC was associated with significantly increased risk of any other assessed complication (AOR 3.06, 95 % CI 2.30–4.08). Specifically, GIC were linked with infectious (AOR 5.17, 95 % CI 3.29–8.12), respiratory (AOR 2.22, 95 % CI 1.69–2.92), and renal (AOR 2.48, 95 % CI 1.92–3.19) complications. Moreover, GIC were associated with significantly increased risk of in-hospital mortality (AOR 4.54, 95 % CI 3.41–6.04).

Resource utilization associated with GIC

The occurrence of GIC was independently associated with increased hospitalization costs (β $ 41,000, 95 % CI $31000–51,000). Moreover, GIC were linked with longer LOS (β 5.80 days, 3.88–7.72) however did not alter odds of 90-day non-elective readmission (AOR 0.76, 95 % CI 0.55–1.04; Fig. 4). Of subjects readmitted within 90 days, those with GIC were more frequently readmitted for gastrointestinal (22.6 % vs 6.5 %; p < 0.001) and infectious (9.8 % vs 5.0 %; p < 0.001) indications compared to others.

Discussion

Although uncommon, GIC following TBAD are associated with significant morbidity, need for surgical intervention, and resource use. In the present study, we identified several factors associated with the development of GIC including age, Medicaid coverage, and receipt of TEVAR. Additionally, we report significantly increased mortality, hospitalization costs, and LOS among patients with GIC. Furthermore, GIC was linked with the development of the development of other complications including respiratory, infectious, and renal. Characterization of these associations among a contemporary cohort of patients with TBAD is lacking; thus, these findings merit further discussion.

Among the GICs identified in the present study, bowel ischemia was the most frequent. This complication likely arises from a myriad mechanisms, including static or dynamic malperfusion due to dissection-related compromise of the mesenteric vessels [17,18]. In the context of TEVAR, restoration of true lumen flow may not always re-establish adequate visceral perfusion. Key technical preventative measures may include meticulous wire and catheter manipulation to maintain placement in the true lumen [19]. Utilizing tools such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and fluoroscopy are essential in ensuring precise deployment of the stent graft and verifying adequate blood flow to mesenteric branches which may be obstructed by false lumen pressurization. IVUS may facilitate real-time assessment of the lumens while aiding in proper stent graft sizing and placement to optimize circulation [20]. For cases involving visceral malperfusion or compromised true-lumen flow, adjunctive techniques, such as deployment of fenestrated endovascular stents can restore blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract [21,22]. Collectively, these measures may enhance the safety and efficacy of TBAD repair while mitigating the risk of complications, including acute gastrointestinal ischemia.

Gastrointestinal bleeding was also a significant cause of morbidity and may arise through mechanisms distinct from that of bowel ischemia. Firstly, anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapies may exacerbate underlying bleeding that may arise from mucosal ischemia and sloughing [23]. While using the current database is unable to capture the location of the GI bleed, it is plausible that a subgroup of patients may also suffer from bleeding gastric and duodenal ulcers attributable to the stress response and use of antiplatelet agents. These risks may lead to prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays. Indeed, Cook and colleagues found that among critically ill patients, GI bleeding was associated with a 4–6 day increase of ICU LOS [24]. The significant morbidity associated with gastrointestinal hemorrhage highlights the importance of careful hemostatic management among patients with TBAD.

In the present study, we noted 16 % of patients with GIC to have a bowel obstruction. Mesenteric hypoperfusion, perioperative opioid use, immobility, and electrolyte abnormalities have all been implicated in the disruption of gastrointestinal motility, especially among patients undergoing aggressive fluid resuscitation [25,26]. Persistent ileus may prolong hospitalization and increase risk of secondary complications [27]. A proactive approach to identifying these modifiable contributors may help mitigate the development of obstruction and facilitate recovery.

Beyond acute outcomes, morbidity in the long-term must also be considered. Frankel et al. report persistently poor survival among open thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair patients developing GIC over a 33-year period [28]. Moreover, a retrospective analysis of 40 aortic dissection patients demonstrates significant changes in the gut microbiome following GIC, potentially contributing to subsequent malabsorption, diarrhea, and bloating [7]. Although infrequent, GIC related to aortic dissection can be catastrophic, underscoring the need for further study of the associated morbidity, mortality, and resource utilization.

While the financial burden associated with GIC may seem intuitive, contextualizing these results with actionable clinical practices may offer strategies for mitigation. In the present study, GIC were associated with significantly increased hospitalization costs, LOS, and need for additional surgical interventions. Previous studies have similarly reported high resource expenditure among patients with aortic dissection. In a Canadian national analysis, McClure and colleagues report an increase in median hospitalization costs from 2003 to 2016 among patients with thoracic AD [29]. Moreover, Carroll et al. find more than 25 % of patients hospitalized for acute TBAD will require readmission within 90-days of hospitalization [30]. These findings highlight the high resource burden associated with TBAD, reinforcing the need for targeted interventions. Interestingly, a meta-analysis from Wang and colleagues found that gastrointestinal bleeding prophylaxis with proton pump inhibitors or histamine-2 receptor antagonists may result in a clinically significant reduction in the rate of GI bleeding [31]. Incorporating such preventive strategies, especially in high-risk populations, could potentially reduce the incidence of GIC and mitigate the associated costs.

The present study has several important limitations. As a retrospective cohort study, we are unable to make conclusions regarding causality. Additionally, the NRD has limited granularity regarding laboratory values, imaging, operative characteristics, and timing of complications. As such, conclusive categorization of TBAD as complicated vs uncomplicated is not possible. Further, granularity regarding reason for readmission is also lacking and our report is limited to DRG descriptions. Additionally, given the structure of the transfer variable, outcomes of the pre-transfer hospitalization and facility characteristics of the transferring center are not available. Moreover, as a large, multi-institutional administrative database, the NRD may be subject to miscoding and differential coding practices between facilities. As with many database analyses, there is the potential for selection bias regarding study inclusion and receipt of intervention. Nevertheless, the present study uses the largest available dataset and previously validated methodology to report a comprehensive assessment of GIC following TBAD.

The current work provides insight into contemporary clinical and financial outcomes associated with GIC among patients with TBAD. We identify several factors associated with the development of GIC in addition to high resource utilization among this patient population. Although GIC are uncommon among patients with TBAD, further research is required to minimize the risk to improve patient outcomes and reduce resource burden.
