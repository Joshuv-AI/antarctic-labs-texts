The SlASR4 Interaction with the Phloem Protein2 (SlPP2) regulating the tolerance to drought in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum)
Authors: Faria Sundas, Tayeb Muhammad, Guoping Huang, Yunzhou Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought stress is one of the major constraints of global agricultural productivity, adversely affecting plant water balance, membrane integrity, and oxidative stability. Abscisic acid-stress-ripening (ASR) is a ubiquitous plant transcription factor family that regulates environmental stress responses. However, the functional mechanism by which these proteins mediate drought stress tolerance remains incompletely elucidated. The current study functionally characterized the role of a novel tomato ASR protein (ASR4) in drought tolerance using overexpression (OE1 and OE3) and CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (CAS3 and CAS17) lines.ResultsThe results revealed that transcripts of SlASR4 differentially accumulated in various plant tissues and were remarkably induced by abiotic stressors. Under drought conditions, SlASR4 gain-of-function improved the performance of overexpressed lines, as evidenced by reduced water loss, higher relative water content, increased chlorophyll retention, and decreased electrolyte leakage compared to wild-type and knockout lines. The protective effect was associated with a significant increase in the activity and transcript levels of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), as well as the upregulation of the drought-responsive factors SlDREB and SlNHX. Further, SlASR4 physically interacts with phloem protein 2 (PP2) to confer drought resistance.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that SlASR4 enhances drought tolerance by modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis and activating stress-responsive transcriptional networks through its direct interaction with Phloem Protein2(PP2). This study provides novel molecular insights into ASR4-mediated stress adaptation and identifies SlASR4 as a promising candidate gene for the genetic improvement of drought resilience in tomato and related crop species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08993-x.Keywords: Antioxidant enzymes, CRISPR/Cas9, Drought, Lipid peroxidation, PP2, Reactive oxygen species, SlASR4

Background

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), a member of the Solanaceae family, is one of the most extensively cultivated vegetable crops [1], valued for its nutritional and medicinal properties [2, 3]. Despite being a frost-sensitive crop, tomato is grown in a wide range of climatic conditions, underscoring significant agricultural adaptability and economic importance [4, 5]. However, the increasing severity of environmental challenges due to climate change threatens tomato production worldwide. Among these, drought stress is a critical factor that impairs plant physiological and biochemical processes by disrupting water and nutrient balance and cellular respiration [6–8]. These disruptions lead to reduced seed germination, decreased photosynthetic activity, and ultimately lower crop yields [9]. The extent of drought effect varies by species, genotype, and growth stage, mediated through both stomatal closure and non-stomatal mechanisms [10, 11]. Consequently, developing drought-tolerant cultivars is essential to enhance crop resilience and sustain productivity in water-limited environments [12]. Plants have evolved complex regulatory mechanisms to counteract drought stress, with a central response being the activation of antioxidant and osmo-protectant systems governed by upstream stress regulators [13]. Among these regulators, the ASR protein family serves as a key component of drought stress tolerance. ASR proteins function as transcription factors induced by abscisic acid (ABA) and various abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and cold [14, 15]. These proteins modulate the expression of downstream stress-responsive genes, alleviating water conservation through stomatal regulation and improving ROS scavenging [16, 17]. Evidence from numerous species demonstrates the promising role of ASR genes in enhancing drought resilience. For example, overexpression of ASRs significantly improves drought tolerance in chickpea and wheat [18, 19]. In maize, ZmASR3 regulates ROS homeostasis and enhances antioxidant activity [20], while OsASR5 mediates ABA-induced stomatal closure in rice to reduce water loss [21]. Beyond stress tolerance, ASR genes also influence developmental processes such as fruit ripening and tuber formation, indicating their multifunctional role in plant growth and productivity [14, 22]. This dual functionality renders ASR genes compelling candidates for future biotechnological strategies aimed at enhancing crop quality and resilience in the context of climate change [23].

The tomato ASR gene family consists of five members localized on chromosome 4, all encoding highly homologous proteins with conserved intron positions [14, 24, 25]. This high degree of conservation is also evident in wild tomato relatives, highlighting the evolutionary significance of the ASR gene family [26]. While ASR1 and ASR2 have been extensively characterized, functional insights into ASR3, ASR4, and ASR5 remain limited [14, 27]. Notably, expression analyses in wild tomato species revealed that drought and cold stress significantly induce ASR1, ASR2, and ASR4 [28], suggesting a potential role of ASRs in abiotic stress responses. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular regulation and physiological roles of all ASR genes in tomato and related species is essential and could provide a foundational basis for the development of drought-tolerant cultivars [26]. Although a few studies have examined the expression and abiotic stress tolerance of tomato ASR family members, specifically analysing only two or three members under relatively limited conditions. A more systematic analysis under well-defined drought stress is needed to fully elucidate the roles of different tomato ASR genes [14, 25]. In this study, we cloned and functionally characterized a novel drought-responsive gene, SlASR4, which exhibited significant upregulated expression in drought-stressed tomato leaves. Overexpression of SlASR4 significantly enhanced drought tolerance, whereas its suppression increased drought sensitivity in tomato. SlASR4 physically interacted with PP2, which may modulate the antioxidant system, thereby conferring drought tolerance by maintaining ROS homeostasis. These findings demonstrate that SlASR4 plays a critical role in conferring drought tolerance by positively regulating key morphological and physiological characters.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

In this study, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L) cv. Ailsa Craig (AC) was used to investigate the expression pattern and functional roles of SlASR4 under drought stress conditions. Seeds from wild-type (WT) and four transgenic lines (two overexpressed lines, OE1 and OE3, and two knockout lines, CAS3 and CAS17) were surface sterilized and subsequently germinated on moist filter paper in petri dishes at a temperature of 25 °C for 24 h. Germinated seedlings were then transplanted into pots containing a mixture of soil and vermiculite 1:1 (v/v). Plants were maintained in a growth chamber with controlled conditions at 25 to 28 °C temperatures, 60% relative humidity, and 16/8 hours (light/dark) photoperiod.

SlASR4 expression analysis

For tissue-specific expression analysis, fresh stem, leaf, root, and seed samples from WT plants were collected. For stress expression assays, five-week-old tomato plants were exposed to various abiotic stressors (drought, salt, heat, and cold), and leaf samples were harvested at 0-, 1-, 3-, 6-, 12-, and 24-hours post-treatments to assess the changes in ASR4 expression. For drought and salt treatments, plants were irrigated with 15% polyethylene glycol (PEG 6000) and 200 mM sodium chloride (NaCl) solution, respectively. Heat and cold stresses were imposed by subjecting plants to growth conditions of 40 °C and 4 °C, respectively. All the collected samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at 80 °C until extraction for qRT-PCR analysis.

Drought stress assay in WT and ASR4 transgenic lines

To evaluate drought tolerance, T₂ generation plants of the SlASR4 overexpression and knockout lines, alongside WT controls, were subjected to a drought stress assay. Uniform seedlings from each line were divided into two groups: a well-watered control group irrigated every alternate day, and a drought-stressed group that underwent 14 days of water withdrawal. After the two-week drought period, the uppermost fully expanded leaves were collected from all plants, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent physiological, biochemical, and molecular analyses. For the germination assay, seventy seeds from WT and transgenic lines were used per replicate and sown on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (½ MS) medium supplemented with or without 200 mM mannitol. Germination was recorded daily for seven days, and seeds exhibiting a radicle length of at least 2 mm were considered germinated.

Genetic transformation and generation of OE and mutant lines

To generate SlASR4-overexpressing transgenic lines, the full-length coding sequence (CDS) of SlASR4 was amplified using gene-specific primers. The resulting PCR product was cloned into the pMD-19T vector and verified by sequencing. The confirmed SlASR4 fragment was subsequently excised via double restriction enzyme digestion and ligated into the plant expression vector pVBG2307. For the generation of SlASR4 mutant lines, two high-efficiency single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the SlASR4 CDS were designed based on the CRISPR/Cas9 system. These sgRNAs were assembled into the pYLCRISPR/Cas9 Pubi-H vector according to a previously established knockout protocol [29]. Both the overexpression and knockout recombinant vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 via the freeze-thaw method. Tomato transformation was then carried out using the standard leaf disc method. Transgenic plants were selected on kanamycin-containing medium. Putative overexpression lines were confirmed by genomic PCR, while mutations in the knockout lines were identified through Sanger sequencing and the Hi-TOM platform. Seeds were harvested from the primary (T0) transformants (Supplemental Fig. 1–4), and homozygous T2 generation lines were used for subsequent experiments.

Physiological and biochemical assays

A series of assays, including water loss assays, relative water content, electrolyte leakage, and chlorophyll content, were carried out to assess the physiological responses of transgenic and WT lines under drought stress conditions.

Water loss assay

The detached leaf approach was employed to evaluate transpiration water loss. The second fully expanded leaf from the apex of each genotype was excised, placed on dry filter paper, and exposed to continuous white fluorescent light at ambient temperature. Leaf weight was recorded at 30-minute intervals for 7 h, and water loss was expressed as a percentage of initial fresh weight in relation to time.

Relative water content (RWC)

Leaf relative water content was determined according to the methodology outlined by Shekhawat et al. 2011 [30]. Freshly harvested leaves were immediately weighed to obtain fresh weight (FW) and then floated on deionized water at 4 °C overnight to determine turgid weight (TW). Subsequently, the samples were oven-dried at 60–62 °C for 48 h to obtain dry weight (DW). RWC was calculated using the following formula:

RWC (%) = (FW – DW) / (TW – DW) × 100

Electrolyte leakage assay

To evaluate membrane integrity under drought stress, electrolyte leakage was measured following the protocol described by Muhammad et al. (2019) [31]. Leaves of a uniform size and morphology were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water to eliminate surface impurities and then blotted dry to remove excess moisture. The leaves were cut into strips and immersed in 25 mL of distilled water in tubes. The tubes were shaken at 300 g at 25 °C for 4 h in the dark, and after incubation, the initial conductivity of the solutions (R₁) was measured using a conductivity meter. The samples were subsequently boiled for 30 min, cooled at room temperature, and the final conductivity (R₂) was recorded. Electrolyte leakage was calculated as:

Relative Conductivity (%) = (R₁ / R₂) × 100

Determination of chlorophyll and photosynthetic activity

Total chlorophyll content was quantified in leaves from control and stressed plants according to the method described by Arnon et al. [32]. Fresh leaf tissues were incubated in 80% (v/v) acetone under dark conditions overnight to extract photosynthetic pigments. Absorbance was measured at 645 and 663 nm using a spectrophotometer, and chlorophyll content was calculated and expressed as milligrams per gram of fresh weight (mg g⁻¹ FW). Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were measured in the third fully expanded leaves following 30 min of dark adaptation using a PAM-2500 portable pulse-amplitude modulated chlorophyll fluorometer (Heinz Walz, Effeltrich, Germany).

Quantification of oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzyme activities

Histochemical detection of ROS species was performed according to Wu et al. [33]. The top leaves from control and stress plants were harvested and incubated in staining solution for the in-situ visualization of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and superoxide anion (O₂⁻) using 1 mg mL⁻¹ diaminobenzidine (DAB, pH 3.8) and 0.1% nitro tetrazolium blue chloride (NBT, pH 7.8), respectively. Endogenous H₂O₂ levels were quantified using a commercial H₂O₂ assay kit (H₂O₂; Catalog No. REFD799773), following the manufacturer’s protocol. This methodology involved the reaction of H₂O₂ with a colorimetric probe, with absorbance measured spectrophotometrically to determine H₂O₂ concentration. Lipid peroxidation was assessed by measuring the malondialdehyde (MDA) content through the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction, as detailed in the MDA assay kit (MDA; Catalog No. REFD799761). The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD) was determined using enzyme-specific kits (Catalog Nos. REFD799593, REFD799597, and REFD799591, respectively), following kits protocol. All the kits used in the current study were provided by Sangon Biotech, China.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the Total RNA Extractor Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and cDNA was synthesized using the Reverse Transcription Kit (Biosharp Life Sciences, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. qRT-PCR was performed on a Thermo cycler CFX96 Real-Time System (BIO-RAD Corp., Hercules, CA, USA) with 2x Realtime PCR Super mix (SYBERgreen, Mei5bio, Beijing, China). The PCR conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 56 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 20s. The tomato Slactin gene served as an internal control for normalization. and relative gene expression levels were quantified through 2−ΔΔCT method [34].

Yeast two-hybrid assay

The cDNA of SlASR4 (lacking stop codon) was amplified by PCR and cloned into the pGBKT7-p53 vector via homologous recombination, generating the pGBKT7-p53-SlASR4 construct (bait). Similarly, the coding sequence of PP2 (lacking stop codon) was cloned into the pGADT7 vector, resulting in the pGADT7-PP2 construct (prey). Positive and negative controls were included using the constructs pGBKT7-p53 + pGADT7+LargeT (positive control) and pGBKT7-laminC + pGADT7-LargeT (negative control). The resulting constructs were co-transformed into the Y2HGold yeast strain and plated on synthetic defined (SD-T-L) under serial dilutions (100, 10− 1, and 10− 2). The cultures were incubated at 30 °C for 3 days to isolate positive yeast transformants. Subsequently, the positive colonies were resuspended, serially diluted, and cultured on SD-T-L-H + 0.25 mM 3AT and SD-T-L-H + 0.25 mM 3AT + X-α-gal media to assess protein–protein interactions.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

For the Co-IP assay, SlASR4 and SlPP2 were cloned into expression vectors containing a 3xHA tag and a 4xMyc tag, respectively. Both constructs were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via agroinfiltration. Total proteins were extracted 48 h post-infiltration, and Co-IP was performed using anti-HA magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immunoprecipitated proteins were detected by western blotting using anti-HA and anti-Myc antibodies (Abcam).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

Full-length cDNAs of SlASR4 and PP2 were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-nYFP and pCAMBIA1300-cYFP vectors, respectively. The resulting constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and subsequently co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After 3 days incubation period, Fluorescence signals of the detached leaves were measured using a Leica SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope system. All the primers used in the experiment are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0. San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analyses of all the recorded data. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) was computed for at least three replicates, and * p < 0.05 and ** p < 0.01 indicate significant differences compared with the control using Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.

Results

SlASR4 gene features

The full-length coding sequence of tomato ASR4 is 894 bp and encodes a protein of 297 amino acids. The homologous protein sequences of various solanaceous plant species were retrieved using Solanum Genomic BLAST, and these proteins showed high sequence conservation (Fig. 1A), indicating that ASR4 may have similar functions across different species. To investigate the structural characteristics, the three-dimensional model of tomato SlASR4 was generated using SWISS-MODEL. The predicted structure contained a conserved ABA_WDS domain near the C-terminus, consisting of a core ABA/conserved block (Fig. 1B). Phylogenetic analysis was performed using cultivated tomato SlASR4 and its homologs from wild tomato and other solanaceous species. The resulting tree exhibited that ASR4 proteins from cultivated and wild tomato species clustered together, followed by S. tuberosum and S. melongena (Fig. 1C), further supporting their close evolutionary relationship.

Expression analysis of ASR4

Tissue-specific expression analysis revealed that transcript levels of the ASR4 gene were significantly higher in leaves and stems compared to seed and roots (Fig. 2A). To elucidate the role of ASR4 in stress adaptation, its expression profile was examined under various abiotic stress conditions. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis demonstrated that SlASR4 transcripts were markedly upregulated in response to drought, salt, heat, and cold treatments. ASR4 expression peaked at 6 h and 3 h following drought and salt exposure, while heat stress led to a maximum induction at 3 h, followed by a gradual decline. In contrast, cold stress triggered a moderate increase in SlASR4 transcript levels, with the highest expression observed at 3 h post-treatment (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that SlASR4 plays a dynamic role in early stress response across multiple abiotic stressors.

Assessment of drought stress tolerance in SlASR4-overexpressed and knockout plants

To evaluate the drought tolerance conferred by SlASR4, we first assessed the seed germination rates in WT and transgenic lines following treatment with a 200 mM mannitol-supplemented medium. Under normal conditions, no significant differences were observed in seedling growth or germination rates between the WT and transgenic lines. However, in the presence of mannitol stress, both WT and slasr4 knockout lines exhibited reduced growth and germination rates compared to the OE lines (Fig. 3A-B). Similarly, there were no significant differences in leaf RWC and REL between WT and transgenic lines under well-watered conditions. After exposure of plants to drought stress, WT and knockout showed substantial reductions in relative water content and significant increases in electrolyte leakage compared to OE lines (Fig. 3C-D). Furthermore, the water loss rate of detached leaves from SlASR4 overexpressing lines was significantly lower than that of the WT plants, but the slas4 lines exhibited a higher water loss rate compared to the WT (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these findings reveal that SlASR4 activity enhances drought stress tolerance by improving membrane stability in the overexpressing lines.

Functional role of SlASR4 in drought-induced ROS accumulation and improving photosynthetic activity

Drought stress is one of the critical factors that triggers excessive accumulation of ROS, consequently leading to oxidative damage within plant cells. To elucidate the functional role of SlASR4 in response to drought stress, five-week-old WT and transgenic tomato lines were exposed to two weeks of drought treatment. Under well-watered conditions, no significant differences were observed in the phenotypes or physiological parameters among the lines (Fig. 4). However, following two weeks of water deprivation, plant growth was markedly inhibited in all lines, with varying degrees of severity. Compared with WT, leaf wilting was more pronounced in the SlASR4 knock-out lines, where it was significantly alleviated in the overexpressed lines (Fig. 4A). To elucidate further the functional role of SlASR4 in the regulation of ROS homeostasis, ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation were analyzed in WT and SlASR4 transgenic plants under drought conditions. Under natural growth conditions, all lines exhibited minimal DAB and NBT staining, indicating low accumulation of H₂O₂, O₂⁻, and MDA content (Fig. 4B-E). However, during a water-deficient environment, SlASR4-knockout lines exhibited high levels of H₂O₂ and O₂⁻, as evidenced by pronounced brown and blue staining. In contrast, the overexpression lines demonstrated significantly attenuated staining, highlighting enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity compared to WT and knockout lines (Fig. 4B-C). Consistent with visual observation, quantitative assessments confirmed that drought-induced H₂O₂ levels were significantly higher in knockout lines but remained lower in overexpression lines relative to WT (Fig. 4D). Similarly, MDA content, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was significantly elevated in drought-stressed knockout and WT plants but restrained in overexpression lines (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these results show that SlASR4 gain of function improves drought tolerance by mitigating ROS accumulation and membrane damage.

Chlorophyll content and fluorescence are essential indicators of photosynthetic integrity, reflecting the ability of plants to maintain photosynthetic function under environmental stress. To investigate this, chlorophyll content and fluorescence were analyzed in 5-week-old plants in both control and drought stress conditions. Under well-watered conditions, no significant differences were observed in chlorophyll content or fluorescence parameters between WT, knock-out, and overexpressing lines (Fig. 5). However, drought stress resulted in a decrease in chlorophyll content across all lines, with a significantly greater reduction in knock-out plants, followed by WT as compared to overexpressing plants (Fig. 5A). Similarly, under drought stress treatments, the maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), the efficiency of energy capture by open PSII reaction centers (Fv’/Fm’), and the efficiency of photochemical quenching (qP) and non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) all decreased, but these parameters remained significantly higher in OE lines compared to both WT and knock-out plants. Notably, knock-out plants showed a more pronounced decline in these parameters compared to WT (Fig. 5B-D). These results show that SlASR4 overexpression effectively mitigates drought-induced impairment of the photosynthetic system in tomato plants.

SlASR4 regulates the antioxidant system to confer drought tolerance

To further elucidate the regulatory role of SlASR4 in drought stress response, antioxidant enzyme activities and the transcriptional profiles of key antioxidant and stress-associated genes were examined in WT and SlASR4 transgenic lines. The results showed that drought stress significantly enhanced the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT across all genotypes, with the most pronounced induction observed in SlASR4-overexpression lines (Fig. 6A-C). In contrast, the knockout line exhibited the minimum enzymatic activities, indicating an impaired antioxidant capacity. Consistent with the biochemical responses, transcriptional profiling revealed significant upregulation of the corresponding antioxidant genes SlSOD, SlPOD, SlCAT, and SlAPX in the OE lines, a response that was substantially attenuated in the knockout lines (Fig. 6D-G). In addition to the antioxidant system, SlASR4 overexpression also activated the drought-responsive signaling pathway, significantly upregulating the expression of SlNHX and SlDREB genes under drought stress (Fig. 6H-I). Overall, these findings highlight that SlASR4 acts as a positive regulator that integrates antioxidant defense and transcriptional signaling networks, thereby protecting against oxidative damage and improving drought tolerance in tomato.

SlASR4 interacts with SlPP2 to regulate drought tolerance

To explore the SlASR4-induced regulatory mechanism, Y2H screening was employed to identify the interacting proteins. SlASR4 was ligated to pGBKT7-p53 to generate a pBD-SlASR4 fusion vector. The pBD-SlASR4 was used to screen the tomato cDNA yeast library utilizing the Matchmaker Gold Y2H system. The X-α-Gal positive clones were selected, sequenced, and analyzed, resulting in the identification of several stress-related proteins, including PP2, as potential SlASR4 interactors. To validate the results, the Y2H interacting assay was performed using SlASR4-bait and PP2-prey constructs. Yeast cells containing both SlASR4 and SlPP2 grew well and displayed blue coloration on SD-TLH + 0.25 mM 3AT + X-α-gal (Fig. 7A), indicating a positive interaction between SlASR4 and SlPP2 in the yeast system. BiFC assays were performed to confirm the direct interaction between SlASR4 and SlPP2 in N. benthamiana. SlASR4 was fused to the N-terminal fragment of YFP (nYFP) and SlPP2 to the C-terminal fragment (cYFP), followed by transient co-expression in tobacco leaves. Strong YFP fluorescence was observed in cells co-expressing SlASR4-nYFP and SlPP2-cYFP, whereas no signal was detected in the negative control combinations (Fig. 7b). The reconstituted YFP signal was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm. Finally, a Co-IP assay was used, which confirmed the interaction in vivo (Fig. 7C). Taken together, these results provide strong evidence that SlASR4 physically interacts with SlPP2.

Discussion

The Abscisic Acid-Stress-Ripening (ASR) gene family represents a group of plant-specific transcription factors that play a critical role in abiotic stress tolerance. ASR encoded proteins typically contain conserved ABA/WDS domains, and are mainly induced by ABA, dehydration, and ripening signals, facilitating stress-responsive gene regulation [35, 36]. Since initial identification in tomato (SlASR1), the ASR family has been consistently characterized across a wide range of monocot and dicot species, exhibiting a regulatory role in osmotic adjustment, antioxidant defense, and cellular protection under stress conditions [14, 24]. Genome-wide analyses in crops like wheat and pear have revealed various ASR isoforms with distinct spatiotemporal expression patterns, underscoring their functional versatility [36, 37]. Despite direct association with ABA signaling, certain ASR genes are also activated through hormone-independent pathways, suggesting their involvement in broader intrinsic stress adaptation mechanisms [35]. The tomato ASR4 in the present study exhibited a highly conserved C-terminal ABA WDS domain, with an identical amino acid sequence and closed phylogenetic clustering with its wild relatives (Fig. 1). The strong sequence conservation and evolutionary association indicate that ASR4 has been functionally preserved and that its introgression from wild species likely contributed critical adaptive functions particularly those associated with stress responses during tomato evolution. In this study, qRT-PCR analysis revealed differential expression of SlASR4 in various tissues and significant upregulation under drought, salt, cold, and heat treatments (Fig. 2A-B). The results align with a previous comprehensive review that determined broad induction of ASR transcription factors under drought, salt, heat, and cold stress, with tissue-specific expression patterns in numerous species [35]. Similarly, in Setaria italica, the ASR4 orthologs serve downstream of the ARDP transcription factor and significantly enhance drought and salinity tolerance by modulating stress-responsive genes [38]. Mannitol-induced osmotic stress is commonly used in controlled experimental systems to simulate drought conditions, as it lowers the external water potential and restricts water uptake without causing direct ionic toxicity. This approach is particularly effective for assessing stress tolerance during the seedling stage, as seedlings are highly sensitive to water deficits due to their dependence on rapid imbibition and early metabolic activation [39, 40]. In line with this concept, the overexpression of the SlAnnSp2 annexin gene in tomato has been shown to enhance germination and early seedling growth under mannitol-induced stress significantly. AnnSp2 OE lines maintained higher germination percentage and exhibited reduced growth inhibition compared with WT under mannitol treatment, demonstrating the potential genetic enhancement of stress-responsive pathways to alleviate osmotic stress at the seedling stage [41]. In the present study, SlASR4 OE lines similarly exhibited enhanced germination and improved early seedling establishment under mannitol-induced stress. In contrast, ASR4 knockout lines exhibited pronounced sensitivity, characterized by reduced germination efficiency compared with wild-type plants. The ASR family members are known to function as ABA-responsive regulators that contribute to abiotic stress tolerance, particularly during early growth stages [24]. For example, overexpression of ASR genes has been shown to enhance abiotic stress tolerance by improving root hydraulic conductivity and regulating stomatal closure in rice [42], maintaining photosynthetic activity and higher chlorophyll content in strawberry [43], enhancing membrane stability and reducing ions ion leakage in wheat and foxtail millet [44], and improving water retention and stabilizing cellular structures in rice and chickpea [18, 45]. In line with these findings, this study provides functional evidence that SlASR4 acts as a positive regulator of drought tolerance in tomato. Specifically, SlASR4-overexpressing transgenic lines exhibited drought resilience, characterized by significantly reduced wilting, higher relative water content, and lower water loss rates compared to WT and knockout lines under drought stress (Fig. 3A-E).

Drought stress significantly impairs photosynthetic efficiency by disrupting the balance between absorbed light energy and its utilization in carbon fixation. Water scarcity limits CO₂ diffusion into chloroplasts due to stomatal closure, resulting in reduced electron consumption by the Calvin cycle and subsequent over-reduction of the photosynthetic electron transport chain. This imbalance leads to inhibition of photosystem II (PSII) activity, which is reflected by decreased chlorophyll fluorescence parameters such as Fv/Fm, (ΦPSII), qP, and NPQ. Several studies have demonstrated that drought stress progressively reduces chlorophyll content and PSII photochemistry, indicating damage or down-regulation of the photosynthetic apparatus under prolonged stress conditions [46, 47]. Consistent with these findings, our results showed a reduction in both chlorophyll content and fluorescence parameters in all genotypes under drought stress. However, the stronger decline was observed in knockout and WT plants compared to overexpressing lines, indicating that SlASR4 overexpression enhances PSII stability and improves photosynthetic resilience within water deficit environment (Fig. 5). Previous studies have demonstrated that drought stress induces NPQ by promoting the accumulation and activation of the PSII subunit PsbS, which facilitates conformational changes in light-harvesting complex II (LHCII) for efficient thermal energy dissipation [48, 49]. In addition, drought-induced stomatal closure intensifies excitation pressure on PSII, further accelerating NPQ activation due to limited CO₂ availability [50]. In the present study, NPQ values were significantly lower under drought stress, yet overexpressing SlASR4 lines showed notably higher NPQ levels compared to knockout and WT plants. This indicates a superior capacity for energy dissipation and photo protection in the overexpressing lines. Collectively, these results suggest that SlASR4 overexpression contributes to drought tolerance by preserving chlorophyll content, stabilizing PSII photochemistry, and maintaining NPQ-mediated photo protection under water-limited conditions.

Drought stress causes an imbalance between the ROS production and the antioxidant defense system in plants, leading to oxidative stress and accumulation of ROS and MDA contents [51]. It promotes excessive ROS generation in various cell compartments due to impaired photosynthetic activity and stomatal closure. During prolong drought conditions, the high ROS production exceeds the detoxification capacity of the enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant defense machinery, resulting in oxidative damage and programmed cell death [52]. It has been established that ASR protein functions as a regulator in osmotic adjustment, antioxidant activation, and cellular homeostasis [53]. In the current study, exposure to drought stress induced oxidative stress in all plants, as evidenced by increased accumulation of MDA, H₂O₂, and O₂⁻ levels. However, the SlASR4 overexpression lines exhibited a markedly attenuated oxidative response, accumulating significantly lower ROS and MDA content compared to the WT and knockout lines (Fig. 4b-e). These findings are consistent with previous observations reporting that TaASR1-D overexpression improves drought tolerance in wheat by regulating ROS homeostasis [54]. Similarly, overexpression of OsASR5 in rice and ZmASR3 in maize has been shown to modulate ROS generation via ABA-associated antioxidant pathways in transgenic lines [38, 55]. The antioxidant enzyme system is crucial for mitigating stress-induced oxidative damage by eliminating ROS and reducing cellular toxicity in plants [56]. ASR genes, such as BdASR2 and ThASR3 from Brachypodium and Tamarix, respectively, participate in ROS detoxification by regulating antioxidant activities under osmotic stress [23, 57]. In addition, the overexpression of ASR genes in various plant species increases drought tolerance, an attribute positively correlated with elevated antioxidant enzyme activities [35, 53]. The conserved function of ASR across plant taxa suggests a universal role in managing combined osmotic and oxidative stress [58]. In this study, SlASR4 overexpression significantly induced the activities of antioxidant enzymes in transgenic lines compared to WT and knockout plants, which indicates that overexpression lines possess greater antioxidant capacity and osmotic regulatory ability, thereby improving ROS scavenging and reducing membrane lipid peroxidation under stress conditions (Fig. 6A-C).

Plant stress tolerance mechanisms involve the coordinated activation of antioxidant defense and stress-responsive genes to cope with adverse conditions. To further explore the molecular mechanism underlying SlASR4-mediated drought stress tolerance, we determined the transcript levels of key genes associated with antioxidant and stress responses. The analysis revealed that the expression levels of antioxidants (SlSOD, SlPOD, SlCAT, and SlAPX), and stress-related (SlNHX and SlDREB) were significantly higher in overexpressed lines compared to WT and knockout lines (Fig. 6D-I). These findings corroborate previous reports indicating that the transcriptional activation of the plant antioxidant system is a defining characteristic of drought tolerance [59]. Similarly, genes such as DREB and NHX have been shown to play regulatory roles in drought adaptation [60, 61]. Particularly, DREB transcription factors are recognized as key regulators of abiotic stress responses, activating drought-inducible genes via ABA-independent pathways [62, 63].

Notably, SlAPX exhibited a moderate induction compared to that of SlCAT and SlPOD in overexpression lines. As an integral component of the ascorbate-glutathione cycle, APX is essential for hydrogen peroxide detoxification. Its relatively limited upregulation may reflect differential regulatory mechanisms or tissue-specific expression patterns governed by SlASR4 under drought stress. This observation is supported by previous investigations demonstrating that APX activity is tightly controlled and often functions in a complementary manner with CAT, depending on the subcellular compartment and stress severity [64]. Collectively, these findings support the model that ASR4 proteins function as transcriptional regulators and molecular chaperones during abiotic stresses [24, 38]. The capacity of SlASR4 to modulate the antioxidant activity and expression of key stress-regulatory genes underscores its pivotal role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis during drought stress in tomato.

Previous study has revealed that ASRs are Zn2+ binding proteins and their activity is dependent on Zn2+ binding [15]. In this study, Y2H, BiFC, and Co-IP assays showed that SlASR4 directly interacts with SlPP2 protein (Fig. 7). PP2 is one of the major P-proteins found in phloem exudates, which plays a central role in vascular tissue defense, particularly through phloem-based defense (PBD) [65]. Multiple studies have highlighted PP2 as a defensive protein involved in drought, salt, heat, and cold stress tolerance [66–69]. Consistent with these findings, PP2 knockdown lines exhibit stress-sensitive phenotypes compared to WT plants [70, 71]. PP2 improves stress-induced ROS burst by interacting with peroxidase and potentially acts as a key controller to enhance oxidoreductase and dioxygenase activities [72]. PP2s have been documented for their role in regulating plant defenses via precise modulation of phytohormone interactions [65]. It has been hypothesized that PP2-A1 improves salt stress tolerance by potentially contributing to the ABA signal transduction pathway [73]. However, the precise mechanism underlying the interaction between PP2 and ABA during abiotic stress remains unclear. ASR genes, which play crucial roles in plant responses to various abiotic stresses, are closely associated with ABA signaling pathways [15]. Moreover, the phosphorylation of protein kinases, such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, has been revealed to be involved in ABA signal transduction during abiotic stress responses [74]. In our previous study, we demonstrated that SlMAPK3 positively regulates drought tolerance in tomato by directly interacting with the SlASR4 protein [75]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that under drought stress, SlASR4 may function as a molecular chaperone, stabilizing SlPP2 or modulating its conformation. This interaction could enhance SlPP2’s ability to bind and transport specific cargoes, such as drought-responsive mRNAs or small RNAs, via the phloem to target tissues, including the roots. Such processes may trigger adaptive responses, including enhanced water uptake and altered growth patterns, thereby contributing to the drought tolerance mechanisms.

In conclusion, we propose a working model for the functional role of SlASR4 in tomato drought stress tolerance (Fig. 8). Our model suggests that drought stress induces the expression of SlASR4, and its overexpression enhances drought tolerance in tomato by improving water retention, maintaining chlorophyll content, and stabilizing cellular integrity. These physiological changes are supported by increased antioxidant enzyme activities and efficient ROS scavenging, which correlate with the upregulation of key stress-responsive genes in the overexpressed lines. Additionally, we demonstrate that SlASR4 physically interacts with SlPP2, and this interaction provides evidence for the involvement of SlASR4 and SlPP2 in phloem-based defense under stress conditions. Our findings reveal novel molecular and physiological mechanisms by which SlASR4 contributes to tomato drought tolerance, offering valuable insights for breeding drought-resistant crops.
