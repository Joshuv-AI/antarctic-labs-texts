Navigating the Aortopathy Dilemma in Pregnancy
Authors: Sarah L Fairley, Chani Tromop-van Dalen, Alyssa Kirby, Andrew Aitken, Jade S Lodge, Sally Gunderson, Vanessa Bowden, Sean Galvin, Ashley Cheung, Katherine Ferrier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAortic dissection during pregnancy most commonly occurs in women with undiagnosed aortopathies. High-risk aortopathies can be managed successfully throughout pregnancy within a pregnancy heart team model of care. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology guidelines provide comprehensive recommendations on the management of cardiac disease during pregnancy. We describe 4 cases of high-risk and very–high risk aortopathies that were successfully managed through pregnancy, including 1 patient with bicuspid aortic valve aortopathy who underwent successful cardiac surgery in the early second trimester. Pregnancy should no longer be a barrier to gold-standard or guideline-directed care for our high-risk cardiac patients.Key words: aorta, dissection, pregnancy, surgery

Acute aortic syndromes in pregnancy are rare but carry a significant risk of maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality. The rate of aortic dissection in pregnancy has been cited at 5.5 per million.1 Traditionally, the higher risk of aortic dissection during pregnancy was attributable to shear stress on the arterial tree, hormonal effects, and the hemodynamic effects of pregnancy. However, not all of the risk is attributable to these factors, and mechanistic questions remain unanswered. The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) has recently published updated guidelines on the management of cardiac disease in pregnancy, with an important focus on patients with aortic disease.2 We describe 4 patients with high-risk aortopathies of differing etiologies who were successfully managed via a pregnancy heart team in a tertiary obstetric, neonatal, and cardiac surgical center.

Patient 1

A 35-year-old woman with known asymptomatic bicuspid aortic valve disease and aortopathy was referred to our pregnancy heart team at 9 weeks' gestation. She had previously been counseled regarding pregnancy. Imaging 3 years before this pregnancy showed moderate to severe mixed aortic valve disease with an ascending aorta of 49 mm on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). She was asymptomatic and normotensive. Urgent transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular systolic function, severe aortic stenosis (maximum flow velocity: 4.1 m/s, mean pressure gradient: 39.3 mm Hg), and moderate to severe aortic regurgitation. Urgent cardiac MRI demonstrated a midascending aortic dimension of 53 mm (Figure 1).

(A) Transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrating a heavily calcified bicuspid aortic valve. (B) Doppler trace of aortic valve showing severe aortic stenosis with peak velocity of 4.1 m/s. (C) Cardiac MRI demonstrating dilatation of the midascending aorta at 53 mm. MRI = magnetic resonance imaging.

Bicuspid valve aortopathy is associated with increased dissection risk during pregnancy. High-risk features include an aortic dimension of >50 mm and the presence of a “root phenotype.”3 The term “root phenotype” describes a pattern of aortic dilatation that is primarily at the aortic root and sinuses, with sparing on involvement of the remainder of the ascending aorta.

Management options were discussed in the pregnancy heart team meeting and at the cardiac-surgical meeting. The primary concern was the risk of aortic dissection with a dimension >50 mm (class IV risk according to modified World Health Organization [mWHO] 2.0). After extensive counseling, the patient opted to proceed with the pregnancy and undergo surgery while pregnant.

An uncomplicated Bentall procedure was performed at 14 weeks' gestation. An On-X valve (Artivion) was chosen to allow a lower target international normalized ratio (1.5-2.0). Conditions during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) to reduce fetal risk were to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 90 to 75 mm Hg, flow >3.0 L/min/m2, cooling to 34 °C, and activated clotting time target >300 seconds. Fetal heartbeat was confirmed postoperatively. The patient was transitioned to twice-daily low–molecular weight heparin at a dose of 1 mg/kg and aspirin 100 mg daily. The pregnancy was complicated by 2 antepartum hemorrhages in the setting of placenta previa. The second antepartum hemorrhage at 34 weeks' gestation expedited delivery. Low–molecular weight heparin was transitioned to unfractionated heparin before delivery. The baby was born by caesarean section at 34 weeks and 2 days' gestation, was transferred to the neonatal unit owing to prematurity, and recovered well. Bridging heparin was restarted, and warfarin was initiated on day 2. The patient and infant remain well at follow-up.4

Patient 2

A 34-year old woman with known psoriatic arthritis and aortopathy was referred to the pregnancy heart team at 17 weeks' gestation. She had 2 uncomplicated pregnancies 14 and 12 years prior. Incidental finding of an aortopathy (49 mm) was made on computed tomography (CT) at 5 years before this pregnancy after asymmetric blood pressures were found on clinical examination. Surveillance CT 1 year later demonstrated an isolated ascending aortic dimension of 51 mm, a hypoplastic left subclavian artery, and a patent left vertebral artery. The aortic valve was trileaflet with normal function. The patient did not have clinical features of Marfan syndrome, and there was no significant family history of aortic dissection. Genetic screening was planned but did not happen. Repeat CT 3 years before the current pregnancy demonstrated an aortic dimension of 55 mm. A plan was made for a CT–positron emission tomography study to investigate for aortitis at this stage with prophylactic surgery, but the patient was lost to follow-up. She was re-referred at 17 weeks' gestation. Urgent noncontrast CT (owing to a prior contrast allergy) showed an ascending aortic dimension of 55 mm. Cardiac MRI confirmed a trileaflet aortic valve with mild aortic regurgitation, and normal root, arch, and neck vessels (Figure 2).

(A and B) Noncontrast CT demonstrating dilated midascending aorta with a maximum dimension of 55 mm. (C) Cardiac MRI demonstrating dilated midascending aorta with sparing of the aortic root. CT = computed tomography; MRI = magnetic resonance imaging.

The case was discussed at the pregnancy heart team meeting and with the cardiac-surgical multidisciplinary team. The etiology of the underlying aortopathy was unclear, but nonsyndromic heritable thoracic aortic disease (HTAD) was considered, and the team felt the pregnancy was very high risk based on the aortic dimension (mWHO 2.0 class IV risk).The rate of aortic dissection in pregnant patients with HTAD in the ROPAC (Registry of Pregnancy and Cardiac disease) III registry was low at 3.5%.5 In patients with HTAD, the risk of distal dissection persists during pregnancy despite surgical repair of the proximal aorta.

The patient met with a cardiac surgeon at 21 weeks and 2 days' gestation to discuss aortic surgery during pregnancy with interposition graft to reduce the risk of acute proximal aortic dissection. She chose not to have surgery during pregnancy and was evaluated every 4 weeks with MRI as well as kept under strict blood pressure control. Elective caesarean section was performed at 28 weeks' gestation to balance the risks of prematurity against prolonged risk of aortic dissection with the hemodynamic stress of advanced third trimester. The baby weighed 1.6 kg and was transferred to the neonatal unit. The patient remained in the hospital for a further 7 days after delivery. She then underwent successful aortic repair with an interposition graft and sinotubular junction remodeling 2 weeks after her caesarean section. The dilated portion of the aorta was thickened, ulcerated, and highly abnormal; tissue was sent for histology to assist with ascertaining the underlying pathogenesis of her aortopathy.

Patient 3

A 28-year old woman with known Loeys-Dietz syndrome aortopathy (positive TGFBR2 mutation), bilateral internal carotid artery aneurysms, and a chronic left vertebral artery dissection was referred to our pregnancy heart team at 12 weeks' gestation with an unplanned pregnancy. She was known to cardiology services with an aortic root dimension of 41 mm demonstrated on MRI 1 year prior to this pregnancy. She had been counseled regarding pregnancy with a plan to perform prophylactic surgery before any pregnancies. There was a significant family history of premature aortic dissection. Blood pressure was normal. Updated MRI showed a sinus dimension of 43 mm (Figure 3).

(A) Cardiac MRI showing dilatation of aortic root with sinus dimension of 43 mm and (B) relatively normal midascending aorta <40 mm. MRI = magnetic resonance imaging.

This was an mWHO 2.0 class IV risk pregnancy given the family history of aortic dissection and the “root phenotype.” Termination of pregnancy was declined by the patient. The woman met with a cardiac surgeon to discuss the option of aortic surgery (valve-sparing aortic root replacement) during pregnancy. The overall risk of aortic dissection in pregnancy in patients with Loyes-Dietz syndrome is estimated to be up to 6%, with the risk thought to be higher in patients with the TGFBR2 mutation and those with aortic root diameters >40 mm.6 Dissection during pregnancy for this patient (high-risk genotype, aortic dimension of 43 mm, previous vertebral dissection, and known family history) was cited at 10% to 20% based on available published experience.7 The risk of maternal morbidity and mortality was cited at 1% to 3% by the cardiac surgeon, with a risk of fetal loss of approximately 30%.8 The patient opted to proceed with conservative management with six-weekly evaluation, serial MRI scans, and continuation of beta-blocker therapy. A decision was made by the pregnancy heart team for elective caesarean section at 36 weeks to avoid prolonged risk of dissection. Elective caesarean section was performed at 35 weeks and 6 days' gestation (birth weight: 2.9 kg). She was discharged on day 7 postpartum. Mother and baby remain well at follow-up evaluation. She has since opted to proceed with prophylactic aortic surgery before further pregnancies.

Patient 4

A 34 year old woman was referred to the pregnancy heart team at 9 weeks' gestation in her fifth pregnancy. She was diagnosed with Marfan syndrome after her fourth pregnancy, prompted by a diagnosis in a first-degree relative. There was no family history of aortic dissection. Genetic testing confirmed a pathological variant in the FBN1 gene. Echocardiography demonstrated an aortic root of 45 mm with a normal aortic valve. CT scan showed the remaining thoracic aorta to be normal (Figure 4). After her fourth pregnancy, she had been counseled regarding the risk of future pregnancies and was advised to consider prophylactic surgery before further pregnancies. She was prescribed losartan but elected not to take it.

(A) Cardiac MRI showing aortic root dilatation with a sinus dimension of 45 mm and (B) normal dimensions for the remainder of the aorta. MRI = magnetic resonance imaging.

Initial attempts to review her early in her pregnancy were unsuccessful, as she lived in a very remote area and had limited social supports. She was evaluated at 15 weeks' gestation, and an updated echocardiogram showed a stable aortic root at 45 mm. The threshold for prepregnancy prophylactic aortic surgery is >45 mm. Marfan syndrome with an aortic dimension of 40 to 45 mm is considered mWHO 2.0 class III, with aortic event rates of up to 10%. Termination was declined by the patient. Beta-blocker therapy was continued throughout pregnancy. Serial echocardiography demonstrated stable aortic root size. Elective caesarean section was performed at 33 weeks' gestation for obstetric reasons rather than cardiac reasons, with an infant birth weight of 1.9 kg. Both the mother and the baby remain well at clinical follow-up.

Discussion

The majority of aortic dissections that occur during pregnancy affect women with undiagnosed pathology.9 The updated 2025 ESC guidelines advise that all women with known aortic disease should be risk assessed and managed through a pregnancy heart team.2 Understanding the underlying disease process is important, as the risk classification varies with primary pathology (Table 1).

In Loeys-Dietz syndrome, the threshold for surgical intervention is dependent on the gene variant, as some are more pathogenic at a lower dimension. Cauldwell et al10 published retrospective data on 20 pregnancies in 13 women with Loeys-Dietz syndrome and reported that in 18 live births, no aortic dissections occurred. Obstetric complications were common, with postpartum hemorrhage in 33% and preterm delivery in 50%. Mode of delivery was via elective caesarean section in 78% of cases.10

The updated ESC 2025 guidelines provide concise recommendations on the management of pregnant women with aortic disease.2 Good blood pressure control is essential. Beta-blockers should be used throughout pregnancy in genetic aortopathies. CT or MRI should be performed within 6 months of pregnancy in patients with known aortopathy. Caesarean section should be considered if the aortic dimension is >45 mm. Prophylactic surgery before conception should be considered based on the underlying diagnosis and dimensions (Table 2). Consideration should also be given to the option of cardiac surgery during pregnancy in high-risk aortopathies. Aortic surgery is the most common form of cardiac surgery during pregnancy. There are no specific guidelines relating to CPB during pregnancy, and each case should be assessed on an individual basis depending on fetal viability and the perceived risk to maternal health if the underlying pathology is not corrected. Meta-analysis data by van Steenbergen et al11 suggest a maternal mortality rate of 7.3% with CPB, with a fetal mortality rate of 26.5%. The risks must be explicitly discussed with the woman to enable informed decision-making to be applied.

This case series demonstrates the successful management of 4 patients with high-risk aortopathies within a well-established pregnancy heart team model of care. Management of each case should be individualized. The pregnancy heart team model aims to ensure that pregnancy is not seen as a barrier to provision of gold-standard care for pregnant women.
