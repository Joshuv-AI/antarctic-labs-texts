Interplay between oxygen redox and interfacial stability of Li-rich positive electrodes in sulfide-based all-solid-state batteries
Authors: Yuqi Wu, Fucheng Ren, Cheng Li, Chuanjing Xu, Huanran Wang, Jianrong Lin, Hanyan Wu, Qin Wang, Yiming Zou, Weidong Zhang, Guozhen Wei, Hongxin Lin, Ying Lin, Zhenyu Wang, Zhengliang Gong, Yong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding and manipulating the interplay between lattice oxygen redox and interfacial stability is critical for realizing the full potential of lithium-rich manganese-based oxide positive electrodes in all-solid-state lithium batteries. Herein, we comprehensively investigate interfacial evolution of Li-rich manganese-based positive electrodes in sulfide electrolyte (Li6PS5Cl) systems and report that a potential mechanism where irreversible lattice oxygen redox at high potential induces severe electrolyte decomposition, forming porous oxygenated by-products that dominate interfacial impedance and hinder anionic redox reactions. Discharge-driven reduction of these by-products—particularly between 2.6-2.0 V—could facilitates interphases reconstruction via lithium incorporation, enhancing ionic transport kinetics and enabling reversible oxygen redox. Building on this identified mechanism, we propose a pre-activation strategy (3.9-2.0 V pre-cycling) with oxygen-modified Li6PS5Cl, which accelerates interphases stabilization and enables high oxygen redox utilization during the first cycle. It delivers a specific capacity of 318 mAh g−1 at 10 mA g−1, and 226 mAh g−1 at 200 mA g−1 with 83.2% retention over 500 cycles at 60 °C. This work has the potential to provide an effective interfacial reconstruction strategy to enhance anionic redox reversibility, providing a design framework for high-energy lithium-rich positive electrodes in all-solid-state lithium batteries.

Introduction

The escalating global demand for renewable energy necessitates the development of advanced energy storage technologies that offer higher energy densities and enhanced safety1,2. Among the available options, all-solid-state lithium batteries (ASSLBs) have emerged as a promising candidate due to their inherent safety and potential for achieving high energy density. Over the past few decades, intensive research has led to the development of a wide range of inorganic solid electrolytes (SEs)—including sulfides, halides, oxides, and borohydrides with high ionic conductivities3–5. These advancements in solid electrolyte chemistry have laid a strong foundation for the practical realization of ASSLBs.

However, positive electrode materials continue to be a major limiting factor in terms of energy and power density in ASSLBs6,7. In this context, lithium-rich manganese-based oxides (LRMOs) have garnered significant attention. Owing to their high theoretical capacity (>300 mAh g−1) and cost-effectiveness, LRMOs are widely regarded as one of the most promising next-generation positive electrode materials8–10. Unlike conventional layered oxide positive electrodes, which rely solely on transition metal (TM) redox reactions, LRMOs derive a portion of their high capacity from the reversible redox activity of lattice oxygen—offering a dual-redox mechanism10–12. Nevertheless, this advantage introduces significant challenges. The activation of lattice oxygen redox in LRMOs typically requires a high charge cut-off potential (>4.5 V vs. Li⁺/Li), which exceeds the electrochemical stability window of most inorganic SEs13,14. This mismatch leads to intrinsic degradation and severe interfacial side reactions. Additionally, oxygen redox processes can produce highly reactive O(2−n)- (0 <n < 2) species and even oxygen gas, aggravating interfacial instability at the positive electrode|electrolyte interface15,16.

To mitigate these issues, numerous studies have explored interfacial modification layers—including LiNbO3, Li3PO4, Li2SO4, Li2B4O7, and Li2WO4 coatings—that serve to stabilize the interface and improve electrochemical performance17–22. Despite these significant advancements, two critical challenges remain: (1) The bidirectional interplay between oxygen redox activity and interfacial stability is not yet fully understood; (2) The incomplete activation of lattice oxygen during initial cycles impairs capacity utilization and introduces variability in activation behavior, complicating state-of-health (SOH) diagnostics.

Addressing these knowledge gaps, Cui et al. introduced the concept of a “spatially asynchronous activation mechanism” in sulfide-based ASSLBs. Their findings reveal that oxygen redox activation is kinetically slower than TM redox and relies on the progressive enhancement of oxygen activity23. Interestingly, this behavior appears to be exclusive to sulfide-based electrolytes and is not observed in halide-based ASSLBs or conventional liquid electrolyte systems—suggesting a unique dynamic coupling between LRMO positive electrodes and sulfide SEs. This exclusivity necessitates deciphering the LRMO/SE interfacial reaction mechanisms, particularly how oxygen redox activity dynamically couples with interfacial chemical evolution, to unlock the full potential of sulfide-based ASSLBs.

Herein, we systematically investigated the interfacial reactivity differences between Li1.2Ni0.13Co0.13Mn0.54O2 (LRMO) and conventional layered LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) positive electrodes when paired with Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl). Through a combination of electrochemical analysis and advanced characterization techniques, we investigated the role of lattice oxygen redox in shaping the positive electrode/electrolyte interphase. By strategically controlling the discharge cut off potentials (2.6 V vs. 2.0 V), we decoupled the effects of by-product reduction and demonstrated that initial interphases reconstruction effectively passivates the interface. Building on these mechanistic insights, we developed a pre-cycling protocol (3.9–2.0 V) incorporating an oxygen-modified LPSCl electrolyte to accelerate interphases reconstruction. This synergistic strategy successfully achieves full lattice oxygen redox utilization within the initial cycle, thereby eliminating the activation lag and associated variability. The optimized cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 318 mAh g−1 at 10 mA g−1, retains 226 mAh g−1 at 200 mA g−1, and exhibits favorable long-term cycling stability with 83.2% capacity retention after 500 cycles. This work provides critical insight into the dynamic interfacial processes governing LRMO-based ASSLBs and offers a viable pathway for optimizing their electrochemical performance—paving the way for the development of next-generation high-capacity solid-state batteries.

Results

Impact of lattice oxygen redox on the interphase evolution

Figure 1a shows the distinctive crystal structure of LRMO materials, which comprises a nanocomposite/solid solution of layered LiNi1-x-yCoxMnyO2 (R-3m) and Li2MnO3 (C2/m) phases24–26. In the lithium-rich system, lithium ions are not only intercalated within the interlayer spaces but also partially incorporated into the transition metal (TM) layers27. The presence of the Li2MnO3 component enables additional reversible capacity through lattice oxygen redox, distinguishing LRMO from conventional layered oxide positive electrodes28,29. To systematically evaluate the influence of lattice oxygen redox on interfacial stability, NCM811—which adopts a pure R-3m structure—was selected as a reference. Unlike LRMO, NCM811 relies solely on TM redox (Ni/Co/Mn) for charge compensation, with negligible oxygen redox involvement. Its lithium ions reside exclusively between the TM layers, and no superlattice ordering or oxygen-active sites are present. The electrochemical behavior of LRMO and NCM811 was investigated in ASSLBs at 60 °C, with utilizing LPSCl as the solid electrolyte and a Li-In alloy as the negative electrode. As shown in Fig. 1b, c, LRMO-based ASSLBs display a distinct activation process: the initial discharge capacity of 194 mAh g−1 gradually increases to 307 mAh g−1 by the seventh cycle, whereas NCM811-based ASSLBs show no such capacity rise. Consistent with Cui’s findings, this progressive activation is attributed to the emergence of oxygen redox activity23. To further compare the oxygen redox behavior in LRMO and NCM811, we conducted O K-edge RIXS measurements to probe bulk oxygen states. Literature reports identify oxidized lattice oxygen by a characteristic RIXS feature—a sharp signal near 531 eV excitation and 524 eV emission energies—associated with O-O bonding32. During the first charge, this signature was limited in both LRMO and NCM811, even up to 4.8 V (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting that lattice-oxygen redox in LRMO is initially suppressed. However, after the third charge to 4.8 V, LRMO exhibits a distinct O-O bonding feature with excitation energies between 529.8 and 532.1 eV and an emission at 524 eV (Fig. 1f). The emergence of this feature coincides with capacity activation, providing direct spectroscopic evidence that progressive oxygen redox underlies the activation process in LRMO-based ASSLBs.

To gain further insight into the activation process, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed over the first seven cycles for both systems (Fig. 1g, h). The Nyquist plots were converted into time-domain spectra using the Distribution of Relaxation Times (DRT) method (Supplementary Fig. 2), enabling quantification of interfacial resistance at the positive electrode/electrolyte interphase—specifically within relaxation times ranging from 10−2 to 1 s15,33. The results clearly indicate a progressive decrease in interfacial impedance in LRMO-based ASSLBs as cycling proceeds, suggesting continuous interphase reconstruction that enhances ionic transport. Conversely, a slight increase in impedance is observed in the NCM811-based cells. These contrasting trends in interfacial resistance correlate well with the observed electrochemical performance and imply that the LRMO/LPSCl interphase fosters more favorable ion transport kinetics during activation process. Therefore, the gradual release of lattice‑oxygen capacity can be attributed to interfacial evolution, underscoring the critical role of interfacial processes in regulating the redox behavior of lattice oxygen.

The evolution of interfacial chemistry in LRMO/LPSCl and NCM811/LPSCl configurations during initial electrochemical cycling was systematically investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). This analysis focused on characterizing the formation and transformation of interfacial by-products at the positive electrode active material (PEAM)/LPSCl interphase. The S 2p and P 2p XPS spectra (Fig. 2a, b) of the pristine NCM811 and LRMO composite positive electrodes both display characteristic doublets corresponding to the PS43⁻ tetrahedra units (S 2p3/2 at 161.31 eV, P 2p3/2 at 132.0 eV) within the argyrodite-type LPSCl34,35. Upon charging to 3.9 V (vs. Li+/Li), new signals corresponding to oxidation by-products—including polysulfides, elemental sulfur, and phosphate species (POx)—were detected. These observations align with the known oxidative decomposition behavior of sulfide-based SEs at elevated potentials36,37. Notably, the quantity and composition of these by-products were comparable between the NCM811 and LRMO systems at this stage, indicating similar interfacial evolution during the early stages of charging. However, upon further charging 4.8 V, a substantial increase in oxidized species, particularly POx, was observed in the LRMO-based ASSLBs. This pronounced accumulation is attributed to the lattice oxygen redox, which intensifies oxidative reactions at the positive electrode/electrolyte interface. In contrast, NCM811—which lacks significant oxygen redox activity—did not exhibit such a marked increase in oxidized by-products, further supporting the correlation between lattice oxygen involvement and interfacial degradation. Following discharge to 2.6 V, most of the by-products, including polysulfides and phosphates, remained detectable but showed partial reduction, suggesting some degree of reversibility or reduction during the discharge process. A subsequent discharge to 2.0 V resulted in a marked decline in by-product intensity, with elemental sulfur signals disappearing entirely. Notably, both LRMO and NCM811 display a short discharge plateau in the potential range of 2.6–2.0 V (Fig. 1b, c), which likely corresponds to the reduction of interfacial reaction by-products. This finding indicates that deep discharge effectively mitigates by-product accumulation, potentially enhancing interfacial kinetics by passivating reactive species and stabilizing the interphase. Tender X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was employed to probe the sulfur chemistry at the positive electrode-electrolyte interphase in both LRMO and NCM811 systems during the first electrochemical cycle across various states of charge (SOC). The S K-edge XAS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 3) reveal pronounced changes in the sulfur electronic structure within the interphase. In the reference LPSCl sample, three main peaks are observed at approximately 2471 eV (Peak 1), 2472.9 eV (Peak 2), and 2476 eV (Peak 3)38. Upon charging, the intensity of Peak 2 increases and shifts to lower energy, corresponding to the formation of polysulfide species (~2471.8 eV) and elemental sulfur (~2472 eV). Simultaneously, the decrease in Peak 3 intensity indicates a structural distortion of PS43- units within the sulfide framework39. These spectral evolutions are most prominent at 4.8 V, reflecting severe interfacial oxidation of the electrolyte. During subsequent discharge, Peak 2 gradually recovers and shifts back toward its original position, signifying a partially reversible interfacial redox process, consistent with XPS observations. A comparative analysis of the S K-edge spectra between LRMO and NCM811 (Supplementary Fig. 3b) further demonstrates that LRMO exhibits markedly stronger interfacial side reactions when charged to 4.8 V. This distinction highlights the critical role of lattice-oxygen redox activity in accelerating interfacial degradation within sulfide-based all-solid-state systems.

The interphase evolution between LRMO and LPSCl during the initial electrochemical cycle was also systematically investigated using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS). Supplementary Fig. 4 presents the ToF-SIMS spectra of oxygen-containing byproducts in LRMO composite electrodes at three distinct states: pristine, charged to 4.8 V, and discharged to 2.0 V during the first cycle. To ensure comparability, the intensity of each fragment peak was normalized against the total intensity of all detected fragments39,40. In agreement with XPS results, a significant increase in phosphate and sulfate signals—oxidative decomposition products of LPSCl—was observed upon charging, followed by a notable decrease during discharge, indicating partial reversibility. To further investigate the spatial distribution of these fragments, Ar+ ion beam depth profiling was conducted. Mapping images of S−/PS2− and MnO3− were used to localize LPSCl and LRMO, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2c, SOx− species were predominantly distributed over the positive electrode particles after charging to 4.8 V, whereas POx− species were concentrated at the electrode/electrolyte interphase, with PSOx− observed within the electrolyte region. This spatial distribution indicates pronounced mutual diffusion of sulfur and oxygen species during cycling. Specifically, sulfur diffusing into the positive electrode undergoes in situ oxidation to form sulfate, while oxygen released from the positive electrode is partially captured by phosphorus at the interphase to form phosphate. Residual oxygen is continuous to diffuse into the electrolyte, generating PSxOy. These results confirm that interfacial reactions between LRMO and LPSCl lead to the generation of oxygen-rich by-products, particularly at high potentials where lattice oxygen participates in redox reactions. The accumulation of these by-products at the interphase hampers further oxygen redox activity. However, they are partially reduced during discharge, facilitating the reconstruction of the electrode/electrolyte interphase and enhancing electrochemical activity.

The microstructural evolution of LRMO and NCM811 positive electrodes charged to 4.8 V was further characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as depicted in Fig. 2d, e. In the SEM images, the PEAM and SE are distinguishable by their relatively bright and dark gray contrasts, respectively, consistent with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping results (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Fig. 6). A well-defined interfacial transition layer with darker contrast was observed at the LRMO/LPSCl interphase, particularly around large LPSCl agglomerates. Comparative analysis of EDS results reveals that pronounced sulfur enrichment within LRMO particles after charging to 4.8 V—evidence of sulfur migration into the positive electrode lattice (Supplementary Fig. 7). Concurrently, oxygen is found to form a distinct ring-like structure surrounding LPSCl agglomerates, spatially coincident with the POx distribution identified in the TOF-SIMS maps. In contrast, no such interphase was detected in the NCM811 system. The negligible oxygen involvement in redox reactions in NCM811 corresponds to the lower formation of oxygen-containing by-products compared to LRMO, confirming the correlation between lattice oxygen redox activity and interfacial degradation.

Supplementary Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 present the dynamic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (DEIS) results of ASSLBs employing LRMO and NCM811 positive electrodes during the first cycle. For LRMO cells, the interfacial impedance rises sharply at high potentials, reaching several kΩ, which is indicative of the formation of an oxygen-rich interphase. In contrast, NCM811 cells maintain relatively low impedance until the onset of high delithiation state. These results suggest that the initial activation of lattice-oxygen redox in LRMO generates substantial oxygen-containing by-products, leading to a pronounced increase in interfacial resistance that kinetically suppresses further oxygen redox reactions. During discharge, the interfacial impedance progressively decreases. Below approximately 2.6 V, the impedance response becomes dominated by Warburg behavior, followed by a continued decline down to 2.0 V. The reduction of these oxygen-derived by-products during discharge plays a key role in facilitating interfacial reconstruction—a mechanism analyzed in more detail in a later section.

Improving interfacial kinetics through reduction-induced interphases reconstruction

Moreover, extended cycling at 200 mA g−1 revealed a severe decline in capacity in LPSCl-based ASSLBs as the discharge cutoff potential was raised—from 208 mAh g-1 at 2.0 V to just 30 mAh g−1 at 2.6 V—accompanied by significant polarization growth (Supplementary Fig. 11). These observations strongly suggest that the discharge process between 2.6 V and 2.0 V is essential for interphases reconstruction and plays a pivotal role in improving interfacial Li+ transport kinetics.

To quantitatively assess the kinetic evolution of LRMO-based ASSLBs, galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements were performed across different cycles and voltage windows. Figure 3e-g shows the GITT curves and associated overpotentials for the first, second and sixth cycles within 2.0–4.8 V and 2.6–4.8 V. During the first charge (Fig. 3e), a sloped plateau with high overpotential was observed up to 3.85 V, corresponding to parasitic electrolyte reactions43. Between 3.85 V and 4.4 V, the overpotential decreased and remained relatively low, indicating efficient redox kinetics of Ni2+ and Co3+44. However, beyond 4.4 V, the involvement of lattice oxygen redox leads to a pronounced increase in overpotential, reflecting sluggish oxygen redox kinetics23. In the discharge process, the persistence of by-products at the interphase resulted in a high overpotential, peaking near 2.6 V. Below 2.6 V, the overpotential declined progressively, indicating improved interfacial kinetics due to interphase reconstruction. In subsequent cycles (Figs. 3f, g), the overpotential decreased significantly compared to the first cycle during discharge in the 2.0–4.8 V range, suggesting progressive activation of the interphase. In contrast, cells cycled between 2.6 and 4.8 V exhibited consistently high overpotential across following cycles, underscoring the necessity of including the lower potential region (2.0–2.6 V) for optimal performance.

The evolution of interfacial impedance was further investigated by EIS, with Nyquist plots shown in Supplementary Fig. 8 and Fig. 12–14. Distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis (Fig. 3h-j) was used to deconvolution of interfacial processes. In symmetrical LiIn|LPSCl|LiIn cells (Supplementary Fig. 15), the DRT response in the frequency range of 10−4 to 10−1 s corresponds to the negative electrode|solid‑electrolyte interphase resistance. Although this frequency range overlaps with that of the positive electrode|SE interphase, the negative electrode contribution is negligible due to its low absolute resistance. Consequently, the total interfacial resistance is dominated by the positive electrode|SE interphase. During the initial cycling (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 8), a distinct DRT peak appears at τ = 10−2–10−1 s, corresponding to the LRMO/SEs interfacial impedance15. The corresponding fitted values of this interfacial impedance are also presented in Fig. 3k. Notably, the interfacial impedance remains relatively stable during initial charging up to ~50% state of charge (SOC), but increases significantly at higher potentials, peaking at 4.8 V. The impedance remains elevated throughout discharge until the potential reaches 2.6 V, and then RLRMO/SEs began to decrease. DRT results from the second and fourth cycles (Fig. 3h, j) show that cells cycled with a discharge cutoff potential of 2.0 V exhibit substantially lower interfacial impedance and better capacity retention than those cycled with cutoff potential of 2.6 V. Furthermore, impedance reduction from the second to fourth cycle was evident only in the cells cycled with a cutoff potential of 2.0 V, highlighting a more effective activation process. In contrast, negligible impedance change was observed in the cycled with a cutoff potential of 2.6 V, indicating limited interphases reconstruction. Similar behavior was also observed for NCM811 ASSLBs (Supplementary Fig. 14). In summary, these results demonstrate that cycling with cutoff potential of 2.0 V facilitates interfacial kinetics optimization and lowers impedance by reconstructing the positive electrode/electrolyte interphase. The reduction of interfacial by-products within 2.0–2.6 V plays a critical role in optimizing Li⁺ transport and enhancing the electrochemical performance of LRMO-based ASSLBs.

To gain deeper insight into the role of interfacial by-product reduction on electrochemical performance, XPS was employed to investigate the chemical evolution of the electrode/electrolyte interphase with cutoff potentials of 2.6 V and 2.0 V. The S 2p and P 2p XPS spectra are presented in Fig. 4a, b, while the distribution of additional elements (Mn, Cl, O, C, Li) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. As evidenced by the first-cycle XPS spectra (Fig. 2a), a considerable quantity of interfacial by-products remains at 2.6 V after discharge. These by-products, originating from the initial charging, persist into subsequent cycles with a cutoff potential of 2.6 V, resulting in sustained interfacial degradation and accounting for the elevated polarization and increased impedance observed during prolonged cycling (Fig. 4a). In contrast, cells cycled with a cutoff potential of 2.0 V exhibit a marked reduction in by-products content during the 2nd and 7th charge cycles relative to the first cycle. This reduction can be attributed to the interfacial transformation during discharging to 2.0 V, which facilitates interfacial passivation, effectively optimizing interfacial Li+ transport kinetics. Notably, sulfate by-products—characterized by XPS peaks at 166.81 eV (SO32-, 2p3/2) and 169.33 eV (SO42-, 2p3/2)—are clearly observed in the charge state after 7 cycles with a cutoff potential of 2.0 V, but are entirely absent in the 2.6-4.8 V counterpart. This discrepancy is attributed to the fact that the discharge cutoff potential governs the kinetics of interfacial reactions, thereby controlling the extent of lattice oxygen redox. Figure 4c-e shows the RIXS results from the third charge to 4.8 V for cells cycled in different voltage windows. At a discharge cutoff of 2.6 V, the oxidation-induced by-products remain largely unaltered, resulting in persistent interfacial resistance that suppresses subsequent lattice oxygen participation. In contrast, at 2.0 V, deep lithiation triggers pronounced interphase reconstruction and passivation. This self-limiting transformation enhances ionic transport across the interphase and progressively improves the reaction kinetics in later cycles. The activated oxygen species generated during this process migrate toward the positive electrode-electrolyte interface, where they act as potent oxidants, attacking the sulfide electrolyte and promoting sulfate formation. These results confirm that the initial interfacial transformation, triggered by discharging to 2.0 V, yields a chemically stable interphase that inhibits further parasitic reactions, while also permitting enhanced electrochemical activity in subsequent cycles. The formation of these protective interfacial species enables greater lattice oxygen utilization and improved performance. Mechanistic insights into kinetic enhancement via interphase reduction and reconstruction

Complementary ToF-SIMS analysis (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 17) reveals significant accumulation of Sx- species upon discharge to 2.6 V, with these species diffusely distributed across the positive electrode surface. This suggests the presence of intermediate sulfur species generated by partial reduction of higher-valent sulfur compounds. Due to their poor ionic conductivity, these intermediates hinder efficient ion transport. However, further discharge to 2.0 V promotes continued reduction, forming conductive LiS− and LiS2− species, as confirmed by intensified ToF-SIMS signals. Corresponding LiSx species were also identified via Raman spectroscopy at this lower cutoff potential (Supplementary Fig. 18)45. Focused ion beam-scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) combined with ToF-SIMS was employed to investigate lithium distribution at the PEAM/LPSCl interphase. As shown in Fig. 4f, initial charging induces the formation of a porous interfacial by-product layer surrounding LPSCl agglomerates. The corresponding Li− distribution map (Fig. 5b) reveals a pronounced decrease in Li signal intensity at these regions, indicative of the formation of a Li-depleted interphase. At this stage, the PEAM is in a highly delithiated state, as evidenced by its low Li- signal intensity (represented by deep blue coloration). Upon subsequent discharge, Li+ re-intercalation into the PEAM leads to a progressive increase in bulk lithium content. Simultaneously, Li signal intensity at the interphase recovers, and the Li-depleted interphase disappears, restoring Li+ transport pathways across the interphase. This observed interfacial regeneration behavior is consistent with the previously described electrochemical mechanisms, wherein discharge-induced reduction of interfacial by-products facilitates reconstruction of a conductive and passivated interphase, enabling efficient lithium-ion transport in subsequent cycles.

Based on comprehensive XPS and ToF-SIMS analyses, three major categories of interfacial by-products were identified in the charged-state LRMO/SE system: (1) phosphorus sulfides (e.g., P2S5, P2S7, P4S9, P4S7…), (2) oxygen-containing sulfur–phosphorus species (e.g., P2S4O, P2S4O, P2S3O2, P2O5…), and (3) elemental sulfur species (Sx). To gain deeper insight into the electrochemical behavior and reducibility of these species, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to evaluate their lithiation-induced reduction potentials. The calculation results reveal that the majority of these by-products, along with their corresponding lithiation intermediates, possess reduction potentials below 2.6 V vs. Li+/Li. This is in strong agreement with the experimental findings, where significant reductions in overpotential and interfacial resistance were observed upon discharging to 2.0 V (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, analysis of the reduction pathways indicates the formation of several lithium-containing products, including Li3PS4, LiPO3, Li4P2O7, P2S5, and LiS4, as the final reaction products. A detailed summary of the calculated reduction reactions, corresponding products, and thermodynamic data is provided in Supplementary Table S1. To clarify the influence of interfacial redox reactions on Li-ion transport, we performed BVSE analyses for representative oxidized species and their corresponding reduced phases (Supplementary Fig. 19). The CIF files required for the calculations are provided in Supplementary Data 1. For sulfide-based species such as P2S7 and P2S5, reduction produces the fast-ion conductor Li3PS4, accompanied by a significant decrease in the Li-ion migration barrier—from 0.348 eV (P2S7) and 0.298 eV (P2S5) to 0.285 eV in Li3PS4. Notably, while P2S7 and P2S5 lack continuous three-dimensional diffusion pathways, Li3PS4 exhibits a fully percolated 3D Li-ion transport network. A similar behavior is observed for oxygen-containing intermediates: the reduction of P2O5 to Li2P2O7 lowers the migration barrier from 0.38 eV to 0.353 eV and enhances the continuity of Li-ion conduction channels. Collectively, these BVSE results demonstrate that the reduction of oxidized interfacial products yields Li-containing phases with intrinsically lower migration barriers and more interconnected diffusion networks—thereby facilitating faster Li-ion transport across the reconstructed interphase at low potentials. The integration of advanced spectroscopic characterizations with first-principles calculations provides a robust mechanistic understanding of the interfacial decomposition and transformation processes. These findings investigate the origin of interfacial impedance evolution in LRMO-based ASSLBs and highlight the critical role of lithiation within 2.6–2.0 V in regenerating a more conductive and passivating interphase.

Interphase pre-activation strategy coupled with oxygen-modified LPSCl

Building upon the experimental insights, it is evident that mitigating the activation process in LRMO-based ASSLBs requires either the suppression of interfacial side reactions or the promotion of interphases reconstruction. Notably, a distinct sloping plateau around 3.9 V was observed during the initial charging of LRMO ASSLBs (Supplementary Fig. 20), corresponding to the oxidative decomposition of the SEs, as reported in previous studies43. To evaluate whether low-potential interfacial redox reactions could facilitate beneficial interphases reconstruction, a targeted pre-cycling protocol was implemented with an upper cutoff potential of 3.9 V—below the onset of significant LRMO redox activity—thereby minimizing activation time. The cells were pre-cycled for 7 cycles between 2.0 and 3.9 V, followed by full-range cycling (2.0–4.8 V). As shown in Fig. 6a, the first charging cycle exhibited a clear sloped plateau attributed to SE decomposition, which largely diminished by the 7th cycle, indicating a self-limiting decomposition behavior. Remarkably, following this pre-cycling, the cell exhibited a charging capacity of 350.5 mAh g−1 in the subsequent full-range cycle, accompanied by effective utilization of lattice oxygen redox (Fig. 6b, d). The discharge capacity reached 291.6 mAh g−1, with no further activation behavior observed, underscoring the crucial role of interfacial reconstruction in enabling lattice oxygen utilization. Thus, pre-cycling enables rapid interfacial reconstruction at low potentials prior to significant electrochemical activity, drastically shortening the activation period for LRMO positive electrodes. Without pre-cycling, full activation typically requires approximately ten low-current cycles (~16 days). In contrast, pre-cycling achieves activation within ~43 h, substantially reducing experimental time while offering a mild and controllable in situ pretreatment. To investigate the chemical effects of lattice oxygen release on interfacial evolution, potassium permanganate (KMnO4) was thermally decomposed to generate oxygen for oxidative treatment of the LPSCl electrolyte. The oxygen release rate was finely regulated by adjusting the initial mass of KMnO4. Two oxygen concentrations (approximately 0.01 mol L−1 and 0.02 mol L−1 per 0.2 g of LPSCl) were applied to investigate dosage effects. X‑ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed that this treatment did not significantly alter the crystal structure of the electrolyte, although a modest decrease in ionic conductivity was observed (Supplementary Fig. 22a). Compared to that treated with 0.02 mol L-1, the electrolyte treated with 0.01 mol L−1 exhibited higher ionic conductivity and reversible capacity (Supplementary Fig. 22b, c). Therefore, a treatment concentration of 0.01 mol/L was selected for the subsequent experiments. XPS depth profiling revealed the formation of a sulfate-enriched surface layer on the oxygen-treated LPSCl (OT-LPSCl), with intensity diminishing with depth (Supplementary Fig. 23).

The OT-LPSCl coupled with LRMO exhibits markedly enhanced electrochemical performance, achieving a post-activation capacity of 343 mAh g−1 at 10 mA g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 24) and 230 mAh g−1 at 200 mA g−1, with 84.1% capacity retention after 500 cycles (Fig. 6f). Although an initial activation stage is still observed, the overall enhanced performance is clearly attributed to the improved oxidative stability of the oxygen-treated electrolytes. Furthermore, the pre-cycled ASSLB employing OT-LPSCl demonstrates favorable discharge capacity and long-term cycling stability, delivering an initial discharge capacity of 315 mAh g−1 (Fig. 6c, e) and maintaining 226 mAh g−1 at 200 mA g−1, with 83.2% capacity retention after 500 cycles (Fig. 6f). Additionally, due to the more stable electrode/electrolyte interface formed through oxygen treatment of LPSCl, voltage fading in this system is significantly alleviated compared to pre-cycled LPSCl without O2 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 25). These findings confirm the synergistic effect of oxygen-mediated interfacial engineering and pre-cycling treatment, which together enable efficient capacity utilization and sustained electrochemical stability in lithium-rich ASSLBs.

XPS was employed to monitor the evolution of interfacial chemistry during pre-cycling and oxygen modification treatment. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26, the S 2p and P 2p spectra reveal significant changes in interfacial composition during the pre-cycling process. After the initial charge to 3.9 V (denoted as Pre-cycle-C1), the decomposition by-products exhibited similar chemical signatures to those observed at 4.8 V (Fig. 6g), although with substantially lower intensities. Subsequent discharge to 2.0 V further reduced the presence of these by-products, indicating an oxidation-reduction process within the 2.0–3.9 V range. By the 7th charge cycle, the by-product levels further decrease relative to the first cycle, indicating progressive interfacial reconstruction and passivation. Notably, when the voltage window is subsequently expanded to 2.0–4.8 V, by-product formation remains significantly lower than in cells that were not pre-cycled prior to high-voltage operation (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 27). Additionally, the OT-LPSCl electrolyte after 2.0–3.9 V pre-cycling exhibited substantially reduced side reaction intensity upon first charging to 4.8 V, relative to pristine LPSCl—further confirming the effectiveness of oxygen-mediated surface modification in enhancing oxidative stability. These observations collectively suggest that the electrolyte-derived interphase formed during 2.0–3.9 V pre-cycling effectively passivates the LRMO/LPSCl interface, while oxygen treatment improves the intrinsic oxidation resistance of the SE. The combination of both strategies promotes reconstruction and accelerates electrochemical reaction kinetics. After 300 long‑term cycles, the LRMO/LPSCl system exhibited minimal interfacial and bulk structural degradation (Supplementary Fig. 28 and Fig. 29). Compared to direct pre-cycling alone, the OT‑LPSCl combined with pre-cycling resulted in significantly fewer interfacial by-products. This outcome confirms that the modified interfacial layer formed through the integrated oxygen-treatment and pre-cycling process functions as an effective and durable protective barrier during extended cycling.

To further confirm the impact of pre-cycling with OT-LPSCl on reaction kinetics, DEIS was conducted after pre-cycling between 2.0 and 3.9 V, followed by full-range cycling (2.0–4.8 V) (Supplementary Fig. 30). For comparison, impedance data from cells without pre-cycling were also analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 8). In the absence of pre-treatment, direct contact between LRMO and LPSCl under high voltage induced pronounced interfacial side reactions, resulting in large impedance values exceeding several kiloohms, which impeded subsequent electrochemical reactions. By contrast, cells subjected to pre-cycling between 2.0 and 3.9 V and utilizing OT-LPSCl as electrolyte exhibited significantly lower impedance throughout the charge-discharge process. Notably, the impedance remained stable even at high states of charge (SOC), without exhibiting the sharp increase typically observed in untreated cells. These results confirm that pre-cycling between 2.0 and 3.9 V prompts the formation of a kinetically favorable intermediate phase, while oxygen modification of LPSCl mitigates its susceptibility to oxidative degradation. This synergistic strategy effectively suppresses interfacial impedance growth at high voltages and enables effective utilization of lattice oxygen redox during the first cycle.

Discussion

In this study, we investigate the dynamic interplay between lattice-oxygen redox and interfacial stability in sulfide-based lithium-rich ASSLBs through an integrated approach combining electrochemical analysis, advanced surface characterization (XPS, ToF-SIMS, RIXS), and DFT calculations. Our findings reveal that the activation process of LRMO-based ASSLBs is predominantly governed by interfacial reactions occurring at the LRMO/LPSCl interphase. Comparative analyses with conventional layered NCM811 positive electrodes demonstrate that lattice oxygen redox in LRMO induces extensive electrolyte decomposition, leading to the formation of oxygen-containing by-products such as sulfate, phosphate and PSxOγ. These by-products form a porous and loosely adhered interphase, resulting in elevated interfacial resistance and suppressed oxygen redox activity. During discharge, these species undergo partial reduction, facilitating the reconstruction of a more compact and passivating interphase. This reconstructed layer effectively mitigates further parasitic reactions, decreases charge-transfer impedance, and enables progressive improvement in the reaction kinetics in subsequent cycles—thereby achieving high reversible capacity. Guided by this, we devised a pre-cycling protocol between 2.0 and 3.9 V, when combined with oxygen-modified LPSCl (OT-LPSCl), significantly accelerates interphases reconstruction and effectively mitigates the activation challenge commonly observed in LRMO-based ASSLBs. Post-treatment cells deliver a discharge capacity of 318 mAh g−1 at 10 mA g−1 and retain 226 mAh g−1 at 200 mA g−1 with 83.2% capacity retention over 500 cycles, highlighting the critical role of oxygen-mediated interfacial engineering in simultaneously achieving high-capacity utilization and long-term durability.

Moreover, lithium-rich positive electrodes containing different proportions of Li2MnO3 phases also exhibit interfacial passivation when discharged to 2.0 V (Supplementary Fig. 31). The pre-cycling window can therefore be rationally designed—based on each material’s potential profile and dQ/dV characteristics—to remain just below the threshold of extensive transition-metal oxidation, thereby enabling controlled and efficient interphase formation.

This work offers mechanistic insight into the coupled evolution of the positive electrode/electrolyte interphase and anionic redox reactions LRMO-based ASSLBs and introduces a robust, scalable strategy to modify interphase properties. The designed low‑potential interfacial reconstruction acts as a mild electrochemical preconditioning step: while introducing only minimal additional processing, it markedly shortens the activation period of the LRMO positive electrode and promotes the formation of a stable, self‑passivating interphase. This approach offers multiple advantages, including operational simplicity, controllable cost, and cycling stability. Together, these findings provide a potential framework for the rational design of next-generation LRMO-based high-energy ASSLBs with extended cycle life that effectively utilizing lattice oxygen redox chemistry.

Methods

Material synthesis

Single crystal Li1.2Ni0.13Mn0.54Co0.13O2 (LRMO) positive electrodes were prepared by the molten salt method. Stoichiometric amounts of Li-rich precursor (Jiangsu Zhichuan Technology Co.) and Li2CO3 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) were mixed with a 10 wt% excess, and supplemented with LiCl molten salt (3 times the total mass). The mixture was heated in air at 920 °C for 10 h with a heating rate of 4 °C min−1. After the high-temperature reaction, the product was washed with deionized water to remove excess salt. NCM811 was purchased from Ningbo Technology Lithium Battery Material Co., Ltd. The LPSCl solid electrolyte was prepared by ball-mixing Li2S (Alfa Aesar, 99.9%), P2S5 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), and LiCl (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) for 24 h at 300 rpm, followed by calcination at 550 °C for 6 h under Ar. The ball-milling procedure was performed using a ball mill from Miqi (China), equipped with a 100 mL ZrO2 jar and 5 mm diameter ZrO2 balls, at a ball-to-powder ratio of 56:1. The ionic conductivity of LPSCl is approximately 2.4 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 25 °C. The oxygen-treated LPSCl (OT-LPSCl) was obtained by decomposing KMnO4 at 220 °C for 5 h. The LPSCl and KMnO4 were sealed in a fritted quartz tube under argon and calcined, yielding a product with an ionic conductivity of 1.6 × 10−3 S cm−1.

Cell assembly and electrochemical characterization

The Li3In7||LRMO/NCM811-based ASSLBs were assembled using a laboratory-made mold-type cell consisting of a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cylindrical die with an inner diameter of 10 mm and stainless-steel current collectors43. The cell configuration comprised a composite positive electrode, a sulfide solid electrolyte separator layer, and a Li3In7 alloy negative electrode. All cell assembly procedures were carried out in an Ar-filled glove box.

The composite positive electrode was prepared by manually mixing LRMO/NCM811 active material, LPSCl solid electrolyte, and acetylene black (AB) conductive additive in an agate mortar for 60 min with a weight ratio of 50:45:5. The resulting LRMO/NCM811-LPSCl-AB composite powder was used as the cathode material.

For cell fabrication, 100 mg of LPSCl powder was loaded into the PEEK cylinder (10 mm diameter) and cold-pressed at 360 MPa at room temperature to form a dense solid electrolyte separator pellet with a thickness of approximately 700 µm. Subsequently, 6.2 mg of the LRMO/NCM811-LPSCl-AB composite powder was uniformly spread onto one side of the LPSCl pellet and pressed at 360 MPa to form the composite positive electrode layer. The areal loading of the active material was approximately 4 mg cm−2, corresponding to a cathode thickness of about 50-60 µm.

The negative electrode consisted of a Li-In alloy foil prepared from lithium and indium foils with thicknesses of 200 and 100 µm, respectively. The indium foil was fabricated from high-purity indium particles (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%), while the lithium foil was prepared from lithium sheets (China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd., 99.9%). To obtain the Li-In alloy (Li:In molar ratio = 3:7), the lithium foil was wrapped with indium foil and cold-pressed at 60 MPa. The resulting Li3In7 alloy foil was then attached to the opposite side of the LPSCl separator pellet and pressed at 120 MPa to complete the cell assembly.

After assembly, the ASSLBs were allowed to rest for 8 h to ensure complete alloying and interfacial stabilization of the Li3In7 electrode before electrochemical testing.

Galvanostatic charge-discharge and GITT tests for the ASSLBs were conducted on a Land Battery Test System (LAND CT-2001A, Wuhan, China) at different specific currents at 60 °C. The voltage window was set to 1.4–4.2 V against Li-In alloy (about 2.0–4.8 V vs. Li+/Li). The correction is calculated by adding 0.6 V to the measured potential versus Li-In (E vs. Li+/Li = E vs. Li-In + 0.6 V), based on the equilibrium potential of the Li-In alloy versus Li+/Li. The external pressure of the ASSLBs during testing were ≈60 MPa. The ASSLBs for long cycling tests are initially activated at a specific current of 10 mA g−1 for 7 cycles, followed by extended cycling at a specific current of 200 mA g−1. All batteries used for characterization testing follow the same procedure. Measurements in the main electrochemical experiment were performed on three cells. The data presented in Fig. 6c, g are representative results from a single cell that exhibited typical performance. The corresponding measurements from the other two cells are provided in Supplementary Fig. 32. For GITT analysis, the cells were tested at a specific current of 20 mA g−1, with a current flow of 0.5 h, followed by 8 h rest to reach the quasi-equilibrium potential. Steady-state EIS measurements employed an Autolab PGSTA302 electrochemical workstation (Eco Chemie, the Netherlands), with a frequency range of 10 mHz to 1 MHz and signal amplitude of 50 mV. The number of data points was 10 points per decade. Before each EIS measurement, the cell was allowed to reach a quasi‑stationary state at 4.5 V for 8 h. DEIS measurements used a Biologic electrochemical workstation. During the cells cycling (2.0–4.8 V/2.6–4.8 V) at a specific current of 10 mA g−1, A 10 mV alternating current (AC) perturbation was superimposed on direct current (DC) currents to acquire consecutive EIS spectra. The frequency range was 100 kHz–20 mHz. For the DRT analysis, EIS data were transformed from the frequency domain to the time domain using the infinite Voigt circuit model. All calculations were performed with DRTtools in MATLAB, employing a code contributed by Prof. Francesco Giucci46.

Characterization

XPS measurements were performed using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer with monochromatic Al-Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) at 12 kV and 6 mA. XPS spectra were calibrated using the signal of the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV and Cl 2p at 198.8 eV47,48. For cross-sectional SEM (JEOL JSM-7900F, Japan) and EDX analysis, specimens were prepared using a JEOL IB-19520CCP cross-sectional polisher. The EDX analysis was performed at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The crystal structure of the active material after prolonged cycling was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai F30, Philips-FEI, the Netherlands). ToF-SIMS analysis was performed on an IONTOF M6 instrument using a Bi3+ primary ion beam (30 keV). The analysis areas were 100 × 100 µm and 20 × 20 µm for different measurements. Data normalization was carried out using total ion intensity. FIB-TOF-SIMS analysis was performed using a Zeiss GeminiSEM 500 scanning electron microscope equipped with FIB and TOF-SIMS accessories. Raman spectroscopy was carried out using an XploRA system (Jobin Yvon, France) with a laser wavelength of 532 nm. The RIXS experiments were carried out at the BL20U2 beamline of the SSRF. To obtain the RIXS maps, emission intensities were measured while scanning the excitation energy from 527 to 532 eV at 0.2 eV intervals49,50. Custom-built software from the beamline team was used for all data acquisition and processing51. All samples were handled within an Ar-filled glovebox and transferred to the XPS, cross-sectional SEM, TOF-SIMS, Raman and RIXS instruments without air exposure. XRD patterns were acquired using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer (Cu Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 10–70° with a 0.02° step size and a scan rate of 10° min−1. A layer of Mylar film was applied to the material surface to prevent air exposure.
