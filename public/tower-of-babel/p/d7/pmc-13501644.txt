Prime editing of Nectin-1 functional domains confers knockout-level resistance to pseudorabies virus
Authors: Tangyuan Xie, Hanxi Ouyang, Yan Wang, Shuhui Zhang, Shuaikang Yang, Jing Kong, Tian Huang, Gengsheng Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Pseudorabies virus (PRV), a prominent member of the Alpha-herpesvirinae subfamily, continues to impose substantial economic losses on the global swine industry. Infection leads to fatal neurological disorders in neonatal piglets, respiratory distress in growing pigs, and reproductive failure in sows [1]. In recent years, the threat posed by PRV has intensified. Since 2011, highly pathogenic PRV variants have emerged and acquired the ability to breach the immune protection conferred by the classical Bartha-K61 vaccine, resulting in recurrent outbreaks [2]. Moreover, an increasing number of human infection cases-manifesting as encephalitis and endophthalmitis-has highlighted the zoonotic potential of PRV and raised new public health concerns [3, 4]. Under the dual pressure of declining vaccine efficacy and expanding cross-species transmission risk, innovative antiviral strategies are urgently needed.

The PRV virion is composed of a double-stranded DNA genome enclosed within an icosahedral capsid, surrounded by the tegument layer and a lipid envelope [5–7]. Viral entry is a multistep cascade requiring precise cooperation among envelope glycoproteins and host-cell receptors. Initial low-affinity attachment is mediated by glycoprotein C (gC) binding to cell-surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), which concentrates virions at the plasma membrane [8, 9]. Subsequent engagement of glycoprotein D (gD) with high-affinity host receptors functions as the decisive trigger for downstream structural signaling. Signals are propagated through the gH/gL complex and culminate in the activation of glycoprotein B (gB), which executes membrane fusion [10–12]. Disrupting the gD-receptor interaction is therefore considered one of the most direct and effective strategies for blocking viral entry.

PRV utilizes a diverse array of host receptors, forming a complex receptor network that underlies its broad host range. These receptors include Nectin-1, a universal entry receptor for multiple alpha-herpesviruses, as well as herpes virus entry mediator (HVEM), 3-O-sulfated heparan sulfate (3-O-S-HS), and thrombospondin-3 (THBS3) [13–18]. This suggests that PRV adopts a “multi-point landing” strategy to enhance the robustness of viral entry. Despite this diversity, Nectin-1 is widely regarded as the dominant entry receptor due to its essential role across alpha-herpesviruses [16, 19–21]. Nectin-1, a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily involved in cell-cell adhesion, uses its extracellular IgV domain to interact with viral gD [22, 23]. Structural and biochemical studies have identified several critical residues-N77, I80, M85, and F129-that mediate gD engagement [24]. In vivo evidence further indicates that mutation of even a single residue, F129, can confer significant resistance to lethal PRV infection in mice [25]. However, the antiviral relevance of these residues has not been systematically evaluated in pigs, the primary natural host of PRV.

The attenuated efficacy of traditional vaccines such as Bartha-K61, driven by ongoing viral evolution, presents a major challenge to current PRV control strategies [26]. Host genetic modification has therefore emerged as a promising alternative approach. While complete knockout of Nectin-1 confers resistance to PRV [27], its essential physiological roles in cell adhesion and signaling pose substantial risks. A more desirable strategy would selectively abolish its function as a viral receptor while preserving its normal biological activity. Achieving such precise functional decoupling, however, is hindered by the low efficiency of conventional CRISPR/Cas9-mediated HDR in livestock species.

Here, we report the first application of prime editing (PE) technology to introduce precise modifications into the porcine Nectin-1 gene. Targeting key residues within the gD-Nectin-1 binding interface (N77, I80, M85, and F129), we generated a panel of porcine cell lines harboring single or combinatorial point mutations. Through systematic virological assays, we quantitatively evaluated the impact of these precise edits on multiple stages of the PRV life cycle, including attachment, internalization, replication, and release. In addition to demonstrating the functional importance of these key residues in PRV infection, our study provides a proof-of-concept framework for selective receptor engineering using prime editing. These findings establish a technical foundation for developing genetically engineered pigs with enhanced resistance to pseudorabies.

Methods

Plasmid construction

The plasmids pCMV-PE5max, pU6-epegRNA, pU6-nicking sgRNA, and SpCas9-EGFP (pX458) were preserved in our laboratory. The epegRNA and corresponding nicking sgRNA sequences were designed using PRIDICT2.0 (https://www.pridict.it/). Synthetic oligonucleotides for pegRNAs were annealed and subsequently ligated into the BsaI-digested pU6-epegRNA backbone vector using T4 DNA ligase. Nicking sgRNAs were constructed in a similar manner by ligating annealed oligonucleotides into the BsaI-digested pU6-nicking sgRNA vector. To facilitate cell sorting, an EGFP expression cassette was inserted into the AgeI restriction site of the pCMV-PE5max plasmid, generating PE5max-EGFP. For standard CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout, sgRNA sequences were designed using CHOPCHOP (http://chopchop.cpu.uib.no/), and the corresponding annealed oligonucleotides were ligated into the BbsI-digested pX458 vector. All oligonucleotides and single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology and purified by PAGE or HPLC, respectively. All primer sequences used for plasmid construction are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–2.

Cell culture and transfection

The PK-15 cells (ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell). Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For plasmid transfection, PK-15 cells were seeded on 6-well plates and electroporated at approximately 70% confluency using Nucleofector 2b device (Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated HDR editing, 5 µg of the endotoxin-free pX458-sgRNA plasmid was co-transfected with ssDNA at varying molar ratios. For prime editing, cells were co-transfected with pCMV-PE5max-EGFP (4 µg), the pegRNA expression plasmid (1.25 µg), and the nicking sgRNA expression plasmid (700 ng). For generating knockout cell lines, 5 µg of the pX458-sgRNA plasmid was used. At 24–36 h post-transfection, EGFP-positive cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and seeded into 96-well plates at a clonal density (approximately one cell per well). Monoclonal colonies were expanded for genotyping after approximately 7–10 days.

Genomic DNA extraction and genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from sorted EGFP-positive cells using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Target genomic regions were amplified using 2×Vazyme LAmp Master Mix (Vazyme). For high-throughput sequencing, a two-step nested PCR approach was employed. The first round amplified the target region using site-specific primers, and the second round incorporated unique barcodes for library multiplexing. The final PCR amplicons were sequenced on an Illumina PE150 platform. Editing efficiencies were quantified from the deep sequencing data using the PEAnalyzer tool. The genotypes of individual clones were first screened by allele-specific multiplex PCR (AMS-PCR) and subsequently confirmed by Sanger sequencing. All primer sequences used for AMS-PCR and deep sequencing are listed in the Supplementary Table e3.

Virus stocks and titration

The PRV strain AH2 was kindly provided by Henan Agricultural University. Virus stocks were propagated and amplified in PK-15 cells. Virus titers were determined by the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID₅₀) assay. Briefly, serial 10-fold dilutions of virus samples were added to 96-well plates seeded with PK-15 cells (eight replicates per dilution). After 3–7 days of incubation, the cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed, and the TCID₅₀ was calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

Virus binding and internalization assays

For the virus binding assay, PK-15 cells grown on glass-bottom dishes were incubated with PRV (MOI = 1) for 1 h at 4 °C. After extensive washing with ice-cold PBS to remove unbound virions, the attached virus particles were visualized by immunofluorescence. For the virus uptake assay, cells were incubated with PRV (MOI = 1) for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were treated with trypsin-EDTA for 2 min at 37 °C to remove noninternalized particles, followed by extensive washing with ice-cold PBS. The internalized virus particles were then visualized by IFA. Parallel sets of cells were prepared under identical conditions for genomic DNA extraction and subsequent qPCR analysis.

Immunofluorescence Analysis (IFA)

Cells grown on glass-bottom dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After washing, cells were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with primary antibody against PRV gD (1:500, PA1-081, Invitrogen) and Nectin-1(1:50, 24713-1-AP, Proteintech). Following washes, cells were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, RGAR004, Proteintech) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime). Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Viral DNA was extracted from infected cells or culture supernatants using the TIANamp Virus DNA/RNA Kit (Tiangen). To quantify PRV genomic copy numbers, a standard curve was generated using 10-fold serial dilutions of a pLB plasmid containing a 455 bp fragment of the PRV US6 gene. qPCR reactions were performed with RealUniversal SYBR Green Premix (TIANGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in the Supplementary Table e3.

Western blot analysis

Approximately 2 × 106 Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) supplemented with 1 mM protease inhibitor (HY-K0010; MedChemExpress). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and probed with a primary antibody against Nectin-1 (1:1000, sc-21722, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), with β-actin (1:5000, 66009-1, Proteintech) used as an internal loading control. The primary antibodies were detected by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (SA00001-1, SA00001-2, Proteintech), and the secondary antibodies were visualized by ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagents (TIANGEN).

Off-target analysis

For each sgRNA, the top three potential off-target sites were predicted. These loci were amplified by a two-step nested PCR approach from genomic DNA of edited cells for high-throughput sequencing, as described above. All primers used for off-target analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± S.D. from at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0 software. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by two-way ANOVA where appropriate. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significance levels are denoted as * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001; ns, not significant.

Results

Prime editing enables efficient and precise modification of Nectin-1

To selectively disrupt the critical interface between Nectin-1 and PRV gD, we designed three editing strategies targeting its IgV domain: (1) a single-site substitution at F129 (F129A); (2) a triple mutation affecting spatially adjacent residues N77, I80, and M85 (X3); and (3) a combined four-residue mutation (X4) (Fig. 1A). As an initial approach, we attempted to generate these variants using a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated HDR strategy. However, despite optimization of homology-arm length and ssDNA donor dosage, precise editing frequencies in PK-15 cells remained exceedingly low (Fig. 1B and C), precluding recovery of homozygous clones. These observations underscore the intrinsic inefficiency of HDR for complex, multi-site engineering in porcine cells.

A Schematic overview of the three prime editing strategies targeting key residues within the Nectin-1 IgV domain. B Comparison of HDR-mediated editing efficiencies using different molar ratios of ssDNA donor to Cas9 plasmid. C HDR efficiencies obtained with ssDNA donors containing homology arms of varying lengths. D Workflow of the prime editing (PE) approach for introducing precise mutations into Nectin-1. E Representative Sanger sequencing chromatograms confirming the precise installation of mutations by PE. The protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) is indicated in green, target codon is underlined, and the intended nucleotide changes are boxed in red. F Summary of single-cell colony screening results. G Representative Sanger sequencing chromatograms of single-cell clones carrying the four-site mutations in Nectin-1

We therefore adopted an advanced prime-editing platform (ePE5max). Given the strong dependence of PE efficiency on the reverse-transcription template (RTT) length within pegRNAs [28]. We constructed epegRNA panels with variable RTT lengths for each locus and co-transfected them with PE5max-EGFP and the appropriate nicking sgRNA (Fig. 1D). FACS enrichment followed by deep sequencing revealed that RTT length dictated editing efficiencies by orders of magnitude (Figure. S1), highlighting the necessity of systematic pegRNA optimization.

Using optimized epegRNAs, single-cell cloning with PE5max yielded homozygous F129A and X3 mutants with high efficiencies of 61.3% and 53.1%, respectively (Fig. 1E and F), far surpassing HDR. For the X4 construct-spanning > 130 bp-we employed a sequential “X3-based secondary editing” strategy and successfully obtained homozygous clones (Fig. 1F and G). We also evaluated the off-target effects of the two sgRNAs used in this study. Predicted potential off-target sites were selected for PCR amplification followed by high-throughput sequencing analysis. No significant off-target mutations were detected in the X4 homozygous mutant clones (Supplementary Table 4). Collectively, these results demonstrate that prime editing enables precise, multi-site remodeling of Nectin-1 in porcine cells with efficiencies unattainable by traditional HDR, thereby providing a robust set of engineered cell lines for downstream mechanistic analyses.

Key-site Nectin-1 mutations selectively abrogate PRV internalization while sparing initial attachment

To pinpoint the entry stage impacted by Nectin-1 modifications, we first assessed viral attachment. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that after 1 h at 4 °C, viral particles adhered comparably to WT and mutant cell surfaces (Fig. 2A and B), indicating that these mutations do not perturb the initial low-affinity binding mediated by HSPGs. Upon shifting the temperature to 37 °C to trigger internalization, all mutants (F129A, X3, X4) exhibited reduced intracellular viral particles relative to WT (Fig. 2C and D). Absolute quantification of intracellular viral genomes by qPCR corroborated these findings, with F129A and X4 cells showing a moderate but statistically significant decrease in genome copy numbers (Fig. 2E). These results demonstrate that mutations at the gD-binding interface specifically block the transition from attachment to internalization, thereby exerting inhibitory effects at the earliest stage of the viral infection cascade.

Precise Nectin-1 Mutations Specifically Reduce PRV Internalization. Control and Nectin-1 mutant cells were infected with PRV for 1 h at 4°C to assess viral attachment (A), or for 30 min at 37°C to assess viral internalization (C). PRV was detected using an anti-PRV antibody (red), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (blue). NC indicates the uninfected control. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ (B, D). Viral genome copy numbers at 37°C were determined by absolute quantitative PCR (E).

Nectin-1 interface mutations reduce PRV replication and progeny-virus release

Given that viral internalization is a prerequisite for subsequent infection cascades, we further examined the impact of the mutations on the entire viral replication cycle. At a low multiplicity of infection (MOI = 0.01), WT cells exhibited extensive infection foci 24 hpi. In contrast, all mutant cell lines developed fewer and smaller plaques, indicating impaired viral spread, with X4 showing the strongest effect among the mutants (Fig. 3A and B). Time-course qPCR revealed that intracellular viral genome levels in all mutants remained significantly lower than those in WT cells from 6 to 24 hpi (Fig. 3C). Correspondingly, progeny virus production in culture supernatants decreased from 12 hpi onward, with the largest differences observed at 36 hpi, reaching approximately 10-fold reductions for F129A and X3, and up to ~ 100-fold for X4 (Fig. 3D). TCID₅₀ assays showed a similar trend (Fig. 3E). Together, these results indicate that the X4 configuration provides the most pronounced reduction in PRV infection among the mutants tested, suggesting synergistic contributions among the four targeted residues.

B Quantification of the mean fluorescence intensity from (A). C Time-course analysis of intracellular PRV genomic replication in WT and mutant cell lines. D Time-course analysis of progeny virus release into the culture medium. Both (C) and (D) were measured by absolute quantitative real-time PCR. E Viral titers in the culture supernatant at 36 hpi, determined by TCID₅₀ assay

Four-site precision editing confers resistance to PRV infection comparable to Nectin-1 knockout

To evaluate the practical performance of this “precise modification” strategy relative to traditional gene knockout, we generated a Nectin-1 knockout (KO) cell line by CRISPR/Cas9 targeting of exon 2 (Fig. 4A). Comparative analyses of viral internalization, intracellular replication, and progeny virus release showed that the efficacy of the X4 mutant were comparable to those observed in the KO line under the tested conditions (Fig. 4B-4I). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups, indicating that targeted modification of four critical amino acids can efficiently reduce Nectin-1-mediated receptor activity. Importantly, unlike complete gene ablation, this “functional knockout” strategy may preserve the potential physiological roles of Nectin-1 while achieving a similar level of resistance to PRV infection, highlighting its potential as a more controlled and biologically conservative approach for antiviral genetic improvement.

Discussion

Pseudorabies virus (PRV) remains a major pathogen in swine, where its ongoing evolution and cross-species transmission potential continue to challenge conventional control strategies. These challenges underscore the need for host genetics-based antiviral strategies. In this study, we demonstrate that combinatorial mutations at four critical residues of Nectin-1 confer resistance to PRV infection approaching that observed with complete gene knockout. Importantly, our findings not only establish the functional importance of these residues in mediating PRV entry in porcine cells, but also highlight the advantages of prime editing (PE) for efficient and precise multi-site genome engineering in livestock.

Mechanistically, our data define the specific stage of viral entry affected by these mutations. Neither single-site nor multi-site substitutions altered viral attachment at 4 °C, indicating that initial binding-primarily mediated by gC-HSPG interactions remains intact. In contrast, upon temperature shift to 37 °C, all mutants exhibited impaired internalization, positioning the functional impact of these mutations at the transition from low-affinity attachment to high-affinity receptor engagement. This is consistent with structural predictions that these residues form the core of the gD-Nectin-1 interface [24], and is further supported by our live-virus infection assays.

By comparing different mutation combinations, we observed combinatorial effects in reducing PRV infection. While the F129A single mutation was associated with a measurable reduction in viral replication, the four-site mutant (X4) exhibited the strongest inhibitory phenotype. Although X4 did not completely abolish PRV replication, it consistently reduced intracellular viral genome accumulation, progeny virion production, and viral spread to levels comparable to those observed in Nectin-1 knockout cells. These findings suggest that multi-site precision editing can disrupt the gD–Nectin-1 interaction and attenuate the entry-associated amplification cascade, leading to a resistance profile approaching that observed with receptor ablation.

Notably, the mutations introduced in this study are confined to the N-terminal V domain of Nectin-1. Previous studies have shown that the gD-binding interface of Nectin-1 largely overlaps with the homodimerization interface of Nectin-1, and that engagement by viral gD can disrupt Nectin-1 dimerization and impair its role in cell-cell adhesion [29]. In this context, targeted mutations within the gD-binding interface may reduce viral engagement while potentially alleviating gD-mediated disruption of Nectin-1 dimerization. However, whether the mutant proteins fully retain their ability to form trans-dimers and support adherens junctions remains to be determined, and warrants further investigation.

The successful generation of these complex mutations also highlights the practical advantages of prime editing over conventional CRISPR/Cas9 HDR approaches. Our initial HDR attempts in PK-15 cells yielded low editing efficiencies even after optimizing, consistent with known limitations in livestock cells. In contrast, PE enabled efficient and precise editing across multiple distant sites without reliance on exogenous DNA donors [30]. The low indel frequency and relatively high rate of homozygous clone generation further support the suitability of PE for large-animal genome engineering, where efficiency and precision are critical.

Despite the robust resistance to PRV infection conferred by the four-site mutation, residual viral replication suggests that additional mechanisms may contribute to viral persistence. THBS3 has been reported to promote viral attachment, membrane fusion, and LDLR has been shown to be upregulated upon PRV infection via SREBP-dependent pathways and to directly participate in viral attachment and entry [18, 31]. In parallel, evidence from the HSV-1 system indicates that, although nectin-1 is essential for viral entry, it is dispensable for direct cell-to-cell transmission, highlighting the existence of receptor-independent spread mechanisms [32]. Nevertheless, the substantial reductions in viral replication and spread suggest that disrupting the gD-Nectin-1 interaction remains highly effective in limiting overall infection dynamics.

Another additional consideration for receptor-targeted antiviral strategies is the potential emergence of viral escape mutants. PRV exhibits considerable genetic variability, particularly in glycoprotein C (gC), which is frequently used as a phylogenetic marker, whereas glycoproteins gB and gD are comparatively more conserved [33]. Consistent with this, vaccination studies have shown that gB- and gD-based immunogens confer higher levels of protection against variant strains than gC [34–36].Given that Nectin-1 primarily interacts with gD, these observations suggest that the gD-Nectin-1 interface may be subject to stronger structural constraints, potentially limiting viral escape through mutation. However, the presence of alternative entry pathways implies that single-target strategies may not achieve complete resistance. Future approaches combining Nectin-1 editing with modulation of additional host factors may further enhance antiviral efficacy.

Finally, as the present findings are based on in vitro cellular assays, their in vivo relevance has yet to be determined. In particular, whether the level of viral attenuation observed in X4 cells is sufficient to prevent clinical disease or reduce transmission at the population level remains unclear. The generation of gene-edited pigs carrying these precise Nectin-1 mutations will be essential to evaluate reduced susceptibility to PRV infection, as well as potential physiological effects and long-term biosafety in vivo.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful application of prime editing to introduce precise, multi-site amino acid substitutions into porcine Nectin-1, and provides systematic functional evidence that these residues play critical roles in PRV entry. We show that multi-site precision editing can confer a level of resistance to PRV infection comparable to that observed with gene knockout, while preserving the overall structural integrity of the receptor. This “functional inactivation without structural elimination” strategy offers a potential alternative for developing PRV-resistant livestock, and establishes a conceptual and technical framework for the future generation of disease-resistant animals with minimal genetic modifications.
