Current prevalence and risk factors of apical periodontitis in healthy Saudi adults attending dental school at Najran University: a cross-sectional study using cone beam computed tomography
Authors: Rami Alhomrany, Faisal Al- Jally, Abdullah Al-Jari, Mahdi Al-Zamanan, Naif Al-Laajam, Abdullah Almakrami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose: This study assessed the prevalence of apical periodontitis (AP) and associated risk factors in healthy Saudi adults attending dental school at Najran university during the period from 2021–2024. Methods: CBCT scans from 420 healthy subjects (6,440 teeth) were reviewed. Data on AP prevalence, both overall and on untreated versus treated teeth, were collected. Scans were retrieved from the School of Dentistry at Najran university, spanning January 2021 to July 2024. Chi-square and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate associations with risk factors such as gender, tooth type, root canal treatment adequacy, final restoration status, dental caries, and periodontal disease. Results: AP was diagnosed in 78.6% of subjects, with a per-tooth prevalence of 12.9%. AP was highly observed in root-canal treated teeth (42.9%) than in untreated teeth (7%). The highest odds of AP were associated with dental caries (OR = 239.8, 95% CI = 151.02–380.72) and endodontic mishaps (OR = 87.03, 95% CI = 38.14–198.64). Conclusion: This study found a high prevalence of AP among healthy Saudi adults attending dental school at Najran university from 2021 to 2024. Significant risk factors included dental caries, periodontal disease, molar teeth, inadequate endodontic treatment, and the absence of full-coverage crowns, while female gender was correlated to greater occurrence of AP. The strong association with dental caries and inadequate root canal treatment highlights the need for early intervention and preventive strategies to reduce the burden of AP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44445-025-00060-9.Keywords: Apical periodontitis, Post-Covid-19, Prevalence, Risk factors, Root canal treatment, Saudi adults, STROBE guidelines

Introduction

Apical periodontitis (AP) is an inflammation of the periapical tissues, commonly in response to persistent microbial infection within the root canal systems (Gazivoda et al. 2009; Marton & Kiss 1993). It results from the host’s immune reaction to microbial invasion and their biological waste products within the root canals (Nair 2004). Understanding the occurrence of this condition is crucial for planning dental care strategies, clinical protocols, and determining long-term prognosis. Due to its clinical importance, contemporary epidemiological assessments are essential to guide treatment planning and improve outcomes.

The prevalence of AP has been documented globally, with findings generally indicating a high occurrence of the disease. However, systematic reviews and meta-analyses revealed a significant variability in prevalence rates across different regions, underscoring the need for further national studies to obtain more precise data (Jakovljevic et al. 2020; Tibúrcio-Machado et al. 2021). In Saudi Arabia, the existing literature on AP is limited and methodologically variable. Two notable studies, by Alrahabi et al. and Alzahrani et al., reported prevalence rates using panoramic and periapical radiographs, with one study focusing on diabetic patients, which may have influenced the outcomes (Al-Zahrani et al. 2017; Alrahabi & Younes 2016). These methodological differences, including imaging modality and patient selection, may have led to under or overestimation of true AP prevalence. Moreover, it is worth mentioning that both studies were conducted before the surge of COVID-19 pandemic, however, even if the pandemic led to temporary disruptions in dental services, its direct impact on AP prevalence remains uncertain. Nevertheless, reassessing AP prevalence in the years following the pandemic may provide insight into emerging trends in oral health.

Additionally, while Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) has demonstrated superior accuracy in detecting apical lesions compared to conventional radiographs, its use has been limited in prevalence studies. The study by Seltzer and Bender in 2003, emphasized that CBCT is more effective in identifying periapical lesions as conventional radiographs only detect periapical radiolucency when lesions penetrate the cortical bone and exhibit mineral loss exceeding 50% (Bender & Seltzer 2003). Despite its advantages, CBCT is not yet widely adopted as a routine diagnostic tool, which could affect the accuracy of prevalence estimates if conventional methods are used (American Dental Association 2012).

The present research seeks to fill these identified gaps by assessing the current prevalence of AP among healthy Saudi adults using CBCT scans during the period from 2021 to 2024. By following STROBE guidelines, this research seeks to provide robust data that can add to the worldwide evidence base on AP and provide guidance for future systematic reviews and clinical practices. The objectives of this research were to determine the occurrence of AP in a Saudi subpopulation and to identify correlated variables, offering better understanding of the ongoing status of dental health in this nation. It was hypothesized that no meaningful relationship exists between the occurrence of AP and variables including gender, tooth type, adequacy of endodontic treatment, presence of crowns, caries, periodontal disease, and endodontic mishaps, in healthy Saudi adults.

Materials and methods

Research framework and ethical approval

This investigation utilized a cross-sectional approach, sought to evaluate the occurrence of AP among Saudi healthy adults based on CBCT scans. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Najran university granted approval for this study under the number (202,401–076-017907–040325), on 30/01/2024. This investigation followed ethical standards consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. Participation in this investigation was preceded by the acquisition of informed written consent from all individuals.

Participants selection and sample size estimation

Subjects enrolled in the study were healthy Saudi adults who had undergone CBCT scans for various dental purposes. All CBCT scans were obtained from the Dental School at Najran university, covering the period from January 2021 to July 2024. The sample comprised individuals of both genders, aged between 19 to 45 years, who received dental treatment at the university's dental clinic, which serves individuals from various regions across Saudi Arabia. This ensured a sample with demographic diversity in terms of regional representation, age, and gender. To reduce the potential for selection bias, all healthy Saudi adults who underwent CBCT scans for various dental reasons were included without pre-selecting participants according to whether AP was present or not. A total of 2,846 CBCT scans were initially reviewed, and sample size calculations indicated that at least 384 subjects were needed to estimate the occurrence of AP with 95% confidence and a margin of error of 0.05. In this formula, n stands for the sample size, Z is the standard score for the 95% confidence interval (1.96), P is the estimated proportion (50%), and E is the chosen margin of error (0.05). This formula was applied using G*Power software to ensure transparency and reproducibility. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 420 CBCT scans were selected for analysis, exceeding the minimum sample size requirement (Fig. 1). Scans were collected from routine clinical cases such as implant planning, third molar tooth assessment, or endodontic evaluation. No scans were specifically taken for the purpose of this research. Eligible participants included dentate individuals aged 18 and above who underwent CBCT imaging. Scans were excluded if obtained prior to 2021, if the patient was medically compromised, under 18, or edentulous. Also, exclusion criteria included non-odontogenic lesions as confirmed by a board-certified endodontist and radiologist, and scans exhibiting distortion or poor clarity.

Radiographic examination and data collection

The CBCT datasets were reviewed using Romexis imaging software (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland). All included CBCT images were obtained with a medium field of view (9 × 15 cm) at 10 mA and 90 kV, and captured at a spatial resolution of 0.2 mm. Images were assessed using a 17-inch display with 1280 × 1024 resolution for standardized viewing (HP L1706, TX, USA). To reduce information bias, two independent examiners, comprising a board-certified endodontist and a board-certified radiologist, evaluated the CBCT scans. Calibration sessions were held before image evaluation to align examiners criteria and ensure consistency. These sessions involved reviewing a set of tests CBCT scans, discussing diagnostic criteria, and reaching consensus on key parameters to ensure consistency in image interpretation. In instances of disagreement between the examiners, consensus was reached through discussion. All CBCT images were reviewed in axial, sagittal, and coronal planes to capture comprehensive diagnostic information.

For each CBCT scan, data were collected on the subject's gender, the occurrence of AP individually for every tooth, the type of teeth, the existence of crowns and restorations, adequacy of root canal treatment, endodontic mishaps, periodontal diseases, and decay. There were no missing data in this study, as all CBCT scans and relevant patient information were complete and available for analysis. Endodontic mishaps were defined as the presence of separated files, apparent apical transportation, perforations, and presence of threaded metal post; acknowledging that the latter may reflect associated structural or restorative challenges rather than being a direct cause for periapical pathology. A tooth was classified as having received endodontic therapy if a radiopaque material was observed in the pulp space or canals. AP was identified by the presence of periapical radiolucency which is measuring no less than double the normal periodontal ligament space, as suggested by De Moore (De Moor et al. 2000). Adequacy of root canal filling was defined by its extension within 2 mm of the apex on radiographic evaluation. An obturation was considered suboptimal if it failed to reach within 2 mm of the apex, was extruded past it, or was restricted to the pulp space. Coronal decay was diagnosed based on a clear radiolucency with no confounding artifacts. Crowns were defined as restorations covering the entire coronal surface of a tooth, visible radiographically as cast-type structures, whereas fillings were partial restorations composed of materials like composite, amalgam, or glass ionomer. Periodontal disease was assessed radiographically based on bone loss patterns consistent with stages outlined in the 2017 world workshop classification of periodontal and peri-implant conditions, accompanied by observable furcation involvement and/or vertical bone loss on CBCT imaging as key indicators (Dutta et al. 2014; Tonetti et al. 2018).

Statistical analysis

Dataset underwent analysis via IBM SPSS software (version 29). The level of concordance between assessors was determined by Cohen's kappa statistic for inter-examiners agreement. Associations between categorical variables and AP were analysed using the Chi-square test. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to identify variables related to the presence of AP, while controlling for potential confounders. The quantitative variables included in the logistic regression model were age, gender, tooth type, the presence or absence of crowns, restorations, adequacy of root canal treatment, endodontic mishaps, periodontal disease, and dental caries. No continuous variables were categorized in this study and no sensitivity analysis was performed as the dataset was complete and robust statistical techniques were applied, with adjustments made for potential confounding variables. Findings with p-values below 0.05 were interpreted as statistically significant.

Results

Overall count of CBCT scans retrieved from dental school at Najran university were 2846 scans. Of which, 2426 scans were excluded and only 420 scans were part of this study`s final sample (Fig. 1). The reasons of exclusion were scans taken before the year of 2021, unclear scans with poor quality, edentulous subjects, younger subjects than 18 years old, medically compromised subjects, and lesions of non-odontogenic origin as diagnosed by experienced endodontist and radiologist at the dental school (Fig. 1). The dataset used in the analysis was fully accounted for, with no missing data on the outcome variable (apical periodontitis) or any associated risk factors. Cohen`s kappa statistic along with asymptotic standard error for inter-examiners agreement are illustrated in (Table 1). Population`s demographic characteristics and prevalence of AP across different conditions, are presented in (Table 2). Participants had an average age of 29.32 years (Standard deviation SD = ± 7.13), with an age range from 19 to 45 years. Of the 420 participants, 240 were male (57.1%) and 180 were female (42.9%). The overall percentage of AP was 78.6%, with 330 out of 420 participants diagnosed with AP in at least one tooth. On a per-tooth basis, the prevalence was 12.9%, with 831 out of 6,440 teeth showing AP. Further, it was observed that greater rates of AP were detected in teeth that had undergone endodontic treatment (42.9%) compared to untreated teeth (7%). Additionally, the occurrence of AP was greater in females (15.8% of teeth) compared to males (10.6% of teeth). AP rates on endodontically treated and untreated teeth were evaluated in this study and summarized in (Table 3). On endodontically treated teeth, failure to place full-coverage crowns after endodontic treatment was associated with the highest percentage of AP, regardless the treatment`s quality (Table 3). However, on untreated teeth, the percentage of AP was reported to be the highest when the tooth had a carious lesion (Table 3). The Chi-square test (unadjusted analysis) revealed several significant associations between AP and the variables evaluated (Table 4). Gender was significantly correlated to greater observations of AP, as female participants exhibited AP more frequently than male participants (p < 0.001) (Table 4). Tooth type was also significantly associated with AP, with molars showing a higher prevalence than premolars and anterior teeth (p < 0.001) (Table 4). Additionally, the existence of crowns and adequacy of endodontic treatment were significantly correlated to AP, as teeth without crowns and those with inadequate endodontic treatment had a greater frequency of AP cases (p < 0.001) (Table 4). The multivariable logistic regression (adjusted analysis) identified several contributors correlated significantly with AP (Table 5). Endodontic mishaps on endodontically treated teeth were linked to the highest odds of AP (OR = 87.03, 95% CI = (38.14–198.64), p < 0.001), while dental caries on untreated teeth also showed a strong association with the condition (OR = 239.8, 95% CI = (151.02–380.72), p < 0.001) (Table 5). In addition to these factors, female gender showed a statistically significant association with higher odds of AP in comparison to male participants (OR = 1.50, 95% CI = (1.35–1.81), p < 0.001) (Table 5). The likelihood of AP occurrence was significantly greater in root canal treated teeth relative to untreated ones (OR = 10.14, 95% CI = (8.63–11.90), p < 0.001) (Table 5). Molar teeth were also more likely to exhibit AP compared to anterior and premolar teeth (OR = 3.2, 95% CI = (2.70–3.85), p < 0.001) (Table 5). Conversely, placement of full-coverage crowns was correlated with decreased probability of AP (OR = 0.60, 95% CI = (0.54–0.73), p < 0.001) (Table 5). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on variables such as gender, tooth type, and treatment adequacy, with results adjusted for potential confounders. No formal sensitivity analyses were performed due to the completeness of the dataset and the robustness of the statistical methods applied.

Discussion

The existing knowledge regarding the global prevalence of AP is limited. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation that examined the occurrence of AP among Saudi healthy subpopulation using CBCT scans. CBCT is known to have higher sensitivity in detecting periapical pathosis in comparison with conventional radiographs (Bender & Seltzer 2003; Estrela et al. 2008). Nonetheless, there remains a lack of consensus to use CBCT as a routine diagnostic tool, as indicated by the latest update from the (ADA) (American dental association 2012). It is pertinent to note that CBCT scans were not specifically solicited for research purpose in this study, but rather, scans were obtained for various dental treatments. The findings of this study suggested that 78.6% of subjects diagnosed with a minimum of a single tooth with AP. On a per-tooth basis, the reported percentage of apical periodontitis reached 12.9%. Another aim of this research was to determine variables linked to an increased frequency of AP cases. Notably, the highest odds of AP were associated with caries lesions and endodontic mishaps in untreated and endodontically treated teeth, respectively. Additionally, factors such as female gender and absences of full-coverage crown after root canal treatment were found to be correlated with increased observations of the condition.

The cross-sectional nature of this investigation represents a primary constraint as it prevents assessment of lesion`s healing and precludes causal inference regarding the identified risk factors. However, the primary aim was to report the presence of AP at a specific time point, independent of any ongoing healing. Nonetheless, many of the detected lesions were associated with known etiological factors such as dental caries, endodontic mishaps, or absence of full-coverage crowns, all of which are established contributors to peri-radicular disease progression. To reduce interobserver variability and information bias, a subjective evaluation method introduced by De Moor was employed by calibrated board-certified endodontists and a radiologist (De Moor et al. 2000). Although this research was conducted at a single institution, we sought to minimize selection bias by including all eligible CBCT scans taken between 2021 and 2024 from a demographically diverse group of healthy Saudi adults who underwent imaging for various clinical reasons. While the scans were not randomly selected, the inclusive approach allowed for a more representative assessment of broader dental health trends within this population.

The global investigation into the prevalence of AP dates to 1976. Two comprehensive systematic reviews with meta-analyses analysed moderate- to high-quality studies conducted between 1976 and 2020 (Jakovljevic et al. 2020; Tibúrcio-Machado et al. 2021). It was reported by Tibúrcio-Machado et al., that the global individual-level AP prevalence was approximately 52%, while the tooth-level prevalence was estimated at 7% and 6.3% by Tibúrcio-Machado et al., and Jakovljevic et al., respectively. Notably, only a few studies utilized CBCT imaging in assessing the prevalence of AP globally, which offers superior diagnostic sensitivity. For instance, Bürklein et al., in 2020, reported an AP prevalence of 3.8% per tooth and 61% per individual in a German population, with 40.2% of endodontically treated teeth affected (Bürklein et al. 2020). Dutta et al., in 2014, reported similar findings in a Scottish cohort, with 5% tooth-level and 69% individual-level prevalence, and 47% of treated teeth showing AP (Dutta et al. 2014). In Belgium, Van der Veken et al., in 2017, found that 59% of subjects diagnosed with a minimum of one tooth affected by AP, reporting an overall tooth-level prevalence of 5.9% and 37% in treated teeth specifically (Van der Veken et al. 2017). In contrast, Lemagner et al., in 2015, reported a higher prevalence in a French population, 78% at the individual level and 40% in treated teeth (Lemagner et al. 2015). These variations may reflect differences in sample size, number of teeth per subject, age distribution, medical status, recruitment source, and statistical approaches employed across studies.

In Saudi-Arabia, only a limited number of studies, of moderate to high quality, have evaluated the occurrence of AP. One such study, conducted by Alrahabi et al., reported a 53% of subjects were diagnosed with AP in at least a single tooth, during the period from 2010 until 2013 (Alrahabi & Younes 2016). Notably, this study solely relied on panoramic radiographs, which, in addition to the different time-frame, might explain the lower reported prevalence in comparison with the present study (Alrahabi & Younes 2016). Another study by Alzahrani et al. indicated that 86% of the participants were found to have a minimum of one tooth affected with AP during the period from 2013 until 2015 (Al-Zahrani et al. 2017). It is crucial to point out that Alzahrani et al. study primarily focused on patients with type 2 diabetes, which could potentially influence the reported occurrence of the disease (Al-Zahrani et al. 2017). Additionally, the sample size included in Alzahrani et al. study comprised only 100 subjects, which is significantly lower than our sample size (Al-Zahrani et al. 2017).

Moreover, this study identified several factors were significantly associated with higher prevalence of AP, including female gender, tooth type, inadequate root canal treatment, absence of full-coverage crowns, dental caries, and periodontal diseases, based on both Chi-square and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Dental caries and endodontic mishaps demonstrated the highest odds of association within the Saudi subpopulation. It is important to note that the presence of threaded metal posts was associated with a high prevalence of AP, however, this may not directly cause the disease, but may instead indicates underlying structural or restorative deficiencies such as poor coronal seal, which might contribute to microbial leakage. These findings align with previous CBCT-based studies from France, Belgium, Scotland, and Germany, which also reported associations between AP and inadequate root canal treatment, molar teeth, and female gender (Bürklein et al. 2020; Dutta et al. 2014; Lemagner et al. 2015; Van der Veken et al. 2017). Notably, the Scottish study reported a higher AP prevalence in adequately treated teeth, likely due to overlooked coronal restoration quality (Dutta et al. 2014). All associated variables, except the gender, are directly linked to microbial infection, the primary cause of pulpal and peri-radicular disease (Kakehashi et al. 1965). While the gender association was consistent with other populations (Lemagner et al. 2015; Dutta et al. 2014), it should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect confounding variables such as healthcare-seeking behaviour, or systemic factors rather than gender itself.

Several factors can influence the reported prevalence of AP, including the study’s geographic setting and timeframe. This study focused on a diverse Saudi subpopulation treated at a Najran university dental clinics, with patients representing various regions across the country. Compared to national and international studies conducted prior to 2021, the present findings suggest a higher prevalence of apical periodontitis. This updated data is valuable for healthcare policymakers aiming to assess current disease patterns and guide resource planning. While AP often follows inadequate endodontic treatment, prevention should begin at earlier stages, by managing dental caries, preserving pulp vitality, and proper referral to experienced clinicians when necessary. These upstream efforts can help reduce the overall burden of AP.

Conclusion

The present research reveals a high occurrence of AP in Saudi-Arabia during the time frame from 2021 to 2024, affecting 78.6% of individuals and 12.9% of teeth. Key risk factors include carious lesions, endodontic mishaps, and absence of full-coverage crowns after root canal treatment, while female gender was associated with higher prevalence of AP. Additionally, further international studies are needed to explore the accurate prevalence of AP globally, to better understand current patterns in the disease`s prevalence and correlated risk factors.
