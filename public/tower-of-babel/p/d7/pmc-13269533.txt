Hypoactivity, abnormal development of dendrites relevant to impaired synaptic transmission of calretinin-expressing interneurons in the medial prefrontal cortex underlies social deficits of mouse model in autism
Authors: Mengyuan Chen, Ke Zhao, Chao Gao, Daoqi Mei, Shengli Shi, Li Wang, Limin Sun, Jisheng Guo, Guo Bin, Shuai Zhao, Xingxue Yan, Miao He, Yaodong Zhang, Xiaona Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by core symptoms including impairments in social behavior and communication. The impaired excitatory and inhibitory signals have been implicated in the pathophysiology of social behavior deficits. Altered calretinin (CR)-containing GABAergic interneurons have been observed in ASD, but their roles and underlying mechanisms remain unveiled. Here, using valproic acid (VPA)-exposed mice for CR-Cre and R26::LS-tdTomato (Ai14) model of ASD, we prove that a decreased number of CR interneurons in the mPFC of an animal model for ASD. Double-staining experiments demonstrated the decreased number of CR interneurons stained for c-Fos. Also, reduction in GCaMP7s fluorescence intensity was elicited in sociability and social novelty preference using in vivo fiber photometry, manifesting VPA-induced suppression of CR-positive cell activation. Additionally, we observed the abnormalities of dendrites in CR interneurons including lower dendritic arbors, decreased dendrite complexity, and spine density, paralleled by abnormal development of spine morphology. Intriguingly, the electrophysiological recordings of tdTomato-labeled interneurons revealed that exposure to VPA depressed intrinsic neuronal excitability by decreasing spontaneous and evoked action potential frequencies. These changes were concomitant with impairments of glutamatergic and GABAergic synaptic transmission of CR interneurons. Strikingly, chemogenetic silencing of mPFC CR-expressing interneurons induced social interaction deficits in mice. These sociability impairments can be rescued by optogenetic activation of CR activity in VPA-exposed mice. Our study indicates that prenatal exposure to VPA induced reduced activities, abnormalities in morphological development, and decreased intrinsic excitability as well as accompanying impaired synaptic transmission of CR interneurons. Our findings provide strong evidence for the notion that the CR interneurons has a critical role in the regulation of social behavior in mice and manifest that CR interneurons dysfunction may be implicated in social impairments in ASD.Subject terms: Autism spectrum disorders, Molecular neuroscience

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a cluster of neurodevelopmental disorders with core features characterized by impairments in social interaction and communication. Several brain structures and functions have been shown to underlie social impairments, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) [1]. The increased clinical and experimental evidence demonstrates that ASD is strongly associated with excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission impairments in an essential brain region controlling social communication [2–4].

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABAergic) interneurons vary in activity patterns, morphological appearances, and electrophysiological properties [5–7]. ASD is related to numerous alterations in GABAergic interneurons [8, 9]. In postmortem human brains and ASD animal models, the number of parvalbumin (PV)-immunoreactive interneurons with certain subtypes in the mPFC is reduced [8, 10, 11]. Considerable attention has been paid to calretinin (CR)-positive cells, a calcium-binding protein, constitute 12–30% of the entire population of GABAergic neurons [12, 13]. CR interneurons have heterogeneities in morphological patterns and intrinsic firing properties [13–16]. Individuals with ASD had a reduced density of CR-expressing interneurons in the caudate [17], which indicated that the loss of CR interneurons had been linked to the vulnerability of interneurons and might influence overall excitability in ASD.

Recent efforts demonstrated that abnormalities in neuronal activity were predominant in ASD [18, 19]. Indeed, it was unequivocally established that PV neurons were hypoactive in several genetic models of ASD causally relevant to social behavior deficits [9]. Moreover, the geometry of dendritic branches and spatial distribution of synapses played indispensable roles for neurons in receiving and integrating signals [20]. Dendritic spines also critically mediate calcium near synapses, neurotransmission, and neuronal plasticity [21]. Social deficits have been postulated to stem from aberrations in dendritic structure which consequently disrupt neuronal connectivity and synaptic function [22, 23]. Alterations in the morphological development of dendritic spines are critical hallmarks of ASD [24]. Available evidence points to the existence of spines in interneurons [25, 26]. CR interneurons had highly tortuous dendrites that bore spine-like appendages [27]. Despite these observations, it remains presently unknown whether CR interneurons within the mPFC are affected in activity, dendritic morphologies, and intrinsic excitability as well as synaptic transmission underlying social behavioral dysfunction in ASD.

Prenatal valproic acid (VPA) exposure in rodents is a commonly used model to induce social impairments, which are reminiscent of some features observed in ASD [28]. In the current study, the homozygous transgenic mice for CR-Cre and Ai14 prenatally exposed to VPA presented social impairments similar to the ASD symptoms. Using immunohistochemistry, in vivo fiber photometry, patch-clamp recording, optogenetics, pharmacological stimulation, and behavioral assays, we proved that the decreased number and hypoactivity of surviving CR interneurons in the mPFC played a key role in social interaction deficits. Importantly, we detected reduced dendrite elaboration and abnormal spine morphological development of CR-tdTomato interneurons in VPA-treated mice. Correspondingly, altered intrinsic excitability and reduced synaptic transmission were observed. Moreover, we found that chemogenetic inhibition of CR interneurons triggered impaired social interaction. Optogenetic activation of these neurons in the mPFC ameliorated social behavior deficits in VPA-exposed mice. Accordingly, these data indicated that VPA exposure resulted in morphological dysgeneses and functional abnormalities of CR interneurons, which uncovered novel mechanisms underlying typical ASD-related social impairments.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University and performed according to the guidelines of the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Homozygous CR-Cre mice were crossed with Ai14 [CgGt(ROSA)26Sortm14 (CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J] to allow for Cre-driven expression of the red fluorophore, tdTomato, in almost all CR-positive cells. Genotypes were determined by PCR using tail DNA. For CR-Cre F-5′-CCA CTA GAT CGA ATT CCG AAG-3′, R-5′- AGG TCT GGG AAG GAG TGT CA -3′, which yielded a product of 280 bp. For Ai14 F-5′- GGC ATT AAA GCA GCG TAT CC -3′, R-5′- CTG TTC CTG TAC GGC ATG G-3′, which amplified 196 bp fragment. CR-Cre and Ai14 mice were gifts from Prof. Miao He (Fudan University, Shanghai, China) and Associate Prof. Ji Xu (Zhengzhou University, Zhengzhou, China), respectively. Mice at 8–10 weeks of age were maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 07:00, off at 19:00), temperature (22 ± 2 °C), and humidity (50 ± 2%). Animals had ad libitum access to food and water.

VPA-induced mouse model of ASD

CR-Cre and Ai14 mice were randomly assigned to mate overnight, the same operation in our previous study [29]. Pregnancy was designated by the presence of a vaginal plug on embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). Pregnant female mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 500 mg/kg sodium salt of valproic acid (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat# P4543) on day 12 (E12.5), while control animals were given an equivalent volume of saline. On postnatal day 21, pup mice were weaned and transferred to cages. Only male offspring on postnatal 30 days were subjected to the subsequent experiments.

Sociability and social novelty testing

The three-chambered social interaction test was carried out as described in our previous study [29]. The test was performed under dim (5 lx) intensity. We used the handheld lux meter to check the lighting in the entire compartment. The three-chamber box (20 × 40 × 22 cm) included dividing walls with 5 × 8 cm openings to allow exploration of each chamber. Test mice were novel animals with matched age, sex, and strain. During the habituation, the mouse was gently placed in the empty central arena and allowed to explore the apparatus for 10 min. In the sociability phase, a selected unfamiliar mouse (stranger 1) was placed in a wire cage (10 cm diameter) on the left side, and an empty wire cage (novel object) was put on the right side. The location of the stranger and novel object on the left or right chamber was systematically alternated to prevent side preference. The test mouse was free to access the new environment for 10 min.

For the social novelty preference test, the subject mouse was put into the central chamber. A second unfamiliar mouse (stranger 2) was positioned within the wire cage on the right side. The subject mouse was free to explore the mouse (stranger 1) from the previous sociability test, and the novel mouse during the following 10 min. The amount of time spent in each region, and time spent in sniffing or interacting with unfamiliar mouse were both recorded using camera-assisted ANY-Maze software (Stoelting Co., IL, USA). A blinded investigator performed behavioral scoring.

To measure sociability, we calculated a discrimination index for each mouse (time spent with unfamiliar mouse 1 - time spent with novel object) / (total time spent with unfamiliar mouse 1 and novel object). To assess social novelty, the discrimination index was calculated (time spent with novel mouse 2 - time spent with unfamiliar mouse 1) / (total time spent with unfamiliar mouse 1 and novel mouse 2).

Stereotaxic surgery

CR-Cre mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane. The scalp was locally sterilized and the skin was opened to eliminate surplus periosteum tissues. The mPFC (AP: +2.00 mm; ML: +0.3 mm; DV: −2.0 mm, relative to the Bregma) was located under a stereotaxic instrument. After drilling a hole with the high-speed microdrill, a total of 300 nl viral dilutions (rAAV-hSyn-FLEX-jGCaMP7s-WPRE; viral titer, 1.10 × 1013 genome copies/ml, OBiO Technology, China) was slowly injected into mPFC at a rate of 40 nl/min using a glass micropipette connected to an ultra-micro injection pump (Xinglin Life Tech., Beijing, China). The micropipette was retained for 10 min before retraction.

In vivo fiber photometry

A commercialized fiber photometry system (Thinker Tech Nanjing Bioscience Inc., Nanjing, China) was used to record the calcium signals from the mPFC. Optic fiber with ceramic ferrule (diameter: 1.25 mm) was implanted in the mPFC (AP: +2.00 mm; ML: +0.3 mm; DV: −1.9 mm) in CR-Cre mice three weeks after GCaMP7s virus injection. Neuronal activity based on calcium imaging and behavioral tests were carried out. The laser power was adjusted at the tip of the optical fiber to 30 μW for 473 nm to minimize the photobleaching by the optical power meter (PM100D, Thorlabs, USA). Normalized GCaMP7s fluorescence (ΔF/F) was used to evaluate the GCaMP7s signal with MATLAB (R2019b, MathWorks) and align it with the starting point of social interaction onset. Positions of the fiber tip of each mouse were verified histologically after recording.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Immunohistochemistry was performed as we have previously described [30]. CR-Ce::Ai14 mice were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), and then transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Brains were rapidly dissected and post-fixed overnight in the same fixative at 4 °C, followed by embedding in paraffin. Coronal 5-μm thick sections were cut using a microtome (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were dewaxed using xylene and dehydrated using graded ethanol. These sections were quenched for 30 min in 3% H2O2 and incubated in 3% normal goat serum for 20 min at room temperature. The tissues were incubated with the anti-rabbit CR (1:2000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA; Cat# ab92341) in the blocking serum at 4 °C overnight. After rinsing in PBS (three times for 5 min), sections were reacted for 2 h at room temperature with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, ZSGB-Bio, Beijing, China; Cat#ZB164117). The slices were visualized with 3’,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB), stained with hematoxylin for 1 min. Tissue slices were sealed with neutral gum. Images were observed and captured by an optical microscope equipped with a camera. Measures of each animal were carried out in at least four sections.

Immunofluorescence

As mentioned above, adult mice were anesthetized and perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were quickly removed, post-fixed overnight at 4 °C, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose diluted in PBS. The tissues were embedded in an optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan). The 40 μm coronal sections through the mPFC were obtained using a freezing microtome (Leica Microsystems). Sections were incubated in a blocking solution which consisted of 5% donkey/goat serum, 2.5% bovine serum albumin, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PBS for 2 h. For CR double labeled with tdTomato and GCaMP7s, sections were incubated with goat anti-CR antibody (1:200, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA; Cat#AF5065) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (1:600, Invitrogen, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat#A32814TR) and Alexa Flour 647 conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (1:600, Invitrogen; Cat# A32849TR) diluted with the blocking buffer. For those labeled with tdTomato and c-Fos, sections were incubated rabbit anti-c-Fos (1:5000; Sigma, Cat#F7799). After washing with 0.1 M PBS 3 times (10 min each), sections were incubated with the Alexa Flour 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:600, Invitrogen; Cat#A48282TR). After washing with PBS, nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:1000, Sigma; Cat#D9542). Sections were mounted on glass slides and cover-slipped with 75% glycerol in 0.1 M PBS. Fluorescent images were acquired on a confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSM980, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Measurements of dendrite length and complexity

To determine dendrite length and the complexity of dendritic arborization, individual tdTomato-labeled interneurons within the mPFC were z-stacked under a 20 × objective with a z-series interval of 0.6 μm and zoom 3. We quantitatively analyzed interneurons with relatively intact dendritic arborizations. After tdTomato-positive soma and dendritic arborization were manually traced under ImageJ with the NeuronJ plugin, the length of each dendritic segment was calculated. The total dendrite length was determined by summing the lengths of all segments. The complexity of dendritic arbors for individual interneurons was analyzed using Sholl analysis with the “Sholl plugin”. For Sholl analysis, the dendritic crossings with concentric circles of enhancing radius at 10 mm increments from the center of soma on the two-dimensional reconstruction were counted.

Quantification of dendritic spines

Z-stacks for dendritic segments of tdTomato-labeled interneurons resided in the mPFC were detected by 63 × oil-immersion objective lens. The appropriate parameters for z-stacks were as follows: 0.5 μm of z-series thickness, zoom 3, and the pixel size of 0.09 × 0.09 × 0.28 μm. A three-dimensional confocal image was reconstructed using individual z-stack scanning. The spine neck of each dendrite and width of the spine head were determined by a semiautomatic program of ImageJ and calculated as mushroom (head ≥ 1.5*neck), stubby (head < 1.5*neck & length ≤ 2*head), long thin (head < 1.5*neck & length > 2*head) and filopodia (length > 2 & length ≤ 5) [31]. Long thin and filopodia-shaped are often dubbed as “immature” spines whereas stubby and mushroom belong to the mature spines [32].

In vitro electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording was carried out as we described previously [29, 33]. Briefly, CR-Cre::Ai14 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice-cold carbonated (95% O2, 5% CO2) cutting solution containing the following (in mM): 120 choline chloride, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgCl2, 25 glucose, 5 sodium ascorbate and 3 sodium pyruvate (300–305 mOsm/L). Coronal slices (300 μm-thick) containing the mPFC (bregma 1.42–0.86 mm) were obtained employing a vibrating microtome (5100 mz, Campden Instruments, Loughborough, United Kingdom). The tdTomato-labeled interneurons were visualized using infrared differential interference contrast at 32 °C by the temperature controller (Warner Instruments, Holliston, USA) in artificial cerebral spinal fluid (ACSF) containing the following (in mM): 124 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 10 glucose, 2 MgSO4 and 2.5 CaCl2 (pH 7.4, 295–300 mOsm/L). The perfusion flow rate was 2.5 ml/min. Patch electrodes were pulled from borosilicate glass (OD 1.5 mm, ID 0.86 mm) using a P97 pipette puller (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA, USA). The resistance of patch electrodes was 3–5 MΩ. Electrical signals were recorded with the EPC 10 amplifier and PatchMaster v2.54 (HEKA, Lambrecht, Germany). Data were digitized with a 5 kHz sampling rate and filtered with a lowpass filter of 2 kHz. The series resistance of CR-expressing cells was < 20 MΩ. Data were excluded if the series resistance changed by > 20% throughout the experiments. The parameters recorded contain spontaneous and evoked firing and miniature post-synaptic currents.

In cell-attached recordings, the patch-pipette solution is based on the standard ACSF. The liquid junction potential was approximately zero on account of the same composition of the pipette as the buffer solutions in the bath. Measurements in current clamp mode were performed by holding the cell at 0 mA.

To record evoked firing, we used a potassium gluconate-based internal solution, which contained (in mM): 130 potassium gluconate, 2 KCl, 8 NaCl, 1 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 2 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na3-GTP, and 0.1 CaCl2 (all were from Sigma) (pH 7.25–7.30, adjusted with KOH; 294–297 mOsm/L). For whole-cell current-clamp recordings, the resting membrane potentials and firing responses to depolarizing current pulses (1 s duration, 0-320 pA with 20 pA increments) were recorded within 5 min from whole-cell break-in. The half-width, threshold potential, rheobase, and discharge frequency of action potentials were calculated by analyzing the first action potential elicited by the current injection.

To record miniature synaptic currents, tdTomato-labeled interneurons were patched and currents were recorded at −70 mV in the whole-cell mode. The recording electrode was filled with an internal solution that contained (in mM): 145 CsCl, 10 HEPES, 10 EGTA, 2 Mg-ATP, 5 QX-314 and 2 CaCl2 (pH 7.25–7.30; adjusted with CsOH; 294-297 mOsm). Miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) were recorded in the presence of 1 µM tetrodotoxin (TTX, Tocris Bioscience, Ellisville, MO, USA; Cat#1078), 50 µM 6-cyano-7-nitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (CNQX, Sigma; Cat#C239), and 50 µM D-2-amino-5-phosphonopentanoate (D-AP5, Sigma; Cat#A8054). Miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) were recorded in the presence of 1 µM TTX and 50 µM picrotoxinin (PTX, Sigma; Cat#P1675). Recordings were 3 min and miniature post-synaptic current events were manually analyzed by the Mini Analysis program (Synaptosoft, Fort Lee, NJ, USA). The frequency and amplitude of current events were statistically compared between the control and VPA-treated mice.

Optogenetic and chemogenetic manipulation

For optogenetic experiments, pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hChR2(H134R)-EYFP-WPRE (300 nL, titer: 6.63 × 1012 genome copies/mL, from OBiO Technology) was unilaterally injected into the mPFC (AP: 2.0 mm; ML: ± 0.3 mm; DV: −2.0 mm). Optical fibers (Outer diameter: 200 μm; Numerical aperture: 0.37) were implanted into the mPFC 0.2 mm above the virus injection site. The power of the light (473 nm, Inper Inc., Hangzhou, China) was 2 mW as measured at the fiber tip. Animals were allowed to recover for three weeks before behavioral testing.

For pharmacological experiments, pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)4-mCherry (300 nL, titer: 2.21 × 1012 genome copies/mL, from OBiO Technology) was bilaterally injected into the mPFC. Clozapine-N-Oxide (CNO, MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA; CAT# HY-17366) was dissolved in 0.9% NaCl solution to a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. CNO (1 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally to mice 30 min prior to behavioral tests. The brain slices were perfused with ACSF containing 5 µM CNO.

Statistical analysis

All results were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was implemented with GraphPad Prism 9.0. All variables satisfied normality and homoscedasticity assumptions. For time spent in social exploration, results were assessed via ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparison test. For Sholl analysis and the firing responses to increasing current injections, data were analyzed with two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Otherwise, comparisons between the two groups were made via unpaired Student’s t-test. The sample size was determined based on established literature [2, 22]. p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Effects of prenatal exposure to VPA on social behaviors in CR-Cre::Ai14 mice

To identify CR-positive interneurons in the mPFC, we crossed a CR-Cre driver line with a Cre-dependent reporter Ai14 to allow the expression of the fluorescent protein tdTomato in these interneurons. PCR analysis confirmed the genotype of these mice (Fig. 1A,B). Subsequently, sociability and social novelty were assessed in the three-chambered social interaction test. As illustrated in Fig. 1C-F, in the sociability test analyzing the total time spent in the three compartments, saline-exposed mice spent substantially more time in the chamber of a novel mouse (S1) relative to the novel object (NO) (S1 = 347.55 ± 5.13 s, n = 20; NO = 128.1 ± 4.20 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). Nonetheless, VPA-treated CR-Cre::Ai14 mice exhibited a pronounced increase in the amount of time spent exploring the novel object relative to the unfamiliar mouse (S1 = 173.04 ± 3.78 s, n = 20; NO = 297.35 ± 5.19 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). In the sociability test analyzing interaction time, mice that received saline injection exhibited more interactions with the novel mouse compared with the unfamiliar object (S1 = 204.25 ± 10.94 s, n = 20; NO = 90.67 ± 4.87 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). For the VPA-injected mouse, there was more time spent sniffing the novel object in comparison with sniffing with the novel mouse (S1 = 77.04 ± 3.87 s, n = 20; NO = 159.09 ± 4.46 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). Furthermore, VPA-operated animals exhibited a lower discrimination index than that of the control group (NaCl= 0.38 ± 0.02, n = 20; VPA = −0.35 ± 0.02, n = 20; p < 0.001).

The time spent in the individual chamber in the social novelty preference test was also measured (Fig. 1G-J). The saline-treated mice spent markedly more time in the compartment with the stranger mice (S2) than that in the chamber with the familiar animals (S1) (S1 = 153.75 ± 3.64 s, n = 20; S2 = 335.29 ± 5.95 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). In contrast, no discernible difference in time spent in exploring two compartments was detected in animals with VPA treatment (p > 0.05). In addition, the sniffing behaviors of mice in the individual chamber were assessed. The saline-injected mice spent more time in sniffing and engaging with a novel animal compared with a familiar mouse (S1 = 85.92 ± 4.87 s, n = 20; S2 = 191.32 ± 7.6 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). Nonetheless, mice with VPA exposure spent similar time sniffing the novel mouse and the familiar animal (p > 0.05), suggesting difficulties in recognizing new partners. In comparison with saline group, CR-Cre::Ai14 mice with VPA treatment displayed obviously lower discrimination index (NaCl = 0.44 ± 0.07, n = 20; VPA = 0.02 ± 0.06, n = 20; p < 0.001). Hence, these data were in excellent consistence with the results derived from the sociability test, indicating that early exposure to VPA can cause social discrimination deficits in CR-Cre::Ai14 mice.

The repetitive and compulsive-like behaviors can be measured by marble burying test and self-grooming test [34]. In the current study, we identified increased number of marbles buried by mice with exposure to VPA relative to that of mice with saline injection (NaCl = 4.35 ± 0.34, n = 8; VPA = 6.15 ± 0.49, n = 8; p < 0.01, Supplementary Fig 1A). For animals with VPA administration, the time spent grooming was obviously longer comparing with that of corresponding control mice (NaCl = 23.63 ± 1.49 s, n = 8; VPA = 49.00 ± 1.95 s, n = 8; p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig 1B).

To determine whether VPA treatment resulted in anxiety-associated behavior in mice, open-field test was performed. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1C-E, rodents prenatally exposed to VPA spent less time in the center of open field comparing with that of the saline groups (NaCl = 60.39 ± 2.62 s, n = 8; VPA = 15.50 ± 0.97 s, n = 8; p < 0.001), while no significant difference for the total distance moved between two groups was observed (p > 0.05). As such, these findings support the view that exposure to VPA in CR-Cre::Ai14 mice led to core behavioral phenotypes of ASD including stereotypical repetition and anxiety-like traits.

CR interneuron numbers are decreased in the mPFC of VPA-treated mice

To evaluate the involvement of CR-containing cells in social impairments, we counted the total number of CR interneurons in the VPA-treated mice. As presented in Fig. 2A-C, the total number of CR interneurons in the mPFC of saline-exposed offspring was 115 ± 3.51/mm2 (n = 5). Nevertheless, in the VPA-induced ASD model, the total number of CR-positive cells was 62.2 ± 4.01/mm2, a 46% reduction as compared to controls (n = 5, p < 0.01). These results implicate that CR interneurons in the mPFC are highly vulnerable in ASD.

Down-regulation of c-Fos in CR interneurons after exposure to VPA

To test the efficiency of tdTomato expression in CR-positive cells, we performed CR immunohistochemistry. On average, 97.9 ± 0.89% of tdTomato-labeled cells in the mPFC were CR-immunoreactive (n = 5) (Fig. 3A,B). Furthermore, the mPFC interneurons were demonstrated to be an essential cell type pertinent to ASD-like social aberrations [4, 9]. c-Fos, serving as a functional marker for identifying neuronal activity, was detected after each phase in the three-chamber test by double-fluorescence staining. To this end, c-Fos was co-labeled with CR interneuron in the mPFC. In the sociability test following VPA exposure, the percentage of c-Fos expression in CR-positive cells is 77.66 ± 1.80% (n = 5) in the saline-operated animals. The percentage of CR-containing for c-Fos significantly decreased to 53.86 ± 4.36% in mice prenatally injected with VPA (n = 5, p < 0.01, Fig. 3C-E). As respected, VPA-treated mice display reduced activation, as evidenced by the decreased number of CR-positive cells stained for c-Fos (NaCl = 72.5 ± 5.71%, n = 5; VPA = 55.3 ± 4.11%, n = 5; p < 0.01, Fig. 3F-H) during the social novelty preference testing compared with control littermates. The discussed results above illustrated that CR interneurons are correlated with sociality and social novelty behavior, and reduced activation in CR interneurons might contribute to social impairments.

The fluorescence intensity of GCaMP7s calcium indicator in CR interneurons is decreased during social impairments

To determine how CR interneurons in the mPFC respond to social dysfunctions induced by VPA, we sought to evaluate their activities during real-time three-chamber social interaction with dual-color fiber photometry. Three weeks after stereotaxic infusion of the Cre-inducible AAV-FLEX-GCaMP7s into the mPFC of CR-Cre mice, a small optical fiber was implanted for chronic recording of GCaMP7s fluorescence signals (Fig. 4A). Histological examination verified the efficiency of GCaMP7s expression on CR interneurons (92.4 ± 0.83% of CR-immunopositive interneurons that expressed GCaMP7s) (n = 8). Approximately 92.3 ± 0.67% of CR-expressing neurons were GCaMP7s positive, indicative of high specificity of viral targeting for CR neurons (n = 8, Fig. 4B).

We then measured the population activity changes of calcium signals of mPFC CR interneurons in mice subjected to social interaction tests. Only the calcium signals recorded when the test mice closely interacted with the stimulus mouse were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 4C-F, the area under the curve (AUC) of calcium signals in CR-expressing interneurons was increased in saline-treated mice in response to the novel animal (S1) relative to the novel object (NO) (S1 = 8.89 ± 2.55%, n = 8; NO = 4.70 ± 0.68%, n = 8; p < 0.001). Similar results were observed in peak values (S1 = 5.84 ± 0.56%, n = 8; SE = 1.45 ± 0.20%, n = 8; p < 0.001). In striking contrast, we did not detect the differences in fluorescence signals measured with calcium indicators of CR-positive cells following prenatal VPA exposure in mice (p > 0.05, p > 0.05).

In the test for social novelty preference, AUC and amplitude of calcium signals of CR-positive interneurons were augmented in saline-exposed mice in response to sniffing and engaging with the novel animal (S2) in comparison to the familiar mouse (S1) (S1 = 4.51 ± 0.89%, n = 8; S2 = 26.58 ± 1.56%, n = 8; p < 0.001). Similar results were seen with amplitude (S1 = 2.78 ± 0.87%, n = 8; S2 = 5.59 ± 0.50%, n = 8; p < 0.001). No substantial differences were found in GCaMP7s fluorescence signals of CR interneurons in VPA-treated mice (p > 0.05, p > 0.05; Fig. 4G-J). Hence, these findings confirmed that the reduced CR interneuron activities in vivo were involved in social deficiencies.

VPA exposure results in abnormal dendritic branching and spine morphology of CR interneurons

Recent evidence has emerged that abnormalities in dendritic development are pathological factors of social behaviors in ASD [31]. We investigated whether basal dendritic branching and spine morphology were altered in CR-positive cells. As seen in Fig. 5A-E, the cumulative dendrite length of tdTomato-labeled interneurons in the VPA group is shorter than in the control group (NaCl = 367.94 ± 11.22 μm, n = 5; VPA = 186.06 ± 11.18 μm, n = 5; p < 0.001). Sholl analysis revealed that dendritic arborization of CR-positive cells after VPA treatment is substantially less complex than that in the control group (NaCl = 9.74 ± 0.22, n = 5; VPA = 5.54 ± 0.17, n = 5; p < 0.001). Notably, Sholl analysis at 10 µm incremental distances from the cell soma center demonstrated that the number of intersections was significantly less interneurons from VPA-exposed animals (between 20 μm and 60 μm away from the soma, p < 0.001), indicating reduced dendritic complexity and consequently decreased synaptic connections with presynaptic terminals.

Dendritic spine development is a major cellular process that is disrupted in ASD [21, 32]. After revealing abnormalities in the dendritic tree of CR-containing cells after VPA treatment, we quantified the spines of basal dendritic segments in the mPFC. As illustrated in Fig. 5F-I, total spine density in the saline group is 43.04 ± 1.39 spines per 20 μm length (n = 5), which was significantly denser than 30.66 ± 1.07spines per 20 μm length (n = 5) in VPA-treated groups (p < 0.001). VPA treatment reduced the dendritic mushroom-like spines (NaCl = 46.88 ± 1.15%, n = 5; VPA = 6.86 ± 1.22%, n = 5; p < 0.001) and increased in thin spines (NaCl = 18.52 ± 0.81%, n = 5; VPA = 27.96 ± 1.32%, n = 5; p < 0.001) of CR-positive cells in mice compared to the control animals. Whereas stubby and filopodial spine densities were comparable between two groups (n = 5; p > 0.05, p > 0.05). Combined, we infer that prenatal exposure to VPA can lead to defects in the dendritic spine morphogenesis of CR interneurons.

Reduced intrinsic excitability of the CR interneurons following exposure to VPA

The abnormalities in dendrite and spine morphology led us to explore whether neuronal and synaptic functions are affected. We inspected the membrane excitability of tdTomato-labeled cells in the mPFC. Fluorescent and differential interference contrast images of the represented CR-positive cells are shown in Fig. 6A. We evaluated spontaneous firing frequency by obtaining cell-attached patches from tdTomato-containing interneurons in a normal bathing medium. Cells expressing tdTomato from VPA-treated mice had a firing rate that was substantially lower than that from control mice (NaCl = 14.04 ± 0.32 Hz, n = 24 cells from 6 mice; VPA = 4.1 ± 0.12 Hz, n = 24 cells from 6 mice; p < 0.001, Fig. 6B,C), suggesting the decreased excitatory input onto these interneurons.

Upon getting into the whole-cell mode, a series of gradient increasing current injection evoked different firing patterns in individual cells. Representative traces of action potential using the 140 pA and 240 pA depolarizing current injection are shown in Fig. 6D. We examined the passive membrane property and intrinsic excitability of CR interneurons in the current-clamp mode. No significant alterations in the resting membrane potential and half-width of evoked action potential were found between two groups (p å 0.05, p å 0.05, Fig. 6E,F). Nevertheless, the threshold potentials were markedly increased in mice after exposure to VPA (NaCl = −36.63 ± 0.58 mV, n = 24 from 6 mice; VPA = −31.61 ± 0.41 mV, n = 24 from 6 mice; p < 0.001, Fig. 6G). Similar results were obtained for rheobase (NaCl = 36.67 ± 4.45 pA, n = 24 from 6 mice; VPA = 58.33 ± 4.33pA, n = 24 from 6 mice; p < 0.01, Fig. 6H). These analyses revealed a reduction in action potential discharge frequency in slices from VPA-treated mice as compared to saline-treated mice (140 pA depolarizing steps; p < 0.05; depolarizing steps ranging from 160 to180 pA, p < 0.01; depolarizing steps from 200–320 pA, p < 0.001; Fig. 6I). The results of these experiments therefore showed that intrinsic excitability in mPFC CR-positive cells was decreased in response to prenatal exposure to VPA.

Altered excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs in CR interneurons of VPA-treated mice

Synaptic dysfunction as a cause of the hallmark social defects in ASD is considered to stem from changes in excitatory and inhibitory activity in synaptic transmission [2, 4]. We next examined whether there might be altered excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs in tdTomato-labeled CR interneurons in the VPA-induced rodent model of ASD, by recording mEPSCs and mIPSCs. As seen in Fig. 7A-I, the mEPSCs frequency of CR-positive interneurons in the mPFC robustly declined in the VPA-exposed mice in comparison with that of the controls (NaCl = 6.03 ± 0.1 Hz, n = 24 cells from 6 mice; VPA = 4.61 ± 0.13 Hz, n = 24 cells from 6 mice; p < 0.001), whereas the mEPSCs amplitude remained unaffected relative to control mice (p > 0.05). Further, we discovered the lower mean frequency (NaCl = 7.99 ± 0.1 Hz, n = 24 from 6 mice; VPA = 3.99 ± 0.13 Hz, n = 24 cells from 6 mice; p < 0.001) and amplitude (NaCl = 17.79 ± 0.31 pA, n = 24 from 6 mice; VPA = 14.85 ± 0.26 pA, n = 24 cells from 6 mice; p < 0.001) of mIPSCs from the VPA-injected mice compared with that in the saline group. It was observed that the decreased number of PV-immunoreactive cells could disrupt synaptic transmission and elicit social deficits of ASD [4, 35]. Taken together, our findings implied that synaptic dysfunction of CR interneurons within the mPFC can lead to social behavior impairments.

Chemogenetic inhibition of mPFC CR interneurons induces social impairments in mice

To further determine decreased excitability of CR neurons was involved in social defects, we selectively inhibited mPFC CR interneurons and investigated effects on outcomes for social behaviors. Specifically, we bilaterally injected AAV carrying Cre-dependent hM4Di or a control virus carrying mCherry into the mPFC of CR-Cre mice (Fig. 8A,B). Histological examination confirmed the efficiency of hM4Di expression on CR neurons (89.13 ± 1.90% of CR-immunopositive interneurons that expressed hM4Di) (n = 8). 91.65 ± 1.72% of CR-expressing neurons were hM4Di positive, suggesting high specificity of viral targeting for CR neurons (n = 8, Fig. 8C). Whole-cell patch clamp recordings manifested that application of 5 μM CNO restrained the current injection-induced spike firing of mPFC CR neurons expressing hM4Di-mCherry, suggesting that the chemogenetic intervention could effectively suppress those interneurons (Fig. 8D).

Next, we carried out the three-chamber social interaction test 30 min following CNO administration (Fig. 8E). As shown in Fig. 8F-H, in sociability test mice expressing mCherry exhibited a remarkable increase in the time spent in the chamber containing an unfamiliar mouse as compared to the novel object (S1 = 333.18 ± 6.75 s, n = 20; NO = 157.19 ± 6.76 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). The hM4Di-treated animals showed a striking enhancement in the amount of time taken to explore the novel object relative to the unfamiliar animal (S1 = 205.39 ± 4.76 s, n = 20; NO = 287.68 ± 3.07 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). Moreover, the AAV-mCherry injected mice spent more time sniffing the unfamiliar animal in comparison to the novel object (S1 = 218.16 ± 3.33 s, n = 20; NO = 83.83 ± 2.39 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). The hM4Di-expressing mice treated with CNO exhibited less interactions with the unfamiliar mouse compared with the unfamiliar object (S1: 92.14 ± 3.00 s, n = 20; NO: 192.36 ± 2.91 s, n = 20; p < 0.001). Mice microinjected with AAV-hM4Di exhibited a lower discrimination index than the control animals (mCherry = 0.44 ± 0.02%, n = 20; hM4Di = −0.35 ± 0.02%, n = 20; p < 0.001).

As illustrated in Fig. 8I, the AAV-mCherry-injected mice spent more time in the compartment of the unfamiliar animal compared to that of the familiar mouse during the social novelty preference phase (S1 = 149.99 ± 5.51 s, n = 20; S2 = 351.15 ± 4.23 s, n = 20; p < 0.001, Fig. 8J). No corresponding differences were observed in the amount of time spent exploring these two chambers in mice that received AAV-hM4Di-mCherry (S1 = 244.51 ± 5.17 s, n = 10; S2 = 239.25 ± 6.21 s, n = 20; p > 0.05, Fig. 8J). The AAV- mCherry-treated mice spent remarkably more time sniffing with a novel mouse relative to a familiar animal (S1 = 100.11 ± 2.74 s, n = 20; S2 = 200.27 ± 2.55 s, n = 20, p < 0.001, Fig. 8K). In contrast, mice administered with AAV-hM4Di spent a similar amount of time sniffing the novel mouse and the familiar animal (S1 = 142.02 ± 3.89 s, n = 20; S2 = 142.10 ± 4.08 s, n = 20, p > 0.05, Fig. 8K). The AAV-hM4Di mice had a substantially lower discrimination index than the control group (mCherry = 0.33 ± 0.02%, n = 20; hM4Di = −0.00 ± 0.03%, n = 20; p < 0.001, Fig. 8L). Overall, these data were in line with the findings of the sociability test, suggesting that chemogenetic silencing of CR neurons in vivo can lead to social novelty deficits.

Additionally, we conducted behavioral experiments to evaluate the anxiety levels of CR-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C). In the open-field test, hM4Di-treated mice exhibited significantly reduced center exploration time compared to mCherry-treated controls (mCherry = 55.24 ± 3.09 s, n = 8; hM4Di = 18.11 ± 1.53 s, n = 8; p < 0.001). The total distance traveled (a measure of locomotor activity) did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.05). These findings demonstrate that activity of CR interneurons play an important role in anxiety-like behaviors in mice.

About 87.31 ± 1.75% of CR-containing neurons were ChR2 positive, indicating high specificity of viral targeting for CR interneurons (n = 8, Fig. 9B,C). The functional expression of ChR2 was detected via whole-cell current-clamp technique. The 473 nm light stimuli (blue bars) evoked action potentials in infected CR interneurons (Fig. 9D).

Discussion

One important finding was that CR-Cre::Ai14 mice exposed to VPA on postnatal day 30 presented ASD-related behavioral phenotypes, as evidence of defects in sociability and social novelty preference. The number of mPFC CR interneurons declined in well-established ASD mouse models. The resulting hypoactivity of the CR neurons underscores a pivotal role in social interaction deficits. In addition, we have observed concurrent dendritic abnormalities of CR interneurons encompass less dendritic branching, reduced dendrite complexity, and lower spine density, but an increased density of long thin-shaped spines. Intriguingly, patch-clamp recordings revealed that VPA exposure suppressed intrinsic excitability by inhibiting spontaneous and evoked action potential discharge frequencies, accompanied by reduced glutamatergic and GABAergic synaptic transmission of CR interneurons. Notably, chemogenetic inhibition of mPFC CR excitability can elicit social deficits. These sociability impairments can be reversed by blue light illumination of CR-positive neurons. Hence, these results suggested that hypoactivity, abnormal dendritic morphogenesis, and reduced excitability correlated with impaired synaptic transmission of CR interneurons due to VPA exposure. The enhancement of social defects induced by optogenetically activating CR neurons. These findings may be a novel mechanism underlying the social behavioral dysfunction in ASD.

Epidemiological studies have verified that taking VPA during pregnancy increases the risk of ASD in offspring [36]. We used mice treated with VPA only once at E12.5 as a mouse model of ASD. It is most effective to mimic an aspect of the pathology of ASD in male animals [28]. The core symptoms of ASD include impaired social representation [37]. An overwhelming body of evidence revealed that prenatal VPA exposure caused social impairments in the mouse model of ASD [38–40]. With a three-chamber social behavior test, our investigation further demonstrated that CR-Cre::Ai14 mice treated with VPA exhibited impairments of sociability and preference for social novelty, which are key characteristics of ASD.

Convergent evidence suggests alterations in GABAergic signaling to ASD [4, 9, 41, 42]. The inhibitory interneurons play a vital role in its neural basis. Loss of inhibitory neurons has been detected in individuals with ASD [43]. Specifically, GAD65 and GAD67, the enzymes required for GABA synthesis, are altered in autistic patients [44]. Postmortem neuropathological studies of ASD have revealed reduced density of PV interneurons in the prefrontal cortex [8, 10]. The patients with ASD had a lower density of CR-containing interneurons in the caudate [17]. Of particular noteworthy, we ascertained that there was an indication of the diminishment of surviving CR-containing interneurons in the mPFC of VPA-treated animals. This finding is in agreement with the conclusion drawn from a study done in Triofl/fl;Dlx5/6-CIE mice [11]. PV knockout mice have social behavioral phenotypes similar to core ASD social symptoms [45, 46]. Accordingly, the present study manifests that decreased CR interneurons represent a principal neuron type that mediates the social dysfunction of ASD.

c-Fos has been utilized to identify neuronal activation of social behaviors [47]. In the sociality test, the c-Fos protein level was decreased in the mPFC of propionic acid-injected mice [48]. Immunohistology analysis revealed that neuronal activity is reduced in the prelimbic cortex of Prkn-deleted mice, serving as a reliable ASD model in the context of sociability [18]. Also, Cao et al. reported that decreased c-Fos-immunoreactive cells in the mPFC of Neuroligin3 R451C knockin mice with defects in social novelty preference [47]. Congruent with previous studies, the c-Fos/CR co-localization experiment demonstrated the down-regulation of the c-Fos in mPFC CR-positive GABAergic interneurons in VPA-exposed mice during each stage in the three-chamber test. Collectively, our findings support the view that reduced activation in the CR interneurons may underlie social impairments in ASD.

It was recognized that genetically encoded calcium fluorescent indicators allowed us to simultaneously record the activity of neural populations in mice with in vivo fiber photometry [49]. Further, we injected Cre-dependent AAV expressing GCaMP7s into the mPFC and implemented fiber photometry. We discovered a drastically augmentation in GCaMP7s fluorescence intensity of CR-positive cells that was elicited in saline-treated mice during two phases in the three-chamber test. In stark contrast, we did not detect the pronounced differences in the AUC and amplitude of calcium signals of CR-positive cells in mice with deficits in sociality and social novelty preference. In line with our results, Sun et al. showed that mPFC PV interneurons showed enhancement in calcium fluorescence signals when subject mice interacted with novel mice [50]. The identical moderate calcium transients were observed when mice with Auts2 knockout closely related to ASD investigated stranger 1 and stranger 2, which were closely social recognition deficits [19]. Therefore, it was conceivable that diminished CR interneuron activities in vivo participated in the social interaction defects.

It is well known that CR-containing interneurons contain extremely tortuous dendrites that bore spine-like appendages [27]. In this study, we found prenatal exposure to VPA triggered an abnormal development of basal dendrites in tdTomato-labeled neurons by confocal microscopy. The characteristics of dendritic abnormality consist of fewer branches, shorter total length of dendrites, and reduced dendritic complexity. Generally, aberrant dendrite morphology is a cellular phenotype in ASD, particularly an impaired dendritic arborization in cortical pyramidal neurons [22]. The decreases in the length and complexity of dendritic arborization in the PFC showed in many well-characterized mouse models of ASD [18, 31, 51]. The morphology of dendrites is crucial in receiving excitatory projections and cellular signal integration [20, 52]. The abnormalities observed following VPA treatment indicated insufficient integration of CR-positive cells and lower excitability, representing a social dysfunction development.

Dendritic spines are the primary sites of excitatory synapse formation and display a wide variety of shapes. The patological alterations correlated with ASD in the spine can be divided into two categories: abnormalities in spine density and distortions in spine morphology from human postmortem and experimental animal studies [21]. In particular, two-photon imaging of cortical neurons exhibited a delay in spine maturation in an ASD mouse model [53]. The decreased spine density with a lower proportion of mushroom spines in the anterior cingulate cortex of Shank3 mutant mice implicated social deficits [51]. Chen et al. reported that the density and volume of mushroom spines were remarkably lessened in Celsr2 mutant neurons, which was coupled with a proportional augmentation in the number of immature, long thin spines [54]. Consistently, we observed that with VPA treatment there was a significantly reduced dendritic spine density and mushroom spines, as well as an increase in thin spines of basal dendrites of CR interneurons. Mushroom spines have better synaptic efficacy than long thin spines owing to the positive correlation between spine size and synaptic strength [55]. Pathological changes in spines may trigger abnormal synaptic plasticity, resulting in core social features of ASD [56]. Following this line of reasoning, we conclude that VPA exposure is sufficient to induce social dysfunction as consequence of dendritic spine dysgeneses of CR neurons.

It was well-documented that dendritic spines play a critical role in the regulation of calcium near synapses [57]. We revealed that abnormal spine morphology of CR interneurons after VPA exposure correlated with altered the calcium activity of mPFC CR interneurons. Nicolini et al. reported down-regulation of the Akt/mTOR pathway in the fusiform gyrus of patients with ASD. This pathway critically regulated dendritic spine formation and function as well as synaptic plasticity, ultimately contributing to social behavioral deficits [21]. Future studies should determine whether down-regulation of the PI3K/ Akt/ mTOR pathway is involved in abnormal spine morphology of CR interneurons after the exposure to VPA.

We have known interneuron spine dynamics were critical in regulating interneuron excitation by their inputs [25]. The tight regulation of dendritic spine morphology is crucial for synaptic transmission [55]. We attempted to investigate whether intrinsic neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission of CR interneurons owing to aberrations in dendrite and spine morphogenesis are influenced. The present experiment demonstrated that a single prenatal exposure to VPA triggered a relatively low spontaneous firing rate from tdTomato-labeled interneurons in the cell-attached configuration. Moreover, we observed that VPA treatment was associated with decreased mPFC action potential discharge frequencies in response to depolarization inputs of CR neurons in slices via whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. VPA exposure also enhanced the threshold voltage for action potential firing and the minimal depolarizing current required to evoke action potential (rheobase). These changes inhibited CR-positive neurons’ excitability. In conjunction with our results, blunted neuronal excitability was observed in the genetically engineered and idiopathic ASD mouse models and strongly related to social behavioral disturbances [9, 31, 58].

Multiple lines of evidence implicate that disruptions in excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission in the mPFC impair social interaction, exacerbating the development of ASD [11, 35, 48]. For instance, pyramidal neurons exhibited hypoactivity caused by reduced excitatory synaptic transmission in Shank3-deficient mice [51]. The average frequency of mEPSC in the mPFC was attenuated in propionic acid-injected and FoxG1 haploinsufficient animals, which are related to ASD-like phenotypes in behavioral aberrations [43, 48]. Our research group recently uncovered that VPA exposure depressed AMPA-mediated mEPSCs in the nNOS interneurons of the basolateral amygdala [29]. Moreover, it confirmed that impaired mIPSC frequency in Triofl/fl; Dlx5/6-CIE and Srcap haploinsufficient mice contributed to ASD-like behavioral abnormalities, including impaired social interaction [3, 11]. In strong support, current observations revealed VPA-induced decreases in the mean frequency of mEPSCs and the frequency and amplitude of mIPSCs of CR-positive interneurons. Accumulating evidence indicated that impairments in synaptic transmission were considered an important mechanism underlying social behavioral defects [3, 32, 35]. Consequently, these results suggested the disruption of neuronal network activity due to perturbation in synaptic excitation and inhibition of CR interneurons, which is ultimately responsible for social impairments in the disease.

Of note, we validated that chemogenetic silencing of mPFC CR neurons disrupted social interactions. These VPA-induced deficits in sociability and social novelty can be rectified by optogenetic stimulation of CR interneurons in mice. In accordance with our results, Bicks et al., identified that chemogenetic activation of PV interneurons activity of dorsal-medial PFC in the adult animal alleviates social defects induced by juvenile isolation [59]. Likewise, another study showed that photoinhibition of mPFC somatostatin-expressing interneurons abolishes affective state discrimination. Synchronized photoactivation of somatostatin interneurons guides social discrimination [60]. Hence, our results reveal a momentous role for CR interneurons action in the mPFC in social behaviors. Optogenetic activation of CR neurons can mitigate social impairments in animal model of ASD.

Limitations

However, there are still the limitations in this study. The complex pathophysiology of ASD encompasses interactions between genetic and environmental factors [22]. The genetic knockout mice of ASD associated gene (e.g., Shank3 [61], FMR1[62], etc.) and other environmental models (e.g., prenatal stress) would provide more comprehensive understanding of the role of CR neurons in social defects, which needs to be explored in the future. Additionally, CR-positive cells target preferentially other GABAergic cells and also send projection to inhibit the pyramidal neurons in the neocortex [12]. Further investigations are required to elucidate that both feed-backward/feed-forward and inhibitions of CR interneurons are crucial, whose disturbances would trigger the inhibitory-excitatory imbalance and contribute to the pathophysiology in the corresponding neuronal network of ASD-like social impairments.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that prenatal exposure to VPA caused decreased activities and inhibited dendritic spine morphogenesis of CR-expressing cells in the mPFC. These interneurons were concomitantly about intrinsic hyperexcitability and impaired synaptic transmission. Activation of mPFC CR excitability can promote sociability in VPA-treated mice. Our findings showed that VPA induced morphological development and functional abnormalities of CR interneurons, deciphering a pathogenic mechanism underlying aberrations in social behaviors related to ASD.
