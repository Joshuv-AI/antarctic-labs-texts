Open surgical management of abdominal aortic aneurysm with duplicate right common iliac artery
Authors: Neha H. Shetty, Alex Rothstein, Krish Kondisetti, Katherine Reitz, Eric S. Hager
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A 69-year-old man with a history of smoking and hypertension was referred for evaluation of an enlarging abdominal aortic aneurysm. A computed tomography angiogram revealed a 5.7-cm infrarenal aneurysm and the presence of a duplicate right common iliac artery and absent hypogastric artery. Owing to the unusual anatomy of the iliac arteries and concerns about potential colorectal ischemia, an open surgical repair of the aneurysm was performed. The patient's recovery was uneventful, with normal perfusion to both lower extremities. This case is the first to report a duplicate right common iliac artery.

Anomalies of the iliac arteries are rare, and they are often found incidentally or secondary to ischemia.1 We present a case of duplicate right common iliac artery found incidentally during a workup for a 5.7-cm aortic aneurysm. The patient consented to the publication of this paper.

Case report

A 69-year-old man with a history of tobacco use and hypertension was referred for evaluation of a progressively enlarging abdominal aortic aneurysm. He had undergone computed tomographic angiography, which demonstrated the 5.7-cm aortic aneurysm, calcified but patent right common iliac artery, and what appeared to be a duplicate right common iliac artery that emerged from the posterior aorta and coursed over the pelvic brim, eventually joining the external iliac artery (Fig 1). Imaging also showed absence of bilateral internal iliac arteries. He had undergone an angiogram at an outside hospital before referral, which further demonstrated the aberrant anatomy (Fig 2). He did not suffer from buttock or thigh claudication and had a robust collateral network arising from the inferior mesenteric artery, and both profunda arteries appeared to supply the pelvis. Operative approach

In our midline, open approach, we used a standard technique to visualize the aortic aneurysm and iliac arteries. We mobilized the small bowel to the patient's right side and elevated the transverse colon. Mobilization of the duodenum exposed the retroperitoneum. We ligated all crossing lymphatics and the inferior mesenteric vein, which was tethered over the left common iliac aneurysm. After the ureter was identified and carefully retracted, the left common iliac aneurysm was dissected and encircled with a vessel loop. The right native calcified common iliac artery was ligated with 0 silk suture, because it was stenotic and was not a suitable target for the outflow anastomosis. The redundant right iliac artery was dissected out and encircled with a vessel loop (Fig 3). We noted very few collaterals arising from the aberrant artery as it coursed posterolateral in the retroperitoneum then medial over the pelvic brim, where it eventually anastomosed with the native iliac artery.Fig 3Intraoperative image of aneurysm sac displaced towards the patient's left side. The patient was then heparinized and an infrarenal aortic clamp was placed in standard fashion. A 16 × 8 mm bifurcated graft was selected and an anastomosis was performed to the left common iliac artery distal to the aneurysm. The proximal portion was then ligated with 2-0 Proline sutures. We performed an end-to-end anastomosis to the aberrant right iliac artery after ligating it proximally. This maneuver allowed the graft to lay relatively straight, and we could bypass the tortuous segment of the vessel. We then reimplanted the inferior mesenteric artery in the standard manner to preserve the robust collateral network to the pelvis and distal colon and rectum. After unclamping, our repair was hemostatic, and the patient had palpable femoral and DP pulses (Fig 4). His postoperative course was routine, and he was discharged on postoperative day 4. Discussion

To our knowledge, there are few documented cases of variations of the iliac arteries. This case report is the first documented case of a duplicate right common iliac artery and absence of internal iliac arteries. Existing case reports regarding iliac artery malformations predominantly discuss the absence or atresia of common, external, or internal iliac arteries. Several case reports describe congenital absence of unilateral1,2 or bilateral common iliac arteries,3,4 but not duplicate common iliac arteries. The only documented case of a duplication iliac arterial anomaly was a duplicate left external iliac artery incidentally found during ovarian cancer resection.5 This duplicate external iliac artery did not feed back to the dominant external iliac artery, but instead connected to the abdominal wall. There is no other documentation of duplicate iliac anatomy beyond this case study. Our patient's imaging also revealed the absence of bilateral internal iliac arteries, a congenial deformity previously documented.2

Furthermore, the patient presented with an aortic trifurcation which has been previously reported by Tamisier et al3; however, instead of duplicate iliac arteries,6 Tamisier et al described external and internal iliac arteries arising from the distal aorta owing to a lack of common iliac artery.

The patient's duplicate common iliac artery was found incidentally owing to computed tomographic angiography assessing the patient's abdominal aortic aneurysm anatomy before repair. Previously documented common iliac artery anomalies were also discovered incidentally secondary to other vascular problems, like chronic lower limb ischemia or genitourinary malformations.2 We elected an open repair as opposed to endovascular aneurysm repair as a precautionary measure owing to the complicated and novel iliac anatomy distal to the abdominal aortic aneurysm. It is unclear if the duplicate arteries contributed to the development of the patient's abdominal aortic aneurysm, because risk factors including hypertension and tobacco use were also present. The duplication of the iliac artery did not affect the procedure outcome, as evidenced by the expected normal perfusion in the lower extremities and postoperative scans. The patient also demonstrated no postoperative complications. The progression and future manifestation of the duplicate common iliac artery and abdominal aorta will be monitored over time, but there is little to no change expected in management or approach of this disease with this anatomical anomaly.

Conclusions

We have presented a patient with a duplicate right common iliac artery and abdominal aortic aneurysm requiring repair. Although several external iliac artery anomalies have been described in the literature previously, this case is the first to document a duplicate common iliac artery. The atypical anatomy prevented us from performing an endovascular aneurysm repair; instead, we performed an aortoiliac open repair. All of this was done without complications, leaving the patient with a well-perfused right lower limb. This case highlights the importance of individualized strategies tailored to each patient's unique anatomy.
