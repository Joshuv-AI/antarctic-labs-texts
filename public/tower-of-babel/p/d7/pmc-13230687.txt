Seal whisker-inspired fully printed MEMS flow sensor via micron-scale soft material additive manufacturing
Authors: Engincan Tekin, Ming Cao, Ajay Giri Prakash Kottapalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeals are renowned for their exceptional ability to hunt, a capability attributed to the uniquely evolved whisker morphology that suppresses vortex-induced vibrations and enables them to track hydrodynamic trails in the water. In this study, we present the first direct experimental comparison of replicated whisker geometries from harbor seals, gray seals, and sea lions, integrated into an artificial follicle–sinus complex flow sensor. A custom-formulated elastic resin was developed to address the limitations of current photocurable soft materials, enabling the fabrication of mechanically compliant structures with high spatial fidelity. All components, including biologically accurate whisker geometries and a soft, cavity-integrated FSC, were fabricated using projection micro-stereolithography, achieving sub-10 μm resolution in a single-step process. Piezoresistive sensing was achieved via capillary infusion of graphene nanoplatelet (GNP) ink into the 500 µm embedded channels with 20 µm corrugated layers within the FSC base. Static and dynamic characterization confirmed high strain sensitivity (GF = 16.57 in tension, 10.67 in compression) and stable performance over 3000 cycles, demonstrating suitability for both steady-state flow sensing and vortex characterization measurements. Comparative flow experiments further revealed that phocid seal whiskers (harbor and gray seals) suppress vortex-induced vibrations and provide superior signal-to-noise ratios compared to sea lion whiskers. This work lays the foundation for high-resolution, species-specific sensing technologies that can advance both biological understanding and engineered flow navigation systems. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Electronic properties and materials

Introduction

Flow-induced instabilities represent a broad class of unsteady fluid–structure interactions (FSI) in which asymmetries in the pressure field generate dynamic responses in immersed or streamlined bodies1,2. Controlling such effects, ranging from vortex-induced vibrations (VIV) to flow separation and acoustic noise, remains a persistent challenge in engineering design. In contrast, biological systems offer elegant solutions refined over millions of years of evolution for managing and exploiting unsteady flow conditions. Inspired by nature, biomimetic strategies have led to impactful FSI innovations: shark skin riblets have been adapted for drag reduction on aircraft3; owl-inspired wing geometries have inspired the design of noise-reducing turbine blades4; and the streamlined beak of the kingfisher bird guided the design of the Shinkansen bullet train’s nose to minimize aerodynamic pressure spikes5. Among many such biomimetic real-world fluid dynamics applications, the vibrissae (whiskers) of seals represent a highly specialized yet largely underutilized biomimetic inspiration for hydrodynamic trail tracking.

Whiskers have attracted considerable interest in biomimetic design due to their ability to convey rich vibrotactile and vibroacoustic information. In many mammals, including rats, otters, pinnipeds, and cats, whiskers (vibrissae) are fully developed at birth, whereas visual systems mature later, with eye opening occurring only after several postnatal days, reflecting the early functional importance of tactile sensing during neonatal stages before the onset of vision6. This developmental sequence has led to the hypothesis that whiskers may have evolved prior to visual perception, providing a critical sensory advantage that was further refined through evolution. Rats use their whiskers to navigate in cluttered and dark environments by extracting information about object texture, shape, and size, while cats rely on their whiskers to gauge spaces to identify whether their bodies can fit through narrow gaps7,8. Beyond tactile sensing, whiskers are also responsive to fluid motion, allowing animals to detect minute changes/movements in their surroundings by sensing air or water currents. Among all mammals, seal whiskers are highly specialized for hydrodynamic trail tracking and flow detection.

Seals have drawn considerable attention for their exceptional precision in long-distance prey tracking in deep and dark aquatic environments, relying solely on their whiskers9. Behavioral studies demonstrated that even when visual and auditory cues are eliminated, seals can detect and follow hydrodynamic trails left by a faraway moving object. This outstanding capability is attributed to the unique morphology of their vibrissae, which features an elliptical cross-section with undulations along both the major and minor axes (Fig. 1). Undulations on whiskers are characteristic of only a subset of pinniped species, and their geometry varies between species. For example, harbor seals and gray seals possess whiskers with pronounced undulatory morphology along the shaft, whereas sea lions have smooth, non-undulated vibrissae (Fig. 1c). In this work, we develop fully printed MEMS whisker sensors to comparatively investigate the hydrodynamic trail-tracking capabilities associated with the whisker morphologies of harbor seals, gray seals, and sea lions. The use of undulatory surfaces to suppress VIV had already been explored in engineering contexts in the late 1990s10–12. However, the detailed morphological analysis of seal whiskers revealed a naturally optimized geometry for this purpose. Specifically, the wavelength-to-diameter ratio (λ/D) of the undulations was found to be remarkably consistent, ranging between 4.4 and 4.6, across harbor seals and closely related phocid species such as gray seals13. In this context, λ is defined as the spatial period of the undulation along the whisker’s longitudinal axis, while D is defined as the average cross-sectional diameter measured. Flow visualization studies and numerical simulations have confirmed that this unique geometry disrupts the formation of coherent Kármán vortex streets, instead, generating complex, spatially distributed three-dimensional vortex structures14. Consequently, the amplitude of flow-induced vibrations is significantly reduced, allowing seals to sense wake-induced vibrations (WIV) of extremely small magnitude, such as those generated by fish trails. This provides a striking example of biologically evolved VIV suppression in a natural system15.

While the VIV suppression mechanism of seal whiskers has been well studied in the literature through numerical and experimental analysis, the quantification of WIV signal relative to VIV remains largely explored. Over the past decade, several research efforts have attempted to translate seal whisker morphology into artificial flow sensors. Kottapalli et al. fabricated true-scale whisker replicas through stereolithography and integrated them with piezoelectric (PZT) membranes to detect VIV signatures in controlled flow environments16. Zheng et al. introduced a piezoresistive flow sensor featuring a whisker geometry (scaled up by a factor of ten) and reported an improved signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compared to cylindrical reference structures17. Liu et al. further examined the VIV response of undulatory whisker designs under varying angles of attack and oscillatory flow conditions, employing piezoresistive sensing elements18. Xu et al. developed a six-times-scaled whisker structure with embedded piezoresistive components, demonstrating its ability to track trailing wakes generated by bluff bodies of varying shapes19.

Notably, all the earlier works to date have relied on simplified whisker models defined primarily by an idealized λ/D ratio, rather than replicating the full complexity of natural morphology. Despite the promising performance demonstrated by these prototypes, the seal whisker’s unique geometry, characterized by subtle phase-shifted undulatory variations and tapering profiles20 has yet to be fully understood, optimized and integrated into a sensing platform. In addition, while these studies represent significant progress in biomimetic flow sensing, challenges remain in areas such as the capability of fabricating reliable MEMS scale ultrasensitive mechanosensory whisker sensors that demonstrate seal whisker-inspired functionality in terms of achieving high sensitivity with low noise. In this work, we present the adaptation of high-resolution 3D scanned whiskers of harbor seals, gray seals and sea lions into a functional MEMS flow sensor, enabling the capture of species-specific flow interactions and offering a more accurate representation of their respective hydrodynamic sensing traits.

A seal whisker-inspired flow sensor typically comprises a whisker-like bluff body coupled with a mechanosensory sensing base capable of transducing mechanical stimuli. The realization of such a complex MEMS structure presents significant fabrication challenges. Conventional MEMS manufacturing methods, predominantly based on planar lithography and subtractive processes, are limited in reproducing the complicated, non-planar geometries characteristic of biomimetic whisker designs. Consequently, additive manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a promising alternative for such applications. Nonetheless, 3D printing at sub-micron resolution and integration of printed sensing elements to form functional MEMS sensors remains a complex fabrication challenge. Recent advancements in AM have introduced projection micro-stereolithography (PµSL), a high-resolution printing technique that achieves sub-10 μm feature sizes, fine surface finish, and sufficient throughput for prototyping and microscale functional devices. It combines the design freedom of AM with the printing resolution and precision required for MEMS fabrication, making it well-suited for manufacturing complex 3D structures, such as seal whiskers. Furthermore, it enables monolithic or intricately interfaced integration between the sensing base and bluff body, supporting more sophisticated and functionally compact sensor architectures.

PµSL 3D printers have been used for a wide range of microscale applications, including microneedle arrays for biomedical delivery21, microlenses for optical systems22, and microlattices for metamaterials23. It has also enabled the fabrication of biomimetic surfaces, such as Salvinia leaf structures24 and wheat-inspired geometries25. With its optically controlled layer-by-layer polymerization, PµSL supports the fabrication of geometrically complex designs featuring internal cavities, surface textures, and non-planar topologies. A major limitation of PµSL printing, however, is the range of printable materials, particularly for soft components. Commercially available elastic resins often exhibit low resolution and reduced elastic modulus compared to true elastomers, such as PDMS or TPU. As a result, PµSL is frequently used to produce rigid molds for subsequent casting, rather than directly printing soft functional components. Mold-based fabrication method26 introduces additional steps and complexity, ultimately constraining the technology’s applicability in monolithic soft device fabrication.

To realize a highly sensitive whisker-inspired piezoresistive sensor with a high gauge factor (GF), the fabricated structure must be coupled with a high-performance piezoresistive transduction material capable of converting mechanical deformation into a measurable electrical signal. This sensing material must exhibit repeatable dynamic loading performance, high strain sensitivity, mechanical flexibility, and compatibility with microscale PµSL printing. Conventional materials used in MEMS transducers, such as metal strain gauges or bulk PZTs, often fall short in either sensitivity or printability required for forming MEMS sensors. As a result, attention has increasingly turned toward advanced nanomaterials, particularly carbon-based materials, for their ability to fulfill these demanding requirements in soft and geometrically adaptive sensing systems.

Graphene, a carbon-based two-dimensional material with inherent piezoresistive properties, has been widely explored for use in high-strain sensing applications due to its exceptional mechanical flexibility, electrical conductivity, and surface-to-volume ratio. These attributes enable both high sensitivity and a broad dynamic range, making it well-suited for integration into deformation-based MEMS devices. Numerous studies have reported graphene-based strain sensors achieving GFs exceeding 10027, along with wide linear strain ranges and high SNRs, even under harsh environmental conditions28. These performance characteristics make graphene a compelling candidate for next-generation deformation-based MEMS sensors, particularly in applications requiring a synergy of mechanical compliance, miniaturization, and robust signal fidelity.

In this work, we present a fully printed, high-resolution biomimetic MEMS flow sensor that combines authentic biological whisker morphology, advanced micron-scale AM and graphene-based piezoresistive sensing materials. These printed MEMS sensors are experimentally tested to demonstrate whisker-inspired functionality in terms of robust VIV reduction and efficient wake-tracking under controlled flow conditions, while providing comparative insights into the evolved flow-sensing capabilities of three closely related species—harbor seals, gray seals and sea lions. For the first time, real seal whisker morphologies captured via high-resolution 3D scans were directly adapted into a functional flow sensing platform, moving beyond prior simplified λ/D-based models. High-resolution 3D scans of harbor seal, gray seal, and sea lion whiskers were digitally reconstructed and fabricated using PµSL. To complement the printed whisker geometries, a soft, cavity-integrated sensing base was also fabricated using PµSL, employing a custom-formulated resin blend designed to achieve low elastic modulus and high stretchability, while maintaining the high resolution afforded by the printing process. A piezoresistive sensing layer was applied to the base using GNP ink, selected for its high strain sensitivity and high dynamic range. The complete sensing system was characterized through linear bending, airflow, and water flow experiments, validating its mechanical and transduction performance. Finally, the sensor was evaluated across whisker geometries from different species to assess and compare their VIV response, SNR at varying source distances, and angular sensitivity, thereby demonstrating both species-specific flow-sensing behaviors.

Results and discussion

Fully printed MEMS biomimetic whisker sensor

The seal whisker-inspired sensing system was mimicked by dividing it into two components: a scanned and printed replicated whisker and an artificial MEMS follicle sinus complex (FSC). High-resolution 3D scans of harbor seal and gray seal whiskers were reconstructed as CAD models, whereas the sea lion whisker geometry was recreated based on cross-sectional measurements taken from multiple segments, as it lacks the complex undulatory morphology characteristic of phocid species whiskers. For each whisker, a basal segment capturing the undulations, but excluding the overall whisker curvature, was extracted and uniformly scaled up by a factor of three, resulting in a final length of ~80 mm. These scaled-up whisker models were printed in a single run in the horizontal orientation using the BMF microArch S240 printer, achieving prints with excellent morphological fidelity. Species-specific undulations and elliptical cross-sections were preserved with high accuracy, as confirmed by visual inspection and quantitative feature measurements (Fig. 2c, d).

In parallel, a soft, elastically compliant artificial follicle–sinus complex (FSC) was fabricated to serve as the sensor base (Fig. 2e). The complete set of FSC design dimensions is provided in Fig. S1. Despite recent advances, soft materials suitable for micron-scale resolution PµSL printing remain limited. Commercially available “soft” resins often fall short in comparison to established elastomers such as PDMS or TPU, especially in terms of elasticity and durability. Furthermore, formulations optimized for softness frequently suffer from low PµSL printing resolution, making them unsuitable for 3D printing micron-scale MEMS structures. To address this trade-off between softness and resolution, we developed a custom resin formulation by blending two commercial materials: PDMS-like resin (3DResyns) and UTL resin (Boston Micro Fabrication) in a 70:30 volume ratio. This mixture enabled high-resolution printing of mechanically compliant parts using a tuned print recipe compatible with the PμSL process. The FSC structure included a central groove on top to house the whisker part, internal microchannels for piezoresistive graphene ink infusion (Fig. 3a, b), and inlet features at the bottom for ink infusion and electrical interfacing. The FSCs exhibited excellent geometric fidelity, with no signs of delamination, warping, or print-induced defects (Fig. 2f). Notably, the internal microchannels were clearly defined without the need for support material, eliminating any post-processing steps and preserving micro-channel design integrity. This confirms the high precision of the PμSL fabrication process attained through parameter optimization and demonstrates that the custom resin provides sufficient structural stability throughout the fabrication and post-processing stages. Finally, the inner channels of the FSC were coated with GNP ink to form the piezoresistive sensing elements (Fig. 3a). The layer-by-layer nature of PuSL results in microscale surface roughness, which can facilitate capillary-driven liquid infiltration by improving surface wetting (Supplementary Video 1). After curing, each FSC unit was mounted onto a custom PCB to complete the sensor assembly (Fig. 2g–I and Supplementary Videos 2 and 3).

Static electromechanical characterization of artificial MEMS FSC

The length of the GNP piezoresistor was optimized based on three-dimensional Solid Mechanics finite element analysis conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 and experimental limitations such as the total build volume of the PµSL printer. The results of the simulation analysis are provided in supplementary information (Supplementary Fig. S3). To characterize the sensitivity of the piezoresistive MEMS artificial FSC, as well as to understand the electromechanical performance, GF characterization tests were performed. A point load with varying amplitude was applied to the FSC, and the corresponding resistance changes were monitored. The resistance change of each piezoresistor induced by tip displacement was measured individually as a voltage variation using a quarter-bridge Wheatstone bridge configuration. The calculated strain values were then correlated with the relative resistance change of the piezoresistors, and the resulting relationship is presented in Fig. 4a. Based on the slope of the output curves, the GFs of piezoresistor 1 and piezoresistor 2 were determined to be 16.57 (tension) and 10.67 (compression), respectively. These results confirm that the deposited piezoresistive GNP layer exhibits a stable and consistent electromechanical response under the applied strain. This demonstrates the suitability of the printed FSC MEMS sensor in acting as a mechanosensory base for further testing of the hydrodynamic flow performance of various whiskers.

Electromechanical durability of artificial FSC under cyclic load

To assess the electromechanical durability of the artificial FSC, the sensor was subjected to 3000 cycles of sinusoidal loading. A sinusoidal excitation signal was selected to demonstrate the repeatable functionality of the FSC, as it closely represents the sinusoidal cross-flow vibrations observed during vortex-induced vibration experiments. The corresponding voltage output was recorded using two piezoresistors connected in a half-bridge Wheatstone circuit. The experimental results are presented in Fig. 4b. The sensor maintained a clear periodic response throughout the test, confirming continuous electromechanical operation under repeated mechanical excitation. These findings indicate that the artificial FSC can sustain cyclic actuation while retaining its functional sensing capability.

Dynamic electromechanical response of artificial FSC

A central challenge was to develop a biomimetic MEMS whisker sensor sensitive enough to detect low-magnitude wake currents across varying frequencies. To assess the sensor’s dynamic response, the MEMS whisker sensor was stimulated with an oscillatory force applied at the tip of the attached whisker-like structure (8 cm long, 1 mm in diameter) (Fig. 4b). The results showed that the sensor can detect oscillatory motions with displacement amplitudes as small as 0.5 µm at 15 Hz (Fig. 4c) and oscillation frequencies as high as 70 Hz at a vibration amplitude of 0.5 µm (Fig. 4d). These findings indicate that the sensor is sufficiently sensitive to capture vortex-induced vibrations (VIV) and WIV under controlled flow conditions. The sensor’s performance as a steady-state flow velocity sensor in different media, including air and water, was also tested using the same whisker-like structure attached to the artificial FSC. In both wind tunnel and water tunnel tests, the sensor output variation was recorded as the wind and water flow velocities were varied, with each experiment comprising three cycles, including recovery phases.

Across both testing media, results demonstrated consistent output in every cycle, with an average standard deviation of 14 mV for the water tunnel experiments (Fig. 4e) and 19 mV for the wind tunnel experiments (Fig. 4f). An exponential increase in sensor output, corresponding to the drag force versus flow velocity, was observed, particularly during the deformation phase. However, the response during the recovery phase ultimately converged to the same output values at lower velocities, indicating the absence of nonlinear response or plastic deformation in the sensor material. Thus, the working capability of the artificial FSC in both high-density (water) and low-density (air) flow environments was successfully validated.

Comparative VIV performance analysis of various pinniped species whiskers using the MEMS FSC

Scaled-up versions of pinniped whiskers from three species—Phoca vitulina (harbor seal), Halichoerus grypus (gray seal), and Zalophus californianus (California sea lion) were fabricated using a PµSL printer. After scaling, the average characteristic diameters were ~2.0 mm for the harbor seal whisker, 1.72 mm for the gray seal whisker, and 2.7 mm for the sea lion whisker, respectively. The length of each whisker model was fixed at 8 cm to maintain consistency with the static and dynamic characterizations conducted on the biomimetic MEMS whisker sensors.

Individual whiskers, attached to the artificial MEMS FSC were tested under varying steady water flow velocities in a water tunnel with corresponding to Reynolds numbers ranging from ~140–700 (Fig. 5a). In the case of the harbor seal and gray seal whiskers, no distinct VIV peaks were observed at any velocity value within the range of 0.06 m/s and 0.32 m/s, supporting the validated hypothesis that phocid species have evolved whisker shapes that naturally suppress VIV (Supplementary Fig. S4). In contrast, tests conducted with the sea lion whisker exhibited distinct VIV peaks at different velocities. Although multiple VIV peaks were observed at different flow velocities, two specific velocity ranges, 0.08 m/s and 0.10 m/s, were selected for detailed discussion because of their closely spaced but distinguishable peak frequencies (Fig. 5c). The clearly discernable small shift between the two peaks further validates the accuracy of the experimental measurements. The experimentally measured VIV peak frequencies closely matched the theoretical predictions, with a discrepancy of <~4%, confirming the accuracy of the scaled models and experimental setup.

Signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) of different pinniped whiskers in wake-tracking

During active hunting, seals protract their whiskers forward, which is known to enhance the sensitivity of the whiskers29. This protraction aligns the whiskers so that the flow passes along their major axis, with the minor axis oriented in the cross-flow direction, corresponding to the configuration in which whisker VIV amplitudes are minimized. Minimizing self-induced VIV noise enables seals to detect biologically relevant WIV signals, such as those generated by prey, even when these signals are weak, thereby enhancing their hunting efficiency. In the experiments, a cylindrical bluff body was placed in the upstream flow to mimic the vortex streets generated by fish wakes (Fig. 5b). To facilitate comparison among the different pinniped whiskers, sensor outputs were quantified using the ratio of WIV to VIV amplitudes, expressed as SNR. In some cases, the VIV signal was too weak to produce a clear FFT peak; the average noise level around the expected VIV frequency was used as the noise amplitude in the SNR.

To experimentally compare the wake-tracking efficiency of whiskers from various seal species, each pinniped whisker (harbor seal, gray seal, sea lion) was positioned in the downstream flow with the minor axis oriented in the cross-flow direction. A cylindrical bluff body (1 cm diameter) was positioned upstream of the whisker at varying distances (8, 10, and 12 cm). SNR measurements were conducted at two different flow velocities (0.08 and 0.1 m/s), both within the laminar flow regime (Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Videos 4 and 5). MEMS FSC outputs were collected from a single piezoresistor, which is acquired as a voltage output through an external voltage divider circuit.

Experimental results show that the harbor and gray seal whiskers exhibited good sensitivity, maintaining higher SNR values even at the farthest measured distances from the bluff body. In contrast, in the case of the sea lion whisker, the VIV signal amplitude began to match the WIV amplitude at a cylinder-whisker distance of approximately 20 cm. These results validate the hypothesis that the undulatory morphology of a whisker enhances the tracking ability of the seal by suppressing VIV noise. Increasing flow velocity resulted in higher WIV amplitudes across all cases. However, the SNR increased only for the harbor and gray seal whiskers, as the VIV component remained less dominant relative to the background noise. An exception was observed in the 8 cm cylinder-whisker distance, where the lower velocity produced a higher SNR for the harbor and gray seal whiskers. This behavior is attributed to the stronger disturbance effects at higher velocities being more pronounced at shorter distances. Measured frequency peaks were found to be slightly lower than the theoretical predictions, primarily due to the variations in flow velocity characterization. In the experimental setup, flow velocity was estimated indirectly from the motor-driven water-tunnel propeller rotation speed. Additionally, the placement of objects within the tunnel, including the flow meter itself, introduced localized disturbances that affected velocity measurements. Consequently, an average error rate of ~9% was observed for the harbor seal whisker and 12% for the gray seal whisker. Considering the structural complexity of the whisker models, the challenges associated with experimental setup alignment, and other experimental factors, minor shifts from the theoretical frequency values are considered expected.

AoA variations on phocid species

For an elliptical cross-section bluff body, the relationship between the projected surface area facing the flow and the resulting VIV amplitude is well established. However, for phocid whiskers, the effect of Angle of Attack (AoA) on flow-induced vibrations is more complex. The undulatory surface morphology of the seal whisker reduces VIV by altering the formation of vortex-induced pressure fields, causing a spatial phase shift between the pressure peaks and the whisker body. This phase shift disrupts vortex formation along the whisker’s length. When the whisker is rotated in the flow, the projected surface area increases, but due to undulations on both axes maintain the same pressure field effect. To investigate this relationship, experiments were conducted by systematically varying the AoA of seal whiskers while maintaining a constant setup: a cylindrical bluff body (1 cm diameter) was positioned 15 cm upstream of the whisker, and a flow velocity of 0.15 m/s (Supplementary Video 6). The resulting vibration behavior was analyzed in terms of the WIV/VIV (SNR) amplitude ratio. The results showed that VIV signals began to appear for both phocid species starting from an AoA of π/8 (22.5 degrees). Beyond 3π/8 (67.5 degrees), VIV amplitudes became more dominant than WIV amplitudes, indicating a transition in the flow-structure interaction dynamics as the whisker orientation changed relative to the incoming flow (Fig. 5d).

Discussion

In this work, we developed a fully integrated, high-resolution biomimetic flow sensor inspired by the evolved morphology of pinniped whiskers. For the first time, high-fidelity 3D scans of actual harbor seal, gray seal, and sea lion whiskers were directly adapted into a functional sensor system, advancing beyond the simplified sinusoidal λ/D-based models commonly used in prior studies. These natural geometries were successfully reconstructed and fabricated using PμSL, preserving species-specific surface undulations and elliptical cross-sections with high morphological accuracy.

To complement the complex whisker geometries, a soft, cavity-integrated FSC was fabricated using a custom-formulated resin blend. The material composition and optimized PμSL printing parameters enabled the reliable formation of internal microchannels without the need for support structures, demonstrating the feasibility of combining elasticity, high resolution, and structural fidelity within a single fabrication process. A piezoresistive sensing element was incorporated by infusing GNP ink into the microfluidic channels of the FSC structure through self-spread capillary action, forming a compliant, high-sensitivity transduction interface.

The experimental results collectively demonstrate that the artificial FSC provides reliable electromechanical transduction under static, cyclic, and flow-dependent operating conditions. Static tip deflection experiments yielded GFs of 16.57 and 10.67, confirming that the printed GNP piezoresistors exhibit good strain sensitivity within the expected range. Cyclic loading measurements performed over 3000 cycles further showed that the device maintained a persistent periodic response under repeated actuation, indicating preserved sensing functionality over the tested cycle range. Dynamic response measurements identified an operating frequency window up to 70 Hz, highlighting the coupling between the structural dynamics of the FSC and its electrical readout. In wind and water tunnel experiments, conducted over flow velocity ranges of 3–11 m/s and 0.055–0.7 m/s, respectively, the sensor consistently detected flow-induced drag.

Comparative analysis of the WIV sensing performance of whiskers replicated from different pinniped species revealed clear differences in output SNR. Replicated harbor seal and gray seal whiskers exhibited SNRs ~5–10 times higher than those of replicated sea lion whiskers across different flow velocities and wake source distances. In contrast, the SNR of the sea lion whisker decreased to ~1 at the furthest tested distance of 20 cm, indicating the sensing limit under those conditions. Such differences are consistent with the known functional role of whisker geometry in modulating fluid structure interaction. Taken together, these findings validate the artificial FSC as a bioinspired sensing platform capable of resolving relevant flow features while capturing morphology-dependent sensing characteristics at biologically relevant conditions.

This work marks a significant advancement in bioinspired flow sensing by demonstrating the successful integration of biologically accurate whisker morphologies into a functional microscale sensor. Future research may explore the miniaturization of these structures into MEMS-scale arrays, integration of full whisker sets to investigate distributed sensing behavior, or adaptation of whisker-inspired morphologies into larger-scale systems for managing FSIs. The fabrication and sensing strategies presented here also provide a versatile platform for studying evolutionary design principles in flow sensing, bridging disciplines across biology, materials science, and engineering.

Materials and methods

Scanned and reconstructed seal whiskers

High-resolution 3D scan files of harbor seal and gray seal whiskers were obtained from the muzzles of deceased juvenile harbor seal (Phoca vitulina, age ≈1–2 years) and adult gray seal (Halichoerus grypus, age ≥5 years), collected at the Seal Rehabilitation and Research Centre, Pieterburen, The Netherlands. The whiskers were scanned using a high-resolution blue light scanning system (GOM ATOS III Triple Scan 8 M). The 3D scanning and reverse engineering processes were performed by TetraVision BV (Belgium), producing high-fidelity 3D models. A sea lion whisker was collected from a deceased animal at Wildlands (Emmen, The Netherlands), and its elliptical cross-sectional geometry was measured at multiple segments along its length. The geometry was reconstructed in SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes). For each species, a 26.6 mm section from the base of each whisker was extracted and scaled up 3× to yield a total length of ≈80 mm for each whisker sample. Scaled-up whiskers were fabricated using a BMF microArch S240 projection micro-stereolithography (PµSL) printer (Boston Micro Fabrication, USA) with HTL resin. Printing was performed in stitch mode with a layer thickness of 10 μm. Printing and post-treatment protocols recommended by BMF were followed.

Artificial FSC fabrication

The sensing base design comprised a total length of 14 mm, with an 11.5 mm long cantilever section of square cross-section (2 mm × 2 mm), and a 2.5 mm long base section measuring 7 mm × 6 mm. The base contained two 1 mm diameter circular inlets connected to 500 μm × 500 μm internal square channels, each with a length of ≈22.7 mm, oriented on the opposite sides of the cantilever. A rectangular groove (400 μm × 500 μm, depth ≈3.85 mm) was positioned centrally on the top surface of the base to accommodate the whisker root. The sensing base was fabricated using a custom-formulated elastic resin, prepared by mixing ‘PDMS-like’ resin (3DResyns, Spain) and ‘UTL’ resin (Boston Micro Fabrication) in a 70:30 volume ratio. Printing was performed with the BMF S240 printer in single mode using a 20μm layer thickness. Each layer was polymerized using [405 nm] UV light at an exposure intensity of [48.8 mW cm−2] for [1.6 s]. The printed part was washed with IPA; the inner channels were flushed with IPA using a syringe multiple times, and residual IPA was removed afterwards. Graphene ink was prepared by mixing GNPs from Graphene Supermarket and ethanol in a 20% weight ratio. Graphene ink was infused into the inner channels via a syringe needle and cured in an oven at 80 °C for 30 minutes. Then, the sensing base was connected to the inkjet printed PCB (BotFactory Inc.) with silver epoxy (EPO-TEK H20E from Epoxy Technology), and the silver epoxy was cured in an oven at 80 °C for 3 hours.

Finite-element structural analysis

COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 was used to perform a 3D Solid Mechanics stationary analysis. The FSC and whisker were modeled as linear elastic solids with user-defined material properties (Table S1). The FSC base was fully constrained, and a uniformly distributed load was applied to the whisker. A rectangular surface patch representing the piezoresistor was defined on the FSC, and its length was swept from 0.5 to 11.5 mm. For each length, the average axial strain within the patch was extracted from the parametric solution (Fig. S3).

Static characterization

For static characterization, a cylindrical body 8 cm long and 1 mm in diameter was printed and attached to the artificial FSC. A tensile tester (CellScale from Univert) with a sharp-tip attachment was prepared to contact the cylindrical bluff body tip perpendicularly. The tensile test was conducted under displacement control with a step size of 200 μm, up to a maximum displacement of 2 mm, repeated over 10 cycles. Displacement at the tip of the cylindrical body was converted to the displacement at the tip of the FSC, and the average hysteresis loop over 10 cycles was derived. Strain measurements were recorded via two piezoresistors connected in a half-bridge Wheatstone circuit, with a data acquisition rate of 100 Hz.

Dynamic frequency response characterization

For dynamic characterization, a cylindrical body (8 cm long, 1 mm in diameter) was attached to the artificial FSC. Oscillatory loading was supplied using a permanent magnet minishaker (model 4810, Brüel & Kjær, Denmark). The experimental setup comprised a function generator, a power amplifier, and an oscillating membrane attached to a stainless steel rod, with a stainless steel sphere (8 mm radius) mounted perpendicularly at the tip of the cylindrical body. The excitation voltage to the function generator could be adjusted and amplified by the user. With this setup, the sensor’s minimum displacement and maximum frequency responsivity were evaluated. Displacement amplitude at the sphere tip was calculated for each applied voltage, amplification magnitude, and signal frequency. One of the strain gauges was connected via a voltage divider circuit for data acquisition. Data acquisition rate was set to 500 Hz.

GF characterization

The sensitivity of the FSC device was evaluated by quasistatic tip loading experiments. A point load tip was mounted on a tensile testing machine to apply controlled mechanical loading to the sensor. The applied load produced a tip displacement, which generated tensile and compressive strain in the piezoresistors located on opposite sides of the structure. The resulting resistance changes were extracted from the output voltage of a quarter-bridge Wheatstone circuit. The circuit schematic and the experimental setup are provided in the Supplementary Information. The output voltage was acquired using a DAQ system. Each experiment was repeated three times to ensure repeatability. The longitudinal strain at the piezoresistor locations, taken as 400 µm below the surface, was calculated from the measured tip displacement. The GF of each piezoresistor was then obtained from the slope of the ΔR/R versus strain curve.

Durability test

To evaluate the durability of the FSC sensor, an 8 cm long whisker-like body was attached to the device and mechanically actuated using a minishaker. The minishaker was controlled by a function generator together with an external power supply. A spherical tip was mounted on the minishaker to provide controlled contact with the whisker-like body, as illustrated in Fig. 4b. During the test, a 70 Hz sinusoidal excitation signal was applied. The output responses of both piezoresistors were monitored through a half-bridge Wheatstone circuit, and the sensor was operated for 3000 cycles. The electrical signals were acquired using a DAQ system.

Flow field characterization

For airflow characterization experiments, a cylindrical bluff body (8 cm long, 1 mm in diameter) was attached to the artificial FSC. The FSC was positioned in the wind tunnel (WT 815235-E from Westenberg Engineering), with the strain gauges facing the flow direction. A flow meter was placed in line with the FSC to measure precise flow velocities during the experiment. Airflow velocity was increased from 3 m/s to 11 m/s in 1 m/s increments, consisting of 3 cycles, including a recovery cycle. At each velocity level, data were recorded for 1 minute per cycle. For water flow characterization, the same configuration was used in a water tunnel, placed in an inverted orientation. Water flow velocities ranged from 0.055 m/s to 0.7 m/s in 11 increments. At each velocity level, data were recorded for 1 minute across three cycles, including a recovery phase (decreasing velocity). Both strain gauges were connected to a Wheatstone bridge circuit in a half-bridge configuration. Data acquisition rate was set to 100 Hz.

VIV and WIV measurements

VIV and WIV experiments were conducted in the water tunnel. 3D-printed artificial pinniped whiskers (harbor seal, gray seal, sea lion) were attached to the artificial FSC, with the major axis of the whiskers aligned parallel to the strain gauges. The sensor was placed upside down in the water tunnel with flow coming along the major axis, mimicking the real-world seal tracking condition. For VIV experiments, each whisker sample was exposed to flow velocities of 0.08 m/s and 0.1 m/s for 3 minutes. Reynolds’ number varies between 140 and 240 in all VIV experiments. For WIV experiments, a 1 cm diameter cylindrical bluff body was placed upstream at varying distances from the whisker. (8–12–20 cm) At each distance and constant flow rate, data were recorded for 3 minutes. Experiments were repeated for 2 different velocities. (0.08m/s–0.1 m/s) For AOA experiments, artificial harbor and gray seal whiskers attached to the FSC were first positioned with the major axis facing the flow (AOA = 0°), while a cylindrical bluff body (1 cm diameter) was placed 15 cm upstream, and the flow rate was set to 0.15 m/s. Without changing the bluff body distance or flow rate, the AOA was sequentially adjusted to 0, π/8, π/4, 3π/8, and π/2. Data were recorded for 3 minutes at each interval. For the VIV and WIV experiments, one of the strain gauges was connected via a voltage divider circuit for data acquisition. Data acquisition rate was set to 500 Hz.
