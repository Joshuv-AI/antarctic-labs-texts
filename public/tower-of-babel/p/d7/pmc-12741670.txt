Glioblastoma Immunotherapy Adjuvants for Glial Cell Polarization Regulation
Authors: Jun Seo Park, Dong Geon Lee, Dal-Hee Min, Sung Joong Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) remains fatal despite maximal surgical resection, temozolomide chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Within the GBM microenvironment, tumor-educated microglia and astrocytes adopt immunoregulatory-like STAT3-high and ARG1/TGF-β-high phenotypes, respectively, which shield GBM cells from adaptive immune attack. In this review, we examine emerging adjuvant strategies designed to molecularly reprogram glial cells toward pro-inflammatory C3-high and IFN/NF-κB-high states, amplifying antitumor immunity. First, we summarize key aspects of GBM pathobiology and identify why conventional treatments fail to achieve durable control. Next, we dissect the signaling networks that govern glial phase states, including NF-κB, STAT3, IRF3, NLRP3, and cGAS-STING axes. We then provide a mechanism-centric analysis of pattern-recognition receptor (PRR) agonists, inflammasome modulators, and cyclic-dinucleotide STING agonists, integrating quantitative preclinical data with early clinical trial results. For each adjuvant, we distinguish between direct astrocytic engagement and indirect cytokine-mediated reprogramming. Modulation of glial phase states holds considerable promise for enhancing personalized vaccine efficacy and for converting immunologically “cold” GBM into a T cell-inflamed tumor. Consequently, targeting glial cell phase modulation is a highly attractive strategy for GBM immunotherapy, with the potential to maximize therapeutic benefit. Despite advances in chemo-, radio-, and checkpoint-blockade therapies, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) of GBM and its failure to establish memory immunity limit their impact. Tumor-polarized astrocytes and microglia form a barrier to effective T cell-mediated attack. Emerging evidence shows that redirecting glia toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes can recondition the TME, creating a more permissive landscape for immunotherapy. This review highlights glial phase reprogramming as a promising immunoadjuvant approach, emphasizing molecular circuits, synthetic modulators, and translational prospects.

INTRODUCTION

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most prevalent primary malignant brain tumor in adults and carries an exceptionally poor prognosis [1]. Despite active standard-of-care treatment, maximal surgical resection followed by focal radiotherapy plus concomitant and adjuvant temozolomide, median overall survival remains approximately 15 months, and long-term remission is exceedingly rare [1, 2]. Several factors underlie this dismal outcome. First, GBM cells invade extensively into adjacent brain parenchyma, migrating along white-matter tracts and perivascular spaces, preventing complete surgical resection [3]. Second, intratumoral heterogeneity is extreme: single-cell transcriptomic profiling has revealed coexisting subpopulations driven by distinct oncogenic events such as amplification of PDGFRA, activation of MET, loss of PTEN, or deletion of CDKN2A/B, each of which converges on core downstream signaling cascades (RTK–PI3K–AKT–mTOR and RAS–RAF–MAPK) to fuel rapid proliferation, invasion, and treatment resistance [4, 5]. Third, the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and its partially compromised counterpart, the blood–tumor barrier (BTB), severely limit delivery of systemic agents, especially large-molecule biologics and novel targeted therapies [6]. Finally, GBM is an immunologically “cold” tumor: it exhibits a low neoantigen burden and harbors an immunoregulatory microenvironment enriched in tumor-associated myeloid cells and reactive astrocytes [3]. Together, these factors explain why GBM continues to carry a dismal prognosis and imposes a high societal burden [1, 2].

Over the past decade, immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of several solid tumors; however, checkpoint inhibitors have provided minimal clinical benefit in GBM [2, 7]. One major reason is the deficiency of key chemokines such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, required to recruit cytotoxic T cells into the tumor parenchyma [2, 7]. Moreover, the GBM microenvironment is dominated by immunoregulatory cell types that actively inhibit T-cell activation, impair antigen presentation, and downregulate effector functions [3, 8]. Resident microglia, infiltrating macrophages, and reactive astrocytes collaborate to suppress pro-inflammatory signaling and secrete factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, reinforcing tumor immune evasion [2]. Without effective reprogramming of these myeloid and astrocytic populations, systemic immunotherapeutic approaches cannot overcome these local barriers [7]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify strategies that remodel the GBM microenvironment toward an immunogenic state, enabling T cells to penetrate and eradicate infiltrating tumor cells [1, 9].

Recent evidence suggests that re-educating glial cells may be the key to converting GBM from an immunologically inert lesion into one that can be effectively targeted by adaptive immunity [10, 11]. Microglia and astrocytes constitute a significant fraction of the cellular composition within GBM; the balance between their pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive “phase states” ultimately determines whether antitumor T-cell responses are supported or thwarted [8, 12]. By modulating signaling pathways within these glial cell populations, it may be possible to restore chemokine gradients, enhance antigen presentation, and amplify Type I interferon responses [8, 12]. Such interventions could collapse the immunosuppressive niche, promote T-cell infiltration, and synergize with existing checkpoint-blockade therapies [7].

In this review, we first outline the fundamental biology of GBM and explain why conventional therapies have been insufficient. We then discuss the rationale for targeting glial cells to overcome the unique immunological barriers of GBM, setting the stage for subsequent sections that describe specific mechanistic and therapeutic approaches.

MOLECULAR ARCHITECTURE OF GLIAL PHASE STATES

Glial phase regulation is driven by the activation of immune responses through various external signals that ultimately determine the functional phenotype of microglia and astrocytes [13]. Broadly speaking, glial polarization can be divided into pro-inflammatory states (microglial IFN/NF-κB-high states and astrocytic C3-high states; traditionally M1 and A1, respectively) and their immunoregulatory counterparts (microglial M2 and astrocytic STAT3-high states; traditionally M2 and A2) [13-15].

In microglia, the pro-inflammatory phenotype arises in response to pro-inflammatory stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or interferon-γ (IFN-γ). These cues induce a metabolic shift toward glycolysis and drive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and TNF-α, defining a “classical” phenotype that amplifies tissue damage and inflammation [15]. Conversely, microglia adopt an immunoregulatory phenotype under stimulation by anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13, which activate fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathways. Immunoregulatory microglia secrete IL-10 and Arg1 (rodent-biased marker) to promote tissue repair and combat inflammation [3, 15].

In astrocytes, the neurotoxic pro-inflammatory phenotype is induced by a triad of microglia-derived cytokines—IL-1α, TNF, and complement component C1q—which collectively drive overexpression of complement factors such as C3 [14]. Pro-inflammatory astrocytes disrupt synaptic function and contribute to neuronal toxicity [16]. By contrast, immunoregulatory astrocytes arise under ischemic or injury-repair conditions via STAT3 signaling. They upregulate genes such as S100A10 and Emp1, secrete neurotrophic factors, promote angiogenesis, and clear cellular debris to provide neuroprotection and support regeneration [14]. Each of these glial states is governed by distinct regulatory axes that coordinate the underlying cellular responses [13, 14] (Fig. 1).

Pattern-recognition receptor (PRR)–NF-κB inflammatory axis

Glial cells detect pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns via surface TLR2 and TLR4 or endosomal TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 receptors [13, 17, 18]. Upon ligand binding, these TLRs oligomerize their Toll/IL-1 receptor (TIR) domains and recruit the MyD88 adaptor complex, which includes IRAK4 and IRAK1 [17]. Autophosphorylation of IRAK kinases drives K63-linked ubiquitination of TRAF6, which activates TAK1 and IKKβ [13]. As a result, p65/p50 NF-κB dimers translocate to the nucleus, and JNK and p38 MAP kinases are activated [13]. Within one hour, transcription of pro-inflammatory genes such as TNF, IL-6, and Nos2 and early chemokines (e.g., Ccl2, Cxcl1) is markedly upregulated, shifting the cell into a pro-inflammatory phase [13, 17]. If NF-κB signaling persists, NLRP3 expression is primed; by contrast, transcription factors such as STAT3 and PPAR-γ can attenuate promoter accessibility at inflammatory loci to restrain excessive activation [19, 20].

Type I IFN axis

Cytosolic and endosomal nucleic acid sensors including cGAS–STING, TLR3, RIG-I, and MDA5 constitute the core of the Type I interferon pathway [13, 21, 22]. Activated sensors recruit TBK1 and IKKε kinases, which phosphorylate IRF3 and IRF7 on C-terminal serine residues [9, 23]. Once phosphorylated, IRF3 and IRF7 dimerize and translocate into the nucleus, driving a rapid, high-amplitude burst of IFN-β and IFN-α transcription within two hours [23, 24]. A subsequent wave of interferon-stimulated genes including Cxcl10, Ifit1, Tap1, and Tapbp enhances antigen processing and cross-presentation [24]. Typified by chemokine production, this axis recruits CXCR3+ effector T cells and reinforces an immunogenic environment [9, 23]. Negative regulators (e.g., SOCS1, USP18) quickly desensitize JAK–STAT signaling to prevent prolonged activation [25].

JAK–STAT3 immunosuppressive axis

Autocrine and paracrine cytokines, most notably IL-6, IL-10, and oncostatin-M, activate JAK1 and JAK2 kinases, leading to phosphorylation of STAT3 on Tyr705 [13, 26]. Dimerized STAT3 then binds GAS elements in DNA to upregulate immunosuppressive genes such as Il10, Vegfa, and Arg1 while simultaneously repressing pro-inflammatory transcripts and MHC-II expression [27, 28]. Beyond these transcriptional effects, STAT3 recruits histone deacetylases HDAC1 and HDAC3 to NF-κB-occupied promoters, imposing a tolerant chromatin state that further suppresses inflammation [28, 29]. Through these combined mechanisms, the JAK–STAT3 axis shifts glial cells into an immunoregulatory phase, upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators and dampening inflammatory gene expression [29, 30].

NLRP3 inflammasome axis

Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome requires two distinct steps: priming and activation. During priming, NF-κB-mediated transcription induces expression of NLRP3, IL-1β, and IL-18 genes [31, 32]. Subsequent activation cues such as potassium efflux, reactive oxygen species generation, or lysosomal damage trigger NLRP3 oligomerization with NIMA-related kinase 7 (NEK7) and adapter protein ASC [31]. The assembled inflammasome cleaves pro-caspase-1 into its active form, which then processes pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature cytokines [31, 33]. Release of IL-1β and IL-18 transiently amplifies Th1 polarization and drives an early pro-inflammatory shift [31, 33].

Metabolic-reprogramming axis

Pro-inflammatory glial activation stabilizes HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions or in response to succinate accumulation [34]. HIF-1α, together with mTORC1 activation, redirects carbon flux into aerobic glycolysis, supporting reactive nitrogen and oxygen species production as well as histone acetylation necessary for inflammatory gene transcription [34, 35]. In contrast, anti-inflammatory glia engage PPAR-γ and its coactivator PGC-1α to enhance CPT1A-dependent fatty acid import and mitochondrial β-oxidation [30]. This FAO/OXPHOS metabolic program sustains tissue repair and extracellular matrix remodeling [34, 35]. Cross-talk between pathways also exists: itaconate produced by IRG1 in pro-inflammatory cells alkylates KEAP1 to activate the NRF2 antioxidant program to restrain excessive ROS production [13, 30]. Additionally, lactate released from the TME can activate GPR81 on glia, which enhances PGC-1α activation and promotes an anti-inflammatory metabolic state [3].

Epigenetic modulators

Epigenetic regulators such as HDAC6 and the demethylase KDM6B, alongside microRNAs (e.g., miR-21, miR-155), determine the imprinting of transient signals into longer-term chromatin landscapes [36, 37]. Inhibition of HDAC6 leads to hyperacetylation of α-tubulin, accelerating endosomal trafficking and facilitating TLR movement, which can prime cells for heightened inflammatory responses [30, 36]. KDM6B removes repressive H3K27me3 marks, opening latent enhancers and amplifying inflammatory gene transcription when needed [37]. MicroRNAs further refine activation thresholds: miR-21 suppresses Pten and indirectly enhances STAT3 activity, while miR-155 reinforces NF-κB signaling loops [36]. Collectively, these epigenetic rheostats fine-tune activation thresholds and help lock glial cells into stable phase identities [37].

MICROGLIA-TARGETED ADJUVANTS

Microglia serve as the dominant innate sentinels within the GBM niche. Owing to their broad repertoire of PRRs and inflammasome components, microglia are very sensitive to canonical vaccine adjuvants. It has become evident that tumor rejection in GBM critically hinges not only on the direct cytotoxicity exerted by tumor cells themselves but also on the regulation and responses of microglia. Below, we discuss each adjuvant class in detail, emphasizing receptor engagement, signal transduction kinetics, downstream transcriptional reprogramming, and quantitative evidence from animal or clinical studies. Recent clinical/translational updates support this strategy: a randomized phase II trial of ATL-DC vaccination plus poly-ICLC/resiquimod showed interferon-dominant immune polarization with favorable safety [38], and an implantable FUS system (SonoCloud-9) enabled repeated, reversible BBB opening in recurrent GBM, a delivery advance relevant to microglia-targeted adjuvants [39] (Fig. 2).

TLR9 agonists: CpG ODN

At the molecular level, phosphorothioate-stabilized CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG ODNs) are internalized by microglia via scavenger receptor A1 (SRA1) and routed to UNC93B1-positive endolysosomes [40]. Within these acidic vesicles, cathepsin L/S and asparagine endopeptidase cleave the TLR9 ectodomain, generating an ~80 kDa C-terminal fragment that can recruit MyD88 [39]. In primary mouse microglia and RAW 264.7 cells, CpG stimulation peaks at 30~60 min for NF-κB p65 Ser 536 phosphorylation [41]. Four hours after Balb/c mouse i.c.v. injection with CpG-ODN, transcriptomic analyses revealed strong (>8-fold) up-regulation of IL-12β, IFN-α2, and Nos2. In addition, 22CpG-ODN 1668 (1 µM) has been confirmed as an effective activator of BV-2 microglial cells toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, inducing marked upregulation of TNF-α, IL-12p40, and NOS2 at the protein level, as verified by ELISA and chemical assays [24]. Moreover, dual targeting of TLR9 and STAT3 yielded similarly potent effects: administration of CpG-STAT3 dsASO (0.1~1 mg/kg) via intracerebral and intrathecal routes, followed by FACS and transcriptomic analyses, demonstrated successful induction of a pro-inflammatory shift in microglia. CpG-STAT3 dsASO enhanced Iba1-based markers of microglial activation and attenuated STAT3-dependent immunosuppressive signaling [29], indicating that concurrent inhibition of intracellular STAT3 and stimulation of TLR9 can reprogram microglia from an immunoregulatory to an activated state.

Although TLR9 is predominantly expressed in human B lymphocytes and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, it is also characteristically expressed by microglia in the central nervous system [39]. This suggests that other TLR9 agonists, despite lacking prior demonstration of microglia specificity, may likewise serve as microglia-targeted adjuvants. Indeed, systemic delivery of NanoCpG via intravenous or intraperitoneal injection has been reported to cross the BBB and provoke TLR9-mediated immune responses [25]. Direct intratumoral injection of CpG (20 µg) similarly elicited immune activation and tumor suppression [42]. In clinical GBM models, convection-enhanced delivery of CpG-28 (20 mg) was well tolerated without significant neurotoxicity and demonstrated potential as an immunotherapeutic target in glioblastoma [43]. Clinically, local CpG-ODN has shown feasibility but limited efficacy in phase II studies, underscoring the need for image-guided distribution and dose control during CED/intracavitary administration [44].

TLR3 agonists: Poly(I:C) and Poly-ICLC

TLR3 exclusively recognizes A-form double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) of at least 40 bp and signals through the TRIF adaptor. Upon dsRNA binding, the N-terminal RHIM of TRIF recruits RIP1 and TBK1; TBK1 then phosphorylates IRF3 at Ser396, promoting IRF3 dimerization and nuclear translocation, while RIP1–TRAF6 K63-linked ubiquitination of TAK1 activates NF-κB [17, 24]. Single-molecule FRET has demonstrated that TRIF assembles into left-handed helical filaments that scaffold TBK1 dimers, ensuring efficient signal transduction [17].

To enhance stability and potency, poly(I:C) is formulated as poly-ICLC by complexing with lysine and carboxymethylcellulose, conferring RNase resistance and enabling concurrent activation of cytosolic MDA5–MAVS pathways. In primary murine microglia, treatment with 5 µg/ml poly-ICLC induced a 200-fold increase in IFN-β mRNA within 3 hours and a 34-fold increase in CXCL10 protein by 8 hours, as measured by ELISA [39]. Chromatin immunoprecipitation further revealed IRF3 occupancy at the Cxcl10 promoter as early as 1 hour post-stimulation [45]. In an orthotopic GL261 glioma model, daily intratumoral injections of 50 µg poly-ICLC for five consecutive days achieved a 35% long-term cure rate; neutralization of CXCL10 abrogated CD8+ T-cell infiltration by 78% and abolished the associated survival benefit [46]. Mechanistic MRI studies demonstrated that poly-ICLC upregulates microglial iNOS, leading to nitric oxide–mediated vasodilation and normalization of aberrant tumor vasculature [45]. Moreover, interim results from an ongoing Phase I/II trial combining autologous tumor-lysate–pulsed dendritic cells with systemic poly-ICLC and pembrolizumab (NCT04201873) indicate a median nine-fold increase in serum CXCL10 by day 7 and expansion of neoantigen-specific TCR clonotypes in cerebrospinal fluid. Importantly, human randomized data support poly-ICLC as an immunologic primer in glioma vaccination, with IFN-response signatures tracking survival [39].

TLR7/8 agonists: imidazoquinolines (R848, 3M052)

TLR7 and TLR8 detect GU-rich single-stranded RNA within acidified endosomes [47]. Agonist binding induces a bivalent TLR8 homodimer that positions the TIR domains for MyD88 recruitment, assembling a 6:1 myddosome complex. This complex drives IRAK4/1 autophosphorylation, TRAF6 ubiquitination, and downstream activation of NF-κB and MAPK pathways [48, 49]. In primary microglia, 1 µg/ml R848 elicits peak TNF-α secretion at 6 hours and sustained IL-6 production through 24 hours [46]. Lipid-conjugated 3M052 embeds within endosomal membranes to create a depot effect, localizing cytokine release to the TME. In vivo, intravenous β-cyclodextrin nanoparticles carrying the TLR7/8 agonist R848 (CDNP-R848) reprogrammed the myeloid compartment in GL261 glioma—upregulating Nos2 and MHC II on TAMs and reducing immunosuppressive MDSCs—while microglial nanoparticle uptake was relatively low. In a separate perioperative model, controlled local release of resiquimod from an acetalated-dextran scaffold produced durable tumor control with ~50% long-term survivors in GL261 without checkpoint blockade [50]. Overall, formulation-controlled delivery of TLR7/8 agonists can push myeloid cells toward antigen-presenting phenotypes in GBM; direct synergy with PD-1 blockade in GL261 has not yet been demonstrated [51, 52].

TLR4 agonists: monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA)

MPLA, a detoxified Lipid A derivative lacking the 1-phosphate group, biases signaling toward the TLR4–TRIF adaptosome over MyD88. Following TLR4–MD2 dimerization, TRAM recruits TRIF, leading to TBK1-mediated IRF3 activation. Compared with LPS, MPLA induces minimal TNF-α but robust IFN-β production by microglia. However, clinical data remain limited; pairing MPLA with precise delivery and pharmacodynamic readouts (e.g., ISGs/CXCL10) may reduce the risk of first-in-brain studies [53, 54].

STING agonists: 2'3'-cGAMP, ADU-S100, and C100 micelles

Cytosolic double-stranded DNA activates cGAS to synthesize 2’3’-cGAMP. Ligand binding drives STING oligomerization and ER-to-Golgi trafficking, where palmitoylation at Cys88/91 is required for downstream signaling. The C-terminal tail of STING recruits TBK1 via its PLPLRT/SD motif; TBK1 then phosphorylates STING Ser366 to recruit IRF3 and phosphorylates IRF3 on activating C-terminal sites. In microglia, increases in p-TBK1 (Ser172) and p-IRF3 have been observed upon STING pathway activation, but a fixed 45-min peak is not generally established and depends on model/assay conditions. STING pathway activation can suppress YAP-driven programs in certain contexts (e.g., endothelium), whereas YAP/TAZ can conversely attenuate TBK1-dependent DNA sensing, indicating that the crosstalk is context-dependent. In GBM models, STING activation elicits innate and NK cell–mediated anti-tumor responses [9].

In an orthotopic GL261 glioma model, a single intratumoral dose of the synthetic cyclic-dinucleotide STING agonist ADU-S100 (50 µg) provoked a >120-fold increase in IFN-β within 2 hours that persisted for at least 24 hours. Mice that cleared their primary tumors resisted subsequent contralateral or metastatic rechallenge, indicating the induction of durable, STING-dependent immunological memory [9, 54]. C100 micelles—assembled from 8 kDa chitosan—form nanoscale micelles that improved intracranial delivery of cGAMP and increased IFN-β expression, driving a 20-fold increase in IFN-β. Co-administration of anti-PD-1 antibodies cured 67% of animals, and all cured mice rejected tumor rechallenge, confirming immunological memory [54]; see also in situ STING-activating hyaluronic-acid conjugates [55]. In the first-in-human dose-escalation trial of the STING agonist MIW815 (ADU-S100) in advanced solid tumors and lymphomas (NCT02675439), intratumoral delivery of 400 µg in combination with pembrolizumab produced a peak serum IFN-β concentration of ~180 pg/ml at 8 hours without any grade ≥3 neurological adverse events, demonstrating a favorable safety profile [56]. Across early trials, on-target cytokine induction has translated to limited clinical responses, prompting development of next-generation non-CDN agonists and targeted/systemic delivery formats to improve efficacy while avoiding T-cell stress [57, 58].

Inflammasome modulators: alum, QS-21, and chitosan nanofibers

Upon LPS-priming of isolated microglia followed by treatment with nigericin, monosodium urate (MSU) crystals, or alum crystals (Al(OH)3), expression of ASC and caspase-1 was detected under all conditions. With nigericin, there was robust secretion of IL-1β—a cleavage product of the NLRP3 inflammasome—together with detectable caspase-1 p20 fragments. Likewise, treatment with MSU or alum elicited pronounced IL-1β release concomitant with caspase-1 activation. These findings indicate that nigericin, MSU, and alum can serve as NLRP3-targeting adjuvants for modulating microglial phase transitions. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing of the central nervous system has demonstrated highly specific expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia [59, 60], a pattern consistent with cell-type transcriptome atlases [61]. Leveraging this specificity, several known NLRP3 activators have emerged as promising microglia-targeted adjuvants in glioblastoma immunotherapy. QS-21, a triterpenoid saponin, when co-administered with MPLA, was shown to induce strong release of IL-1β and IL-18, and QS-21 alone can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [62, 63]. Similarly, chitosan targets NLRP3 to promote downstream release of IL-1β and IL-18 [63]. Collectively, these agents represent effective adjuvants capable of activating the microglial NLRP3 inflammasome and driving a pro-inflammatory-like phenotype. Because sustained inflammasome signaling risks astrocytic hypertrophy/excitotoxicity, spatially-restricted, time-gated dosing (e.g., depot or rapidly cleared formulations) is recommended for CNS use [40].

ASTROCYTE PHASE MODULATION

Astrocytes, once considered passive support cells, actively shape the immune milieu within GBM. Their phase state is governed by direct PRR signaling and by cytokines derived from microglia and infiltrating T cells [13]. Consistent with contemporary consensus, we describe astrocyte reactivity using state-based labels (e.g., complement-C3–high, STAT3-linked, IFN-responsive) rather than the legacy A1/A2 dichotomy, reflecting a context-dependent continuum of reactive states [64, 65] (Fig. 3).

TLR3 agonists: Poly-ICLC

Among the PRRs, Toll-like receptor-3 (TLR3) is constitutively expressed in the endolysosomal and rough-endoplasmic-reticulum membranes of human and murine astrocytes. Engagement of this receptor with the double-stranded RNA mimetic poly-ICLC rapidly phosphorylates IRF3 and drives a pronounced transcriptional surge of antiviral chemokines such as CXCL10, CCL5, and ISG15, while simultaneously increasing the amounts of surface MHC class II and the co-stimulatory ligand ICOS-L, conferring antigen-presenting capacity on the astrocyte population [66]. The functional importance of this pathway has been demonstrated in vivo: conditional deletion of TLR3 in astrocytes markedly reduces local type I interferon production and diminishes the therapeutic benefit of poly-ICLC, underscoring the non-redundant contribution of astrocytic TLR3 signaling to antitumor immunity [67]. Translational support was shown in a randomized phase II study in malignant glioma in which ATL-DC vaccination combined with poly-ICLC (or resiquimod) was well tolerated and polarized peripheral immunity toward interferon-dominant programs; higher IFN-response signatures correlated with longer survival [39].

STING agonists: C100

Astrocytes also mount robust cyclic-GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS)–STING responses. Systemic administration of a hyaluronic-acid-conjugated STING agonist accumulates within GFAP-positive astrocytes within hours and initiates a canonical interferon-stimulated-gene program that is accompanied by enhanced expression of antigen-processing machinery and augmented infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells into the tumor margin [55]. However, early human trials of STING agonists—despite clear on-target cytokine induction—have shown limited objective responses to date, emphasizing the need for cell-selective delivery and dose windows that avoid T-cell dysfunction, as well as rational combinations with vaccination or checkpoint blockade [56-58].

Indirect cytokine-driven astrocyte conversion

Astrocyte reprogramming is further shaped by a cytokine relay originating from activated microglia. The triad of IL-1α, TNF-α, and complement component C1q is sufficient to induce the so-called pro-inflammatory, or neurotoxic, astrocyte phenotype characterized by up-regulation of C3, Serping1, and Ptx3 and down-regulation of synaptogenic factors such as Gpc4 and Gpc6 [68]. Neutralization of these cytokines abrogates the pro-inflammatory switch and limits effector-T-cell recruitment, illustrating the functional significance of indirect astrocyte polarization. Several adjuvants that primarily target microglia amplify this process. For example, the saponin QS-21 activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, precipitating caspase-1-dependent release of IL-1β and IL-18 [62]. Although this acute burst augments Th1-skewing, sustained inflammasome activity can provoke astrocytic hypertrophy and glutamate discharge, predisposing surrounding neurons to excitotoxic injury. Pharmacokinetic strategies that curtail systemic QS-21 exposure are being explored to dissociate therapeutic benefit from collateral damage. These considerations argue for spatially restricted and temporally controlled dosing strategies when deploying inflammasome-biased adjuvants in the CNS [40, 62].

Functional ramifications of the astrocytic pro-inflammatory shift

The functional ramifications of the pro-inflammatory shift are contrasting. On one hand, pro-inflammatory astrocytes secrete CXCR3-ligand chemokines that attract effector T cells and natural killer cells, upregulate antigen-presentation pathways, and liberate reactive nitrogen species and glutamate that disrupt tumor metabolic homeostasis. On the other hand, prolonged activation of this phenotype threatens neuronal integrity, emphasizing the need for spatially confined or temporally controlled interventions. Viewed through a state-based lens, transitions toward C3-high or IFN-responsive programs can transiently “heat up” the TME, but sustained C3-dominance risks neurotoxicity—reinforcing dose and schedule optimization as central design variables [64, 68]. Collectively, these observations position astrocytes as modulators rather than mere bystanders of innate and adaptive immune responses in glioblastoma, and they highlight astrocytic PRR and inflammasome pathways as tractable targets for next-generation glial-reprogramming adjuvants [62, 66, 67, 69]. In parallel, delivery platforms that improve brain exposure to adjuvants will be essential to translate these mechanistic levers into durable clinical benefit [40, 70]. However, the negative phase-III result with nivolumab in newly diagnosed MGMT-unmethylated GBM underscores the need for adjuvant-enabled strategies [71]. As a complementary axis, inhibition of the methyl-CpG reader MBD2 with the brain-penetrant small molecule KCC-07 has been reported to suppress growth of neural tumor models and to de-repress tumor-suppressive programs (p53/BAI1), producing an epigenetic “adjuvant” lever to reprogram glia–tumor interactions [72, 73].

Clinical and translational landscapes

Early clinical studies indicate that glial-reprogramming adjuvants can be administered safely to the human brain and elicit meaningful immune activation. CpG-ODN delivered directly into glioblastoma surgical cavities has been associated with prolonged patient survival and a shift of microglia/macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype [43]. Combining systemic double-stranded RNA agonists with dendritic-cell vaccination and checkpoint blockade further amplifies peripheral and intratumoral immune responses, often presenting radiographic signs consistent with immune cell infiltration [39]. Central nervous system delivery of STING agonists is also under investigation: first-in-human trials demonstrate robust type I interferon induction without severe neurotoxicity [56], and brain-penetrant formulations achieve durable antitumor immunity in pre-clinical models when paired with checkpoint inhibitors [54]. Although less advanced clinically, inflammasome-biased adjuvants such as saponin-based liposomes and alum scaffolds have shown the ability to boost T-helper-1 polarization without causing chronic neuroinflammation [62]. Toll-like-receptor-7/8 agonist depots likewise reprogram microglia toward an antigen-presenting phenotype and act synergistically with checkpoint therapy in murine glioma [51]. Parallel drug-delivery strategies—including self-assembling micelles, receptor-targeted polymers, and transient BBB opening with focused ultrasound—markedly improve intratumoral uptake while maintaining acceptable neurobehavioral profiles [6, 40, 70]. Collectively, these advances support the emerging feasibility of glial-phase–targeted immunotherapy and underpin ongoing early-phase trials testing combinations with checkpoint inhibitors, oncolytic viruses, and therapeutic vaccines. Recent data further strengthen this outlook: (i) a randomized phase II study of ATL-DC vaccination plus poly-ICLC/resiquimod confirmed safety and IFN-dominant polarization, with IFN-response signatures associating with longer survival [39]; (ii) the implantable SonoCloud-9 platform achieved repeated, reversible BBB opening with a favorable safety profile in recurrent GBM [40]; (iii) multi-lamellar RNA lipid-particle aggregates elicited rapid cytokine/chemokine induction and immune trafficking in an initial GBM experience [70]; and (iv) negative phase-III results with nivolumab in newly diagnosed GBM underscore the need for adjuvant-enabled combinations and biomarker-guided selection [71]. In addition, (v) an epigenetic reader approach using the brain-penetrant MBD2 inhibitor KCC-07, which reduced tumor growth and activated p53/BAI1 signaling in preclinical systems, suggests a rational combination partner for immune-stimulatory adjuvants [72, 73].

DISCUSSION

Immunotherapy has begun to erode the long-held view that GBM is fundamentally impervious to host immunity, yet responses remain imbalanced because the tumor conscripts resident microglia and astrocytes into immunoregulatory states that stifle T-cell entry and function [7, 11]. Redirecting these glia (microglia and astrocytes) toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes reframes GBM treatment from “forcing” lymphocytes across a hostile microenvironment to “rewiring” the microenvironment itself. Early clinical studies with CpG oligodeoxynucleotides, poly-ICLC, and intratumoral STING agonists demonstrate that such re-education is feasible, produces on-target type I interferon and CXCL9/10 bursts, and coincides with cytotoxic T-cell influx, all without prohibitive neurotoxicity [39, 43, 56]. These signals mirror pre-clinical results in which glial activation converts otherwise “cold” tumors into inflamed niches that are very sensitive to PD-1 blockade, PARP inhibition, or oncolytic virotherapy [29, 51, 54].

The chief obstacles are ensuring that potent innate agonists reach diffusely infiltrating margins, limiting neuro-excitotoxicity, and rapid detection of glial switch activation. Focused-ultrasound BBB opening and BBB-penetrant nanocarriers increase delivery but need greater cell-type precision [40, 66]. Time-gated formulations such as depot-forming imidazoquinolines and rapidly cleared QS-21 saponins aim to deliver brief, high-amplitude pulses that avoid chronic NF-κB or inflammasome stress, which otherwise provokes astrocytic hypertrophy and tolerance [51, 62]. Parallel development of serum or CSF pharmacodynamic read-outs—IFN-β spikes, CXCL10 surges, or extracellular-vesicle miR-155—should allow clinicians to titrate exposure before irreversible damage occurs [36, 43]. Crucially, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics reveal intricately interwoven immunoregulatory islands and rare inflammatory micro-domains [4, 9, 74]; biomarker-led stratification; for example, by baseline CXCL10 or STING-pathway methylation, will be essential to identify patients most likely to benefit [23, 58].

Moreover, because the immunoregulatory roles of microglia and astrocytes remain incompletely elucidated, additional targets for GBM therapy may be identified. For example, astrocytes have been shown to activate microglia via NF-κB–dependent secretion of granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), promoting a pro-inflammatory microglial phenotype. In addition, both astrocytes and microglia can reshape immune responses and the TME through metabolically driven epigenetic reprogramming. Such pathways represent promising glial cell–targeted adjuvant strategies in GBM treatment. In the central nervous system, targeting not only neurons and malignant cells directly but also the surrounding glial populations that modulate—and potentially amplify or suppress—neuroinflammation may prove highly effective.

Taken together, the emerging evidence positions glial-phase modulation as more than an adjunct to GBM immunotherapy—it is a fulcrum on which durable, systemic immune control may pivot. By aligning precision delivery with real-time biomarker feedback and mechanism-matched drug partners, the field is poised to transform GBM from an archetype of immune privilege into a cancer that can be held in check, and possibly cured, by a re-educated neural immune compartment [4, 66, 75, 76].
