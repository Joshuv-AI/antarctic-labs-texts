Cutaneous Adverse Drug Reactions Associated With BRAF and MEK Inhibitors: A Real‐World Analysis of WHO Pharmacovigilance Data
Authors: Natalia Sauer, Piotr Giedziun, Jacek Calik, Anna Wiela‐Hojeńska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBRAF inhibitors and MEK inhibitors (MEKi) have reshaped the treatment of BRAFV600‐mutant malignancies; however, cutaneous adverse drug reactions (ADRs) remain a frequent and clinically impactful toxicity. Although clinical trials provide insight into their safety profiles, real‐world data on dermatologic ADRs are limited. We conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis of the WHO VigiAccess database, examining individual case safety reports (ICSRs) for seven BRAF and MEKi up to May 2025. Disproportionality analyses (reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs)) were performed for high‐frequency dermatologic ADRs. Shannon entropy was used to assess the diversity of toxicity profiles across agents. Among 72,720 ICSRs, skin‐related ADRs accounted for 39.78% of reports with vemurafenib, 16.49% with dabrafenib, and 14.62% with encorafenib. Among MEKi, the proportion of skin‐related ADRs was highest for selumetinib (40.03%) and cobimetinib (34.31%). Rash was the predominant ADR across agents, but selumetinib demonstrated a significant disproportionality for dermatitis acneiform (ROR = 6.46, 95% CI [5.10, 8.18]). Photosensitivity reactions were most frequently reported with vemurafenib (11.31%) and cobimetinib (12.02%). Shannon entropy analysis identified two groups with differing ADR profile diversity: a higher‐diversity group (cobimetinib, H = 3.66; dabrafenib, H = 3.60) and a lower‐diversity group (trametinib, H = 3.51; binimetinib, H = 3.47); all cross‐group comparisons were statistically significant after Holm‐Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05). Chi‐squared tests confirmed significant differences in skin ADR frequencies among agents (BRAF inhibitors: χ 2(2) = 1393.21, p < 0.001, Cramér's V = 0.255; MEKi: χ 2(3) = 1129.77, p < 0.001, Cramér's V = 0.175), with effect sizes indicating clinical relevance. Cutaneous ADRs are a defining toxicity of MAPK pathway inhibitors, with substantial interagent variability in frequency and phenotype. Real‐world pharmacovigilance data underscore the necessity for agent‐specific dermatologic monitoring strategies. Clinical pharmacists play a pivotal role in early ADR detection and management, enhancing adherence and optimizing therapeutic outcomes.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

BRAF and MEK inhibitors (MEKi) are standard‐of‐care treatments for BRAFV600‐mutant malignancies, including melanoma, non‐small‐cell lung cancer, and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma. Cutaneous adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are their most frequent toxicity, with known patterns including rash, photosensitivity, and proliferative skin lesions. However, evidence from clinical trials is limited by strict eligibility criteria, short follow‐up, and underreporting of low‐grade events.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study examined the real‐world frequency, diversity, and disproportionality of dermatologic ADRs across seven BRAF and MEKi (vemurafenib, dabrafenib, encorafenib, trametinib, cobimetinib, binimetinib, selumetinib) using global spontaneous pharmacovigilance data from the WHO VigiAccess database.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Among 72,720 individual case safety reports, this analysis identified significant interagent variability in cutaneous ADR frequency and phenotype. Vemurafenib and cobimetinib showed the strongest disproportionality signals for skin‐related ADRs. Selumetinib had a distinctly acneiform‐predominant profile (ROR = 6.46 for dermatitis acneiform). Shannon entropy analysis revealed that cobimetinib and dabrafenib displayed significantly broader ADR diversity compared to trametinib and binimetinib.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

These findings support the development of agent‐specific dermatologic monitoring and management strategies rather than a class‐wide approach. Real‐world disproportionality data can guide clinical pharmacists and oncologists in anticipating drug‐specific skin toxicities, optimizing patient counseling, and informing treatment decisions to improve tolerability and adherence.

The development of targeted therapies against the MAPK pathway, particularly BRAF and MEK inhibitors (MEKi), has fundamentally transformed the management of BRAFV600‐mutant malignancies. BRAF inhibitors such as vemurafenib, dabrafenib, and encorafenib achieve high response rates and significant survival benefits in advanced cutaneous melanoma compared with conventional chemotherapy. 1 The addition of MEKi, including trametinib and cobimetinib, further improves outcomes by delaying resistance and mitigating paradoxical MAPK pathway activation. 2 As a result, combined BRAF–MEK inhibition has become the standard of care, leading to superior progression‐free and overall survival, with nearly one‐third of patients alive at 5 years when treated in the first‐line setting. 3 , 4 , 5

Although initially developed for metastatic melanoma, the clinical application of BRAF and MEKi has expanded to adjuvant settings and other BRAFV600‐mutant malignancies. Approximately 40–50% of melanomas harbor BRAFV600 mutations, 6 but meaningful clinical benefit has also been demonstrated in non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC) 7 and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma. 8 With increasing indications and longer treatment durations, a comprehensive understanding of toxicity profiles has become essential alongside assessment of antitumor efficacy.

Cutaneous ADRs represent the most frequent and clinically relevant toxicities associated with MAPK‐targeted therapies. 9 BRAF inhibitors are commonly linked to rash, photosensitivity, hyperkeratosis, and secondary skin neoplasms, including cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cuSCC), largely driven by paradoxical MAPK pathway activation in wild‐type keratinocytes. 10 , 11 , 12 Clinical trials have reported cSCC in up to 30% of patients treated with BRAF inhibitor monotherapy. 10 In contrast, MEKi substantially reduce the incidence of proliferative skin lesions but introduce a distinct spectrum of dermatologic toxicities, most notably acneiform rash, xerosis, folliculitis, mucosal inflammation, paronychia, and hair changes. 10 , 11 , 13 Although often classified as low grade, these reactions may persist, negatively affect quality of life, and necessitate dose modifications or treatment interruptions. 9

Although randomized clinical trials provide critical information on efficacy and common adverse events, their ability to capture the full spectrum, frequency, and severity of cutaneous toxicities is inherently limited. Strict eligibility criteria, controlled treatment conditions, relatively short follow‐up, and systematic underreporting of low‐grade or delayed reactions restrict the generalizability of trial‐based safety data. In contrast, spontaneous pharmacovigilance reports derived from routine clinical practice reflect a broader and more heterogeneous patient population, prolonged drug exposure, and real‐world patterns of toxicity. Such data are particularly valuable for identifying rare, severe, or unexpected adverse reactions, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), which have been reported predominantly with vemurafenib‐based regimens. 14 , 15 Moreover, real‐world data suggest clinically relevant differences between specific BRAF–MEK combinations, such as a higher risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions with vemurafenib plus cobimetinib and a greater burden of systemic toxicities, including pyrexia, with dabrafenib plus trametinib. 16

Given the expanding use of MAPK pathway inhibitors across tumor types and disease stages, there is a clear need for a detailed, agent‐specific characterization of dermatologic ADRs in routine clinical practice. Real‐world pharmacovigilance analyses can complement clinical trial data by elucidating the true burden, diversity, and disproportionality patterns of cutaneous toxicities, thereby supporting more informed risk assessment and management strategies.

The objective of this study was to provide a comprehensive real‐world evaluation of dermatologic ADRs associated with BRAF and MEKi using data from the WHO VigiAccess database. By analyzing spontaneous reports from global clinical practice, this study aimed to characterize the frequency and phenotypic diversity of cutaneous toxicities beyond controlled trial settings and to identify drug‐specific safety signals that may inform clinical monitoring and therapeutic decision‐making in patients with BRAFV600‐mutant malignancies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source and study design

This retrospective pharmacovigilance study used data from VigiAccess™, the public portal of VigiBase®, the WHO global database of individual case safety reports (ICSRs) maintained by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre. VigiBase collects anonymized ADR reports from > 130 national authorities and classifies them using MedDRA. The analysis focused on MAPK/ERK‐targeted therapies, specifically BRAF inhibitors (vemurafenib, dabrafenib, encorafenib) and MEKi (trametinib, cobimetinib, binimetinib, selumetinib), approved for BRAFV600‐mutant cancers including advanced melanoma, NSCLC, and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (Table 1 ).

Selection of ICSRs and eligibility criteria

Individual case safety reports (ICSRs) associated with the drugs of interest were retrieved from the WHO VigiAccess database. VigiAccess provides aggregated pharmacovigilance data and does not allow differentiation between suspected, interacting, or concomitant drug roles at the individual report level. Therefore, all ADR reports publicly attributed to each drug were included in the analysis, in line with established VigiAccess‐based pharmacovigilance studies. Reports with missing or unspecified sex were excluded from sex‐stratified analyses but retained for overall frequency and disproportionality analyses, as sex information is not required for signal detection in spontaneous reporting databases.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the frequency and proportion of dermatologic ADRs for each drug, expressed relative to the total number of reported ADRs. Dermatologic Preferred Terms (PTs) were compared across agents, and disproportionality analyses were performed using ROR and PRR. For disproportionality analyses, a two‐by‐two contingency table approach was applied, where:

a represents the number of reports for the specific cutaneous ADR of interest for the drug,

a represents the number of reports for the specific cutaneous ADR of interest for the drug,

b represents the number of all other ADRs reported for the same drug,

b represents the number of all other ADRs reported for the same drug,

c represents the number of reports of the same ADR for all other drugs,

c represents the number of reports of the same ADR for all other drugs,

d represents the number of all other ADRs reported for all other drugs.

d represents the number of all other ADRs reported for all other drugs.

The PRR was defined as the proportion of a specific ADR among all ADRs reported for the drug of interest divided by the proportion of the same ADR among all ADRs reported for other drugs.

Preferred terms (PTs) are standardized medical terms within the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), each representing a single clinical concept (e.g., rash, pruritus, alopecia), enabling consistent coding and comparison of adverse events across pharmacovigilance datasets.

Both ROR and PRR values were presented with 95% CIs, estimated using standard logarithmic transformations and error propagation methods. Disproportionality was considered statistically significant if the point estimate exceeded 1.0 and the lower bound of the 95% CI was also > 1. Confidence intervals were calculated using the Woolf method (Wald‐type intervals on the log‐transformed scale).

To assess the robustness of disproportionality findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using three complementary approaches:

(1)within‐class comparisons, where each BRAF inhibitor was compared against other BRAF inhibitors and each MEKi against other MEKi;

within‐class comparisons, where each BRAF inhibitor was compared against other BRAF inhibitors and each MEKi against other MEKi;

(2)pairwise comparisons using dabrafenib as reference (largest BRAF inhibitor dataset, n = 20,067);

pairwise comparisons using dabrafenib as reference (largest BRAF inhibitor dataset, n = 20,067);

(3)pairwise comparisons using trametinib as reference (largest MEKi dataset, n = 22,076).

pairwise comparisons using trametinib as reference (largest MEKi dataset, n = 22,076).

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12) with NumPy (version 2.3), SciPy (version 1.16), and pandas (version 2.3). A Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes for chi‐squared tests were quantified using Cramér's V.

Disproportionality analyses were performed using aggregated reporting counts provided by VigiAccess, consistent with signal detection methodology applied to publicly available pharmacovigilance datasets.

Shannon entropy analysis

To quantify the distributional complexity of dermatologic adverse events (AEs), Shannon entropy was calculated for each drug. The analysis was based on the relative frequency of 20 high‐prevalence skin and subcutaneous tissue ADRs extracted from VigiAccess. RESULTS

As of May 18, 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) VigiAccess database contained a total of 72,720 individual case safety reports (ICSRs) related to targeted therapies inhibiting the MAPK pathway. Of these, 39,085 reports (53.76%) were associated with three BRAF inhibitors—vemurafenib (n = 10,577; 14.55%), dabrafenib (n = 20,067; 27.60%), and encorafenib (n = 8441; 11.61%)—whereas 33,635 reports (46.24%) concerned four MEKi—trametinib (n = 22,076; 30.36%), cobimetinib (n = 3250; 4.47%), binimetinib (n = 6,568; 9.03%), and selumetinib (n = 1741; 2.39%) (see Table 2 ).

Distribution of adverse drug reaction (ADR) reports for BRAF and MEK inhibitors by sex, age group, continent, and year

After excluding 8169 reports with unknown sex, 64,551 reports remained for analysis. Among them, 30,505 (47.3%) were from female patients and 34,046 (52.7%) from male patients, yielding a female‐to‐male ratio of 0.90:1. A chi‐square test revealed that this distribution significantly differed from the expected 1:1 ratio (χ 2(6, N = 64,551) = 103.79, p < 0.001); however, the effect size was negligible (Cramér's V = 0.040), suggesting this difference likely reflects the male predominance in melanoma incidence rather than drug‐specific reporting patterns.

Age distribution analysis (excluding unspecified ages) showed that ADRs for MEKi were mainly reported in patients aged 45–64 (24–25%) and 65–74 years (14–19%), with 9–15% in those ≥ 75 and <2% in pediatric patients. Selumetinib was the sole MEKi that displayed a pediatric‐biased profile, where pediatrics comprised 38% of reports in patients. Chi‐square tests confirmed significant inter‐drug differences (MEKi: χ 2(8, N = 15,525) = 389.01, p < 0.001, Cramér's V = 0.158; BRAFi: χ 2(12, N = 24,914) = 10,194.67, p < 0.001, Cramér's V = 0.640).

Geographically, most reports originated from the Americas (36–60%), followed by Europe (34–54%) and Asia (1–10%), with minimal contributions from Africa and Oceania. Selumetinib had the highest proportion from Europe (54%), while vemurafenib and binimetinib were most often reported in the Americas.

Temporally, ADR reporting increased between 2018 and 2024, with dabrafenib and trametinib peaking earlier, and encorafenib and selumetinib showing the steepest recent growth, consistent with evolving approvals and clinical uptake (Figure 1 ).

Distribution of 20 SOCs

Analysis of ADRs for three BRAF inhibitors and four MEKi revealed substantial variability across system organ classes (SOCs). General, gastrointestinal, and skin disorders predominated but their relative proportions differed by class (Table S2 ).

Chi‐squared testing confirmed significant class‐specific differences in SOC distribution, χ 2(19, N = 72,720) = 426.07, p < 0.001, Cramér's V = 0.077, underscoring the need for tailored pharmacovigilance and clinician awareness when selecting targeted therapies.

To further visualize drug‐specific differences, a heatmap was constructed using normalized ADR proportions across 20 SOCs. Hierarchical differences became apparent across molecular classes and individual compounds (Figure 2 ). Notably, selumetinib and binimetinib displayed the most concentrated SOC profiles, as quantified by the Shannon entropy index (H = 2.36 and 2.42, respectively), showing a more focused toxicity pattern. In contrast, dabrafenib and trametinib exhibited higher entropy values (H = 2.74 and 2.79), reflecting a broader distribution of ADRs across SOCs.

Distribution and frequency of skin and subcutaneous tissue‐related adverse drug reactions

Analysis of WHO VigiAccess data confirmed cutaneous ADRs as one of the major categories of reported adverse events for both BRAF and MEKi (Table S2 ). In the BRAF inhibitor group (vemurafenib, dabrafenib, and encorafenib), rash was the predominant cutaneous ADR across agents (39.4% with vemurafenib, 36.2% with dabrafenib, and 33.1% with encorafenib). Vemurafenib additionally showed higher rates of photosensitivity (11.3%) and DRESS (3.4%). Other common reactions included pruritus, alopecia, dry skin, and erythema; alopecia was most frequent with vemurafenib (12.8%) and least with dabrafenib (5.5%). Dabrafenib and encorafenib displayed similar distributions for several less common ADRs (e.g., urticaria, panniculitis, acne).

Among MEKi (trametinib, cobimetinib, binimetinib, and selumetinib), rash accounted for a higher proportion of ADRs for trametinib (41.1%), cobimetinib (34.6%), and binimetinib (34.4%). Selumetinib exhibited a distinct profile, with acneiform dermatitis (27.3%) exceeding rash (20.9%), and showed higher alopecia and hair color changes. Cobimetinib was associated with increased photosensitivity (12%).

Across all seven agents, rash occurred in 35.2% of cases and alopecia in 8.5%. Photosensitivity clustered with vemurafenib and cobimetinib. A chi‐squared test confirmed significant differences in rash incidence across drugs, χ 2(6) = 120.70, p < 0.001. Clopper–Pearson 95% CIs supported these differences, with no overlap between trametinib (39.5–42.6%) and selumetinib (18.0–24.2%). Pairwise Z‐tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.00238) showed significant differences between trametinib and four agents (dabrafenib, binimetinib, cobimetinib, and selumetinib) and between selumetinib and all six others (p < 0.002 each).

These results confirm both shared and agent‐specific dermatologic risk profiles, emphasizing the need for personalized monitoring strategies (Table 3 ).

Disproportionality analysis based on skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders

To comprehensively characterize the dermatologic safety profile of MAPK pathway inhibitors, a disproportionality analysis was conducted across seven agents: the BRAF inhibitors—vemurafenib (n = 4208), dabrafenib (n = 3308), and encorafenib (n = 1234) and the MEKi—trametinib (n = 4055), cobimetinib (n = 1,115), binimetinib (n = 924), and selumetinib (n = 697). ROR was calculated for high‐frequency cutaneous adverse events (Table 4 ) and complemented by a Shannon entropy analysis to quantify the distributional diversity of dermatologic toxicity for each agent.

Among MEKi, rash remained the most common AE, with trametinib showing the highest count (n = 1665, ROR = 1.57, 95% CI [1.45–1.70], χ 2 = 168.1, p < 0.001). For pruritus, binimetinib displayed a strong signal (n = 131, ROR = 1.99, 95% CI [1.59–2.50], χ 2 = 39.7, p < 0.001), whereas trametinib (n = 364, ROR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.83–1.08]) and selumetinib (n = 68, ROR = 0.94, 95% CI [0.72–1.21]) were neutral. Cobimetinib was associated with reduced reporting (n = 65, ROR = 0.54, 95% CI [0.42–0.70]).

Selumetinib showed the strongest signals for acne (n = 89, ROR = 2.50, 95% CI [1.96–3.18], χ 2 = 69.2, p < 0.001) and dermatitis acneiform (n = 190, ROR = 6.46, 95% CI [5.10–8.18], χ 2 = 184.3, p < 0.001), whereas trametinib (n = 240, ROR = 1.16; n = 194, ROR = 0.46), binimetinib (n = 24, ROR = 0.46; n = 31, ROR = 0.48), and cobimetinib (n = 31, ROR = 0.41; n = 56, ROR = 0.64) showed neutral or suppressed signals.

Among BRAF inhibitors, vemurafenib showed a modest but significant rash signal (ROR = 1.19, 95% CI [1.09–1.30], χ 2 = 15.4, p < 0.001), whereas dabrafenib (ROR = 0.93, 95% CI [0.85–1.02]) and encorafenib (ROR = 0.79, 95% CI [0.70–0.90], χ 2 = 13.1, p < 0.001) were lower.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the disproportionality signals. Within‐class comparisons yielded consistent findings: vemurafenib (ROR = 3.49, 95% CI [3.32, 3.67]), cobimetinib (ROR = 2.27, 95% CI [2.10, 2.46]), and selumetinib (ROR = 2.83, 95% CI [2.56, 3.12]) maintained elevated signals regardless of comparator selection. Pairwise analyses against dabrafenib and trametinib as references produced similar results (Table S1 ), supporting the reliability of the primary findings.

Shannon entropy analysis revealed two distinct groups: a higher diversity group comprising cobimetinib (H = 3.66) and dabrafenib (H = 3.60) and a lower diversity group comprising trametinib (H = 3.51) and binimetinib (H = 3.47). Selumetinib (H = 3.58) fell between these groups. All four cross‐group pairwise comparisons were statistically significant after Holm‐Bonferroni correction: dabrafenib vs. trametinib (Δ = 0.11, z = 3.03, p = 0.002), dabrafenib vs. binimetinib (Δ = 0.15, z = 2.41, P = 0.016), cobimetinib vs. trametinib (Δ = 0.11, z = 2.30, p = 0.022), and cobimetinib vs. binimetinib (Δ = 0.14, z = 2.12, p = 0.034). These entropy values represent 80–85% of theoretical maximum diversity (H_max = 4.32 bits), indicating moderately diverse profiles across all agents, with the higher diversity group showing significantly broader ADR spectra (Figure S1 ).

Taken together, these data show that dermatologic toxicity profiles vary substantially between MAPK inhibitors, not only in frequency and disproportionality but also in overall complexity and heterogeneity. This may reflect drug‐specific pharmacodynamics and tissue selectivity, with potential implications for patient management and AE monitoring.

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world pharmacovigilance analysis of WHO VigiAccess data, cutaneous ADRs emerged as a dominant toxicity across both BRAF and MEKi, with substantial interagent variability in frequency, phenotype, and overall diversity. Vemurafenib and selumetinib demonstrated the highest dermatologic burden and strongest disproportionality signals, whereas dabrafenib, encorafenib, trametinib, and binimetinib showed comparatively more favorable cutaneous safety profiles. Rash remained the most frequently reported event across all agents, but distinct drug‐specific patterns were observed, including proliferative lesions with vemurafenib and acneiform dermatitis with selumetinib.

Targeted inhibition of the MAPK pathway is well known to produce characteristic cutaneous toxicity profiles. Previous clinical trials and observational studies have shown that BRAF inhibitors (BRAFi), particularly vemurafenib and dabrafenib, commonly induce rash, photosensitivity, and proliferative skin lesions. 17 Vemurafenib‐associated cuSCCs and keratoacanthomas have been attributed to paradoxical MAPK pathway activation in RAS‐mutant keratinocytes, 18 with secondary skin neoplasms reported in 19–26% of treated patients. 19 Real‐world cohort data further suggest a high overall burden of skin toxicity, affecting up to 87% of patients receiving vemurafenib–cobimetinib combination therapy. 3 This high incidence is not directly comparable to pharmacovigilance‐derived reporting proportions because VigiAccess (a public interface to VigiBase) does not provide patient‐level exposure data (including concomitant therapies) nor a denominator, and spontaneous reports preferentially capture clinically salient, unexpected, or treatment‐modifying events while under‐ascertaining common low‐grade toxicities routinely recorded in prospective cohorts.

In contrast, MEKi are primarily associated with inflammatory cutaneous reactions, including papulopustular rash, xerosis, folliculitis, and paronychia, whereas squamoproliferative lesions are rare. 20 , 21 , 22 Acneiform eruptions have been reported in 14–77% of patients treated with MEKi, particularly trametinib and selumetinib. 20 , 21 , 22

Our findings are largely consistent with these observations but extend them by quantifying drug‐specific differences in a large global pharmacovigilance dataset. Vemurafenib accounted for approximately 40% of all reported ADRs involving the skin, compared with 16% for dabrafenib and 15% for encorafenib. Among MEKi, cobimetinib and selumetinib showed the highest dermatologic burden (~34% and ~40%, respectively), whereas trametinib and binimetinib were associated with lower proportions of skin‐related reports (~18% and ~14%). Although rash predominated across all agents (~33–41%), selumetinib displayed a pronounced signal for acneiform dermatitis (~27%), binimetinib was more frequently associated with pruritus, and vemurafenib remained strongly linked to proliferative lesions.

Importantly, Shannon entropy analysis demonstrated significant differences in the diversity of dermatologic ADR profiles, with cobimetinib and dabrafenib showing broader and more heterogeneous toxicity spectra than trametinib and binimetinib. This dimension of toxicity complexity is rarely addressed in clinical trials and represents an added layer of safety characterization.

The added value of this study lies in its real‐world, agent‐specific evaluation of cutaneous ADRs across the entire MAPK inhibitor class using global pharmacovigilance data. Unlike randomized clinical trials, which are limited by strict inclusion criteria, shorter follow‐up, and underreporting of low‐grade or delayed toxicities, spontaneous reporting systems capture a wider spectrum of patients, treatment durations, and adverse event phenotypes. By integrating disproportionality metrics with entropy‐based diversity analysis, this study provides a more nuanced assessment of both the magnitude and complexity of dermatologic toxicity, supporting personalized risk assessment and monitoring strategies.

Combination BRAFi–MEKi therapy is known to reduce paradoxical MAPK activation and lowers the incidence of cuSCC from approximately 11% with vemurafenib monotherapy to about 3% combined with cobimetinib. 23 Nevertheless, monotherapy remains in use in selected settings and requires close dermatologic surveillance, including bi‐monthly skin examinations. 24 Photosensitivity, particularly with vemurafenib (approximately 11% of its reported ADRs), necessitates strict photoprotection measures. 25 Among BRAFi, dabrafenib demonstrates a broader but generally milder adverse event profile, while encorafenib appears to offer the most favorable dermatologic safety profile, with skin ADRs accounting for roughly 15% of reports.

It is important to emphasize that BRAF and MEKi differ not only in the frequency but also in the severity and clinical consequences of serious cutaneous ADRs. The clinical relevance of these class‐specific toxicity profiles extends beyond dermatologic morbidity and has direct implications for patient quality of life, treatment adherence, and therapeutic decision‐making. Serious skin ADRs associated with BRAF inhibitors—such as cuSCC, keratoacanthomas, pronounced photosensitivity, and rare but potentially life‐threatening SCARs, including DRESS and Stevens–Johnson syndrome—often necessitate intensified dermatologic surveillance, procedural interventions, or treatment interruption. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Even when not life‐threatening, these events may substantially impair physical functioning, body image, and psychosocial well‐being, thereby increasing the risk of nonadherence or premature discontinuation of otherwise effective oncologic therapy.

In contrast, MEKi are predominantly associated with milder, inflammatory cutaneous toxicities, including acneiform dermatitis, follicular eruptions, xerosis, and paronychia; however, these reactions are frequently chronic, symptomatic, and cosmetically distressing, particularly when involving visible or periungual areas. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Although grade 1–2 events are rarely treatment‐limiting, they can significantly affect daily activities and patient‐reported outcomes if inadequately managed, while higher‐grade toxicities may still require dose modification or temporary treatment interruption. 30 , 31 Combination BRAF–MEKi therapy, while reducing the incidence of hyperproliferative and malignant skin lesions characteristic of BRAF inhibitor monotherapy, introduces a distinct spectrum of inflammatory reactions that likewise demands proactive supportive care. 33

Taken together, these considerations underscore the need for structured, anticipatory management strategies embedded within multidisciplinary oncology care. Cutaneous ADRs significantly impair quality of life and may compromise treatment adherence, 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 reinforcing the importance of proactive intervention. Pharmacists play a central role within multidisciplinary oncology care teams 38 by providing patient education, preventive counseling (e.g., sun protection, use of emollients and gentle cleansers), 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 and facilitating early detection and reporting of adverse reactions. 43 They further contribute by reviewing concomitant therapies to minimize exacerbating interactions, 44 , 45 , 46 recommending safer alternatives, and initiating prophylactic interventions such as doxycycline for acneiform rash. Through these activities, pharmacists support guideline implementation, interdisciplinary coordination, and optimization of therapeutic outcomes.

Study limitations

This study used spontaneous reporting data and is subject to inherent limitations, including underreporting, regional and temporal variability, lack of exposure denominators, and inability to establish causality. Adjustment for confounders, comorbidities, or concomitant therapies was not possible; therefore, findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. As all data were anonymized and publicly available from a global pharmacovigilance database, no ethical approval or informed consent was required.

CONCLUSIONS

Cutaneous ADRs are a hallmark of BRAF and MEKi, with notable interagent variability. Vemurafenib and selumetinib showed the strongest disproportionality signals, whereas dabrafenib and encorafenib exhibited more favorable profiles. Rash predominated, but selumetinib‐related acneiform eruptions highlight the need for personalized monitoring. Pharmacists can play a pivotal role in early detection and management, and integrating real‐world pharmacovigilance data into practice is essential to reduce treatment disruptions and optimize outcomes in BRAF‐mutant cancers.
