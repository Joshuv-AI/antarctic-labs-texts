Bayesian optimization of capillary pressure data in hydraulic flow units using NMR
Authors: Hasan Jehanzaib, Muhammad Khurram Zahoor, Muhammad Haris, Yasir Saleem, Atif Ismail
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) data provides a comprehensive picture of the petrophysical description of a reservoir through effective characterization of fluid-rock properties. However, estimating the correct capillary pressure curves from NMR T2 data in particular has been challenging with varied fluid saturation as it requires hydrocarbon correction. The earlier methods, either do not incorporate the hydrocarbon correction or exhibit limitations in their implementation, negatively impacting reservoir characterization. Therefore, in this work, a new methodology has been presented that estimates the Pc in the reservoir at hydraulic flow units (HFUs) by using features of NMR T2 and cumulative desaturation rate ∑(dSnw/dT2) through a newly developed workflow. Which incorporates the NMR hydrocarbon correction and encompasses the ensemble-committee machine model (ECMM) that has been purpose-formulated with the Bayesian optimized best-performing algorithms of machine, ensemble, and deep learning through a systematic approach. Results show that the ECMM workflow gives a much better mean squared error (MSE) than individual intelligent models while predicting Pc. ECMM has also been utilized to analyze the capillary pressure variability at HFUs which reveals that higher variance in capillary pressure values among HFUs cause model to underperform in terms of MSE and vice versa. The new methodology introduces a robust and cost-effective machine-learning incorporated workflow to estimate continuous capillary pressure for reservoirs having varied lithologies for effective characterization.

Introduction

NMR has been a logging tool of choice since its establishment, because of its ability to characterize rock and fluid properties comprehensively1,2. The ability to differentiate between free and bound water became possible because of the intricate magnetization-based engineering principle employed by NMR tool. This also lead to reliable estimation of porosity, pore size distribution (PSD), permeability, relative permeability (Kr) and capillary pressure (Pc) among other properties3–10.

Capillary pressure estimation from NMR response was initially introduced by Kleinberg11. Later, the NMR to Pc transformation was particularly studied by Hofman et al. through linear method by utilizing multiple core samples12. Based on empirical observations, he explained that T2 relaxation time and pore throat size are linearly related to pore body size in a homogenous porous medium. Therefore, capillary pressure can be determined by NMR T2 as Pc depends upon pore throat size distribution by employing a conversion constant ‘C’ between T2 and Pc. Afterward, the linear method was employed by Liu et al. to study T2 distribution and core capillary pressure13. Among other authors, Arogun and Nwosu also reported the application of Hofman et al. T2-Pc conversion method to estimate capillary pressure14. The linear method is easy to implement but it does not provide us with reliable capillary pressure values as it employs the same conversion constant ‘C’ between T2 and Pc for the entire formation, making it shady. Later, by using fractal theory, He et al. used a power function method to transfer T2 spectrum to Pc curves. Power function method showed good results while converting lab derived T2 spectrum to Pc curves, but it is not suitable for field implementation15. Another approach for construction of Pc curves from NMR log by using a combination of Schlumberger Doll Research model and Leverett J-function was proposed by Xiao et al.16. This method can be used for field implementation, but it can only be applied to specific formations. Afterward, a new approach of determining capillary pressure was given by Xiao et al. through which he showed that Hofman et al. method of T2-Pc conversion was not accurately predicting capillary pressure in low permeability reservoirs as it did in conventional formations. So, he proposed a classified piece wise power function (CPPF) method for transforming the T2 distribution to capillary pressure17. CPPF method has its advantage over power function method, and it can be employed for field applications, but this method is case specific. Another approach for synthesizing of capillary pressure from NMR data was given by Xiao et al.18. In which he used seven T2 cutoffs for the classification of T2 spectrum to calculate pore component compositions and related them to mercury saturations (SHgs) and capillary pressure increments for calculation of Pc. This method can be extended to field implementation but it has only been implemented in tight formations. Afterwards, T2 distribution was utilized for construction of capillary pressure curve through selecting formation appropriate conversion constant by Hosseinzadeh et al.19. But the application of this method is case specific and lacks wider application.

The machine learning methods in this regard have also been utilized to estimate capillary pressure. Eslami et al. used combinable bin porosities from NMR to predict capillary pressure but R2 was low20. Busaleh et al. used porosity, permeability and grain density features for estimation of Pc but decision tree model was heavily overfitted and also showed overall poor results21. Artificial Neural Network with cuckoo optimization algorithm was used by Jamshidian et al. for capillary pressure but continuous estimation of capillary pressure is not feasible through this technique22. Multi-layer perceptron (MLP) Neural Network with three hidden layers was employed by Liu et al. based on physical features and simulated flow properties to model the capillary pressure and relative permeability but the methodology is resource-intensive23. Alawi et al. used resistivity values to predict capillary pressure through Neural Networks, but the natural variability in resistivity values make his model vulnerable24. Alekseevich et al. used simulated NMR response from 3D digital rock samples to predict Pc but the predictions can only be accomplished in 100% water-saturated conditions and no hydrocarbon correction is part of the method. Moreover, the method only works for a specific range of porosity and permeability25. Later, 214 rock samples were utilized, by Kasha et al. and based on data, leveret J functions of the sample rocks were developed. Then sequentially unsupervised and supervised clustering was applied to sample properties to predict the leveret J function and eventually the average representative capillary pressure. But this method requires large MICP data which makes it less desirable26. Centrifuge pressure data-based calculation of capillary pressure was presented by Bursey et al. but his method is susceptible to issues related to centrifuge setup and pitfalls related to correlation employed in this technique27.

Afterwards, 2-D petrographic thin section image data from 109 samples was analyzed through the K means clustering method for establishing clustered HFUs by Esmaeili et al.28. He employed Pittman equations on image data for the formulation of capillary pressure curves, but his method is prone to image resolution and interpretation anomalies. Stochastic modeling has also been utilized to generate pore networks imitating tight sandstones of Ordos Basin NW China with their physical constraints incorporated through experimentation by Ji et al.29. Deriving pore size distribution, porosity, permeability, and mineral composition from stochastically produced networks a physics-informed neural network (PGA + NN) was used by him to predict the capillary pressure (relative permeability) but this methodology is resource and time intensive. Later, from unsteady state core flooding on a carbonate rock sample, a dataset of 100 measurements was retrieved for average water saturation against time and distance along the core length by Kalule et al.30. Reinforced learning was used to optimize the relative permeability model LET’s parameters. Following this, the optimized parameters were used in a forward model to predict the relative permeability and capillary pressure by him through Physics-informed machine learning (PIML) model but this method can not be rolled out for its application to entire pay section, which makes it lackluster. Moreover, as mentioned in the abstract, NMR estimated properties are only reliable under 100% water-saturated rock pores31. That’s why since the inception of capillary pressure curves from NMR data, methods have been given out to address the issue of non-conformance to 100% water saturation condition12,14,32–35. Although these methods used varied approaches for the hydrocarbon correction of T2 relaxation curves and the resultant capillary pressures, but they were either case-specific or their implementation had limitations. Table 1 shows a summary of the prior research studies and their limitation(s). In this perspective, it can be noted that the need for a less resource-intensive, non-case-specific, machine learning optimized, and hydrocarbon-correction encompassing framework for estimating capillary pressure still exists. Therefore, in this study we introduce a workflow of Bayesian optimized ECMM to estimate Pc to mitigate the identified gap for effective characterization of the reservoir.

NMR working mechanism

NMR’s working principle revolves around the hydrogen nuclei’s transverse relaxation attributed to the in-situ fluids of a reservoir. The NMR measurement methodology uses a large magnet for the alignment of nuclei of hydrogen and then tips them over through a radio frequency over equal echo spacings to record their magnetization decay in the form of T2 distribution. The T2 distribution shows the proportion of small and large pores in a formation through its characteristic shape, hence helping in better characterization of a reservoir. The T2 distribution is made up of bulk, surface and diffusion relaxations. Since the effect of surface relaxation is most pronounced the effect of the other two components is ignored. The Eqs. From above it is clear that to devise an efficient capillary pressure estimation method from given formulae for the entire reservoir, it is needed to characterize the conversion constant ‘C’ rightly. Some prior methods use a fixed value of conversion constant for entire formation which is a wrong approach, as the morphological character of the reservoir is mostly varied12–14. While others which use variable ‘C’ approaches are formation specific and do not incorporate hydrocarbon correction17,19.

Therefore, to bridge the gap left by previous approaches we introduce a comprehensive framework for estimation of Pc. The introduced workflow enables us to recognize the variations in reservoir’s character through Amaefule at al.’s concept of hydraulic flow units (HFUs) for the optimal demarcation of different flow zones or HFUs using a set of equations as discussed in material and methods Sect.39. Moreover, HFU-based hydrocarbon correction method given by us has also been incorporated into the developed framework to mitigate the absence of reliable hydrocarbon correction in prior methods37. By implementing the newly introduced framework every demarked HFU will have its own representative ‘C’ which can be estimated through the reservoir’s petrophysical/core characterization for existing wells. For new wells the cores can be planned and retrieved from optimally demarked HFUs to get high certainty ‘C’ values post core-log calibration. Furthermore, to comprehensively model the estimation of change in capillary pressure values due to variability of conversion constant ‘C’, we have incorporated a progressive metrics (mean squared error (MSE)) driven machine learning workflow called ensemble committee machine model (ECMM) to our newly developed framework. ECMM utilizes Bayesian optimized high performing algorithms from machine, ensemble and deep learning to systematically achieve superior modelling results in terms of MSE. In a nutshell, the developed methodology combines HFU demarcation, Hydrocarbon correction and optimal features incorporated ECMM to achieve higher reliability and predictive performance for capillary pressure estimation thus bridging the gap left by previous methodologies. The schematic and details of newly introduced framework encompassing ECMM workflow have been presented in the material and methods section.

Geological setting

The study was conducted on Pirkoh limestone from Eocene era, this formation is located in southern Pakistan. It is a heterogeneous formation that has quite a few microfacies to it. It mainly consists of marl, shale, and limestone. It is categorized as a gas reservoir with gas overlaying water Sect.37. This formation is part of the Kirthar group as shown by the regional stratigraphic column (Fig. 1), with other surrounding formations being Nari, Habib Rahi, Sui main limestone and Ranikot. The existence of multiple microfacies makes this reservoir’s characterization a challenging but insightful task.

Materials and methods

The methodology combines three approaches for estimating capillary pressure. The first approach comes from Amaefule et al.’s concept of demarcation of hydraulic flow units (zones of identical petrophysical properties and character) using porosity and permeability through the establishment of reservoir quality index (RQI), normalizing porosity index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varphi\:}_{z})$$\end{document} and flow zone indicator (FZI) parameters39. Their representative equations are given below:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\varphi\:}_{z}}_{\:}=\frac{\varphi\:}{1-\varphi\:}$$\end{document}11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:RQI=\sqrt{\frac{K}{\varphi\:}}$$\end{document}12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:FZI=\frac{RQI}{{\varphi\:}_{z}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{F}_{s}\tau\:{S}_{gv}}}$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:$$\end{document} is tortuosity, Fs is shape factor, Sgv is known as surface area per grain volume. Employing these equations the reservoir can be divided into hydraulic flow units by using RQI and FZI values. The attained results can be graphed for ease of establishment of HFUs by visual and cutoff demarcation. Moreover, HFU-based hydrocarbon correction workflow given by us has also been incorporated into this approach to compensate for the hydrocarbon correction gap from earlier methods37.

The second approach incorporates the NMR to capillary pressure HFU-based data transformation workflow (Fig. 2) aided by better feature engineering and selection. This is accomplished through rigorous analysis of HFU-based NMR-Pc curves obtained from NMR log data. The analytics of NMR log from Pirkoh formation and Pore network modeling data (see section method implementation for details) reveal that ∑(dSnw/dT2) in general and at higher non-wetting saturations in particular has a linear relationship with capillary pressure (See Fig. 10a,c,e). Also, prior studies reveal that NMR T2 is pertinently linked to Pc12,20,36. Therefore, we have selected ∑(dSnw/dT2) and NMR T2 relaxation to be two features that are crucial for the estimation of capillary pressure in this study (See Fig. 10). The significance of taking ∑(dSnw/dT2) as a feature entity is that it imitates the underlying physics of degree of desaturation in the core vs. change in capillary pressure values as exhibited in the centrifuge capillary pressure measurement methodology.

The third incorporated approach uses the concept of the ensemble-committee machine model (ECMM) which is based on the systematic cross-comparison of algorithms and integration of top-performing machine, ensemble and deep learning models for better predictive performance (See Figs. 2 and 3). The ECMM model takes features of NMR T2 and ∑(dSnw/dT2) as input and is trained for the estimation of capillary pressure (target entity) values at various saturations. After the model gets trained, it helps us predict the capillary pressure curves in an accurate, robust and cost-effective manner (reducing coring/core data requirement, due to optimal HFU Pc data usage for training of ECMM) at any logged depth in the reservoir. The ECMM compares the MSE of the finalized premium algorithms of Adaboost, XGBoost and GBM for every predicted Pc value and assigns 100% weight to Pc value associated with the least MSE while it does not assign any weight to the predicted Pc values having higher MSE. Hence optimal predicted Pc values are obtained this way, which are further aggregated for formulation of complete Pc curves. Moreover, what makes this method easily implementable and universal is that the input features of NMR T2 and ∑(dSnw/dT2) for ECMM’s model can also be acquired from routine log data in the absence of NMR data for continuous estimation of capillary pressure40.

Hence, altogether these three approaches collectively constitute the newly developed framework. Which in a nutshell combines HFU demarcation, hydrocarbon correction, optimal features of T2 and ∑(dSnw/dT2) and Bayesian optimized ECMM to formulate a comprehensive methodology to predict optimal Pc values for effective reservoir characterization. The schematic of the newly developed Pc estimation framework can be seen in Figs. 2 and 3.

The NMR data for this study have been obtained through combinable magnetic resonance tool from Shlumberger, which is also known as CMR-plus. CMR magnetizes the hydrogen nuclei of the formation through its permanent magnet and then uses the radio frequency to tip the aligned hydrogen nuclei. Which is followed by180 degree phased pulses of CPMG technique with an echo spacing of 200 microseconds. This ends up generating a decaying echo-train, which is then further converted into NMR T2 relaxations for ease of interpretation. The NMR T2 relaxation amplitude is directly associated with the size of pores in the formation with the larger pores exhibiting a higher value of T2 and the smaller pores showing lower values of T2 relaxation. Hence, the tool provides a very good pore characterization methodology.

After careful data quality control 34 NMR sample depths were chosen from Pirkoh formation for study, the Fig. 4 shows logging profile of Pirkoh formation37. Moreover, the logging curves of SP, GR, Rt, CNL (Neutron porosity) and RHOB (bulk density) logs have been corrected for borehole effects and CNL is additionally corrected for gas effect. The Pirkoh formation consists of a hydrocarbon column underlaid by water. Out of 34 points, 13 are associated with hydrocarbon zone and they have been named HC1, HC2…HC13 respectively. While the other 21 points that are from water zone and have been categorized as W1, W2 through W21. Where notation HC means hydrocarbon and W stands for water. Also 1 stands for the shallowest point in the hydrocarbon and water zone and 13 and 34 are the deepest points in hydrocarbon and water zones respectively. Other than NMR logging data, the NMR data from 3D reconstructed Berea sandstone samples was also utilized for this study. Pore network modeling data samples were generated through pore network code41. In this regard conversion constant ‘C’ holds special significance, and it is because of the fact that the value of ‘C’ depends upon the character of the terms Fs, rpb, rpt, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}. In a homogeneous medium their values can be conveniently assumed constant, this in turn gives us a constant value of ‘C’ similar to Hofman et al., who used 3 as a universal conversion constant between NMR T2 and Pc for sandstone samples12. Pc prediction through ECMM

ECMM was formulated utilizing all the three ML domains of supervised learning for regression, which includes machine, ensemble and deep learning. After preliminary analysis following nine (09) models/algorithms were chosen to start the ECMM formulation process which include Linear Regression and Decision Tree (DT) algorithms from machine learning domain, Xgboost, Adaboost, Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Stalking and Random Forest (RF) from ensemble learning domain and Feedforward neural networks (FFNN) and Multilayer perceptron (MLP) from deep learning domain (see Table 6).

The output of ECMM’s high-performing algorithms is weighted and combined to output the most optimally predicted capillary pressure curves. This ensures that the predicted capillary pressure values are very close to the target values. Hence the resultant value of MSE for ECMM is very low. This shows that as the HFUs will have a higher degree of heterogeneity between them, their characterizing machine learning model’s performance will get impacted negatively.

Results and discussion

The results of the study show that the ECMM predicts capillary pressure values very effectively. The ECMM estimated capillary pressure values closely match original Pc values for Case-1 having low variance among capillary pressure data for HFUs. But for Case-2 and 3 having medium and high variance respectively the ECMM’s predictive ability suffers a little decline due to increased heterogeneity among HFUs (Figs. 15 and 16). The regression predictive metric of MSE shows a low overall value of 0.508 psi for Case-1 whereas MSE overall values of Case-2 and 3 are on the higher side having values of 2.906 and 7.073 psi respectively due to increased variance (Fig. 19). Further, ECMM gives R2 values ranging from 0.967 to 0.999 for all three cases (see Tables 7, 8 and 9), which means that the input features of NMR T2 and ∑(dSnw/dT2) are explaining the variations in the target capillary pressure values very effectively. Moreover, as this model is based on a progressive ECMM formation, so even in the instance of highly nonlinear and heterogeneous data at HFU level or at every Pc curve, the model can exploit a variety of algorithms to increase its predictive efficiency. The development of ECMM is data driven and the model keeps getting better by rejecting the low performing algorithms through cross-comparison of the evaluation metrics. And eventually only keeps the high-performing machine learning models which are optimized through Bayesian search.

The highlight of ECMM is that it utilizes algorithms from all supervised machine learning regression domains like machine, ensemble and deep learning. Which makes sure that the bias and variance of the resultant ECMM will be optimal. As due to the inherent variable nature of these algorithms coming from different domains, some are good at reducing bias, while the others are good at reducing model variance and a good mix of these algorithm ensures that the final model gets even better at reducing the bias and variance, hence giving us an optimal predictive ability. The high R2 shows us that ECMM has done a really good job in learning and predicting the capillary pressure values of the dataset being used for the current study. Low values of MSE also points towards the successful implementation of ECMM for prediction of Pc. The better prediction of capillary pressure ensures the effective characterization of the reservoir. Which eventually means higher recovery from the reservoir with a better insight of petrophysical and fluid properties. It is a well-known fact that high capillary trapping causes more oil and gas residual saturations in the reservoirs. This methodology will help us make informed decisions (due to the availability of continuous Pc information in the reservoir) to get the maximum hydrocarbons out of the reservoir through optimal planning and management during primary, secondary, and tertiary recovery phases as the characterized information of capillary pressure is key to the development and planning of the reservoir in any phase. Further, better characterization of the reservoir will also lead to the optimal utilization of numerical simulation modeling which is key to the exploration and production sector for increasing production and recovery from micro to macro level. Moreover, the estimated capillary pressure values can also be used to determine the relative probability values by using suitable correlations like Purcell, Burdine and others for enhanced reservoir characterization43,44.

This study introduces a quick, generalized, continuous, cost-effective, easily implementable and robust methodology to estimate Pc from NMR data for effective reservoir characterization.The study results show that the workflow incorporating ECMM is very accurate and efficient in continuous estimation of capillary pressure values in the reservoir.The introduced workflow also incorporates a newly developed, novel hydrocarbon effect correction method for correcting distorted NMR T2 and associated capillary pressure for effective reservoir characterization.The ECMM predictive metrics of MSE and R2 show very good results with an overall ECMM MSE of 0.508 psi for Case-1 and 2.906 and 7.073 psi for Case-2 and 3 respectively.
