Revealing the Antarctic marginal ice zone with a decade-long wave-in-ice climatology
Authors: Alexander D Fraser, Noah Day, Zhaohui Wang, Luke G Bennetts, Qingxiang Liu, Siobhan O’Farrell, Richard Coleman, Joey Voermans, Shiming Xu, Weixin Zhu, Matthis Auger, Robert A Massom, Pat Wongpan, Lisa Craw, Jill Brouwer, Takenobu Toyota, Petra Heil, Christopher Horvat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe marginal ice zone (MIZ) is the region of sea ice that is strongly influenced by open-ocean processes, particularly ocean waves. The width of the Antarctic MIZ is often quantified by applying thresholds to satellite-derived maps of sea-ice concentration, although this definition lacks any connection to waves. Laser altimetry provides snapshots of wave penetration, but is restricted by cloud cover. To overcome these limitations, we refine radar altimetry techniques to estimate the Antarctic MIZ width from Ka-band radar altimeter data (2013-2024), producing a decade-long climatology of the wave-affected MIZ. Our analysis reveals the regionality and seasonality of the MIZ width, highlighting the under-appreciated dependence of wave penetration on ice-edge aspect (the alignment of the ice edge, relative to north). The wave-affected MIZ covers around 16% of the sea-ice zone. This technique offers a tool for measuring MIZ width and could be applied to earlier satellite datasets, enabling creation of a multi-decade climatology of this important zone and assessment of long-term changes in wave-ice interactions in the Southern Ocean.Subject terms: Physical oceanography, Cryospheric science

Introduction

Around Antarctica, waves can travel hundreds of km into the MIZ, and have a major impact on the ice cover1–3. Such wave–sea-ice interactions have considerable physical and biological impacts: ice floes fracture due to flexure1,4, increasing their perimeter-driven lateral melt rates in the austral spring-summer5, and enhancing their mobility6,7; impinging waves can impart considerable momentum to the ice cover, thereby shifting the ice edge and enhancing floe collisions8; resultant altered heat and moisture fluxes in the MIZ influence polar weather systems9; and wave over-washing removes snow, triggering biological, chemical and albedo consequences10,11.

On large scales (100s–1000s of km), detecting the region where sea ice is influenced by the open ocean is not straightforward. Until now, MIZ width has been measured and monitored by applying an arbitrary threshold (typically between 15 and 80%) to daily maps of satellite passive microwave-derived sea-ice concentration. This method yields a minimum Antarctic MIZ extent from February to May, and a maximum extent in December (see Supplementary Information Fig. 1). However, weak correlation between wave passage and ice concentration, particularly around Antarctica12, suggests that concentration-based definitions tend to underestimate2,6 or overestimate13 the true MIZ width, depending on the concentration product used. Furthermore, evidence has emerged of a springtime seasonality peak in wave penetration2,13, again highlighting the discrepancy between the wave-based and concentration-based definitions, although these laser altimetry-based studies are temporally limited.

Several ICESat-2 satellite laser altimetry-based studies have recently observed instantaneous wave passage through Antarctic sea ice2,13,14, offering insights into the MIZ aligned more closely with its connection with waves, compared to sea-ice concentration-based metrics2. However, the time series of ICESat-2 extends back to only late 2018, and the surface is obscured during times of cloud cover, which can be associated with high wave heights on the eastern side of cyclonic systems15, potentially biasing the resulting MIZ width retrieval. In 2015, synthetic aperture radar imagery was used to capture snapshots of wave-affected MIZ extent, via velocity bunching detection16, but use of this technique has focussed on wave attenuation17, rather than quantification of the wave-affected MIZ width.

However, as early as 1984, radar altimeter waveform pulse shapes have been recognised as sensitive to waves propagating in sea ice, enabling early mapping of the wave-affected MIZ width on a 3-day cycle18. Similar techniques have recently been used in the Atlantic Arctic to generate the first decade-long wave-affected MIZ climate record using CryoSat-219, revealing an absence of significant correlation between the wave-affected MIZ and sea-ice concentration-based MIZ definition, but large-scale radar altimeter-based mapping of the Antarctic MIZ has not been reported until now. Here, we revisit this earlier satellite radar altimeter-based method18 to automate wave-affected MIZ detection around Antarctica, with the aim of providing a high-resolution, long-term climatology of the wave-affected Antarctic MIZ width and exploring its seasonality and regional variability. Further, the climatology allows us to examine the relationship between sea-ice concentration and wave-affected MIZ width, and to quantify the fraction of the overall sea-ice zone influenced by waves.

Results

We generate along-track estimates of wave-affected MIZ width from almost 12 years (March 2013 to December 2024) of data from AltiKa (described in the “Methods” section), a pulse-limited Ka-band radar altimeter onboard the polar-orbiting SARAL (Satellite with Argos and ALtiKa) platform20. Antarctic MIZ width estimates from these tracks are then averaged by day-of-year and longitude to produce a climatology, shown in Fig. 1.

Seasonality is determined by ice-edge latitude and wave height

The seasonality of mean circum-Antarctic wave-affected MIZ width is shown to reach a minimum of ~35 km in late February (Fig. 1a, b), with a broad peak of around 180 km from mid-July to early October. This seasonality aligns with recent ICESat-2 results2, with an earlier peak than the December MIZ peak indicated when using a passive microwave sea-ice concentration definition (see Supplementary Information Fig. 1 for a comparison between the concentration-based and wave-affected MIZ width definitions). In our processing, we filter out tracks containing incomplete wave attenuation before encountering the coast, which decreases austral summertime values more than wintertime ones, as many more instances of waves penetrating to the coast occur when meridional ice extent is smallest (February-mean = 4.7% vs year-round mean = 1.2%).

The wave climatology for the open (ice-free) Southern Ocean (shown in Supplementary Information Fig. 2) exhibits strong seasonality, with peak annual wave heights in late austral winter/early spring, and minima in summer. A sharp meridional gradient places maximum wave height at ~50∘S, decreasing poleward21. In February to March and at the time of minimum annual sea-ice extent, low mean significant wave heights of <2 m at high latitudes and near the Antarctic coast result in the lowest MIZ widths in austral summertime. Additionally, thick summer sea ice22 enhances wave attenuation23, despite often low concentration.

From April, the ice edge rapidly advances northward24 into higher wave-height regions, with wave intensification until September21 promoting deeper wave penetration. The ice edge at this time is characterised by thinner floes, e.g. pancake ice22,25,26, which facilitates deeper wave propagation27,28.

The close agreement in seasonality with the CICE6 sea ice model (see “Methods” section) strongly suggests that the primary driver of MIZ seasonality is the wave height north of the ice edge (itself determined by Southern Ocean wave heights together with the latitude of the ice edge). The CICE6 model is forced by the hindcast model wave height at the northern edge. The AltiKa-observed wave-in-ice seasonality has a very close correspondence to both the wave height north of the ice edge (R2 = 0.83) and the ice edge latitude (R2 = 0.88), and the AltiKa-derived MIZ width seasonality is highly correlated with that of the model (R2 = 0.85), leaving only a small fraction of variance to be explained by other mechanisms. Further evidence of the key role played by the wave height north of the ice edge in driving wave-affected MIZ width is given in the “Methods” section, which shows very high correlations between wave height and MIZ width for AltiKa and the CICE6 model (R2 = 0.59 and 0.76, respectively).

Regionality determined by wave height and ice-edge aspect

In line with previous studies showing that the local (within a degree or two of the ice edge) wave height is a key driver of wave-affected MIZ width (e.g. refs. 5,18,29,30), we also find that wave height distribution strongly influences MIZ-width regionality (Fig. 1c). Significant wave heights adjacent to the ice edge are typically very large (>4 m) from 30∘ to 90∘E during late winter/early spring (see Supplementary Information Fig. 2), but are generally large (>3.5 m) from the prime meridian, eastwards across East Antarctica and the Ross Sea to the Bellingshausen Sea (~65∘W). A sharp decrease in wave height (~3 m) occurs eastward across Drake Passage (~65∘W), due to the physical sheltering effect provided by the Antarctic Peninsula and South America, reducing wave height from longitudes 60∘ to 20∘W21. This results in a pronounced decline in wave-affected MIZ width in the western Weddell Sea (Fig. 1ii). This region harbours extensive multi-year sea ice31, which enhances the local wave attenuation rate further, due to its thickness (2.50 ± 1.08 m in May, making it Antarctica’s thickest sea ice32) and compactness, both of which encourage higher attenuation rates; see Fig. 6b, c of ref. 23.

On a macro scale, ice-edge aspect (i.e. the direction the ice edge faces, relative to true north) also appears to modulate the wave-affected MIZ width. Similar to the aforementioned sheltering provided by continental landmasses across Drake Passage, we find that the local sea-ice edge morphology itself modulates local wave heights during times when the ice edge is sufficiently far north to encounter high wave energy (i.e. from approximately May to October): wave heights west of major northward sea ice edge protrusions are higher than those to the east (see red boxes annotated on Supplementary Information Fig. 2b, and such sheltering is also evident in observational datasets of wave height, e.g. Fig. 3b of ref. 21). This zonal gradient in near-ice-edge wave height manifests as a zonal gradient in wave-affected MIZ width. The three largest zonal gradient structures (which coincide with the three largest ice-edge aspect gradients) are schematically illustrated for September in Fig. 1a, shown in boxes 1–2, 3–4 and 5–6. In each zonal gradient structure, deeper wave penetration occurs in the western box during winter/early spring.

The Southern Ocean is characterised by high persistence in wave propagation directionality: incoming wave energy is highly directionally-persistent year-round, but is most persistent in direction from July to December, where 84% of all waves arrive from a 90∘ quadrant between WSW (247.5∘) and NNW (337.5∘; as shown in Fig. 2a, based on a global wave hindcast33). As such, other factors which may contribute to this observed ice-edge aspect/MIZ width relationship include the geometrical interception of more eastward-propagating wave energy by ice edges that face slightly to the west, and the path lengths being longer for more grazing-incidence waves to reach further south, allowing more attenuation to take place34.

These zonal gradient structures are illustrated during September in Figs. 1a and 2b as a way of schematically depicting the presence of these structures, since September: (a) has the furthest-north ice edge of any month; (b) has relatively high significant wave height north of the ice edge; and (c) has relatively zonally-symmetric significant wave height throughout most of the Southern Ocean. Other months also exhibit these structures to a lesser degree, particularly during the winter and early spring (see Fig. 1a), but their expression is confounded by spatially varying wave height (particularly in August), so we demonstrate this feature in September.

To quantify the relationship between climatological-mean September ice-edge aspect and MIZ width, we computed Pearson’s correlation coefficient between these variables. The analysis is conducted with 3∘ longitude aggregations in September to maximise meridional ice extent, and yields R2 = 0.20 (one-tailed, p ≃ 0). Considering all other factors contributing to the wave-affected MIZ width (on a climatological timescale, i.e. removing the effect of individual storm passage): (a) incident swell energy, (b) ice thickness, (c) ice concentration, (d) floe size, and (e) other physical properties, we consider that a correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.20 between ice edge aspect and MIZ width (i.e. variance explained = 20%) gives evidence that ice-edge aspect is an important determinant of MIZ width.

Wave-affected MIZ width is, in some regions, constrained by meridional sea-ice extent, even in September when sea ice is at its maximum. This is evident in Fig. 1a, box (i), where a ~15∘ longitudinal zone centred at ~ 130∘E exhibits a relatively narrow MIZ (year-round mean ~75 km). This narrow MIZ, second only to that found in the sheltered western Weddell Sea, results from a narrow sea-ice zone extending only a few degrees in latitude from the continent to the ice edge, even at peak ice extent35, providing not only a limited width of sea ice for MIZ to exist, but also insufficient reach north into the high wave climate parts of the Southern Ocean21.

Sea-ice concentration: a poor proxy for wave-affected MIZ

As recently demonstrated in the Arctic19, we show here that a sea-ice concentration-based definition of the MIZ is unrelated to the wave penetration-based definition presented here. Figure 3 presents the frequency distribution of National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC) Climate Data Record (CDR)-reported sea-ice concentration36 at the AltiKa-derived inner MIZ boundary. Due to bi-modality in monthly results, we separate histograms into advance months (May to October; blue) and retreat months (November to April; yellow). Neither phase exhibits a strong peak at 80% concentration, suggesting the inner limit of the wave-affected MIZ is largely independent of ice concentration. During advance months, the inner MIZ limit often occurs at concentrations >80%, with a pronounced peak at 90–100%, indicating wave penetration generally extends well beyond the 80% contour, in line with other recent publications showing wave passage in high concentration regions19,37.

Around 16% of the Antarctic sea-ice zone is wave-affected

We determine the fraction of sea ice influenced by waves by assessing the width of the wave-affected MIZ relative to that of the total sea-ice zone (Fig. 4, orange line, produced from the ICESat-2-derived wave-affected fraction13 using the quality-control method of ref. 38 and available online at ref. 39). Including satellite tracks where waves reach the coast, we find that the wave-affected MIZ constitutes ~16.2% of the sea-ice zone, averaged in time and space (blue line). This is systematically wider than automated ICESat-2 estimates of wave-affected fraction (9.7%, averaged over the period October 2018 to December 202339), likely due to radar altimetry’s ability to observe the surface even during stormy conditions, which generate both larger amplitude swell and increased cloud cover, especially on the eastern side of cyclonic systems15, producing a large southward fetch. Despite this difference in magnitude, both datasets exhibit near-identical seasonality, with the MIZ fraction peaking in April and reaching a minimum from November to February. We also note the apparent underestimate of ICESat-2 wave-affected MIZ width here compared to our detailed validation provided in the “Methods” section—these are produced from different ICESat-2 datasets. The automated ICESat-2 data13 (required for producing year-round estimates of the percentage of sea ice which is wave-affected) appear to be less sensitive to wave passage than that of the manually-produced selected-track data of ref. 2 used for validation of individual AltiKa satellite passes. This difference is likely attributable to biased sampling toward high-wave height tracks in the manual data2, together with relative insensitivity to smaller waves in the automated data13.

Discussion

We report wave-affected MIZ width based solely on the (AltiKa-reported) latitude of the ice edge and the inner limit of wave penetration, i.e. representing its meridional extent. We do not assume predominantly southward wave propagation; rather, as shown in Fig. 2, the incident wave direction is generally eastward, although refraction toward the normal due to a reduction in group speed in the MIZ may enhance the southward component40, and wave scattering by floes pushes the wave directionality toward being isotropic41. In general, changes in the speed and direction of waves in the MIZ are poorly constrained, mainly due to limited knowledge of ice physical properties42. Unlike previous ICESat-2 studies2, our method does not require precise wavelength estimates, making directional uncertainty unimportant for our MIZ width retrievals. However, future work estimating wave attenuation rates in ice—a key parameterisation for the wave component of climate models, which currently vary greatly in their method for dissipating wave energy43—must account for wave direction, either through externally-estimated incident wave data19 or direct propagation measurements14.

Our study shows a very high correlation (R2 = 0.85) and low RMS error (25 km) between the seasonality of AltiKa-retrieved and modelled wave-affected MIZ width, when considering a wave height threshold of 5 cm in the model. Given the model sensitivity to wave attenuation parameterisation choices (e.g. as demonstrated in ref. 43), we do not necessarily consider AltiKa to be sensitive to waves-in-ice higher than 5 cm in height; rather, we use this model-observation combination to gain confidence in the seasonality of the wave-affected MIZ, and understanding of the wave height north of the ice edge as the primary driver of MIZ width seasonality.

On shorter time-scales than those covered by the climatological study presented here, a complex interplay exists between ice-edge morphology, wave interception by the ice edge, and ice-edge evolution in response to the wave-radiation stress imparted by the wave-ice interaction44. Although a single-instrument study such as ours does not garner sufficient resolution of individual wave-stress events, a multi-instrument approach (e.g. following ref. 45) would allow a detailed study of such effects and produce a more complete description of the evolution of the wave-affected MIZ as storm systems propagate eastward near the ice edge.

Our ~12-year data produced a wave-affected MIZ width time series showing considerable inter-annual variability (see individual yearly MIZ width figures in the Supplementary Information, Figs. 7–18), but analysis of long-term trends is beyond the scope of the work presented here—indeed, we see a slightly positive (but marginally-significant; p = 0.06) trend in wave-affected MIZ width of 3.4 ± 1.8 km/decade—but regional variability is considerable. Time series analysis would benefit from a longer instrumental record, and our choice of a pulse-limited radar altimeter for this study paves the way for extension of this record with earlier radar altimeters (e.g. Envisat, back to 2002).

The recently launched Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission46 offers considerable potential for advancing wave-ice interaction studies. With cm-level precision in ice-covered ocean height, ~200–500 m spatial resolution along and across track, and being unaffected by clouds and polar darkness, SWOT can estimate wave height within the MIZ and determine the propagation and attenuation of swell that penetrates furthest into the ice. Its 2D-resolved surface height field will enable detailed swell attenuation studies, even in complex ice edge regions, while concurrent freeboard estimates will provide critical context for interpreting attenuation signals23. However, as demonstrated in the Supplementary Information here, single-instrument MIZ records have coverage limitations for time-series studies, necessitating a multi-instrument approach for studies on changes in wave-affected MIZ width45.

Using a single instrument, we have generated a fine-scale, decade-long climatology of the wave-affected MIZ around Antarctica, revealing a mean width that varies from ~35 km in February/March to ~180 km in August/September. This variability is strongly influenced by regional factors, including wave sheltering in the western Weddell Sea, a narrow sea-ice zone near 140∘E, and ice-edge aspect, where westward-facing ice edges typically intercept greater wave energy and experience higher incoming swell/wave heights. This decade-long dataset provides a critical benchmark for validating the new generation of fully-coupled models that incorporate wave-ice interaction (e.g. version 3 of the Community Earth System Model47). Our choice to use a traditional pulse-limited radar altimeter also enables extension of the data record back in time using earlier radar altimeters, paving the way for studies on changes in wave-affected MIZ width, and its relationship to other sea-ice properties such as overall sea-ice extent and floe size distribution.

Methods

Datasets

AltiKa, a Ka-band (35.75 GHz) altimeter mounted on the SARAL satellite launched in 2013, is used here. This altimeter achieves a footprint of radius ~1.4–6 km depending on sea state/footprint roughness, and records at a frequency of 40 Hz20. The satellite’s orbital inclination of 98.55∘ allows for comprehensive sampling of the Southern Ocean, extending up to the Antarctic coast on all tracks. This high inclination gives roughly north-south-aligned tracks at Antarctic MIZ latitudes (at 60∘S, tracks are aligned at ±17.3∘ of north-south).

AltiKa’s 480 MHz bandwidth enables a vertical resolution of 0.3 m48. By comparison, the first satellite radar altimeter used for estimating MIZ extent, Seasat18, allowed a vertical resolution of 0.5 m. Additionally, AltiKa has demonstrated greater accuracy in measuring surface roughness over calm or moderately rough seas compared to Ku-band altimeters49. Noise levels in significant wave height measurements are reported to be under 2 cm for a 2 m significant wave height50,51. We use the AltiKa Sensor Geophysical Data Record (SGDR)-Final—a Level 2 product that includes not only waveforms but also derived physical products (e.g. open-ocean wave height) at the native 40 Hz. Data are freely available after AVISO registration (https://www.aviso.altimetry.fr/) from ftp://ftp-access.aviso.altimetry.fr/geophysical-data-record/saral/sgdr_f, and we use all data from commissioning in early 2013 until the end of 2024.

Radar waveforms in the open ocean typically demonstrate a relatively gentle ramp up to peak returned power, indicating a rough, wave-covered surface which is intercepted by the incident wavefront at a variety of times. As the surface becomes smoother (e.g. due to a lower wave height), the return waveform has a more sudden ramp-up to its peak returned power (known as the slope of the leading edge), and the peakiness of the waveform increases. Such waveform changes were also noted to occur as waves become progressively attenuated from the open ocean throughout the marginal ice zone18, and we extend this technique to the AltiKa radar altimeter here. Specifically, returns become peakier with a steeper leading edge slope as waves become progressively attenuated because the upper snow surface (where the Ka-band return occurs) becomes smoother within each footprint. At some point, waves become sufficiently attenuated for the snow over level ice to produce a quasi-specular return, where the snow freeboard is smooth with respect to the range resolution of the altimeter52. In particular, we denote the most northerly point where the product of the slope of the leading edge and peakiness exceeds a critical threshold as the inner limit of wave penetration, and calculate the wave-affected marginal ice zone width as the difference between the ice-edge latitude and the inner limit of wave penetration.

For significant wave height retrieval in the open ocean, Ku-band (e.g. Seasat) and Ka-band (e.g. AltiKa) pulse-limited radar altimeters are broadly equivalent53, although the Ka-band measurements benefit from a smaller footprint20. In terms of the interaction with a snow/ice-covered surface, Ka-band microwaves are generally considered to primarily return from the top of the snow, whereas Ku-band microwaves penetrate to around the snow-ice interface54. As such, the ‘smoothing’ effect of snow overlying rougher sea ice may increase the sensitivity of Ka-band to the underlying wave passage, further indicating the utility of AltiKa for this purpose.

Validation of AltiKa-estimated wave penetration is provided by selected ICESat-2 tracks demonstrating wave attenuation, as processed in an earlier study2. From their ICESat-2 track dataset55, we choose all tracks labelled good, indicating unambiguous identification of wave attenuation from north to south, and crucially, a well-constrained inner MIZ boundary. For the validation presented here, we use the median estimate of wave penetration for four wavelengths (498 m, 345 m, 239 m and 165 m), as suggested by ref. 2. We consider the wave penetration for both linear and exponential wave-decay models, noting the small difference in wave penetration between the models (the exponential decay model generally estimates deeper wave penetration, but only by ~27 km on average2).

The NSIDC-distributed CDR of sea-ice concentration (version 4; ref. 36) is used here (a) as an ancillary method for determining the ice edge (thresholded at 15%); (b) for determining if the altimeter encounters a complex MIZ geometry, both cross-track and along-track (resulting in track exclusion); (c) for retrieving the ice concentration at the inner MIZ location; and (d) for plotting the along-track ice concentration in each waveform plot (examples are given in the Supplementary Information, Figs. 3–6). We do not use this product for determining the location of the ice edge, since this can move in the time between the data acquisition for the NSIDC product and the AltiKa pass.

Wave-affected MIZ retrieval methods

We define the wave-affected MIZ width to be the north-south (meridional) component of the distance between the ice edge and the point at which waves become undetectable from the radar altimeter. Such a definition requires discussion of both the location of the ice edge and the inner MIZ boundary. We use the AltiKa SGDR-reported ‘open_sea_ice_flag’ (which takes a non-zero value between 1 and 5 for first-year, wet, multi-year or ambiguous sea ice types, based on the radar backscatter and the onboard K and Ka-band radiometer) to determine the ice-edge location, rather than calculating it from the CDR concentration product, but we check that the AltiKa-reported and CDR-reported ice-edges are within 1∘ latitude, and exclude tracks that violate this check. The automated determination of the inner MIZ boundary is the main focus of this work, and is outlined in the following section.

In the seminal 1984 work, ref. 18 used the Seasat altimeter onboard-computed significant wave height parameter (derived from the slope of the leading edge) together with the automatic gain control parameter as a measure of backscatter intensity to determine the extent of the wave-affected MIZ. The latter parameter contains considerable seasonal variability (e.g. in summertime when liquid water exists within the snow matrix, microwave extinction will be high, resulting in lower backscatter), but this was not an issue in the 1984 Seasat radar altimeter study since the radar altimeter only operated from June to October 1978. Here, we use similar principles as the 1984 study (i.e. returns are more strongly peaked and with a steeper leading edge as waves become progressively attenuated). As such, we use only the slope of the leading edge (computed here) and peakiness from the AltiKa SGDR product. Peakiness is defined as the ratio of the peak power to the total power (details provided in ref. 56). The product of these two measures of waveform shape was found to be largely seasonally invariant as a measure of when the level ice within a footprint is not wave-affected.

Peakiness is provided in the AltiKa SGDR product (dimensionless). Leading edge slope is not provided, so it is calculated simply for each 40 Hz waveform, by calculating the maximum waveform power, determining the range gate where 20% and 80% of the maximum power is first exceeded, and calculating the slope across these points (in units of dB/range gate, which are equally spaced in time at 2.381 ns), after57. After validation and comparison with wave-affected MIZ width estimates determined from ICESat-2 laser altimetry (ref. 2; and described in the following section), it was determined that a seasonally-static threshold on the product of leading edge slope × peakiness was sufficient for determining the inner MIZ location. Example waveform tracks from February (near minimum sea-ice extent), May (advance), September (near maximum extent) and December (retreat) are provided in the Supplementary Information, Figs. 3–6.

Processing strategy and AltiKa track selection

For each waveform, we calculate the slope of the leading edge (SLE), as discussed above. Along-track sea-ice concentration is not provided in the AltiKa Level 2 product, but it provides useful ancillary and contextual information. We extract this from the aforementioned CDR product. This is used both to determine whether a track enters and leaves sea ice multiple times per pass—so-called banded MIZ tracks are flagged. We also extract the sea-ice concentration parallel to each track at ±2∘ longitude, which is then used to assess the ice edge orientation: tracks where the ice edge latitude varies by more than 1∘ within ±2∘ longitude indicate a highly sloping ice edge—such tracks are flagged due to a high probability of the satellite track not intercepting the ice edge near-orthogonally. Ice edge latitude is retrieved from the AltiKa ‘open_sea_ice_flag’, as described above. We flag tracks where the AltiKa-reported ice edge differs from the along-track CDR-calculated ice edge (i.e. exceeding a concentration of 15%) by 1∘ of latitude. The product of peakiness and SLE is then calculated, and smoothed with a 41-point centred rolling mean, corresponding to a timescale of ~1 s, or an along-track distance of ~7.7 km, i.e. broader than the pulse-limited footprint under calm ocean conditions of ~1.4 km58,59. We determine five inner MIZ candidates based on the closest local maximum of five SLE × peakiness thresholds, and use the spread in these as a measure of confidence in the inner MIZ location. When exceeding a SLE × peakiness product value of higher than ~6000 dB/range gate, most of the radar return occurs within one or two range bins, indicating a relatively smooth, wave-free surface, within the range resolution limit of AltiKa. Rather than a static threshold of 6000 dB/range gate, our array of five prescribed thresholds (from 5400 to 6000 dB/range gate, determined experimentally) is used as a measure of confidence in the location of the inner MIZ. If all thresholds are exceeded within a small geographic area, we have greater confidence in the inner MIZ location. The processing pipeline is depicted in Fig. 5.

Limitations associated with wave-affected MIZ retrieval

Retrieval of wave-affected MIZ width from our satellite radar altimetry is subject to a number of caveats, which we list and discuss briefly here:

The sub-satellite track may not necessarily align with the wave direction, but our choice to report the north-south extent of the MIZ mitigates this to some extent;

Validation of wave-affected MIZ width retrieval

We choose not to use wave-sensing buoy deployments to validate the AltiKa retrievals, primarily since we are unable to retrieve wave-height-in-ice estimates with our technique, and since such deployments are generally designed to quantify wave height, with buoys rarely placed in a configuration suitable to precisely determine the limit of wave penetration. Indeed, a lack of a suitable validation dataset—until now—may have been a limiting factor in extending the early Seasat radar altimeter18. Instead, we cross-compare with independent wave penetration estimates determined from ICESat-2 tracks2. Due to its extremely fine spatial footprint of ~17 m, ICESat-2 laser altimetry is able to accurately determine the extent of wave penetration into the MIZ.

We perform this cross-comparison in two ways: (a) at a monthly summary level, regardless of track longitude (i.e. by comparing the distribution of wave penetration in the four months covered by the ICESat-2 selected-track dataset2: February, May, September and December of 2019); and (b) a more detailed validation investigating cases where cloud-free ICESat-2 and AltiKa passes occurred over the MIZ within six h, and at a similar longitude. Both validation results are presented here.

More robust validation is provided by comparing the MIZ width between all tracks passing over the MIZ within a certain window in time and space. We compare tracks within six h, i.e. the wave decorrelation time-scale2 determined from wave-sensing buoys in the Antarctic MIZ. Detailed validation was attempted in all four months covered by ref. 2, i.e. in February, May, September and December of 2019; however, only September is shown here (Fig. 7). Insufficient ICESat-2 data points were available for comparison in February. In May and December, autocorrelation of MIZ width with day-of-month artificially inflated the correlation coefficient, resulting in unreasonably high correlation coefficients at large spatial separations. We suggest that the origin of this autocorrelation lies in the temporal gradient (across May, and across December) in wave height encountered north of the ice edge: Resulting low wave-affected MIZ widths at the beginning of May and the end of December (compared to the end of May and the beginning of December) give naturally low wave-affected MIZ widths regardless of longitude, and this temporal signal artificially boosts the correlation. Other factors, such as ice thickness and stage of development (likely thin pancake ice in May and thicker ‘rotting’ ice in December), may also contribute. This autocorrelation was unable to be accounted for, so we show September only here (Fig. 7) and extend validation to other months using a wave-ice interaction model (described in the following subsection).

Wave-ice propagation model

We compare the extent of wave penetration determined by AltiKa to that of a standalone global numerical sea ice model with surface wave forcing (Community Ice Code version 6-Waves in Ice Module; CICE6-WIM30), as a way of extending the detailed September validation study to other months. A simple wave-ice propagation module was implemented into the CICE6 sea-ice model30, with a prognostic floe size distribution29, to identify the impacts of waves on the properties of the ice cover. The WIM takes average wave quantities (significant wave height, peak period, and mean wave direction) from a WAVEWATCH III hindcast one grid cell north of the 1% sea-ice concentration edge, where wave-ice interactions are assumed to be negligible. These quantities are used to initialise the prescribed incident frequency-directional wave energy spectrum, Sin. The southward wave energy (i.e. the southward segment of the directional spectrum within due south ±90∘) is then propagated southward. In addition to waves, CICE6-WIM is forced by surface atmospheric61 and oceanic data62. We simulate CICE6-WIM at 0.25∘ (latitude-longitude) resolution from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2019 with daily outputs. We note that with daily outputs, the modelled wave penetration may often differ in time from the AltiKa overpass by greater than the 6-h wave decorrelation timescale mentioned above; we therefore perform a temporal comparison indicating wave-affected MIZ seasonality, instead of a full spatio-temporal comparison.

For our determination of the waves-in-ice region in the model, we analyse only the wave height as it propagates through the icepack in the model—we do not analyse any modelled sea ice properties such as ice thickness or floe size. We investigate three wave height thresholds (30, 5 and 1 cm) and report these in Fig. 8b, as a way of extending the detailed altimeter cross-comparison validation study described above. We note here a very close match in seasonality of wave-affected MIZ width (R2 = 0.85), and a low root-mean-squared error of 25 km when using a 5 cm threshold on wave height (Fig. 8b).
