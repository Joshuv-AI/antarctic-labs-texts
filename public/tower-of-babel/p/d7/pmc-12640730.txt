Role of Medical Editors in the Age of Generative Artificial Intelligence
Authors: Sun Huh
Published: 2025

Less than three years after the introduction of the generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot ChatGPT in November 2022, generative AI has become deeply integrated into daily life. Numerous platforms now exist that can generate not only text but also voice, images, and videos, and they are widely used across diverse fields. Large language models (LLMs) constitute a core sub-technology specialized in language generation and comprehension [1], and they are actively employed in medical and nursing research [2–4].

Authors can use AI for various research-related tasks, including identifying research topics, designing studies, collecting and preprocessing data, analyzing results, generating diagrams, writing manuscripts in accordance with reporting guidelines, searching for references, translating and proofreading English text, and drafting English abstracts [5]. Reviewers can use AI for manuscript evaluation, editors for screening and editing [6] (Figure 1), and publishers for tasks such as manuscript editing and creating Journal Article Tag Suite XML [7] and PDF files. In short, AI can be applied throughout the entire research process—from conception to publication.

Establishing Journal Policy on AI Use

Organizations such as the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, the Council of Science Editors, and the Committee on Publication Ethics have each proposed various policies regarding AI use, and individual journals have also established their own [8]. Editors therefore need to clearly articulate appropriate policies for their journals. For example, Healthcare Informatics Research has published specific guidelines on the use of AI by authors.

A particularly contentious issue concerns whether AI use should be permitted during peer review. Among the 100 top-ranking medical journals, 78 provided guidance on the use of AI in peer review, with 46 of them (59%) explicitly prohibiting such use [9]. Meanwhile, only 18% of 100 surveyed Korean medical journals had guidelines addressing generative AI [10].

Concerns have also arisen that uploading a manuscript or dataset to an AI service could violate confidentiality, as the content might be used for model training. However, OpenAI’s GPT-5 specifies that it does not use user-uploaded materials for training when the emoji icon in the top right corner of the initial screen is selected (Figure 2). Likewise, Google Gemini states that it does not use user-uploaded materials for training. Thus, understanding these platform-specific environments is essential when formulating journal policies.

Competencies and Applications for Medical Editors in the AI Era

As it has become commonplace for authors to prepare and submit manuscripts using AI tools, editors must also be proficient in employing various AI applications. When a manuscript is submitted, an editor should first check that it aligns with the journal’s aims and scope and conforms to style and format requirements during the screening stage. Moreover, an editor can efficiently assess the scientific rigor of a manuscript—particularly whether the study design adheres to the relevant reporting guidelines—by providing precise instructions, or prompts, to an AI system.

For instance, an editor may upload both the manuscript and its corresponding reporting guideline checklist to Google NotebookLM (Figure 3). The editor can then issue a prompt such as, “Check whether the manuscript complies with the reporting guideline and identify points for improvement,” and review the AI-generated output. The editor must be capable of judging whether the AI’s assessment is appropriate and of using the information to decide whether the manuscript should proceed to peer review.

One of the most challenging aspects of manuscript screening is ensuring reproducibility. Many medical journals still do not mandate data sharing. Nevertheless, to enable rigorous peer review, it is necessary to obtain and verify underlying data to confirm reproducibility. AI can be employed to analyze these data and assess reproducibility, and techniques such as vibe coding (an approach allowing data analysis based solely on textual descriptions) have proved particularly useful [11].

As the number of studies analyzing newly available large datasets continues to grow, an increasing number of low-quality papers are also emerging [12,13]. Therefore, editors should instruct AI tools to generate detailed revision suggestions based on the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [14] to enhance the scientific quality of manuscripts. In addition, for studies applying machine learning or LLM-based methods, AI can be used to verify adherence to the TRIPOD+AI statement [15] and the TRIPOD-LLM statement [16].

To acquire these competencies, editors must learn to use various AI tools such as NotebookLM, possess a thorough understanding of reporting guidelines, develop skill in crafting precise prompts, and cultivate the ability to apply vibe coding for efficient data analysis.
