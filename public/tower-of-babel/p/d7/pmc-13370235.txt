Light at the heart of time: How photoreceptors entrain the circadian clock
Authors: Chen Shi, Xiaoyu Jia, Shuai Qi, Hongtao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the primary driver of photosynthesis, light also serves as a crucial environmental cue that directs plant growth and developmental programs. The circadian clock functions as an intrinsic timing mechanism that synchronizes physiological and metabolic rhythms with daily environmental light–dark cycles. Sunlight acts as a major zeitgeber, resetting the clock and activating photoreceptors that modulate the transcription and activity of core oscillator components. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the interactions between distinct photoreceptors and major signaling components of the circadian clock in Arabidopsis thaliana. We emphasize how light perception pathways influence circadian rhythm regulation and how the clock, in turn, feeds back to shape light-dependent responses. We further highlight the pivotal role of light in fine-tuning plant–environment interactions, illustrating how specific wavelengths regulate the circadian system through photoreceptors and downstream signaling factors.

Introduction

Solar energy underpins life on Earth, serving both as the primary source of metabolic energy and as a crucial environmental cue that regulates plant growth and development. This is particularly evident in plants, which are entirely dependent on sunlight. In addition to fueling photosynthesis, sunlight acts as a key environmental factor that coordinates plant development, growth, and metabolism. Sunlight consists of a spectrum of monochromatic light of varying wavelengths. To adapt to and exploit this spectral diversity, plants have evolved specialized photoreceptors capable of distinguishing among different light qualities. Acting in concert, these photoreceptors decode dynamic light signals to regulate plant growth, morphogenesis, and adaptive responses.

The daily cycle of sunrise and sunset creates predictable light–dark rhythms. To perceive and adapt to these cycles, living organisms have evolved an endogenous timekeeping system known as the circadian clock (Nowak et al., 2021; Zhuang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). In plants, the circadian system is typically organized into three interconnected components: environmental input pathways, a central oscillator, and downstream output networks. Input pathways integrate diverse environmental signals—most prominently light and temperature—and transmit this information to the oscillator, thereby synchronizing internal rhythms with external cycles. Light perception primarily depends on photoreceptors, whereas ambient temperature resets circadian timing through its direct effects on protein activity and transcriptional regulation (Jung et al., 2020; Kidokoro et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2025). Other environmental and metabolic signals also regulate the circadian clock (Haydon et al., 2013; Martí Ruiz et al., 2018; Mwimba et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2024). The central oscillator generates self-sustained rhythms through interconnected transcriptional–translational feedback loops. For conceptual clarity, it is typically organized into three modules: the core, morning, and evening loops (Venkat and Muneer, 2022). The core loop consists of the morning-phased transcription factors CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) and LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY), which repress TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION 1 (TOC1), while TOC1 in turn suppresses CCA1 and LHY expression, forming a mutually inhibitory feedback circuit (Pruneda-Paz et al., 2009; Lau et al., 2011). The morning loop is governed by CCA1/LHY, as well as the PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATORs PRR7 and PRR9. At daybreak, light signaling induces CCA1 and LHY expression, which repress the transcription of PRR7 and PRR9. As PRR7/PRR9 proteins accumulate later in the day, they feed back and inhibit CCA1/LHY expression, thereby stabilizing morning-phase oscillations (Farre et al., 2005; Adams et al., 2015; Kamioka et al., 2016). The evening loop centers on the Evening Complex (EC)—composed of LUX ARRHYTHMO (LUX), EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), ELF4,and TOC1. The EC and morning regulators function antagonistically: the EC indirectly promotes CCA1/LHY expression, whereas CCA1/LHY suppress the transcription of EC components. In parallel, TOC1 represses GIGANTEA (GI), which in turn enhances CCA1/LHY activity through a pathway that remains incompletely understood (Sanchez and Kay, 2016). Oscillatory signals generated by the central clock are transmitted to downstream targets through output pathways, enabling rhythmic regulation of a wide range of physiological, metabolic, and developmental processes, including stomatal movement, leaf nyctinasty, flowering time, cell-cycle progression, and hypocotyl elongation. Through this temporal coordination, the circadian system enhances plant fitness and improves adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions (Hsu and Harmer, 2014; Inoue et al., 2018; Creux and Harmer, 2019; Li et al., 2025).

The relationship between sunlight and the plant circadian clock is both intimate and highly structured. Sunlight acts as a dominant zeitgeber, but its information is inherently multidimensional, encompassing spectral quality, intensity, photoperiod, and the temporal dynamics of dawn and dusk. To interpret these complex cues, plants deploy multiple photoreceptors whose signals are hierarchically integrated and relayed to the central oscillator, thereby entraining endogenous rhythms to the 24-h environmental cycle. Importantly, light input to the circadian clock is not transmitted through a single linear pathway, nor does light simply drive oscillator activity. Instead, light signals are filtered, gated, and buffered by the circadian network itself. Oscillator components interact with light-signaling modules in a time-of-day-dependent manner, ensuring that photic inputs modulate phase, amplitude, and period without compromising oscillator robustness. This bidirectional, multilayered coupling between light signaling and the circadian system provides a general framework for understanding how plants achieve precise temporal coordination under fluctuating natural light environments. Reviewing this two-way regulatory framework will deepen our understanding of timekeeping and provide a theoretical basis for precisely manipulating flowering time and optimizing light environments for plant growth.

Regulation of the circadian clock by blue light

Cryptochrome

Cryptochromes (CRYs) function as blue-light photoreceptors (Guo et al., 1998). As early as 1998, CRY1 and CRY2 were identified as key photoreceptors that transmit blue-light information to the Arabidopsis circadian system, with the two isoforms contributing differently to clock regulation (Somers et al., 1998). This study represented the first report demonstrating the involvement of CRYs in circadian clock regulation. Around the same time, a study in Science and two back-to-back articles in Cell revealed similar roles for CRY proteins in the circadian clocks of mice and fruit flies, respectively (Emery et al., 1998; Stanewsky et al., 1998; Thresher et al., 1998). These findings raised an important question: how does blue light regulate the circadian clock through blue-light photoreceptors?

Arabidopsis thaliana possesses three identified members of the CRY family. Among them, CRY1 and CRY2 have been the primary focus of functional studies, whereas CRY3 remains relatively less characterized. In 1980, Koornneef and colleagues described the hy4 mutant, which exhibited a specifically elongated hypocotyl under blue light, indicating that the corresponding gene is involved in blue-light perception (Koornneef et al., 1980). Later, in 1993, the HY4 gene was confirmed to encode a blue-light photoreceptor and was subsequently renamed CRY1 (Ahmad and Cashmore, 1993). Genetic studies revealed that CRY1 overexpression shortens the circadian period under high-intensity blue or white light, whereas cry1 mutants display weakened rhythmicity or a lengthened period (Somers et al., 1998). In addition to CRY1, CRY2 also contributes to circadian regulation through partially overlapping yet mechanistically distinct pathways. Beyond its role in the circadian system, CRY2 also regulates root growth in darkness, temperature responses, and photoperiodic flowering (Ma et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2025). Genetic evidence further shows that loss of CRY2 function prolongs the circadian period under blue light (He et al., 2022). Together, CRY1 and CRY2 function as major blue-light input nodes that transmit information about light intensity and photoperiod to the circadian oscillator, modulating clock phase, amplitude, and period in a light-dependent manner rather than acting as core components of the oscillator itself.

Protein–protein interactions as a key mechanism of CRY signaling

At the protein level, blue light-activated CRY1 interacts with the CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1)/SUPPRESSOR OF phyA-105 (SPA) complex, thereby modulating the stability of the transcriptional regulator ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), a central factor that promotes photomorphogenesis and links light signaling to circadian gene expression (Wang et al., 2001; Yang et al., 2001; Saijo et al., 2003; Li et al., 2011). COP1, an evolutionarily conserved regulator across eukaryotes, functions as a key regulatory hub controlling genome-wide transcriptional responses to light (Nan and Hou, 2025). Acting as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, COP1 negatively regulates photomorphogenic development by promoting the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of light-regulated transcription factors (Deng et al., 1991; Huang et al., 2014; Han et al., 2020). SPA1, a negative regulator of photomorphogenesis, suppresses light signaling by interacting with PHYA and forming a functional complex with COP1. The COP1–SPA1 complex inhibits light-induced developmental processes by promoting the degradation of key transcription factors in the light signaling pathway (Holm et al., 2002; Tao et al., 2025). Upon blue-light activation, CRY1 associates with the gibberellin (GA) receptor GA INSENSITIVE DWARF1 (GID1), as well as DELLA repressors of GA signaling, thereby interfering with the interaction between DELLAs and GID1. This disruption leads to the accumulation of DELLA proteins, which subsequently bind to PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs), promoting their degradation and attenuating their ability to associate with the promoters of target genes (Zhong et al., 2021) (Figure 1A; Table 1). PIFs belong to the basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) family of transcription factors and were originally identified as major downstream mediators of photoreceptor signaling, particularly that of PHYB (Ni et al., 1998). Upon blue-light activation, CRY2 interacts with the COP1/SPA1 complex, disrupting its ability to suppress key transcription factors, notably HY5 and CONSTANS (CO), which integrates circadian and photoperiodic signals, thereby indirectly influencing both clock input and rhythmic output (Laubinger et al., 2006; Jang et al., 2008) (Figure 1E; Table 1).

Transcriptional regulation downstream of CRY signaling

At the transcriptional level, CRY1 associates with PIF4 and PIF5, attenuating their transcriptional activity on downstream target genes (Ma et al., 2016; Pedmale et al., 2016) (Figure 1B; Table 1).

In addition to its role in light perception, CRY2 fine-tunes circadian rhythms through multiple coordinated mechanisms. Upon blue-light activation, CRY2 binds the core circadian regulator PRR9, disrupting its interaction with TOPLESS (TPL) and preventing phosphorylation by PPK kinases. These actions modulate PRR9 transcriptional activity and ultimately influence the pace of the circadian clock (He et al., 2022). Similarly, CRY2 interacts with PRR5 to relieve its transcriptional repression of CCA1 and LHY; the CRY2–PRR5 interaction also promotes dissociation of the PRR5–TPL complex, further contributing to circadian clock regulation (Shi et al., 2025). Together, these parallel mechanisms illustrate how CRY2 modulates clock function by tuning the repressive activities of key oscillator components. Beyond regulating the central oscillator, CRY2 also integrates light signals with developmental programs. For example, its interaction with the transcription factor CRY2-INTERACTING bHLH1 (CIB1) activates the expression of the flowering regulator FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), thereby modulating photoperiodic flowering time (Liu et al., 2008). In addition, CRY2, CIB1, and CO form a complex that activates FT transcription in a blue light-dependent manner. The formation of this complex is photoperiod-regulated and peaks at dusk, coinciding with elevated FT expression (Liu et al., 2018). Concurrently, CRY2 physically interacts with PIF4 and PIF5 to suppress their transcriptional activity (Pedmale et al., 2016). Recent studies have revealed an additional mechanism of CRY2 action. Blue light-activated CRY2 forms a complex with the transcription factor TCP DOMAIN PROTEIN 22 (TCP22) within nuclear photobodies, which recruit PHOTOREGULATORY PROTEIN KINASE 1 (PPK1) and LIGHT-REGULATED WD 1 (LWD1). This condensate upregulates CCA1 expression through recognition of the TCP-binding site (TBS) in the CCA1 promoter, thereby integrating blue light signals into the central circadian pacemaker (Mo et al., 2022) (Figure 1F; Table 1). This phase-separation-based regulatory mechanism expands our understanding of how CRY2 orchestrates circadian regulation at multiple levels.

Chromatin-associated regulation of circadian outputs by CRY1

At the epigenetic level, blue light-activated CRY1 interacts with SERRATED LEAVES AND EARLY FLOWERING (SEF/SWC6) and ACTIN-RELATED PROTEIN 6 (ARP6) to enhance the activity of the ATP-dependent SWR1 chromatin-remodeling complex. Through HY5, this complex is subsequently recruited to specific HY5-binding loci, where it facilitates H2A.Z deposition and modulates the transcriptional output of HY5 target genes (Mao et al., 2021) (Figure 1C; Table 1).

Post-transcriptional regulation and RNA metabolism controlled by CRYs

CRY1 also directly interacts with FKBP12 INTERACTING PROTEIN 37 (FIP37), a core subunit of the m6A methyltransferase complex. This interaction alters the m6A methylation status of PIF transcripts, thereby affecting their mRNA stability and abundance (Yang et al., 2023) (Figure 1D; Table 1).

Blue light-induced liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) of CRY2 leads to the formation of photobodies that recruit core components of either the METTL3/14-type N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methyltransferase complex (including MTA, MTB, and FIP37) or the METTL16-type m6A writer complex (composed of FIONA1 [FIO1] and SPA1). These condensates enhance m6A deposition on target mRNAs, including transcripts of core circadian regulators such as CCA1 and LHY, thereby fine-tuning circadian rhythms and photomorphogenesis by modulating mRNA stability and translational efficiency (Wang et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2023). In addition, under blue light, CRY2 interacts with the splicing factor CRY2-INTERACTING SPLICING FACTOR 1 (CIS1) to regulate the alternative splicing of FLOWERING LOCUS M (FLM), thereby fine-tuning plant responses to temperature and photoperiod (Zhao et al., 2022) (Figure 1G; Table 1).

ZTL/FKF1/LKP2

In addition to CRYs, Arabidopsis harbors a distinct group of blue-light photoreceptors characterized by the presence of LOV (light, oxygen, or voltage) domains, including ZEITLUPE (ZTL), FLAVIN-BINDING KELCH REPEAT F-BOX 1 (FKF1), and LOV KELCH PROTEIN 2 (LKP2) (Demarsy and Fankhauser, 2009). The ztl mutant exhibits an extended circadian period, whereas overexpression of ZTL further prolongs the rhythmic cycle or can even lead to arrhythmicity (Somers et al., 2004).

Post-translational control of core clock protein stability by ZTL

ZTL reaches maximal expression in the evening and promotes the proteasomal degradation of the evening-phased oscillators TOC1 and PRR5 (Mas et al., 2003; Kiba et al., 2007; Fujiwara et al., 2008). By facilitating the turnover of TOC1 and PRR5, ZTL enhances the responsiveness of hypocotyl elongation to high-temperature conditions (Seo et al., 2023). During the day, blue light-activated ZTL interacts with GI. GI simultaneously recruits HEAT SHOCK PROTEINS 70 and 90 (HSP70/90), which in turn facilitate ZTL maturation and stabilization. Additionally, GI recruits the deubiquitinating enzymes UBIQUITIN-SPECIFIC PROTEASE 12 (UBP12) and UBP13, thereby preventing ZTL degradation during the light period. Under these conditions, the ZTL–GI–UBP12/13 complex suppresses the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of ZTL, protecting core clock proteins such as TOC1 and PRR5 from proteasomal degradation (Kim et al., 2007, 2011; Cha et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2019) (Figure 1H). Moreover, PRR3 directly associates with TOC1, disrupting its interaction with ZTL and thereby protecting TOC1 from ZTL-dependent proteolysis (Para et al., 2007). Similarly, PRR5 interacts with TOC1 and promotes its accumulation in the nucleus, preventing ZTL-mediated cytoplasmic degradation (Wang et al., 2010) (Figure 1H; Table 1).

FKF1-centered integration of circadian timing and photoperiodic flowering

In addition, ZTL interacts with FKF1 and GI, sequestering the FKF1–GI complex in the cytosol. This interaction interferes with FKF1–GI-mediated degradation of CYCLING DOF FACTOR 1/2 (CDF1/2) and reduces CO stability in the nucleus, ultimately repressing flowering (Imaizumi et al., 2005; Zoltowski and Imaizumi, 2014). Two core circadian transcription factors, NIGHT LIGHT-INDUCIBLE AND CLOCK-REGULATED GENE 1 (LNK1) and LNK2, directly bind the FKF1 promoter and promote its expression, thereby activating FT transcription (Gao et al., 2025). Under long-day (LD) conditions, the FKF1 homodimer accumulates and stabilizes CO in the afternoon. Later in the evening, blue light-activated FKF1 associates with GI, converting FKF1 from a homodimer into a monomer. The FKF1 monomer subsequently interacts with COP1, inhibiting COP1 homodimerization and thereby reducing its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, which further enhances CO stability in the afternoon (Lee et al., 2017; Cho et al., 2024) (Figure 1H; Table 1). Collectively, these findings highlight the pivotal role of FKF1 as a blue-light integrator that coordinates circadian timing with photoperiodic flowering.

Additional transcriptional regulators of blue light–clock signaling

COLD-REGULATED GENE 27 (COR27) and COR28, whose expression is transcriptionally repressed by blue light, are key regulators of the circadian clock. Their transcription is activated by CCA1 and LHY in the morning, and their protein levels peak in the evening before rapidly declining at night, exhibiting typical diurnal oscillations. COR27 and COR28 proteins associate with the promoter regions of evening-phased genes such as TOC1 and PRR5, repressing their transcription and thereby shortening the circadian period (Figure 1I). Consistent with this role, the cor27 cor28 double mutant displays an extended circadian period (Li et al., 2016).

Conservation and diversification of blue light-mediated circadian regulation in crops

In soybean (Glycine max), the blue-light receptor GmFKF1 is activated by blue light and subsequently interacts with GmGI (E2) to form a complex that promotes the degradation of JUVENILE (J), the soybean homolog of Arabidopsis ELF3, thereby modulating the activity of the EC. The EC, in turn, binds to the promoter of E2 and represses its transcription, forming a feedback loop that fine-tunes photoperiodic responses (Zhao et al., 2024). Similarly, in rice (Oryza sativa), OsFKF1 physically interacts with OsGI and OsCDF1 (also referred to as OsDOF12), analogous to the FKF1–GI–CDF1 module in Arabidopsis. These findings suggest that the FKF1–GI–CDF signaling hub is evolutionarily conserved across plant species (Han et al., 2015).

Regulation of the circadian clock by red light

Phytochrome

Red light, another major component of sunlight, also plays an important role in regulating the plant circadian clock. The phytochromes PHYA and PHYB function as primary red/far-red light photoreceptors and operate under different light intensities: PHYB predominantly mediates circadian regulation under high-intensity red light, whereas PHYA functions mainly under low-intensity conditions. Notably, CRY1 and PHYA can also transmit low-intensity blue-light signals to the circadian clock, indicating crosstalk between blue- and red-light signaling pathways (Somers et al., 1998).

Protein interaction networks linking PHYs to the circadian oscillator

In their light-activated states, both PHYA and PHYB interact with the COP1/SPA1 complex, thereby modulating the stability of light- and photoperiod-associated regulators such as HY5 and CO. Through these interactions, phytochromes indirectly influence both the input and output pathways of the circadian oscillator (Sheerin et al., 2015). In addition, phytochromes participate extensively in protein–protein interaction networks with core circadian components. Early studies demonstrated that the core EC component ELF3 interacts with PHYB in both yeast and in vitro systems (Liu et al., 2001). Later evidence further expanded this interaction network, showing that PHYB associates with multiple core oscillator components, including LUX, CCA1, LHY, TOC1, and GI, although the precise functional consequences of these interactions remain to be fully elucidated (Yeom et al., 2014) (Figure 2C).

Transcriptional regulation of circadian genes downstream of PHY signaling

The EC component ELF3 promotes morning-phased transcription of LHY and CCA1 by repressing the expression of PRR9 and PRR7. Under ambient light conditions, phytochrome-mediated signaling inputs can modulate the circadian clock by attenuating the repressive activity of ELF3 (Kolmos et al., 2011).

Upon exposure to low red/far-red (R:FR) ratios accompanied by reduced photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), PHYA promotes accumulation of core circadian regulators such as TOC1, PRR7, ELF3, and ELF4 in the early evening. These regulators collectively suppress stem elongation, thereby mitigating the shade-avoidance growth response typically observed under canopy shading. The transcription factor REVEILLE 8 (RVE8) is required for PHYA-dependent induction of TOC1 (Fraser et al., 2021) (Figure 2B).

FHY3/FAR1-centered transcriptional feedback loops in PHY-mediated circadian control

FAR-RED ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 3 (FHY3) and FAR-RED IMPAIRED RESPONSE 1 (FAR1) were initially characterized as transcription factors that mediate far-red light signaling downstream of PHYA (Wang and Deng, 2002). Within the circadian network, FHY3 and FAR1 bind to the promoter regions of CCA1, LHY, and ELF4, thereby promoting their transcription at dawn. In the afternoon, PIF5 and TOC1 proteins accumulate and interact with FHY3, thereby inhibiting the DNA-binding activity of FHY3 and consequently suppressing CCA1 transcription (Ma and Li, 2018; Liu et al., 2020). Moreover, after reaching their morning peak, CCA1/LHY proteins themselves interact with FHY3/FAR1 to repress FHY3/FAR1-mediated activation of ELF4 transcription, thereby establishing a reciprocal feedback loop within the circadian network (Li et al., 2011).

FHY3 and FAR1 primarily function in the PHYA signaling pathway by regulating FHY1 and FAR-RED ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 1-LIKE (FHL), which are essential for PHYA nuclear import. Subsequent studies have shown that FHY3 and FAR1 also act downstream of the light-stable phytochromes PHYB, PHYD, and PHYE. These red-light receptors enhance the abundance of FHY3 protein, and FHY3/FAR1-dependent induction of ELF4 expression requires the presence of these stable phytochromes (Siddiqui et al., 2016) (Figure 2D).

Additional transcriptional regulators of PHY–clock signaling

In addition to FHY3/FAR1, other transcriptional regulators, including WRKY DNA-BINDING PROTEIN 2 (WRKY2) and WRKY10, also contribute to the integration of red/far-red light signaling with the circadian clock. These WRKY factors promote the diurnal rhythmic expression of PIF4 through cooperative interactions with CCA1/LHY and PHYB during the day. Specifically, WRKY2 and WRKY10 bind to cis-elements located near MYB motifs within the PIF4 promoter that are also recognized by CCA1/LHY and SHORT HYPOCOTYL UNDER BLUE 1 (SHB1). These interactions enhance the recruitment of CCA1/LHY to this locus. Both WRKY proteins also physically associate with PHYB, further stabilizing CCA1/LHY binding to the PIF4 promoter and reinforcing circadian regulation of PIF4 transcription (Wang et al., 2022a) (Figure 2D; Table 1).

In addition, light promotes the expression of LNK1 and LNK2 through PHY-dependent mechanisms, which subsequently enhance the expression of afternoon-phased genes such as PRR5 and TOC1 (through interactions with RVE proteins) as well as ELF4. Transcriptional repression of LNK1/2 during the afternoon and evening is mediated by the direct binding of PRR9, PRR7, PRR5, and TOC1 to their promoters (Rugnone et al., 2013; Xie et al., 2014; Shalit-Kaneh et al., 2018) (Figure 2E).

Conservation and diversification of red light-mediated circadian regulation in crops

PHY-mediated circadian regulation is broadly conserved across plant species but shows diversification in specific signaling modules. In soybean, E3 and E4 (GmPHYA3 and GmPHYA2, respectively) function as phytochromes that perceive photoperiodic changes under red and far-red light. E3/E4 directly interact with LUX, a core component of the EC, and this interaction promotes LUX protein degradation (Lin et al., 2022). Additionally, E3/E4 enhance the expression of the circadian clock genes PRR3a (Tof11) and PRR3b (Tof12), whose encoded proteins subsequently repress LHY expression (Lu et al., 2020). In rice, OsELF3-1 physically interacts with OsPHYB, attenuating phytochrome signaling and thereby modulating flowering time. Moreover, OsPHYB acts as an upstream activator of OsPRR37 (a homolog of Arabidopsis PRR7), inducing its transcription upon light perception (Hu et al., 2021). At the protein level, activated OsPHYA/B can antagonize OsGI function by sequestering and stabilizing Ghd7, a major photoperiodic flowering repressor, thereby delaying flowering under LD conditions (Zheng et al., 2019).

Regulation of the circadian clock by UV-B and green light

As a component of sunlight, ultraviolet-B (UV-B) radiation acts at low intensities as an important environmental signal perceived by the plant-specific photoreceptor UV RESISTANCE LOCUS 8 (UVR8). UV-B fine-tunes plant growth, development, and stress adaptation by triggering key photomorphogenic responses, including inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, induction of flavonoid biosynthesis, and enhancement of resistance to environmental stresses (Jenkins, 2017; Chen et al., 2022). Recent studies indicate that UV-B modulates both the period and phase of the plant circadian clock through UVR8, while the circadian clock, in turn, regulates UV-B-responsive gene expression and UV-B stress tolerance. Specifically, UV-B induces the transcription of several core clock genes, including CCA1, PRR9, GI, and ELF4, thereby resetting the circadian oscillator. Supplementation with low-intensity UV-B under continuous white light significantly shortens the free-running circadian period. Both UVR8 and COP1 are required for this UV-B-mediated clock regulation, whereas HY5 and HYH are dispensable. In addition, the circadian clock gates plant responses to UV-B by rhythmically modulating their sensitivity. This gating mechanism restricts the expression of most UV-B-induced genes to specific times of day, thereby preventing the activation of energetically costly response programs during biologically irrelevant periods, such as nighttime (Feher et al., 2011).

Under short-day (SD) conditions, UV-B radiation markedly delays flowering in wild-type plants, an effect that is partially dependent on UVR8. In contrast, under LD conditions, UV-B significantly increases the expression of FT in a UVR8-dependent manner but does not alter flowering time in either wild-type or uvr8 mutants, likely due to offsetting promotive and inhibitory effects under LD conditions (Takahashi et al., 2025). Under UV-B–noninducible SD conditions, the negative regulator REPRESSOR OF UV-B PHOTOMORPHOGENESIS 2 (RUP2) binds to CO and suppresses its transcriptional activity, thereby delaying the floral transition (Arongaus et al., 2018).

Under strong UV-B irradiation, numerous DNA damage repair genes—including the photolyases PHOTOLYASE 1/UV RESISTANCE 2 (UVR2) and UVR3, as well as the nucleotide excision repair gene UVH1—exhibit pronounced diurnal oscillations. This coordinated regulation suggests that plants have evolved a highly integrated adaptive mechanism that couples DNA repair with circadian and photoperiodic regulation (Horak and Farre, 2015). UV-B also indirectly influences circadian-regulated outputs by reshaping photomorphogenic signaling networks involving COP1, HY5, and PIFs. Upon UV-B perception, activated UVR8 binds to and sequesters COP1, resulting in stabilization of HY5 and suppression of PIF activity and abundance. Through PIF-dependent transcriptional reprogramming, UV-B signaling can influence circadian-regulated growth and physiological outputs (Tilbrook et al., 2013; Sharma et al., 2019; Tavridou et al., 2020).

Although the primary wavelengths driving photosynthesis are blue-violet and red light—and much green light is reflected by leaves—accumulating evidence indicates that green light also exerts important regulatory functions in plant physiology. Recent studies have shown that green light modulates hypocotyl elongation via the brassinosteroid signaling pathway (Hao et al., 2023) and regulates noncanonical photomorphogenesis by influencing phytochrome signaling (Xu et al., 2024). Notably, spectrally purified short-wavelength green light (∼527 nm, 1–16 μmol·m−2·s−1) is sufficient to sustain CCA1/TOC1 luciferase rhythms in Arabidopsis (Battle and Jones, 2020). Under CO2-limiting conditions, the amplitude of these rhythms is reduced and the circadian period is lengthened; however, exogenous sucrose restores rhythmicity in cry mutants, suggesting that photosynthetic outputs and CRY signaling act synergistically to regulate circadian phase (Sellaro et al., 2010; Battle and Jones, 2020). Consistently, cry1 and cry2 mutants exhibit an approximately one-hour period lengthening under constant green light, further supporting a role for CRYs in mediating green-light input to the circadian system. Mechanistically, the semi-reduced flavin (FADH⋅) in blue light-activated CRYs absorbs green light and is converted to its inactive, fully reduced state (FADH−), thereby attenuating blue-light signaling outputs, including FT expression and flowering regulation (Banerjee et al., 2007; Bouly et al., 2007). Under natural canopy conditions, shading reduces the blue/green light ratio (to approximately 0.5), and such spectral shifts can modulate clock gene expression through CRY1 (Sellaro et al., 2010). However, compared with blue and red light, the roles of green and ultraviolet wavelengths in circadian regulation remain poorly characterized. Current evidence is derived primarily from isolated physiological and reporter-based assays, with comprehensive mechanistic understanding remaining limited.

We analyzed transcriptome datasets in our laboratory together with published datasets for UV-B and green light treatments. The circadian rhythm pathway is significantly enriched among differentially expressed genes under both UV-B and green light conditions. Several core circadian components, including PRRs, CCA1, LHY, and GI, are differentially regulated by UV-B or green light (Figure 3). Regulation of the circadian clock by core light-signaling components

COP1

Photoactivated photoreceptors suppress the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of COP1, thereby preventing COP1-mediated repression of light-responsive transcription factors (Hoecker, 2017; Podolec and Ulm, 2018). In the absence of light, COP1 targets key transcription factors—including HY5 and HYH—for 26S proteasome-mediated degradation (Holm et al., 2002). This process suppresses photomorphogenesis and the expression of circadian rhythm-associated genes. Upon light exposure, activated photoreceptors inhibit COP1 activity, leading to stabilization of its downstream transcriptional regulators.

Beyond its role in seedling development, COP1 plays a pivotal role in photoperiodic flowering by modulating the stability of CO. In darkness, COP1 promotes CO degradation, thereby delaying flowering (Jang et al., 2008). In parallel, COP1 mediates the light-dependent degradation of CONSTANS-LIKE 12 (COL12), which indirectly derepresses CO activity and fine-tunes the transcription of flowering-related genes (Ordonez-Herrera et al., 2018) (Figure 4A). COP1 also interfaces directly with core components of the circadian network. For example, the EC component ELF3 physically interacts with COP1 in a dark-dependent manner, thereby linking COP1 to GI and promoting its degradation. This process indirectly influences CO expression and modulates light input to the circadian oscillator. In this context, ELF3 itself is ubiquitinated and degraded via the COP1 pathway, which destabilizes the EC and consequently affects circadian rhythm maintenance (Yu et al., 2008) (Figure 4A; Table 1).

In addition to directly interacting with and promoting the degradation of COR27 and COR28 in darkness (Li et al., 2020b; Zhu et al., 2020), the COP1–SPA complex also functions as a positive regulator of skotomorphogenesis by stabilizing PIF proteins. Specifically, COP1–SPA promotes the accumulation of PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5, thereby reinforcing the transcriptional network that drives skotomorphogenic growth (Martinez-Garcia et al., 2000; Tavridou et al., 2020) (Figure 4A). In darkness, the COP1–SPA complex utilizes PIF1 as a cofactor to target positive regulators of photomorphogenesis, including HY5 and HFR1, for degradation. Upon illumination, activated PHYB translocates into the nucleus and interacts with the PIF1–SPA1 module, initiating a phosphorylation cascade. The phosphorylated PIF1 is subsequently recognized by the CUL4–COP1–SPA E3 ligase complex and targeted for polyubiquitination and 26S proteasome-mediated degradation (Xu et al., 2014; Paik et al., 2019). This mechanism effectively links nuclear light perception to the repression of PIF-centered transcriptional networks.

The negative regulator DE-ETIOLATED 1 (DET1) also participates in light signaling by forming a complex with COP1 to modulate transcription factor stability. DET1 directly associates with PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5, stabilizing these proteins in darkness and synergistically repressing photomorphogenesis (Dong et al., 2014). As a transcriptional repressor, DET1 also binds the evening element (EE) in the promoters of TOC1 and GI, thereby inhibiting their transcription through interactions with the core circadian regulators CCA1 and LHY (Lau et al., 2011). In parallel, CSU4 fine-tunes circadian rhythms and growth by repressing CCA1 at dawn and PIF4 in the evening. This temporal regulation is mediated by the direct binding of CSU4 to CCA1, which blocks CCA1 transcriptional activity, establishing CSU4 as an important node integrating light signaling with circadian regulation (Zhao et al., 2018) (Figures 4A and 4B; Table 1).

HY5

The basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor HY5 is a central regulator of light-mediated transcription. It recognizes ACE (ACGT-containing) cis-elements in the promoters of light-responsive genes and promotes photomorphogenesis, functioning as a master transcriptional activator in light signaling (Lee et al., 2007). Beyond this canonical role, accumulating evidence indicates that HY5 also exerts extensive regulatory functions within the circadian clock network (Sanchez et al., 2020). Under blue-light conditions, HY5 occupies the promoters of numerous circadian or circadian-related genes, including CCA1, LHY, TOC1, PRR5, PRR7, PRR9, ELF3, ELF4, and LUX. Consistent with this regulatory role, most rhythmic genes display a shortened circadian period in the hy5 hyh mutant under blue light. Moreover, the expression levels of PRR5, LUX, and BOA are significantly elevated in this mutant background (Hajdu et al., 2018). In the evening, HY5 physically interacts with FHY3/FAR1 and co-binds the ELF4 promoter to activate ELF4 transcription, thereby contributing to evening-phased clock gene regulation (Li et al., 2011).

HY5 accumulation is strongly light-quality-dependent, with higher levels under blue light than under red light. Accordingly, its association with circadian gene promoters is significantly enhanced under blue light, suggesting that HY5 may function as a spectral-ratio decoder that interprets the blue:red light composition of the white-light environment to fine-tune circadian rhythmicity (Hajdu et al., 2018).

Recent findings further expand the role of HY5 in integrating photoperiodic and circadian signaling. Under LD conditions, HY5 forms a ternary complex with the photoperiodic regulator CO and the circadian component PRR5, which binds to G-box motifs in the promoters of core oscillator genes such as CCA1 and LHY (de Los Reyes et al., 2024; San Martin and Yanovsky, 2024). This HY5–CO–PRR5 complex converts an otherwise repressive transcriptional state into an activated regulatory module, thereby modulating circadian periodicity and enhancing photoperiodic adaptability under LD conditions (Figure 4E; Table 1).

The circadian clock feeds back to modulate light signaling

Light acts as the principal environmental cue that calibrates the circadian clock, fine-tuning its phase and amplitude. In turn, the clock gates photoreceptor activity and downstream signaling with hourly precision, establishing a bidirectional feedback loop between light signaling and the circadian system.

Circadian gating of UVR8-mediated UV-B responses

The circadian clock also modulates UV-B-induced gene expression and the daily sensitivity of plants to UV-B damage. Under 12-h light/12-h dark cycles, wild-type plants exhibit greater sensitivity to UV-B at night, whereas circadian clock mutants lose this temporal difference. Under 6-h light/6-h dark cycles, wild-type seedlings remain more resistant during the subjective day, demonstrating that the circadian clock confers UV-B tolerance when the internal timer predicts daytime conditions. Thus, the circadian system schedules costly UV-B defense responses during periods of highest risk, implementing a “defense-on-demand” strategy (Takeuchi et al., 2014).

Circadian regulation of growth and shade avoidance mediated by PIFs and HY5

PIFs are under tight circadian regulation, with both their transcription and activity exhibiting rhythmic oscillations throughout the diel cycle (Leivar and Monte, 2014). Extensive crosstalk between PIF proteins and core circadian components ensures that PIF-mediated transcriptional activation occurs only during specific phases of the day. In the early night (ZT12–ZT18), TOC1 directly interacts with PIF3 and PIF4 at the promoters of their shared target genes, thereby repressing their transcriptional activation (Soy et al., 2016). Before dawn, PRR9, PRR7, and PRR5 associate with PIF3 and PIF4 and co-occupy the promoters of growth-related genes such as CYCLING DOF FACTOR 5 (CDF5), suppressing their activity (Martin et al., 2018). This circadian gating restricts PIF-induced hypocotyl elongation to the late-night phase, preventing excessive growth. Similarly, PRR9/7/5 interact with PIF7 to inhibit its transcriptional activation capacity, thereby modulating shade-avoidance responses (Zhang et al., 2020). In addition, GI regulates PIF activity through multiple mechanisms, including transcriptional repression, promotion of PIF degradation, and interference with PIF chromatin binding (Nohales et al., 2019). The EC component ELF3 also physically interacts with PIF4, inhibiting its DNA-binding activity (Nieto et al., 2015) (Figure 4C). At the transcriptional level, PRR1/5/7 and the ELF3–ELF4–LUX EC sequentially repress PIF4 and PIF5 expression by binding to their promoter regions, with the EC exerting its repressive effect specifically in the evening (Nusinow et al., 2011; Li et al., 2020a) (Figure 4D). Consistent with these multilayered regulatory mechanisms, PIF transcript levels oscillate rhythmically throughout the day, a pattern directly governed by the circadian clock (Nozue et al., 2007). Through precise temporal control of PIF activity, the circadian clock enables spatiotemporally coordinated growth regulation and balances light competition (shade avoidance) with stress resilience. This regulatory framework not only enhances environmental adaptability and resource-use efficiency but also provides promising molecular targets for agricultural applications, such as optimization of plant architecture under dense planting conditions.

CCA1 directly interacts with HY5 to modulate its binding to G-box elements in the promoter of the CAB (Lhcb) gene, which encodes light-harvesting chlorophyll a/b-binding proteins, thereby maintaining the normal diurnal rhythmic expression of this gene (Andronis et al., 2008). The clock component COR27 is induced by light at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels. The accumulated COR27 protein interacts with the transcription factor HY5, inhibiting its DNA-binding activity. Meanwhile, COR27 itself functions as a transcriptional regulator that binds to the PIF4 promoter, enhancing the transcription of PIF4 and its downstream target genes and ultimately promoting hypocotyl elongation (Zhu et al., 2020) (Figure 4E; Table 2).

Coordination between photosynthesis and the circadian clock

The plant circadian clock not only monitors external light–dark cycles through photoreceptors but also integrates metabolic signals generated by photosynthesis, thereby coupling environmental photoperiods with the cell’s internal energy and metabolic status. Among these signals, sugars represent the most prominent metabolic inputs.

Haydon et al. (2013) first demonstrated that sugars accumulating during the day act as endogenous, light-independent zeitgebers that target the circadian oscillator. A key molecular target of this regulation is the morning-expressed gene PRR7. Under carbon-limited conditions, PRR7 transcription increases, leading to repression of the core morning genes CCA1 and LHY. As photosynthesis accelerates and sugar levels increase, PRR7 expression is suppressed, thereby relieving this repression on CCA1/LHY (Haydon et al., 2013). This regulatory circuit operates through the SnRK1-bZIP63 signaling axis. Under low-energy conditions, SnRK1 phosphorylates and activates bZIP63, which binds to the PRR7 promoter and enhances its expression. As sucrose levels increase, trehalose-6-phosphate (Tre6P) accumulates and inhibits SnRK1, thereby reducing bZIP63 activity and lowering PRR7 expression (Frank et al., 2018). In addition to the SnRK1–bZIP63 module, PIFs serve as key transcriptional hubs linking photosynthetic carbon availability to the circadian oscillator. Shor et al. (2017) demonstrated that the ability of sugars to modulate circadian period and phase is markedly attenuated in pifQ mutants, indicating that PIFs are required for metabolic entrainment of the clock. Mechanistically, PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5 directly bind G-box elements in the promoters of the core clock genes CCA1 and LHY, enabling sucrose-induced transcriptional activation near subjective dawn. Through this mechanism, PIFs integrate metabolic cues derived from photosynthesis with light signaling pathways, ensuring efficient transmission of carbon availability to the core circadian network (Shor et al., 2017). Through these regulatory modules, the oscillator senses the efficiency of daytime carbon assimilation and adjusts its phase and period, particularly the duration of the night and the timing of dawn-associated transcriptional programs.

Sucrose also stabilizes the GI–ZTL complex at dusk. When sucrose levels decline, the complex dissociates, allowing ZTL to degrade its substrates TOC1 and PRR5, thereby further shaping evening-phase oscillator dynamics (Haydon et al., 2017).

Beyond sugars, redox states provide an additional layer of circadian input. Zhou et al. (2015) identified a redox rhythm that runs in parallel with transcriptional oscillations and gates immune responses (Zhou et al., 2015). Paeng et al. (2025) extended this concept to chloroplast-derived signals, showing that the chloroplast NADPH-thioredoxin reductase NTRC generates an organellar redox rhythm that communicates with the nuclear clock and modulates CCA1 expression. Conversely, TOC1 represses NTRC transcription, thereby synchronizing chloroplast and nuclear rhythms and directly linking organellar metabolism to the central oscillator (Paeng et al., 2025). Román et al. (2021) further demonstrated that sucrose-induced superoxide production amplifies circadian amplitude at dusk, indicating that photosynthetic metabolites shape circadian dynamics through both carbon and redox cues (Roman et al., 2021).

In the opposite direction, the circadian clock exerts prospective control over photosynthesis. Harmer et al. (2000) first demonstrated genome-wide circadian regulation of genes encoding light-harvesting complexes (e.g., the LHCB family) and carbon-fixation enzymes, with expression peaking shortly before dawn, thereby preparing plants for the upcoming light period (Harmer et al., 2000). CCA1 directly binds to the promoters of Lhcb1/3 and other photosynthetic genes, providing a direct molecular link between the circadian oscillator and photosynthetic gene expression (Wang et al., 1997). The circadian clock also regulates the GOLDEN2-LIKE (GLK) transcription factor network to coordinate photosynthetic gene expression and sends nucleus-encoded timing signals that rhythmically control chloroplast transcription, thereby influencing chloroplast biogenesis and photosynthetic capacity (Waters et al., 2009; Noordally et al., 2013). In contrast, nuclear circadian transcript oscillations remain unaffected, indicating that phototropins specifically maintain the robustness of photosynthetic output rhythms at the chloroplast level and do not function as components of the nuclear circadian oscillator (Litthauer et al., 2015, 2016).

Concluding remarks, remaining questions, and perspectives

As a dominant environmental signal, sunlight entrains the plant circadian clock by directly modulating its input pathways, thereby synchronizing internal timing with the external light–dark cycle. This process begins with photon perception by photoreceptors, including PHYs, CRYs, ZTL, and UVR8, and is subsequently transmitted through central signaling components such as COP1, HY5, and PIFs. These signaling modules ultimately converge on core oscillator genes (e.g., CCA1, LHY, PRRs, and GI), leading to precise adjustment of circadian rhythms. Despite their distinct photochemical properties, blue- and red-light photoreceptors regulate the circadian clock through shared design principles. Rather than functioning as core oscillator components, CRYs and PHYs modulate clock parameters—such as phase, amplitude, and pace—by converging on a limited set of regulatory nodes, including the COP1–SPA complex, HY5, PIFs, and PRR proteins. Consequently, light entrainment emerges from a distributed and multilayered regulatory network that integrates transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and biophysical mechanisms, rather than operating through a single linear pathway.

In addition to direct light signaling, sunlight also influences circadian rhythms indirectly by modulating other physiological processes, including photosynthesis, temperature responses, and hormone signaling. These multilayered mechanisms underscore the central role of light in circadian regulation and highlight the intricate, highly coordinated interplay between light signaling and circadian oscillation. Through this integration, plants align their internal rhythms with external environmental cues, thereby optimizing growth, development, and reproductive success. Despite substantial progress in elucidating the molecular links between light signaling and the circadian clock, many mechanistic details remain unresolved and warrant further investigation.1)How light inputs are integrated by the circadian network system: Although most photoreceptors (e.g., CRY, PHY, and UVR8) can suppress COP1 activity and thereby promote HY5 accumulation, their respective circadian phenotypes are not identical. Likewise, CRY2 binds to and inhibits PRR5 and PRR9 to regulate the clock, yet prr5 and prr9 mutants display opposite circadian phenotypes. Furthermore, although COP1 represses photomorphogenesis by targeting HY5 for degradation, both cop1 and hy5 mutants exhibit short-period circadian rhythms. These observations indicate that the circadian clock is not simply a downstream readout of the COP1–HY5 regulatory module. Rather, such paradoxes highlight that light entrainment of the plant circadian clock is not mediated by a single dominant pathway but instead emerges from multilayered, context-dependent integration of photoreceptor signals operating across transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and biophysical regulatory levels. Beyond their classical roles in light perception and signal transduction, photoreceptors such as CRYs and PHYs are increasingly recognized as participants in hormone signaling, temperature responses, and metabolic regulation. For instance, both CRYs and PHYs contribute to the temperature-dependent modulation of circadian timing and flowering, indicating extensive crosstalk between light and thermal signaling pathways. Despite these advances, the molecular mechanisms through which photoreceptors interact with diverse signaling components to fine-tune circadian regulation remain largely unresolved. This spatial heterogeneity suggests that different organs or cell populations may perceive and process environmental cues in distinct ways to optimize growth and environmental adaptation. An important question is whether differences in photoreceptor activity and clock component expression confer local circadian autonomy. For example, CRY2 has been reported to remain active in darkness in roots, and circadian components exhibit distinct expression patterns among the root, leaf, and shoot apical meristem. Phase transitions can occur within seconds, allowing the circadian clock to adjust its phase more rapidly than transcription–translation feedback loops. Thus, photoreceptor condensates are more than signal amplifiers; they function as intracellular spectro–phase computing centers, whose physicochemical properties enable plants to recalibrate circadian timing with sub-hour precision in naturally complex light environments. As such, they merit exploration as a distinct regulatory paradigm.

To address these gaps, more advanced instrumentation and integrated experimental strategies are urgently needed. The central goal of future research is to break away from traditional paradigms focused on single pathways or regulatory layers and instead decode the integrated regulatory network linking light signaling to the circadian clock. This network operates across multiple biological scales—from molecules to cells, organs, and whole plants—and across multiple regulatory layers, including transcriptional, translational, and biophysical processes. For example, single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics can be applied to resolve the spatiotemporal specificity of light–clock interactions. Plant phenomics platforms equipped with cooled CCD cameras enable real-time monitoring of circadian phenotypes under complex environmental gradients. In addition, the use of circadian output genes as reporters to monitor expression under different light spectra may facilitate the discovery of novel photoreceptors and previously unknown clock components. Looking ahead, the circadian regulatory functions of photoreceptor-derived nuclear condensates are likely to emerge as a key link connecting biophysical processes with rhythmic phenotypes. At the same time, investigations into the spatiotemporal specificity and environmental adaptability of circadian regulation will provide a theoretical foundation for improving crop photosynthetic efficiency and stress tolerance.
