Gender Disparities in the Prevalence, Trends, and Determinants of Later‐Life Urinary Incontinence: A Disproportionate Burden on Women
Authors: Xiyin Chen, Shaoxiang Jiang, Muqi Guo, Hao Zhang, Mouwang Zhou, Yao Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesUrinary incontinence (UI) significantly deteriorates the quality of life in the elderly, with a disproportionately higher burden among women. With populations aging in the USA and the UK, understanding the gender‐specific prevalence, determinants, and future care needs in UI is critical for shaping gender‐responsive health policies and care strategies.MethodsData from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS, 2010–2020) and the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA, 2009–2019) involving participants aged 50 and older were analyzed. UI was assessed through self‐reported urinary leakage during the last 12 months. The prevalence, determinants, and future care needs were estimated by gender.ResultsThe analysis included 111,049 and 52,972 observations from HRS and ELSA, respectively. The findings revealed a steady increase in UI prevalence during the 2010s in males (HRS 11.2%–15.1%, ELSA 7.2%–12.0%) and females (HRS 31.4%–36.3%, ELSA 21.0%–28.1%). Contributory factors included aging, higher education levels, and comorbidities. Projections indicate a rise in UI‐related care needs in the USA (HRS Female: from 22.1 to 25.7 million, Male: from 7.8 to 9.8 million) and the UK (ELSA Female: from 3.6 to 4.2 million, Male: from 1.4 to 1.6 million) during the 2020s, with women accounting for 65% of this growth, among which the fastest growth was among those aged 80 and over.ConclusionsThe rising prevalence of UI, particularly among women, alongside population aging in the USA and UK necessitates an escalation in gender‐responsive expansion of care services. Gender‐sensitive counseling on tailored prevention strategies and care services such as weight loss and Kegel exercises should be provided to support quality of life and healthy aging, especially in women.

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) is a prevalent condition, impacting not only the individual's quality of life but also imposing care burdens on societies and families. Compared to those without UI, people with UI use more primary care and specialist visits and have much higher health care costs [1]. UI care intrigues billions of dollars in both the USA and UK [1]. UI is also associated with a reduced quality of life (QoL), increasing psychological distress, and lower self‐esteem [2]. Caregivers of the elderly with UI often experience physical and mental strain compared to those without UI [3]. Thus, understanding UI patterns, trends, related care needs is crucial for advocating for efficient and tailored healthcare.

Gender disparities in UI are well documented but remain insufficiently addressed in both clinical practice and health policy [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Women are significantly more likely than men to experience UI, particularly stress and mixed types, due to a range of biological, hormonal, and reproductive factors—including pelvic floor trauma during childbirth and postmenopausal hormonal changes [13, 14]. However, the disparity extends beyond biology: sociocultural norms often lead women to normalize UI symptoms, delay care‐seeking, and underreport severity [15]. Conversely, men with UI may face stigma that further suppresses reporting and treatment uptake. These gendered patterns affect not only prevalence and symptom burden, but also health system engagement and access to appropriate care [16]. Revisiting these disparities in the context of demographic aging is essential to designing equitable and gender‐responsive approaches to UI prevention and management.

Additionally, most prior studies were either cross‐sectional without projection of future trends using small sample sizes, or were conducted over a decade ago. Despite two studies forecasting UI care needs in 2027 and 2050 in the US, neither provided a gender‐specific breakdown nor a yearly forecast through 2030 [17, 18]. This gap in the literature hinders the ability of health systems to anticipate and address emerging gender disparities in UI burden over time.

To fill the gap, we utilized two nationally representative prospective cohorts from the US and UK to analyze the UI patterns and trends related care needs over a decade across both genders in a population age 50 and over. By offering the gender‐specific forecasts of UI burden and care needs through 2030 and a comparative cross‐country analysis that situates the US and UK in a global aging context, this study delivers comprehensive and timely evidence to inform the development of gender‐responsive policies and interventions to meet the growing challenge of UI in the aging society.

Methods

Data

Data were extracted from two longitudinal studies: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [19] and the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA) [20], with a nationally representative sample of the elderly aged 50 or over in the U.S. or in England, U.K., respectively. Given the availability of UI measurements reported in the past 12 months and similar time ranges, reflecting the recent trend, we utilized data collected from 2010 to 2020 for HRS and from 2009 to 2019 for ELSA. Participants under the age of 50 were excluded from the analysis due to the relatively lower prevalence of UI, resulting in a final sample of 111,049 observations from 27,410 participants in HRS and 52,972 observations from 13,814 participants in ELSA. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research. This study was a secondary analysis of de‐identified, publicly available survey data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA), which were not specifically commissioned or co‐produced with patient or public involvement.

Measurements

In both HRS and ELSA, UI was defined by one survey question. Participants were asked whether they had experienced involuntary urine loss in the past 12 months, with responses coded as 0 for “No” and 1 for “Yes”. Mental health was assessed using discrimination and loneliness summary scores. The discrimination score reflected responses to five questions related to everyday discrimination experiences, including whether the respondent is treated with less courtesy or respect than other people, whether the respondent receives poorer services than other people at restaurants or stores, how often the respondent feels other people act as if they think the respondent is not smart, how often the respondent feels they were threatened or harassed, and how often the respondent receives poorer service or treatment than other people from doctors or hospitals. The summary score ranged from 0 to 5 with higher scores indicating more discrimination. This scale was evaluated using a well‐established questionnaire [21], and the reliability of this scale is high, with Cronbach's alpha at 0.800 in 2010 and at 0.820 in 2008 [22]. The loneliness score was based on three questions gauging feelings of social isolation and companionship, including how often the respondent feels they lack companionship, how often the respondent feels left out, and how often the respondent feels isolated from others. The loneliness score ranged from 0 to 3 with higher scores indicating that the respondent feels more lonely, which was adapted using the 3‐item UCLA Loneliness Scale (Cronbach's alpha 0.770) [23].

Covariates including sociodemographic status (age, gender, residence area, ethnicity/race, educational attainment), lifestyle (smoking, drinking), comorbidity of diseases (hypertension, diabetes, cancer, stroke), and cognitive impairment were considered. A cross‐country harmonized education attainment classification was used to enable UK/USA comparability. Education was therefore coded as lower than the secondary/upper secondary and vocational training/tertiary. Cognitive impairment was defined as a score of 8 or below on a 35‐point scale for HRS [24, 25], while a score between 0 and 11 on a 27‐point scale for the ELSA [26]. The cognitive impairment scales have been respectively validated [10, 11, 17].

Statistical Analysis

We calculated the prevalence and its 95% confidence interval of UI by age, gender, and race in each survey year from 2010s to 2020s, by applying individual‐level weights provided by HRS and ELSA to present nationally representative estimates. As the population‐based survey of the USA and UK population aged 50 or older, HRS and ELSA provide personal‐level sampling weights to account for the differential probability of selection and differential non‐response in each wave. Detailed descriptions of the weighting principles applied by the HRS and ELSA Study can be found elsewhere [27, 28].

The decomposition of these changes, factors responsible for changes in UI prevalence, and projections of UI‐related care needs for the next decade were then demonstrated. Changes in UI cases from 2010 to 2020 in HRS were obtained by multiplying UI prevalence (P) by the entire older population (N); we used a similar method to calculate total population estimates of UI in ELSA: N20*P20−N10*P10=N20−N10P20+P102+P20−P10N20+N102 in which we segregated the population change into two components. The first component accounted for changes in the population base, influenced by population aging, while the second component related to changes in UI prevalence, wherein we investigated underlying infrastructural determinants. Due to data limitations, the cognitive impairment data in the last two waves for HRS and the first three waves for ELSA were constructed from the average of the waves for which data were available. The future changes in UI between 2022 and 2030 for the US and between 2021 and 2030 for the UK were projected using regression coefficients and Equation (1). The age and gender structure of older people were predicted by the UN World Population Prospects [29]. We assumed that the association between covariates and UI remained stable during the projection period [30]. This structural stability assumption is standard in short‐term epidemiological forecasting, in which covariate trends are extrapolated from past data and applied to a fixed‐effect regression model [31].

Study findings are reported according to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) recommendations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with a two‐sided test, and all analyses were conducted using StataSE 18.0.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, through the utilization of two nationally representative cohorts from the US and UK, our study is among the first to identify the prevalence, trajectories, and factors responsible for the growth in UI prevalence, while also projecting the forthcoming UI care demands till 2030 across gender among the elderly population. Our findings reveal a marked increase in UI prevalence during the 2010s, largely driven by population aging, higher educational attainment reflecting greater awareness and reporting, and rising comorbidities. These structural and health‐related changes collectively explain the upward trend and underscore the need for policies addressing both demographic pressures and modifiable risk factors. Projections indicate that by 2030, the number of elderly living with UI will increase by approximately 5.5 million in the USA and 0.700 million in the UK. Notably, around 65% of this growth is expected among women, with the fastest rise observed in those aged 80 and over. These trends highlight not only the escalating care burden associated with UI but also the persistent gender disparities that disproportionately affect older women.

The prevalence and trends of urinary incontinence (UI) among the elderly have been previously investigated in the US and UK [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 32]. However, few studies have specifically addressed gender differences in UI prevalence over the years. Besides, most of them were conducted a decade ago, with smaller sample sizes and shorter follow‐up periods. There are also forecast studies based on data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the 2013 Medicare Beneficiary Files predicting future UI care needs in the US. Wu and colleagues focused solely on females, projecting a 55% increase in the number of American women with UI from 18.3 million in 2010 to 28.4 million in 2050 using the 2005–2006 cohort of NHANES [18], which would be much more conservative than ours. The conservative prediction is mainly due to the static prevalence rates and demographic assumptions used. Real‐world factors such as increasing risk factors, improved healthcare access, and changes in population demographics could lead to higher actual numbers. Puckrein and colleagues' projections of overactive bladder (OAB) prevalence in the Medicare 2013 dataset (7.2%, male: 7.7%, female: 6.7%) suggested a 48.1% increase by 2027, based on data from 2013 to predict trends for 2027 [17], which didn't target younger the elderly aged under age 65 in the US.

Gender disparities in UI are influenced by a combination of biological, sociocultural, and behavioral factors. Biologically, women are more susceptible to stress and mixed UI due to anatomical differences, pelvic floor dysfunction, and hormonal changes—particularly following childbirth and menopause [13, 14]. These physiological factors partly explain the consistently higher prevalence observed in women. However, sociocultural norms also play a critical role [33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. The stigma surrounding UI significantly impacts mental well‐being by disrupting social connections and normal life, stemming from the loss of bodily control and speculation surrounding a vague “issue” [33, 34, 35, 37, 38]. This stigma leads individuals to resist seeking social support from those willing and able to help, exacerbating both physical and mental health challenges. The extent of stigma associated with urinary symptoms depends on their perceptibility, whether visually, due to odor, or behaviorally. Males experience stigma related to frequent restroom visits, fearing implications of impotence, while females fear perceptions of uncleanliness or compromised social identity [36, 39]. Additionally, many women normalize UI symptoms as a natural consequence of aging or motherhood, leading to delayed care‐seeking and underutilization of treatment [40, 41, 42]. The emotional toll on women is often compounded by caregiving responsibilities and gendered expectations about bodily control. These gendered patterns of symptom experience and health‐seeking behavior reinforce disparities.

Our study boasts several methodological strengths. Firstly, the analysis benefits from exceptional generalizability due to its utilization of two large, diverse, well‐characterized longitudinal studies involving the elderly from two developed nations. Secondly, the validity of our findings is enhanced by the use of nationally representative samples for participant recruitment, standardization of survey methodologies for database comparability, an extensive follow‐up period spanning nearly a decade, and demographic data weighting, resulting in more precise.

Our study has limitations. Firstly, the reliance on self‐reported measures for urinary incontinence (UI) and mental health indices rather than clinical diagnoses introduces the possibility of recall and social desirability biases. Additionally, despite the consideration of numerous potential confounding variables, there may still be unaccounted‐for confounders due to limited data availability, such as residential area information in the ELSA. Thirdly, the lack of specification regarding the type of incontinence in the longitudinal cohorts presents a challenge for further exploration of underlying mechanisms. Last but not least, HRS only includes individuals who reside in households. However, those who were homeless or institutionalized in nursing homes or long‐term care facilities were not included in the survey. Presumably, these individuals would have a higher prevalence of UI compared with their relatively healthy community‐dwelling counterparts; thus, the results of this study would be conservative estimates.

This study underscores significant policy implications regarding the escalating care burden associated with UI in the US, UK, and globally, particularly among older women. On the one hand, urgent attention is needed to implement gender‐sensitive, personalized strategies that address both prevention and management, especially concerning cardiometabolic comorbidities—where primary care systems must serve as the first line of intervention. Governments should prioritize the training and recruitment of professional caregivers and long‐term care workforce capacity to address the anticipated surge in care needs of an aging, female‐dominated patient population. Furthermore, unlike other reproductive health concerns, UI in later life has historically received limited attention in women's health policy. By framing UI as part of the aging trajectory of women, this study supports calls to expand the scope of women's health beyond reproductive years and to integrate pelvic floor and continence care into national strategies for healthy aging.

On the other hand, although the psychosocial burden of UI is also significant among men, especially due to stigma and underreporting, prior research has historically centered on women, often without a nuanced understanding of gender‐specific mental health needs. As such, future strategies require a dual‐pronged approach: improving access and visibility of continence care in women's health programs while also tailoring strategies to increase awareness and engagement among men. Health systems should prioritize gender‐disaggregated surveillance, develop targeted public education campaigns to destigmatize UI across genders, and provide community‐based support groups and counseling services to aid patients in better coping with the social pressure caused by UI. Ensuring equitable access to prevention, diagnosis, and treatment is not only a matter of efficiency—but of gender justice in healthy aging.

Notably, Kegel exercises created by Dr. Arnold Kegel, or pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), are widely recognized for their effectiveness in both preventing and rehabilitating UI according to previous literature, especially among women and the elderly [43]. Studies have shown that regular performance of Kegel exercises can significantly reduce the symptoms of stress UI, which supports the integration of Kegel exercises into standard care for individuals at risk of or currently experiencing UI [44, 45, 46, 47]. For example, a Cochrane review by Dumoulin et al. highlighted that pelvic floor muscle training is more effective than no treatment or inactive control treatments in women suffering from UI [44]. Subak et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial that confirmed the benefits of behavioral therapy, including PFMT, for UI in older women [47]. Consistent practice of Kegel exercises provides long‐term benefits, including the prevention of recurrence of UI and the maintenance of pelvic floor health [46]. Thus, Kegel exercises should be given greater importance when designing tailored interventional strategies.

In conclusion, the projected rise in UI among older adults, driven by population aging and increasing prevalence, will place significant strain on families, caregivers, and health systems. Gender‐responsive interventions and strategies are essential, particularly for women, who bear a disproportionate burden, to promote both physical and psychological well‐being. Further research is needed to assess the cost‐effectiveness of early interventional packages and to examine how mental health and social determinants influence UI outcomes. These efforts will help build equitable, evidence‐informed systems that support dignity, autonomy, and healthy aging, especially for women.
