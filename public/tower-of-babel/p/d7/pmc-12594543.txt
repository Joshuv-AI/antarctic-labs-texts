Predicting drug overdose and death after “before medically advised” hospital discharge
Authors: Hiten Naik, Daniel Daly-Grafstein, Xiao Hu, Mayesha Khan, Benjamin M. Kaasa, Jeffrey R. Brubacher, Trudy Nasmith, Jennifer R. Lyden, Jessica Moe, Alexis Crabtree, Amanda K. Slaunwhite, John A. Staples
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:“Before medically advised” (BMA) hospital discharge is associated with higher risks for subsequent death and drug overdose. We sought to develop prediction models to estimate the absolute risk of death from any cause and illicit drug overdose after BMA discharge for a given patient.Methods:We used retrospective population-based administrative health data of a 20% random sample of all British Columbia residents to derive and internally validate regression models to estimate the risks of death (model A) and illicit drug overdose (model B) within 30 days after BMA discharge. Model A included all nonelective, nonobstetrical adult hospitalizations ending in BMA discharge between 2015 and 2019. Model B included only hospitalizations from model A for patients with evidence of prior substance use. We fitted prediction models using logistic regression and validated models using bootstrap-based optimism correction. Candidate predictors included sociodemographic and clinical characteristics available to clinicians during BMA discharge.Results:Of 6440 hospital admissions included in model A, 102 (1.6%) were associated with the death of a patient within 30 days of BMA discharge. Predictors for death included a Charlson Comorbidity Index of 2 or higher, cancer, and heart disease. Model A exhibited good discrimination (C-statistic = 0.78) and excellent calibration. Of 4466 hospital admissions included in model B, 233 (5.2%) were associated with a patient subsequently overdosing within 30 days of BMA discharge. Predictors for drug overdose included homelessness, receipt of social income assistance, opioid use disorder, non-alcohol substance use disorder, overdose in the past year, and discharge from a surgical service. Model B exhibited good discrimination (C-statistic = 0.79) and excellent calibration.Interpretation:Risk prediction models may help clinicians and hospitals identify patients who might benefit from intensive support to prevent death or illict drug overdose after BMA discharge.

“Before medically advised” (BMA) hospital discharges (also known as “against medical advice” discharges) are complex challenges for clinicians, hospitals, and patients.1 Around 500 000 BMA discharges occur every year in the United States.2 Compared with patients undergoing routine, physician-advised hospital discharge, patients who initiate a BMA discharge are about twice as likely to die and about 10 times more likely to experience an illicit drug overdose within 30 days.3–6 Hospitalizations ending in BMA discharge have become more common, with recent data indicating that they represent about 3% of all hospitalizations and about 11% of hospitalizations related to opioid use.4,7

Specific interventions might reduce the risk of adverse outcomes after BMA discharge. For example, early notification of community providers and deployment of transitional care services could help prevent the progression of untreated medical illness and death.8 Opioid agonist therapy (OAT),9–12 intensive case management,13 and low-barrier follow-up for patients with addictions14 could prevent illicit drug overdose. However, more intensive follow-up and outreach after BMA discharge can be logistically complex and expensive,15–18 which may explain why these services are not universally provided to patients after BMA discharge.

Estimates of an individual’s risk of adverse outcomes after BMA discharge might help identify patients who should be prioritized for more intensive follow-up and outreach. To this end, we aimed to develop prediction models to estimate the risk of death from any cause in the first 30 days among all patients who initiated a BMA discharge and the risk of illicit drug overdose in the first 30 days among patients with a history of substance use who initiated a BMA discharge.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We performed a population-based retrospective cohort study set in British Columbia, Canada. At the study midpoint, BC had around 4.9 million residents.19 Publicly funded health care insurance provides eligible BC residents free access to primary care, emergency care, and hospital services, and subsidized access to prescription medications.

We analyzed data from the BC Provincial Overdose Cohort’s Reference Cohort (ODCr), a population-based database administered by the BC Centre for Disease Control, which includes a 20% random sample of all BC residents.20 The ODCr is a repository of linked individual-level data on ambulance services, emergency department visits, hospitalizations, outpatient physician services, overdoses for which clinicians or the public sought telephone advice from the BC Poison Control Centre, outpatient prescriptions filled in any community pharmacy in BC, community mental health service use and substance use history, social income assistance, homelessness, and date of death (Appendix 1, eMethods 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250492/tab-related-content). Data were deidentified before release to investigators and were rarely missing (Appendix 1, eMethods 1). We report definitions of the variables we used in the analysis in Appendix 1, eMethods 2.

Cohorts and outcomes

We developed a model to predict death from any cause in the first 30 days after BMA discharge (model A) and a separate model to predict fatal and nonfatal illicit drug overdose in the first 30 days after BMA discharge among patients with a history of substance use (model B). We chose these outcomes because they are important to patients, clinicians, and policy-makers, and because they are more common after BMA discharge.3–6 We developed 2 separate models because we anticipated the predictors of these outcomes would differ. We used different cohorts to develop the models because we anticipated that clinicians would be interested in understanding the risk of death for all patients with BMA discharge, but that the risk of drug overdose would be a concern primarily for those with a history of substance use.

We developed model A (death from any cause) in cohort A, which included all nonelective, nonobstetrical adult hospital admissions in the ODCr that ended in BMA discharge between 2015 and 2019. We excluded obstetrical admissions because they rarely end in BMA discharge. We excluded hospitalizations with a length of stay of 0 days because they may reflect miscoded elective outpatient procedures.4

We developed model B (illicit drug overdose) in cohort B, which included all hospital admissions from cohort A for patients with evidence of prior substance use, defined as an overdose in the past year, any substance use disorder diagnosis in the past 5 years (opioid, non-opioid, or alcohol), or a prescription fill for OAT in the past 5 years. We identified drug overdoses using a standard ODCr case definition derived from ambulance, emergency department, hospital, and coroner data. In BC, most fatal overdoses involve multiple drugs, and more than 80% involve fentanyl and fentanyl analogues.21 Detailed toxicology is not available for most nonfatal overdoses.

Predictors and model development

We first used multivariable logistic regression to fit global models, which served as the starting point for development of the final models. Each global model included all candidate predictors for the outcome of interest. We selected candidate predictors based on literature review and clinical experience (Appendix 1, eTable 1). For model A, we focused on variables previously shown to be associated with in-hospital death or death after hospitalization. 22–25 For model B, we focused on variables previously associated with substance use or adverse outcomes among substance users.4,5,26–32 To avoid overfitting, we ensured that at least 10 outcome events were available for each candidate variable in each model.33 Following the approach recommended by Riley and colleagues,34 we confirmed sample sizes were sufficient to derive stable predictions and predictor effect estimates.

We forced both models to include age and sex, then selected predictors for the final model using a backward elimination procedure that maximized the Akaike Information Criterion. We chose this method to optimize the interpretability of the final models.

In both models, individual hospitalizations were the unit of analysis. We calculated cluster-robust standard errors using the sandwich estimator to account for the potential nonindependence of hospitalizations involving the same individual.35,36 We conducted analyses in R version 4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) reporting checklist for multivariable prediction model development.37

Model validation

We validated model performance using bootstrap-based optimism correction after generating 1000 bootstrap resamples from each cohort.38 We assessed model discrimination by generating receiver operating characteristic curves and calculating the C-statistic. We assessed calibration by generating calibration plots and calculating the calibration slope and intercept. We visualized the risk of the outcomes over time by stratifying each cohort into risk groups and plotting Kaplan–Meier curves.

Ethics approval

The University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board approved the study (no. H20-03688) and waived the requirement for individual consent.

Results

The median age was 47 years, 64.5% were male, 59.1% had a non-alcohol substance use disorder, 67.7% had a psychiatric disorder, and the median length of stay was 3 days (Table 1). A total of 102 deaths (1.6%) occurred within 30 days of BMA discharge.

Illicit drug overdose

Cohort B included 4466 BMA discharges involving patients with a history of substance use (Figure 1). The median age was 42 years, 65.9% were male, 80.0% had received a health insurance subsidy, 76.0% had a comorbid psychiatric disorder, 23.7% had a drug overdose in the past year, and the median length of stay was 3 days (Table 1). A total of 233 (5.2%) illicit drug overdoses occurred within 30 days of BMA discharge.

The final model B included 12 of the 23 candidate predictors (Appendix 1, eTable 1). Strong predictors of drug overdose after BMA discharge included homelessness, receipt of social income assistance, a history of opioid use disorder, a history of non-alcohol substance use disorder, at least 1 drug overdose in the past year, and discharge from a surgical service (Table 2). Model B exhibited good discrimination (optimism-corrected C-statistic = 0.79; Figure 2B) and excellent calibration (optimism-corrected intercept < 0.001, optimism-corrected slope = 0.93; Figure 3B and Appendix 1, eTable 1). Most patients had a low predicted risk of drug overdose within 30 days. Interpretation

We performed a population-based retrospective cohort study that used demographic and medical information present at the time of hospital departure to derive and internally validate 2 risk prediction models, one that examined 6440 hospitalizations to estimate individual risks for death from any cause in the first 30 days after BMA discharge, and another that examined 4466 hospitalizations for patients with evidence of prior substance use to estimate individual risks for illicit drug overdose in the first 30 days after BMA discharge. Both models demonstrated good calibration and excellent discrimination. These findings have several implications for clinicians and hospitals.

Death after BMA discharge was less common than clinicians might expect (i.e., 1 death within 30 days for every 63 BMA discharges). Further research is needed to understand how the causes of death after BMA discharge differ from deaths following physician-advised discharge.

We found that multimorbidity, heart disease, and cancer were strong predictors of death from any cause within 30 days of BMA discharge (model A). These comorbidities also feature prominently in other risk prediction models such as the modified Elixhauser index (which predicts in-hospital death) or the Hospital-patient One-year Mortality Risk (HOMR-Now!) model (which predicts 1-year death after hospital admission).15,17 Further research should examine whether these other models could also be used to predict death after BMA discharge.

Among patients with a history of substance use, illicit drug overdose was a relatively common outcome soon after BMA discharge (i.e., around 1 illicit drug overdose within 30 days for every 19 BMA discharges), suggesting this period is a critical but largely unexplored opportunity for overdose prevention.4,5 More research is required to determine which overdose prevention strategies are most effective after BMA discharge. In the meantime, clinicians should consider deploying clinically sensible, evidence-informed interventions, including pre-departure evaluation by an in-hospital addiction medicine consult service,39–41 discharge prescriptions for OAT,42,43 and referral to low-barrier, rapid-access community addiction services,14 community intensive case management,44 and peer support services.45

We found that homelessness, receipt of social assistance, a history of opioid use disorder, a history of non-alcohol substance use disorder, and 1 or more overdoses in the past year were strong predictors of drug overdose. These findings underscore the importance of proactive discharge planning for socially vulnerable patients at risk of BMA discharge. For example, collecting comprehensive contact information for the patient and supportive community members during admission is crucial for offering follow-up after BMA discharge, particularly for patients with unstable housing. After BMA discharge, use of multiple outreach methods (e.g., phone calls, texts, emails) may increase the chances of contact. Automated phone calls or text messages directed to patients at high risk of post-BMA adverse events might improve the reliability of communication and reduce some of the coordination-of-care burden that is sometimes borne by clinicians.46–48 However, successful follow-up after BMA discharge also depends on patient receptiveness and community clinician capacity to provide follow-up.

Model B revealed that patients who initiate a BMA discharge from a surgical service were nearly 50% more likely to have a drug overdose than those discharged from a medical service. Other research has found an elevated risk of overdose after surgery, an outcome more common among patients receiving opioids for pain.49,50 Perioperative care teams should be cognizant of these risks and be prepared to provide sufficient addiction support to reduce the risk of BMA discharge.

Calculating a specific patient’s risk of death and drug overdose — combined with clinical judgment and other risk scores — might help clinicians and patients have a constructive, patient-centred discussion about the decision to initiate a BMA discharge, including assessing the patient’s capacity to make such a decision and discussing ways to mitigate risks after BMA discharge. 51–53 By reducing uncertainty, risk estimates might also reduce clinician moral distress when faced with a BMA discharge.54 We have developed online risk calculators to predict individual patients’ risk of death and illicit drug overdose after BMA discharge (https://stapleslab.shinyapps.io/ama-od-risk-shiny-app/), but they must be used with caution because they have not been externally validated.

Hospitals and health systems could use externally validated prediction models to automate the response to higher-risk BMA discharges. Automated alert systems integrated into modern electronic medical record systems could improve the efficiency and safety of care transitions.55–57 Immediately after a BMA discharge occurs, an alert sent to hospital- and community-based clinicians could include the patient’s predicted risk of overdose and death, along with best practices to ameliorate these risks. Patients at high risk of death or overdose could be automatically enrolled in more resource-intensive interventions (e.g., intensive case management strategies, transitional care services, outreach by public health nurses).

Although our study findings indicated that adverse outcomes after BMA discharge may be predictable, further research is required to determine whether these outcomes are preventable and by what means. Researchers and health systems should design and evaluate interventions to prevent harms after BMA discharge.6 Our models could aid in this research by identifying the patients who are at the highest risk of harm and who might thus be most likely to benefit from the intervention.

Limitations

These prediction models require external validation before widespread clinical use. Although we examined a large cohort of BMA discharges, the relatively low number of outcome events precluded us from using more sophisticated model generation techniques (e.g., including nonlinear effects and interaction terms) or a larger number of covariates. For model B, non-overdose death, although rare, was a competing event that we did not account for. We underestimated the rate of drug overdose because the ODCr does not capture nonfatal overdoses that did not result in a medical contact. Several possible predictors of death (e.g., illness severity at the time of discharge) and drug overdose details (e.g., route of drug use) were not captured in our data set, which may partially explain shortcomings in model performance. The models were derived from data collected between 2015 and 2019. Changes in the composition of the drug supply since then might affect the current performance of the models and is a reason for external validation using more recent data sets.58 Lastly, we did not compare model performance to clinical gestalt, which can sometimes perform as well as, or better than, some clinical prediction models.59

Conclusion

We developed models to predict the risks of death from any cause and illicit drug overdose after BMA discharge from hospital. These models offer a starting point for identifying patients who are high risk and may benefit from greater support.
