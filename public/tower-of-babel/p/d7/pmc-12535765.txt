Optimizing Scanning in Youth Female Football: The Role of Verbal Instructions
Authors: Mirjam Hintermann, Michael Romann, Dennis Lüdin, Wolfgang Taube, Jörg Fuchslocher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVisual exploration, or scanning, is a key perceptual skill in football, enabling players to collect environmental information. It was shown that elite players scan more frequently than less experienced players and make better decisions in subsequent actions. However, to date, it is not clear how to improve and develop scanning behavior. This study examines the immediate effects of verbal instructions on scanning behavior and performance in 4v4 small‐sided games among 25 elite youth female football players (mean ± SD age: 17.6 ± 0.7 years). Participants completed two training sessions: one with and one without verbal instructions. Verbal cues were specifically designed to encourage players to scan for teammates, opponents, and free spaces before receiving the ball. Scanning behavior and performance were analyzed using video footage, and data were statistically evaluated through multilevel generalized linear models. Verbal instructions increased the number of scans before ball reception by 27% (rate ratio: 1.27 and 95% CI [1.12, 1.44]), demonstrating their effectiveness in enhancing scanning behavior. Players who performed at least one scan were significantly more likely to achieve successful subsequent actions compared to those who did not scan (e.g., 1 scan: odds ratio = 3.40 and 95% CI [2.59, 4.62]). Notably, the additional scans did not negatively impact the relationship between scanning and the success of subsequent actions. The findings suggest that verbal instructions are a promising coaching tool for increasing the number of scans. Integrating scanning‐focused instructions with broader tactical and decision‐making training could further optimize perceptual skill development in elite youth football players.Keywords: coaching, female athletes, football, small‐sided games, visual exploration

Introduction

In dynamic sports, such as football, there are often sudden changes to game situations (Bangsbo et al. 2006; Duarte et al. 2013; Lames et al. 2021). Therefore, collecting information about the playing environment through visual exploration is essential for successful performance (Eldridge et al. 2013; Jordet et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021). This has led to a growing body of research on visual exploration, both in real‐world settings using video‐based analyses (Jordet et al. 2013; McGuckian, Cole, Jordet, et al. 2018; Phatak and Gruber 2019; Jordet et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021; Pokolm et al. 2022; Caso et al. 2023; Pokolm et al. 2023) and in laboratories using advanced technologies such as gaze tracking or inertial measurement units (McGuckian, Cole, Chalkley, et al. 2018; Aksum et al. 2020; McGuckian, Cole, et al. 2020b; Rojas Ferrer et al. 2020). A visual exploration is often referred to as “scanning” (Jordet et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021) or “visual exploratory activity” (McGuckian, Beavan, et al. 2020; Eldridge et al. 2022) and involves a self‐initiated movement of player's eyes, head, or body away from the ball to gather visual information about teammates, opponents, and surrounding space (Jordet et al. 2020). This information can help players to make informed decisions during a game. Scanning behavior has been extensively studied in elite football and has shown that elite players scan more frequently than their less experienced counterparts (McGuckian, Beavan, et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021), which was associated with better performance (Jordet et al. 2013; Jordet et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021; Pokolm et al. 2022).

Despite the recognition of scanning as a vital perceptual skill in elite football, the knowledge of training methods to develop this skill remains limited in both observational and interventional studies (Pocock et al. 2017; McGuckian, Cole, and Pepping 2018). Two studies have explored coaches' perceptions regarding the introduction, delivery, and evaluation of visual exploration training (Pulling et al. 2018; Eldridge et al. 2022). The results indicate significant differences in whether and how scanning is taught, with more experienced coaches generally displaying a more positive attitude toward coaching visual exploration (Pulling et al. 2018). In terms of delivery, coaches often reported using direct verbal instructions, such as “check your shoulder” or “head up,” to encourage scanning during practice sessions and games (Ford et al. 2010; Partington and Cushion 2013; Pulling et al. 2018). In the study by Eldridge and colleagues (Eldridge et al. 2022), nine elite football coaches from the English Football Association Academy were interviewed to gain a better understanding of coach's perspective on visual exploration. However, when it came to developing this skill in their players, many expressed uncertainty about which methods are most effective, possibly due to a lack of evidence available on different training approaches. Although verbal instructions are commonly employed to direct attention and reinforce key behaviors during practice (Williams et al. 1999), there is a lack of research about both the immediate (acute) and sustained (longitudinal) effects of such instructions (Buszard et al. 2013).

The importance of visual exploration for successful subsequent actions is well‐documented in men's football (Jordet et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021). Recent research highlights its equally critical role in women's football, demonstrating that scanning significantly enhances the quality of the subsequent action in youth female football, independent of player's competition level (Feist et al. 2024; Hintermann et al. 2024). Furthermore, youth football is a pivotal stage for developing perceptual skills, which are essential for achieving long‐term success in sport (Williams et al. 2011).

Repetition is a well‐established factor in the acquisition and consolidation of new motor behaviors. The same is also assumed to apply to the improvement of scanning behavior, making it essential to employ training formats that increase the number of scanning actions. Small‐sided games (SSGs) have gained recognition as a high‐intensity alternative to traditional drills as they present players with more opportunities for on‐ball actions (Dellal et al. 2008; Castellano et al. 2013; Ometto et al. 2018). They are well documented for their effectiveness in developing technical and tactical behaviors and also provide physiological and physical stimuli simultaneously (Sarmento et al. 2018; Clemente et al. 2021). Specifically, their position‐independent nature helps minimize the physiological demands associated with specific playing positions across various exercise modalities (Skala and Zemková 2023; Slimani et al. 2025). In regard to visual exploration, SSGs create a dynamic environment—characterized by frequent ball contacts, rapid transitions between attack and defense, and reduced decision‐making time—that promotes active scanning. By reducing available space and increasing time pressure, SSGs encourage greater exploratory behavior as evidenced by a study on 3v3 formats (McGuckian et al. 2017). Thus, SSGs expose players to dynamic game‐like scenarios that challenge both their technical execution and tactical awareness, making their training adaptations transferable to competition (Rico‐González et al. 2022).

Therefore, this study examines the immediate effects of verbal instructions on scanning behavior and subsequent performance during 4v4 SSGs among elite youth female football players. Specifically, it addresses two research questions: (1) Do verbal instructions increase the number of scans performed before receiving the ball? (2) Does the quality of players' subsequent actions improve in response to verbal instructions? By answering these questions, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of verbal coaching in developing perceptual skills in young female athletes, contributing to evidence‐based training strategies for elite player training. The findings will provide coaches with guidance on the short‐term effects of verbal instructions on scanning.

Methods

Participants

Twenty‐five female football players (mean ± SD age: 17.6 ± 0.7 years) from two Swiss U19 women's national league teams participated in this study. To ensure comparability in playing level, teams were selected from the top three ranked teams of the previous season. The original sample comprised all 44 field players from these two teams; however, only 25 players completed both training sessions and were thus included in the final analysis. The remaining 19 players were not analyzed due to illness or injury at either the first or second training session. Players have been playing football for 10.2 ± 1.7 years, participated in four football‐specific training sessions and one competitive match per week. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Ethical approval for the study was received by the institutional review board of the Swiss Federal Institute of Sport Magglingen SFISM (196_LSP_04_2023) and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures

Data collection was conducted over two regular outdoor training sessions per team between September and October 2022. To ensure the preservation of regular training conditions and natural player behavior, and to maintain ecological validity within elite sport setting, players remained in their usual training groups and were analyzed based on their team affiliation. As a result, the study adopted a quasi‐experimental design rather than a fully randomized one. Coaches divided their outfield players into four subteams of equal strength based on their physical, technical, and tactical abilities. Each training session involved SSGs in a 4v4 format. The first session was conducted without any coaching intervention (control, CON), with coaches explicitly instructed to refrain from providing any technical or tactical guidance during this period, whereas the second session included verbal instructions from the coach (intervention, INT). Verbal instructions were delivered in a standardized format to the entire team at consistent intervals: once immediately before the first game and at the beginning of each 2‐min break between games, resulting in a total of eight instructional prompts per session. These instructions were delivered audibly and clearly at a normal coaching volume, ensuring all players could hear the messages without shouting or disrupting the training atmosphere. Each prompt lasted about 10–15 s and was given by the same coach. The verbal cues were carefully formulated to promote an external focus of attention (Wulf 2013) and were developed in consultation with experienced football coaches and the team coaches involved in the study. The standardized prompts included the following instructions, which were rotated across the sessions: “Before you receive the ball, see where your open teammates are positioned,” “Locate the opponents’ positions before your first touch,” and “Check your surroundings for open spaces before you get the ball.” Instructions were delivered at the group level rather than individually, ensuring uniform exposure and minimizing disruption to the natural training flow.

The training sessions began with a standardized 15‐min warm‐up, followed by a 3‐min passive recovery period. Each session consisted of two sets of 4 × 4‐min games, with a 2‐min break between games and a 5‐min break between sets. To ensure comparability across conditions, the study followed standardized game formats and environmental setups. This aligns with recent calls in sport‐science research to standardize external load quantification across training modalities, facilitating more valid comparisons and minimizing confounding influences due to inconsistent training exposure (Dhahbi et al. 2024). The SSGs were conducted on two 40 × 30 m pitches, which is in line with the dimension used in previous studies (Rampinini et al. 2007; Hill‐Haas et al. 2009; Owen et al. 2014). Minor adjustments were made to the standard 11v11 rules, such as dismissing the offside rule, and goalkeepers restarting play after goals or out balls. Multiple balls were available to ensure continuous gameplay. A 1:2:1 playing system was specified to structure gameplay consistently across all sessions. Within each set, players rotated through all four positions in a predetermined sequence. This rotation ensured that each player experienced the same positional demands within a session, thus eliminating position‐specific effects and enabling comparability between INT and CON.

All 4v4 games were manually recorded using two 4K digital cameras (HDR‐CX700VE, Sony, Minato, Tokyo, Japan). The cameras were positioned on tripods 2 m from the long side of the pitch and 7 m from the short side to provide optimal coverage of the players' movements with and without the ball. This diagonal angle allowed clear visibility of the players' scanning behaviors. Cameras were positioned to allow horizontal panning throughout data collection, while no zoom was applied to avoid motion blur that could result from rapid zooming during fast‐paced actions, ensuring consistent video quality and accurate observation of scanning behavior. The recorded footage was stored and accessed via Dartfish TV (Dartfish version 2022, Dartfish, Fribourg, Switzerland).

Variables

Scanning

A scan was defined as an active movement of a player's head in which their face is temporarily directed away from the ball, with the assumed intention of assessing the position of teammates, opponents, or available space relevant to subsequent actions (Jordet et al. 2020). Scanning behavior was analyzed for each game situation in which the player received a pass from a teammate (Aksum et al. 2021). A game situation was thus defined as the period beginning 5 s before the first ball contact and ending when the ball was released through a subsequent action (pass, dribbling, or shot) (Hintermann et al. 2024). Hintermann et al. adapted this timeframe from prior 11v11 studies (e.g., Jordet et al. 2013; Aksum et al. 2021) due to the higher frequency of events in 4v4 SSGs, where a new event typically occurs every 2 s (S. Jones and Drust 2007). This shorter window ensures that only a single game situation is captured for each scanning analysis. In total, 1867 game situations were included in the analyses.

Quality of Subsequent Action

The performance of each subsequent action was evaluated based on its effectiveness. A subsequent action was considered successful if the intentionally played ball reached a teammate and ball‐possession was maintained. However, if the intentionally played ball left the playing field boundaries or was intercepted by the opposing team, that is, resulting in a loss of ball‐possession, the action was classified as unsuccessful. This approach ensured consistency and feasibility across a large dataset of naturalistic game situations and aligns with methods used in previous studies (McGuckian, Cole, Jordet, et al. 2018; Jordet et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021).

Team Affiliation

Team affiliation (Team A or Team B) was included as a control variable in the analyses to account for potential team‐related effects.

Data Analysis

Filming and tagging procedures were conducted according to Hintermann et al. (2024). All video footage was systematically reviewed and coded by two expert analysts. To maintain consistency in assessments, the analysists underwent a familiarization process with the tagging panel, during which they analyzed four players across eight games over a 6‐week period.

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated using mixed‐effects models, assuming absolute agreement and a single rater. Estimates and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were obtained using R's psych‐package (Revelle 2023). A subset of 108 events (5.8% of total events), randomly drawn from four games involving four players, was independently analyzed by both raters. The resulting ICC was 0.89 (95% CI [0.86, 0.92]), indicating good to excellent interrater reliability (Koo and Li 2016).

To further evaluate intrarater reliability, both raters independently re‐analyzed four games of two randomly selected players (4.7% of total events). The ICCs revealed a good to excellent intrarater reliability (rater 1: ICC = 0.86 and 95% CI [0.79, 0.90]; rater 2: ICC = 0.93 and 95% CI [0.81, 0.99], respectively) (Koo and Li 2016).

Main Statistical Analysis

Main statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software R (R Core Team 2023). To account for clustered data, multilevel generalized linear models with random intercepts at the participant level were executed using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). Team affiliation was included as a fixed effect in the model to capture systematic differences between Team A and Team B.

To address the first research question, where the outcome variable consisted of count data (i.e., number of scans), a Poisson regression model was applied (Coxe et al. 2009). For the second research question, involving a binary dependent variable (i.e., successful and unsuccessful), a logistic regression model was utilized (Stoltzfus 2011).

Effect sizes were interpreted using rate ratios (RRs) for Poisson regression and odds ratios (ORs) for logistic regression. An increase of one unit in the predictor variable corresponds to the outcome variable being multiplied by e β . The following thresholds were used to interpret the magnitude of effect sizes: 1.00 ≤ OR/RR < 1.22 negligible, 1.22 ≤ OR/RR < 1.86 small, 1.86 ≤ OR/RR < 3.00 medium, and OR/RR ≥ 3.00 large, respectively (Olivier and Bell 2013).

To further interpret the effects of predictor number of scan (research question 2) on the quality of the subsequent action, estimated marginal means (EMMs) were computed using the emmeans package (Lenth 2024). EMMs, also known as least‐squares means, represent the predicted means for each factor level, adjusted for other variables in the model. Pairwise comparisons among factor levels were conducted with Tukey adjustments for multiple testing.

Mean number of scans between control and intervention groups were compared using an unpaired t‐test. The result is presented as mean ± SD, with statistical significance set at an alpha level of 5%. Effect size was calculated using Cohen's d (Cohen 1992).

Results

Verbal Instructions and Scanning Behavior

In the control condition, in which no verbal instructions were given to the players, 951 events were evaluated, whereas 916 events were analyzed during the intervention condition. On average, players performed 0.99 ± 0.29 scans per event in CON and 1.14 ± 0.33 scans per event in INT. Figure 1 illustrates individual differences in scanning behavior between CON and INT. On average, players performed significantly more scans when they received verbal instructions on scanning behavior from their coaches (t(24), p = 0.007, d = 0.59, and 95% CI [0.23, 1.10]).

Individual changes in mean number of scans before ball reception for the control (without verbal instruction) and intervention (with verbal instruction) conditions. Thick lines with error bars represent the mean number of scans for Team A (black) and Team B (gray).

The Poisson mixed model revealed that both conditions (CON and INT) and team affiliation significantly affected the number of scans before ball reception. The intercept for the reference category (i.e., Team A and no verbal instruction) corresponds to approximately 0.84 (95% CI [0.73, 0.97]). When players received INT, they performed 27% more scans compared to during the CON (RR = 1.27 and 95% CI [1.12, 1.44]). Additionally, the results indicate that players from different teams exhibited differences in the number of scans, with players from Team B performing 36% more scans than those from Team A (RR = 1.36 and 95% CI [1.10, 1.68]). These differences were consistent across conditions, and no significant interaction effect between conditions and team affiliation was observed (RR = 0.85 and 95% CI [0.71, 1.01]).

Verbal Instructions and Performance

Figure 2 provides a descriptive overview and illustrates the relationship between scanning behavior and the quality of the subsequent action, highlighting the influence of verbal instructions on scanning behavior. In the CON condition, when no instructions were given, 384 events occurred without scanning before ball reception, whereas 567 events involved scanning. In INT, when instructions were provided, 313 events were performed without prior scanning and 603 events involved scanning. The distribution of successful and unsuccessful actions following scanning remained relatively consistent across both conditions.

Distribution of the number of scans across all analyzed events for the control (without verbal instruction) and intervention (with verbal instruction) conditions. The bar height represents the total number of events, whereas the percentage distribution of the quality of the subsequent action is visualized as successful (bright gray) and unsuccessful (dark gray).

The logistic mixed model showed that the number of scans before ball reception positively influenced the quality of the subsequent action (1 scan: OR = 3.40 and 95% CI [2.59, 4.62]; 2 scans: OR = 3.04 and 95% CI [2.33, 4.33]; and > 2 scans: OR = 3.59 and 95% CI [2.40, 5.79]) compared to no scanning. However, verbal instructions were not significantly associated with the success of the subsequent action (OR = 0.89 and 95% CI [0.70, 1.14a]). The intercept of the model, which reflects the odds of a successful action in the reference category (i.e., no scanning and no verbal instruction), was 1.64 (95% CI [1.32, 2.04]).

Pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means revealed no significant differences between the effect of number of scans on the quality of the subsequent action for 1 and 2 scans or between 2 and > 2 scans (p = 0.927 and p = 0.892, respectively). Discussion

This study investigated the impact of verbal instructions on scanning behavior and subsequent performance in elite youth female football players during 4v4 SSGs. The results indicate that verbal instructions significantly enhanced the number of scans performed before ball reception. Players who performed at least one scan were significantly more likely to achieve successful subsequent actions compared to those who did not scan. Furthermore, the additionally conducted scans through verbal instructions did not negatively impact the relationship between scanning and the success of subsequent actions. These findings suggest that instructions on scanning behavior during SSGs are an effective tool to increase the number of scans and avoid wrong decisions due to lack of knowledge about the environment (i.e., opponents, teammates, and free space).

Verbal Instructions and Scanning Behavior

Verbal instructions on scanning behavior led to a 27% increase in the number of scans performed by players before their first ball contact, demonstrating that elite youth female players can actively adjust scanning behavior in response to verbal instructions from the coach. This observed increase is in line with effect sizes reported in previous motor learning studies where participants received instructions emphasizing an external focus (Zachry et al. 2005). Although these explicit instructions led to an immediate increase in the number of scans, the long‐term effectiveness of such prescriptive explicitly learned skills in enhancing and retaining scanning behavior remains uncertain. As previously discussed, we propose that SSGs represent an effective training tool for increasing the frequency of actions that require scanning. Compared to traditional game formats, SSGs are assumed to be more effective in fostering and consolidating scanning behavior.

Not expected and somehow surprising, clear differences in scanning behavior were also evident between the two teams, although both teams competed at the same playing level. In a previous study, we have shown that the playing level greatly influences the scanning behavior with more scans in the higher‐level teams (Hintermann et al. 2024). The differences between teams of the same playing level may reflect underlying contextual factors, such as differing coaching philosophies, training emphases, or the degree, to which perceptual‐cognitive skills, such as scanning, are explicitly targeted in practice sessions. For example, one team may have received more instruction focused on environmental awareness or decision‐making under pressure, contributing to a greater habitual use of scanning. Additionally, variations in team culture, communication styles, or the quality of coach–player relationships could influence players' perceptual development and attentional focus during play (R. L. Jones 1997). These factors might shape not only what behaviors are encouraged but also how consistently they are reinforced across training environments. Such factors could potentially influence each player's baseline level of scanning behavior. However, the absence of an interaction effect indicates that these differences remained consistent across the two conditions.

Verbal Instructions and Performance

Players who performed at least one scan before receiving the ball were more likely to achieve a successful outcome than those who did not scan, confirming the importance of scanning for performance (Jordet et al. 2020; Aksum et al. 2021; Hintermann et al. 2024). Interestingly, despite the positive influence of scanning on the quality of the subsequent action, no significant differences were found between performing one, two, or more than two scans. This suggests that the initial scan may already provide sufficient information to guide effective decision‐making. Additional scans might not substantially enhance the quality of the subsequent action, especially in fast‐paced SSGs scenarios where time for information processing is limited. It is also possible that excessive scanning could lead to cognitive interference as proposed by the constrained action hypothesis (Wulf et al. 2001), where conscious control disrupts the fluidity of well‐learned skills. These findings emphasize that although encouraging players to scan is beneficial, more is not always better—quality and timing of scanning may be just as critical as frequency.

Figure 2 illustrates that the intervention predominantly increased the number of events involving multiple scans (i.e., two or more) rather than solely elevating the number of single‐scan events compared to no scans. Notably, the additionally conducted scans through verbal instructions did not negatively impact the relationship between scanning and the success of subsequent actions. Thus, the quality of the subsequent action did not decline, suggesting that players did not experience cognitive overload (Sweller et al. 2019) and were able to maintain their success rate despite increased scanning before receiving the ball. Therefore, verbal instructions can be considered a promising coaching tool to increase the number of scans and potentially enhance the quality of the subsequent action. However, from a practical standpoint, it is essential to encourage players to perform at least one scan before ball reception, transitioning from zero to one scan, to enhance situational awareness.

Practical Implications

Although explicit verbal cues can effectively reinforce immediate scanning behavior during training, achieving long‐term improvements may require further methods that implicitly encourage players to shift their focus away from the ball and actively explore their surroundings. Coaches should recognize that instructing players to scan has an immediate impact on their scanning behavior. Specifically, our findings suggest that consistently providing verbal instructions at the beginning of SSGs and during short breaks, such as every 4 minutes (totaling eight prompts per session), can be an effective strategy. Additionally, they should closely observe and evaluate player performance following such instructions to refine their coaching strategies and optimize training designs accordingly.

SSGs could serve as a valuable tool for developing scanning in football. Their position‐independent and versatile structure creates opportunities for scanning under time pressure. Furthermore, SSGs promote frequent on‐ball actions (Castelao et al. 2014), which can naturally encourage players to engage in visual exploration. Coaches may consider varying the SSGs format, such as adjusting player numbers and/or pitch size, to further challenge players' perceptual skills and decision‐making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, scanning behavior was assessed through video analysis of head movements, which may not fully capture eye movements. Secondly, the study focused on short‐term effects—future research should explore the long‐term impact of training interventions with verbal instructions on scanning behavior. It might be assumed that long‐term training of scanning behavior may not only increase the number of scans but may also increase the quality of the subsequent action. Thirdly, the 5‐s timeframe used to analyze scanning behavior prior to ball contact was adapted from 11v11 studies but has not been empirically validated in either 11v11 or 4v4 formats. Further research is needed to determine whether this duration appropriately captures relevant perceptual activity in smaller‐sided games. Fourthly, potential confounding variables, such as fatigue, motivation, and time of day, were not explicitly controlled or assessed between the two testing sessions. Finally, the fixed order of test sessions was determined by the structure and timeline of a larger research project, which required consistent scheduling across multiple teams. Furthermore, the use of nonrandomized group allocation was chosen to maintain ecological validity of the elite sport context, in which players train and compete within their established team structure. Although this may have introduced potential bias or order effects, it reflects real‐life conditions often neglected in more controlled studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential of verbal instructions to improve scanning behavior in youth female football players and offers valuable insights into the development of perceptual skills. The findings highlight the importance of adopting integrated training approaches that address both scanning behavior and action execution.
