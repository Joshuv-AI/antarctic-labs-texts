Cu–Mn Bimetallic Mesoporous Silica Nanosonosensitizers Enable Oxeiptosis-Mediated Sonodynamic Therapy for Ultra-Minimally Invasive Treatment of Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
Authors: Mingyu Chang, Jingcheng Lv, Lianhui Sun, Aihong Chen, Fengbo Zhang, Guangjian Fan, Zhanliang Liu, Jun Li, Hongliang Shen, Mingjun Shi, Yinong Niu, Ye Tian, Xijian Liu, Boyu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) presents a significant clinical challenge, with conventional therapies carrying substantial risks, including urinary retention, sexual dysfunction, and prolonged recovery. To address the urgent need for safer, ultra-minimally invasive alternatives, we developed a sonosensitizing nanoplatform using copper–manganese-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Cu–Mn@SiO2) for ultrasound-induced sonodynamic therapy (SDT). Here, we demonstrate that this innovative strategy provides highly effective and precisely targeted therapy for BPH. In vitro studies revealed that Cu–Mn@SiO2-mediated SDT significantly amplified intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, triggering oxeiptosis characterized by PGAM5 cleavage, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, and mitochondrial damage in both BPH-1 epithelial and WPMY-1 stromal cells. In a testosterone-induced BPH rat model, a Cu–Mn@SiO2-combined, localized SDT treatment resulted in a substantial 60% reduction in prostatic weight and a marked improvement in urinary function. Thermal imaging confirmed prostate-specific hyperthermia without affecting the surrounding tissues. Histopathology revealed reduced collagen deposition and restored glandular morphology with comprehensive biosafety assessments showing no systemic toxicity. By precisely inducing localized oxeiptosis within hyperplastic prostatic tissue via focused ultrasound activation, this Cu–Mn@SiO2-based nanoplatform represents a clinically translatable, ultra-minimally invasive therapeutic strategy offering a superior safety profile for BPH management.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) represents a prevalent age-associated urological disorder characterized by androgen-driven stromal-epithelial hyperplasia, manifesting as lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) that substantially compromise patients’ quality of life and result in severe complications, including acute urinary retention and bladder stones. , Current therapeutic interventions, ranging from α-blockers and 5α-reductase inhibitors to surgical procedures including transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), are limited by incomplete efficacy, considerable side effects, and high recurrence rates. These limitations underscore an urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies that combine precise targeting with ultra-minimal invasiveness.

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) has emerged as a promising noninvasive approach for localized disease treatment through ultrasound-activated sonosensitizers that generate therapeutic levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS). , The spatial and temporal control afforded by focused ultrasound enables selective therapeutic effects within target tissues while sparing surrounding healthy structures. , Recent advances in nanomedicine have demonstrated that metal-doped nanoparticles can serve as efficient sonosensitizers, with bimetallic systems exhibiting enhanced catalytic properties through synergistic effects. −

The pathophysiology of BPH involves a complex interplay between oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and androgen signaling, collectively establishing a pro-proliferative microenvironment. − Recent discoveries have identified oxeiptosis, a novel ROS-dependent, caspase-independent cell death pathway mediated by PGAM5-dependent AIFM1 dephosphorylation, as a critical mechanism for eliminating oxidatively damaged cells. PGAM5, a mitochondrial serine/threonine phosphatase, serves as a molecular switch that maintains mitochondrial homeostasis under physiological conditions but undergoes oxidative cleavage to amplify ROS production and trigger cell death under pathological stress. − Despite the established role of oxidative stress in BPH progression, the potential therapeutic exploitation of oxeiptosis for BPH treatment remains unexplored. ,

To address this knowledge gap and therapeutic need, we developed copper–manganese-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Cu–Mn@SiO2) as sonosensitizers for SDT-mediated BPH treatment. The incorporation of Cu and Mn creates a heterometallic redox system that efficiently catalyzes ROS generation upon ultrasound activation, while the mesoporous silica framework ensures biocompatibility and controlled drug delivery properties. We hypothesized that ultrasound-activated Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles could selectively induce oxeiptosis in hyperplastic prostatic tissue through PGAM5-mediated pathways, achieving therapeutic efficacy with minimal systemic toxicity.

Our study demonstrates that Cu–Mn@SiO2-mediated SDT triggers oxeiptosis in both BPH epithelial and stromal cells with PGAM5 cleavage and mitochondrial damage. In a testosterone-induced BPH rat model, localized SDT treatment achieved a significant prostatic volume reduction and functional improvement without systemic adverse effects. This work establishes SDT-enhanced nanotherapy as a paradigm shift toward ultra-minimally invasive BPH management, offering a clinically translatable approach that addresses current therapeutic limitations.

Experimental Procedures

Synthesis of Hollow Mesoporous SiO2

The mesoporous SiO2 was prepared according to the previous method with modification. 1 g of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) was dissolved in 120 mL of deionized water, and 18 mL of glycol and 8 mL of ammonia (25–28%) were added. Then, the mixture was stirred at 50 °C for 15 min. 8 mL of TEOS was added and stirred for 3 h, and then the mixture was washed with deionized water. After roasting at 550 °C in a Muffle furnace for 6 h, the white mesoporous hollow SiO2 powder was obtained.

Synthesis of Cu–Mn@SiO2

Solution A: Briefly, CuNO3·3H2O (0.4 mmol) and NH4Cl (10 mmol) were mixed in 20 mL of water, and then 1 mL of NH3·H2O (28–30%) was added. The obtained copper ammonia coordination complex solution is denoted solution A. Solution B: The above mesoporous SiO2 (50 mg) and MnCl2·4H2O (0.4 mmol) were mixed in 20 mL of water to obtain solution B. Solutions A and B were mixed and stirred for 30 min, then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave (50 mL) and maintained at 180 °C for 24 h. After the reaction, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation and washed with deionized water. Finally, Cu–Mn@SiO2 was obtained.

Cell Culture and Experimental Treatments

The immortalized human benign prostatic hyperplasia epithelial cell line BPH-1 (Procell, catalog no. CL-0865, China) and the prostatic stromal cell line WPMY-1 (Procell, catalog no. CL-0467, China) were obtained from Procell (China) and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling.

BPH-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, cat. no. PMM150110, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Procell, cat. no. 164210–50, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Procell, cat. no. PB180120, China). WPMY-1 cells were cultured in DMEM-high glucose (DMEM-hg) (Procell, catalog no. PMM150210, China) with the same supplements. All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 96-well plates (Corning, catalog no. 3599) for viability assays, 6-well plates (Corning, catalog no. 3506) for other assays, or 100 mm tissue culture-treated dishes (Corning, catalog no. 430167) for bulk experiments. For confocal fluorescence microscopy imaging, cells were cultured in glass-bottom dishes (Cellvis, Mountain View, CA) and allowed to adhere overnight.

For Cu–Mn@SiO2 treatments, cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations. For the control group, cells were incubated with the same volume as Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). For SDT treatments, the cells were passed through a calibrated ultrasound transducer. For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 + SDT group, cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations for 24 h and SDT was subsequently performed using a calibrated ultrasound transducer.

Cell Viability and Proliferation

Cell viability and proliferation were assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, catalog no. R252, Japan) in 96-well plates. For the cell viability assay, 6000 cells were seeded per well, while for the proliferation assay, 3000 cells were seeded per well. After incubation with the CCK-8 reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Cell viability or proliferation (%) was calculated using the formula cell viability or proliferation(%)=(Abs‐treated−Abs‐blank)/(Abs‐control−Abs‐blank)×100 Abs-treated: absorbance of the treated group.

Abs-blank: absorbance of the blank control (medium without cells).

Abs-control: absorbance of the untreated control group.

For Cu–Mn@SiO2 treatments, cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations. For SDT treatments, the cells were passed through using a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency, power density, and duration are shown in Figure B–D). For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 + SDT group, the cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations for 24 h and SDT was subsequently performed using a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 1 min).

Results were expressed as the relative cell viability compared to the untreated controls. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Apoptosis Detection by Flow Cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V, FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Dojindo, catalog no. AD10, Japan). For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 group, the cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations. For the control group, the cells were incubated with the same volume as Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). For the SDT group, the cells were passed through using a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 1 min). For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 + SDT group, the cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations for 24 h and SDT was subsequently performed using a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 1 min).

Cells were gently trypsinized, washed with PBS, and stained according to the kit protocol. Stained samples were analyzed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Early and late apoptotic populations were quantified by using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Evaluation of Intracellular ROS Generation

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the DCFH-DA probe (Dojindo, catalog no. R252, Japan). For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 group, cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations. For the control group, cells were incubated with the same volume as Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). For the SDT group, cells were passed through a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 1 min). For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 + SDT group, cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations and SDT was subsequently performed using a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 1 min).

Treated cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing with PBS, fluorescence was detected by both confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) and flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II). Data was expressed as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) relative to control groups. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were presented as mean ± SD.

Western Blot Procedure

Protein Sample Preparation

For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 group, cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations. For the control group, cells were incubated with the same volume as Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). For the SDT group, cells were passed through using a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 1 min). For the Cu–Mn@SiO2 + SDT group, cells were incubated with the nanoparticles at designated concentrations and SDT was subsequently performed using a calibrated ultrasound transducer (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 1 min).

Cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Epizyme, catalog no. PC101, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (100×, EDTA-free) (Epizyme, catalog no. GRF103, China) and phosphatase inhibitors to prevent protein degradation and dephosphorylation. The lysates were incubated on ice for 15 min and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

SDS-PAGE and Protein Separation

Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg) were mixed with loading buffer (Epizyme, catalog no. PS105s, China) containing β-mercaptoethanol and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. The denatured protein samples were loaded onto 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels prepared using the Omni-Easy PAGE Gel Kit (Epizyme, catalog no. PG212, China) and separated by electrophoresis at 120 V for 1.5–2 h.

Protein Transfer to PVDF Membranes

Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to poly­(vinylidene difluoride) (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA) using a mini transfer system (Epizyme, catalog no. PTM-mini4, China) with prechilled Omni-Flash Membrane Transfer Buffer (Epizyme, catalog no. PS201S, China) under 400 mA for 30 min. Membranes were activated by soaking in methanol for 1 min and equilibrated in transfer buffer before assembly.

Blocking and Antibody Incubation

After transfer, membranes were blocked in 5% BSA (w/v) (Epizyme, cat. no. PS113, China) dissolved in PBS-T (0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature to minimize nonspecific binding. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including:

Keap1 Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, catalog no. 10503–2-AP, China, 1:5000), PGAM5 Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, catalog no. 28445–1-AP, China, 1:10000), Phospho-AIFM1 (Ser116) (ECM Biosciences, catalog no. AP5501, 1:1000), and β-Actin Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, catalog no. 20536–1-AP, China, 1:5000).

Membranes were washed three times with TBS-T buffer (10×) (Epizyme, cat. no. PS103s, China) for 10 min each to remove unbound antibodies. Secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP (Proteintech, China) were applied at room temperature for 1 h at dilutions of 1:5000–1:10000, followed by another set of washes with TBS-T buffer.

Protein Detection and Visualization

Protein bands were visualized using the Omni-ECL Ultra-Sensitive Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (Epizyme, cat. no. SQ201, China) and imaged on the Omni-ECL Imaging System (Epizyme, cat. no. XF101, China).

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (MMP) Determination

MMP was measured using the JC-1 MitoMP Detection Kit (Dojindo, catalog no. MT09, Japan). Cells were incubated with JC-1 dye following the manufacturer’s protocol, and fluorescence was analyzed by confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. A decrease in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio indicated MMP depolarization, reflecting mitochondrial dysfunction.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) for Ultrastructural Analysis

Ultrastructural changes were assessed by using a Tecnai G2 20 transmission electron microscope (FEI). Cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Biosharp, cat. no. BL911A, China) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through an ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (∼70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before imaging.

siRNA Transfection-mediated PGAM5 Knockdown

siRNA Design and Synthesis

Human PGAM5-targeting siRNAs were designed using the following target sequences: siPGAM5 #1:5′-CCCGCCCGTGTCTCATTGGAA-3′; siPGAM5 #2:5′-TCCAAGCTGGACCACTACAAA-3′; siPGAM5 #3:5′-CTCGGCCGTGGCGGTAGGGAA-3′. A nontargeting scrambled siRNA was used as negative control. All siRNAs were synthesized by Qiagen or Dharmacon with standard desalting purification.

Cell Transfection

BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells were seeded at 2 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured overnight to reach 60–70% confluence. For transfection, cells were transfected with 50 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, siRNA and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX were separately diluted in Opti-MEM medium, combined at a 1:1 ratio, and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The siRNA-lipid complex was then added dropwise to cells in an antibiotic-free medium.

Knockdown Validation

Cells were harvested 48–72 h post-transfection for analysis. PGAM5 knockdown efficiency was evaluated by Western blot analysis. Protein lysates were prepared in a RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. PGAM5 protein levels were detected using anti-PGAM5 antibody (1:10000 dilution) with β-actin as the loading control.

Induction of Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) Model

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (200–250 g, 8 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature and humidity and free access to food and water. Rats were randomly divided into five groups (5 rats per group): NC group (negative control), BPH–CON group (0.9% NaCl-treated), BPH-SDT group (SDT-only), BPH–Cu-Mn@SiO2 group (nanoparticle-only), and BPH–Cu-Mn@SiO2 + SDT group (nanoparticle + SDT combination therapy).

BPH was induced by daily subcutaneous injections of testosterone propionate (Aladdin, cat. no. T101368, China) and benzoestrofol (Aladdin, cat. no. E102160, China) for 4 weeks. The NC group received equivalent volumes of corn oil (Beyotime, catalog no. ST1177, China).

Intervention and Treatment Procedures

For the BPH–Cu-Mn@SiO2 group, rats received local injection of Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles (5 mg/kg) into the prostate under isoflurane anesthesia. For the NC group and BPH–CON group, rats received equivalent volumes of 0.9% NaCl saline under isoflurane anesthesia. For the BPH-SDT group, rats received prostatic injection with equivalent volumes of 0.9% normal saline under isoflurane anesthesia, and received twice ablation of SDT by a calibrated ultrasound transducer after 2 h and 2 days (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 2 min). For the BPH–Cu-Mn@SiO2 + SDT group, rats received local injection of Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles (5 mg/kg) into the prostate under isoflurane anesthesia, and received twice ablation of SDT by a calibrated ultrasound transducer after 2 h and 2 days (frequency: 1 MHz, power density: 0.5 W/cm2, duration: 2 min).

Thermal Imaging Analysis

For the BPH-SDT group and BPH–Cu-Mn@SiO2 + SDT group, rats were placed in supine position on a temperature-controlled platform maintained at 37 °C, and the abdominal area was shaved using electric clippers, followed by application of depilatory cream for 3–5 min to ensure complete hair removal. After thorough cleaning with saline and a 10 min equilibration period in a controlled environment (22 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity), thermal images were captured using an infrared camera (HIKMICRO-P09, China) positioned perpendicular to the abdomen at a fixed distance of 30 cm. Baseline images were acquired before treatment, followed by sequential imaging at predetermined time points (0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 s post-treatment). The thermal data were analyzed using HIKMICRO software to extract maximum, minimum, and mean temperatures from defined regions of interest (ROI) on the abdominal area.

Evaluation of Urinary Function

Urinary function was assessed using a 6 h water load urine test. Rats were gavaged with 30 mL/kg of 0.9% sodium chloride and placed in metabolic cages for urine collection. Total urine volume was recorded, and this test was repeated weekly for 5 weeks to monitor the therapeutic effects.

Prostate Tissue Analysis

After 1 week of treatment, rats were euthanized and the prostate glands were collected, weighed, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathological examination. Tissue sections (4 μm) were prepared and stained using the Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE), Masson, and Sirius red Stain Kit (Solarbio, cat. no. G1120, G1340, G1472, China) to assess glandular architecture and stromal remodeling. Images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti–U microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0) (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and graphical representations were generated in GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). One-way or two-way ANOVA was applied to compare groups, followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization

We successfully synthesized copper and manganese codoped mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Cu–Mn@SiO2) using facile method (Figure ). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed an ellipsoidal morphology with an average diameter of approximately 200 nm (Figure A–C). Nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis confirmed the material’s mesoporous nature, revealing a typical Type IV isotherm (Figure D). The nanoparticles exhibit a high Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area of 310.252 m2/g and a substantial total pore volume of 0.8614 cc/g. The pore size distribution is narrow and uniform with a Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) adsorption mode centered at 3.44 nm, providing an ideal architecture for catalytic applications (Figure D). The Cu–Mn@SiO2 displayed well-defined mesopores and embedded crystalline structures due to doping with Cu and Mn ions, providing substantial active sites for sonodynamic applications. Elemental mapping demonstrated a homogeneous distribution of copper and manganese within the silica matrix (Figure E), further confirming successful doping. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis identified mixed valences of metal ions. The Cu 2p spectrum (Figure F) exhibited two well-defined spin–orbit split peaks at 935.30 eV (2p3/2) and 955.07 eV (2p1/2), with a characteristic splitting energy of 19.77 eV. The presence of shakeup satellite features at 942.3 and 962.1 eV confirms the paramagnetic Cu2+ configuration, consistent with octahedral coordination geometry. Moreover, in the Mn 2p spectrum (Figure G), three distinct oxidation states are resolved through deconvolution: Mn2+ (2p3/2 at 641.2 eV), Mn3+ (2p3/2 at 642.8 eV), and Mn4+ (2p3/2 at 644.5 eV), with corresponding 2p1/2 peaks maintaining an 11.5 eV splitting interval. The coexistence of these valence states established a redox-active interface. Hydrodynamic diameter measurements yielded 268 nm (Figure H), which is marginally larger than TEM-derived dimensions.

To investigate the reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation capacity of Cu–Mn@SiO2 under ultrasound (US) irradiation, we employed electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy. While a negligible •OH signal was detected in ultrasound-only controls (Figure I), the Cu–Mn@SiO2 + US group exhibited pronounced •OH formation. The Cu–Mn@SiO2 demonstrated superior •OH generation than monometallic counterparts (Cu@SiO2 or Mn@SiO2) under US irradiation. These results may be attributed to codoping providing more active sites for SDT. Furthermore, significant O2 – production emerged exclusively in the codoped system under ultrasound exposure (Figure J), highlighting the synergistic effect between copper and manganese species.

Validation studies utilizing methylene blue (MB) and 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) probes corroborated these findings. Ultrasound-activated Cu–Mn@SiO2 induced progressive MB degradation through ROS-mediated pathways (Figure K), and the degradation kinetics substantially exceeded ultrasound-only control group (Figure L). Parallel DPBF experiments demonstrated analogous absorbance reduction patterns (Figure M), confirming multimodal ROS generation capabilities encompassing both •OH and O2 – species by Cu–Mn@SiO2 under US irradiation.

The release kinetics of copper (Cu) and manganese (Mn) ions from the Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles were investigated under varying pH conditions to simulate different biological environments. Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the nanoparticles exhibited minimal ion leakage, confirming their structural integrity in a neutral environment. In stark contrast, exposure to acidic conditions (pH 5.6), which can mimic the prostatic intracellular microenvironment, triggered a rapid and sustained release, with approximately 90% of both Cu and Mn ions being released within 10 h (Figure S1A,B).

In addition to ion release, the long-term colloidal stability was assessed to predict the in vivo performance. The nanoparticles were dispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and monitored at 37 °C for 7 days. The Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles demonstrated excellent long-term dispersion stability, as both the hydrodynamic diameter and the polydispersity index (PDI) remained remarkably constant with no significant variation observed throughout the entire 7-day monitoring period (Figure S1C). This high stability under physiological conditions is crucial, suggesting that the nanoparticles are unlikely to aggregate in vivo and ensure predictable biological behavior and therapeutic performance.

Collectively, these comprehensive physicochemical analyses validate the rational design of Cu–Mn@SiO2 as a multifunctional sonosensitizing nanoplatform. The nanoparticles possess an ideal nanostructure for catalysis, characterized by a high specific surface area and uniform mesopores, which facilitate access to the redox-active catalytic sites. These sites are formed by the synergistic codoping of mixed-valence copper (Cu2+) and manganese (Mn2+, Mn3+, Mn4+) ions, as confirmed by XPS. This unique electronic configuration is fundamental to their superior sonosensitizing performance, enabling the efficient and synergistic generation of dual ROS species (•OH and O2 –) upon ultrasound activation. Furthermore, the platform exhibits two crucial properties for biological application: a pH-responsive ion release mechanism, ensuring targeted payload delivery in acidic microenvironments and excellent long-term colloidal stability under physiological conditions, which is essential for predictable in vivo behavior. Taken together, these featuresstructural optimization, potent ROS generation, and intelligent biological responsivenessestablish Cu–Mn@SiO2 as a robust and highly promising nanoplatform, providing a solid foundation for the subsequent in vitro and in vivo therapeutic evaluations.

Cu–Mn@SiO2 Combined with Sonodynamic Therapy Induces Synergistic Cytotoxicity in BPH Cells

The therapeutic efficacy of Cu–Mn@SiO2 was systematically evaluated in BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells through standalone and SDT-combined modalities. Monotherapy with Cu–Mn@SiO2 exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxicity across both cell lines (Figure A). Similarly, SDT efficacy correlated with ultrasound parameters, where cell viability decreased proportionally with escalating frequency, power density, and exposure duration (Figure B–D).

When Cu–Mn@SiO2 and SDT were combined, the cytotoxic effects were significantly enhanced, demonstrating a synergistic interaction. At the optimized Cu–Mn@SiO2 concentration of 5 μg/mL, which caused minimal cytotoxicity when used alone, the combination with SDT induced a substantial increase in cell death (Figure E,F) and a decrease in cell proliferation (Figure A–C). Time-course proliferation assays further validated this synergy, revealing sustained growth suppression throughout the 48 h observation period compared to monotherapy groups (Figure D). To confirm the underlying mechanism of enhanced cytotoxicity, apoptosis assays using Annexin V–PI staining were performed. The combined treatment significantly increased both early and late apoptotic populations compared to untreated, Cu–Mn@SiO2-alone, or SDT-alone groups. In BPH-1 cells, the apoptotic population increased from 2.6% (SDT-alone) and 4.4% (Cu–Mn@SiO2-alone) to 49.4% in the combination group (p < 0.001). Similarly, in WPMY-1 cells, apoptosis increased from 5.3% (SDT-alone) and 3.7% (Cu–Mn@SiO2-alone) to 55.5% in the combination group (p < 0.001) (Figures E,F and S2). These results confirm that the synergistic effects of Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT are mediated through cytotoxicity. Collectively, these findings highlight the robust synergistic interaction between Cu–Mn@SiO2 and SDT, demonstrating its potential as an effective and targeted therapeutic strategy for the treatment of BPH.

Sonodynamic Therapy Combined with Cu–Mn@SiO2 Significantly Elevates Intracellular ROS Levels and Activates the Oxeiptosis Pathway

Intracellular ROS generation under Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT treatment was systematically quantified using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe with dual analytical validation (flow cytometry and confocal microscopy) in BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells. Confocal imaging revealed distinct spatial patterns of ROS accumulation with combination groups exhibiting higher fluorescence intensity versus SDT-alone controls (Figure A). While Cu–Mn@SiO2 monotherapy demonstrated moderate ROS elevation (p < 0.05), combination treatment with SDT induced dramatic oxidative stress escalation, confirming synergistic ROS amplification (Figures B–D and S3).

Oxeiptosis is a recently identified ROS-dependent, caspase-independent form of regulated cell death that plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis under oxidative stress. Unlike apoptosis, oxeiptosis does not rely on the activation of caspase-3 but is mediated through the KEAP1/PGAM5/AIFM1 axis. Elevated ROS levels promote the activation of PGAM5 via KEAP1, which in turn dephosphorylates AIFM1 at Ser116. This dephosphorylation event is essential for triggering oxeiptosis. Despite its emerging role in redox pathologies, this pathway’s involvement in BPH remained unexplored. To examine whether elevated ROS induced by Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT activates the oxeiptosis pathway, we analyzed key regulatory proteins, including KEAP1, PGAM5, and phosphorylated AIFM1 (Ser116). Western blot analysis revealed significant alterations in their expression levels following combination therapy. Notably, Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT specifically induced dephosphorylation of AIFM1 at Ser116, a critical event mediated by PGAM5 in response to oxidative stress (Figure E–G). To further validate the role of PGAM5 in this process, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of PGAM5 expression in BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells, which was confirmed by Western blot analysis, showing effective depletion of PGAM5 protein levels (Figure S5A). Colony formation assays were performed to assess whether PGAM5 knockdown influences baseline cell proliferation, revealing no significant impact on the proliferative capacity of either BPH-1 or WPMY-1 cell lines (Figure S5B–D), indicating that PGAM5 depletion alone does not alter cellular growth under nonstressed conditions.

Subsequent experiments evaluated the functional consequences of PGAM5 knockdown in the context of the Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT treatment. Flow cytometry-based apoptosis assays demonstrated that PGAM5 knockdown significantly enhanced cell survival rates in both BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells following combination therapy, with reduced apoptotic populations compared to control vector-treated cells (Figure S5E,F). This protective effect was further corroborated by Western blot analysis of oxeiptosis pathway proteins post-treatment, which showed that PGAM5 knockdown markedly inhibited the dephosphorylation of AIFM1 (Ser116), thereby attenuating the activation of the oxeiptosis cascade (Figure S5G–I). These findings underscore PGAM5′s pivotal role in mediating Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT-induced oxidative stress responses, as its depletion disrupts downstream signaling events critical for cell death induction. These results provide the first evidence that Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT can simultaneously amplify intracellular ROS production and activate the oxeiptosis pathway by modulating key regulatory proteins including PGAM5-dependent dephosphorylation of AIFM1. The siRNA-mediated knockdown of PGAM5 further confirms its essential mechanistic involvement, as it attenuates oxeiptosis activation, enhances cell survival under treatment, and inhibits AIFM1 dephosphorylation without affecting baseline proliferation. This multifaceted mechanism of action, encompassing ROS elevation, PGAM5 cleavage, and targeted pathway modulation, highlights Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT as a promising therapeutic strategy for selectively inducing cell death in hyperplastic prostate cells through oxidative stress-based approaches.

Sonodynamic Therapy Combined with Cu–Mn@SiO2 Induces Mitochondrial Dysfunction via Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Depolarization, PGAM5 Cleavage, and Mitochondrial Damage

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of cellular stress, characterized by the depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), disruption of mitochondrial dynamics, and failure of quality control processes. MMP depolarization is an early indicator of mitochondrial damage, impairing ATP production, disrupting calcium homeostasis, and triggering downstream pathways that drive cellular dysfunction. − To assess the impact of Cu–Mn@SiO2 and its combination with SDT on mitochondrial integrity, we measured MMP by a JC-1 fluorescent probe in BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells. Cu–Mn@SiO2 alone caused moderate but significant depolarization in MMP, indicating partial mitochondrial impairment. By contrast, Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT resulted in severe MMP depolarization, as evidenced by a pronounced shift from red to green fluorescence (Figure A,B). Quantitative analysis showed a greater reduction in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio with combination therapy compared with Cu–Mn@SiO2 alone (p < 0.001, Figures C,D and S4). To further investigate the process by which Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT treatment induces mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization in cells, we monitored the changes in the cellular mitochondrial membrane potential at different time points following Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT stimulation. The results revealed that approximately half of the cells exhibited mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization as early as 6 h after Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT stimulation (Figure A–C), demonstrating the synergistic effects of Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT in exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction. PGAM5, a mitochondrial serine/threonine phosphatase, plays a pivotal role in mitochondrial quality control and stress responses through its distinct isoforms. The full-length isoform (PGAM5-L) is essential for clearing damaged mitochondria, promoting mitochondrial clearance, and preserving cellular homeostasis under stress conditions. In contrast, the cleaved short isoform (PGAM5-S) has been implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction and fragmentation, although its precise functions remain less well-defined. PGAM5-S is thought to impair mitochondrial dynamics and disrupt fusion machinery, contributing to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and further amplifying stress signals. These functional distinctions underscore the critical role of PGAM5-L in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and highlight the pathological consequences of its cleavage. To investigate PGAM5 cleavage dynamics under Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT treatment, we performed temporal Western blot analysis. Cu–Mn@SiO2 monotherapy induced partial PGAM5 cleavage, as indicated by the coexistence of the PGAM5-L and PGAM5-S isoforms. In contrast, combination therapy markedly accelerated the PGAM5 cleavage kinetics, showing progressive PGAM5-L depletion and concomitant PGAM5-S accumulation over time (Figure D). These results demonstrate that Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT exacerbates mitochondrial regulatory dysfunction through enhanced PGAM5 proteolytic processing. Given PGAM5-L’s critical role in mitochondrial damage, we next assessed the functional impact of Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT-induced PGAM5 cleavage on mitochondrial quality control. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis demonstrated divergent mitochondrial responses: monotherapy with Cu–Mn@SiO2 maintained largely intact mitochondrial morphology in most organelles, while selectively eliminating damaged mitochondria through clearance mechanisms (Figure E,F). In contrast, combinatorial treatment with SDT induced severe ultrastructural damage to mitochondria, as evidenced by the complete loss of normal organelle morphology (Figure G,H). This therapeutic dichotomy stems from PGAM5-L depletion, which disrupts mitochondrial quality control pathways, leading to accumulation of structurally compromised mitochondria through impaired damage resolution mechanisms.

No mortality occurred during the modeling process, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental protocol.

In conclusion, the therapeutic potential of Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticle-driven SDT for BPH was validated in a hormone-induced rat model. This approach effectively reversed key pathological hallmarks, leading to a significant reduction in prostate volume, restoration of urinary function, and amelioration of both glandular hyperplasia and stromal fibrosis at the histological level. Critically, the therapy demonstrated excellent target specificity; thermal imaging confirmed that sonication-induced hyperthermia was precisely confined to the prostate, safeguarding the surrounding tissues. Comprehensive biosafety evaluations, encompassing both short- and long-term multiorgan histopathology and blood analyses, revealed no discernible systemic toxicity. Collectively, these findings establish Cu–Mn@SiO2-mediated SDT as a highly promising, safe, and targeted nanotherapeutic strategy for the treatment of BPH.

Discussion

BPH remains a prevalent, age-related urological disorder where current therapeutic options, including pharmacological agents and surgical interventions, are circumscribed by systemic side effects and procedural risks. Our study introduces a nanotheranostic platform that addresses these limitations by leveraging sonosensitizing Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles to achieve targeted prostatic tissue ablation. Our approach demonstrates significant prostate volume reduction and restoration of urinary function in a preclinical model, establishing a new paradigm for ultra-minimally invasive BPH management with a superior safety profile.

However, the translational relevance of our findings necessitates careful consideration of the hormone-induced rat BPH model’s inherent limitations. While this model successfully recapitulates key pathological hallmarks prostatic enlargement, glandular hyperplasia, stromal fibrosis, and lower urinary tract symptomsit achieves these changes through supraphysiological hormone administration over 4 weeks, contrasting sharply with the decades-long multifactorial progression of human BPH. Human disease involves complex interactions among aging-related hormonal fluctuations, genetic predisposition, chronic inflammation with T-lymphocyte and macrophage infiltration, metabolic syndrome, and cellular senescence, factors largely absent in young hormone-treated rats. These pathophysiological differences may underestimate our platform’s anti-inflammatory potential and its efficacy across the heterogeneous BPH phenotypes observed clinically.

Nevertheless, the hormone-induced model provides valuable proof-of-concept validation for our therapeutic approach. The model’s reproducibility and predominance of androgen-driven hyperplasia align with dihydrotestosterone’s central role in human BPH pathogenesis, making it appropriate for evaluating tissue ablation strategies rather than hormonal interventions. Notably, our histological analyses revealing reduced collagen deposition and restored glandular architecture demonstrate that Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT effectively addresses both epithelial and stromal components, suggesting potential efficacy across diverse pathological presentations. For successful clinical translation, future investigations should incorporate aged animal models that better recapitulate chronic inflammation and senescence, validate the oxeiptosis mechanism using ex vivo human BPH tissues, and establish patient stratification criteria based on inflammatory biomarkers and prostatic volume to identify the optimal candidates for this novel therapeutic approach.

The therapeutic efficacy of the Cu–Mn@SiO2 platform is rooted in both its sophisticated nanostructure design and the precision of the SDT activation. Based on our BET analysis results, the nanoparticles exhibit an exceptionally high specific surface area of 310.252 m2/g with uniform mesopores centered at 3.44 nm, providing an optimal architecture for catalytic ROS generation. The bimetallic copper–manganese doping within this mesoporous silica framework creates a synergistic catalytic system, amplifying ROS generation far beyond what could be achieved with monometallic counterparts. Importantly, our stability studies demonstrate that these nanoparticles maintain excellent colloidal stability under physiological conditions (pH 7.4) with minimal ion leakage, while exhibiting pH-responsive behavior with rapid ion release (∼90% within 10 h) at acidic pH 5.6, which mimics the prostatic intracellular environment. This pH-triggered release mechanism ensures targeted therapeutic action specifically within the pathological tissue, while maintaining structural integrity during circulation. This controlled induction of oxidative stress disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, leading to cumulative damage to critical biomolecules. This strategy is consistent with our prior work where nanomaterial-mediated redox modulation effectively treated other pathologies, reinforcing the versatility of this approach. , In the context of BPH, where chronic oxidative stress is a known pathological driver, our platform transforms a disease-promoting factor into a therapeutic tool. By confining these ROS surges spatiotemporally, we achieve targeted cell death while minimizing the off-target effects that plague systemic therapies.

A key finding of our study is the elucidation of the specific cell death mechanism: oxeiptosisa caspase-independent programmed cell death pathway operating through KEAP1-PGAM5-AIFM1 axis. Our work provides the first experimental validation of oxeiptosis as a therapeutically viable target in BPH pathophysiology and medical application. Central to this pathway is mitochondrial phosphatase PGAM5, which acts as a molecular switch. To rigorously validate PGAM5′s mechanistic role, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments. PGAM5 depletion alone did not affect baseline proliferation in either BPH-1 or WPMY-1 cells, confirming that PGAM5 is not essential for normal cellular growth. However, PGAM5 knockdown significantly attenuated Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT-induced cell death, with markedly reduced apoptotic populations and inhibited AIFM1 dephosphorylation at Ser116. These rescue experiments definitively establish PGAM5 as the critical mediator linking ROS generation to oxeiptosis execution. Moreover, under a barrage of oxidative stress, full-length PGAM5 (PGAM5-L) is cleaved into its short, prodeath isoform (PGAM5-S). Our therapeutic regimen accelerates this isoform shift, creating a feed-forward loop of mitochondrial damage and dysfunction. This accumulation of defective mitochondria synergistically lowers the threshold for cell death, coupling targeted oxidative damage with enhanced cellular susceptibilitya dual therapeutic leverage point that redefines precision intervention in BPH.

The therapeutic efficacy extends beyond cellular mechanisms to tissue-level remodeling. Our histopathological analyses reveal that Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT not only reduces prostatic volume but also reverses pathological tissue architecture. The combination therapy significantly decreased both total collagen content and the collagen I/III ratio compared to monotherapy groups, indicating effective suppression of stromal fibrosisa hallmark of BPH progression. This antifibrotic effect is particularly noteworthy as prostatic fibrosis contributes to urethral compression and LUTS severity. The restoration of normal glandular morphology and reduction in periglandular collagen deposition suggest that our approach addresses both the epithelial hyperplasia and the stromal expansion components of BPH pathogenesis.

From a clinical perspective, the Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT strategy emerges as a transformative clinical alternative for BPH patients contraindicated for conventional surgical interventions. Approximately 20–30% of patients are excluded from transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), laser prostatectomy, or prostatic artery embolization (PAE) due to comorbidities or anesthesia risks. − While these procedures effectively alleviate symptoms, their utility is counterbalanced by complications including urinary retention, sexual dysfunction, and extended recovery periods. These limitations accentuate the critical demand for ultra-minimally invasive alternatives, particularly in patients with elevated perioperative risk profiles. Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT addresses this clinical gap through SDT-confined oxidative stress induction, achieving therapeutic efficacy while circumventing systemic toxicity and preserving genitourinary functionalitya vital advantage for comorbid patients.

BPH management presents unique therapeutic challenges compared to prostate cancer (PCa), primarily due to the absence of distinct molecular signatures and tumor-specific metabolic vulnerabilities in benign prostate tissue. While conventional therapies operate within finite therapeutic windows, the prostate’s anatomical adjacency to the rectum provides an optimal acoustic window for SDT-based interventions. This anatomical advantage enables noninvasive transrectal delivery of therapeutic ultrasounda modality already clinically validated for diagnostic imaging and biopsy guidance. , The prostate’s multiaccess capability (transrectal/transperineal) further enhances treatment flexibility, allowing precise energy deposition tailored to individual prostatic topography. These inherent anatomical merits position SDT-activated Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles as a paradigm-shifting solution, aligning with the escalating clinical demand for precise noninvasive therapies.

Addressing the technical challenges of ultrasound delivery to the deep-seated prostate is paramount for successful clinical translation of Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT. The prostate’s anatomical location, typically 0.5–1 cm from the rectal wall, presents both opportunities and challenges for SDT implementation. Our thermal imaging data demonstrated a precise temperature elevation localized to the prostatic region with minimal spread to adjacent tissues, validating the feasibility of focused ultrasound delivery. However, several strategies should be implemented to further enhance precision and safety. First, real-time image guidance using transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) or MRI-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) can enable accurate targeting and monitoring of the acoustic focal zone. These clinically established modalities allow for patient-specific treatment planning based on individual prostatic anatomy and pathology distribution. Second, phased array transducers with electronic beam steering capabilities can dynamically adjust the focal depth and volume, compensating for tissue heterogeneity and patient movement. This technology, already employed in high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) systems for prostate cancer, can be adapted for lower-intensity SDT applications.

Third, the implementation of advanced acoustic monitoring techniques can provide real-time feedback on treatment progression and tissue response. Temperature mapping using MR thermometry or ultrasound thermometry enables precise control of acoustic energy deposition, ensuring therapeutic temperatures remain within the target zone while protecting critical structures such as the rectum, bladder neck, and neurovascular bundles. Additionally, passive acoustic mapping (PAM) can detect and localize cavitation events, allowing operators to adjust ultrasound parameters in real-time to maintain optimal therapeutic conditions without exceeding safety thresholds. Our study’s ultrasound parameters (1 MHz, 0.5 W/cm2, 2 min) represent conservative values well below the threshold for thermal ablation, providing a significant safety margin. The successful outcomes achieved with these modest parameters suggest that further optimization could enhance efficacy while maintaining the safety profile observed in our preclinical model.

Our study bridges this therapeutic gap through spatiotemporal precision in prostatic foci ablation via SDT-confined ROS generation and biological safety through healthy tissue preservation. The nanoparticle-SDT synergy achieves therapeutic amplification unattainable through single-modality approaches, while transrectal administration eliminates risks associated with transurethral instrumentation. This ultra-minimally invasive paradigm enables accelerated recovery trajectories and lower complication profiles, particularly critical for patients with surgical contraindications, thereby expanding accessible treatment modalities in urological practice.

The in vivo behavior of Cu–Mn@SiO2 nanoparticles warrants careful consideration for clinical translation. While our study focused on therapeutic efficacy and short-term safety, understanding nanoparticle biodistribution, homing, and degradation kinetics is crucial for predicting long-term outcomes. Based on the established literature on mesoporous silica nanoparticles, several key trends can be anticipated. Following local prostatic injection, nanoparticles of similar size (200–300 nm) typically exhibit limited systemic circulation due to size-dependent retention at the injection site, which enhances therapeutic specificity while minimizing off-target accumulation. The mesoporous silica framework is known to undergo gradual biodegradation through hydrolysis of Si–O–Si bonds, releasing biocompatible silicic acid that is readily excreted via urine. Our pH-responsive ion release data showing minimal leakage at pH 7.4 but rapid release at pH 5.6 suggest that Cu and Mn ions remain largely sequestered until reaching the acidic microenvironment of inflamed prostatic tissue or intracellular compartments.

Previous studies on metal-doped silica nanoparticles indicate that released Cu2+ and Mn2+ ions are efficiently processed through existing metallothionein and superoxide dismutase pathways, with excess ions eliminated via hepatobiliary and renal routes. The local administration route employed in our study further minimizes systemic exposure compared to intravenous delivery. Nevertheless, comprehensive pharmacokinetic profiling using techniques such as inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and fluorescence imaging will be essential to validate these predictions and optimize dosing regimens for clinical application.

While our study establishes Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT as a mechanistically innovative therapy and a promising intervention for BPH, several critical questions merit further exploration. First, although we have demonstrated PGAM5-mediated oxeiptosis as the primary death mechanism, the potential interplay between oxeiptosis and parallel death pathways (e.g., ferroptosis, apoptosis) requires elucidation through multiomics analyses. Second, while our nanoparticles show excellent stability at physiological pH and controlled release in acidic environments, longitudinal studies must assess chronic toxicity and durabilityparticularly regarding nanoparticle biodistribution and delayed inflammatory responses. The pH-responsive behavior we observed suggests potential for targeted accumulation in pathological tissues, but comprehensive pharmacokinetic profiling remains essential for clinical translation. Finally, building on the oncotherapeutic success of analogous nanoplatforms in cancer models, adapting Cu–Mn@SiO2–SDT for malignant indications could unify therapeutic strategies across benign and malignant prostatic disease, leveraging shared anatomical targeting advantages while addressing distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. −

Conclusions

Our study establishes Cu–Mn@SiO2-mediated SDT as a mechanistically precise, ultra-minimally invasive therapeutic strategy for BPH. By integrating optimized nanostructure design, pH-responsive behavior, PGAM5-dependent oxeiptosis activation, and antifibrotic tissue remodeling, this platform addresses multiple pathological dimensions of BPH while maintaining an exceptional safety profile. These findings pave the way for the clinical translation of nanomedicine-based approaches in urological practice.
