Quantitative resource utilisation of patients with limited English proficiency (LEP) at a community hospital emergency department (ED) observational controlled study (LEP-ED2 study)
Authors: Matthew Mo Kin Kwok, Rupinder Thandi, Arshpreet Singh Manku, Rebecca Zhu, Janice Castillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with limited English proficiency (LEP) face communication barriers that can impact clinical assessment, prolong triage and affect diagnostic decision-making. Our Canadian community teaching hospital serves a linguistically diverse population and sought to understand the operational impact of LEP on emergency department (ED) workflow and resource use.We conducted a prospective observational controlled study (LEPED2) involving adult patients presenting to the ED with chest pain. 31 patients with LEP, identified as needing professional interpreter services, were compared with 43 patients with non-LEP. All patients with LEP received interpretation via a virtual video device. The primary outcome was triage time. Secondary outcomes included the utilisation of CT scans and hospital admission rates. Statistical analyses included one-tailed t-tests and χ2 tests.Patients with LEP required significantly more time for triage (mean 7.76 min) than patients with non-LEP (5.41 min), representing a 43% increase (p=0.0003). Although differences in CT utilisation and admission rates were not statistically significant, a trend towards increased diagnostic testing among patients with LEP was observed.Keywords: Emergency department, Health Equity, Health policy, Patient Advocacy, Standards of care

Introduction

Effective communication is foundational to delivering safe, timely and patient-centred care in emergency departments (EDs). However, communication barriers, particularly among patients with limited English proficiency (LEP), can significantly impede these efforts, resulting in disparities in care delivery and outcomes. Patients with LEP may face challenges in accurately describing their symptoms, understanding care instructions or navigating follow-up plans, leading to delays, miscommunication and suboptimal clinical decisions.

There is growing evidence that LEP is associated with increased healthcare utilisation and poorer outcomes in emergency care settings. For example, prior research has shown that patients with LEP are 24% more likely to return to the ED within 72 hours for unplanned visits, a potential marker of inadequate assessment, treatment or discharge communication.1 2 Other studies report that patients with LEP tend to have longer ED lengths of stay, undergo more diagnostic testing due to clinical uncertainty and are admitted to the hospital at higher rates than patients who are English-proficient.3,5 These findings raise concerns not only about the equity and quality of care for LEP individuals but also about the strain on healthcare system resources.

At our institution, we previously conducted an observational study that further explored these dynamics and found a trend towards higher hospital admission rates among patients with LEP, reinforcing the hypothesis that language barriers may contribute to increased resource utilisation and distinct care trajectories in the ED.6

The objective of this project is to examine the impact of LEP on resource utilisation in the ED. In particular, we examined the amount of triage time required to care for patients with LEP compared with patients with non-LEP. Triage time was selected as the primary outcome because it captures the immediate operational impact of communication barriers on ED workflow. Unlike downstream metrics, triage time reflects real-time clinician workload and the front-end equity implications of interpreter-mediated care. In addition, we examined secondary outcomes of whether patients with LEP are more likely to undergo a diagnostic CT scan and whether they are more likely to be admitted to the inpatient unit from the ED. CT utilisation and hospital admission were selected as secondary outcomes to indicate diagnostic uncertainty and clinical risk tolerance when language barriers limit history-taking. We hypothesise that language barriers result in measurable differences in care processes and outcomes. By systematically evaluating these variables, we aim to gain a deeper understanding of the operational and clinical implications of language barriers in the ED and identify areas where targeted interventions can improve care quality, efficiency and equity for this vulnerable patient population.

Methods

Participants

The study enrolled adult patients (aged ≥19 years) who presented to the ED at a community teaching hospital in British Columbia, Canada, with the chief complaint of chest pain. Participants were categorised as LEP or English Proficient (non-LEP) based on the triage nurse’s assessment of their ability to communicate in English. Patients were classified as LEP if they were ‘unable to communicate in English’ or had ‘limited ability to communicate in English’. Patients with non-LEP included native English speakers or those who demonstrated sufficient proficiency to communicate effectively.

Exclusion criteria included patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) on initial ECG, those requiring immediate resuscitation, those unable to provide informed consent due to cognitive or physical impairments and patients with LEP whose triage nurse was fluent in their primary language. These criteria were applied to ensure consistent triage timing and interpretation procedures across participants.

Procedures

This study employed a prospective observational design. Eligible patients underwent standard triage procedures performed by ED triage nurses. Patients presenting with chest pain underwent standard clinical triage immediately on arrival, including the assessment of vital signs, clinical stability and an ECG. Exclusion of STEMI was performed as part of routine triage care prior to the confirmation of study eligibility. LEP status was identified during triage. For patients with LEP, professional interpreter services were provided via a video-enabled device called Interpreter on Wheels (IOW).4 Triage time was defined as the duration from initiation to completion of clinical triage and did not include research consent. Triage time was measured in minutes by the triage nurse. Informed consent was obtained after triage by a research associate, who had no direct clinical role with the patient, using IOW. Demographic data (age, gender, preferred language) were collected, and relevant clinical outcomes, including CT scan utilisation and hospital admission status, were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system.7

Data collection

Data were collected prospectively by the dedicated research associate. Triage time was directly recorded by the triage nurse during the patient encounter. Clinical variables, including whether a CT scan was performed and whether the patient was admitted to the hospital, were extracted from the electronic medical records.

All data were anonymised, deidentified and stored in a secure, password-protected database accessible only to the study team. Each participant was assigned a unique study ID to ensure confidentiality and prevent linkage to personal identifiers.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using standard statistical software. Continuous variables (eg, triage time) were summarised using means, medians and IQRs and compared between groups using a one-tailed two-sample t-test, chosen due to unequal sample sizes and variance. Categorical outcomes (CT utilisation and hospital admission) were compared using χ2 testing. Statistical significance was defined as a p value <0.05.

Public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research. However, our research group has frequent surveys and quality improvement initiatives involving patients with LEP in our ED, addressing community-identified gaps in service delivery, and aims to inform future policy and planning to enhance equitable emergency care for linguistically diverse populations.

The linguistic profile of the LEP group showed that Mandarin was the most frequently reported primary language among patients with LEP, spoken by 19 individuals. This was followed by Cantonese (8), Punjabi (2), Portuguese (1) and Korean (1). The LEP group had a higher average age of 64.6 years compared with the non-LEP group, which had an average age of 54.9 years. Within the LEP group, females comprised a slight majority, with 18 females compared with 13 males. The non-LEP group showed a similar pattern, with 23 females and 20 males.

Among patients with LEP, the most common emergency diagnosis was chest pain not yet diagnosed (NYD), recorded in 15 individuals. This same diagnosis also appeared most frequently in the non-LEP group, affecting an equal number of patients (15). Other notable diagnoses in the LEP group included pneumonia (2 cases), chest wall pain (2), atrial fibrillation, arrhythmia not otherwise specified and viral illness. There was also a broad distribution of less frequent conditions, such as shortness of breath and unstable angina, each of which was seen in one patient. For patients with non-LEP, a similarly diverse range of diagnoses was observed. Along with chest pain NYD, there were multiple cases of abdominal pain, back strain, probable sternal fracture with vertigo and pulmonary embolism.

Primary outcome—triage time

This analysis compares triage times between patients with LEP and those who are English-proficient (non-LEP) at the ED, presented with the chief complaint of chest pain. Time values were originally recorded in MM:SS (minutes:seconds) format and then converted into minutes for analysis. The boxplot in figure 1 illustrates that patients with LEP typically experience longer triage times compared with their non-LEP counterparts. Specifically, the mean triage time for patients with LEP was 7.76 min compared with 5.41 min for patients with non-LEP. The median triage time was 7.5 min for patients with LEP, compared with 5.4 min for patients with non-LEP. This represents a 43% increase in mean triage time and a 39% increase in median triage time for patients with LEP. It is noted that an outlier triage time is recorded in the non-LEP cohort as a circle data point in figure 1, as one of the participants had a triage time that is more than 1.5 times the IQR from the lower or upper quartile.

Triage time comparing patients with limited English proficiency (LEP) versus patients who are English-speaking (non-LEP).

To assess whether triage times were significantly longer for patients with LEP compared with patients with non-LEP, a one-tailed two-sample t-test was conducted. This one-tailed test was chosen because the hypothesis specifically anticipated longer triage times for patients with LEP, making it appropriate to test for a directional difference rather than a general one. The analysis yielded a t-statistic of 3.66 and a one-tailed p value of 0.0003, indicating a statistically significant difference.

Secondary outcome—CT utilisation and hospital admission

A comparison was made of CT scan utilisation between patients with LEP and those who are English proficient who presented to the ED with the chief complaint of chest pain (figure 2). Among the 31 patients with LEP, four received a CT scan (12.9%), while among the 43 patients with non-LEP, four also received a CT scan (9.3%). The χ2 test yielded a test statistic of 0.013 with a p value of 0.91, indicating no statistically significant association between language proficiency and CT scan utilisation.

CT utilisation rate of patients with limited English proficiency (LEP) versus patients who are English-speaking (non-LEP).

Among the 31 patients with LEP, two were admitted to the hospital (6.5%) (figure 3). In comparison, five out of 43 patients with non-LEP were admitted (11.6%). To evaluate statistical significance, a χ2 test was performed. The χ2 test yielded a test statistic of 0.12 with a p value of 0.73, indicating no statistically significant difference in admission rates between the two groups.

Discussion

Study challenges

Recruitment and implementation challenges limited the study’s sample size and introduced potential bias. Triage nurses, already managing high workloads and time pressures, often lacked the capacity to identify or refer eligible patients with LEP consistently. The absence of an automated system to flag LEP status further complicated real-time recruitment. On the patient side, reluctance to participate was common, potentially due to anxiety, misunderstanding of the consent process or cultural hesitancy to engage in research. These factors reflect real-world complexities in conducting studies involving vulnerable populations in acute care settings.

Operational issues also affected protocol adherence. The IOW device, although mandated for LEP triage encounters, was occasionally unavailable due to technical issues or competing clinical demands. In such cases, ad hoc interpretation, by family or untrained staff, was used, resulting in the exclusion of those encounters. Moreover, some staff may have underestimated language barriers, opting not to use interpretation when a patient’s English appeared ‘good enough’, further limiting enrolment and potentially underestimating the full impact of LEP. LEP classification relied on triage nurse judgement, reflecting real-world ED practice in the absence of a standardised screening tool. This may have resulted in the misclassification of patients with borderline proficiency, biasing results towards the null.

The study design was powered for triage time, and secondary outcomes were exploratory. While triage time occurs in nearly all ED encounters and is well suited to detect operational differences with modest sample sizes, secondary outcomes in our study of CT utilisation and hospital admission occur less frequently and require substantially larger samples to detect statistically significant differences. As a result, analysis of these secondary outcomes should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive.

Demographics of participants

In our study, patients with LEP were older (mean age 64.6 vs 54.9), consistent with national data indicating that older immigrants are more likely to face language barriers.8 Most LEP participants were Mandarin or Cantonese speakers, reflecting local census data and reinforcing the need for language services tailored to community needs.9

Primary outcome—triage time

This study quantitatively demonstrates that patients with LEP experience significantly longer triage times in the ED. On average, triage for patients with LEP took 7.76 min, compared with 5.41 min for patients with non-LEP, a 43% increase. Although modest in isolation, this delay becomes operationally significant when scaled across high-volume EDs, highlighting the impact of language barriers on clinical workflows and resource planning. From a system’s perspective, a 2.35-min increase per LEP patient may appear modest. However, this could translate into nearly two additional triage hours in an ED seeing 50 patients with LEP per day, thereby impacting staffing, throughput and care equity. Health systems must plan for this time burden and ensure interpreter services are reliably available and efficiently integrated.

Interpreter-mediated communication inherently takes longer due to the sequential nature of translation and the need for clarification. Nurses may also take additional time to ensure accurate histories and make informed, safe decisions. However, the increase in triage time was not as large as expected, raising concern that communication may have been abbreviated in the busy ED. This underscores the risk that patients with LEP receive fewer comprehensive assessments and highlights the importance of integrating interpreter access into standard workflows, rather than treating it as an optional or secondary step.

The observed increase in triage time should not be interpreted as a deficiency of virtual interpretation technology. Rather, it reflects the inherent time required for accurate interpreter-mediated communication. Any interpretation modality—virtual or in-person—necessitates additional time. The operational challenge is therefore not the elimination of this time but proactive planning to ensure equitable care delivery.

Secondary outcome—CT utilisation and hospital admission

There was no statistically significant difference between patients with LEP and non-LEP in CT utilisation or hospital admissions, although trends suggested higher CT use and lower admissions among patients with LEP. These patterns may reflect cautious diagnostic behaviour in the face of communication uncertainty or premature discharge due to incomplete understanding of risk.

Generalisability of findings

The generalisability of the study is limited by several factors. It focused only on chest pain, a relatively protocolised complaint, and may not reflect care challenges associated with more subjective symptoms. The study also assumes a uniform LEP impact across language groups, which may oversimplify real-world variation in language and cultural nuance. Additionally, the findings reflect a context with relatively strong interpreter infrastructure, while other sites may face even greater challenges.

Ultimately, this study reinforces that language proficiency is a determinant of care quality, efficiency and equity. As healthcare systems become increasingly diverse, planning must include staffing models that account for language-related workflow demands and investments in accessible, consistent interpreter services. Evidence-informed strategies, such as those supported by this study, are crucial for ensuring equitable care delivery for all patients, regardless of their language ability.

Future research

Future research should expand the scope beyond chest pain to include other common ED complaints where language barriers may affect clinical decision-making, such as abdominal pain, dizziness or psychiatric presentations. Larger sample sizes across multiple sites will improve generalisability and enable subgroup analyses by language or acuity. Additionally, health system leaders should explore sustainable funding models that account for the additional time and complexity of LEP care, as well as the long-term cost-effectiveness of professional interpretation in reducing diagnostic errors, return visits and adverse events.

Conclusions

This study provides quantitative evidence that patients with LEP require significantly more time during triage in the ED compared with their English-proficient counterparts. Specifically, patients with LEP experienced a 43% increase in mean triage time, a statistically significant difference that has important implications for ED operations, staff workload and equitable care delivery. While secondary outcomes, such as CT utilisation and admission rates, did not show statistically significant differences, trends suggest that clinical caution during LEP encounters may lead to increased diagnostic testing. These findings reinforce the importance of structured language support and underscore the operational burden of communication barriers.

Based on the study’s findings, several actionable recommendations emerge. First, EDs serving linguistically diverse populations should incorporate LEP-related triage time into human resource planning. Adjustments to staffing models, especially during peak hours, may be necessary to accommodate the cumulative time burden imposed by language barriers. Second, interpreter services must be further integrated into ED workflows to ensure timely, consistent access. This includes ensuring the availability of tools such as IOW, minimising downtime caused by technical issues and embedding interpreter activation into electronic triage systems. Third, triage nurses and frontline staff should receive ongoing training on how to efficiently engage interpreter services and communicate with patients with LEP without compromising assessment quality.

As healthcare becomes more linguistically and culturally diverse, studies like this are critical to advancing equity-informed healthcare delivery. A proactive, data-informed approach to language access planning will help ensure that all patients, regardless of English proficiency, receive timely, safe and high-quality care in emergency settings.
